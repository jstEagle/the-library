## 19. The Inquiry

The carriages did not come for celebration.

Petra knew it before the first wheel touched the harbor road, in the way she knew weather before it showed itself — a pressure behind the ears, a flatness in the gulls' voices. Government horses at dawn on Armistice morning were not government horses at all. They were Corrigan's horses, and Corrigan had not waited for bells to make his move.

She was right on both counts. By the time she'd pinned her hair and woken Nell, two carriages stood in the lane below the lighthouse, steaming like cattle, and Magistrate Corrigan descended from the first with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once been late for anything that mattered. Behind him came a clerk carrying an iron document case, and behind the clerk — walking, because no one had offered him a seat — came Quill, coat collar up, eyes red-rimmed from a night he had clearly spent awake.

"Mrs. Halloran." Corrigan removed his hat as though the wind had forced him to. "A historic day. I thought we ought not let it pass without concluding our small business."

"Our business concluded three days ago," Petra said. "You were told so."

"I was *told* many things." He smiled with all the warmth of a ledger's ruled line. "Telling and proving are different trades. Officer Quill has been instructed to take your formal statement this morning. Here, if you prefer. I know how attached you are to your walls."

Quill said nothing. That silence said more than any objection would have.

"The kitchen, then," Petra said. "It's the warmest room, and I'll want tea. If I'm to be written into history, I'd rather not shiver while it happens."

* * *

They sat where the village had always sat across from her: Corrigan and the clerk on the bench side of the table, Quill at the end with his notebook open, Nell sent upstairs but plainly listening through the floorboards, because every board in that house had learned to carry sound the way the sea carried light.

"State your name and station," the clerk recited.

"Petra Halloran. Keeper of the Carrickmoor light."

"For the record," Corrigan began pleasantly, "how long have you kept the ledger of debts and favors maintained by your late husband?"

Petra folded her hands. She had rehearsed this moment the way she rehearsed lamp changes — not the words, but the hands. Steady hands, steady voice. A keeper's testimony, nothing more.

"I keep accounts of flour, oil, net-mending, and kindness," she said. "I have done since Tomas died. Before that, they were his."

"And do those accounts record shipments received at Carrickmoor harbor between the years 1915 and 1918?"

"They record what was owed and what was paid. The sea brings what it brings. I log the weather."

Corrigan's smile thinned by a degree. "Mrs. Halloran. You are aware that obstruction of a lawful inquiry carries penalties that a widow alone on this rock cannot easily survive."

"I am aware," Petra said, "of what a widow alone on this rock can survive. I have survived four years of it. You'll forgive me if your penalties don't frighten me more than February does."

Quill's pen scratched. She could not tell what he was writing, and understood suddenly that it didn't matter — that whatever he wrote down this morning would be shaped less by her answers than by what he had decided already, somewhere in the sleepless dark, to do with them.

"Then let us proceed simply," Corrigan said. "Produce the ledger. All of it. Every page."

"There is no ledger to produce."

The room went very still. Even the kettle seemed to hold its breath.

"Explain," Corrigan said softly.

"A fire," Petra said. "In the workshop forge. Two nights past, during the storm work. Sparks caught some old papers I'd brought out to burn — packing paper, spoiled records, pages gone damp and moldy beyond reading. I saved what could be saved." She turned to Quill, and only to Quill. "What's left is the honest book. Flour owed the O'Shea widow. Nets mended for the Byrne boys. Oil advanced against winter. Debts of bread and labor, entered fair, paid fairer. That book I will put under your hand today. The rest went up in sparks, and I mourned it no more than one mourns spoiled paper."

"You burned—" Corrigan started.

"*Sparks,*" Petra repeated, and looked at him until he heard the word properly.

Quill closed his notebook. Opened it again. Wrote one line.

"Mrs. Halloran," he said, and his voice was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour, "I have no further questions regarding the coded entries, as no such entries have been produced to this inquiry. I would, however, like to enter into the record your account of the storms of November last, the keeping of the light through both, and the condition of the lamp mechanism. The Board will want it."

"The Board," Corrigan began, "will want—"

"The Board," Quill said, still writing, "will want accurate testimony about a navigational aid that failed catastrophically mid-storm and was repaired by hand by a twelve-year-old girl. Which is the matter actually within my commission, Magistrate. Contraband requires evidence. I have none. What I have is a light that stayed lit, a lens ground true by civilians, and a report due in Dublin by Friday. I intend to file it."

* * *

She gave him the rest of the morning.

She testified to the debts — read aloud, entry by entry, from the surviving book while the clerk's pen raced: Mrs. O'Shea, flour, repaid in darning and salt fish. Byrne boys, nets, repaid in herring shares. Dr. Fennelly, oil, repaid in attendance at Nell's fever. Page after page of a village keeping itself alive on credit and trust, and Petra watched Quill understand, line by line, what she had understood years ago: that the ledger was never really about money. It was a map of who could be counted on, and the counting was the point.

She testified to the storms. The seawall breached, the church full of families, the sandbags passed hand to hand down a chain of neighbors who owed each other everything and nothing. Quill's own name appeared in her account — hauling bags till his palms bled — and he noted it without expression, though the clerk glanced up twice.

She testified to the light. Both nights. The crack in the clockwork. Her daughter's hands on the crank beside hers through the worst hours, turning the backup lens against the shoals until the mechanism was mended and the ships stood off safe.

"And in all this time," Quill said quietly, when she finished, "did you observe anything at Carrickmoor harbor inconsistent with lawful trade?"

Petra met his eyes. There it was — the door he was leaving open, the last exit on the line. He was offering her the chance to say it herself, to hang the village or save it with one sentence, and both of them knew she would not take it.

"No, Officer," she said. "I observed fog, scarcity, and neighbors helping neighbors through a war. If there was more than that, it never showed its face to me, and it isn't in my book."

"Noted," Quill said, and closed the notebook for good.

Corrigan surged to his feet. "This is a farce. This woman has obstructed—"

"This woman," Quill said, rising more slowly, gathering his papers with the deliberation of a man setting out chess pieces, "has given me a complete statement on every matter within my commission. What lies outside my commission, Magistrate, is a question I've been asking myself since the day you handed me this assignment. Why does a customs inquiry into a fishing village require a magistrate's personal oversight? Why do requisition orders arrive stamped with your office weeks before the Admiralty confirms them? Why does a man who claims to want the truth get visibly angry every time someone starts telling it?"

"I am *protecting*—"

"You are *named*," Quill said.

The word dropped into the kitchen like a stone into a well. Petra heard it land all the way down.

"Entries in a ledger I no longer possess, Magistrate — which means I can neither confirm nor deny them, which means they exist now only as questions, and questions travel to Dublin faster than carriages. My report will state that the inquiry into Carrickmoor was initiated on information traceable to your office, that no contraband was found, and that the conduct of oversight raised sufficient concern to warrant independent review. I'd advise you to be in the city when it arrives. Men in your position generally prefer to hear such things from friends first."

For a long moment Corrigan looked at Quill the way a man looks at a tide gauge — measuring how much water had risen while he wasn't watching. Then he straightened his coat, replaced his hat, and smiled at Petra with something that was almost admiration and entirely a threat.

"History will judge who kept faith here, Mrs. Halloran."

"History," Petra said, "keeps better books than either of us. Good morning, Magistrate."

* * *

He left first. The carriages ground away down the harbor road, taking their steam and their menace with them, and the quiet they left behind was so complete that Petra could hear the sea working the shingle below the point, patient as ever.

Quill stayed. He stood in her kitchen holding his cap, looking suddenly like what he probably was underneath the office: a tired man from a town she'd never seen, who had come to the edge of the world to push paper and had found, instead, people.

"You lied to me," he said. Not accusing. Cataloguing. "Three weeks ago. About the ledger being complete."

"Yes."

"And I've just committed professional suicide in your kitchen on the strength of a lie I can't prove and won't try to."

"Yes," Petra said again.

He almost smiled. "Why should I believe anything you told me today? The storms, the light, the debts — why aren't those lies too?"

"Because you watched me keep the light," Petra said. "You hauled the sandbags yourself. Everything else I said today, you saw with your own eyes or held in your own hands. That's the only kind of testimony worth giving, Officer. It's the only kind worth keeping." She nodded toward his satchel. "Write it however you must. But write it true."

He looked at her for a long moment — the look of a man checking a bearing against a known star. Then he took out the statement copy, signed it, dated it, and slid it across the table to her.

"One copy for the file. One for you. In law it means nothing. Keep it anyway."

"I keep things," Petra agreed.

At the door he paused. Outside, the bells had begun again — distant, real this time, rolling up the coast from Dunmore, from Waterford, from everywhere the news had reached overnight. The armistice. Actually signed. Actually over.

"They'll ring all day," Quill said. "And tomorrow the world will start pretending the last four years made sense. It didn't. Neither did this inquiry. But the light was kept, Mrs. Halloran. Whatever else gets written, that part's true, and it's yours."

He shook her hand — formally, in front of the clerk, so it would appear in no one's memory as anything but procedure — and walked down the path toward the village, where the bells were waiting for him too.

* * *

Nell came downstairs when the carriage sounds had faded entirely. She stood in the doorway holding the signed copy's twin thought — Petra could see it in her face, the same arithmetic running behind her eyes that ran behind her mother's.

"He named him," Nell said. "Quill. He put Corrigan's name in the report instead of ours."

"He put the truth where it would do the most good," Petra said. "That's what testimony is. Not confession. Authorship. You decide what the record says about you, and you sign it."

Nell considered this with the gravity of a girl who had spent a season learning that records could be forged, decoded, burned, and redeemed. "And what does ours say now?"

Petra took down the honest ledger — the survivor, smelling faintly of smoke — and set it on the table between them. Flour. Nets. Oil. Bread. Kindnesses entered fair and paid fairer, four years of a village feeding itself on trust while the world burned.

"It says we kept the light," she said. "Every other line, we wrote ourselves."

Below them, the bells rang on, and out on the water the first free ships of peacetime stood in past the shoals, homeward bound, guided in by everything they had chosen to carry and everything they had chosen, finally, to let the sea take.
