## 20. The Signing

The bells began before dawn.

Petra was at the lamp when she heard them — a thin, wavering sound carried up from Carrickmoor on the wind, the church bell and then the chapel bell answering it, and then, improbably, the school bell that hadn't rung for anything but fire drills in four years. She stood with her hand still on the lens housing, oil rag forgotten in her other hand, and let the sound move through her like weather.

Nell came up the stairs two at a time, hair unbraided, eyes wide. "Mam. Mam, they're saying it's signed. They're saying it's over."

"Who's saying?"

"Everyone. Mrs. O'Shea sent the Byrne boys down the hill to tell us — they're banging on every door in the village." Nell caught her breath, pressed both hands flat against the cold iron rail. "Is it true? Can it just be *over* like that? On paper?"

Petra looked out the lantern room at the grey sea, the grey sky, the light turning its slow morning arc toward extinction as the sun gathered itself behind the clouds. Four years of war, and it ended the way most things ended here — with a sound carried on the wind and everyone asked to believe it.

"Paper's how most of the world ends and begins," she said. "You know that better than anyone."

Nell didn't smile at that. Petra wished she had. But the girl only nodded, solemn as a clerk, and went down to put the kettle on, because even at the end of a war someone had to make tea.

* * *

By eight o'clock the village had come up the cliff path in a loose procession, the way people came to a funeral or a launching, and stood about in the yard outside the lighthouse gate not quite knowing what to do with themselves. There were no flags. Nobody owned flags anymore; bunting had gone to the wrapping of parcels years ago. Mrs. O'Shea had brought a jar of the blackcurrant preserves she'd been hoarding against some occasion she'd never been able to name until this one. The Byrne boys were running circuits around the fence posts, burning off something enormous in their blood.

Petra opened the workshop because there was nowhere else to put them all, and because she wanted them to see it standing.

They filed through quietly, these people who had spent the autumn watching requisition notices go up and come down, watching officers measure the windows and note the dimensions of the door. The grinding bench where she'd spent three months restoring the spare Fresnel elements. The polishing cloths hung in rows like washing. The new brass fittings Nell had fitted herself, her small hands steady on the tap wrench while her mother held the work true.

"They said they'd have it by Christmas," old Byrne said from the doorway, his cap in his hands. "The workshop. Said the navy wanted the glass for range lights somewhere."

"The order died unsigned," Petra said. "Quill told me last night. Whatever clerk was meant to countersign it was busy with bigger paperwork." She ran her thumb along the bench edge, feeling the grooves Tomas had worn into it, the ones she'd worn deeper. "It stands."

The words did more work than she intended. Mrs. O'Shea put her hand briefly on Petra's arm, and Petra felt in that touch everything the woman had confessed in the church vestry after the second storm, everything they had agreed never to say aloud again: the crates in the caves, the coded entries, the pages gone into the forge smoke before dawn. Survival itself had been the crime, and survival was what they'd all been granted.

"It stands," Byrne repeated, as if testing the load-bearing quality of the phrase.

The village moved through the workshop like pilgrims through a shrine, touching nothing, and Petra understood that this too was testimony — wordless, collective, the only kind left.

* * *

Quill came at noon, walking up from the harbor road with his kit bag over his shoulder and his greatcoat collar turned up against the wind. He looked, Petra thought, as though he'd finally slept — the bruised exhaustion around his eyes softened, his step lighter by some measurable degree. A man who had set down something heavy.

Corrigan had been recalled the evening before. Two men from Dublin Castle had arrived on the afternoon packet, quiet as thieves, and taken the magistrate away on the same boat without ceremony or explanation. The village had watched from the quay in a silence that Petra recognized: the silence of people who knew exactly why and would die before saying so. Quill's report, she understood, had named names — Corrigan's names, entries she'd decoded in the ledger's margins now transposed into an officer's careful prose, stripped of every villager, implicating only the powerful. She had never asked how he'd known which parts to keep. He had never offered to explain. That was the pact.

She met him at the gate.

"Mrs. Halloran." He removed his hat. Up close she could see the greying at his temples that hadn't been there in October, when he'd first stood in her kitchen asking after her husband's ledger with tired courtesy. Three weeks, he'd said then. He'd given her five.

"You're leaving," she said.

"This afternoon's packet. There's nothing further to investigate." He glanced past her at the lighthouse, white against the scudding clouds. "The inquiry is closed. My report is filed. What remains of—" he paused, chose the word with visible care, "—of the record, is sufficient."

"What remains," Petra agreed.

He shifted the kit bag on his shoulder. For a moment the bureaucrat's mask slipped and she saw the man beneath: tired, decent, compromised in ways he'd spend years not examining. "I've been doing this work nine years," he said. "Customs, excise, inquiries. I used to believe the files were the country. Write the file correctly and the country follows." He looked out at the sea. "Your husband understood something I'm only learning. That a ledger can be two documents at once. The one you show and the one you keep."

"Tomas kept insurance," Petra said. "Against the living."

"And you burned it."

"I burned what couldn't be carried."

Quill nodded slowly, and she saw that he would never ask her directly, that the question and the answer had already exchanged themselves between them somewhere in the long autumn, probably in the forge-light of the night she'd stood witness to nothing at all. He extended his hand.

She took it. His grip was dry and firm and brief.

"There'll be a commendation in the file," he said. "For the light kept through the storms. November thirteenth especially." He put his hat back on. "It's earned. It's also useful — a lighthouse keeper commended for duty distracts from other questions. Consider it the last favor I do anyone in this village."

"And if they come asking again someday? Someone else, another inquiry, another seal on an envelope?"

Quill smiled for the first time since she'd known him — a small, crooked, genuinely human thing. "Then they'll find a file that says what happened here is exactly what your testimony says happened. Debts. Storms. A light kept burning." He turned to go, then stopped. "You know, Mrs. Halloran, in my experience the armistice doesn't end wars. It just moves them indoors, onto paper, where people like me fight them line by line. But today—" he tipped his head toward the village, the bells, the smell of woodsmoke and preserves, "—today the paper lost. Enjoy it. You've paid for it."

He walked down the cliff path without looking back, and Petra stood at the gate until the sea took him from view.

* * *

The light turned at sundown.

Petra had lit it at dusk as she had every night for eleven years, but tonight the whole village was there to watch — packed into the lantern room and spilling down the stairwell, breath fogging the glass, children perched on the landings. The storm damage was repaired, the mechanism oiled and true, the spare elements polished to a shine that hurt the eye. When she released the clockwork brake and the great lens began to turn, catching the flame and throwing it seaward in its long patient sweep, a sound went up from the stairwell that was half cheer and half sob.

The first rotation in peacetime. The same light that had guided blockade runners and patrol boats and fishing crews home through four winters of fear, now sweeping over water that belonged, tonight, to no one's enemy.

Nell stood beside her at the lamp, shoulder to shoulder. Neither spoke. There was nothing that needed saying that their hands hadn't already said across three months of grinding glass and cranking the backup wheel through the worst hours of the second storm — reconciliation conducted entirely in the language of work, apology unnecessary where forgiveness had been built joint by joint.

Below, in the yard, someone had started a fiddle. The sound rose thin through the tower's iron bones.

Petra watched the beam swing out and return, out and return, and felt the grief move through her at last — not the sharp grief of the letter arriving, nor the curdled grief of Tomas's hidden hand in the ledger, but something older and simpler: he wasn't here. He should have been here. He should have been standing at this lamp with his coat smelling of pipe smoke, telling her the mechanism wanted adjusting, keeping his own counsel about crates and caves and tides. Whatever else he had been — smuggler, insurer, stranger — he had been hers, and the war had taken him before she could finish finding out who he was.

The joy was real. It simply cast shadows, like everything the light touched.

"Mam." Nell's voice was low, under the fiddle music. "What happens tomorrow?"

Petra considered the question honestly. Tomorrow the village would wake with the war over and the smuggling buried and the ledger gutted, and they would have to learn to live inside the shape of what they'd done — the burned pages, the silences agreed upon, the debts of a kind no book could hold. Tomorrow Mrs. O'Shea would pass her in the lane and they would nod and neither would say the word *insurance*. Tomorrow the Admiralty clerks would process the dead requisition order and the world would roll on over the small true history of Carrickmoor like tide over footprints.

"Tomorrow," Petra said, "I start a new ledger."

Nell looked up at her. In the rotating light her face went gold, then dark, then gold again, like something surfacing and submerging in a bright sea. "And what will you put in it?"

Petra reached into her apron pocket and drew out the fresh book she'd bought from the stationer's the week before — plain cloth boards, unruled, waiting. She set it on the ledge beside the log, open to the first blank page.

"Not debts," she said. "I've kept score long enough for one lifetime. So has everyone in this village, one way or another." She picked up the pen. "Something else. Something worth carrying forward."

The beam swept the horizon. Out beyond the shoals, far off, a ship's light answered — a small green star rising and dipping, homeward bound, trusting the shore to keep its promise.

Petra dipped the pen, and wrote the first entry.
