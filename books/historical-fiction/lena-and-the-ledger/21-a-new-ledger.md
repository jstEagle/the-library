## 21. A New Ledger

The stationer in Dunmore had only the one book left, and it was wrong for the purpose — a school copybook, its cover soft as cloth from sitting too long on a shelf, its pages ruled for a child's arithmetic. Petra bought it anyway. She had gone in intending to buy nothing at all; she had simply needed to be somewhere the walls did not smell of salt and old smoke, somewhere a person could stand among ledgers and not feel them leaning.

The shopkeeper wrapped it in brown paper without being asked. "For accounts?" she said.

"For gifts," Petra said, and heard how strange it sounded, and let it sound strange.

She walked back along the coast road with the parcel under her arm and the wind at her back doing most of the work, which was the sea's way of apologizing after November. The storms had moved off southward, leaving behind their calling cards: a new channel scoured between the rocks below the point, the seawall patched with fresh mortar that looked like scar tissue, half a fence belonging to the Byrnes lying in a field forty yards inland as though the water had grown tired of carrying it. The village was out mending itself. She could hear hammers from the harbor road, and someone laughing — actually laughing — at something that had probably not been funny a month ago.

At home, Nell had claimed the kitchen table with her drawings spread across it, and Petra cleared a corner without comment. This was their treaty now, unwritten but firm: the table belonged to both of them, and neither asked what the other kept there.

Petra set the new book down, untied the string, and opened it to the first page.

Her hand hovered above the ruling. It should have been easy. She had been writing entries since before she could properly hold a pen straight — Tomas had taught her, laughing, saying a keeper who couldn't keep accounts would soon keep nothing at all. Date, name, article, amount owed or settled. Forty years of that grammar lived in her wrist. But the grammar was exactly what she meant to break, and her hand knew it, and hesitated like a horse at a bridge.

*What do I write*, she thought, *when no one owes anything?*

She thought about that for a long while. Outside, the gulls were working the tide line, screaming their small greedy arguments. The kettle ticked toward boiling. Nell's pencil moved steadily beside her, and the sound of it — that ordinary, patient scratching — steadied Petra more than any prayer had managed in twenty years.

Then she picked up the pen and wrote:

*December 2nd, 1918.*

And beneath it, because an entry needed a subject and she refused to invent one:

*A loaf of bread and two tins of condensed milk, sent to Officer Quill, care of the Dunmore station, for his journey.*

Not *owed*. Not *to be repaid*. She wrote *sent*, and found that the word fit her hand better than any she had written all year.

Quill had left on Tuesday's train, three days after the inquiry closed, three days after he'd stood in her kitchen with his hat in his hands and said, in the voice of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten its shape: "I've filed my report, Mrs. Halloran. It says what it says." What it said, the whole village understood by now, was Corrigan's name in the first paragraph and hers barely in the last. He'd shaken her hand at the door like they were equals, which — she was still deciding whether this flattered or insulted her — was perhaps the truest thing anyone had done to her all year.

He had not eaten on the train. She knew this because Nell, who missed nothing, had watched him buy a newspaper and nothing else at the kiosk and had come home and reported it with the flat gravity of a court witness. So: bread. And milk, because a man who forgot to eat surely forgot to drink. It was not gratitude, exactly. Gratitude implied the debt had been discharged and the account closed. This was something else, some new instrument she was learning by ear.

The second entry came easier:

*Lamp oil, one full can, delivered to the O'Shea house. No charge. Mrs. O'Shea's lamp has been burning dry since the storm took her stores.*

Mrs. O'Shea had protested, of course. Had stood in the doorway with her chin up and her hands knotted in her apron and said, "I'll not take charity off you, Petra Halloran," and Petra had said, "It's not charity, it's oil," and something passed between them then that neither of them would ever put into words — because what could be said between two women who had each, in different ways, held the same terrible knowledge through the same terrible winter? Mrs. O'Shea had taken the can. That was the whole conversation, and it had been sufficient.

Petra turned the page and found herself writing a third entry without having decided to:

*Nell Byrne, twelve years, repaired the backup lens gearing unassisted during the storm of the 14th. Entered here so it is never lost.*

That one made her throat tighten. She read it over twice, and left it, and understood that this book was going to be a stranger thing than she'd planned — part account, part chronicle, part whatever a mother writes when she cannot say the words aloud. Let it be strange. Strange books survived; she had proof of that.

Nell leaned over, read upside down as children do, and said nothing, but her ears went pink, and she bent lower over her drawing, hiding her face in the honest labor of shading a boat.

* * *

They walked the shore on Sunday, when the tide went out far enough to expose the caves below the black rocks — the ones Nell had explored in October, the ones where the crates had hidden through the blockade years with their shipping marks stamped neat as scripture.

Petra hadn't been down there since before Tomas died. There was no reason to go; the caves were empty now, had been empty even before the inquiry, the last shipment vanished the same week her husband's heart stopped on the gallery stairs. But Nell asked — asked plainly, the way she asked everything now, no more circling, no more testing, just the direct question: "Will you come see them with me?" — and Petra heard in it everything the girl wasn't asking. Whether her mother could look at the place without flinching. Whether the family that had lived above a smuggling route for years could walk its floor together and still be a family walking.

So they went, boots sinking in wet sand, the December light low and silver and forgiving of nothing.

The cave mouth was wider than Petra remembered. Inside, the air smelled of cold stone and weed, and their lantern threw shadows that climbed the walls like slow animals. Nell led her past the first chamber to the back, where a ledge ran along the wall, and stopped.

"Here," Nell said. "This is where I found the crate."

Petra set the lantern down. The ledge was bare. Of course it was bare. But she crouched and put her palm flat against the stone anyway, the way you touch a grave, and felt the cold come up through her glove like a message from somewhere patient.

"He used this room," she said. It wasn't a question anymore. The coded entries had settled that months ago — the dates, the tides, the initials in that second hand she now recognized as Tomas's own, disguised the way a man disguises himself when he expects someday to be read. Her husband had sat on this stone, or one near it, counting contraband by lamplight while she trimmed wicks overhead and believed herself the keeper of the light.

"Yes," Nell said. Then, carefully: "Do you hate him?"

The question hung in the cold air. Petra stayed crouched, looking at the blank wall where a shipping mark had once been stenciled, and let herself actually consider it instead of performing an answer.

"I hated the ledger," she said finally. "For a long time I hated the ledger, and then I hated the parts of it I couldn't understand, and then I burned those parts, and what was left—" She stopped. Started again. "Your father kept score because keeping score is how frightened people survive. Every entry was a wall around us. Some of the bricks were stolen. I know that now. I'm not going to pretend otherwise, not to you." She looked up at her daughter, whose face in the lantern light was Tomas's face younger, minus the secrets. "But I don't hate him. I think he was a man drowning quietly in a village full of drowning people, and he grabbed whatever floated, and some of what floated was wrong. And at the end I think he was trying to make sure we'd float too."

"By insurance," Nell said. "Against everyone."

"Including us, if it came to that." Petra stood, knees cracking. "Which is why the new book is different."

"The gift book."

"The gift book." She smiled despite herself. Nell had coined it in November, half-mocking, wholly serious, the way twelve-year-olds coin things — and the name had stuck because names stick when they're true. "Come on. Tide's turning."

But Nell didn't move. She was looking at the back wall of the cave, at a spot Petra's lantern had already swept twice without finding anything, and her brow had the particular furrow Petra had learned to dread and love in equal measure.

"There's another chamber," Nell said. "Past the ledge. I couldn't get in in October — too much rockfall. The storm must have shifted it, because the gap's open now."

"Nell—"

"It's not contraband. Whatever's in there, the ring didn't know about it, because if they had, they'd have emptied it like everything else." She was already moving, sideways through the gap, her voice echoing oddly. "It's small. Come look."

Petra followed, because she always would, because that was the whole architecture of her life — following her daughter into dark places with a lantern and her eyes open.

The inner chamber was a pocket, hardly bigger than a confessional. And there on a natural shelf of stone, dry above the highest tideline, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age: a box. Small. Wooden. With Tomas's initials cut into the lid — not disguised this time, not coded, carved plain and deep, in the hand she'd known across a breakfast table for fifteen years.

Nell reached for it. Petra caught her wrist — gently, but she caught it.

"Together," she said.

They lifted it down between them. It was heavier than its size suggested. Somewhere inside it, something shifted with a dull metallic slide, and Petra's heart performed a slow, complicated maneuver she had no ledger to record.

Above them, faint through the rock, the lighthouse horn sounded the turn of the tide.

"Home," Petra said. "We open it at home, in the light."

Nell nodded. But as they climbed back out into the silver afternoon, Petra saw her daughter glance once more at the dark mouth of the inner chamber, and saw on her face the expression of a girl already composing the first entry in a ledger of her own.
