## 1. What the Tide Keeps

The tide went out at four o'clock, and Odile Ishikawa went out with it, the way she had gone out with it every morning for as long as she could remember — barefoot on the wet sand, a tin box under her arm, her mother's voice still hanging in the dark behind her like smoke.

*Carry only what you can lift.*

It was the first lesson her mother had ever taught her, before letters, before numbers, before she could have understood what it meant to be owned by a piece of paper. Her mother had said it while packing their whole household into three bundles the year they came to Meridian Harbor, and she said it now every time Odile left the company house: not as comfort, but as arithmetic. Everything you love must fit in your arms. Everything else is already lost.

Odile was fifteen years old and had been indentured for fourteen of them. Tomorrow would make fifteen exactly — her sixteenth year of service beginning, though no one at the Meridian Trading Company called it a birthday. The ledgers called it *renewal of term*. There would be no cake. There would be a clerk named Mr. Pell who licked his thumb before turning pages, and a line in a book that said her debt stood at eleven years, four months, and some odd days that never seemed to diminish by quite as many as actually passed.

She did not think about this as she walked the flats. She thought about it every day, which is different from thinking about it, the way a stone in the shoe is different from walking.

* * *

The shallows at low tide were Odile's alone, or near enough. The company divers worked the deep beds beyond the point, men and women in canvas suits breathing through hoses tended by deckhands, hauling up shell after shell for the counting house. They were paid by weight and docked for empties. What they missed — the small pearls, the misshapen ones, the strays the counters wrote off as seed — those sank back into the sand, and the sand gave them up twice a day to anyone patient enough to kneel in the cold and look.

The company did not count what the company did not see. This was the only law Odile had ever found that held.

She waded out past the old pilings where the water reached her knees, then her waist, then she tipped forward and let the sea take her weight the way it had taken her father's. Diving was the one inheritance the company couldn't ledger. Her father had been a free diver before he signed with Meridian — one of the last who needed no hose, no suit, no tender counting his minutes from above. He could stay down so long the deckhands swore he'd made a bargain. Odile had his lungs. She felt them open now, the cold pressing in around her ears like hands, and below her the sandy bottom lay rippled and pale in the early light, scattered with broken shell.

She worked by feel. Fingers sifting the sand the way her father had taught her — *don't grab, ask* — reading the small differences, the weight of a shell against an empty one, the faint wrongness of a pearl among stones. Her world shrank to the size of her two hands. That was the mercy of it. Underwater there was no ledger, no term of years, no name written in someone else's book. There was only breath, and the body's old argument with the water, which her body always won.

She came up gasping, hair plastered to her face, and opened her fist over the tin box floating beside her on its cork line. A pearl sat in her palm, grey-pink, the size of a barley grain. Not worth much. Worth something.

*What the tide keeps,* her father used to say, *the tide keeps badly.* He'd meant that the sea gives everything back eventually — pearls, drowned men, promises. Odile had decided long ago that if the sea kept things badly, then it was her business to keep them well.

* * *

By full light she had nine pearls in the tin and numb feet, and she climbed out onto the flat to wring her skirt and eat the bread she'd brought wrapped in oilcloth. From up here she could see the whole architecture of her life laid out along the harbor: the company house where they slept in rooms numbered like crates; the counting house with its green shutters and its smell of ink and brine; the drying racks; the long shed where the shells were cracked; and beyond it all, the masts, and beyond them, the open water that went everywhere and led nowhere she could afford to go.

Her mother was already on the racks, turning shell meat in the sun, one of forty women doing the same work in the same rhythm, so that from a distance they looked like a single creature with forty arms. Her brother Tomas was somewhere on the boats — he'd turned twelve in the spring and already the company had begun keeping a page for him too, a thin page, a beginning page, the way you'd start a file on something you intended to own later. Their lease — the little strip of shore and orchard-land behind the house that her grandfather had held before there was a Meridian Trading Company — was recorded in the harbor master's rolls, safe as anything was safe, which was to say safe until someone wanted it.

And her father. Her father was nowhere on the water, because her father was in it.

They said he'd gone down on a working dive six years ago, a routine bed off Cutter Point, and the hose had fouled, and by the time they brought him up he was past bringing. The company had paid out a death gratuity of forty dollars, deducted from it the cost of recovering the body, and entered the balance against the family account — so that in the end, the ledger showed, the Ishikawa household owed the company one dollar and sixty cents more than it had the day he died. Odile had been nine. She remembered most clearly not the funeral but Mr. Pell's pen, scratching, scratching, converting her father into a figure, and how she had wanted to take the pen away from him and break it and be hanged for it.

She had not taken the pen. She had learned instead to dive the shallows for strays, and to keep a tin box, and to say nothing. It was not the same thing as being brave. But it was not nothing.

* * *

The tin box lived under a loose board beneath her sleeping pallet, and she visited it the way other people visited graves. Into it, over seven years, had gone everything she owned that mattered: thirty-one pearls, graded by size in cotton wool; her father's diving knife, its handle worn smooth as driftwood; a photograph of the four of them taken at a fair when Tomas was a baby, everyone squinting into the sun; and a single silver coin her mother had pressed into her hand once, saying only, *for the boat, someday,* without explaining what boat, or when, or how.

Thirty-one pearls. She knew the number the way she knew her own age. Some nights she took the box out and counted them again, not because the number changed but because counting was a form of prayer, and prayer was the only religion permitted to people whose lives belonged to a ledger. Each pearl was a hour of the tide given back to her. Each one was proof that the company's count was not the only count, that there existed a parallel accounting, secret and small, in which Odile Ishikawa was slowly, grain by grain, buying herself.

At the current rate of stray-finding, she calculated, she would have enough in nineteen years. She had done this sum so many times the numbers had worn smooth, like the knife handle. Nineteen years. She would be thirty-four. Tomas would be grown and bound. Her mother would be old. The sum was hopeless and she did it anyway, every night, because hopelessness done nightly becomes a kind of stubbornness, and stubbornness was the one crop that grew on leased land.

Tomorrow Mr. Pell would renew her term. Tomorrow the pen would scratch. Tonight the tide would go out again, and she would go out with it, and the company would not see, and the sea would keep what it kept, and she would keep it better.

* * *

She was crossing the flats on her way back, the tin warm from her hands, when she saw the riders come down the coast road — three of them, in company grey, moving fast for that hour, their horses throwing up spindrift. One carried a leather dispatch case, the kind the harbor master signed for with both hands. They passed the drying racks without slowing, and Odile saw her mother straighten among the forty arms and watch them go, and saw the other women stop turning their shells, all at once, like birds going quiet.

Riders before breakfast meant papers. Papers meant names. In the six years since her father drowned, Odile had learned the grammar of the harbor: that bad news traveled on horseback and good news walked, if it bothered to come at all.

She stood in the wet sand with her box of uncounted pearls and watched the riders disappear toward the customs house, and she felt the first small motion under her feet — not the tide, which she knew like her own pulse, but something else, something farther out, a swell that had begun beyond the horizon of her understanding and was even then traveling toward the shore where she stood.

That evening, the harbor master posted a notice on the customs board, and the notice bore a seal, and the seal was fresh wax over a sealed letter, and the letter was addressed to the Meridian Trading Company concerning certain households of foreign sympathy — and among the names, in a clerk's careful hand, was written *Ishikawa.*

But that was evening, and the tide was hours away yet, and Odile did not know it. She climbed the steps to the company house carrying her tin box and her nine new pearls, and inside, her mother looked up from the stove with a face Odile had never seen on her before — a face like a door closing — and said:

"Odile. Come here. There's something you need to see."
