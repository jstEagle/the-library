## 4. Enemy Before the Century Turns

The wind changed in the third week of September, and with it the talk.

Odile noticed it first at the drying racks, where the fisherwives wrung salt from their nets and wrung gossip from each other in the same motion. Their voices had always carried the harbor's news the way gulls carried scraps — dropping some, fighting over others — but lately the words had grown heavier, and they did not scatter. They settled. *Mobilization.* *The Straits.* *A gunboat sighted off the cape, flying colors nobody could name.* The women said these words the way they said a storm was coming: not to discuss it, but to mark it, so that afterward they could say they had known.

She kept her head down and her hands moving. Sixteen years of indenture taught a body that posture; she had been eleven when her mother first folded her fingers around a shell knife and said, *the company counts what you finish, not what you feel.* But even bent over the racks, she heard. You learned to hear everything and answer nothing. It was the second lesson, and it had kept her family fed through every hard season since her father drowned.

*They're calling up the reservists at Meridian Point.*

*Mine went Tuesday. He says the trains are full.*

*Full going which way?*

Nobody answered that one. Nobody wanted to be the first to say it aloud, that the trains were full of boys pointed north, and that the papers were printing maps with borders drawn in red ink like wounds, and that in the taverns men who had never owned a map were suddenly experts on all of them.

The century had turned thirteen years ago, and everyone had promised then that it would be a century of electric light and flying machines and an end to old hatreds. Odile had been a child standing on the seawall with her father's hand heavy on her shoulder, watching the fireworks bloom over the water, and he had said something she'd only half understood: *New centuries are for people who can afford new things. The rest of us get the same old ones, repainted.* She understood it now. The paint was peeling, and underneath, the old wood showed through: the old fear, the old arithmetic of who belonged and who did not.

* * *

It was the customs board that made it real.

The board stood at the harbor mouth, beside the weighhouse, under a slanted roof of green copper gone white with bird lime. Company notices went there — tide tables, quarantine flags, auction dates, the price per pound of dried kelp. Odile passed it twice a day, most days, and had long ago stopped seeing it, the way you stop seeing your own fence.

But on the morning after the gunboat rumor reached the racks, there was a crowd around it, and crowds at the customs board meant either money or trouble, and no one had posted a price.

She worked her way along the edge of it, tin pail in hand, small enough and unremarkable enough that bodies made room without looking at her. And then she saw the paper.

It was a broadsheet, fresh from the company press, the ink still smelling of lampblack. At its top, in letters sized for reading from a boat: **NOTICE OF EXAMINATION.** Below that, a list.

Not goods. Names.

*Nakamura, Kenzo — leaseholder, Wharf Three. Nakamura, Sana. Nakamura, Hideo (minor).*

*Osei, Daniel — leaseholder, Netmender's Row. Osei, Ruth. Osei, Daniel Jr. (minor).*

*Kovač, Marko — leaseholder, Salt Lane. Kovač, Irena.*

*Ishikawa, Yuki — widow of record, Pearl Walk. Ishikawa, Odile. Ishikawa, Tomas (minor).*

Her own name sat there in cold column, between strangers' griefs, spelled correctly, which somehow made it worse. Someone at the company had taken care to spell it right.

**All persons listed will present themselves for examination of goods and papers within seven days. Persons of foreign descent or sympathies residing within the harbor district are advised that unusual holdings may be construed as evidence of intent to aid enemy interests.**

*Foreign descent or sympathies.* Odile read it three times, because the first two times her eyes slid off the words as if they were oiled. Her mother had been born forty miles up this coast, in a house her grandfather built with timber he'd fished out of the bay himself after the typhoon. Her father had been born across the sea, yes — but so had half the fleet, and half the fleet's fathers before them, and no one had ever thought to write it down on a board until now, until the red-inked maps and the full trains made it useful.

Her mother's name was on a board nailed up beside the price of kelp.

And below the names, smaller, almost an afterthought: **By order of the Harbor Master, for the Meridian Trading Company.**

The crowd murmured around her. A woman near her — Mrs. Osei herself, Odile realized, the netmender's wife with her hands still white from the morning's bleach — stood very straight and read the board the way people read their own death sentences: slowly, once, as if stillness might change the grammar. Then she turned and walked home, back straight, chin level, and Odile watched the whole crowd watch her go, and watched them step slightly aside as she passed, and watched the space close behind her like water behind a hull.

That was how it began, then. Not with torches. With a foot of distance, politely given.

* * *

She told herself, walking home along Pearl Walk with the pail knocking against her knee, that it meant nothing. Notices were notices. The company posted things it didn't mean all the time — fines it never collected, rules it never enforced, holidays it never granted. Paper was the company's cheapest product. Her whole life had been spent inside other people's paperwork; she knew better than to be frightened of a sheet of it.

Then she got home, and her mother had already seen it.

Yuki Ishikawa was not a woman who frightened easily, and she was even less a woman who let fear show, but Odile knew the signs the way sailors know weather. Her mother had swept the step twice. She had taken down the household shrine cloth — just the good embroidered one, the one with the characters Grandmother had stitched — and put away the plain one, the one that looked like anyone's cloth, anyone's house. She was standing in the kitchen deciding, visibly, whether the family photographs ought to come off the wall.

"Mama."

"They posted the Nakamuras too," her mother said, without turning. "And the Oseis. And a man named Kovač I've never met." Now she turned, and her face was composed into the particular calm that Odile had only ever seen on her once before — the day the company's boat came back without her father, the day Yuki walked down to meet it with dry eyes and signed whatever they put in front of her because signing was the only way to see his chest brought ashore. "Four households. All leaseholders. All of us."

"Half the harbor has foreign blood somewhere in it," Odile said. "Old Marta at the chandlery. The Delgados. Half the—"

"The Delgados aren't leaseholders." Her mother's voice was flat and quiet, the voice she used for facts too large for any other tone. "Look again at the list, Odile. Not one renter. Not one shopkeeper. Every name on that board holds ground the company wants."

The kitchen went very still. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

Odile thought of the letter.

It lived now inside the false bottom of her sewing basket, wrapped in waxed linen, and she had not opened it since the night she palmed it from the harbor master's tray — not fully opened it, only lifted one corner by candlelight to confirm what she already knew: the company seal, her family name, the word *sympathizer* in a clerk's careful hand. She had told herself she stole it out of stubbornness. Out of a diver's greed for anything the company tried to throw away. Standing in the kitchen, watching her mother take down photographs, she admitted the truer thing: she had stolen it because some part of her had known, even then, that paper like that did not arrive alone.

"I need to check something," she said.

Her mother looked at her for a long moment — the look that weighed and measured and decided, the pearl-grader's look, though her mother had never graded a pearl in her life. Whatever she found in Odile's face, she nodded once. "Be back before the lamplighter. And Odile—" a pause, the weight settling, "—if you find out what I think you'll find out, you bring it to me before you do anything brave. Promise me that much."

She promised. It was easier than explaining that she had stopped knowing the difference between brave and necessary somewhere around the moment her own name appeared above the word *examination.*

* * *

The harbor master's office kept its correspondence in the outer room — that much Odile had learned in sixteen years of being sent there, sent everywhere, the errand girl with the quick feet and the face nobody bothered to remember. Notices, duplicates, drafts. The clerks burned the true secrets, but they were lazy about drafts, because drafts were only paper, and paper was cheap.

The customs notice had gone up that morning. Which meant the draft — or something like it — had crossed that desk days ago, and days ago was before.

Before the letter. Before the word *sympathizer.* Before everything, if her suspicion held, and God forgive her, she needed it to hold, because the alternative was that the world had simply turned against them overnight for reasons of state, and reasons of state could not be fought, only endured. But a mistake — a lie — a forgery with a date on it — those had edges. Those could be gripped.

She waited for the evening rush of boats, when both clerks went down to help tally the landings and the office stood empty except for the smell of ink and cold tea. She went in carrying a returned receipt, legitimate, dull, the kind of errand no one questioned, and while she stood at the counter waiting for a clerk to come back and stamp it, she looked at the spike by the window where discarded drafts waited for the burner.

There were four of them on the spike. Four notices of examination.

She should not have touched them. She touched them.

The first was the Nakamura notice, in the clerk's fair hand — the same text, nearly word for word, that hung on the board. The second, Osei. The third, Kovač. She fanned them out on the counter with fingers that had steadied themselves on a hundred rope rails in rough water, and she read, and the dread that had been sitting in her stomach all day sharpened, slowly, terribly, into something else. Something with a point.

Because it wasn't only the wording of the charges that matched. It was the wording of everything. *Unusual holdings.* *Intent to aid enemy interests.* *Examination of goods and papers.* Four families, four different trades, four different histories — and the same accusations, cut from the same cloth, laid over each household like a pattern laid over fabric. Even the errors matched. All four drafts accused the household of receiving *crates of contraband steel via coastal lighter,* and none of the four households owned a lighter, or a dock big enough for one, or any trade but pearls and nets and salt. Steel. The Nakamuras grew vegetables on their wharf-garden. What would Kenzo Nakamura do with contraband steel?

Unless the crates weren't theirs. Unless the crates had never existed at all, except in the place where documents are born — which is to say, in the mind of whoever needed them to exist.

She stood in the empty office with four sheets of paper in her hands and felt her childhood end quietly, without ceremony, the way a diver feels the last light of the surface fade above her. For sixteen years she had believed the company was weather — vast, indifferent, dangerous the way storms are dangerous, striking where it willed and explainable by nothing. Weather you survived. You did not argue with weather.

But this was not weather. This was a hand. A hand had written *steel* four times, and a hand had sealed a letter naming her family conspirators, and a hand had nailed her mother's name to a board beside the price of kelp — and hands had owners, and owners had reasons, and the reason was sitting in plain sight on the list itself: every name on that board held ground the company wanted. Leaseholders, one and all. Leases that would fall vacant — forfeit — the moment the holders were branded enemies and driven off or worse.

The harbor leases. The best moorings, the deepest channels, the rights to the pearl beds her father had died diving. All of it, gathered up not by force but by paperwork — by the cheapest thing the company made.

Somewhere down the hall, boots. Voices returning, the tally done, the evening coming on. Odile squared the four drafts, slid them back onto the spike in their proper order, took up her receipt, and stood at the counter with her heart beating slow and enormous, the way it did underwater, when panic was a luxury and breath was arithmetic.

The clerk came in, stamped her paper without looking at her, and said, "That all, girl?"

"That's all," Odile said.

She walked home past the customs board, where the notice fluttered in the evening wind, and past Pearl Walk, where her mother's window glowed behind the plain shrine cloth, and she did not go in. She turned instead down the lane toward the company offices — the real ones, the big stone building with the barred windows, where the true ledgers lived and the true secrets burned — and she stood in the gathering dark looking up at those windows, counting them, the way she'd once counted pearls in a tin box.

Seven days, the notice said. Seven days before her mother had to stand before someone with a stamp and answer questions written by the same hand that wrote *steel.*

Six nights, then, for her to learn what was locked behind those windows. She had picked locks before — the company's pawnshop lockbox, once, to retrieve Tomas's jacket from hock, a crime so small no one ever caught it. But this would not be small. This would be the deepest dive of her life, into black water, alone, holding her breath.

Her father had drowned doing honest work for dishonest men. Odile stood beneath the barred windows of the Meridian Trading Company and made the only vow that had ever come to her whole and entire:

She would not drown for their lies. She would drag the truth up into the light if it killed her — or better, she decided, turning toward home to fetch her candle stub and her patience —

she would steal it out of the dark the same way they had stolen her name: quietly, at night, with paper.
