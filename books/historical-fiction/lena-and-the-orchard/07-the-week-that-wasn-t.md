## 7. The Week That Wasn't

### I.

Elias Vane had never before understood that a week could have a weight, but the seven days Odile left with him pressed down on his desk like a stone taken from the harbor bed — cold, wet, and growing heavier each morning he failed to lift it.

He had told her to wait. He could still hear himself saying it, reasonable as arithmetic: *A week. Let me take it through channels. My father trusts me. If I bring him the copies myself, quietly, before anyone else sees them—*

She had looked at him then the way divers look at weather. Not angry. Measuring.

"You're asking me to put my family inside your week," she said. "And your week belongs to your father's house."

"It belongs to *me*," he'd answered, and the moment it left his mouth he knew how young it sounded, how much like a boy standing in a doorway of a house he did not own, insisting the door was his because he could open it.

Now it was Tuesday — the third day of his week — and he sat in his father's study under the mounted swordfish that had hung there since before Elias was born, watching Magistrate Aldous Vane read a letter that had nothing to do with forged warrants, because Elias had not yet found the courage to make it about forged warrants.

"The Meridian accounts come before me Friday," his father said, tapping the page. "Corvus will present the lease transfers. Four holdings, all seized under suspicion of sympathizing with foreign interests." He glanced up. "You look pale. You've been working too hard at those ledgers of yours."

"I've been reviewing them," Elias said carefully. "The warrant filings."

"Good. A magistrate who doesn't know his own paperwork is a magistrate other men file through." His father smiled, brief and dry, and returned to his letter, and Elias understood that the door was closing, politely, the way doors closed in this house — not slammed but *curated*, shut by someone who decided in advance what would be permitted through it.

That night Elias took out the copied pages again and laid them across his bed in the lamplight. Candle smoke and salt; that was how the copies smelled now, Odile's candle stub baked into the paper. He read the columns for the fourth time, fifth time, looking for the error, the innocent explanation, the thing that would let him bring this to his father without bringing his father's own seal into the room like a knife.

There was no error. There never had been. The duplicate manifests matched the seized goods crate by crate: pearl-shell, copra, silk thread, all of it Meridian property moved onto Ishikawa and Nakamura and Osei wharves on nights when no one but the company's own boats were licensed to cross the harbor bar. Then the customs men had found it, exactly where the manifests said it would be. Then the warrants, bearing his father's signature — his father's *actual* signature, which meant either his father had signed without reading, or Corvus had a hand practiced enough to pass for it, or both, and Elias did not know which possibility sickened him more.

He thought: *Friday.* The accounts came before the magistrate Friday. If he could place the pages among the review documents — not accuse, merely include — his father would see it himself. His father was not a stupid man. He was a careful one, and careful men, given evidence arranged properly, would follow it to protect themselves even when they would not follow it for justice.

It was a good plan. It was a plan built entirely of patience.

On Wednesday morning, Odile Ishikawa came to the side gate of the magistrate's residence, soaked to the knees, with salt drying white along her hem, and told him her brother was gone.

* * *

### II.

They had come for Tomas at the oyster beds, Odile said, standing in the gate's shadow so the street could not see her. Two company men and a customs officer, arriving at low tide with the warrant already unfolded, as though they'd known precisely which mudflat he'd be working and at what hour.

"They didn't search," she said. Her voice was very flat, the way water goes flat before wind arrives. "You'd think a man arrested for smuggling would have his boat searched. His shed. They didn't look at anything. They read his name off a paper and took him."

"Odile—"

"I went to the customs house. They wouldn't tell me where he was held. A clerk told me the Ishikawa lease transfer was filed yesterday." She laughed once, a sound with nothing in it. "*Yesterday,* Elias. Filed while you were being patient."

He reached for her arm and she stepped back, not far, just enough that his fingers closed on gate iron instead.

"I'm not blaming you yet," she said. "I want you to hear that. I haven't spent the blame yet. But your week is half gone and my brother is in a company cell, and every day from now on costs something I can't get back. So whatever you were going to do on Friday — do it tonight."

Then she was gone down the lane, fast, light, carrying nothing visible and everything she owned, and Elias stood holding the gate until his knuckles ached.

He did not do it that night. He wanted to write that he had; he would carry the wanting of it to his grave. But his father dined with Corvus that evening — Elias saw them through the study window, two heads bent over port glasses, Corvus's face smooth as a river stone — and something in Elias, some last instinct of a son toward a father's table, made him wait for the morning. Made him believe there would be a morning in which this could be done gently.

Thursday he drafted the memorandum three times. Thursday afternoon his father sent word he'd been called to the provincial seat and would return Monday. Thursday evening, Elias walked to the company offices to demand where Tomas Ishikawa was held, and was received by a junior clerk who consulted no ledger and consulted no one and said, with the serenity of a man reciting something rehearsed, that the prisoner had been transferred inland pending disposition.

"Inland where?"

"That information isn't held at this office, sir."

"Then who holds it?"

The clerk smiled. It was not an unkind smile. That was the worst of it — the whole machine was not unkind, only indifferent, a mill that ground what was fed to it and felt nothing about the grain. "The company holds it, sir."

Elias stood on the office steps in the blue dark with the harbor smell all around him and understood, finally, completely, that there had never been a week. The week had been a courtesy extended to him the way a fisherman extends line — not because the fish is free, but because it is easier to land something that believes it is swimming.

He had asked Odile for seven days. The company had needed four.

* * *

### III.

Odile learned the lease was seized from a notice pasted to the customs board between a shipping schedule and a warning about dynamite in the channel.

**NOTICE OF TRANSFER OF LEASE HOLDING. The harbor lease designated Ishikawa, Plot 14, having been forfeited under warrant of sympathetic association, is hereby transferred to the Meridian Trading Company effective—**

She stopped reading after *effective*. The date didn't matter. Nothing after the word *forfeited* mattered; it was all machinery, all the sound a loom makes after the cloth is already cut.

Her mother read the notice standing beside her, and Odile watched her mother's face do the thing it had done at the funeral — go smooth and distant, a face retreating into a house whose windows had been shuttered against a storm years ago and never reopened. Her mother said nothing. Her mother had learned, somewhere between a drowned husband and a burned letter, that words gave the company things to use.

But that night, packing, her mother broke the silence.

"Tomas kept his boots by the door," she said. She was folding his spare shirt with hands that did not shake, which was somehow worse than shaking. "Left side. He always put them left, even as a boy, so I'd know they were his and not his father's." She set the shirt in the basket. "When he comes back, they'll be wrong. Everything will be wrong. He'll put them wherever the soldiers taught him to put them."

"Mama—"

"Don't apologize." She looked up then, and her eyes were the diver's eyes, the ones Odile had inherited without ever seeing them used at this depth. "Your father held his breath for pearls. I can hold mine for this. But listen to me, girl. Whatever you're carrying around in that head of yours — the papers, the plan, whatever it is you think I don't know about — you finish it. You don't stop for grief and you don't stop for fear. Your father dove with a bad ear the whole last season because stopping meant we didn't eat. We are past the season for stopping."

Odile knelt and took her mother's hands, rough as rope, warm as bread.

"Where will you go?"

"To the Ferreiras' attic, above the net loft. Old Mrs. Ferreira owes your grandmother a debt older than any ledger." A ghost of a smile. "The company thinks debt is something they hold. Let them keep thinking it."

Dawn was still an hour away when Odile slipped out the back and stood a last time in the yard where she'd grown up. The lease was a piece of paper; the land was still the land. The persimmon tree her father had planted leaned over the fence line, heavy with fruit nobody official had thought to seize. She took three of them — food, and besides, her father's trees owed her nothing and she owed them everything — and ate one walking down the hill toward the harbor, sweet and astringent at once, tasting of the life that had just been signed away.

At the bottom of the hill, folded small inside her jacket against her heart, the copied ledger pages waited. And beneath them, in the tin box, wrapped in oilcloth: her father's pearl, gray-green, imperfect, worth almost nothing and everything.

She did not go home again. Home was a word on a transfer document now.

* * *

### IV.

Elias found the notice on the customs board himself, later that same morning, and stood in front of it while the market noise went on around him like weather.

A woman beside him was reading it too — old, broad-shouldered, a net-mender's knot of scars across her forearms. She read it twice, slowly, lips moving, and then she spat, deliberately, onto the cobbles below the board.

"They did the Nakamuras the same way," she said, not to Elias particularly, but to the air, to anyone. "Same words. Word for word, I'll swear it. 'Sympathetic association.' Like sympathy were a crime you could weigh." She rolled up her reading spectacles. "My father fished next to old Ishikawa thirty years. Thirty years, and a piece of paper undoes it in an afternoon."

Elias's throat worked. "Do you know where the son was taken?"

The woman looked at him properly then — took in the coat, the collar, the magistrate's-son cut of him — and something in her face cooled by degrees, like a forge left to stand.

"He was taken where they take everyone," she said. "Inland. Where papers go to die and boys go with them." She gathered her mending. "You want to find him, don't ask the board. Ask who wrote it."

He stood there long after she'd gone, and the question stayed with him longer than the crowd, longer than the market bells: *who wrote it.* Because he knew the answer, had known it since Wednesday night, had watched the answer drink port with his father through a window while he composed a memorandum no one would ever read.

Corvus. Or the machine Corvus served. Or his father's signature, riding on top of both like a figurehead on a ship whose hull was rotten below the waterline.

That afternoon Elias did the thing he should have done five days ago. He did not go through channels. He went to the harbor.

He found her at the net sheds, or rather she found him — stepped out of shadow between drying racks with the unhurried precision of someone who had been waiting and had expected him to take this long.

"I'm sorry," he said. The words fell out of him like ballast. "The week — I thought I was protecting you by doing it properly. I kept thinking if I just arranged it right, put it in front of the right eyes at the right time—" He stopped. She was watching him with those measuring eyes, and he made himself say the rest. "There is no right time inside their house. I understand that now. I'm sorry it cost you Tomas to learn it."

"It didn't cost me," Odile said quietly. "It cost *him*. That's different, Elias. That's the whole difference, and it's the difference your week never accounted for." She looked out at the harbor, at the masts, at the plot of water where her father had drowned within sight of shore. "Are you finished hesitating? Truly? Because I'm about to do something that can't be un-done, and I'd rather not do it twice."

"What are you going to do?"

She turned back to him, and the last of the dawn light caught her face, and he saw that the fear in her was gone — not conquered, not absent, but *converted*, the way coal becomes fire.

"I'm leaving," she said. "Tonight, before the tide turns. With the ledger. To whoever in this country still reads paper and tells the truth about it afterward." A pause, and her voice gentled by one degree, which from Odile was a flood. "You can come with me or you can stay and be your father's son. Both are honorable. Only one is useful."

The gulls wheeled over the harbor, screaming their thin cries over the water where the Ishikawa lease lay, signed and sealed and stolen. Elias Vane stood on the dock with his commission in his pocket and his father's name on his forged warrants, and understood that the week was over — had been over since Wednesday — and that everything he chose now would be chosen after.

"I'll need until nightfall," he heard himself say. "To leave a letter. And to take my father's seal impression from his desk drawer — you'll want proof of the forgery that can't be dismissed as a diver's copy."

Odile studied him a long moment. Then, for the first time in five days, something that was almost trust moved behind her eyes.

"Nightfall," she agreed. "The north boathouse. Don't be late." She was already turning away, already becoming motion, the way she always was. "The tide won't wait, Elias. I've finally stopped expecting anything else to."

He watched her go down the dock, small and straight-backed, carrying everything she owned and none of what she'd lost, and he turned toward the magistrate's house with the terrible clarity of a man who has finally, finally run out of patience with himself.

Behind him, unnoticed by either of them, a company man in a gray coat noted the meeting in a small book, and closed it, and smiled without kindness or cruelty, the way a mill might smile.

The week that wasn't was over.

What came next belonged to the road.
