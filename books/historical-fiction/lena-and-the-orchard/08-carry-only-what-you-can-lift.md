## 8. Carry Only What You Can Lift

The warrant came at dusk, and my mother burned the soup.

Not on purpose. She set the pot on the iron plate and then stood in the doorway with the paper in her hands, and the soup went to charcoal while she read the same three lines over and over, her lips moving, her thumb pressed white against the company seal as if she could push it back through the page into whatever dark place it had been born.

*Thomas Ishikawa, taken under warrant of the Meridian Trading Company, charges of conspiracy to smuggle—*

Tomas was not home to be taken. That was the one mercy of it. He had gone up the coast two days before with the herring crews, sleeping on salt decks and eating out of other people's pots, which meant that when two company men came to our door with a lantern and a list, they found only my mother's hands wringing flour, and mine, and an empty chair where my brother should have been.

They were almost apologetic about it. That is the thing no one tells you about the machinery — how polite its teeth are. The taller one took off his cap. He said, *We'll take him when he turns up, ma'am, nothing personal,* and the shorter one looked around our kitchen the way men look at a house they are already pricing, and I understood, standing there with flour drying in the creases of my knuckles, that we had already lost. Not the argument. There had never been an argument to win. We had lost the way a leaseholder loses: quietly, on paper, before anyone knocked.

My mother saw them to the gate. Then she stood in the yard a long time, looking at the water, and when she came back inside she did not mention Tomas again for eleven hours.

That was how I knew she had gone somewhere else entirely — some interior room where she kept things too heavy for the house. My mother had rooms like that. She had learned them from my father, or he from her; they had built their marriage partly out of silences, load-bearing silences, and I had grown up inside the architecture without noticing what held up the walls.

She fed us the burnt soup anyway. We ate it in silence because there was nothing else and because wasting food felt, that night of all nights, like handing the company another small victory. It tasted of ash and salt and I swallowed every mouthful and thought: *this is the last meal I will eat at this table.*

I did not decide it. I recognized it, the way you recognize a change in the current by the pull against your legs before your eyes see anything move.

* * *

Afterward my mother washed the pot and I dried it, and when the last dish was racked she said, without turning from the basin:

"Show me."

I did not pretend ignorance. There was no use pretending anything in that house anymore; the letter had cracked us open like oysters and everything inside lay exposed on the ice. I went to my room and came back with the tin box, and inside the tin box, wrapped first in a scrap of oilcloth and then in a strip of my old petticoat, the pages I had copied by candle stub in the ledger room — the true accounts, the ones that showed the 'smuggled goods' were never smuggled at all, only moved, house to house, like pieces on a board, so that the company could find them wherever it needed contraband to be.

My mother unwrapped them with the care of a woman handling something wet and living. She read. The lamp hissed. Outside, the tide was going out with that long sighing sound it makes, the sound I have slept to my whole life, and I watched my mother's face do the thing faces do when hope arrives after the deadline — when it is true, and useful, and too late to be simple.

"They'll hang him for this," she said at last. Not *they might.* They will. "Or worse. A half-Japanese boy with smuggling papers and no father to speak for him." Her voice did not shake. That frightened me more than shaking would have. "And if they find these pages here, they'll hang you beside him, and burn this house for the pleasure of it."

"I know."

"Do you." She set the pages down. She looked at me — really looked, the way she used to when I came home with a split lip from the shallows and swore I hadn't been diving past the markers. "You've already decided. You decided before you sat down to supper. You decided before I did, probably. Before the soup."

"The soup was the last thing," I said, "that made me sure."

Something moved across her face then — not a smile, nothing so kind — more like the acknowledgment one diver gives another across dark water. *You see the bottom. Good. Now go down.*

"Then we pack tonight," she said, "and you leave before first light. And Odile—" and here her voice did finally crack, one clean fracture down the middle, quickly mended, "—you leave *alone.*"

* * *

We packed by lamplight, and the packing was a lesson, though neither of us called it one.

My mother took a sheet off the bed and spread it flat on the floor, and onto it she placed, one by one, the contents of our life, holding each item up to the light before setting it down or casting it aside. This was not sentiment. This was arithmetic. I had seen her do the same thing with catch-bags on the beach, sorting shell from pearl, keep from discard, and now she did it with a household, and her face never changed.

A blanket — yes. One, not two; two was weight and weight was death on a road. The good knife, the one with the bone handle my father had carved. No books. No photographs; photographs were paper, and paper could be found, and found paper could be read, and read paper could hang you. A change of clothes, worn, so she would not look new. Dried fish wrapped in cloth. The little brass compass my father had won in a card game the winter before I was born, and which had never once pointed anywhere but home, until now.

And then she stopped, and stood very still, and went to the shelf above the hearth.

On that shelf, in a jar of rice grains that had been changed out faithfully every season for fifteen years, lay my father's pearl. Not the biggest pearl he ever brought up — he'd sold those, all of them, feeding us, paying the company, keeping the ledgers of our lives balanced in someone else's favor. But the first one. The one he'd found the summer he married her, diving alone in the gray water off Kestrel Point, and had carried home in his cheek like a coin, laughing, spitting it into her palm on the doorstep. It was rose-colored, faintly, the way dawn is rose-colored if you catch it at the right minute, and my mother had kept it the way other women keep wedding rings.

She poured away the rice and put the pearl in my hand and closed my fingers over it.

"He would say pearls are for selling," she said.

"He'd be wrong."

"He usually was," she said, "about the things that mattered most," and pressed my fist shut again, harder.

The ledger pages went back into the tin box, and the tin box went into the bundle, and then my mother looked at the sheet with everything left on it — the rest, the residue, fifteen years of a family boiled down to what remained — and she tied the four corners together and handed it to me, and it weighed almost nothing.

"That's all?" I said. I hadn't meant to say it aloud. "That's all I can carry?"

"That's all you should," she said. "Everything else you own owns you back. Your father taught me that. He drowned with boots on, Odile — did you know that? They told me it was the current, but I know my husband, and he dove barefoot always, always, except that last day, because he was carrying something he shouldn't have been carrying, something heavy enough that he dressed for it like work instead of like water." Her jaw tightened. "The company's manifest says he went down overloaded and careless. I say he went down refusing to lie on their paper, and the weight they gave him to hold was the price. Either way — the lesson holds. Carry only what you can lift. And lift only what you can swim with."

I thought of him then — not the drowned man from the stories, but my father as I actually remembered him, which was mostly hands: huge, scarred, gentle hands shelling prawns on the step, teaching me to tie a bowline, cupping seawater for me to drink off his palm. Hands that had held a pearl in his cheek and a lie in his teeth and had finally, somewhere out past the marker buoys, chosen which one to keep.

"Come with me," I said. It came out young. I hated how young it came out.

"And leave Tomas a house to come home to? And leave them a reason to hunt the coast road for two of us instead of one?" She shook her head, and in the lamplight I saw for the first time how gray had gotten into her hair without my noticing, all that gray, accumulated night by night the way silt accumulates on a hull. "No. I stay. I am a widow of the company's own drowning, with a son arrested on false papers and a daughter who has done nothing anyone can prove. I am *safe*, Odile. Safe as stone, safe as a stone." She touched my face, once, briefly, with the backs of her fingers, the way you check a child for fever. "Go to Vane. You said he tore up the debt ledger once. Make him tear up a bigger one."

* * *

I slept two hours, maybe less, in my clothes, on top of the blanket I would carry, so that leaving would cost nothing but standing up.

I woke before the birds. In that hour the world is all sound and no color: the tide turning in the dark, the creak of the neighbor's boat on its mooring, my mother in the kitchen already, doing something quiet with the stove. When I came out she had made tea, real tea, hoarded tea, and we drank it standing up because sitting down would have meant talking.

At the door she did not embrace me. She took my shoulders in her hands instead, and turned me toward the window, where the east had just begun to go the color of my father's pearl.

"What do you see?"

"Light."

"Wrong. You see *water.* Everything between here and the capital is water wearing different clothes — rivers, rain, fog, the sea itself when you get there. You are a diver's daughter walking across the bottom of the world. So you breathe when there is breath to take, you rest when there's rest to be had, you don't fight the current, you *read* it. And when they come for you—" her grip tightened, "—you go down. Under. Quiet. You can hold your breath longer than any man on that company payroll can hold his patience. Your father proved it. Hold yours longer than they hold theirs, and you'll live."

Then she embraced me, one arm, hard and fast, the way you lash cargo to a deck before weather, and let go before either of us could make it into something we couldn't survive.

"Carry only what you can lift," she said to my back, as I stepped into the blue dark of the lane. "And remember whose name you're carrying it under."

* * *

The tide was out. Of course it was; the tide has always known when I'm leaving.

I went down across the flats the long way, avoiding the harbor road, my feet finding the old paths by feel — the firm sand between the channels, the shingle bar, the smell of weed and cold mud rising in the gray light like breath from the world's lungs. Behind me our house sat black against the paling sky, and I did not look back at it, because my mother had taught me that too, in her way: divers don't look up at the surface they've left. They look down at the bottom they're making for.

In the tin box against my ribs: forty-one copied pages, a knife, a brass compass, dried fish, a rose-colored pearl, a strip of petticoat, a dead man's luck. Fifteen years of a family, weighing less than a bucket of water.

Somewhere north, in a company cell or a company boat or a company grave, my brother was breathing air I wasn't sharing. Somewhere behind me, my mother stood in a doorway growing smaller with every step I took, becoming, as I walked, not a person but a point on a chart — *last seen, pre-dawn, coastal path, alone.*

And ahead, past the headland where the flats ended and the real coast began, the Salt Road unrolled invisible in the half-dark, stitched along the shore, running through villages full of people who owed my family debts no ledger had ever recorded.

I shifted the bundle high on my back the way my father shifted his catch bag, settled the strap where the muscle takes the weight gladly, and walked.

The light came up slowly, the way truth does. By the time the sun cleared the water I was past the last marker buoy, past the last thing with our name on it, and the sea on my left lay flat and shining and patient, and I understood at last what my mother had spent my whole childhood trying to teach me:

The water doesn't care what you carry. Only whether you can swim.

Behind me, far down the coast, a thread of smoke rose straight into the windless morning — too thin to be a chimney, too steady to be a gull-farmer's fire — and I watched it for a long moment, marking it the way you mark a shark fin, before I turned my face north and did not look back again.
