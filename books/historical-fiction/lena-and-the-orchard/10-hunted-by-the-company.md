## 10. Hunted by the Company

Corvus had a theory about people, and it had never once failed him: everyone carried more than they could lift. A woman with a satchel was a woman with a destination. A man who looked over his shoulder at a bridge was a man with a debt. The trick of the work — thirty years of it, first in the customs sheds of Meridian's home office, then on the coast, then wherever the company decided its paper had grown legs — was not to chase what ran, but to wait for what it refused to put down.

So when the report came up from the Salt Road district — Ishikawa girl, sixteen days gone, seen buying bread in Ternwick with coin that rang wrong for a fugitive — Corvus did not send riders after her. He sent a clerk.

"Find out what she bought," he said. "Not where she went. What she bought."

The clerk, a nervous young man named Pell whose collar had been ironed by his mother that morning and would not survive the week, wrote it down as though it were scripture. Bread. Oil for a lamp. A candle stub. A spool of waxed thread.

Corvus turned the list over twice in his mind and smiled without any pleasure in it. Bread was hunger; anyone could read hunger. But waxed thread and oil were maintenance. She was keeping something dry. Keeping something lit. You did not maintain your luggage if you intended to abandon it.

"She still has the ledger," he said to the empty room, and the room, being an office in a customs annex with salt-rotted window frames and a map of the coast pinned up in four overlapping sheets, agreed with him in silence.

He poured himself the tea he allowed himself at nine each morning — one cup, never two, because a man who rationed his own comforts could be trusted to ration other men's — and studied the map. The girl was moving north along the water, village to village, the way a crab moves: sideways, always sideways, never committing to the open ground where horses could run her down. Smart. Her father had been smart too, and the sea had taken him anyway, which Corvus considered a lesson in the limits of cleverness.

The file on Odile Ishikawa sat in his satchel, thin as all their files were thin. Indentured at six when the father's drowning converted the family debt into labor. No incidents. No thefts. Fourteen years of quiet compliance, noted in a clerk's hand so small it seemed apologetic. And then a sealed letter, an accusation, a lease — and a brother sitting now in the holding shed at Graywater checkpoint, and a mother whose name had been struck from the rolls like a smudge wiped off wet ink.

Corvus had read the warrant himself before signing the pursuit order. He had read it the way he read everything: twice, slowly, looking for the seam. There was a seam. There always was, on this job. The goods listed in the smuggling manifest had moved on a company barge under a company flag on a night when no leaseholder's boat had left the harbor at all. He knew this because he had been the one who signed the barge's clearance chit, back when the whole affair was only an internal transfer of surplus tin, before someone above him had looked at a map of expiring leases and seen opportunity lying there like a purse on a road.

He had signed anyway. That was the part he never flinched from, because flinching was vanity. A man in his position learned early that the work was made entirely of signatures, thousands of them, layered year over year until no single one could be called the crime. He had once tried to explain this to a magistrate's clerk over brandy — *no one does it, that is how it gets done* — and the clerk had laughed, thinking it wit. It was not wit. It was arithmetic. And arithmetic, unlike cruelty, required no appetite.

Pell appeared in the doorway with his ruined collar and his notebook. "There's word she sheltered two nights at the Maren place, sir. Old widow Maren. Fish nets."

"And?"

"And nothing, sir. Widow Maren swears she saw nobody."

"Of course she does." Corvus set down his cup. "Did you believe her?"

Pell hesitated, which was honest of him. "I believed she wanted me to."

"That's better than belief. Write that down somewhere you won't lose it." He stood, took his coat from the peg — heavy wool, oilskin-lined, unglamorous, the coat of a working man pretending at respectability — and looked at the map one last time. The villages strung along the coast like beads on a line that kept fraying. "She's being fed," he said. "She's being dried and fed and pointed north by people with nothing to gain and everything to lose. Which means we are not hunting one girl, Pell. We are hunting a coastline."

* * *

They caught up with the shape of her at Lowmarsh, three days later, in the way one catches up with weather: evidence rather than encounter. A fisherman fined for unlicensed passage who had, upon questioning, described a young woman with a diver's shoulders and a stranger's boots. A ferryman who remembered being paid in pearls — two of them, small, seed-grade, worth perhaps a tenth of what he'd charged her, and he'd taken them anyway, and had not reported it until Corvus's men sat down across from him and began, politely, to discuss his licensing.

Everyone on this coast owed the Ishikawas something. Corvus had expected that. What he had not fully priced in — and he resented himself mildly for the oversight — was that they paid those debts forward. The ferryman hadn't shortchanged her out of greed. He'd shortchanged himself on purpose, and the pearls were a courtesy, a receipt for a kindness the girl hadn't asked for. It made the whole coastline into a machine designed by grief and gratitude, running quietly on fuel the company had never thought to meter.

"You can't arrest gratitude," Pell said on the second evening, half to himself, as they watched the lights of another village come on across the water — fishing hamlet, forty roofs, one boathouse long enough to sleep a runner in.

"No," Corvus agreed. "But you can make it expensive."

He stood a while longer on the seawall, hands clasped behind him, feeling the wind come in off the flats with the smell of mud and iodine and cold smoke. Somewhere out there, past the sandbars and the channels he'd spent two days learning from a chart stolen off a pilot's wall, a nineteen-year-old girl who should have been counting pearls in a company shed was sleeping in a net loft or a chapel ruin or a stranger's kitchen, holding a ledger full of his own signatures against her chest like a child holds a doll.

He did not hate her. That was the thing people got wrong about him, the ones who bothered to get anything wrong about him at all. Hate was appetite, and appetite made mistakes. He respected her, in the narrow professional way he respected good lockwork or a well-forged chit. She had understood faster than most grown men that the paper trail was the whole war — that whoever held the true pages held the truth, and truth, inconveniently for everyone including him, was not actually on the payroll.

But respect did not slow him down either. That was the other thing people got wrong. They assumed a man must be either kind or effective, and that effectiveness purchased with unkindness was some special sin. Corvus had watched kind men fail for twenty years, and he had watched their failures get other people hurt — usually the same people the kind men had been trying to protect. Mercy, misapplied, was just negligence wearing a nicer coat. He had chosen his coat years ago and had never once been cold.

"The boathouse," he said.

Pell blinked. "Sir?"

"Across the channel. Big one, stone footings, tarred roof — I counted the doors this afternoon. Two boats inside, one of them rigged to leave on a tide within the next day or so. Somebody has been preparing somebody else's departure." He turned up his collar. "We take it tonight. Not her — she won't be in it, don't waste the hope. We take the building."

"Take the—sir, it's not ours to—"

"It belongs to a man named Odo Ferrin who owes the customs office eleven seasons of back duties he cannot pay," Corvus said, in the tone of a man reciting a recipe. "By morning it will belong to the fire brigade as a training loss. By afternoon it will belong to ash. These are the same building, Pell. Buildings are like people that way. They're whatever the paperwork says."

* * *

They crossed at slack water in a hired skiff, six men, oars muffled with cloth, and Corvus sat in the stern doing the thing he always did on approach, which was nothing — hands flat on his knees, breathing evenly, letting the younger men spend their nerves so he wouldn't have to spend his own. Fear, properly distributed among a team, was a resource like any other.

The boathouse rose black against the lesser black of the sky, tar and timber, smelling even from fifty yards of pitch and old fish. Inside, he knew, were Odo Ferrin's two boats and probably a third hull under canvas that belonged to no license in the district. And possibly, in some dark corner, a bedroll still warm, a candle stub still soft, the ghost-shape of a girl who had slept here last week or last night and moved on ahead of them like water finding its level.

That was acceptable. That was, in fact, the design.

He posted two men at the water door and two at the land path, and then he stood in front of the building with the lantern raised, and he gave the coast a moment — a genuine, unhurried moment — to see him. Word traveled fast here. Faster than horses, faster than telegraph. A light on the flats at midnight meant one thing and one thing only.

Then he set the fire himself. He did not delegate it. He had learned long ago that delegated violence curdled into enthusiasm, and enthusiasm broke things you needed intact. His own hand was steady, economical: lamp oil laid low along the tar seams where it would catch and climb and do its work thoroughly without theatrics. He stepped back, nodded to the man with the match, and watched the roofline begin to breathe orange.

Odo Ferrin came out of his cottage across the lane in his nightshirt, screaming his wife's name before he was fully through the door, and Corvus let him scream. There was no cruelty in standing aside. There was only sequence. The man would run to the boats first — they always ran to the boats — and find the water door blocked by polite men with truncheons, and then he would turn around and look at Corvus, and Corvus would explain the situation in the language of accounts receivable, and by dawn every soul in Lowmarsh would understand the new exchange rate: sheltering the Ishikawa girl now cost buildings.

It cost the company nothing. That was the elegance of it. The building was already forfeit on paper. The fire merely accelerated a foreclosure. If anyone ever asked — and no one ever asked, but if they did — there was no arson here at all, only misfortune arriving punctually.

Ferrin did exactly what Corvus knew he would do, right down to the sound he made when he found the water door held — a short, punched-out noise, less a word than the memory of one. Then he turned, nightshirt glowing at the hem in the firelight, and found Corvus standing at a respectful distance with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the flames with the expression of a man auditing columns.

"You," Ferrin said. His voice cracked in the middle like green wood.

"Your duties are forgiven," Corvus said. "Consider it compensation. The company regrets the loss, and there will be no further action regarding your licenses." He paused, and gave the man the only honesty he ever gave anyone, because honesty, rationed correctly, was more frightening than threats. "I'm told she stayed with the widow Maren before you. Tell your neighbors that I know, and that I did nothing to the widow Maren yet. Let them decide what *yet* is worth."

Ferrin stared at him. Behind him the roof groaned, and a sheet of flame rolled out through a vent like something exhaling. "You're burning my life for a girl who isn't even *here*—"

"I am not burning anything," Corvus said. "I am adjusting incentives. The distinction matters to the record." He turned to go, then stopped, because there was one more thing, and he had found over the years that the last thing was the thing that worked.

"Ferrin. When she comes back through — and she will come back through, they always double back — she'll ask you what happened. You'll tell her it was the man with the grey coat and the tea habit, and she'll add it to whatever ledger she keeps in her head, and she'll hate me a little more efficiently. Good. Hatred is predictable. I can navigate by it." He resettled his coat on his shoulders. "What I can't navigate is where she's carrying that book, and who she means to give it to. So think, tonight, while your roof comes down, about whether there's anyone on this coast who'd die for her. Because that person is my next address."

* * *

They watched the fire from the skiff, pulling slowly back across the channel, and Corvus let his men enjoy it — the heat on their faces, the sparks going up in strings like signal rockets, the village dogs losing their minds along the shoreline. Enjoyment was cheap and it built cohesion, and cohesion kept men obedient on nights when obedience mattered.

He himself watched the reflection instead of the fire. The whole bay had turned to hammered copper, and the burning boathouse floated doubled on the water, perfect and upside-down, the way everything on this coast existed twice — once as fact and once as its own reflection, and no way to say which was the original.

Pell rowed near the tiller, face lit unsteady orange, and finally worked up the courage that had been visibly accumulating in him all night. "Sir. May I ask — the girl. If we find her. What happens to her?"

"What the warrants say happens."

"And what do the warrants say?"

"They say she's a fugitive and an accessory." Corvus watched the copper water slide past the gunwale. "What they don't say is that the manifest she's carrying proves the warrants were written by the same hand that wrote the manifest. Which makes her, technically, the only honest bookkeeper this company currently employs."

Pell stared at him. "Then why are we—"

"Because the company pays me to retrieve property, Pell, and that ledger is company property. Every page of it. Even the parts that indict us." He said it without bitterness, and that was the worst of it — Pell would remember tonight not the fire but the calm, the terrible administrative calm of a man discussing his own guilt as a line item. "One day someone may ask why I burned a boathouse for a document that damns my employers. The answer is that I wasn't asked to judge it. I was asked to carry it. We are all of us carriers on this coast — her, me, the ferryman, poor Ferrin in his nightshirt. The only question worth asking is what we agree to carry."

The fire was collapsing inward now, the roof going down in sections, sending up a column of sparks that bent north on the wind — north, toward the high cliffs and the drowned chapel country and whatever route she was walking with her oilskin bundle strapped to her body like a second heart.

Corvus checked his pocket watch by the light of the burning building, noted the time in his log with a pencil stub — *structure loss, Lowmarsh, cause under investigation* — and closed the cover.

"Maren's cottage," he said. "First light. And send a rider to Graywater — tell them to move the brother. If she hears he's been transferred inland, she'll stop running and start bargaining, and I want her bargaining." He looked north once, at the darkness where the sparks had gone, and permitted himself the closest thing he had to a smile. "A girl who carries only what she can lift," he murmured, mostly to himself. "Let's see what happens when we start taking things she can't."
