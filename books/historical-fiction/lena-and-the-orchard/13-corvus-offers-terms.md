## 13. Corvus Offers Terms

They came for her at the river, three days out from the Nakamura house, where the water ran wide and brown and slow as poured tea.

Odile had known the country was narrowing around her the way a diver knows a current has turned — not by sight but by pressure. The fisherfolk who had fed her at Salt Row now met her eyes and looked away. A boy who might once have carried word down the coast for a copper piece took her coin and gave no message. Even the gulls seemed thinner here, the air scrubbed of the small commerce of living things. Somewhere behind her, methodical as tide-work, Corvus was closing the coast road lane by lane, burning or buying whatever shelter she'd counted on, and the country itself was learning to be empty of her.

So she turned inland, to the river. Rivers could not be burned.

She reached the crossing at dusk on the third day, when the ferryman's hut stood dark and the flat-bottomed boat knocked gently against its mooring post like something asleep. On the far bank the reeds went gold in the last light, and beyond them the road north, and beyond that — eighteen days gone now, twelve remaining — the capital, and the newspaperman, and Tomas behind wire at the checkpoint with her name on his transfer papers.

The ledger rode against her ribs in its oilskin wrap, tied twice with waxed cord. Her father's pearl was sewn into her sash. She owned nothing else. She had learned that lesson so young it had become the shape of her thinking: carry only what you can lift, know your water before you enter it, and never — *never* — let them see you measure the distance.

She was measuring it anyway when the lanterns bloomed.

* * *

There were four of them, spaced along the near bank the way men space themselves who have done this before — one upstream in the reeds, one downstream by the mooring post, two walking toward her unhurried, their boots loud on the shingle. Corvus came last, carrying his own lantern instead of letting a subordinate carry it, which told her more about him than any interrogation could have. He did not ask men to hold things he wanted held well.

He stopped six paces off, set the lantern down between them on the stones, and crouched beside it like a man warming his hands at a modest fire.

"You look tired," he said.

"I've been walking."

"Eleven days." He said it without checking anything, without notes, and she understood that he carried her route in his head the way she carried the ledger against her ribs — weighed, wrapped, kept dry. "Salt Row, then the chapel at Merrow Point — you were clever there; I lost you two full days among the flooded tombs — then inland through the orchard country, though that surprised me. You don't strike me as a woman who trusts trees."

"Trees can't be arrested."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Corvus's face was built for economy; everything on it cost him something. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the neat, unremarkable hands of a clerk, and he wore his coat buttoned to the throat despite the river damp. She had imagined him cruel. Cruelty would have been easier. What sat across the lantern light from her was worse: a man doing competent work, who would do it competently whether she wept or spat or begged.

"I read your file," he said. "Ishikawa, Odile. Father drowned in company service, 1904. Mother indentured, debt transferred to daughter on the mother's death. Sixteen years of service marked, none disputed." He paused. "Your ledgers are clean. Do you know how rare that is? In sixteen years I found one entry corrected in your favor — a miscounted shell weight in your eleventh year — and even that you reported yourself. Most divers would have kept the difference."

"What do you want, Mr. Corvus?"

"The pages you copied from the Meridian counting room. All of them." He said it plainly, the way a man states a fare. "And I've come to make you an offer, because I'm told you respond to offers better than to threats. Your brother is at the Harrow checkpoint. Sergeant's custody, transfer pending to the interior prison — that order goes through in nine days unless someone countersigns otherwise, and no one will. Nine days, Miss Ishikawa, and then Tomas Ishikawa disappears into a system that does not keep lists it intends to honor." He tilted his head. "You've been to the checkpoint country. You know what the interiors are."

The river said nothing. The reeds said nothing. Inside her chest her heart worked steadily, diver's heart, slow and deep, and she was grateful to it — grateful to her father for the body he'd left her, this calm machine that refused panic even while the rest of her wanted to scream.

"You're offering his freedom," she said.

"I'm offering his *release*. Those are different instruments and I want you to understand the difference, because you strike me as a woman who reads documents carefully." He drew a folded paper from inside his coat but did not open it. "Release: his warrant voided, his name struck from the transfer list, a pass written to get him home. It requires my signature and one counter-signature above me, both of which I can obtain tonight if the terms are agreed. Freedom — true freedom, papers in his own hand, no company mark anywhere on him — that comes later, if it comes at all, and not through me. But release means he walks out of Harrow alive. That part I can deliver."

"And the price."

"The originals. Not copies — I know you copied them, copying is what clerks' daughters do — the *originals*, every page, surrendered complete, plus your sworn silence and your return to Meridian service under amended contract." He finally opened the paper, smoothed it on his knee without looking at it. He knew it by heart too. "In exchange: Tomas released, your mother's grave left unmarked by the customs office — yes, we know where she's buried, and yes, there was a plan regarding it — and your own record annotated *rehabilitated* rather than *fugitive*. You go back to diving. The company keeps its leases. Everyone returns to the positions they occupied before you made this difficult."

"And the families whose names are already posted. The Nakamuras. The Oyels. The Ferreira girl's people."

"They were posted before my involvement. Their disposition is above my authority."

"Their disposition," Odile repeated. The words tasted like harbor mud. "You mean whether they're deported."

"I mean," Corvus said, with the first flicker of something that was almost honesty, "that I burn boathouses and draft warrants and I am very good at it, and none of it changes what happens to those families, which was decided in rooms I have never entered, by men whose names you will never learn. You cannot save them with paper. You cannot even inconvenience them with paper. What you can do, right now, tonight, on this riverbank, is decide whether your brother spends the rest of his short life breaking rock in the interior." He held the paper out across the lantern light. "That is the whole of the arithmetic. I've watched you calculate all week, Miss Ishikawa. Calculate this."

* * *

She took the paper. Of course she took it — refusing to touch it would tell him she'd already decided, and she needed him to believe, for another hundred heartbeats yet, that she was weighing it.

And she *was* weighing it. That was the terrible thing, the thing she would carry afterward like a swallowed stone: the offer was real. Tomas, alive, walking out of Harrow with a pass in his pocket. Against it, a bundle of pages proving that the Meridian Trading Company had manufactured treason out of whole cloth, had forged warrants under a magistrate's seal, had branded four families enemy sympathizers to strip their harbor leases — proof that would die in her hands or his fire, and with it the Nakamuras, and the Oyels, and everyone else whose names the customs board had already taught the country to hate.

One brother against a nation's worth of strangers.

Her mother would have taken the trade. Her mother, who had taught her *carry only what you can lift* while quietly drowning herself in debts she couldn't lift, would have signed, would have knelt, would have called it sense. And Odile loved her mother, and understood her, and knew with a cold certainty that had settled in her over eleven days of walking like ballast in her hull that she was not her mother.

Because she had read the duplicate ledgers by candle stub. She knew what paper could do. Paper had drowned her father's good name, paper had indentured her at six years old, paper was at this moment holding Tomas in a cage — and paper, these particular pages, oilskin-wrapped against her ribs, was also the only weapon in the world that could cut back. Surrender it and every future family got the same warrant with a new name inked in. Keep it and maybe, maybe, twelve days from now, a newspaperman with a sympathetic press turned the truth into a fire hot enough to melt the whole rotten charter.

Maybe. It was a thin plank to put a brother's weight on.

But she had walked out over thin planks her whole life, over oyster beds that might hold her and might not, and her father's first rule had never been *don't take risks.* It had been *know your water.*

She looked past Corvus, at the river.

It ran wide here, forty feet at least, brown-green, opaque with silt, moving slower than it looked — rivers always lied about their speed; the surface crawled while the middle ran hard. Cold, this time of year, cold enough to stop an untrained heart. The far bank was reed-gold and darkening. There was a boat at the mooring post, and men downstream, and lanterns, and Corvus's four pairs of eyes reading her face line by line for the decision he had come three hundred miles to buy.

They saw a cornered girl. Tired, eleven days hunted, standing at the last water between her and nowhere.

What they did not see — because no clerk had ever thought to write it in any file — was that Odile Ishikawa had been entering water since before she could walk. That she had dived the Meridian shallows at dawn in winter, breath held past pain, in waters where her father died. That her body knew exactly what cold did, and when, and how long it could be endured. That she had spent sixteen years learning the one trade the company could neither own nor confiscate: how to leave the surface, and stay down, and come up where no one was watching.

Forty feet of river. One bundle, wrapped in oilskin, tied twice with waxed cord.

Corvus watched her read his paper. He saw her lips move slightly, tallying terms. He saw her shoulders lower by a fraction — the surrender of a woman setting down a weight. Every signal she gave him was true except for the meaning.

"The counter-signature," she said. "Above you. Who holds it?"

"The district supervisor at Vane's Crossing. He'll telegraph it tonight, once I confirm the goods are secured." He produced, from his breast pocket, a second document — the countersign form, blank but for his signature, waiting. "Confirm now, and Tomas sleeps in his own bed within the week."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I take the pages from you however they must be taken, and Tomas goes to the interior regardless, and I will regret the inefficiency." He said it without heat, which was the worst of it. "I'd prefer not to. Wet work leaves witnesses, and witnesses leave questions, and questions are how companies end up in newspapers. Give me the bundle, Miss Ishikawa. Walk away dry. It's the best bargain anyone will ever offer you."

The lantern hissed. Downstream, one of his men shifted his weight on the shingle, and the sound was very loud.

Odile looked at the paper in her hand a moment longer. Then she folded it, neatly, along its existing crease, and tucked it into her sash beside the pearl — and Corvus, watching her hands, relaxed by one degree, because a woman who saves the paperwork is a woman intending to use it.

"You're right about one thing," she said. "It's a fair offer. Let me see the water before I sign."

"The water?"

"I'm a diver's daughter, Mr. Corvus. I don't sign anything on a riverbank without knowing what's under it." She was already turning, already walking toward the mooring post, unhurried, a woman going to look at a boat. "Your men can watch if it pleases them. I'll be thirty seconds."

He hesitated — she heard the hesitation in his silence, felt it the way you feel a current change — but what could he do? Refuse a girl a look at a river? Drag her back and search her in front of four witnesses and lose the bargain's shape entirely? She heard him rise, heard the gravel shift as his men closed casually inward, and she kept walking, measuring without appearing to measure: twenty paces to the mooring post, the boat knocking at its rope, the drop from the post's edge straight into dark water, the current running left to right toward the downstream man — *toward* him, damn it, she'd have to angle hard against it —

— and the cold. Forty feet of brown cold, and the oilskin bundle, and lungs that had been trained since childhood on winter shallows.

Thirty seconds. Her father's letter, unsent, hidden in a drowned chapel for nine years, had waited longer than that.

She reached the mooring post. Put one hand on it, as if steadying herself to peer down. The water below was black and smooth and utterly silent, and it smelled of silt and depth and, faintly, impossibly, of her father's trade — salt ghosting up from the estuary miles downstream, the sea reaching inland to remind her what she was.

Corvus's voice came behind her, reasonable, patient: "Well?"

"The bottom's silt," she said. "Good holding ground. You'd never drag it clean."

And she stepped off the post and was gone.

* * *

The cold hit her like a wall of iron and she welcomed it, because pain was information and information kept you alive. Down, down — she didn't fight the plunge, she *rode* it, arms crossed over the bundle, legs together, knife-straight, taking the depth the way her father had taught her to take it: fast through the surface layer where the shock lived, then level, then swim.

Brown darkness. No light but a dim gold smear somewhere above where the lanterns touched the water, and that smear was a map: it told her the bank, told her the men were gathered at it, told her that downstream — left, against the current — the gold faded into nothing, and nothing was where she wanted to be.

The current took her immediately. It gripped her like hands and pulled, and she angled her body against it, swimming not up but *along*, low over the silt, feeling the riverbed roll beneath her fingers when she reached down — silt, then a snag of root, then open clay. Her lungs began their argument. She ignored them the way she'd learned to ignore them at eight years old, hanging in green shallows while her father counted her kicks from the boat: *the body panics first, little fish, and the diver's job is to outlast the panic. Breathe is a wish. Time is a fact.*

Time was a fact. She had perhaps ninety seconds of clear thought, two minutes of ugly survival after that, and the far bank was forty feet of current away and she had to arrive *upstream* of wherever she meant to surface so the water would carry her into the reeds instead of past them.

She swam. Stroke and glide, stroke and glide, the way her father had taught her — never fighting the water, borrowing it, spending nothing she didn't have to. The cold closed around her chest like a fist. Somewhere above, muffled through fathoms of brown water, she heard shouting — thin, distorted, the syllables stripped out of it, just human noise on a bank — and the gold smears of lanterns swinging wildly, converging, sweeping the surface where she had gone down.

Let them watch the surface. The surface was a lie. Everything worth knowing happened underneath.

Her hands found the far shelf — the riverbed rising, clay giving way to gravel, then to rooted silt — and she followed it up slowly, angling hard against the current, until her head broke water inside the reeds with barely a ripple, in shadow, in silence, forty yards downstream of where every lantern on the near bank was pointing.

She hung there in the reeds, chest heaving, arms locked around the bundle, and listened.

"—drag the reach, all of it, she can't have crossed—" Corvus's voice, still level, still economical, but with a hairline crack of anger running through it now, and Odile floated in the freezing shallows and felt a fierce, unmotherly joy. "The boat. Is the boat still tied? *Then untie it and get across.* Lamps on the far bank, both edges, quarter-mile intervals. She's wet, she's cold, she'll need fire within the hour — watch every smoke on the horizon. Go."

Boots thundered. Rope creaked. The ferry boat pushed off into the dark with two men and a lantern, sweeping its beam across the water in long, confident strokes — searching the surface, the banks, the obvious places, the places a frightened animal runs to.

Odile lay back in the reeds and let the cold have her teeth, and smiled at the sky.

They were searching for a fugitive. They should have been searching for a diver. A fugitive runs from the water; a diver treats it as road. By morning she'd be stiff with chill and lucky not to lose two fingers to it, and she'd pay that bill gladly, because the bundle against her chest was dry — dry as the day she'd wrapped it, waxed cord tight, oilskin sealed — and every page of the truth was still hers.

Corvus stood on the far shore now, a silhouette against his own lantern, and for a long moment he simply looked at the river, the way a clerk looks at a column that refuses to sum. Then, faintly, across the water, she heard him speak — not to his men, not quite to himself:

"Ninety seconds underwater in winter current. Carrying cargo." A pause. "File note: subject's father trained her. Underestimate the family again and this becomes expensive."

He turned and gave the orders, and the lanterns began to move, and Odile slid backward into the reeds like a fish returning to deep water, and started walking north — cold-soaked, half-numb, lighter by nothing, richer by everything, with ten days left and the hardest water of all still waiting: the military checkpoint at Harrow, where Tomas slept behind wire, and where no trick of breath or oilskin could carry her through.

But that was tomorrow's dive. Tonight she knew her water, and her water had held.

Behind her, on the far bank, Corvus stood a moment longer at the edge of the black river, lantern raised, studying the water as though he could read what lay beneath it. Then he snuffed the light.
