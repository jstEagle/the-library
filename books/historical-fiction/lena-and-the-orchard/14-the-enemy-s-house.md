## 14. The Enemy's House

The Nakamura house sat on the shoulder of a hill above the estuary, and it had been beautiful once — Odile could see that the way you can see the shape of a face in an old photograph, all its living features gone but the bones still speaking. A long veranda faced the water, its railing broken now in three places like teeth knocked from a jaw. The garden terraces had gone to bramble. Paper lanterns no longer hung under the eaves; only their empty iron frames remained, black against the evening sky, like small cages with nothing left to hold.

She had walked two days since the river, her clothes stiff with salt and drying mud, the ledger wrapped in oilskin against her chest beneath her coat where Corvus would never think to look twice at a woman built thin as a gull. Two days of walking with her hood up and her eyes down, past pearl boats she didn't dare hail, past a customs post she went four miles inland to avoid. Her feet had stopped hurting somewhere on the second morning, which frightened her more than pain would have. Pain was information. Numbness was just the body deciding to stop reporting.

The hill path was overgrown but not abandoned. That was what made her notice it — someone had cut back the gorse recently, and there were footprints in the clay, small ones, child-sized, going up and coming down. People still came here. She followed the prints because her body was done making decisions and wanted only to be told what to do, and because the alternative was sleeping in the open again with frost coming off the water like breath.

She smelled woodsmoke before she saw them.

* * *

There was a woman kneeling in the ruined kitchen garden, cutting winter greens into a basket, and when she looked up Odile understood in one cold drop why the house was ruined and who lived in it anyway. The woman's face was the face from the customs board — not this woman's own board, but the one in Meridian harbor, the one Odile had stood before while the harbor master read names aloud. Nakamura. The word had been posted in ink so fresh it shone. And here was the face attached to it, older and thinner than the printed accusation, with grey coming through her black hair and hands stained green to the wrist.

They regarded each other across the garden rows. Odile waited for the scream, or the running, or at minimum the hard flat look that strangers gave her now that foreign blood was a crime you could read about on a public wall.

"You're soaked through," the woman said. "Come by the fire before you take your death."

It was said the way you'd say it to a neighbor. Odile did not move. "You know my face?"

"I know your kind of tired." The woman rose, joints cracking, and lifted the basket. "My daughter will have laid the fire already. Come."

"I'm Ishikawa," Odile said, because some stubborn honest thing in her refused to enter this house under any name but her own. "Odile Ishikawa. My family's lease is in Meridian harbor. The company has—" She stopped, recalibrated. "I saw your name on the customs board."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes, quick as a fish turning. Not fear. Recognition, maybe. Or grief finding its old address.

"Then you're the girl they're hunting," she said. "The one who ran with papers." She said it without judgment, the way you'd say *the one who brought the rain*. "Come inside, Ishikawa-san. We'll talk after you've eaten. Nothing true is ever said well on an empty stomach, and nothing safe either."

* * *

The inside of the house was a lesson in what ruin means. The formal room had been stripped — the company men had come through in spring, neighbors told her later, and taken everything of value under the authority of the seizure warrant, the good lacquer, the silk, her husband's diving weights and his father's sword that had crossed an ocean wrapped in oilcloth three generations ago. But the walls still held the pale rectangles where the pictures had hung, and the family had not left those rectangles empty. In each one, tacked or pasted or pinned, hung paper: a pressed spray of plum blossom, a child's drawing of a boat, a page of sheet music, a photograph of a wedding party squinting into sun.

"They took the frames," the woman said, following Odile's eyes as she set out bowls. "Frames sell. So we put our things directly on the walls. It annoys them, I think, that we are still decorating."

Her name was Hana. Her daughter was Miki, fourteen, sharp-elbowed and quick-eyed, who fed the fire and pretended not to study Odile with the frank hunger of a girl starved for news. There was a son too, Kenji, eleven, who had been sent to sleep at a neighbor's down the valley because — Hana said this lightly, arranging bowls — "he has his father's temper and no sense yet for when to swallow it."

"And your husband?"

"Gone ahead." A pause with weight in it. "He went east in the autumn to find work and send for us. Letters come sometimes. Slowly." She smoothed the mat beside her. "Sit. Eat."

The meal was rice and the winter greens, a little dried fish, tea brewed from something that wasn't quite tea — roasted barley, Odile guessed, the drink of people stretching a pantry. It should have tasted like poverty. It tasted like the best food she had ever eaten, and she ate too fast and was ashamed of it and Hana simply refilled her bowl without comment, which was somehow worse, or better, Odile couldn't decide.

"The ledger," Hana said at last, when the bowls were empty. "That's what you carry. The whole coast is whispering about it. Papers that prove the smuggling charges were made of nothing."

Odile's hand went to her chest before she could stop it. Old instinct. Wrong instinct here.

"You don't need to be afraid of me," Hana said. "But you should be careful of how afraid you are everywhere else. Fear shows on a person like wet cloth. Sit differently. Drink your tea. If soldiers come to this valley, I am a widow keeping house alone and you are my sister's daughter visiting from upcoast, and your name is—" she paused— "well. Your name is whatever we decide it is five minutes before they ask."

"Why?" The question came out of Odile raw and unguarded. "Your family lost everything because of the company. Because of families like mine, whose names got put on the same list, whose leases—" She stopped again. Started over, plainer. "I've done nothing for you. I'm a stranger carrying trouble. You'd be safer if you turned me out tonight."

Hana considered this with the unhurried attention of someone who had spent a year thinking about exactly such questions.

"When the company men came," she said, "they read the warrant aloud on our doorstep. Smugglers. Enemy sympathizers. Traitors to a country most of us have never seen." She turned her bowl slowly on the mat. "Our neighbors watched from their windows. Some believed it. Some knew it was lies and said nothing, because saying something costs. One man — old Ferro, the eel-fisher — he stood in the road and argued until they pushed him down. He's eighty. They pushed an old man into the mud for us." Her voice stayed level. "Do you understand? One person in the whole town stood in the road. I have thought about that every day since. What it cost him. What it would have cost the others. And I decided that whatever years I have left, I will be the one standing in the road."

Miki, by the fire, had gone very still.

"So no," Hana said, "you owe me nothing, and I don't help you because you might someday repay it. I help you because the company taught my family what it feels like to be alone in a doorway with everyone watching. And I will not be one of the watchers. Not ever again. Now — tell me where you're going, and don't say you can't, because a girl doesn't walk two days through frost without a destination in her chest."

* * *

Odile told her some of it. Not the river dive, not yet — that story felt too much like showing a wound to strangers — but the shape of it: the copied pages, the checkpoint where Tomas was held, the newspaperman waiting in the capital, the month's end breathing down her neck like weather. Hana listened the way her father used to listen to the sea before a dive, entirely, without interrupting, and when Odile finished she was quiet a long moment.

"There's a boat," Hana said. "Old Sato's ketch, the *Kestrel*. He runs charcoal down the coast, and once a fortnight he goes past the point near Harrow Bay, where the military road meets the river. He owes my husband money he'll never admit to, which makes him generous in a nervous way. He could put you ashore below the checkpoint road without anyone marking a strange face arriving by land."

"And if the company has watchers on the water?"

"Then you'll need to be smarter than watchers, which by all accounts you already are." Hana's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Sato sails Thursday. Three days. You'll stay."

"Three days is a long time to hide me."

"Three days is a short time to mend boots," Hana said, nodding at Odile's feet, where the sole was flapping loose as a fish's mouth. "And you cannot deliver justice to a nation barefoot."

* * *

The three days rearranged something in Odile that she didn't fully understand until later.

In the mornings Miki drilled her in the local accent, laughing at the way Odile flattened her vowels, until a stranger would hear only a coast girl and not a harbor girl. Hana mended the boots with waxed thread and a sailmaker's palm, muttering that whoever had made them deserved to be indentured herself. Odile chopped firewood, hauled water from the spring, helped Miki cut the greens — useful work, work that filled her hands so her mind could stop circling Tomas's face the day they took him, the way he hadn't cried, the way he'd looked at her over the constable's shoulder as if memorizing her.

On the second night she found Kenji's hiding place. The boy kept a tin box — a tin box, and Odile had laughed before she could stop herself, a real laugh that startled them both — filled with marbles, a bird feather, a brass button, and a folded paper on which he had written, in enormous careful letters, THE COMPANY IS A LIAR. He showed her because she was the first person outside the family to enter the house in months, and children hoard their evidence the way adults hoard coin.

"That's contraband," she told him solemnly. "If the magistrate comes, you eat it."

His eyes went wide and delighted. From the kitchen Hana called, "Kenji. Bed," in a tone that meant *stop corrupting our guest*, but she was smiling when she said it.

Later, sitting by the banked fire while the house breathed around her, Odile took out her own tin — she still carried it, though it held nothing now but her father's unsent letter, the pearl having gone to the captain at the checkpoint like a coin dropped in a river for luck. She weighed it in her palm. Once, it had measured her whole life: what fits, what lifts, what survives the run. Now it measured something else. A dead man's honesty. A boy's marble collection. The same small stubborn faith, passed hand to hand like embers.

*You carry more than paper now,* she thought, remembering the drowned chapel, the letter she'd found wrapped in oilskin behind the loose stone where her father used to hide pearls. *He died refusing to sign a lie. And these people live refusing to believe one.*

* * *

On the third evening, Hana packed her a bundle: barley tea, rice balls wrapped in leaf, a strip of dried fish, and — pressed into her hand last, folded small — paper money.

"I can't take this."

"You can and you will." Hana's voice had the same gentle finality as when she ordered more rice into a bowl. "It's not charity. Call it fare. When you reach the capital and the truth is printed, write to us. Care of old Ferro, the eel-fisher, Harrow Town. He reads letters aloud to the whole street whether they want him to or not." This time the smile came all the way. "Let them hear it from a stranger's mouth. Let them hear we were right."

Odile stood in the doorway of the ruined house, under the empty lantern frames, and found that her throat had closed. She had spent sixteen years learning to leave — leaving was easy, leaving was the one skill the world had made sure she mastered. But leaving this house felt like tearing something that had begun, quietly, without her permission, to grow roots.

"Hana." She got the word out past the knot. "Why do they call it the enemy's house? On the customs boards, the posters — people cross the street from this gate. I heard a boy singing a rhyme about it in the village below."

Hana looked out past her, down the darkening hill toward the water, where the first stars were coming up over the estuary like pearls spilled on black silk.

"Because it's easier to hate a house than to wonder about the people in it," she said. "Go safely, Odile Ishikawa. And remember — mercy isn't a debt. That's the whole point of it. That's what makes it stronger than anything the company sells."

Miki hugged her quickly, fiercely, and whispered, "Come back when it's over. I want to see your brother." Kenji, half-asleep on the mat, raised one arm in a solemn salute.

Then Odile went down the hill through the gorse with the bundle on her back and the ledger warm against her heart, and behind her the enemy's house stood watch over the water with its paper pictures on the walls, decorating its ruins, waiting — like her, like everyone the company had ever wronged — for the light to change.

Thursday, Sato's ketch would sail. And somewhere between here and the capital, between her and Tomas, between her and the printing press that could burn the company down with ink instead of fire, lay the hardest miles of all.

She did not look back. But she carried the house with her anyway — she was, after all, a diver's daughter, and divers always bring something precious up from the deep.
