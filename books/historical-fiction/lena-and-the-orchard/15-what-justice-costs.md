## 15. What Justice Costs

Elias Vane had learned to read his father's silences the way sailors read weather, and by the third week of October the house on Magistrate's Row was forecasting a storm that would not break indoors.

He noticed it first in the way his father stopped saying Odile's name. For years it had surfaced at dinner like a bone in fish — *the Ishikawa girl*, spat with a curl of distaste — but now there was nothing, and nothing from Aldous Vane was louder than any accusation he'd ever made. Elias understood what the silence meant. His father knew the warrants were forged. He had known since the night Elias laid the copied ledger pages on his desk and watched the candlelight gutter across them, and instead of rage there had been only that terrible, measured quiet, and then: *"You will speak of this to no one."*

Not *this is false*. Not *who dared*. Just *you will speak of this to no one* — which was its own confession, sealed and stamped.

Elias had spent three weeks being a magistrate's son in a house built on paper. He ate breakfast across from a forger. He polished the boots of a man who had sold four families to a trading company the way one sells a bad season's wool. He signed his own name beneath his father's on documents whose contents he no longer trusted himself to read, because reading them made him complicit, and not reading them made him complicit more cheaply.

The copied pages stayed where he'd hidden them, sewn into the ticking of his mattress, and every night he lay on top of them like a man lying on coals.

It was the newspaperman who finally broke him.

* * *

His name was Ambrose Quill, and he came to the magistrate's office on a Tuesday with ink stains to the second knuckle and a hat that had been fashionable during the last reign. He was writing a piece on harbor tariffs, he said, nothing political, just numbers and names, and could he trouble His Honor for the lease registries going back five years?

His father granted it with the smoothness of long practice. And Elias, fetching the registries as he fetched everything in that office, watched Quill's eyes move down the columns of names and stop — not at the entries, but at the gaps between them. Four leases transferred within six weeks. Four families posted on the customs board as sympathizers. A pattern visible to anyone who looked, and almost no one ever looked.

Quill caught him looking.

They spoke in the records room among the smell of dust and binding glue, voices low, and Quill did not waste time on preamble. "You're Vane's son," he said. "Which means you either know something or you're paid not to."

"I know things I can't prove."

"Proving is my trade, not yours. Give me what you have and let me do the proving in print, where your father's seal carries no weight." Quill's voice dropped further. "There's a paper in the capital. The *Meridian Standard* — ironic name, we kept it when we bought it out from under them. My editor will run the ledgers if they're genuine. But they have to be in his hands by the last day of the month. After that—" He shrugged, and the shrug contained the whole machinery of the state: the session closing, the company's charter renewal coming before a committee already salted with Meridian men, the window shutting like a door in winter.

"Why are you doing this?" Elias asked. "What do you get?"

Quill smiled without humor. "A story that will be remembered after I'm dead. That's the only currency worth anything, young man. Everything else is just rent." He pressed a card into Elias's hand. An address in the capital. A date. "Bring pages, not copies of copies if you can help it. Bring originals, or bring enough that no court in the country can call it forgery. And bring them yourself — don't trust the post, don't trust the trains' baggage cars, don't trust anyone whose wages depend on the company. The road through Harrow's Crossing is the fastest route. It's also—"

"I know what's at Harrow's Crossing."

Quill studied him for a moment. "Then you understand the arithmetic," he said, and left Elias alone in the records room with the dust settling around him like slow snow.

* * *

That night Elias took the pages from his mattress and read them again by candlelight, though he knew them by heart now.

Four households. The Nakamura lease, voided on evidence of smuggled silk that had never existed except as an entry in a duplicate ledger written in the company's own hand. The Osei family, their oyster beds seized on a manifest falsified so neatly that the forgery bore the date of a day the harbor had been closed for storm. The Brandts. The Ishikawas. Four names, four identical phrasings in four separate warrants — *goods entered contrary to statute, consigned to parties of enemy sympathy* — the same sentences repeated like a form filled out four times, because that was exactly what it was. A template. Someone in the company offices had committed treason against four families with a pen and a blotter and had gone home to supper afterward.

And beneath it all, page after page, the seal of Aldous Vane, Justice of the Peace, authorizing each seizure.

His father's seal. Whether his father's hand or merely his father's rubber stamp applied by another's fingers, Elias could not prove — and it did not matter. It was his name on the machine, and Elias had spent twenty-six years benefiting from the machine's turning.

He thought of Odile standing in his father's study three weeks ago, her hands still smelling of brine even after she'd scrubbed them, holding out the pages she'd copied by candle stub in a room where discovery meant the debt ledgers and worse. He had begged her to wait. *Proper channels,* he'd said, as if the channels were proper, as if the word *proper* had any meaning left in a province where his father was the law. *Give me a week.*

She had given him the week. And in the week the company had moved — had been moving all along, he understood now; the week had never been a reprieve, only a courtesy the company extended to no one — and Tomas Ishikawa had been taken off the fish quay in front of witnesses, and the Ishikawa lease had passed into Meridian hands with a signature that might have been his father's.

Odile was gone by then. Run, they said. Fugitive. There was a price on information concerning her, and Corvus — Elias had heard the name spoken in his father's presence with the particular respect reserved for effective instruments — was hunting her along the coast.

He had done that. He had asked the woman with nothing to wait while he consulted his conscience, and his conscience had cost her brother.

Elias dressed in the dark. He took the copied pages, his commission papers, the clothes that would pass in the capital, and forty-one pounds saved against a future he no longer believed in. He wrote his father no letter. There was nothing to say that the silence between them had not already said.

At the door he paused once, looking back at the house where he had learned justice as a boy — the high ceilings, the law books, his mother's portrait with its painted serenity — and felt the strangest sensation, not of leaving home but of finally admitting he had left it years ago, on an afternoon when he tore up a debt ledger on a whim and made a promise to a girl with salt in her hair.

Promises were made in one world, she had said once, and kept in another. He was crossing over now.

* * *

Finding her took nine days, and he found her because he stopped looking for a fugitive and started asking after a diver.

The coastal people lied to him magnificently at first — a magistrate's son, even a disgraced one, got the flat faces and blank words that the coast reserved for officialdom. But Elias had grown up on the edges of these villages, had swum in their coves as a boy while his father dined with company men, and some of the older fishermen remembered him as something other than a Vane. Old Seraf, who mended nets at Greywater Point, looked at him a long time and then said only: "The tide brings things in past the drowned chapel, some mornings. Things it keeps." And that was direction enough.

He walked the last stretch at dawn, along a shingle beach where the light came up the color of pearl shell, and found the chapel half-sunk in the tidal pool as the stories said — roofless, its stones furred with green, its bell arch standing in water like the ribcage of something patient. She was there at the water's edge, kneeling, washing something in a rock pool, and for a moment he simply stood and watched her, because grief and relief arrived together and canceled each other into stillness.

She heard him — of course she did; the shingle announced every footfall — and she rose without hurry and turned, and he saw what three weeks and a hunted autumn had done to her. Thinner. Harder at the eyes. Her hair cut short and ragged, hacked off probably with a knife for practicality, and somehow that small violence hurt him worse than anything else he saw.

"You shouldn't have come here," she said. No greeting. "If you were followed—"

"No one followed me. I left in the night. Odile, I—"

"If you say you're sorry, I'll walk into the sea and save everyone the trouble." But her voice cracked on the last word, betraying her, and she turned away sharply and crouched by the pool again, scrubbing at nothing.

He came and stood beside her. In the pool below, the tide had stranded a scatter of shells and one dull coin, green with age. "I brought the pages back," he said. "You should have them. If something happens to me—"

"Nothing will happen to you. You're a magistrate's son."

"Not anymore." He told her then, quickly, before courage failed: the disownment, the stripping of his commission, his father's face going white and then cold and then closed like a ledger balanced. *You will choose strangers over your own blood,* his father had said, and Elias had answered, *I am choosing what you taught me the law was for,* and had seen in his father's eyes that the words landed somewhere true and were hated precisely for that. "I'm nobody now," he finished. "I've never been so glad to be nobody in my life."

She looked up at him then, really looked, and something in her face eased — not forgiveness, not yet, but the suspension of the sentence she'd been carrying. "You broke," she said quietly. "I wondered how long it would take. I used to think you wouldn't. I hated you for it."

"I know."

"Good." She wrung out the cloth in her hands and stood. "Now tell me why you crossed half the country to be nobody at my side. Nobody walks nine days for sentiment."

So he told her about Quill. About the *Meridian Standard*, the capital, the last day of the month. About the editor who would print the ledgers — the real ones, hers, the pages copied by candle stub in the company offices — and turn four destroyed families into a scandal that no charter committee could survive. He watched hope come into her face the way dawn comes into a harbor, slowly and then all at once, and he hated what he had to say next.

"There's one road fast enough," he said. "Through Harrow's Crossing."

Her whole body changed. It was subtle — a diver learns stillness better than anyone alive — but he saw it: the moment the name landed, the moment the arithmetic began behind her eyes.

"Tomas is held there," she said. Flatly. Factually. As if reporting the depth of water over a wreck.

"Yes."

"And the ledger has to reach the capital by month's end."

"Or the window closes. Charter renewal in spring, Quill says. After that, the company owns the government openly instead of privately, and no paper in the country will dare print it."

She was silent for a long time. The tide moved in the pool, small sounds, indifferent. Somewhere inland a cart creaked along a road, someone's ordinary morning proceeding on schedule while theirs hung suspended.

"So," she said at last, and her voice was terribly steady, "either I carry the ledger past the checkpoint where my brother is caged — past the soldiers, past whatever remains of Corvus's hunt, past every eye trained this season on the coast roads for a runaway girl — or I leave it here and go to him, and the truth stays folded in oilskin until it's worthless." She laughed, short and airless. "My mother taught me to carry only what I can lift. No one warned me the weight would be this kind."

"We could split it. Copy the pages again, send half each way—"

"Half a ledger proves nothing. You said so yourself — enough that no court can call it forgery. It's everything or nothing." She turned to face him fully, and he saw that she had already decided something, had perhaps decided it the moment he said *Harrow's Crossing*, and was only arranging the pieces now. "You take the ledger."

"What?"

"You take the ledger to the capital. You're a Vane — you can travel the roads with papers, with a name that opens gates even stripped of office. I'm a posted fugitive; every mile I walk toward a military checkpoint is a mile bought with luck, and my luck has been spent for weeks." She was talking faster now, building the plan aloud the way she'd build a dive, testing each hold before committing her weight. "But I'm not sitting on this beach waiting for news, Elias. Tomas is in that checkpoint because I waited once already. Because you asked for a week. I'm done paying for patience."

"Alone? Odile, you can't walk into a military post alone."

"I wasn't planning to walk in blind." Something moved behind her eyes — a card held close to the chest, an inheritance he didn't yet know about. "There's a captain at Harrow's Crossing. Rennick. He served under my father, before the drowning, before the company owned everything that floats. My father died refusing to falsify a manifest for them, Elias. Whatever else the company has taken, it hasn't taken that — and men like Rennick remember things like that, whether they admit it or not." She picked up her pack, small and hard-edged, everything she owned fitting inside it with room to spare. "I have proof of what my father was. I'm going to see whether it buys anything."

"It could buy you a cell next to his."

"Then we'll be a family reunion with bars." But her mouth trembled around the joke, and she pressed her lips together until it steadied. "How many days to month's end?"

"Nine. Eight, if the capital post is slow."

"Then you leave tomorrow, at first light, with the ledger sewn into your coat. I leave tonight." She looked east, where the water went on forever, gray-green and patient. "Elias. When this is done — if it's done — I want you to remember that you chose this. Not for me. Don't make it for me, or you'll resent me by spring, and I couldn't survive being resented by the only person left who believes me."

"It's for both of us," he said. "It was always both of us. Since the day I tore up that ledger, I've been walking toward this — I just took the long way."

She almost smiled. Then her gaze went past his shoulder, up the shingle, and stiffened. Far off along the ridge road, thin in the morning haze: a rider, unhurried, pausing at the crest to look down at the shore. Too far to name. Close enough to count.

"The chapel," she said, low. "Now."

They moved together into the drowned ruin's shadow, the cold water rising over their boots, the bell arch above them holding its silence, and Elias understood with sudden clarity that the impossible choice had never truly offered two roads at all. There had only ever been one road. It ran through Harrow's Crossing, and both of them would walk it — she to her brother, he to the presses — and neither of them knew yet which of them the month would keep.
