## 21. Diving the Shallows Again

The water off the reclaimed Ishikawa lease was the same water it had always been — green-gold in the morning, cold as a held breath at three fathoms — but it felt different now, the way a room feels different after the furniture has been moved out and carried back in again by other hands. My hands. My boat. My bottom, as the harbor master had been obliged to write on the new papers, though he wrote it sourly, pressing so hard the nib nearly went through.

I stood at the gunwale in my diving dress and looked down through the early light. Below me the sand rippled like the skin of something sleeping. A school of silver fry turned all at once, the way fish do, as if they'd heard a thought. Somewhere under that sand were pearls. Maybe. The sea never promised anything; that was the first thing my father taught me, before he taught me to hold my breath, before he taught me that a diver's whole life is an argument with her own lungs.

*Keep only what you can lift,* my mother used to say, back when keeping was all we did and lifting was all we were allowed.

This morning I intended to lift very little. That was the point.

Tomas sat in the stern with two hand lines coiled in his lap, mending a leader by feel, his fingers moving without his eyes. He had grown thin in the company's custody and had not entirely grown back — there was still a hollowness at his cheeks, a way he had of sitting slightly sideways, as if making room for someone who wasn't there anymore. But he was here. He was beside me. And for eleven months now, slowly, like tide coming back over a flat, he had been speaking again.

"Wind's backing east," he said. "You'll get your clear water till noon."

I grunted agreement around the mouthpiece stem. Some mornings we talked like ordinary brother and sister, about prices at the fish market, about the Nakamuras' roof, which Mr. Nakamura swore would be mended by autumn and which had said so every week since spring. Some mornings we said almost nothing, and the nothing was comfortable, which was its own kind of miracle. A year ago the nothing had been full of screaming. You couldn't hear it, but it was there, in the way he watched the customs board when our boat passed it, in the way he flinched at sealed envelopes.

No sealed envelopes today. Today there was only work, and the good kind — the kind nobody could take a percentage of.

I spat into my palm, pressed the pearl shell's edge against my palm the old way, and went over the side.

* * *

Down, the world simplified. Sound went away except for my own blood, ticking like a clock in my ears. Light came down in slanted columns, and the sand below was written over with ripples, and among the ripples lay the oysters, clustered dark as spilled ink, their shells furred with weed.

My father had dived these same beds. Not these exact ones — the company had moved the boundaries twice since his death, redrawing the water the way they'd redrawn everything else — but the same water, the same cold, the same tick of blood in the ears. He had gone down for them eleven hundred times, maybe more, and come up each time with his hands full of their property. And once, only once, he had refused to lie about what those hands had found, and the sea had kept him.

That was the story I had told myself for years: *the sea kept him.* It was gentler than the truth, which was that men in a boat above him had made a decision about the value of a manifest versus the value of a man, and the man had lost. I knew it now from his own hand, from the unsent letter I had pulled out of the drowned chapel wrapped in oilskin and wax, the letters still legible, the anger still legible, the love most legible of all. *They asked me to sign and I would not. Tell Odile the water is honest even when the ledgers are not.*

I worked the beds with the old economy, thumb and knife, prying only the shells that felt heavy, leaving the young ones to grow. The company divers had stripped these waters for twenty years and called it harvest. I meant to do it differently. Take a little, leave a lot, let the bed remember kindness. It was not sentiment. It was arithmetic — the long kind, the kind the Meridian Trading Company had never learned because it planned to be dead before the bill came due. Well. Its charter was suspended now. The bill had come due while it still drew breath, and I had helped present it.

A shell came free in my hand, heavy, and I thumbed it open on the bottom, half expecting nothing. There was a pearl in it. Small, misshapen, the color of weak tea — a pearl no broker would pay ten coppers for.

I almost let it go. Then I closed my fist around it and kicked for the surface, laughing into my mouthpiece, bubbles streaming up past my face like a handful of thrown coins.

Because it was mine. Because I found it, and I would keep it, and no one would weigh it, or ledger it, or enter it into any book with a company seal on the spine. Ten coppers' worth of ugly pearl, and worth more to me than everything the Meridian Company had ever claimed to own.

* * *

I broke the surface near the boat and Tomas leaned over to take my bag.

"Anything?"

"One," I said, holding it up between finger and thumb before dropping it in the shell bucket. "Ugly as sin."

"Like your cooking."

"My cooking has improved."

"It has not." But he was smiling — the small, real smile, not the careful one — and he went back to his line, and the morning opened out around us like a hand unclenching.

We worked until the sun stood high. I made six more dives, took four more shells, left a hundred. Tomas caught enough fish for supper and threw back a snapper that was honestly too small to keep, which he would have sold without a thought a year ago, and neither of us remarked on it, because some changes you don't point at or they spook, like herons.

Around noon we anchored in the lee of the old breakwater and ate bread and salt fish and the last of the pickled radish Mrs. Nakamura had pressed on us, refusing payment, as she always did, saying we had paid already, saying it in the tone that ended discussion. The breakwater stones were warm. Gulls argued over something behind the far rocks. The customs board stood white and blank across the harbor, its notices taken down, its lists of foreign-named families unpinned and burned the week after the charter fell. Someone had scrubbed it so hard the wood had gone pale. It looked younger than it was. Most things that survive look younger than they are.

"Tomas," I said, "do you remember the tin box?"

He chewed. Swallowed. "Mother's biscuit tin. Blue, with the ships on it."

"You hated it. You said she trusted a biscuit tin with our whole lives."

"I was twelve and stupid." He turned the fish skeleton over in his fingers, considering it, then flicked it to the gulls. "She was right, though. Wasn't she. Everything that mattered fit in it."

I hadn't opened the tin box in months. It lived now on the shelf above my bunk, where the company's inventory list used to hang, and I found I had developed a superstition about it: that it should stay shut until I had a reason. Reasons came rarely. That was the nature of reasons worth having.

But today, sitting in the sun with my brother beside me and my own papers folded in my pocket — *freeholder, Odile Ishikawa, no conditions* — today seemed like a reason.

* * *

The cabin smelled of tar and dried kelp and, faintly, of the cedar the box itself was lined with, though the box was tin; some smells live in things they've touched. I set it on the table between us and lifted the lid.

No pearls. That was the strangeness of it, the thing that still caught in my chest sometimes when I looked. For sixteen years — my whole life, and Mother's before me — the tin box had been a measure of wealth, and wealth had been measured in what the sea gave up reluctantly and the company counted jealously. Now it held paper. Two pieces of paper, and nothing else.

The first was my father's letter, softened at the folds now, read so many times. I didn't take it out. I knew it the way Tomas knew his fishing grounds — by feel, in the dark, without looking. *Tell Odile the water is honest.* Somewhere in the capital there was a newspaper office where a typesetter had set those words in metal and printed them for forty thousand readers, and somewhere in the wreckage of the Meridian Company there were men who had read them and known exactly which manifest, which night, which drowned man. Justice had not come as a trial. It had come as a crowd, and ash, and signatures surrendered to save skins. But it had come carrying my father's sentence in its pocket, and that was more than I had ever dared hope for.

The second paper was newer. One page, written in my own hand, in ink I had bought with my own money from a shopkeeper who charged me fairly and did not lower his eyes.

It was the letter I had finally sent.

For eleven months after the charter fell, I had written to Elias Vane and not sent it. Drafts piled in the drawer like shed skins — formal ones, angry ones, one so tender I burned it in the stove rather than risk anyone ever reading it, including him, including especially him. We had parted without promises, on the dock, in the rain of that first free spring, and I had told myself the parting was clean, and then spent a year discovering that clean is not the same as finished.

What finally went into the post was none of the drafts. It was four sentences. It said: *The orchard ground is cleared. The plum trees are in. If you are ever on this coast, the water is honest here too.* And I had walked it to the post office myself, and bought the stamp, and stood outside for a quarter hour watching the harbor, and then gone in and posted it, because my mother raised me to finish what I carry.

That had been five weeks ago. No answer had come.

"Tomas." I tapped the second paper without picking it up. "Do you think he got it?"

Tomas looked at the letter, then at me, with the particular patience of a younger brother who has been dragged through hell and therefore reserves the right to be wise. "The capital post takes three weeks when the roads are dry. It's been five."

"So he got it."

"So he got it." He shrugged, elaborately casual, which from Tomas was a shout. "And either he's writing back, or he's standing in front of a mirror practicing what to say, or he's already on a train. Men like him don't ignore letters. They rehearse."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true." He reached over and closed the lid of the tin box gently, the way you close a book you've agreed to reread someday. "You know what I keep thinking? In there. When it was bad." He didn't say *the cell*, or *the transfer order*, or *the nights*. He never did. "I kept thinking about this box. About how everything we owned fit inside it, and how they still wanted it. Wanted the lease, wanted the boat, wanted the names on the customs board. People who need that much from people who have that little." He shook his head. "I'm done being little for anybody's ledger."

"You were never little."

"I know that *now*." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, and I understood that this — the smile arriving all the way — was the thing I had been diving for all year, deeper than any oyster bed.

Outside, the tide had turned. I could hear it in the slap of water against the hull, changing its mind. Tomas gathered his lines and went out to check the anchor, and I stayed a moment alone with the box, my hand flat on its cool lid.

Peace earned, not granted. That was the difference, I thought, between this year and every year before it. Before, whatever quiet we had was on loan — granted by the company's indifference, revocable at a signature. This quiet I had paid for. Father had paid. Mother had paid, years of it, in silence. Tomas had paid in ways he might tell me about someday, or might not, both his to choose. Even Corvus, in his gray pragmatic way, had paid — dismissed, disgraced, walking out of the smoking ruin of the company house with his single valise, pausing only to tell me that the villainy had been a thousand small signatures, mine included, once. I had hated him for saying it. I had also, later, alone, admitted it was true, and forgiven myself the signature of a frightened fifteen-year-old, which is the hardest forgiveness there is.

The water was honest. The ledgers were not. And I had spent my life learning which to believe, and now, at last, I believed with my whole weight.

I picked up the shell bucket and went out into the light.

* * *

The afternoon dives were poor and I didn't mind. Poor days teach patience, and patience was the one pearl I had in surplus. By the time the light went long and coppery across the water, we had a fair catch, a full fish basket, and a sky building no weather, just color.

We ran for home with the sail eased, Tomas at the tiller, and the reclaimed land came up out of the haze the way it always did now — first the low green line of the orchard, then the white scar of the cleared ground, then the house with its new roof beams, and beyond it the harbor, and beyond the harbor the whole ordinary evening world: smoke from cookfires, bells, a dog barking at a cart.

The orchard. I had planted it in March, thirty plum trees on the ground the company had held for nineteen years and called an asset. Everyone said fruit trees were a fool's crop for a woman who'd made her name running — trees can't run, they can't be lifted, they tie you to a place worse than any indenture. That was exactly why I planted them. I had spent my whole life carrying only what I could lift. I wanted, at last, to carry something by staying.

Tomas brought us alongside the mooring with the neatness of a boy who'd learned boats before he learned fear, and I stepped onto the Ishikawa ground — *my* ground, the deed in my pocket warm from my body — and stood a moment in the smell of turned earth and salt and blossom-to-be.

The trees stood in their rows like patient children. At the end of the third row, a figure was waiting by the gate, hatless, travel-dusted, holding a leather satchel the way a man holds a thing he has rehearsed a long speech about and forgotten every word of it.

Tomas saw him first. He said nothing. He just put his hand briefly on my shoulder — the whole message delivered in that touch, *go on, I'll see to the boat* — and turned to the lines.

Elias Vane stood at my gate in the last of the light, and the letter in the tin box, the one I had finally sent, seemed suddenly very small payment for five weeks of silence.

"The water's honest here," he said. "So I'm told."

I set down the shell bucket. Whatever I answered, I would answer it standing on my own ground, carrying nothing I could not lift — and, for the first time in my life, wanting to carry everything.
