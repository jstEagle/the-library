## 1. The Weight of Lead

My father says a compositor's hands should be like a nun's: clean, patient, and entirely without opinions. He has said this for twenty years, usually while his own hands are black to the wrist with ink, setting opinions by the column-foot for men who would deny having any.

I have inherited the hands and, despite my best efforts, the opinions.

It was the last week of 1899, and the shop smelled the way it always did in December — lamp oil, molten lead, and the ghost of oranges my father bought every Christmas and never ate. Outside, the city was doing its impression of calm, which is to say the harbor bells rang on schedule and the fishwives shouted their usual blasphemies, but underneath it all there was a hum, low as the press before it catches. You could feel it in your teeth if you bit down on anything metal. I spent my days biting down on metal.

"Beatrix." My father's voice from the back room, where he sorted the day's accounts with the grimness of a man doing arithmetic on a sinking ship. "The delivery from the foundry."

"We've no order in."

"It came anyway."

That alone should have told me something. The Meridian Type Foundry had not sent us so much as an apology for the cracked fourteen-point we'd complained about since August. Deliveries arrived when ordered, or they did not arrive at all, and one did not arrive *unannounced* — that word belonged to tax collectors and to my aunt Constanza, who visited precisely when the household could least afford her.

The crate sat on the loading step in the blue hour before lamplight mattered. It was heavier than lead had any right to be, and it was not alone. On top of it, folded with a care that made my stomach drop before I understood why, lay a soldier's coat.

Not new. Not regulation-neat. A uniform coat of the colonial garrison, sun-faded at the shoulders, missing two buttons from the left breast, and smelling faintly of smoke — not pipe smoke, not lamp smoke. The other kind. The kind you do not smell in a port city unless someone has been burning something out past the hills, which officially no one had been doing all autumn, per the *Gazette*, which we printed.

"Father," I called. "There's a coat."

Silence, of the specific quality that means a man has stopped breathing in order to listen better. Then his footsteps, quicker than a man of fifty-three should move across uneven floorboards.

He lifted the coat as though lifting a sleeping animal that might yet bite. He turned it over once. Twice. And then his thumb found the seam along the inner lining, below the left shoulder, and stopped.

I have watched my father set type for twenty years. I know what his hands look like when they are certain. They went certain now.

"Get the scissors," he said quietly. "And then, Beatrix, get yourself somewhere you cannot see this."

I am my father's daughter, which means I got the scissors and stayed exactly where I was.

* * *

The letter was stitched into the lining with surgical thread, in stitches so small and even they must have been sewn by lamplight, by someone who understood that haste kills. My father cut them open with the same awl he used for cleaning the type channels, and what fell into his palm was a sealed envelope, wax unbroken, addressed in a hand I knew better than my own.

I knew it because it was my mother's.

I should explain about my mother, since the city has spent eleven years politely pretending she never existed and our family has spent eleven years pretending to agree. Adaeze Oyelaran died — officially — of fever in 1888, the same year the governor's men closed three newspapers in a single week, the same year my father began printing under four different imprints and teaching me to cut type by touch so I could work after dark with the shutters barred. She was a quartermaster's clerk before she was ever a wife, and she had a uniform coat of her own, and she taught me my letters using galleys of banned pamphlets as primers. *A is for agitation*, she used to say, laughing, and my father would pretend not to hear.

She had been dead eleven years. Dead women do not receive letters. Dead women especially do not receive letters sewn into soldiers' coats by hands trained in field surgery, delivered to print shops by foundries that owe us nothing.

"Father." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Who brought this?"

"A boy. Sixteen perhaps. Said the driver told him to leave it and go." My father had not opened the seal. He was holding the envelope the way other men hold sleeping infants, terrified of waking something. "He didn't wait for payment."

"No one delivers lead without payment."

"No," he agreed. "No one does."

We stood there in the gathering dark, two invisible people in an invisible shop, and I felt the ground shift under a certainty I'd built my whole life on. Because here is what I believed, what I had *built* myself upon: that I was safe the way furniture is safe, unnoticed, unremarked, beneath anyone's interest. That my father's careful life — the four imprints, the barred shutters, the type cut by touch — had purchased us obscurity, and obscurity was a kind of armor. That no one knew what the Oyelaran print shop really was.

But someone knew. Someone knew enough to send a sealed thing to a dead woman, through a living courier, wrapped in a dead soldier's coat. Someone knew that the Oyelarans keep things. Keep *secrets*. Which meant that all these years we had not been invisible at all. We had been *watched* — patiently, the way you watch a fuse you are not yet ready to light.

"You should burn it," I said.

"I should." He did not move.

"The governor's men burned the Okonkwo warehouse in November for less than this. Father. Whatever she was keeping—"

"She kept *evidence*, Beatrix." He said it fast, the way you spit out something hot. Then slower, as though the words cost him by weight: "Names. Dates. What the Second Regiment did at Ilara, and who ordered it, and why. She hid it where no one would think to look because everyone believes a dead woman keeps nothing." His thumb moved over the wax seal, over the crest pressed into it — the governor's own crest, which told me the letter's contents were either loyal correspondence or its opposite, and given the coat, the smoke, and the season, I did not need to be a compositor to read which way the sentence ran. "If we burn it, we burn the only copy of the truth that those people at Ilara will ever get. If we don't—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. We both heard the arithmetic: one family against a governor, in a week when the governor had begun answering questions with kerosene.

Outside, the harbor bell struck six, and somewhere beyond it — past the warehouses, up in the terraced streets where the garrison lived — a whistle answered, off-pattern, wrong, the sound of patrols moving on a schedule the *Gazette* hadn't reported.

"Set the twelve-point," my father said at last, handing me the letter. "You'll know where to keep it."

Which was how I learned that he had known about my hiding places all along, but that is another confession for another chapter of my sins.

I carried the letter to the case where my mother's old type still lived, untouched these eleven years because neither of us could bear to melt it down. I slid the envelope flat behind the upper compartments, against the wood, where a woman's fingers had worn grooves into the grain. The lead around it was cold and heavy, and I thought: *this is what she left me. Weight.*

I was still standing there, hand pressed to the case like a girl at a shrine, when the knock came at the front door — three raps, military-precise, nothing like a customer.

Through the shutter slats I saw him: a young officer in garrison blues, too tall for his uniform in the way of boys still growing into their fathers' ranks, standing very straight in the lamplight with his cap under his arm.

"I'm looking for the printer Oyelaran," he said, loud enough for whoever might be listening on the street. And then, softer, almost under his breath, in a voice pitched for one person only: "*Please.* I know what was in the delivery. And I think my father sent the men who are coming for it."
