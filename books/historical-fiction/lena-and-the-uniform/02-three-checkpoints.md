## 2. Three Checkpoints

*Beatrix*

My father always said the river was the city's honestest citizen. It took everything — dye, ash, secrets, the occasional body — and told no one. I had never appreciated this until the night a man in a borrowed uniform offered to walk me through three checkpoints to reach it.

"You're staring at my buttons," Elias said.

"I'm staring at what your buttons mean." The coat fit him badly in the shoulders, which was its own kind of testimony; it had been cut for his father, and fathers are always broader than the sons they intend to be. "If they stop us, that coat is a confession you can't take back."

"They won't stop us." He said it the way he said most things — plainly, with that terrible calm of his, as though certainty were simply a thing one carried like a parcel. "I grew up walking through those gates beside him. The soldiers don't check the lieutenant-colonel's son. They check *for* him, hoping he'll notice them."

"And if he notices us instead?"

He didn't answer that, which was answer enough.

The letter lay against my ribs, sewn into the lining of my mother's uniform coat where the delivery-man's crude stitches had been, mine neater. Mama had worn this coat delivering pamphlets before I was born, back when she was a rumor with legs instead of a photograph on the mantel. It smelled of camphor and lead dust, and when I moved, the letter moved against me like a second heartbeat, arrhythmic and accusing.

"Your father knows?" Elias asked.

"He thinks I'm delivering proofs to the harbormaster."

"That's a lie."

"That's a mercy," I said. "Lies get kinder the older you get. You'll learn."

Something crossed his face — quick, swallowed. He knew about lies already. He'd lied to his father to borrow the coat, and to himself for nineteen years about what the uniform meant, and now here we were at the mouth of Copper Lane with the gas lamps burning low and the curfew drum sounding its two warning beats, and only one of us had ever been searched by soldiers before.

It wasn't me. That should have frightened him more than it did.

* * *

*Elias*

The first checkpoint was a plank table across Quayside Street and a lantern hung on a nail, and Sergeant Mbeki, who had taught me to fish off the north pier when I was nine and had reported my father's temper to no one, ever, out of some loyalty I had mistaken for love.

"Halt," said Mbeki, without halting us. He squinted into the lantern light. "God in heaven. Little Elias."

"Sergeant."

"In that coat." His eyes went from the brass buttons to Beatrix, hooded beside me, then back. "You'll catch your death. Or cause it."

"I'm escorting a printer's daughter to her father's colleague. Family business."

"At midnight. During the searches." Mbeki scratched under his chin where the beard came in patchy. Behind him, two younger soldiers watched us with the flat attention of men being paid to be suspicious. "There's word going round. A girl carrying papers. And an officer's son gone soft."

"Gossip travels faster than couriers these days," I said.

"Gossip is the only thing that travels honestly." But he waved us through, and his hand on my shoulder as I passed lasted half a breath too long, and in that half-breath he said, very quietly, so the young ones couldn't hear: "Your father was asking after you at the garrison. He didn't look like a man asking after his son. He looked like a man asking after a debt."

I thanked him. I have thought since about how strange gratitude sounds when it is also a farewell.

Beatrix didn't speak until the lantern was behind us. Then: "He lied for you."

"He looked the other way. It's not the same thing."

"It's the same thing wearing better boots." She glanced up at me, and even hooded, even afraid, there was something in her face that refused to be small. "Why did he do it?"

Because I fished with him. Because he buried a daughter. Because twenty years of watching my father burn things buys a man a private arithmetic of guilt. I said none of this.

"Because he's tired," I said, "and tired men choose whichever side lets them sleep."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh. "You say that like you know."

"I'm learning."

* * *

*Beatrix*

Here is what nobody tells you about fear: it makes the world vivid. Every lamppost burned like revelation. Every shadow had opinions. Walking beside Elias through streets that had gone quiet as held breath, I noticed absurd things — a cat sitting in a doorway like a magistrate, a shutter left open, the way his breath made small ghosts in the cold and how I kept almost counting them.

At the mouth of Rope Walk the second checkpoint rose out of the dark, and my heart performed a maneuver I did not authorize.

This gate was different. This gate had Captain Osei, who was not tired and did not fish, and whose lantern swept our way like a searchlight finding a hull.

"Papers," Osei said.

Elias produced his father's travel warrant. My hands stayed folded inside the coat, over the letter, over the stitches, over everything.

Osei read the warrant twice. "The lieutenant-colonel's warrant. Carried by—" he looked up, and I watched him assemble Elias out of the boy he remembered and the man standing there, "—the son. In the father's coat."

"The tailor's delayed," Elias said. Wry, easy. Where did he keep all that ease? I wanted to search him for it.

"The tailor's delayed," Osei repeated, tasting it. "And the girl?"

"My fiancée. We're to be married at her uncle's across the river. The banns won't wait for politics."

Fiancée. The word went through me like type through a press — I felt myself inked with it, pressed, changed. I lowered my hood and smiled the smile Mama must have worn, the one that says *I am exactly what you assume*, and Osei looked at me for three seconds that lasted a fiscal quarter.

"There's a reward," he said at last, to Elias, not me. "For a girl matching her description. And for whoever's helping her."

"Descriptions are generous this time of year," Elias said. "Half the city's girls would match."

"Half the city's girls aren't wearing their mothers' coats."

The world stopped. I am certain of it — the river stopped, the drums stopped, the cat back on Quayside stopped mid-stretch. Osei let the silence sit just long enough to prove he could, then stamped the warrant and stepped aside.

"Merry Christmas," he said, in the tone men use for *I own you now*. "Tell your father the tailor and I should talk."

We walked. We did not run. Running is a confession with legs. Only when we'd turned twice did Elias exhale, and it shook, and hearing it shake undid something in me — because if *he* could shake, then the ground itself was shaking, and I had been standing on him this whole time thinking him stone.

"He knew about the coat," I whispered.

"He suspects. There's a difference, but it's thinning." He stopped under an awning, out of the lamp's reach, and turned to face me, and up close his eyes were doing something complicated. "Beatrix. If he reports it—"

"He won't. Not yet. Men like him hold knowledge the way misers hold coin — spending it costs them more than keeping it." I heard my own voice and marveled at it: steady, strategic, my father's daughter down to the marrow. Inside, I was a girl counting heartbeats that weren't hers. "But we've lost time. The third checkpoint—"

"—is the bridge. And the bridge has my father's adjutant, and by now my father knows the coat is gone." He said it flatly, the calm cracking at last along some seam I couldn't see. "Beatrix, there's a version of tonight where I get you across, and there's a version where I don't, and I need you to know that in neither version do I hand you to them."

"You don't know what you're offering."

"I know exactly." He said it too fast, too sure, the way people say things they've decided rather than things they believe. "Do you trust me?"

What a question, between two liars. I trusted his hands when he'd stitched nothing but had stood guard while I stitched. I trusted the way he'd said *fiancée* like a door opening rather than a wall. I did not trust the uniform, or the blood in it, or the midnight deadline I could feel approaching like weather.

"Ask me again tomorrow," I said, "if there is one."

He laughed — startled, real — and for a moment we stood in the dark grinning at each other like conspirators, which was precisely what we were, and precisely what we were lying to each other about.

Then the bells began. Not midnight — the alarm. From the direction of the barracks, rolling over the rooftops toward the bridge, and Elias went still in a way I recognized from my father's face the day the presses were named in a decree.

"That's for us," he said. "Or for me. Come on — the bridge, before they close it."

And somewhere behind us, boots on cobbles, many, keeping pace.
