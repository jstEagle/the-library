## 3. What the Coat Remembers

My father taught me to shoot before he taught me to read. I have often wondered since whether that was an oversight or a philosophy.

He also taught me the uniform — not how to wear it, but how to *read* it. A soldier's coat is a ledger, he said. Every button a debt, every frayed cuff a confession. He could stand at parade distance and tell you where a man had been, what he'd eaten, how long since his last bath, and whether he'd been promoted honestly or bought his way up with someone else's blood. It was the only poetry my father ever spoke, and he spoke it about cloth.

So of course it was buttons that undid us.

* * *

The second checkpoint sat in the shadow of the customs house, a lantern-lit gap in a barricade of crates and indifference. I had walked through it a hundred times as a boy, trailing behind my father like a small dog hoping to be mistaken for a large one. I knew the sergeant on duty by his snoring. I knew which plank of the barricade you stepped on if you wanted the whole structure to groan theatrically, a joke my father never laughed at and therefore a joke I treasured.

Beatrix stood beside me wearing her mother's coat under a plain shawl, and I was wearing my father's uniform, which was either the most loyal thing or the least loyal thing I had ever done, depending entirely on who was asked and when.

The sergeant did not snore tonight. That should have been my first warning. The world always gives you warnings; it simply doesn't wait for you to take them.

"Oyelaran," he said, reading Beatrix's papers by lantern light, and something in the way he pronounced it — carefully, twice, like a man tasting a word he'd been told to remember — made my stomach drop through the floorboards of the earth and keep going.

"Her father's shop does our printing," I said. "I'm escorting her home."

"Are you." Not a question. The sergeant folded the papers along their existing creases, which meant they'd been unfolded many times recently, by many hands. "And your coat, Lieutenant. Turn around a moment."

There are perhaps three moments in a life when you understand that everything has already happened, that you are merely arriving late to your own catastrophe. This was mine. Because I knew what he would see before he saw it: the buttonholes at the collar, empty. Two brass buttons missing from the left breast, pried off in a print shop by a girl with ink-stained fingers because the letter needed somewhere to hide and thread holds secrets better than paper does.

The coat remembered. My father had said so all my life, and now the coat was remembering out loud, in front of witnesses.

"Sergeant," I began, in the voice I imagined my father used for executions.

But it wasn't the sergeant who answered. It was the voice itself, coming from the doorway of the guardhouse, attached to the body I would have known anywhere — even faceless, even dead, even across a burning field. My father stood with his greatcoat unbuttoned and his hat in his hand, which he only ever removed indoors or before violence, and he looked at the two empty buttonholes on my chest the way other men look at wounds.

"Walk with me, Elias."

It was not an order. That was the terrible part. It was almost gentle.

* * *

We walked along the quay while Beatrix waited — I prayed invisibly — among the crates, and my father said nothing for the length of four moored fishing boats, five, six. My father's silences were architectural. He built them to be lived in.

"You took the buttons off yourself?" he asked finally.

"No."

"But you know where they are."

"Yes."

"And what they were holding."

I could have lied. I want that recorded somewhere, in some future court of whatever history survives us: there was a moment, on the quay, in the salt wind, when lying was still technically available to me, and I stood at its door and considered it the way one considers a coat that doesn't fit.

"Yes," I said again.

He nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed the weather. Then he took off his glove, finger by finger, and held his bare hand up between us, palm open, an old gesture from my childhood that had once meant *give me the stolen sweets* and later meant *give me your oath* and now apparently meant both.

"The governor's office received a report this morning," he said. "A girl and a young officer, moving toward the river checkpoints with documents. Desertion charges have been drafted against the officer. His name begins with V and ends with Oyelaran's daughter." He flexed the bare hand. "You will bring me the girl and the letter by midnight, and none of this will have happened. You'll be posted north. Clean air. New coat."

"And if I don't."

"Then at dawn I burn the print shop myself. It's already assigned to me, did you know that? Your mother's God has a sense of humor. They gave the order to *me*, Elias. The Oyelaran shop. So you see, midnight isn't a deadline. It's a mercy. It's the only version of this story where everyone I love is still breathing at breakfast."

Everyone I love. He said it plainly, without drama, the way he said everything, and that plainness was worse than any threat, because it was true. He loved me. He loved me the way the uniform loves the body inside it — completely, structurally, without asking whether the body consents.

"I've read the letter, Father."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not surprise. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing a sound he's been pretending not to hear for years.

"Then you know what we burned," I went on, quieter, because the sentry at the end of the quay had turned. "Ashanka. You know why the reports say bandits. You know what the governor was protecting, and you know it wasn't the colony."

"I know what orders are," he said. "They're the only thing standing between eight hundred men and becoming animals. You obey first and understand later, or you never obey at all, and then—" He stopped. His jaw worked like a man chewing glass. "Then you become me, standing on a dock at night negotiating treason with a boy I taught to clean a rifle before he could spell treason."

"Then don't give the order."

"I don't choose the orders. That's the entire point of them." He put his glove back on, and the transformation was total — hand sheathed, heart sheathed, my father replaced by Major Villalobos of Her Majesty's colonial garrison. "Midnight, Elias. Bring me the girl, the letter, and your apology, in whichever order hurts least."

He walked back toward the checkpoint. I watched him go and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he already knew I wouldn't come. Fathers know. That's their punishment for teaching us to read coats.

* * *

Beatrix was exactly where I'd left her, pressed into the dark between a crate marked *sugar* and a crate that smelled decisively of nothing that had ever grown near sugar. When I reached her she didn't ask what he'd said. She read it off me the way her fingers read type — by touch, by the shape of my silence.

"Midnight," I said.

"Then we have four hours."

"We have less than that, and I can't walk you through the third gate. If I'm seen with you again, they won't wait for dawn. But—" and here my plan, such as it was, revealed itself to be the desperate architecture of a man who had learned loyalty from a coat, "—no one questions anyone entering the barracks after evening muster. No one looks at faces. They look at shoulders and stride. And the barracks connect to the ferry road through the laundry yard."

"You want to hide me in your regiment's barracks."

"I want to hide you in the one place my father will never search, because searching it would mean admitting his son is a deserter, and my father would rather burn the city than admit that."

She looked at me for a long moment. Lantern light came through the crate slats in thin gold lines, striping her face like the bars of a cage we hadn't entered yet but were clearly headed for. I braced for her to refuse. She had every reason. Every reason I had given her was a reason against me.

"All right," she said. "But Elias — when we get there, I need to tell you something about the letter. Something I should have told you at the first checkpoint."

"What?"

"Not here." She pulled the shawl tighter and stepped out of the shadows, chin up, walking like a woman with papers. "Somewhere with a lock on the door."

The barracks bell rang nine. Three hours to midnight, one secret I didn't yet know I was carrying, and the whole garrison between us and the river.

I followed her into the dark, wearing my father's coat, feeling it remember.
