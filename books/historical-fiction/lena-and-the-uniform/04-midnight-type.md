## 4. Midnight Type

The third checkpoint was a lantern and a bored boy with a rifle, which is to say it was the most dangerous thing in the city.

The first two gates had been manned by men who wanted something — a bribe, a name, a reason. A man who wants something can be reasoned with, delayed, deceived. But Corporal Adeyemi was nineteen and cold in his boots and had been told that anyone crossing the river bridge after eleven o'clock was to be stopped on principle, and principles are the one currency I have never learned to counterfeit.

"You're out past curfew," he said, lifting the lantern so the light crawled up my coat.

My mother's coat. I had spent the walk rehearsing this moment the way my father rehearses a page before locking it into the chase: every word set, every space justified. *I am the daughter of Lieutenant Oyelaran's housekeeper, sent for medicine.* It was a good lie. It would not survive the lantern, because the lantern had already found what it was looking for.

"Name," the corporal said.

And behind him, coming up the bridge through the fog like a figure walking out of a bad dream I'd been having all week, Elias.

He wore his father's uniform. Of course he did; that had been the whole architecture of our deception, three checkpoints walked on the bones of his father's rank. But tonight there was something wrong with the way he wore it — or rather, something right, something finally honest in the wrongness, as if the coat had been a costume all along and only now had he decided to stop performing.

"Corporal," Elias said. "That woman is with me."

Adeyemi straightened so fast his rifle stock cracked against the parapet. "Lieutenant— sir— I wasn't told—"

"You weren't told because you didn't need to be told." Elias took the lantern from him with an ease I will never be able to imitate, held it up, and looked at me.

That was the terrible part. Not the corporal's fear, not the rifle, not the fog rolling off the river thick enough to chew. The terrible part was that Elias looked at me the way he looks at type — reading me, weighing me, deciding whether I would hold under pressure — and I could not tell him the truth, and he knew I could not tell him the truth, and we stood there in the light of a borrowed lantern conducting an entire conversation in the grammar of silence.

*Go,* his eyes said.

*I'm sorry,* mine said, because I was. Because at the second checkpoint his father had given him until midnight to surrender the girl and the letter, and Elias had chosen instead to hide me in the barracks among the sleeping soldiers with his father's own letter folded against my ribs, and whatever happened after midnight, one of us was going to pay for it in a currency neither of us had.

"The bridge is closed," Adeyemi said, finding his courage somewhere near his boot heels. "Orders, sir. No crossings after eleven."

"I wrote those orders," Elias said quietly. "I am amending them."

He stepped aside. He did not look at me again. That was the gift of it — he made himself a wall between me and the corporal's curiosity, and let me walk past, and if his jaw was tight when I passed close enough to smell the lamp oil on his collar, well. Fog does strange things to faces.

I crossed the bridge.

Halfway across I heard it: the small metallic sound of insignia being torn free. I did not turn around. If I turned around I would go back to him, and if I went back to him the letter would die in my coat along with everything my mother had sewn into its lining, and ten thousand people who had never heard her name would never hear it now.

So I kept walking, and behind me a lieutenant's son became a deserter with four quick rips of thread, and followed me into the dark like a man following a bell he couldn't yet hear.

* * *

My father was setting the headline when we came through the back door of the press, and I watched him read the situation off our faces the way he reads galley proofs — quickly, ruthlessly, without sentiment.

"They're coming for you," Elias said. "My father. Before the bells."

"Yes," my father said, and reached for the composing stick. "Then we haven't time for goodbyes."

You should understand something about my father: he is not a brave man. He has told me this himself, over the case, over the years — *I am a coward who happens to own a press, Beatrix, and God in His mercy arranged it so that cowardice and printing look identical from the outside.* But watching him that night, his hands moving over the type with the calm of a man buttering bread, I understood that he had been lying to us both. Cowards do not stand in the doorway of their life's work and begin setting type they know will be the death of them.

"Page two is yours," he said to me. "Your mother's words. Set them exactly as written. She'll know if you cheat."

So we worked. The shop smelled of ink and coal smoke and the ghost of my mother, who had died believing this day would come and had stitched proof of it into a uniform coat so heavy with someone else's crimes that I had carried it across a city like a woman carrying weather. Elias fed the press. My father locked the forme. And I sat at the stone with my mother's handwriting in front of me and cut and composed until my fingers bled at the knuckles, and did not stop, because stopping meant thinking about midnight, and midnight was coming up the street with lanterns.

The first blow against the door came at five minutes to twelve.

The second came with voices — his father's voice, level and terrible, calling for Elias by name, offering him one last version of himself to put back on.

Elias looked at me across the press. "Tell me to stay," he said. "Order me. Then it isn't my choice and I don't have to live inside it."

"Elias," I said, "stay."

He stayed.

They took my father at the door itself, gently, almost courteously, the way you remove a drunk uncle from a christening. He did not resist. He blew me a kiss over the shoulder of the sergeant holding him, mouthed something I will decode for the rest of my life — I believe it was *the kerning*, but it may have been *I love you*, and knowing my father it was somehow both — and then the door closed and the street filled with boots, and the press stood silent in the middle of the room like a black piano no one would ever play again.

The church bells began.

Midnight. 1899 dying in the belfries, 1900 arriving wet and screaming like everything else born in a port city in winter. And Elias threw the lever.

The first sheet came off damp and crooked and perfect. My mother's name in the masthead where the governor's had always been. Her testimony in columns. The village, the regiment, the order signed in his father's hand — all of it running free now, multiplied beyond recall, ten thousand copies of a dead woman's voice saying the one thing empires cannot abide, which is *I saw, and here is what I saw.*

We printed through the bells. We printed while the city below the window lit itself with fireworks it believed were celebration and would learn within hours were something closer to prophecy. Elias worked the press shirtless in his undershirt with his torn insignia on the floor beside the ink slab, and every time our eyes met across the flying paper, I thought: *this is what it costs, this is what it buys.*

By dawn the bundles were gone — into fish carts, into coffin deliveries, into the hands of dockworkers who asked no questions because questions are heavier than lead. By dawn the story was loose in the streets like water finding every crack in the city, and there is no checkpoint on earth built to stop water.

My father was in the military prison, alive. I made Elias swear it before I let him sleep — alive, unharmed, a printer arrested for sedition, nothing more, because martyrs sell papers but fathers raise daughters. He swore it with his forehead pressed to mine, smelling of ink and gun oil, a deserter in an undershirt who had traded a uniform for a girl and had not once, even at the door, looked back.

Outside, the new century was making noise. Somewhere down the hill, a newsboy was shouting my mother's name in a voice still cracking with adolescence, selling her truth for a penny a copy.

"He'll be tried," Elias said quietly, watching the grey come up over the harbor. "My father. They won't hang him — too many names in that letter, too many officers implicated. But he'll be tried, and he'll call me a traitor in front of the whole colony, and he'll be right."

"Then you'll sit in the gallery," I said, "in plain clothes, and let him be right."

He laughed — a raw, surprised sound, the first I'd heard from him since the bridge. And then he went quiet, because we both heard it at the same moment: boots on the stairs. Many boots, unhurried, ascending toward a print shop that officially no longer had anything left to seize.

Elias moved toward the door. I moved in front of him.

"No," I said. "This time, they've come for me."
