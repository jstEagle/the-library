## 1. The Man Who Wound the Clock

The clock at Gannet Rock wanted winding at six o'clock precisely, and Coral Amberdale had never once in eleven years made it wait past the half-minute.

He rose in the dark for this. That was the whole architecture of his days: dark to dark, with a bright seam of lamplight running down the middle of it like a vein of ore through granite. He lit the stub of candle on the kitchen shelf—wax saved, wax counted—and climbed the ninety-one steps to the clock room, where the great mechanism sat fat and patient as a sleeping gull, its weights hung down the shaft like the innards of some gutted whale. He fitted the crank. He turned it. Thirty-two turns, no more, no less; thirty-three strained something he couldn't name and thirty-one left the mechanism sulking by midnight.

"Good morning," he said to the clock.

The clock did not answer. Nothing at Gannet Rock answered, which was the entire point of the place, though Coral would have denied the point if pressed, and no one had ever pressed, because no one had ever come.

* * *

Let me tell you about the rock, since the rock and I have an arrangement of long standing, and one of us ought to introduce us properly.

Gannet Rock sits a mile and a half off the Cornish coast, a fist of granite rising forty feet out of water that is either the color of pewter or the color of murder, depending on the weather's mood. The Board of Trade saw fit to plant a tower on it in 1861, after the *Providence Jane* went to pieces on the outer shoal with thirty-one souls aboard, and the newspapers spent a fortnight saying *something must be done* before moving on to scandals more entertaining than drowned fishermen. What was done was this: a lighthouse, eighty-seven feet of dressed stone, and a keeper posted to it, first two men, then one, then—in 1882, when the second berth was quietly abolished—one man alone.

That man was me.

I had been here four years when they took away Mr. Teague, my colleague, a Welshman who snored like a landslide and talked like one too, and I will confess—I have confessed it to the gulls, which is nearly as good as confession proper—that when the boat carried him off I stood on the landing stage and felt something that a respectable man ought not feel at a colleague's departure. Relief. No, stronger than relief. Something with the shape of joy but the temperature of still water.

Eleven years alone on a rock. Most men would call it a sentence. I called it—well. There is the difficulty. I have never found the word, and I am a man who keeps accounts, so the absence of a word is itself a kind of debt outstanding.

"Contentment," I say sometimes, testing it against the inside of my teeth. It doesn't fit. It slides around like a coin too small for the slot.

* * *

The routine, then, since routine is the only biography I possess.

Six o'clock: wind the clock. Half past: trim the wicks. This is a delicate business and I perform it with a surgeon's vanity. A lamp wick trimmed unevenly burns unevenly, and an uneven light tells a lie to every mariner who reads it—a stutter where there should be steadiness, a doubt where there should be law. I clip the charred lace off the tops with small scissors kept for nothing else, square the wick true, pinch the blackened crumbs from between my fingers and flick them into the sea below the gallery rail, where they die without ceremony.

Seven o'clock, breakfast: porridge, tea, bread baked on Sundays and going stale by Thursdays. Seven-thirty, the logbook, in a hand I keep deliberately dull because a logbook is a legal document and not literature, whatever temptations the fog offers. *Wind moderate SW. Sea slight. Lamp trimmed. All well.*

All well. Two words I have written perhaps four thousand times. Sometimes I write them and wonder what they are guarding against—the possibility, presumably, that all is *not* well, that somewhere in the machinery of the world a gear has slipped, and the logbook is the last line of defense against chaos. Four thousand times. And in four thousand entries, the most remarkable thing ever recorded by this hand was the day a crate of lamp oil arrived with two bottles of port wine substituted by some clerk's error, and I drank neither bottle, being unsure whether error entitled me to them, and eventually poured both into the sea, which struck even me, at the time, as a gesture of almost theatrical virtue.

Eight o'clock: polish the lens. Ah, the lens. The Fresnel. I could describe her for you—first-order, six panels, each a cathedral arch of ground glass assembled in Paris by men who never saw the rock she'd sit on—but description would be disloyalty, somehow. Better you understand that she is the reason for everything else. The clock winds the weights, the weights turn the lens, the lens gathers my little flame and throws it fourteen miles out to sea, and somewhere beyond the horizon there are ships' masters who know Gannet Rock's character—one flash every ten seconds, white, unvarying—as intimately as they know their wives' tempers. They have never seen me. They never will. I am not a man to them; I am a characteristic.

I have made peace with being a characteristic.

* * *

In the afternoons, weather permitting, I walk the gallery. Forty feet up, the wind comes straight off the Atlantic with nothing to interrupt it between here and Newfoundland, and I lean into it the way other men lean into conversation. The gulls hang beside me on the wind, riding the updraft off the tower's shoulder, and I talk to them. Not madly—I want that understood. Not the muttering of the deranged. Conversationally. As one gentleman remarks upon the weather to another.

"You're late," I told a herring gull once, in October, when the flock came back to winter on the rock's lee side. "The lot of you. Three days late. I'd half a mind to give your ledge to the cormorants."

The gull looked at me with its flat yellow eye, entirely unmoved by the threat, and I respected that. A gull has never pretended interest in anything I've said. There is an honesty in it you don't find ashore, I'm told. Ashore, people ask how you do and then look over your shoulder at someone better.

Maren used to say that. *They ask how you do and then look over your shoulder.* She said a great many sharp things like that, Maren, my wife, dead now these nine years, buried on the mainland in a churchyard I visit by proxy, meaning not at all. Nine years, and I find I have stopped speaking of her even to the gulls, which is a strange arithmetic: the living get my voice daily and mean nothing by it; the dead got it once a year, briefly, and meant everything. Or I meant everything. Hard to apportion meaning between a man and a bird.

There is a photograph of her in the sitting room. Small cabinet card, taken in Penzance the year we married, her hands folded, her mouth set in that expression Victorian photographers demanded of women—which was, I think, supposed to read as dignity but reads, across twenty years, as patience worn thin. I dust the frame on Saturdays. I have not turned the frame to face the room in nine years. It faces the wall. This is not grief, exactly. Grief implies an event with a beginning. This is more like a room I keep shut in a house I otherwise maintain perfectly.

* * *

You will want to know why a man chooses a rock. Everyone wants to know why a man chooses a rock, and everyone who asks imagines the answer is a woman, or a debt, or a disgrace—some wound, some flight. The supply boatmen used to speculate among themselves until they learned I could hear them. Old Curnow, who piloted the tender for seven years, once worked up the courage to ask me outright, standing on the landing stage with his cap in his fists like a man at a funeral.

"What drove you out here, Mr. Amberdale, if you don't mind my asking?"

"Nothing drove me, Curnow. I applied."

"Yes, but—" and here he gestured at everything: the sea, the rock, the tower, the general proposition of my existence. "But why?"

And I said, "Because history happens elsewhere," and he looked at me the way men look at a compass needle spinning, and he never asked again, and I counted the exchange a success.

But it was the truth, and truth deserves better than to be deployed merely as a conversational door slammed in faces. History happens elsewhere—to other men, in other rooms, in London and Lisbon and wherever parliaments sit and wars are declared and scandals are printed in three columns. History is a thing done to men who own things, or want things, or have married into things. I owned a clock, a lens, ninety-one stairs, and a set of small scissors. I wanted nothing that could be gotten ashore. Therefore history passed me by, as weather passes a man in a cellar, and I heard only the muffled roar of it overhead and slept soundly beneath.

This is what I believed in the autumn of 1893, and I record the belief here faithfully, because a logbook should be honest even when its keeper has been a fool.

* * *

October came early that year, and hard. By the middle of the month the barometer had begun a slow, thoughtful decline, the way a man descends stairs when he intends to stay down. I checked the storm shutters. I counted the oil—eleven drums full, two part-used, enough for the season if the season behaved, and seasons rarely behave. I baked bread. I re-caulked the kitchen window where the putty had gone to crumb. I performed, in short, all the small ceremonies of a man preparing to be sealed inside his own life for five months.

On the seventeenth there was a squall—not a proper storm, just a squall, the sea clearing its throat—and afterward I walked the rock at low tide, as was my habit, checking the mooring rings and the condition of the landing stage, kicking loose kelp off the lower ledges. The light was that flat silver light that follows weather, and the air smelled of iodine and cold, and the gulls were rioting over something on the far side of the rock, screaming their opinions at one another.

Gulls riot over fish, ordinarily. Over crab leavings, offal from passing boats, the usual refuse. But they were gathered on the northern ledges, below the high-water mark, in a scrum of wings and noise that had the particular fury of birds disputing ownership, and I thought: *fish, big fish, some poor cod dumped by the squall,* and I went to see, partly from curiosity and partly because a man alone will walk toward any interruption whatsoever.

It was not a fish.

Wedged in the crevice where two ledges meet—a crack no wider than my forearm, above the reach of ordinary tides—was an oilskin packet, tarred canvas folded double and bound with waxed cord, the kind of wrapping used for documents that matter more than the paper they hold. It was wet through, naturally; everything here is wet through; the sea permits nothing dry. But it was intact. The cord was knotted with a care that spoke of deliberate hands, and on the outer face, written in some ink that had survived the salt, were words in a fine, upright hand:

**FOR THE KEEPER OF THE LIGHT.**

I stood over it for some time. The gulls screamed. The tide, far out, ticked and shifted among the stones like a large animal resettling.

Now, I receive letters. Let that be understood. The Board writes quarterly, invoices mostly, and once a year a printed circular about the dangers of alcoholism among isolated servants of the Crown, which I read with genuine amusement. The supply boat brings newspapers a week old, which I read cover to cover including the advertisements, because a week-old newspaper is still a window, however fogged the glass. But those letters come by boat, in Curnow's canvas mail bag, addressed in clerks' hands to *C. Amberdale, Esq., Keeper.*

Nothing in nineteen years of service had ever washed ashore addressed to me. The sea delivers driftwood and dead gulls and, once, a ship's figurehead—a blind wooden woman I kept in the store room for a year before giving her to the tide again, having concluded she was unlucky. The sea does not deliver correspondence. The sea does not know my address.

Yet there it lay, wedged tight in a crevice above the wrack line, wrapped against water with a deliberateness that no accident produces. Things thrown from ships land where physics puts them. Things *placed* in oilskin, knotted with waxed cord, addressed in a fine upright hand, arrive where somebody intended.

I picked it up. It was heavier than paper should be. Inside the tarred folds, something stiff—board, perhaps, or several sheets—and a faint rattle, like a seal or a signet pressing against the underside of the wrapping.

And here is the detail I would give much to un-know, the detail that made the cold come up through my boots and into the marrow of me, standing there in the flat silver light with the gulls going silent all at once, the way birds do, as if the rock itself had drawn breath:

Beneath the direction, beneath **FOR THE KEEPER OF THE LIGHT**, there was a small stamp in red wax, cracked by salt but legible, pressed by a signet ring into sealing wax of a particular deep crimson.

A crest. A crest I knew. A crest I had seen on the spine of a Bible in a house in Falmouth twenty-six years ago, on the invitations to a wedding I attended as a stranger among strangers, on the cufflinks of a loud young man who laughed too readily and called me *brother* before we'd shared a single meal.

A crest I had seen last on a coffin plate, and expected never to see again in this world.

The Verrin arms. My dead wife's family.

I did not open it on the rocks. I want that recorded. I carried it up the ninety-one steps with the careful slowness of a man carrying something explosive, set it on the kitchen table under the candle, sat down opposite it, and did not touch it for three hours while the light burned and the clock turned and the lens threw her ten-second flash out over water full of coming winter.

Then I blew out the candle, put the packet in the drawer of the table where I keep the scissors and the spare wicks, locked the drawer, and put the key in my waistcoat pocket, where it lay against my heart like a small cold fact.

Three hours, and I hadn't opened it, and already the packet had done its work. Already the cellar I had lived in for nineteen years had sprung a hairline crack, and through the crack, faint and unmistakable, came the sound of history—not roaring overhead anymore, not muffled and elsewhere, but descending. Coming down the stairs. Looking for me.

The gulls knew before I did, probably. They always do.

But that night the wind backed to the southeast, the barometer fell another tenth, and the sea began making the low, grinding noise it makes when it is thinking about storms—and I lay in my bunk listening to it, the key cold against my chest, and thought: *whatever this is, it has been at sea a long time to find me, and things that travel that far do not travel gently.*

I slept badly for the first time in years.
