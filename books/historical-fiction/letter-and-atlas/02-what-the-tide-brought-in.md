## 2. What the Tide Brought In

The squall came in from the southwest on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to me and everything to the gulls, who have opinions about weather the way bankers have opinions about interest rates. I had watched it build all morning—a bruise on the horizon that swelled into a proper temper—and by three o'clock Gannet Rock was taking green water over the lantern gallery, which happens perhaps four times a year and always makes me glad of my decision never to marry a lighthouse. It would be a jealous spouse.

I spent the afternoon doing what keepers do in weather: nothing, loudly. That is to say, I checked every fitting twice, walked the gallery rail with a rope around my waist because I am stubborn but not stupid, and ate a bowl of barley soup while the wind tried to negotiate with the chimney. The soup won. Soup usually does.

By dusk the worst had blown itself out to sea, sulking, and left behind that washed-clean quality of air you could almost drink. The barometer climbed like a man remembering an appointment. And the tide, having spent its fury, began giving things back.

* * *

I should explain about what the sea gives.

In eleven years at Gannet Rock, the ocean has delivered unto me: driftwood beyond counting; one entire ship's door, painted green, still swinging on its hinges as though inviting me to some very damp party; forty-one glass floats, which I strung along the store room rafters until they looked like the eggs of a very ambitious bird; two dead porpoises (returned to the sea promptly, with apologies); a crate of oranges, sealed and sound, which I ate with the guilty pleasure of a man who has done nothing wrong but feels it anyway; and once, memorably, a piano. An upright piano, salt-swollen, missing its middle C, wedged between the rocks below the low-water shelf where only the boldest tides go shopping. I could not play it before and cannot play it now, but for a season I struck keys when the mood took me, and the rock rang like a bell with something wrong inside it.

That is the catalogue. Timber, fruit, wreckage, death, and music. The sea is generous in the way of a rich uncle who has no idea who you are—it gives freely, indiscriminately, and never anything with your name on it.

Nothing personal. Not once in eleven years. The sea has opinions about everyone, but it does not correspond.

Which is why, on Wednesday morning, walking the tideline with my boots in the wrack line and my collar up against the leftover wind, I stopped as though I'd walked into glass.

* * *

It was wedged in the rocks below the old boat slip—the place where, in the previous century, someone had attempted a landing stage before concluding that the sea had other plans. The granite there folds into a deep crease, a slot that collects what the water wants to forget: kelp, bones, the occasional boot. I check it after storms the way a man checks his teeth with his tongue—habitually, without expectation.

Wedged into that slot, jammed fast above the wrack line—which was wrong already, because nothing rides that high except by intent—was a packet wrapped in oilskin.

I stood over it for a long moment, hands in my pockets, conducting the internal negotiation that any sensible man conducts with an object he doesn't understand. The sensible conclusion was: flotsam. Fishing gear, perhaps. A sailmaker's bundle gone overboard. The sea takes things off boats the way pickpockets work a crowd, and returns them at random.

But the oilskin was tied with tarred twine, and the twine was knotted in a way that spoke of care rather than accident, and the whole package was roughly the size of a book. A book-sized thing, deliberately wrapped, deliberately knotted, deliberately lodged in the one crevice on this rock that would hold it above the reach of ordinary tides.

Deliberate, deliberate, deliberate. Three words that do not belong in the same sentence as *the sea gave me*.

I got down on my knees in the wet and worked it free. The oilskin was good stuff, supple, recently dressed. The knot held against my fingers until I remembered I owned a knife.

Inside was a letter. A single letter, thick paper gone soft at the edges from whatever journey it had taken, wrapped in a second skin of waxed canvas. On the outer wrapping, in ink that hadn't faded because it hadn't been given time to, were two words:

*To the keeper.*

Not *Coral Amberdale*. Not *the keeper of Gannet Rock*. Just *the keeper*, in a hand that slanted forward like a runner leaning into wind.

And beneath the direction, pressed into a blob of red sealing wax that had survived its soaking better than most men would have, was a crest.

I knew that crest. That is not a figure of speech; I mean I knew it the way you know a face you've seen in a crowd and spent twenty years pretending you hadn't. A gull rising over three wavy bars, water beneath, sky above. Stamped into the wax small enough to fit under a thumbnail, but there was no mistaking it. There was no mistaking it because for six years of my life I had watched a woman press that same stamp into her correspondence, back when she wrote letters at all, back before she decided—like me—that the world was best experienced at a distance.

Maren's crest. The Verrin arms, if a family that proud of its own name could be said to have arms. A gull over water. She used to say it suited them: birds that live on the margin, belonging to neither land nor sea, screaming at anyone who comes close.

I sat down on the rocks, which was undignified, and turned the packet over four times, which was unhelpful, and then put it inside my coat, against my chest, where it lay like something with a heartbeat.

* * *

Here is the trouble with a solitary life: it trains you beautifully for everything except surprises.

A man who lives alone becomes a specialist in the predictable. My days are scored like sheet music—wind the clockwork at seven, trim and light at sunset, log entry, rounds, sleep, repeat—and I have come to take a craftsman's pride in the absence of variation. Other men have lives that happen to them. Mine is a thing I maintain, like the lens, like the clock. History, I have long since concluded, occurs elsewhere. It happens in London drawing rooms and colonial outposts and the offices of newspapers, to men whose names get set in type. It does not happen on a rock nine miles from anywhere, to a man whose closest living relation is a cat named Barge who visits when the fishing is poor and leaves without so much as a by-your-leave.

This arrangement suits me. I want to state that plainly, even to myself, even alone on a rock with nobody to perform contentment for. It suits me the way a well-made coat suits a man: closely, warmly, and without pinching anywhere that matters.

Except.

Except that a man who says *it suits me* eleven years running begins to sound less like a statement and more like a plea entered into evidence. Except that there are evenings, particularly after the equinox gales begin, when the lamp is lit and turning and I stand in the watch room listening to the clockwork tick through its great patient circles, and I think: this is the whole of it, then. This is everything there will be. And the thought doesn't hurt exactly. It's more like a stone in the boot—you can walk on it fine, but you know it's there, and knowing changes the shape of every step.

And except that the sea has just handed me a letter with my dead wife's family seal on it, and my first feeling—not my second or my third, but my first, honest, immediate feeling—was not curiosity.

It was dread.

* * *

Barge arrived at noon, as cats do, materializing on the store room step as though he had merely been out of the room. He is a grey brute with the manners of a customs official and the appetite of a famine, and our friendship consists of my feeding him and his tolerating my existence. I mention him because when I carried the packet inside and set it on the kitchen table, Barge looked at it, looked at me, and left the room. Cats are said to dislike sealed letters. I believe they simply object to anything that might displace them at the center of attention.

I made tea. I did not drink it. I sat across from the packet with my hands flat on the table, the posture of a man interviewing a suspect.

Let me describe the temptation accurately, because accuracy matters in a logbook and this chapter of my life deserves honest logging. Part of me wanted to open it immediately—wanted it the way a dry man wants water. Eleven years of driftwood and oranges and one drowned piano, and finally, *finally*, the sea has sent something addressed. Something personal. Something that said *to the keeper* in a hand that leaned forward like it was in a hurry, and bore the mark of the Verrins, whom I had buried along with every question I ever had about my wife's first twenty-five years.

And another part of me wanted to build a small fire in the stove, feed the packet into it, and return to my scheduled life, where history happened elsewhere and the sea kept its opinions to timber and fish.

Both parts were loud. Neither part moved. I am, when it comes to decisions, less a man than a committee.

* * *

You will want to know about Maren. Everyone eventually wants to know about Maren, though no one alive now is in a position to ask, which is how she would have wanted it. My wife managed her past the way a careful housekeeper manages linen: folded away, out of sight, brought out only for occasions.

We met in Penzance, in the winter of '71, at a chandlery where she was buying rope and I was buying rope, and we discovered we were the only two people in Cornwall purchasing rope in January without an obvious reason to need it. I liked her before she spoke. I liked her more when she spoke, because her voice had gravel in it, as though she'd swallowed the coast. We were married within the year. She was twenty-six and I was thirty, and I thought—I genuinely believed—that we were two of a kind. Two people who had looked at the world and agreed to step back from it together.

She died in '86. Fever, quick and ugly, the kind that takes a strong woman in four days because strength is no defense against bad water. I buried her on the mainland, in a churchyard she chose herself, and I remember standing at the graveside thinking: she planned this too. Even her leaving was arranged in advance. Even her death had a system.

What I did not ask—what I chose, actively, deliberately, with the full weight of a grown man's will not to ask—was why a woman like Maren Verrin, daughter of money and older pride, raised in a house with servants and silver, had been buying rope in Penzance with no obvious reason to need it. Why she never wrote home. Why, once a year, a letter would arrive from somewhere in the west country, and she would read it standing by the window, and burn it, and never say a word, and I would drink my tea and let the silence lie there between us like a dog we both pretended wasn't in the room.

I told myself it was respect. Privacy, dignity, the right of every wife to her own locked drawer. There was truth in that. But underneath the truth there was also this: I didn't ask because I was afraid the answer would require something of me. Attention, action, departure—I didn't know what. So I let the dog lie.

Now here is the arithmetic of grief, which I offer without comment: she has been gone seven years, and in seven years I have not once dreamed of her face. I dream of her hands instead, pressing a seal into red wax, and waking, I feel I have missed something important that occurred entirely inside a gesture.

And now the sea, my oldest colleague, my mute and generous uncle, has delivered her crest to my doorstep.

* * *

The tide went out and came back. The gulls resumed their grievances. I performed my duties—there is always a lens to clean, a log to enter, a wick to trim, the small liturgies of a tower that does not care whether its keeper's hands are steady—and through all of it, the packet sat on my kitchen table where I could see it from the doorway, the way you position a dog you don't trust.

Toward evening I picked it up again and examined it properly, by lamplight, with the patience I bring to machinery. The oilskin wrapping was seaman's work, tight and double-folded. The tarred twine was the kind sold in every harbor chandler from Falmouth to Fowey—no clue there. The wax seal had cracked along one edge in the water but held its impress perfectly: the gull, the bars, and beneath them, smaller, the letter V entwined with a flourish, the Verrin mark entire.

*To the keeper.*

I turned it over. Nothing on the reverse. No date, no port of origin, no ship's name. Whoever wrote it had stripped the packet of everything except the one fact they wished known: that it concerned the keeper of Gannet Rock, and that it came wearing Maren's family colors.

Which raised a question I did not enjoy. Because there were, by my count, exactly three categories of people who knew both where I lived and whose widow I was. The first category was the Trinity House officials, who knew everything about everyone and communicated exclusively in forms. This was not a form. The second category was the fishermen of the mainland villages, who knew me as the Gannet Rock man and Maren, vaguely, as *she that was*, and who could not have produced a Verrin crest under torture—they barely produce their own names under torture. Which left the third category, the one I had spent eleven years pretending was empty:

Her family. Or someone speaking for them. Someone who knew the crest, knew the address, and knew—this was the part that made the back of my neck prickle, sitting alone in my warm kitchen with the lamp turning overhead—that a letter marked *to the keeper* would find the keeper, because the keeper was always here. The keeper was always here. That was the one thing about me that could be relied upon by friend, stranger, or enemy alike.

The sea gives and the sea takes, but the sea does not aim. Somebody had aimed.

* * *

I did not open it that night. I want that recorded plainly, because in later days—when the tower filled with voices and accusations and the smell of fear—I looked back on these hours as the last quiet ones, and I wanted the log to show that I had behaved, initially, like a rational man.

I put the packet in the drawer of the chart table, beneath the spare nibs and the tin of pencil leads, and I closed the drawer, and I went up and lit the lamp, and the great lens gathered my little flame and threw it nine miles in every direction, faithful as ever, asking nothing.

From the gallery I looked south toward the mainland, a darkness with a few sparks in it. Somewhere over there was a churchyard with Maren in it, and a county full of Verrins, and whatever business had wrapped itself in oilskin and taken to the water. Somewhere out there, tonight, the sea was working the problem the way it works everything—patiently, indifferently, moving pieces it has no stake in.

"History happens elsewhere," I said aloud, to the wind, to the gulls roosting in the gallery rail, to nobody. It sounded different out loud than it did in my head. Out loud, it sounded like a man whistling past a graveyard.

I stayed up until midnight, which I never do, drinking tea I didn't taste and telling myself I would open the letter tomorrow, or the day after, when my hands were steadier, when I'd had time to think, when—

When. The favorite word of every coward on the coast.

The packet lay in the chart table drawer, and through the floorboards I could hear the clockwork ticking, and outside the beam swung its slow white arm over the black water, over and over, like a man searching a dark room for something he knows is there.
