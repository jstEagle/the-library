## 3. The Name Verrin

I put the letter under the clock.

Not in the drawer—under it, where the case met the stone floor, weighted by nothing but its own guilt and three pounds of brass gearing. It seemed a fitting place. The clock was the one object in my keeping that demanded honesty. Wind it or it stops. No letter had ever made such demands of me, and I saw no reason to begin a correspondence with one now.

Three days, I told myself. I would give it three days, and if the sea wanted me to read it badly enough, the sea could find some way to tell me so.

The sea, being the sea, declined to negotiate.

* * *

The first day I did everything twice. Trimmed both sets of wicks though one set could have gone another week. Scrubbed a lens that needed no scrubbing. Counted my oil drums as though they might have multiplied out of spite—they had not; I had forty-one on Tuesday and forty-one still, which is the arithmetic of a man alone. I spoke to the gulls, who answered as gulls do, which is to say they screamed at me until I gave them biscuit and then screamed at me for the quality of it.

"You're nosy," I told the largest of them, a gray-backed tyrant I've called Admiral since before Maren died. The Admiral tilted his head, unconvinced that the accusation applied to him rather than me.

Because that's what it was, wasn't it? Nosiness. A man who has spent twenty-two years congratulating himself on knowing nothing of anybody else's affairs does not get to open sealed letters bearing crests he half-recognizes. That is the whole covenant of solitude: nobody knows your business, and you don't know theirs, and everyone goes home satisfied. Except there was no home here but mine, and the letter had come ashore at my feet like a debt collector who'd swum.

Maren's family crest. I'd seen it once, at our wedding, stamped in gold on the flyleaf of her prayer book—a gull over crossed anchors, if you can believe the vanity of it. A gull. Her family put a gull on their arms, and I have spent two decades shouting at them off my railings without once making the connection until now. There's a joke in that somewhere. I haven't found it yet. I suspect it's on me.

* * *

The second day I tried to remember her talking about them, and discovered how thoroughly I'd helped her not to.

It's an art, a marriage between two people who agree not to look too closely. She never asked about the years before the light—the merchant service, the wreck off Flamborough Head that I still hear sometimes when the wind comes from the northeast, though I've told no living soul that. And I never asked about the Verrins beyond what she volunteered, which was nearly nothing, delivered in that bright flat voice she used for things she wanted closed.

"My brother manages money," she said once, early on, kneading bread in the kitchen of the old station house. "Badly, but with great confidence."

"Brothers often do," I said, and that was the whole of it. Twenty years of marriage founded on the mutual understanding that some doors stay shut because both parties are leaning against them from opposite sides.

She laughed, though. I remember that. She laughed at her own joke and flour went up in a cloud around her like weather, and I thought: this woman has decided to be happy here, and whatever she left behind, she left it on purpose. I admired the decision. I still admire it. What I did not do—what I am only now, holding a wax-sealed envelope over a tide pool, beginning to understand—is ask what a woman has to be leaving behind to need that much resolve.

There were other fragments. A Christmas she went quiet over the plum pudding and said, "Aldous will have got into debt again by Boxing Day," the way you'd remark on fog. A night, near the end—she was ill already, we both knew it, though we kept the pretense polished like brass—when she gripped my wrist hard enough to leave marks and said, "Coral. If anyone ever comes asking after me. Anyone." And then she'd looked at me for a long moment, waiting, and I said, "Then they'll find a lighthouse keeper who knows nothing," and she'd relaxed so completely, so suddenly, that I took it for gratitude instead of what I now suspect it was: relief that I hadn't asked the obvious question.

Which was: why would anyone come asking?

I was very good at not asking questions. I want that on the record. It may even have been my finest skill, and skills like that don't announce themselves. They just sit there, smug, while the tide brings you things.

* * *

On the third day the wind came round to the southeast and the barometer climbed, and the weather turned so mild and reasonable that I ran out of excuses entirely. A storm would have been a kindness. Give a man a gale and he can spend the whole day simply surviving it; give him a calm afternoon and all his unfinished business sits down across the table from him and orders tea.

I brought the letter up from under the clock at noon. Sat with it in my chair by the stove. Turned it over. The oilskin had dried stiff and pale, like skin. My name was not on it—it said only THE KEEPER, in a hand I didn't know, slanted and hurried—and yet the crest sat above it plain as a confession. Gull over crossed anchors.

"Well," I said to the room, because the room was the closest thing I had to company and it has never once interrupted me. "Either I open this, or I spend the winter inventing what's inside, and I already know I'll invent something worse."

That was optimistic of me, as it turned out.

I broke the seal. The wax crumbled along a line someone had already scored with a knife—no, not scored. Cut cleanly, long ago, and resealed. Someone had read this letter before me. I noted that the way you note a loose tread on a stair: a small cold fact filed away for later.

The hand was the same as the address, slanted and hurried, and it began without ceremony:

Keeper—

If you are reading this, then the sea has done what no honest post could, and I must trust it chose well. You will not know my name and I will not write it. What you must know is this: the name VERRIN is marked. There are men—call them merchants of the unlit coast—who have waited eleven years to settle an account, and they have lately learned where the last of that family has gone to ground. They will come to your rock. Not for you. For shelter, at first, which is how such men begin everything.

Eleven years ago a member of that household informed upon their trade to the Revenue, and four of their number went to prison, and one of those four did not survive his sentence. The ring believes the informer was the brother. The ring is wrong. But belief is a more dangerous cargo than truth—it rides lower in the water and sinks faster.

When they come, keeper, you will have a choice to make, and I will not insult you by pretending it is an easy one. I will say only this: the wife you buried knew more than she told you, and less than they think. Guard the light. Guard the rest as your judgment allows.

Burn this.

No signature. Just the crest drawn again at the bottom, small, in ink—a gull, wings folded, looking almost apologetic.

* * *

I read it four times. Then I did burn it—or started to. Got it to the stove, held it over the coals, watched the corner catch and curl. And then I pulled it back out, slapped the flame dead against the iron, and stood there feeling like a man who has just argued himself out of his own good sense.

A keeper burns strange letters. That's the protocol. That's sanity. A keeper who keeps a strange letter is a keeper building a small fire in his own hold.

But the thing is—I know this coast. Eleven years ago I was here. Eleven years ago there *was* trouble on the water: the Revenue cutter out of Penhale running night exercises, a lugger burned to the waterline off the shoals, talk in the supply boat of a ring broken and men transported. I remember it the way you remember a distant thunderstorm—you note it, you go back to your wicks. History happened elsewhere, to other men, in towns with newspapers. It always had. That was the arrangement.

Except now the arrangement had washed up on my landing stage with Maren's crest on it, informing me that history had been happening all along at a distance of precisely one marriage.

*The wife you buried knew more than she told you.*

I sat down on the stair to the lamp room—not properly sat, more collapsed onto a step like a sack of dropped rigging—and let that sentence work through me. Because there are two ways to take it. One: my wife carried secrets, as most people do, and died without unloading them, as many people do, and it is sad but it is common, and I can fold it away. Two: my wife lived twenty years beside me conducting a second life in the spaces I politely didn't look at, and the polite not-looking—which I had always counted as love, as respect, as the finest courtesy a private man can offer—was actually just the dark she needed to operate in.

I wanted to be angry. Anger would have been comfortable, anger has furniture you can sit on. But every time I reached for it I found curiosity underneath, worse than anger, itchier. What did she know? When did she know it? Who wrote this letter, that they knew her, that they cut it open and read it and sealed it again and set it on the sea like a bottle—and why send it to *me*, the husband who knew nothing, unless the writer thought the husband who knew nothing was exactly the man who needed to finally know something?

And beneath all of it, quieter, the question I kept stepping around like a hole in the deck:

*They will come to your rock.*

Not might. Will. Written as fact, in a hurried slanted hand, by someone who signed nothing and trusted the postman Neptune. Which meant that somewhere out past the horizon there were people—a brother named Aldous, perhaps children, whoever survived of the gull-and-anchors Verrins—who were coming here. To my tower. To the one place on earth I had built specifically so that nobody would ever come.

I went up to the lamp room, mostly to be near the lens, which is the only object I know that makes sense of everything it touches—takes a small flame and throws it six leagues, asks nothing back. I stood with my hand on the brass and looked southeast, toward the channel, toward wherever Verrins kept themselves.

The horizon did its usual trick. Empty, all the way to the curve of the world. It has always been good at pretending.

But I noticed, as I turned to go down, that I had already begun doing the arithmetic a keeper does when he expects company: the stores counted against mouths, the berths counted against bodies. Four oil drums spare. One bed, one cot, floor space enough for grief and no more.

Whatever was coming had already started rearranging my house. That's the thing about other people's storms. They arrive ahead of the weather.

The Admiral landed on the gallery rail and fixed me with his yellow eye, and for once neither of us screamed at anything.

"They're coming," I told him.

He said nothing, which from a gull is practically discretion—and far below us, at the edge of sight where the water goes from slate to silver, a sail was standing in toward the rock.
