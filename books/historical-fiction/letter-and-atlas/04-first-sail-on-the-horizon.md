## 4. First Sail on the Horizon

The letter stayed in the oil store for three days, and I told myself this was prudence rather than cowardice. There is a difference between the two, though at Gannet Rock the line blurs like the horizon in fog. Prudence says: a sealed packet bearing a dead woman's family crest should not be opened by lamplight when your hands smell of whale oil and your nerves are already frayed from trimming wicks. Cowardice says the same thing but in a smaller voice.

On the third day I read it, and I have regretted it every morning since with the particular vigor of a man who has learned something he cannot unlearn.

The Verrins were coming.

Not "might come." Not "should circumstances permit." The letter — written in a cramped, hurried hand that was not Maren's and yet made my stomach turn as if it were — said they *were* coming, that they would come by sea because there was no other way to reach a rock, and that I was to shelter them because the sea does not honor grudges even when the men riding it do.

I had not heard the name Verrin spoken aloud in nineteen years. Maren had said it exactly once, early in our marriage, when she explained — briefly, in the tone one uses for weather — that her family considered her dead and she intended to return the courtesy. Then she had gone back to shelling peas, and I had understood that this was a door I was not to knock on. So I hadn't. Twenty years of marriage and I never once knocked. I tell myself now that this was respect. At three in the morning, with the clockwork ticking its slow heart below me, I am less certain. It may have been laziness dressed as respect, which is the most common garment in any man's wardrobe.

I burned nothing. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept for keepers who talk to gulls. I folded the letter along its original creases and put it in the drawer beneath the logbook, under the spare pen nibs and the brass key to a door that hasn't locked since '81. And then I went about my day, because that is what a keeper does. The lamp does not care whose brother-in-law is sailing toward you across cold water. The lamp cares about oil, and wicks, and whether you wound the clockwork at noon or forgot again.

* * *

Two days later — the fourth of October, wind southwest, a light haze off the water that made everything look painted rather than real — I saw the sail.

I was on the gallery rail with the morning tea I allow myself despite knowing it's stewed beyond decency, doing what I always do at that hour: counting nothing, watching everything, letting the horizon perform its usual empty theater. And then the theater cast an actor.

A small boat. Sloop-rigged, patched sails, sitting low in the water the way boats sit low when they are carrying more than cargo — carrying fear, which weighs more than lead and is harder to stow. She came out of the haze from the east-northeast, which is not the direction any honest vessel approaches Gannet Rock from in October. Honest vessels come from the mainland supply route, southwest, hugging the deep channel. This one came the way you'd come if you were avoiding being followed, or if whoever was following you knew the honest routes better than you did.

I did not light the lamp.

Understand — it was morning, broad daylight, no lamp needed. But my hand went to the gallery door all the same, some old instinct insisting that light was mine to give or withhold, and that today I would withhold it. Let them find the rock themselves. Let them work for it. A petty thought, I grant you. I have found that resentment rarely announces itself with trumpets; it arrives disguised as procedure. I stood there telling myself I was simply observing maritime protocol while what I was actually doing was making three — no, I could see four heads eventually, small and bobbing — make them sweat for their landing.

Maren's people. Coming to my rock.

I had buried her past along with her, you see. That was the arrangement, though nobody had ever said so out loud. When they lowered her into the ground on the mainland — I had come ashore for it, one of perhaps six times in twenty years — I stood at the grave's edge and made a private contract with the earth itself: *you take her history, I'll take her memory.* The earth kept its side. I kept mine. For nineteen years the arrangement held beautifully, and I flattered myself that I was a man whom history passed by, the way weather passes over a rock and leaves it unchanged.

The sail grew larger. The lie grew thinner.

* * *

By midday I could make out details, and details are where hope goes to be disassembled.

There were three of them, plus whatever was lashed under the tarp amidships. A man at the tiller, older, narrow through the shoulders, wearing a good coat ruined by salt spray — the kind of coat that tells you its owner once had money and recently lost the argument about keeping it. He steered the way anxious men steer, correcting constantly, second-guessing the water. That would be Aldous Verrin, then. Maren had described him to me exactly once, in the same conversation as the door-not-to-be-knocked: *my brother Aldous, who borrows and does not repay, but loves well enough in his fashion.* I remember thinking at the time that this was a strange way to describe a brother, love qualified like a legal clause. I was younger then and did not know that most love arrives with qualifications attached, including mine.

Beside him at the mast sat a young woman, and I will confess something here that I would deny to any living soul: my first thought upon seeing her was *Maren*, and my second was *no*. Not Maren. Maren had been soft-edged and quick to laugh and moved through rooms like she was leaving them anyway, pre-departed, a woman always half-packed. This one sat like ballast. Still, upright, dark-haired with the hood down despite the spray, watching the rock approach with an expression I recognized from mirrors — the look of someone taking inventory of a place and finding it insufficient before ever setting foot in it.

His daughter, then. Sable, the letter had said. The letter had also said, in its cramped hand: *the girl is sharper than is convenient.* I had wondered at the phrasing. Watching her watch me, I wondered no longer.

And a third figure, small, wrapped in canvas near the stern, who spent a good portion of the approach hanging over the gunwale in the universal posture of the seasick. A child. They had brought a child. To a smuggler-haunted rock, in October, they had brought a child, and some cold clerk in the back office of my mind stamped the file and noted: *this changes the arithmetic.*

Because that is what the letter's arithmetic had been, though I had refused to do the sum. Shelter the Verrins, it said. Winter closes the channel by December. The supply boat comes monthly until then. And somewhere underneath all those plain words, the sentence I had not let myself read: *and how long does a man feed three extra mouths on stores calculated for one?*

I went below and counted. Rice, flour, salt pork, the tinned milk I hoard against winter, forty-one jars of the preserves I trade for with the supply boat's cook. Enough for one man, generously. Enough for four, if generosity took a holiday and discipline signed on instead. I stood in the store room with my ledger open and did not write anything down, because writing it down would make it true, and there are still, even now, things I prefer to leave in pencil.

* * *

They came around the north face at half past two, where the water lies calmest at mid-tide, which meant either that Aldous Verrin had studied the charts or that someone aboard had. I doubted it was Aldous. The man handled his tiller like a debt he was trying to renegotiate.

I went down to the landing stage. Not to help — I told myself I went to secure the ring bolts, which technically needed securing, in the way that many things technically need doing. The truth, since we are being honest in the privacy of my own head: I wanted to see them clearly before they saw me. Nineteen years of not knocking on doors builds up a terrible appetite for looking through windows.

The boat bumped the stage. A rope came across. I caught it without thinking, hands remembering their work while the rest of me stood apart, arms crossed, watching.

"Amberdale." Aldous Verrin said my name the way you set down something heavy. He climbed onto the stage with more dignity than grace, and up close he was worse than the telescope had suggested — gray-faced, hollowed, with the restless eyes of a man who checks horizons professionally. He did not offer his hand. He did not say thank you. He looked at me and I looked at him and between us lay twenty years and one dead woman, and neither of us reached across.

"You got the letter," he said. Not a question. As though the sea were a reliable postal service and I its diligent clerk.

"The sea delivered it," I said. "Whether I received it is another matter."

Something flickered across his face — I couldn't name it then, though later I would learn its name. Guilt has many faces and I was about to become fluent in all of them. "We're grateful," he said, in the tone of a man reading gratitude off a card.

Behind him, the girl stepped onto the stage without waiting for help, which I noted, and steadied herself with one hand on the wet stone, which I also noted, and then she looked at me — really looked, head tilted slightly, the assessment frank as an auctioneer's — and I felt nineteen years of careful invisibility slide off me like wet oilskins.

"You're shorter than I imagined," she said.

"Sable." Her father said the name like a warning shot.

"Well, he is." She wasn't being cruel. That was the unsettling part. She was simply accurate, the way weather is accurate. "In the stories Mama told, you were—" She stopped herself. Whatever I was in the stories Maren had told her daughter, I apparently was not going to hear it on the landing stage. "Taller," she finished.

"Maren talked about me?" I heard my own voice and disliked it — too quick, too hungry, a dog hearing its name after years of silence.

Sable looked at me for a long moment, and in that moment I understood that she could see the hunger, and was filing it away, and would take it out and examine it later by lamplight the way I examined strange wreckage. "Everyone talks about everyone in our family," she said. "It's mostly why we need a rock."

The child came last. Aldous lifted him over the gunwale — a boy of seven or eight, greenish, trembling, with his mother's fine bones somewhere under the seasickness, though whose mother I couldn't have said and didn't ask. The boy looked at the tower rising above us, all hundred and twelve feet of granite and duty, and then at me, and asked the question children ask, which is always the wrong question and always the right one:

"Are you the keeper?"

"I'm the keeper."

"Is it haunted?"

"Not yet. Give it time."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then his stomach remembered the sea, and he turned aside with the terrible dignity of the young and ill, and Sable put a hand on his back without looking away from me, doing two things at once, which I would learn was simply how she lived.

Aldous was already turning toward the tarp amidships. "We'll need help with the chest—"

"There's a handcart," I said. "Inside. You'll carry your own chest."

For a heartbeat I think he thought I was joking. Then he saw that I was not, and something in his shoulders accepted it, the way men accept terms they've agreed to in principle long before the meeting.

* * *

I lit the lamp that night, of course. Duty is duty; the shipping lanes don't pause for family drama. But I lit it late — four minutes late, maybe five, and I stood in the lamp room with my hand on the clockwork mechanism and admitted to myself what I'd been refusing to admit on the landing stage:

I was angry.

Not at them, particularly. Anger is rarely so tidy. I was angry at the sea for delivering the letter, angry at Maren for having a past shaped exactly like a trapdoor, angry at Aldous Verrin for standing on my rock with his borrowed gratitude and his ruined good coat, angry at the girl for seeing through me in under a minute, angry most of all at myself — at the version of me who had stood at a graveside nineteen years ago and made a deal with dirt. *You take her history, I'll take her memory.* Well. The history had washed back up, as history does. It had come ashore in an oilskin packet and then it had come ashore in a leaking sloop, and it was currently eating my stores and asking about hauntings, and there was nowhere left to bury it that the tide wouldn't reach.

Below me I could hear them moving through my tower — footsteps in the stairwell, unfamiliar rhythms in rooms that had known only one rhythm for two decades. My home sounded different with other hearts in it, the way a clock sounds different when you realize it's been ticking all along and you'd simply stopped hearing it.

The lamp swung its beam out across the darkening water. Somewhere behind me, small feet on iron stairs. Somewhere ahead, winter closing the channel like a book.

I wound the clockwork. I did not go down.

Not yet.
