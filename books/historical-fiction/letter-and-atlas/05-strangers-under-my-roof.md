## 5. Strangers Under My Roof

I had rehearsed the greeting perhaps forty times in the three days since reading the letter, and every version of it had been dignified, measured, and brief. "You're expected," mostly. Perhaps a nod toward the kettle. In none of my rehearsals did I stand on the landing stage holding a boathook like a halberd while three drowned rats scrambled out of a dinghy that had no business crossing four miles of open water.

The sea had been kind to them, which was the sea's idea of a joke. Kind enough to get them here alive, rough enough to make sure they arrived humbled and dripping.

Aldous Verrin came up the iron ladder first, and I knew him at once, though I'd never laid eyes on him. He was Maren made masculine and worse — same high cheekbones, same grey eyes that took inventory of a room before acknowledging the people in it. Twenty years older than she'd been when she died, and carrying all twenty years in his shoulders. He gripped my hand without looking at it, the way a man shakes hands with a doorpost.

"Coral," he said. Not *Mr. Amberdale*. Not *thank you for having us*. Just my name, said the way you'd say *Tuesday*.

"Aldous." I matched him syllable for syllable, warmth for warmth, which is to say none for none.

Behind him came a girl of maybe nineteen who climbed the ladder as if she'd been born doing it, which told me she hadn't — Verrins were merchants and clerks, desk people, ink people. She paused at the top, wrung seawater from the hem of her coat with two efficient twists, and looked at me. Not past me, not through me, but *at* me, the way an assessor looks at a house he intends to value low.

"You're shorter than I imagined," she said.

"Sable," Aldous said sharply, and I gathered this was the daughter.

"Most people are," I told her. "In imagination."

The boy came last, hauled up by his father's collar like a kitten, and he was green as pond water and shaking. Pip, the letter had called him. Eight or nine. He got one foot on the rock, vomited into the channel with a thoroughness I could only admire, and burst into tears.

And there they were. Three Verrins on my rock, soaked silent, and me holding out a boathook to nobody.

* * *

I want it recorded — in whatever logbook the Almighty keeps of small men's days — that I did everything correctly. I carried the boy up ninety-one steps because he couldn't manage them. I lit the stove. I put the kettle on. I found dry blankets that smelled of lamp oil and camphor and gave them the good chairs, such as they were, and said not one word about the fact that these people had let my wife die thinking them dead to her, and had now arrived on my doorstep expecting soup.

Aldous sat by the stove with his hands spread toward the heat like a man being shown instruments of torture. He was drying, slowly, into a shape even more diminished than wet. I remembered Maren describing her brother once, early in our marriage — the only time she ever spoke of home — *"Aldous could talk the paint off a boat when he wanted something, and be struck dumb the moment anyone asked what it cost."* She'd laughed when she said it. It had not been a kind laugh. I'd thought nothing of it at the time. I thought a great deal of it now.

"The letter said three," I said. "You are three."

"We are three," Aldous agreed.

"That's all the conversation we require tonight, then."

Sable made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Her father shot her a look, and she examined her fingernails with great scholarly interest.

Pip, wrapped in a blanket on the settle, watched me with the round unblinking attention of the very young and the very seasick. Children always watch me that way. They haven't yet learned that lighthouse keepers are furniture.

"There's bread," I said, because there was bread, and because silence in a kitchen has a way of curdling. "Cheese that's seen better months. Tea."

"Thank you," Sable said. It was the first thing anyone had said to me that contained manners, and it startled me enough that I cut the cheese crooked.

We ate standing, mostly, in the manner of people too tired to sit and too proud to say so. The rain worked at the windows. Somewhere below us the tide turned and began its long argument with the rock.

It was Aldous who finally broke, and he broke sideways, which I would learn was his habit.

"This is decent of you," he said, staring into his teacup. "Given everything."

"Everything," I repeated. "That's a broad word."

"Coral—"

"I buried your sister nineteen years ago, Mr. Verrin. You sent no word then either. So we're even, in a way. Neither of us has ever given the other a reason to expect anything."

He flinched. Good, some petty cellar in me said. Let him flinch. But he recovered quickly — too quickly — and smoothed his face into something conciliatory, and I understood that I was looking at a man who had spent his whole life recovering quickly. That's a skill. It's also a confession.

"We can discuss the past when we're dry," he said.

"We can discuss the past whenever you like. We have all winter." I stood, took his empty cup from his hand, and set it in the basin harder than the crockery deserved. "The lamp needs lighting at half past six. There are bunks in the store room. The privy is outside and downwind, and I recommend you remember which way downwind goes."

I went up the stairs to light the lamp, and behind me the tower swallowed three strangers whole.

* * *

From the gallery, trimming the wick with hands that knew the work without consulting me, I could see their boat still tied below, banging itself stupid against the pilings. Four miles of open October channel between here and the mainland. No supply boat due for weeks. Whatever I felt about the arrangement, the arrangement was now weatherproof. That's the thing about rescuing people: the rescue part takes an hour, and the *having them* part takes as long as it takes.

Maren had loved this family once. I tried to reconstruct her from that fact the way you try to reconstruct a face from a description — *she had loved these people* — and found I could not make it fit the woman who'd kept our rooms so bare of her own history you'd have thought she'd been issued from the supply boat along with the paraffin. Nineteen years of marriage and I never knew she had a brother until the sea handed me his name in an oilskin packet.

Or so I'd believed. The packet was downstairs in a drawer, and the drawer was locked, and I intended to do some more believing before I did any more opening.

Below me, faintly, through the glass, I heard the boy cry himself out and go quiet. I heard Sable's voice, low, asking her father something, and Aldous's voice answering lower, and I heard the particular tone of a lie being administered to a child, which is gentler than most lies and twice as load-bearing.

I trimmed the wick and lit the lamp and let the great lens take over, throwing its white arm across the water mile after mile, and I thought: history happens elsewhere. It always has. And now, apparently, elsewhere has a rowboat.

* * *

**Sable**

Father slept like a man paid to do it — instantly, completely, dishonestly. I lay awake in the bunk above Pip and listened to the tower breathe.

It breathes. Nobody had told me that. The wind comes around the lantern room and the whole structure answers, a long groan and settle, like an old man turning over in bed. Below us the keeper's footsteps went up and up and stopped somewhere overhead, and I lay counting the sounds of the house: creak, drip, the tick of something clockwork in the walls, Pip's breath gone ragged-then-even at last.

They'd told me about him, back in Fennmouth, in the weeks before we ran. Uncle Coral, the coward. Aunt Maren's husband, the man who never came, who never wrote, who let her vanish into a rock and a routine and didn't chase her. Father said it with a curl to his mouth: *a man who married our Maren and then hid from her.* Mrs. Tallow at the chandlery said it with pity: *poor woman, married to that cold fish.* Everyone said it, and I'd said it too, in my head, building him out of other people's sentences: tall, joyless, a man who preferred lamps to people.

He wasn't tall.

That was the first thing the sentences got wrong, and it unsettled me more than it should have. He was middling, spare, grey at the temples, with forearms roped from hauling rope and a face that had been handsome before the weather finished its edits. He moved like a man who lived alone — economical, nothing wasted, doors opened with the shoulder because both hands were usually full. When he carried Pip up those stairs he didn't grunt or dramatize; he just adjusted the weight once, near the top, the way you adjust a cargo you've decided to see delivered.

And he was angry. Not hotly. Coldly, thoroughly, the way a hold fills with water — quietly, evenly, from the bottom up. Every courtesy he'd offered us tonight had been exact and every one had been a wall. *There's bread. The bunks are in the store room. Downwind.* He fed us like a quartermaster feeds prisoners he doesn't hate but doesn't trust either. And underneath it, in everything, that anger, and under the anger something else I couldn't name yet, something that looked almost like hurt wearing anger's coat.

Cowards don't look hurt. Cowards look relieved. That's how you know them — Father taught me that much, though his lessons were mostly about other men's cowardice.

I slid down from the bunk without waking Pip and went into the main room, drawn by habit. When a family falls apart you learn to read houses fast: where the money is, where the exits are, whether the man of the place drinks. Father calls it morbid. Father calls it many things while packing us onto boats at midnight.

The keeper's house was the tidiest poor place I'd ever stood in. Everything squared, labeled, accounted. Brass polished until it apologized. On the wall by the stove hung a slate with the week's meals chalked in a hand so precise it looked printed: *Mon — salt cod. Tue — salt cod.* I stood in front of that slate longer than I meant to. Nineteen years of Tuesdays. A man could go mad politely, eating salt cod alone under a light he lit for strangers.

His books were on a shelf above the table, spines cracked with use: charts, almanacs, a Bible, a book on optics, and — this caught me — a slim novel, cheap paper, a romance, the sort of thing sold at railway stalls. Its spine was broken in three places. Somebody had read it to pieces. I checked the flyleaf. Nothing written there. Of course nothing. A man who wrote his name in things might have to admit he existed.

Then the drawer.

It wasn't locked — I'd expected locked; I don't know why I expected competence — but it stuck, and I had it half open, enough to see the corner of an oilskin packet, folded flat, when the floorboard behind me spoke.

I turned. He filled the doorway with a lamp in one hand, and his face did something complicated and settled on *nothing*, which was somehow worse than fury.

"It sticks," I said. "Drawers."

"It does." He crossed the room, and I stepped aside because stepping aside was correct, and he shut the drawer with his palm, gently, the way you close a coffin lid on someone you liked. "Looking for something?"

"Reading material," I said. "Your novel's ruined. Spine's gone."

"Mm." He looked at me, and I looked back, and we performed the oldest duet in the world, two liars keeping time. He had his wife's eyes. Nobody had warned me of that either. Looking at him was like looking at a photograph of Father aged wrong.

"You should sleep," he said. "The light turns at dawn, and mornings here start whether you've finished with the night or not."

"In Fennmouth we were told you were—" I stopped myself. He waited. He was very good at waiting; he must have had decades of practice waiting for gulls to leave. "—that you kept to yourself," I finished.

"I do," he said. "You're the exception that proves it." And then, at the door of the store room, without turning around: "Miss Verrin. The drawer sticks because I want it to. Remember that, and we'll get along."

He went up the stairs, unhurried, and the tower breathed around him.

I climbed back into the bunk beside Pip and stared into the dark, recalculating everything. A coward who reads romances to death. A recluse who locks one drawer and warns you about it instead of pretending it isn't there. A man who hated us on sight and still carried the child up ninety-one steps.

Mrs. Tallow's sentences were going to be no use at all. I'd have to build this man from scratch, from evidence, the way you rebuild anything after a wreck.

Overhead, faint through the floors, I heard him stop moving. Then a sound I nearly missed: paper. One page, turned. Another.

He was sitting up there in the dark with that oilskin packet, reading it again — or reading something else — while his house full of strangers slept below him and trusted him for their lives.

So was I, come to think of it.

I resolved, lying there in the smell of camphor and lamp oil, to find out which of us was right about him before winter closed the channel. Because one of us had to be wrong, and I had a feeling the wrong one was going to pay for it.

Outside, the wind shifted, and the barometer hanging by the stove — I'd noted it on my way through, out of habit, out of Father's training — ticked softly downward, like a clock deciding something.
