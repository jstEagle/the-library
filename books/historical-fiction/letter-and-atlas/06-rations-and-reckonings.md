## 6. Rations and Reckonings

The morning after the Verrins arrived, I did what any sensible man does with a catastrophe: I made a list.

Not a list of feelings. Feelings are like gulls—loud, numerous, and interested only in what you're carrying. No, this was a list of tins. Of sacks. Of the honest arithmetic of a larder, which never lies to you the way people do, because a tin of biscuit cannot pretend to be two tins of biscuit no matter how politely you ask it.

I sat at my table with the ledger open before me—the stores ledger, not Maren's, though her ghost seemed to lean over my shoulder as I wrote—and I counted what four souls would eat in a winter that might run long.

"Flour," I said aloud, because I'd grown used to saying things aloud and saw no reason to stop just because the rock had acquired an audience. "Forty-one pounds."

Aldous Verrin stood at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, a man studying the horizon as if he expected it to apologize to him personally. He did not turn around. He did not say good morning. We had exchanged perhaps nine words since the boat put them ashore, and I had counted every one of them the way a miser counts coins.

"Salt pork," I continued. "Two casks, half the second gone already. Tea—" I paused here, because tea was a sensitive subject. "One pound and some dust."

Sable looked up from the chair where she sat with Pip's head heavy in her lap, the boy still gray-faced from the crossing. Her eyes went from me to the ledger and back again, and I recognized the look. It was the look of a woman doing sums of her own, and I suspected her sums were less charitable than mine.

"You're telling us we've eaten your winter," she said.

"I'm telling you what there is," I said. "The telling of what isn't can wait until after breakfast."

* * *

Here is what I had learned about provisioning Gannet Rock in eleven years of learning it: the supply boat *Merry Alice* came twice, once in October and once—if the weather permitted, which the weather often declined—in late December. After that, the channel closed itself like a book nobody meant to reopen until March. A keeper planned for five months alone and then added a margin, because the sea respected margins the way it respected nothing else.

I had provisioned for one man. One man who ate like a gull and drank tea strong enough to trot a horse across. My margin had been generous by my standards—a full extra sack of flour, a third cask of pork, a case of condensed milk I pretended was for emergencies but was really for the evenings when the wind made the tower moan and a man wanted something sweet to argue against it.

Now there were four mouths. One of them belonged to a boy of perhaps seven who had spent the previous day being ill over the side of everything, including, memorably, my oilskin coat, and who would therefore need feeding gently and often. Another belonged to Aldous, who ate the way rich men do even when they are poor—quickly, and looking about for more. And Sable ate little but watched everyone else eat much, which told me something about her history that I hadn't asked to know.

So: arithmetic. Forty-one pounds of flour, divided among four, through five months, came to rather less bread than any of us had been raised to expect. The potatoes in the root box would keep if the damp stayed out of it. There were beans, rice, a crock of salted butter going amber at the edges, forty-odd eggs that would last a fortnight if I turned them daily, and a ham I had been saving for Christmas for so many years running that saving it had become the point of it.

"We'll manage," I said, closing the ledger. "Nobody starves on Gannet Rock this winter. But we'll manage on rations, and rations mean rules, and rules mean—"

"Rules," Sable said, "mean you get to be in charge of them."

I looked at her. She looked at me. Outside, a gull screamed something that sounded suspiciously like agreement.

"That is generally how rules work in my house," I said, "which is to say, in my lighthouse, which is to say, in the only place between here and the mainland where Corven Hale's men won't find you."

That landed. I watched it land—the small flinch around her eyes, the way her hand tightened on Pip's shoulder. I hadn't said Hale's name before. None of us had. It hung in the room now like weather coming.

"My father," Sable began, and then stopped, because Aldous had turned from the window.

"The danger," Aldous said, in the mild tone men use when they are about to say nothing of the kind, "has been exaggerated. I want that understood. We imposed on Mr. Amberdale—we have imposed on him grievously—but there is no cause for dramatics."

There was a silence of a particular quality, the kind I imagine fills courtrooms just before somebody confesses to something. Sable's face did something complicated. I filed it away.

"Is that so," I said, and opened the flour sack, and measured out our breakfast with the scrupulousness of a moneylender.

* * *

Rationing, it turns out, reveals character faster than fortune ever could. Within three days I knew these people better than I had known some shipmates across whole voyages.

Aldous took his portion without comment and then lingered near the crock, hovering, a large man practicing small thefts of presence if not of food. He talked constantly and said nothing—a trick I'd seen in customs officers and con artists both. He spoke of the mainland as though he'd left it voluntarily, of his business interests as though they were thriving, of Hale as though the man were a business rival rather than a wolf. Every sentence had a door in it, closed.

Pip took his portion in both hands like a communion and thanked me each time with a formality that broke something in my chest I preferred unbroken. The boy had learned young that food was conditional. I caught myself giving him the larger share of egg and telling myself it was because growing boys needed it, which was true, and because I am a soft-hearted fool, which was also true, and neither truth improved my ledger.

And Sable—Sable weighed her portion, literally, holding the bread in her palm and estimating its ounces with an accuracy that suggested hunger had once been a teacher of hers too. Then, more often than not, she shaved a corner off hers onto Pip's plate when she thought I wasn't watching.

I was always watching. That was the job. But I let her believe otherwise, because there are kindnesses that only work in the dark.

* * *

On the fourth evening I laid it all out on the table where they could see it, because I had decided that secrecy about stores was a luxury I couldn't afford and suspicion about stores was a poison I refused to brew.

"This is the winter," I said. I set the ledger open, the tins ranked, the sacks squared. "Five months, possibly six. Four mouths." I tapped the page. "By my figures, we come through with a week to spare if the December boat runs. If it doesn't run, we come through thin and hungry and hating each other by February, but alive."

"And if the boat doesn't come at all?" Sable asked.

"Then I signal the coastguard station at Pellow Head, and they send something, eventually, if the sea allows. 'Eventually' has been known to be a generous word."

Aldous cleared his throat. "You see," he said, "this is why I say the danger has been overstated. Whatever comes, we sit tight, and the world forgets us. That has always been the wisdom of remote places."

I set down the tin I was holding. Slowly, the way you set down a thing when the alternative is throwing it.

"Mr. Verrin," I said, "I have kept this light for eleven years. In that time I have pulled four drowned men off these rocks, watched a barque go to pieces on the Outer Shoal with all hands, and once spent nine days eating boiled gull because the October boat came late. I know precisely how remote this place is and exactly how thoroughly the world forgets it." I turned the ledger toward him. "What I don't know is why a smuggling ring burned a warehouse in Falmouth and shot a harbormaster's clerk in Penzance, per your own letter, and then lost interest the moment three Verrins rowed out of sight. Either Hale is a forgiving man—which the letter gives me no reason to believe—or he knows something we don't."

The lamp hissed. Pip, curled in the corner with a slate I'd given him, had stopped writing.

Aldous's face performed a series of small adjustments, like a man straightening his collar in a mirror. "You read the letter closely."

"I read it eleven times."

"Then you know it says they will come to the rock seeking shelter. It says nothing of pursuit."

"It also," I said, "says nothing about who wrote it, or how it came to be wedged in my rocks in an oilskin packet, addressed to 'the keeper,' bearing my dead wife's family crest. Letters don't swim, Mr. Verrin. Somebody delivered that letter to my doorstep, and I have been wondering since the day I found it whether that somebody was warning me—or marking me."

For the first time since he'd stepped dripping onto my landing stage, Aldous Verrin looked genuinely unsettled. Not afraid, precisely. Recalculating. A man discovering that the remote place he'd chosen for its remoteness contained, of all inconveniences, a mind.

"You think too much, keeper," he said. "That's a dangerous habit in a lonely man."

"It's kept me alive. Loneliness and thinking get along fine; it's loneliness and *not* thinking that fill the churchyards." I gathered up the tins. "Tomorrow we start the strict rationing. Half measures waste time. And Mr. Verrin—one more thing."

He waited.

"When you're ready to tell me what the letter didn't say, I'll be winding the clockwork. I'm there most mornings. I listen well. It's another of my dangerous habits."

* * *

That night I climbed the tower for the midnight watch, and the climbing did what climbing always does: sorted my thoughts into their proper order, one stair at a time.

Irritation, I found, had been quietly hardening into something else while I wasn't attending. I had spent four days resenting these people—resenting their noise in my silence, their grief in my order, their claim on a hospitality I'd never offered because I'd never imagined needing to. And beneath the resentment, older and colder, lay the other thing: they were *hers*. They carried Maren's blood the way the rocks carried the sea's salt, invisibly, everywhere. Every time Aldous gestured with his left hand I saw her in it. It was unbearable. I intended to bear it.

But now the resentment was changing into something with more weight and less comfort. Responsibility. The word arrived unwelcome, like a bill. Because here was the plain fact of it: I had counted the tins. I knew to the biscuit how long we could hold. Nobody else on this rock could say that—not Aldous, whose arithmetic extended only as far as his own advantage, not Sable, sharp as she was, not Pip, who thought a tin of condensed milk was a form of Christmas.

Which meant that whether I liked it or not—and I did not, I want that entered into the record—I was the one standing between these three strangers and the winter. And possibly, if the letter's author had meant what I feared, between them and worse than winter.

I lit the lamp. I trimmed the wick. The great lens gathered the flame and threw it ten miles out to sea, steady as a promise, and I stood inside the turning beam feeling like the axle of some enormous machine that did not care whether I was adequate.

"Maren," I said to the glass, as I sometimes did, though I had promised myself I'd stop. "Your family is eating my ham."

The beam swung. Somewhere below, Pip coughed in his sleep, a small sound in a stone throat of a tower.

"And whoever sent that letter," I added, because the dark deserved honesty, "knew your name before I did anything but grieve it. So either you've got friends I never met, or enemies I never earned, and either way, love, I'm beginning to suspect you were never the woman who left this rock every spring to buy ribbon."

The light turned. The sea breathed against the foundation stones, patient as a creditor.

In the morning, I decided, I would wind the clockwork, and I would be there, and I would listen. And if Aldous Verrin didn't finally tell me what the letter hadn't said—

Well. I had other ways of reading a man. I'd had eleven years of practice reading the sea, and men, in my experience, were easier: they came with fewer currents and far more wreckage.
