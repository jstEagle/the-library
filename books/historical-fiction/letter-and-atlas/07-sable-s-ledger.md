## 7. Sable's Ledger

My father taught me two things before I was old enough to choose my own lessons: never trust a man who keeps his accounts in his head, and never trust a man who won't let you see his books. Coral Amberdale, as far as I could tell on my third morning at Gannet Rock, was both.

He kept the stores locked. Not locked with any ceremony—a simple hasp and a padlock gone green with salt—but locked all the same, and he carried the key on a string around his neck like a relic of some saint nobody had heard of. Every second day he opened the store room, counted out flour and tea and lamp oil with the tenderness other men reserve for their children, wrote figures in a ledger he then tucked under his arm, and closed the door again. When I asked—politely, I thought, for a woman who'd been shipwrecked onto his doorstep without so much as a change of stockings—how long our flour would last, he said, "Long enough," in the tone of a man closing a window against weather.

Long enough. That was the whole of my uncle-in-law's philosophy, apparently. Long enough for what? For winter? For the supply boat? For whatever it was he imagined we were running from, which he had not once asked us to explain?

I had come to Gannet Rock prepared to dislike him. This was not difficult. Papa had spent twenty years disliking him on my behalf, with the thoroughness of a scholar preparing an edition of a hated text. *Coward*, Papa called him. *The man who hid on a rock while Maren drowned herself in our family's troubles.* A keeper who kept nothing but his own comfort. A man who married into the Verrins and then vanished from them as neatly as if he'd been paid to do it.

So yes: I arrived prejudiced, and I am not a woman who apologizes for her prejudices. They are usually correct. But they are also, I have learned, a kind of ledger themselves—entries made in haste, in ink that smears—and every ledger deserves an audit. Papa would have said auditing was distrustful. Papa, I was beginning to suspect, had opinions about trust that deserved their own audit.

The trouble with Coral Amberdale was that he was *quiet*. Not sullen-quiet, not stupid-quiet, but quiet the way deep water is quiet. He rose before dawn, wound the clockwork that turned the lens, trimmed wicks, polished brass until you could see your face in it distorted and slightly ashamed of itself. He spoke to Pip in short, gruff sentences that somehow contained more kindness than Papa's longest speeches. He spoke to me almost not at all, which I took personally, and to Papa with a flat courtesy that had frost on its edges. And he spoke to the gulls constantly, in a low muttering voice, as though they were colleagues whose work he respected and whose conversation he did not require.

A man that quiet is either empty or full. My father's version of him was empty—a vessel with the label worn off. But vessels don't send money to orphanages.

I didn't know that yet, on the third morning. On the third morning I only knew that the padlock on the store room was green with salt, and that green salt means old iron, and old iron means habits, and habits can be read like handwriting if you're patient. I was patient. Patience is the one virtue the sea insists on teaching before it teaches anything else.

* * *

Pip was my accomplice, though he didn't know it. Children make excellent instruments because they are honest by accident. On the fourth morning I sent him up to the lamp room with a rag and instructions to "help Mr. Amberdale polish," which was a lie, since no one had instructed Pip to help anyone, but Pip went gladly enough—he had taken a silent, seasick-boy devotion to the tower and everything in it, including, God help us, the gulls—and while the two of them were up there breathing brass polish, I went down to the store room with a hairpin.

I want to be clear that I felt no shame about this. Locks are arguments, and a hairpin is a rebuttal. Papa kept his desk locked too, all through my childhood, and I picked it at eleven years old, and what I found there taught me more about the world than any school would have dared. A locked door is a confession. It says: *there is something here someone doesn't want seen.* The only question worth asking is whether the someone is protecting the thing or hiding it.

The Gannet Rock lock surrendered in under a minute. It wasn't even embarrassed about it.

Inside, the store room smelled of paraffin and flour and cold stone, and it was tidier than any room I had ever entered in my life. Sacks stacked by weight, chalked with dates. Oil tins ranked like soldiers. Rope coiled in fathoms. Candles sorted by length, which struck me as either madness or genius and which I have still not decided between. And on a shelf at eye height, exactly where a careful man would put it and a careless man would never think to look: the ledger.

I sat down on an oil tin—I remember it was the third from the left, because I marked it later in my mind as the place where my contempt first cracked—and I opened my uncle-in-law's books.

The first pages were what I expected. Stores received, stores expended, dates, initials, the crabbed upright hand of a man who learned writing from a government manual. Flour, tea, oil, wicks. Boring as a sermon and twice as repetitive. I skimmed. I nearly stopped skimming when I noticed the entries ran back nineteen years—the same hand, aging slowly, the letters loosening a little after Maren died and tightening again after, as though grief had shaken his pen and then he had gripped it harder out of spite—but boring was what I wanted. Boring proved he was what Papa said: a small man keeping small accounts on a rock at the edge of the map.

Then I reached the page dated the fourteenth of March, six years past, and found this entry:

*To St. Brinnow's Home for Fatherless Children, Penzance — £4 — "from a friend."*

I read it twice. Then I turned back through the book, page by page, faster now, my finger running down the columns like a keel through water, and found them everywhere. Twice a year, spring and autumn, regular as the equinoxes, going back nineteen years. *£3. £3. £5. £2 10s.* Small sums—a keeper's wage is not a river of gold; each of these payments must have been a tooth pulled. Always the same notation, always in the same hand: *to St. Brinnow's, "from a friend."*

And beside the very first entry, dated the year of my aunt's marriage, one line in different ink, older, blotted, written by a younger version of the same man:

*M.'s wish.*

I sat on that oil tin for a long time. The store room was cold enough that my breath showed, and I watched it drift and vanish, drift and vanish, like something trying to say a word it couldn't finish.

Here is what I had believed, going in: that Coral Amberdale was a coward who had abandoned my aunt to her family's dangers and then abandoned the family itself, retreating to this rock to keep a light for strangers because strangers ask nothing of you. Here is what the ledger said: that for nineteen years, beginning the month he married her, this man had been quietly starving his own stores—because that's what those sums were, I know arithmetic when I see it bleeding—to pay an orphanage in Maren's name, anonymously, asking nothing, telling no one, not even, I would wager, Maren herself after the first year.

Cowards do not keep secrets that cost them money and buy them nothing.

Unless they do. Unless the secret is the payment. Unless guilt works that way in some men—not as a fire but as a drip, steady, corrosive, wearing a channel through a life. I know guilt. I grew up in a house where it was load-bearing. So I did not leap to the generous conclusion. I am my father's daughter; I leapt to the suspicious one, and then stood on it and looked around.

Because there was another question, and it would not sit down. *Why St. Brinnow's?*

An orphanage is not a random charity. Nobody wakes up at twenty-five and thinks, *I shall send half my wages to fatherless children in Penzance.* There was a reason. There was always a reason with money; Papa taught me that too, though he meant it cynically and I mean it forensically. Either Maren had a connection to that place—or Coral did—or the money was not charity at all but something wearing charity's coat. A debt. An apology. A purchase.

I copied the entries. All of them, into the back of my own notebook, in pencil, with dates. If you're going to audit a man, audit him completely. Then I relocked the padlock, straightened the oil tins so the third-from-the-left bore no memory of me, and climbed out into the grey noon light feeling as though I had opened a door in a house I thought I knew and found a corridor where the wall should have been.

* * *

That evening I watched him across the kitchen table with new eyes, which is to say I watched him at all. Before, I had catalogued him and filed him: tall, stooped already at forty-odd, hands like knotted rope, a face built for being overlooked, eyes the colour of the sea on a day when the sea can't be bothered. Filed. Done. Now I found the file wouldn't close.

He was mending a net—not because we needed a net, but because mending was what his hands did while his mind went elsewhere—and Pip sat beside him learning the knots, and Coral explained each one in that low gravel voice, and Pip nodded solemnly, and neither of them knew they were having the warmest conversation in the building.

"Mr. Amberdale," I said, because I cannot leave a thing alone, it's a family failing, "how long have you kept this light?"

"Nineteen years in April." He didn't look up. His fingers went on working the twine.

"And before that?"

"Before that I was nowhere much." A pause. The knot took shape under his hands, neat as scripture. "Your aunt used to say I was born standing still."

"Maren said that?" I kept my voice light. Lightness is a tool; I keep it sharpened.

"She said a great many things." Something moved behind his face—quick, submerged, gone. "Most of them true."

"And after she—" I stopped. I had been about to say *after she died*, and something in the set of his shoulders told me the sentence would land harder than I intended, and I found, to my irritation, that I cared. "After," I finished, uselessly.

"After," he said, "the light needed keeping."

Which was not an answer, and was entirely an answer, and told me precisely nothing except that the man had a door inside him with the same green-salt padlock as the store room, and I had only a hairpin, and some locks you cannot pick from the outside. You have to wait for the owner to open them, or break them open yourself, and I hadn't yet decided which sort of burglar I was prepared to be.

Pip looked up at me then, needle halfway through the mesh, and gave me the look children give when they sense adults circling something sharp. "Sable's good at sums," he offered, into the silence, bless him, bless and curse him. "She does Papa's books."

"I know," Coral said.

"You *know*?"

"He watches everyone," Pip informed me, with the gravity of a boy reporting troop movements. "He watches the boats and the birds and us. He just doesn't say."

I looked at Coral Amberdale. Coral Amberdale looked at his net. And I understood suddenly that I had spent four days believing myself the observer in this tower, the auditor, the one who saw clearly while the others blundered—and that the quiet man had been reading me the entire time the way I read ledgers: patiently, completely, missing nothing, saying nothing.

It should have frightened me. It did frighten me, a little. But underneath the fright was something worse and better, something that cracked along the fault line Papa had built in me, something that sounded like this:

*Who sends money to orphans for nineteen years and tells no one? Who marries a Verrin and vanishes, and mourns her in instalments, twice yearly, in anonymous sums? Who keeps a dead woman's wish in a store-room ledger for two decades, chalked between the flour and the oil?*

Not the coward in Papa's stories. Or else a coward of a species Papa had never described—one whose courage was of the slow, unglamorous, unrewarded kind, the kind that looks exactly like hiding from the outside and is something else entirely from within. Or else—my suspicion insisted on its seat at the table—else the kindness was camouflage, the orphanage a blind, the humility a costume, and the real man was folded up somewhere in these nineteen years of entries like a letter in an oilskin packet, waiting.

Waiting. The word snagged, the way a word will.

Because I had seen the packet. We all had—Papa most of all, turning it over and over in the first days, that sealed oilskin addressed to *the keeper* bearing the Verrin crest, the letter that had delivered us to this rock like a fish on a line. Papa had read it aloud to us and burned nothing and explained nothing, and I had watched Coral listen with his face shut like a shop at night.

But I had also seen, that very first afternoon, how Coral had looked at the crest stamped in the wax. Not with surprise. With recognition, yes—that was expected—but underneath the recognition, something I had filed away without understanding and now took out and held to the light:

Relief. He had looked relieved. As if the sea had finally delivered something he had been waiting nineteen years to receive.

I finished copying the last entry by lamplight that night, my pencil scratching in the dark corner of the sleeping loft while Papa snored below and Pip murmured at his dreams. The final figure was £4, dated that autumn, three months ago. Three months ago the ring was already hunting us; three months ago Coral had no way of knowing what was coming; and still he had paid, punctual as tide-tables, *from a friend*.

I blew out my candle and lay in the dark listening to the sea work the rock beneath us, patient as a locksmith, and I admitted it to myself at last, in the privacy of the black, where admissions are cheap:

I did not know who this man was. Not the coward, not the saint, not the keeper, not the friend. And the not-knowing itched like wet wool, because I had built my whole journey here on knowing—on Papa's knowing, on the family's knowing, on the clean comfortable story of the man who hid.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would begin on the rest of the house. Because a man who keeps one secret for nineteen years keeps others, and somewhere in this tower—under the floorboards, behind the brickwork, in the sea chest I had seen him glance at twice and touch never—was the rest of the account.

And I meant to balance it.
