## 8. Pip and the Lamp Room

I had a system, in those first days of the Verrin occupation, for avoiding my guests. It was not cowardice; it was engineering. The tower has one hundred and eight steps, and I know every creak of them the way a man knows the faults in his own character—intimately, and without much hope of correction. When I heard Aldous's boots on the stair, I went down. When Sable's door opened, I found urgent business with the oil store. It was a dance, and I was light on my feet for a man of fifty-four.

The boy was harder to avoid. Pip was six years old and moved through the tower like weather—everywhere at once, silent until he wasn't, leaving dampness behind him. He asked questions the way gulls fight over a fish: relentlessly, from all sides, with no regard for dignity. *Why does the sea go out so far? Why is your chair by the window? Why don't you talk to them?* That last one he'd asked on his second morning, watching me pass through the kitchen where his grandfather sat hunched over tea he didn't drink.

"Because there's nothing to say," I told him.

"That's what Grandpa says," said Pip, "but he says it louder."

I did not have an answer to that, so I wound the clockwork instead, which is my answer to most things.

On the fourth day of their stay, the wind came around to the northeast and the barometer began its slow, sullen slide downward, and I spent the afternoon in the lamp room checking the mechanism against bad weather. This was work I did alone, always. It was the one room in the tower that felt like mine entire—the brass, the glass, the great lens turning its slow circles like a patient animal that trusts you. Maren had never come up here. She'd said the height made her dizzy, though she'd grown up on cliffs and thought nothing of walking the headland path. Another small lie in a marriage I was only beginning to understand had been upholstered with them.

So when I heard the hatch lift behind me—a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat—I turned expecting gulls, or wind, or my own imagination.

It was the boy.

He stood at the top of the ladder with one hand still on the rail, his hair standing up as though he'd lost an argument with it, and he looked at me the way a man looks at a dog he's been told will bite.

"You're not supposed to be up here," I said.

"I know." He didn't move. Neither did I. Somewhere below us, Sable's voice called his name once, twice, and faded—the tower swallowing it—and then there was just the two of us and the tick of the clockwork and the enormous hush of the lens turning.

"How did you get past your mother?"

"I'm small," Pip said, as if this were a profession he took seriously. "And she's reading Grandpa's letters again. She reads them and her mouth goes like this—" he pressed his lips into a thin line, a fair impression of Sable at her most dangerous, "—so I left."

I should have sent him down. Any sensible keeper would have. There are a hundred ways to die in a lamp room if you're careless, and children are careless the way water is wet. But he was looking past me at the lens with an expression I recognized from the inside: the look of a person who has found the exact center of something and wants, more than anything, to be allowed to stay there.

"The steps are slippery near the gallery rail," I heard myself say. "You keep away from them, and you can look."

He came forward the way you'd approach a horse—flat-palmed, breath held—and stopped an arm's length from the lens and stared up into it. The afternoon light came through the glass in long bent bars and laid itself across his face, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because there was nothing that needed saying. The lens did its slow turn. The sea lay gray-green to the horizon, gathering itself for whatever it intended next.

"What's it for?" he asked finally.

"It bends the light," I said. "One flame in here would be a candle any sailor might miss. Through this glass, it becomes a beam they can see twenty miles off. Same fire. Different truth."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "It makes small things big?"

"It makes one thing reach farther than it should."

"I like that," he said, and I confess it caught me somewhere under the ribs, that plain little verdict, delivered without irony, which is the only way six-year-olds deliver anything.

* * *

*(Pip)*

The man's name was Coral, which was a funny name, like a rock pretending to be a fish. Uncle Aldous said he was a coward who hid on his rock, but Uncle Aldous said lots of things and Mama said *hush, Aldous* in the voice that ended conversations. The rock man didn't seem like a coward. He seemed like the clock in our old house in Penzance, which ticked all night and never let on what it knew.

The lamp room smelled of brass and oil and salt, and it was the best room in the world. There was a stool exactly his size, or nearly, and the glass made rainbows on the floor if you stood in the right place, and when Coral showed him, Pip stood in the right place for a very long time.

"You can polish it, if your hands are clean," Coral said.

They were not clean. They were the hands of a boy who had spent four days on a boat and three days in a strange tower, and they had investigated everything. So Coral pumped water into a basin—he had a pump right there in the corner, which was another miracle—and made Pip wash to the elbows while he watched like a schoolmaster, and then he gave him a cloth softer than anything Pip had ever touched, a cloth like a cloud that had retired.

"Now," Coral said, "you hold it flat. Not fingers—you're polishing glass, not petting a cat. Small circles. You follow the curve. The glass is older than both of us put together, and it has survived worse than you, so be humble about it."

Pip was humble about it. He polished one facet, and then, because nobody stopped him, another, and the rainbow-bars slid across the floor and up the wall and across Coral's face, which did something Pip couldn't name—it unclenched, the way Grandpa's hand unclenched when he fell asleep in his chair.

"Why do you live here?" Pip asked, because asking was free.

"Somebody has to."

"But why *you?*"

Coral was quiet for three turns of the lens. Pip counted them. Then he said, "Because I'm good at being alone, and it's a job for someone who is."

"That's sad," Pip said.

"So I'm told," said Coral, and there was a twist at the corner of his mouth that wasn't quite a smile but was keeping it company, and Pip decided that was enough talking for now, because there was glass to polish, and glass did not ask sad questions back.

* * *

(Coral)

I want to be honest about what happened in that lamp room, since honesty became the currency of that winter whether we could afford it or not: I forgot, for the better part of two hours, that I resented these people.

It was not forgiveness. It was something smaller and more useful—an armistice. The boy's hands on my lens, clumsy and earnest, doing in ten minutes what I'd done ten thousand times, and doing it badly and then less badly, and asking his questions between facets: *Did you build it? Did your father? Has anyone ever fallen? Have you?* (I had, once, on the gallery rail in '81; I showed him the scar on my palm and he examined it like a relic.) And somewhere in the middle of it I realized I was talking—not the rationed sentences I dealt out to Aldous, not the careful nothing I gave Sable, but actual speech, running on like a tap left open, telling him about the Fresnel rings and the Frenchman who invented them and how the old keepers before me had cursed the day the government replaced their good simple Argand lamps with this "jeweled contraption," and Pip laughing at the word *contraption* and making me say it twice.

When Sable appeared at the hatch—white-faced, having torn the tower apart looking for him—I braced for the scene. The accusation. Instead she stood on the ladder with her son asleep against my spare coat on the watch shelf, because the boy had polished eleven facets and then simply powered down mid-sentence, the way children do, and I had not had the heart to wake him or the sense to carry him down.

"He climbed past you," I said.

"He climbs past everyone." She came up slowly, taking in the room—she'd not been above the second landing since arriving—and her eyes went over the lens, the logbook open on its stand, the basin of gray water, the retired cloud-cloth. Something moved through her face that I couldn't read and didn't try to. "You didn't shout at him."

"There wasn't anything broken."

"There usually is, with Pip. Or there's about to be." She lifted the boy, and he murmured and hooked his arms around her neck without waking, and she looked at me over his head with an expression that was almost, nearly, in the neighborhood of gratitude. "Thank you," she said, in the tone of a woman paying a debt she intended to dispute later.

"Ma'am," I said, and touched my cap, and hated myself a little for the formality, and kept it anyway, because a man needs some walls even when he's begun leaving doors open.

She paused at the hatch. "He talked all through supper about the *contraption.* He wants to know if he can help tomorrow."

I looked at the lens, turning its patient circles. "If his hands are clean," I said, "he can polish."

* * *

*(Pip)*

Mama carried him down the stairs and her heart was going fast, the way it did when she was scared-angry, but she didn't shout, which meant it hadn't been a bad thing he'd done, which was new information about the world.

That night in the bunk he asked her, "Is Coral our family?"

Mama went still. In the dark you could hear Grandpa coughing in the next room, and the sea working at the rocks below like a dog with a bone.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because he let me touch the lamp. Family lets you touch things."

Mama was quiet a long time. Long enough that Pip thought she'd gone to sleep. Then she said, "Family is complicated, love," and kissed his hair, and Pip thought: *that's what people say when the answer is yes but they haven't finished being angry yet.*

He fell asleep counting facets. There were one hundred and thirty-two in the lens, Coral had said, and Pip intended to polish every single one, and after that, whatever came next.

* * *

(Coral)

That night I sat in the kitchen with the logbook and wrote the day's entry, and found I had written more than the weather. *Taught the boy the lens,* I'd written, and then crossed it out, and then, being unable to think of anything truer, written it again.

Aldous came down late, as he did, moving through the tower like a man trying not to disturb a sick relative. He poured himself tea he wouldn't finish and stood at the window with his back to me, and I noticed—for the first time, or perhaps for the hundredth and finally—that his hands were shaking. Not the shaking of cold. The shaking of a man carrying something heavy in very small containers.

"The boy spoke well of you," he said, without turning.

"He spoke well of the lens. I was incidental."

"Maren used to say you'd make a good father." He said it to the window, quietly, the way you'd set down a coin you weren't sure was yours. "She said it was a waste, you living out here with no one to teach."

The words went into me at an angle I didn't understand yet. I set down my pen. "Your daughter-in-law seems to think you know more about my wife than I did," I said. "Sable. She reads your letters like scripture."

Aldous turned then, and his face in the lamplight was gray and old and frightened, and I understood that whatever he was carrying in those small containers was close to breaking them.

"Coral," he said. "There are things about Maren—about what she did, and why—that you ought to hear from me before you hear them any other way."

The clockwork ticked above us, faithful as ever. Outside, the barometer needle had begun, very slowly, to fall.

"Then say them," I said.
