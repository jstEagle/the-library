## 10. The Letter Again

Three days is a long time to share a roof with a piece of paper.

I had put the oilskin packet in the drawer of the desk in my quarters—the desk I'd bought secondhand from a retiring keeper at Wolf Neck who swore it had never once been rained on, which tells you what sort of man retires from lighthouse work—and for three days I did not open it. I told myself this was discipline. A keeper learns to wait out weather; a squall that looks endless on Tuesday is a memory by Friday. The letter would keep. Letters always keep. That is their whole talent and their whole crime.

But a sealed thing in a small house is worse than an unsealed thing. An open letter you can read, argue with, fold away. A sealed one sits there radiating questions like a damp stove radiates cold. By Thursday morning I was winding the clockwork with one hand and listening to the drawer with the other ear, which is not how ears work, but nobody was about to correct me.

The Verrins were asleep—or pretending to be, which amounts to the same courtesy. Three strangers under my roof for two nights now, and the tower had already learned their sounds: Aldous coughed like a man apologizing to his own lungs; Sable moved through the gallery at odd hours on feet so quiet I only knew she'd passed because the gulls complained after her; Pip slept the way children do, as if sleep were a country he owned outright. And me, in between them all, lying awake doing arithmetic I didn't want to do—how long the flour would stretch, how long my patience would, and which of those ran out first.

I got up before dawn, as I always do, and I took the packet out of the drawer, and I set it on the desk beside the lamp, and I looked at it the way you look at a dog that has bitten somebody.

Then I opened it.

* * *

Not because I'd found courage overnight. Because I realized, somewhere around the fourth hour of not sleeping, that I was being ridiculous. Here sat a letter naming my dead wife's family as targets of men who presumably meant them harm, and here stood I, the sole responsible adult within nine miles of rock, treating it like correspondence from a magazine subscription office. If the sea went to the trouble of delivering a thing to my door, the least I could do was read it. The sea does not deliver often. When it does, it expects a signature.

The oilskin came off stiff, the knots swollen with salt. Inside: a letter, folded twice, written close on both sides of a single sheet, and beneath it—a smaller paper, folded again, which I will come to, because I did not come to it first, and that ordering matters.

The letter began without salutation. No *Dear Keeper*, no *To whom it may concern*, no *Dear Sir, though God knows why you of all people*. It began the way telegrams begin, mid-crisis:

*The Verrins are known to Corven Hale's people. What was done in '81 has been remembered longer than anyone wished. They will come to Gannet Rock. Shelter them or turn them away, but know that whichever you choose, you have chosen.*

And then a date, and then nothing. No name signed at the bottom. Just a mark—a small pressed seal in grey wax, bearing the Verrin crest: the heron and the broken mast.

That was the whole of it. Ninety-odd words to rearrange a man's life.

I sat with it while the window went from black to the colour of wet slate. *What was done in '81.* I turned the phrase over. Maren and I married in '79. In '81 she was twenty-three years old and living forty miles up the coast with her father's people, before her father died and the family scattered like a dropped plate. Whatever was done in '81, it was done in the country where she grew up, among people whose names I mostly knew from her rare, guarded mentions—Aldous above all, Aldous whom she spoke of the way you speak of weather in another hemisphere. Interesting, perhaps. Not yours.

Except now the weather had arrived at my door, dripping.

*They will come to Gannet Rock.*

They had come. That was the part that sat worst with me, if I'm honest—not the smugglers, not the revenge, but the fact that the letter had been right about something that hadn't happened yet. Prophecy is a terrible quality in a document. It makes every other line feel loaded.

* * *

It was on the third reading—I want to be precise about that, because I have a reputation to maintain with myself as a careful man—that I noticed the handwriting.

Here is what I know about Verrin hands. Maren wrote a small, upright script, economical as ration bread; she crossed her t's with a single impatient stroke and never lifted the pen higher than she had to. She thought ink cost money even when she'd bought it herself. I still have her grocery lists, tucked in the back of the tide tables, and I could pick her writing out of a hundred samples the way you'd pick her voice out of a crowd—which is to say instantly, and with a pain attached that doesn't lessen so much as learn better manners.

Aldous writes large. This I learned in two days, because he insisted on leaving notes—he was forever leaving notes, apologetic little flags planted around my tower: *Taken two candles, will replace. Hope that is agreeable. A.V.* Everything he wrote had a flourish on the capitals and a hesitancy in the body, a hand trying to look confident the way a borrowed coat tries to fit. He dotted his i's heavily, like a man driving nails.

The letter was neither.

The letter's hand was small but not cramped, slanted forward as if hurrying somewhere, with letters that connected in long confident chains except when they stopped altogether mid-word—as though the writer kept changing their mind about whether to be legible. The capital T in *they* had a loop I stared at for a full minute, because loops are habits, and habits are signatures more honest than signatures. Whoever wrote this letter had been taught penmanship by someone particular, someone old-fashioned, and had rebelled against the teaching just enough to leave fingerprints.

It was not Maren's. I checked against the grocery lists twice, holding them side by side in the lamplight like a magistrate. Not hers. But near hers—cousinly, if handwriting can be cousinly—the same economy underneath, the same reluctance to waste a stroke, wearing different clothes.

And it was certainly not Aldous's. There was no hesitation anywhere in it. Whoever wrote those ninety words wrote them knowing exactly what they wanted to say and exactly what each word would do.

Which raises a question I'd like you to sit with, because I sat with it until my tea went cold: if Aldous didn't write the letter that warned him of danger—who did? And if the warning was true, and the danger real, why disguise the hand at all? You don't disguise your writing to protect your family. You disguise it to protect yourself. Or to protect someone else.

Or—said the small cold voice at the bottom of the well—to make sure that whoever received it believed precisely what its writer wanted believed.

* * *

There was also the smaller paper. I'll admit I left it till last. Partly from a superstition I won't defend—big trouble first, little trouble after breakfast—and partly because it frightened me more, and I say that as a man who once climbed down the outside of this tower in a gale to free a fouled ventilator cap.

It was a list. Seven names, written vertically, each struck through with a single horizontal line—except two. Six names meant nothing to me at all: merchants, I'd guess, or harbour officials, the sort of names that appear on shipping manifests and nowhere else. One name made me stop breathing for a moment:

*Verrin, M.*

Struck through. Crossed off. Finished.

Now, there are several ways to read a struck-through name on a list delivered by the sea alongside a letter about revenge. The kind reading is: *she's dead, nothing to do there.* I am, after all, a widower; the sea itself had certified that entry years ago. But the unkind reading sat down across from me and refused to leave: struck through could mean *done*. Could mean *accounted for*. Could mean that whatever was done in '81 had been done *to* her, or done *by* her, and either way the ledger considered her settled—and settled accounts are the ones that frighten me most, because they imply a bookkeeper, and bookkeepers keep books, and books get reopened.

The two unstruck names were *Verrin, A.* and *Verrin, S.*

My guests. My unwelcome, rain-soaked, note-leaving, gull-disturbing guests, alive on paper in a stranger's hand, marked outstanding.

Sable. Which meant the girl had been on this list since before she was—well. Since before she mattered to anybody on this rock. Since she was a child with a name worth crossing off someday. I looked toward the wall that separated my quarters from theirs and felt the strangest sensation, half pity and half vertigo: these people had walked into my tower carrying their deaths folded in someone else's pocket, and I had given them soup.

* * *

By the time the sun hauled itself up—I use the word loosely; the sun that morning was less a presence than a rumour behind cloud—I had arranged my suspicions into the shape I liked best, which is to say I had built three theories and knocked two of them down and rebuilt them, the way you do when you're alone with a problem and no one to tell you you're being a fool.

Theory the first: Aldous wrote the letter himself, disguised his hand, and sent it ahead to prepare me—an odd courtesy, preparing a stranger to receive you—but why disguise it from the very people it protected? Unless the disguise wasn't for me. Unless Aldous wanted the letter to look, upon later inspection, like it came from someone else entirely. From whom?

Theory the second: the letter came from inside Hale's ring. Someone there had gone soft, or ambitious, or vengeful in a new direction, and mailed a warning to the one place the Verrins might run. But that required the writer to know Maren's history, my location, and my connection—all things a stranger shouldn't hold. Though the crest argued otherwise. The crest said: *this comes from family, or from someone who owns family things.* Wax seals aren't lying, exactly. They're just very easy to steal.

Theory the third—the one I kept coming back to the way your tongue returns to a broken tooth—was that the letter was exactly what it appeared, a true warning from someone who loved the Verrins enough to save them and feared someone enough to hide behind a borrowed hand. And the only person I could think of who loved them, feared the right people, and wrote a hand cousinly to Maren's, was someone I'd never met, from a branch of the family I'd never been told existed.

You'll notice all three theories agreed on one point: everyone in this story knew more about my wife than I did. Twenty-two years of marriage, and I was the only man on the coast reading her obituary as a complete text.

I don't record that bitterly. Or—I record it bitterly, yes, but it was a clean bitterness, the useful kind, the kind that sharpens rather than corrodes. I had spent twenty years believing I was a man history happened around, the way water happens around a rock. Now history had washed a letter up against me personally, addressed to *the keeper*, and the letter suggested that history had been happening *to* me for some time, quietly, in rooms I wasn't invited into, in handwriting I couldn't place. Invisibility, it turns out, is not the same as safety. It just means you're the last to know.

* * *

I went up to the lamp room to put the lens through its morning cleaning, because a man can only stare at his own theories so long before they start staring back, and brass does not ask questions. The work steadied me. Cloth on glass, glass reflecting the grey morning, the horizon doing its usual impression of nothing whatsoever happening. Somewhere below, Pip was awake—you could hear him narrating his own breakfast to no one—and Sable's silent feet crossed the gallery overhead, and Aldous coughed his apology to the stairwell.

Ordinary sounds. Family sounds, almost, if you squinted, which I declined to do.

But I brought the letter up with me anyway, folded in my breast pocket, because apparently I've become the sort of man who carries a mystery around his own lighthouse. And standing there in the lamp room with the great lens turning its slow blind circles, I took it out and read it a fourth time, and noticed the last thing.

The date. The letter bore a date eleven days past. Eleven days, and it had arrived wedged in the rocks below the tower—meaning it had travelled, meaning someone had posted it and it had gone astray, or someone had carried it and lost it, or—

Or someone had thrown it into the sea deliberately, close aboard, trusting the current to bring it to my doorstep. Trusting it the way you trust a thing you've tested before. The rocks below the lantern receive very specific debris; any keeper knows the anatomy of his own tideline. That packet hadn't drifted. It had been *placed*.

Eleven days. The boat with my guests aboard had arrived four days after the storm that brought the letter. Which meant that whoever placed it had known, nearly a week in advance, exactly when the Verrins would come—and had wanted me warned, or wanted me *prepared*, or wanted me watching the horizon with a name in my head before the hull ever broke its silhouette.

Someone out there was directing this play. Casting me. Handing me my lines in a disguised hand and waiting to see if the keeper knew his part.

Below me, faint through the iron deck, I heard Aldous's voice rise—just a few words, just enough—asking Sable something urgent, and her answer, low and flat: *"Not here. Later."*

Later. Everyone in my tower was living in later. And far out past the shoals, where the grey water met the greyer sky, the barometer in the lamp room had begun—quietly, patiently, the way all the worst news arrives—to fall.
