## 11. Storm Warning

My father taught me to read a barometer the way other fathers teach daughters to read scripture: with reverence, and with the understanding that it will eventually ask something terrible of you.

The instrument hung in Coral Amberdale's kitchen—because everything in this tower was his, including apparently the weather—and that morning it had fallen so far I half expected to find the needle lying on the floor of the glass.

"Thirty days," I said, tapping the dial. "That's what you told us about the supply boat."

Coral didn't look up from the bread dough he was punishing. "Twenty-nine now. It comes at month's end or not at all."

"And the storms?"

He wiped flour from his hands with the deliberation of a man buying time. "The great ones usually hold off until midwinter. Usually." He said *usually* the way a doctor says *we'll know more after the tests*.

Pip looked up from the corner where he'd been teaching himself knots out of one of Coral's manuals, which is either a charming hobby for a boy of ten or the first symptom of a career in crime. "What happens if the storms come early?"

"They come," Coral said simply, and went back to the dough as though he could knead the sky into better behavior.

I had been on Gannet Rock eleven days by then, and I want to be precise about what those days were, because memory is a liar and this chapter of my life deserves an honest account. The rock was refuge—I don't dispute that. Corven Hale's men were somewhere behind us, patient as arithmetic, and no smuggler's sloop was going to pluck us off a lighthouse surrounded by November water. But a refuge and a trap are frequently the same structure viewed from different moods, and my mood that morning was trending toward trap.

Consider the inventory. One tower, seven floors if you counted generously. Four souls: myself; my father Aldous Verrin, who had perfected the art of being apologetic without ever apologizing; Pip, who was handling our circumstances with the adaptability children possess precisely because they haven't yet learned what they should be afraid of; and Coral Amberdale, widower, keeper, and the most unreadable man I have ever shared a kitchen with.

Eleven days of his cooking. Eleven days of watching him wind the clockwork mechanism that turned the lens, trim the wicks, polish brass that was already brighter than my prospects. Eleven days of a man who spoke in measurements—gallons, hours, fathoms—as though feeling were a unit he hadn't been issued.

And beneath all of it, the barometer falling like a confession.

* * *

By afternoon the sea had changed its mind about being scenery. The swell came in long and green-black, hitting the rock below with a sound less like waves than like furniture being rearranged in a room above ours. The gulls had gone quiet or gone elsewhere, and I have since learned that when the birds leave a place, you should take their advice.

I found Coral in the oil store doing inventory, because of course he was. The man inventoried the way other men prayed.

"How bad?" I asked.

He held up the ledger without turning. "Burning oil through February if we're careful. Lamp wicks for the season. Water's the trouble—cistern's half full and rainwater's only useful if the storms leave the catchment intact."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we learn to like salt tea." He closed the ledger. "You've been reading my stores again."

It wasn't a question. I'd made no particular secret of it—counting things calms me, always has, and a woman who grew up in a household built on her father's debts learns early exactly how much of anything there is. "Someone in this family should know where the edges are," I said.

Something moved across his face—not quite a flinch, but the shadow one casts. "Your mother used to say that."

"My grandmother," I corrected, before I thought about it, and then wished I hadn't, because Maren Verrin was a subject this household handled like unexploded ordnance. My aunt by marriage, dead nine years, mentioned by my father in fragments and by Coral not at all. Every conversation on this rock orbited her absence the way the lamp orbited its flame.

"I'm sorry," I said, because I was raised to apologize whether or not I meant it, a family trait my father carried to professional extremes.

"No need." He put the ledger back on its shelf, spine aligned with a precision that would have satisfied a museum. "You're right to count. Edges matter." And then, quieter, almost to the oil cans: "They matter more when the tide's coming in."

I want to record that I understood him then. I didn't. I thought he was talking about the weather.

* * *

Evening brought the wind properly. Not the storm itself—that was still two days out, gathering itself beyond the horizon like a debt collector—but the outriders of it, gusts that hit the tower and made the whole structure hum a low note I felt in my teeth. Pip thought it was wonderful. He stood at the gallery door under strict orders not to pass it, hair whipping, shouting observations about wave heights with the enthusiasm of a boy who did not yet understand that the ocean was currently composing a list.

My father sat in the kitchen with a cup of tea he wasn't drinking, watching the barometer instead of the window, as though staring at the instrument might shame it upward.

"It'll be rough," he said to no one.

"You keep saying that," I said. "As though repetition improves the forecast."

"As though saying it makes me feel I've done something." He smiled, and it was one of his good smiles, the ones that worked on strangers and had stopped working on me around age fourteen. "We're safe here, Sable. That's what I keep telling myself too. Stone walls. A light. No one finds anyone they don't want found on a rock in November."

"That's rather the point of a lighthouse, isn't it? To be found."

His smile thinned at the edges. "Found by ships, love. Not by men."

There it was—the thing beneath the thing, surfacing the way wreckage surfaces before it sinks for good. My father had spent three weeks telling us the danger was manageable, contained, a matter of staying quiet and staying mobile, and every day on this rock the story had grown a little heavier, the way wet wool does. Hale believed a Verrin betrayed him. My father insisted it was Aunt Maren, may she rest and may I never fully believe him. But insistences are cheap, and I had watched him check the horizon eleven times a day like a man expecting a bill.

"Coral thinks the storms will make the crossing impossible even for Hale's boats," I said.

"Coral thinks a lot of things." My father turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, a habit I'd catalogued years ago alongside his other tells. "Coral has lived here twenty years and believes the world can be kept outside by keeping a schedule. He winds his clock, trims his lamp, and history goes around him like water round a stone."

"You sound resentful of a man's punctuality."

"I sound—" He stopped. Started again. "I sound tired, Sable. Forgive me. It's the waiting. Waiting's worse than running. When you're running you're at least certain of the direction."

I left him with his tea and went up.

* * *

The stairwell of Gannet Rock is ninety-three steps—I counted, obviously—and between the fourth and fifth turns there is a window no wider than a book, set there by some Victorian pragmatist so keepers could check the weather without climbing the full height. I stood at that window longer than the weather warranted.

Below, the rock. The landing stage with its iron rings, slick black. The boat skids where the supply dinghy sat chained down. The cistern shed hunkering against the wind. And beyond, the channel, which in fair weather was a road and in foul weather was a wall, and which for the next four weeks would decide what we were: rescued people or marooned ones.

Because that was the arithmetic nobody said aloud. The supply boat came at month's end. If the storms arrived first and stayed—if the season turned the way old winters turn, slamming the channel shut with weeks of heavy sea—then the boat couldn't come at all. Or it would come and fail to make the landing, stand off in the white water, and leave. Either way the result was the same: four people, finite oil, finite water, finite patience, and a winter with no exit except over water that kills professionals.

A refuge. A trap. Same stones.

I climbed on up to the lamp room, because it's the one place in the tower where the confinement stops mattering. You step into that glass drum at the top and suddenly there's nothing between you and everything—the whole grey circuit of the world, horizon to horizon. Coral keeps the lens covered by day, the great Fresnel stacked in its cage like a sleeping animal, and the brass fittings throw your reflection back at you warped and multiplied until you're standing in a crowd of yourself.

I talked to the lens. I'm aware how that sounds. But eleven days of careful sentences in a small household will drive a person to it.

"He sent money," I told the glass. "For years. Anonymously. To an orphanage, in her name."

This was my own secret discovery, made two nights earlier with his ledgers, and I still didn't know what to do with it. Coral Amberdale, the silent man, the human lock, had been posting coins to strange children because his dead wife once—what? Cared about them? Guilted him from beyond? I had built an entire portrait of the man out of his silences and the portrait had a hole in it shaped like generosity, and I resented that more than I can cleanly explain. It's easier to distrust someone consistent.

"Or he's hiding something," I told my reflections, and they all nodded, being agreeable sorts.

The wind chose that moment to hit the lamp room hard enough to make the glazing flex—a groan up through the mullions, the whole lantern swaying on its stone neck like a held breath. Below me, the sea was coming apart against the outer rocks in columns of spray higher than the gallery rail. Somewhere out in the murk, the barometer's promise was rolling toward us at thirty miles a day, and I stood inside the brightest thing on the coast and felt, for the first time in my adult life, genuinely small.

Not frightened, exactly. Frightened has edges. This was the other thing—the understanding that the edges were about to be tested, all of them at once, and that we had volunteered for it by degrees. Pip by accident of birth. My father by whatever he'd done or failed to do. Me by loyalty, which I had always assumed was a virtue and was beginning to suspect was just a slower form of stubbornness.

And Coral by choice. Twenty years of choice, renewed daily, one winding of the clock at a time. I thought about that while the lantern groaned. There was a version of safety available to him—any mainland town, any ordinary employment—and he had declined it every single morning for two decades. Either the bravest man I'd met or the best-hidden coward on the coast, and I could not tell which, and the not-knowing was starting to itch like wet wool.

* * *

I came down near dark and found the kitchen transformed. Coral had lit the stove against the dropping temperature and laid out charts across the table, weighting the corners with cups, and he and Pip were bent over them like conspirators. Pip had flour on his sleeve, which meant Coral had let him help with the bread, which meant the thaw I'd been charting in my private ledger had advanced another degree.

"—and the shoals run out here," Coral was saying, tapping a scatter of dotted marks east of the rock, "which is why the channel bends. Boats that ignore the bend feed the crabs."

"What's the shallowest bit called?"

"The Nipple. Because of the shape."

Pip laughed with his entire body. Ten-year-olds do that; it's one of their few reliable virtues.

I took the seat opposite and studied the chart upside down. "How deep is the channel in a storm?"

Coral looked at me across the lamplight, and I watched him decide how much truth the question deserved. He did that constantly—rationed facts the way he rationed oil—and I had begun keeping score.

"Depends on the storm," he said finally. "A common gale, the boat can still work it. The great ones—" he straightened, pressed both palms flat on the chart as though holding the sea down, "—in the great ones, Gannet Rock is alone. Truly alone. Last time was six years back. Nine days of it. No landings, no signals answered. Just the work."

"Nine days," I repeated.

"Nine days of the work." He said it like a man reciting a creed, and maybe he was. "The lamp doesn't care how you feel. The mechanism wants winding every four hours regardless. The log wants entries. You keep the light because keeping the light is the job, and the job doesn't pause for weather, and—" he glanced at Pip, and softened by a single degree, "—it doesn't pause for fear, either. Fear rides along. It just doesn't get a vote."

I thought of my father upstairs, checking the horizon. Fear got a full ballot in him.

"When did the barometer last sit this low outside of season?" I asked.

His hands stilled on the chart. For a long moment the only sounds were the wind working the tower and the clockwork ticking its patient circles overhead.

"Nineteen years ago," he said. "The year I brought Maren here."

And then he rolled up the charts, because apparently that was all the past we were getting today, and began explaining to Pip why salt tea was going to become an adventure.

* * *

I write this next part plainly, because I earned the knowledge honestly and I refuse to dress it up.

That night I lay in my narrow bunk in the room I shared with Pip—he slept through anything, bless him, even the wind now screaming around the eaves like a creditor at the door—and I listened to the tower talk. Old buildings speak in storms, and Gannet Rock had a vocabulary: the tick of the clockwork above, the boom of sea on the foundation, the rattle of some loose fitting on the gallery that Coral would certainly climb out and fix at first light, because that was the kind of man he was, fixing things in weather that would kill a postman.

And underneath it all, the barometer's verdict, falling and falling.

Four weeks. Possibly fewer. Possibly many more, if the season sealed the channel and the supply boat stood off and turned away, and we watched it go from the gallery with our hands up like castaways on our own fortress. Finite oil. Finite water. Finite everything, including each other's company, and I give us until roughly day five of a true siege before my father and Coral Amberdale found something irreconcilable to say to each other. Two men circling one dead woman's memory, one of them lying about her, the other refusing to speak of her—yes, I gave us five days, and I am a generous estimator.

Here is what I concluded somewhere in the small hours, with Pip's breathing steady beside me and the wind trying the windows like a burglar: the rock had stopped pretending to be a refuge. It had shown its second face. We were not sheltered; we were stored, like the oil and the water, against a winter that would spend us at its pleasure. And the boat that was supposed to save us was a rumor at month's end, and the sea between us and it was already learning to bite.

Trap. I stood at the window a while before sleep took me, watching spray blow sideways past the glass in the moonless dark, and I admitted it to myself the way you admit any true thing: reluctantly, and then completely.

But a strange thought followed it, the kind of thought that arrives at 3 a.m. wearing sensible boots: traps hold things in. They also hold things out. Whatever Corven Hale wanted from us, whatever he believed we knew, he would have to cross that same biting sea to get it, and the same storms sealing our exit were sealing his entrance. The barometer fell for everyone. The channel would close for everyone. If we were prisoners, then for one winter, so was the war.

I slept badly after that, but I slept.

In the morning the wind had backed and strengthened, and Coral was on the gallery at first light re-securing the loose fitting with frozen fingers, and Pip was asking to help, and my father was watching the southern horizon with a face I pretended not to study. And Coral, coming in stamping and red-handed with cold, paused by the barometer on his way to the stove and read it the way my father read scripture—with reverence, and with the understanding that it asks something terrible.

"Worse than I said," he announced to the kitchen generally. "If this holds, the great storm arrives inside three days. Maybe two." He hung his coat on its hook, squared it to perfection, and added, in the tone of a man reporting a delivery:

"So if anyone has business with their consciences, I'd see to it now. Storms have a way of bringing things ashore."

I laughed, because it sounded like a joke.

It was not a joke. I would learn that within the week, when I caught him burning letters in the stove—her letters—and stood in the doorway of the kitchen holding the truth of it like a coal I didn't dare drop.

But that was not yet. That morning there was only bread to bake, oil to count, water to measure, and three days in which the rock was still, technically, a refuge—and the barometer fell, and fell, like something being lowered into a grave.
