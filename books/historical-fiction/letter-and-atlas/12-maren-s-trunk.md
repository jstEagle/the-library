## 12. Maren's Trunk

The trunk had sat at the foot of my bed for twenty years the way a headstone sits in a churchyard—present, permanent, and not to be trifled with. Sea chest, iron-banded, her initials burned into the lid in a hand far too fine for the rest of her: *M.A.* Maren Amberdale, née Verrin, deceased these two decades and still, apparently, keeping secrets.

I had opened it exactly once, the week after they buried her, when I'd gone looking for something black to wear and found instead that she owned nothing black at all. She'd have taken that as a joke. I hadn't been able to laugh about it then, and I hadn't touched the thing since. You get good, living alone, at not touching things. It's a discipline, like winding clockwork. The hands move whether you attend to them or not.

But Aldous Verrin had said his piece over supper—or half of it—and then retreated to the guest bunk with the wounded dignity of a man who has just discovered that twenty years of silence did not, in fact, settle anything. Sable had looked at me across the table with those grey eyes of hers, cataloguing me again, filing me somewhere between *obstacle* and *resource*. And Pip had asked, with the terrible directness of children, "Was Aunt Maren brave?"

I'd said yes. I'd said it too quickly.

And now here I was at midnight, oil lamp turned low, kneeling on cold flagstones before a dead woman's luggage like a man come to beg forgiveness from furniture.

The lock was a simple thing. She'd never trusted locks—her father's house had been full of them, she'd told me once, doors within doors, everyone hiding ledgers from everyone else. She'd laughed when she said it. I'd thought it was a joke about rich people. It occurs to me now, twenty years late, that it may have been a confession.

I didn't force the lock. That felt like theft, and whatever else this was, it wasn't theft. Instead I pressed the hasp the way you'd press an old bruise, worked the keyhole with a hairpin of hers I'd kept in my coat pocket for reasons I never examined, and after ten minutes of profanity conducted mostly under my breath, the lid came up with a sigh of camphor and salt.

Clothes first. Her good wool dress, folded with military precision, which surprised me because Maren had never folded anything in her life; she'd shed garments behind her like a comet sheds its tail. Someone had packed this trunk for her. Or she'd packed it for someone. The distinction seemed suddenly important.

Beneath the dress: a shawl I remembered. A pair of gloves worn through at the fingertips from lamp work—I'd forgotten she used to help trim the wicks back when we were young enough to think two people could run a light together. And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth tied with string gone brittle as biscuit, a bundle of letters.

I sat down on the floor. My knees informed me of their opinion regarding midnight archaeology. I ignored them.

* * *

There were perhaps thirty letters. I knew the handwriting on most of them immediately—it was hers, that slanted, impatient script that leaned forward like a woman walking into wind. What stopped my breath was the handwriting on the others. Not Aldous's careful clerk's hand. Something harder. Compressed. The writing of a man who learned penmanship in a place where a crooked letter might be held against you.

I unfolded the oldest one. Dated eleven years before I met her.

*Mrs. Verrin,—Your terms are received and understood. Understand also that generosity is not my nature. You will have your fortnight and not a day more. After that, your brother's debts become his own flesh to pay with, and I am told he has soft flesh.—C.H.*

Corven Hale. Even then, even before I knew the name meant anything, the letters themselves had a smell to them—not physical, nothing so useful—but the way certain rooms smell wrong before you know why.

I read them in order, because disorder would have been worse. And what emerged, letter by letter, was a story I had never been told, starring a woman I had apparently never met.

She had been paying her brother's debts. For years. Aldous Verrin, the dignified man asleep one floor below me, had gambled or speculated or simply spent past the reach of the family money, and had fallen into the hands of Corven Hale, who ran contraband up that coast the way other men ran mail routes—reliably, seasonally, with a customer base that included half the magistrates. And Maren, nineteen years old and furious, had been buying her brother back instalment by instalment, with money whose source the letters made increasingly clear.

Because there it was, in her own hand, dated the spring before she left home forever:

*I have done what you asked. Names, dates, the cove at Harrow Point, the revenue cutter's schedule as your man wanted it. You will find Aldous's account settled and his paper returned to him, and if a single finger is laid on him afterward, every page of what I copied goes to the Collector at Wexmouth and to three newspapers besides. I am not my father's daughter in this one respect only: I do not negotiate twice.*

She had informed on them. On Hale's whole ring. To buy Aldous free.

I set the letter down on my knee and discovered my hands were shaking, which annoyed me. I am fifty-eight years old. I have carried hundredweight of oil up spiral stairs in gales. My hands do not shake for paper.

But it wasn't the informing that shook them. I'll be honest with myself, since no one else is available: some mean little corner of me had already suspected it, ever since Aldous stood in my kitchen and said *it was Maren, not me*, wearing grief like a borrowed coat. No. What shook me was simpler and much worse.

She had done it before she met me. Before Gannet Rock. Before me.

All those years I'd believed myself her refuge—the quiet harbor where a woman with a difficult family had come to be safe—I had been, what? An accident of geography? A lighthouse she'd steered for because the water behind her was full of rocks she'd made herself?

No. That's not fair, and she'd have told me so, in that voice she used when I was feeling sorry for myself, the one that could take the skin off a whelk. But fairness was not what I was feeling. What I was feeling was twenty years old and had been lying in a trunk at the foot of my bed the entire time, patient as ballast.

* * *

I read until the lamp guttered and refilled it without noticing I'd done so. Somewhere in the small hours I found the letter that undid me, and it was not dramatic. It was domestic. That's the trick of grief; it doesn't ambush you with thunder. It waits in the laundry.

*He is kind,* she wrote—to whom? There was no salutation. These last pages weren't letters at all but something rawer, drafts of a confession never sent, or a diary kept in the second person as though she couldn't bear to say *I*. *He is kind and he does not ask questions, which is either a virtue in a man or a wound, and I have not decided which. He thinks history happens to other people. He thinks the storms pass us by out here. I let him think it. Is that a lie, if the lie is the only roof I have to give him?*

I read that paragraph four times. Then I got up, walked to the window, and looked out at a sea so flat and black it might have been the inside of the trunk itself.

She had chosen me. That was the unbearable part. Not fate, not shipwreck, not the romantic nonsense I'd built our beginning out of over two decades of telling it to myself. She had read the coast, weighed her dangers, and picked the one rock where nobody from her old life would ever think to look—a keeper so far outside the world's business that even the smugglers' charts probably marked him as scenery. I had thought I was invisible to history. She had known it. She had married my invisibility the way you marry a house.

And she had never told me. Twenty years of winding the clock beside me, trimming wicks beside me, laughing at my jokes—the good ones and the terrible ones—and not once, not in all that weather, had she said *there are men who would kill me if they knew where I slept.*

Unless. Unless she had tried. Some nights she'd stood at that same window with a look I'd filed away as homesickness. Some evenings she'd started sentences and abandoned them mid-keel. I had, being a man gifted with peace and determined to defend it, always assumed the weather would pass.

It hadn't passed. It had been waiting in a trunk.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and indifferent. Below me I heard the sounds of the tower waking around strangers: Pip's cough, the clatter of Sable laying the fire like someone who'd done hard chores in bad light, Aldous moving with that deliberate quietness of his, the quietness of a man managing how much noise he's worth.

I should have gone down. Made porridge, rationed the tea, played the host I'd never volunteered to be. Instead I sat on the floor of my own bedroom with my dead wife's correspondence spread around me like wreckage, and I did the arithmetic of anger.

At Aldous, for letting her pay his debts with her safety and then, twenty years later, standing in my kitchen suggesting she'd been the coward. At Hale, for existing—for being the sort of man who writes *generosity is not my nature* to a girl of nineteen and means it as courtesy. At her, God forgive me, at her, for the lie of omission that had lasted longer than most marriages. And underneath all of it, at myself, because I had helped build the silence. I had been so grateful for the quiet. I had mistaken her discretion for contentment and called it love.

A knock at the door. Sable's voice, level as a spirit gauge: "Mr. Amberdale. The barometer's dropped since yesterday. Thought you'd want to know."

"Thank you," I said, and heard how strange my own voice sounded, and hated it.

A pause. "Are you all right?"

"I'm reading," I said, which was true, and insufficient, and she knew it. Her footsteps went away down the stairs, unhurried, and I understood that she had added the pause to her ledger. That woman misses nothing. It's exhausting. It's familiar. It's Maren all over again, and I don't know yet whether that's a comfort or a threat.

I gathered the letters back into their oilcloth. All but a few. Those—the ones naming names, the ones that proved what she'd done and why—I set apart on the bed, because a thought had arrived that I didn't invite and couldn't dismiss:

If Maren informed on Corven Hale, then somewhere in this tower, in this trunk, in the record of a life I thought I'd memorized, there might be proof of everything Aldous feared. Proof that could hang a man or save four lives, depending on whose hands held it.

I looked at the few letters I'd set apart. Then I looked at the stove in the corner, cold now, but patient.

Some things, I thought, a keeper burns to keep the light clean.

I picked up the first one.
