## 13. What Sable Saw

There are two kinds of men in the world, my father always said: those who lie to you, and those who lie to themselves so thoroughly that they've forgotten where the first kind went.

I had spent three weeks in Gannet Rock tower deciding which category Uncle Coral belonged to, and I was beginning to suspect the answer was *both*, which struck me as a feat of engineering on par with the Fresnel lens itself.

The morning it happened, I had risen early—not by choice but by inheritance. Pip had a cough that rattled like gravel in a tin cup, and I'd gone down to the store room for the last of the honey, rationing myself to one spoonful because the arithmetic in this place was merciless. Coral kept his stores written in a ledger with a hand so neat it looked typeset. Sugar: eleven pounds. Flour: enough for bread until February if nobody got greedy. Lamp oil: sufficient, provided the winter behaved itself, which winters on this coast famously do not.

I had learned to read that ledger the way other people read tea leaves. It told me everything about the man who kept it—except the thing I most wanted to know.

Because here is what I knew, and what I had known since the second night under this roof: Coral Amberdale sent money to an orphanage in Penzance every quarter, anonymously, in the name of a woman who had been dead six years. I had found the receipts folded inside his navigation almanac, of all places, as if charity were something you charted between tides. A coward does not do that. A cold man does not do that. And yet the man himself stood at his window each evening scanning the horizon like a heron waiting for a fish, giving away nothing, asking nothing, warming nothing in this tower except the lamp.

I wanted to know what burned underneath all that granite. So yes—I watched him. I make no apology for it. When you have grown up in a house where your father's silences could fill a cathedral, you learn to read the spaces between a man's words, and Coral Amberdale was mostly space.

That morning, the space did something interesting.

* * *

I had taken the honey and was climbing back up the iron stairs—the tower's spine, that endless spiral we had all learned to climb without thinking, our hands finding the rail in the dark the way sailors find rigging—when I smelled it.

Smoke. But not lamp smoke, not coal, not the particular char of Coral's porridge when his attention wandered to the window. This was paper burning. Paper has a smell all its own—sweetish, acrid, mournful—and once you've smelled a letter dying, you don't forget it.

I stopped on the landing below the kitchen. The stove door stood open, and through its grate I could see the orange pulse of flame eating something pale, and standing over it, his back to me, was Coral Amberdale holding a sheaf of letters with the expression of a man performing surgery on himself.

He didn't hear me. The wind had come around to the northeast overnight and was worrying at the tower like a dog with a bone; the whole structure hummed with it. Or perhaps he heard me and chose not to turn, which would have been its own confession.

Three letters remained in his hand. Three had already gone into the fire. I watched him read the topmost one—watched his lips move slightly, the way Pip's did when he sounded out hard words—and then watched his face do something I had never seen it do. It broke. Not dramatically; nothing about this man was dramatic. It broke the way ice breaks on a pond in spring, quietly, from beneath, all at once.

Then he fed it to the flames too.

"Those are hers."

My voice came out harder than I intended. Harder than I'd planned, and I had planned nothing, which was the problem. He turned slowly, and I will give him this much: he didn't startle, didn't drop the remaining letters, didn't reach to hide anything. He simply looked at me across the kitchen with the firelight moving in his eyes, and he said:

"Yes."

Not *what letters*. Not *they're private*. Not *this is none of your business*. Just *yes*, laid down between us like a card he couldn't take back.

I came the rest of the way up the stairs. "You're burning my aunt's correspondence."

"I'm burning part of it." He closed the stove door with a care that made my teeth ache. As though the matter were settled. As though the ashes cooling behind that little iron door were not evidence, not history, not the only unmediated voice of a woman I had loved and lost and been forbidden to grieve properly because my father forbade us all from speaking her name.

"Give them to me," I said. "The ones you haven't burned."

"No."

"You don't get to—" I stopped, breathed, started again in the tone I used on Pip when he tried to eat jam with his fingers. "Mr. Amberdale. You buried my aunt twenty years before she died. You married her and never asked her a single question about her life, and now she's dead and the questions are ash, and you are standing there finishing the job. Give me the letters."

Something moved behind his face. I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the one currency I refuse to counterfeit even when lying would serve me better: something moved behind his face, and it was not anger. It was closer to gratitude, which unsettled me far more than anger would have.

"You think I never asked her questions," he said.

"I know you didn't. Papa told—"

"Papa." He almost smiled. It was not a kind smile, but it wasn't cruel either; it was tired, the smile of a man lifting something heavy. "Aldous Verrin telling you what happened in my marriage is like a man who sank the ship writing the account of the voyage."

"And you'd write it differently?"

"Yes," he said simply. "But I'm not going to write it at all. That's rather the point of me."

* * *

We stood there while the fire ticked and settled. Outside, the gulls were arguing over something on the rocks—the eternal parliament of Gannet Rock—and the light fell through the salt-hazed glass in long grey bars. I made tea, because in this family tea was what you did instead of screaming, and he sat across from me and wrapped both hands around his cup like a man warming himself at a fire that gave no heat.

"I'll tell you what I can," he said at last.

"Which is half."

"Which is half." No flinch. I was learning that Coral Amberdale did not flinch; he simply declined, politely, immovably, like a lighthouse declining to move for weather. "The rest isn't mine to tell."

"Whose is it? Hers? She's dead. Dead women can't keep secrets. That's practically the definition."

"Maren kept secrets the way I keep this light." He looked toward the lamp room above us, up through the open stairwell, where the lens turned its patient circles whether anyone watched or not. "Not because she had something to hide. Because someone might be wrecked by it if she didn't."

"That's a beautiful sentence," I said. "It's also evasive."

"It's both. Most true things are."

I drank my tea and studied him and let the silence do work. Silence was a tool my father had taught me without meaning to—he wielded it so often and so clumsily that I'd learned both its uses and its limits. With Aldous, silence collapsed him; he'd fill any quiet with confession just to end the pressure. With Coral, silence seemed to be his native language, and I suspected I could sit here until the flour ran out and he'd say nothing further.

So I changed tactics.

"The letter that washed ashore," I said. "The one addressed to the keeper. You've read it a dozen times. I've seen you. Last night you had it out again after everyone was asleep—you thought the lamp shadow hid you, but the oil light carries up the stairwell."

For the first time, he set down his cup.

"What did it say?" I pressed. "Exactly. Word for word. Because Papa told us what he thinks it said, and Papa's version has conveniently placed every danger on Maren's shoulders and none on his own, and I have recently begun to notice that Papa's versions have a certain... editorial quality."

Coral looked at me for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere below, Pip coughed in his sleep, and we both glanced toward the sound—both of us, simultaneously, the same small involuntary motion—and I saw him register it too, see the child sleeping three floors down become a weight we suddenly shared.

"She informed on Corven Hale," he said finally. "Years ago. Before I knew her, or just after—Aldous won't say which, and the letter doesn't say. Hale's ring was broken by her information. Men went to prison. Hale didn't. And now Hale is free, and he holds the Verrins responsible, and the letter warns that they'd come here seeking shelter, and that Hale knows where they'd go."

"Why would Hale know that?"

"Because the letter says Maren told him herself, once—that if ever she had somewhere safe, it would be a rock with a light on it."

I felt that sentence go through me like a keel through shallow water. My aunt—the ghost, the name-not-spoken, the woman whose portrait in Papa's hallway had eyes that followed you with an expression I'd always privately called *amused pity*—had named this tower. Had imagined it. Had perhaps, in some season I knew nothing about, imagined the man in it.

"When did she tell him that?"

"I don't know."

"You're doing it again."

"I'm doing what I can," he said, and there was steel under the mildness now, the first steel I'd ever heard in him. "Miss Verrin. Sable. Listen to me, because I'll say this once. There are things in those letters that would put your father in a certain light. There are things that would put Maren in another light entirely. I have spent six years building my understanding of my wife out of memory, and memory is a liar with excellent manners. Now I have documents, and documents are worse, because they can't be argued with. I am deciding—letter by letter—what is true, and what is merely written down, and those are not always the same thing. When I finish deciding, you may have every page that remains. Until then, the fire and I have an arrangement."

"That's not half the truth. That's a tour of the fence around it."

"Perhaps." He rose, carried his cup to the basin, rinsed it with the economy of a man who had lived alone so long that waste had become physically painful to him. Then, with his back still to me, he added, so quietly I nearly missed it under the wind:

"There's one thing I'll give you now. Since you'll only pick the lock otherwise—I've seen how you look at my trunk."

Heat climbed my neck. I said nothing, which confirmed everything.

"Aldous knew," Coral said. "Whatever Maren did with Corven Hale—whatever she risked, whatever it cost her—her brother knew about it beforehand. That part isn't speculation. That part is in her own hand. So when your father tells you his sister acted alone, and he grieves her recklessness—remember that grief can be a performance, and rehearsal leaves marks."

He hung the dish cloth on its nail. Precisely. Squarely. A man restoring order to a small corner of the world because the large corners had caught fire.

"And the rest?" I managed.

"The rest is mine," he said. "For now. A man's allowed one trunk, Miss Verrin. Even a coward."

He went down the stairs toward the watch room, and I heard him begin the morning winding of the clockwork, the great ratchet breathing its slow counts, and I understood that the conversation was over in the way that storms off this coast are over—officially, technically, with the worst of it still out at sea, coming around.

* * *

I waited until he was deep in his rounds, then I did exactly what he predicted, because being predictable is sometimes a form of courtesy.

The trunk lock was old brass, older habits. I'd studied it for two weeks with a hairpin and patience, and it surrendered in four minutes. Inside: clothing smelling of cedar and sea salt, a wedding shawl gone ivory with age, a bundle of ribbon, and—beneath a false bottom I found by pressing the seams, because my father taught me card sharping before he taught me scripture—a thin stack of letters.

Most bore Maren's hand. Two bore another's—a bold slanted script I hadn't seen before, signed only with a *C.H.* that made my skin tighten.

And at the bottom, folded twice, a page in Maren's writing that began: *Aldous—if you are reading this, then you have already done what you always do.*

I read it twice. Then I sat down on the floor of Coral's bedroom, amid the smell of cedar and dead years, and read it a third time, and the third time was the worst, because by then I couldn't pretend I'd misunderstood.

My father had known. Had asked. Had agreed. And when the storm came, he had stepped behind his pregnant wife and called it protecting her.

I folded the letter along its original creases and put everything back exactly as I'd found it, because I wanted Coral to believe his lock had held. Let him keep his half-truths a few days longer. He'd earned that much, and I needed something else first: I needed to watch my father's face when I asked him one question, casually, at supper, in front of witnesses.

Outside the salt-glazed window, far out past the shoals where the water went from green to slate, a white shape showed against the grey—the wing of a gull, I told myself. Just a gull.

But gulls fly with the wind, and this one held station, motionless, the way a man stands when he's watching a house.
