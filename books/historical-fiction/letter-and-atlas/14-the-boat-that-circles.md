## 14. The Boat That Circles

I have kept this light for nineteen years, and in that time I have learned to read the sea the way a parson reads scripture—by heart, and with a certain professional skepticism. The sea says one thing with its mouth and means another with its hands. A smooth morning can be a lie. A gray evening can be honest as a debt collector. But a sail that will not come in and will not go away—that is a sentence I had never needed to translate before.

It was dusk when I saw her. I was in the lamp room trimming the second wick, Pip's polishing rag still folded on the sill where he'd left it that morning like a bird's nest of ambition, when the light caught something out past the shoal marker. Not a fishing smack. Fishing smacks work the water; they tack, they drift, they argue with the wind the way fishermen do. This was different. This was a sloop sitting off the rock at perhaps three miles, sails shortened to almost nothing, holding station like a man loitering outside a bank.

She was dark-hulled. She flew no colors. And she had been there, I would later reckon, since well before I noticed her—which meant she'd been watching us watch nothing, all afternoon.

My hands did not shake. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in my own head: Coral Amberdale's hands did not shake. They simply went about their business while the rest of him caught up, which is how it has always been with me. The body keeps the light; the mind arrives late, breathless, asking what happened.

* * *

I came down the stairs without hurrying, because hurrying is a broadcast, and found Aldous in the kitchen attempting to teach Sable a card game that appeared to consist mostly of his winning. Pip was asleep on the bench by the stove, one arm dangling, his cheek printed with the weave of the cushion. There is no sight in this world more likely to make a man lower his voice, so I lowered mine.

"There's a boat," I said.

Aldous looked up, and I watched the word land on him like a dropped anchor. "A boat."

"A sloop. Three miles out, maybe less. Holding position."

"Holding—" He stood up too fast and knocked the deck of cards to the floor. Fifty-two small failures scattered across my clean boards. He didn't stoop for them. "That'll be Hale. That'll be Corven Hale's men. They've found us."

"They've found the rock," I corrected. "Whether they've found you is a question I'd like answered before anyone starts packing."

Sable had risen too, silent as her father was loud. She was already at the window, angled so she could see without being seen—a trick I recognized from myself, which unsettled me more than the sloop did. "Describe her," she said.

"Dark hull. No flag. Short canvas. Sitting fat and patient." I paused. "Like a cat outside a mousehole."

"Then they know." Aldous's voice climbed toward the ceiling. "They know we're here, they're reporting back, by tomorrow there'll be two boats, then four, then—" He was reaching for his coat, which hung on the peg where I'd let it hang for eleven days now, growing into the wall like furniture. "We should take the dory. Tonight. Row for the mainland."

"In what?" I asked. "The tide turns against you at eight. You'd row all night to travel backward. By dawn you'd be further from shore than you are now, and tired besides, and Hale's men would find four exhausted Verrins bobbing in an open boat like corks in a pail." I said it mildly. I have found that mildness infuriates panicking men far more efficiently than shouting, and gives me time to think, which is the actual purpose.

"You don't understand what these men do," Aldous said.

"No," I agreed. "But I understand this rock, and this channel, and this weather, and you don't. So sit down."

He sat. It may have been the first order anyone had obeyed in my tower since the Lighthouse Board installed me, and I confess the sensation was not entirely unpleasant.

* * *

What I understood, standing at that window with the last gray light going out of the sky, was arithmetic. It has been the curse of my winter, this arithmetic—the rations divided by mouths, the oil divided by nights, the berths divided by souls. Now it added a new column: one sloop against one lighthouse.

A sloop cannot climb a rock. A sloop can, however, wait. And men who are waiting for something generally intend to take it or burn it, and both intentions are bad for the thing waited upon.

But there was another column, and this was the one that steadied me. We were visible to them—but only our lights were visible, and lights tell stories whether you mean them to or not. A keeper's light speaks. It says *all is well* when it burns steady and lawful. It says *distress* when it flashes wrong. Every man who works water knows the grammar, and Corven Hale's men worked water for their bread.

Which meant the tower could talk back.

"Sable," I said. "How many lamps do we have besides the big one?"

She turned from the window. She has her mother's face, I am told—I never met Maren's sister—and Maren's way of answering a question with a look before she spends any words. "Two hurricane lanterns. The one Pip uses in the stairwell. The kitchen lamp."

"And the storeroom light?"

"Yes."

"Good. From tonight, every light in this tower goes dark except the lamp itself. No lanterns in windows. No glow in the stairwell. If you must move after dark, you move by memory, and you mind the third stair, which is loose, and has been loose since 1889 despite four letters to the Board." I heard myself giving orders in the voice of my father, a harbor pilot dead thirty years, and decided not to examine that. "Hale's men will read our lights tonight. I intend to give them nothing to read but the lawful beam."

"And the beam?" Sable asked.

"The beam stays exactly as it is. On schedule, on character, boring as a sermon." I allowed myself the smallest smile. "A lighthouse that behaves strangely tells watchers something is wrong. A lighthouse that behaves perfectly ordinarily tells them either that nothing is wrong—or that whoever is inside is very good at pretending. I can't help which they choose. But ordinary costs them certainty, and certainty is what scouts are sent to fetch."

Aldous stared at me from the floor, where he was gathering his cards with the wounded dignity of a man whose whole life had just been described as a sermon. "You've thought about this."

"I've thought about fog," I said. "It's nearly the same subject."

* * *

We doused the tower room by room, and I will tell you honestly what it felt like: it felt like being buried. Nineteen years I had lived in a tower that glowed from within, warm yellow squares in the dark that I could see from the rocks below when I walked the landing stage at night—my own house waving hello to me. Now the windows went black one by one and the rock became indistinguishable from the sea, and I understood for the first time what the Verrins must have felt arriving here: that they had come to a place that did not wish to be seen, kept by a man who had made an art of it.

There is a difference, though, between hiding and choosing not to show yourself. I held onto that difference the way a drowning man holds a spar. It floats, barely, if you don't think too hard.

Pip woke during the extinguishing, confused, and cried a little—not loudly, the way children cry when they have learned that loud crying brings trouble. Sable gathered him onto her lap in the dark and I heard her murmuring the explanation she thought he could carry: *Uncle Coral is playing a game with the lights, love. A hiding game.* And Pip, sniffling, asked the question that went through me like a fishhook:

"Is someone looking for us?"

And Sable, after a pause long enough to break your heart if you were listening for it, said, "Yes. But they won't find us. Uncle Coral knows how to hide better than anyone."

Better than anyone. From the child's mouth it was praise. From the dark it was an indictment with a bow on it. Nineteen years of practice, girl. Ask me sometime what I was hiding from, and watch me fail to answer, because I have been failing to answer that question since before you were born.

* * *

I went up alone to keep the first watch. This was partly strategy—one man at a glass makes less noise than four—and partly because I could not sit in the dark kitchen with Aldous rehearsing his catastrophes. The man has a gift. Given silence, he will fill it with ruin, the way water fills a hole.

From the lamp room, the sloop was a shadow on shadows. The moon was down to a fingernail and the cloud cover was coming up from the southwest in long woolly ranks, so I lost her twice and found her once more by the faint blockage of stars where her masts stood. She hadn't moved. Patient as arithmetic herself.

I got out the glass and studied her until my eye ached. Two figures on deck, maybe three, moving without hurry. No boat swung at her stern davits that I could see—or maybe there was, half-lowered. That detail bothered me all night. A scout lowers her boat when she intends to send someone ashore. A scout that only circles doesn't need one. Half-lowered might mean nothing. Half-lowered might mean tomorrow.

At nine o'clock the lamp completed its turn, the great lens gathering its beams and throwing them out over the water in the old faithful wheel, and I watched the light sweep across the sloop's sails—one white flash of her canvas, gone, then again at the interval, gone. Like a hand passing over a prisoner's face with a lantern, checking, checking.

And then I saw it, and my stomach went cold and clear as seawater: as the beam crossed her, the figures on her deck stepped behind the mast. Both times. At the exact interval.

They didn't want to be counted. Which meant they knew I had a glass. Which meant they had scouted Gannet Rock before—knew its habits, its equipment, its keeper's routines—and had merely never had a reason to care who lived here until eleven days ago, when three Verrins washed up on my doorstep like something the tide owed me.

History, I have often said, happens elsewhere. Apparently elsewhere had gotten in a boat.

* * *

Near midnight, Sable came up the stairs without a lamp, feeling her way along the wall exactly as instructed, and stood beside me at the glass. She didn't ask permission. Nobody in this family asks permission; it must be a Verrin trait, like the widow's peak and the talent for making a man feel audited.

"Still there?"

"Still there."

"Pip's asleep again. Papa's pacing, which for him is sleeping."

I passed her the glass. She found the sloop faster than I had on my first attempt, which told me things about her childhood I chose not to inquire into. "Three men," she said presently. "No. Four—the fourth is at the tiller. They're standing watches. That's discipline, not panic."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning Hale doesn't rush. Mama used to say—" She stopped. The name of her mother sits oddly in her mouth; grief does that, it makes some words heavy as lead shot. She started over. "Maren used to say that about him. That his danger wasn't his temper, it was his patience. That he could wait a year to collect a debt because the waiting cost him nothing and cost you everything."

"Maren spoke of Corven Hale to you?" I tried to keep my voice at the temperature of the room. I am not sure I managed it. My wife's voice, alive in her niece's memory, describing the man who would hunt her family—there are doors in a conversation you don't see until you've walked through them.

"She spoke of everything to everyone except you, I think," Sable said, and handed the glass back, and left me with that, descending into the dark with her hand on the wall.

I have been angry at Maren for eleven days now, off and on, in the way you're angry at weather—generally, without a target. But in that moment, alone in the lamp room with the beam turning its endless wheel above me, I was angry at her specifically and precisely, the way you're angry at a person: she had known this man's habits and spoken them aloud to children, and she had sealed all of it away from me, and now her secrets were circling my rock at three miles' distance with four disciplined men aboard, and I was learning her the way strangers learn her—secondhand, in fragments, from people who loved her longer than I did.

Or thought they did. The ledger of who loved Maren best is still open, and I begin to suspect every entry in it, including mine.

* * *

Toward three in the morning the sloop finally moved. I watched her come about, slow and unhurried, and stand away to the southward, and I did not believe in her departure for one minute. Boats that circle return; that is what circling means. She had taken her report. Somewhere in the dark to the south, a message was already composing itself: *the light burns lawful, the tower shows nothing, the rock holds four souls, one old.*

Four souls, one old. Let them write it. An old keeper with a lawful beam and nineteen years of fog.

When the gray came up, thin and unwilling, I went down and put the kettle on and lit the kitchen lamp again, because the game of darkness ends at daylight and a household needs its mornings. Aldous appeared first, hollow-eyed, followed by Pip, who took one look at the restored lamplight and announced that the hiding game was over and he had won it, and I saw no reason to dispute the claim.

"We'll need rules now," I said, pouring tea into the chipped cups, counting silently—four cups, four souls, the arithmetic again, always the arithmetic. "Darkness at dusk, every night, until that boat stops coming back. Watch rotations. Sable takes the midnight glass, I take the small hours, Aldous takes dawn if his nerves permit."

"My nerves—" Aldous began.

"Your nerves are the most reliable instrument in this tower," I said, and meant it, and watched him decide whether to be insulted. "A barometer falls before a storm. Your nerves fall before trouble. I intend to read you daily."

Sable laughed—one short note, quickly closed, as if surprised to find it in her—and Pip drank his tea with the solemnity of a man signing articles, and outside the window the sea lay flat and innocent as a lied-about ledger, and the sloop was gone over the horizon, taking her report south to a patient man.

I stood at the window with my cup cooling in my hand and thought: *he bought a berth on the supply boat. Whoever nailed that message to my door wanted one of us to leave. And now his eyes are on my light, reading it nightly.*

Fear is a strange fuel. It burns dirty and it smokes and it stinks, but it burns. I set the cup down, and I went to get the logbook, and I wrote in it, in my plainest keeper's hand, the only strategy I have ever trusted:

*Sloop sighted at dusk, dark hull, no colors, holding off three miles. Doused house lights at 1940. Beam normal throughout. She departed south at 0300. She will be back.*

And under it, because no one audits a logbook but God and the Lighthouse Board, and neither of them knows my wife's name: *Let him read the light. He will find it says nothing. It has had practice.*
