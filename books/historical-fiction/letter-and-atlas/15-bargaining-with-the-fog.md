## 15. Bargaining with the Fog

The fog came the way bad news always comes to our family: quietly, from the sea, and at exactly the hour when no one was prepared for it.

I had been awake since before dawn, not because of any sound but because of a silence — the particular silence of my father's boots stopping outside my door and then moving on again without knocking. Aldous Verrin has never once in his life knocked on a door when he could hover outside it instead, radiating intention like a wet dog radiates smell. I lay in the narrow bunk Coral had given me — the one that still smelled faintly of lamp oil and, beneath that, of some older soap, Maren's perhaps, though nobody said so — and listened to him pace the gallery twice, three times, and then go down to the waterline, where he stood for the better part of an hour doing whatever it is cowards do at dawn.

Praying, probably. Though in my experience my father's prayers run less to *deliver us from evil* and more to *please let it be someone else's turn*.

By the time I came down, the fog had arrived in earnest. It did not roll in so much as settle, the way a great grey bird comes down over something it intends to keep. One moment there was the horizon, the roadstead, the whole wide catalogue of the world; the next there was nothing but white, and the rock, and us inside it like flies in a jar of milk.

"The sloop's gone," Coral said.

He was standing at the south window of the kitchen with a cup of tea he had evidently forgotten he was holding. He'd been holding cups of tea in forgotten places all week. It was becoming his signature, the way other men have watch chains or nervous coughs.

"Gone where?" I asked, though I knew the answer. Fog doesn't hide ships. Fog *is* ships, is everything, is nothing. The sloop hadn't gone anywhere. It had simply stopped being distinguishable from the rest of the weather.

"Out of sight," Coral said. "Which off this rock means out of mind, if you've any sense."

"And if you haven't?"

He looked at me then — that long, level look he does, the one I had spent two weeks cataloguing and still couldn't file properly. He has the kind of face that gives away nothing except by giving everything away slowly, over hours, like a tide going out and leaving things exposed you'd swear weren't there yesterday.

"If you haven't," he said, "you keep the light anyway. The light isn't for us. It's for whoever's fool enough to be out in that." He nodded at the window, at the white nothing pressing against the glass like a beggar's palm.

I want it recorded somewhere, in case anyone ever writes this down — and someone will, because I intend to — that I understood even then what kind of man Coral Amberdale was. I simply didn't yet understand what kind of man he was *to us*. Those are different questions, and it took the better part of a winter and one drowned smuggler to answer the second one.

* * *

Fog days in a lighthouse are a special species of punishment. You cannot work outside; you cannot see anything worth looking at; and the foghorn — God help us, the foghorn — went off at intervals like the rock itself clearing its throat before saying something unpleasant. Pip hated it. Pip hated it with his whole small body, clapping his hands over his ears and announcing that the rock was *shouting*, which was, I had to admit, exactly what it sounded like.

"It's telling other ships where we are," Coral explained, crouching down to the boy's level. "So they don't come find us by accident."

"Why would anybody come find us on purpose?"

There was a silence of precisely the wrong length. My father, bless his evasive heart, had gone very interested in the stove. And Pip — who is nine, and therefore sees through adults the way gulls see through fish — looked from one of us to the other and filed the silence away in whatever drawer children keep such things, to be opened later at the worst possible moment.

I took him up to the lamp room after that, mostly to get him away from the grown-ups' weather. We polished brass. There is a great deal of brass in a lighthouse and polishing it is the closest thing to prayer that housekeeping offers: repetitive, humbling, and effective mainly at revealing how many fingerprints you've left on things. Pip asked me questions the whole time, the way he does — why the lens was made of a thousand little pieces, why the light turned, whether the fog would eat us — and I answered what I could and invented the rest with a clear conscience, because I have decided that childhood runs on invention the way the lamp runs on oil, and I'll not be the one to let the flame gutter early.

It was while we were up there, both of us leaning on the rail, looking out at a world that had been erased, that I saw the rowboat.

Or rather, I saw where the rowboat wasn't. There was a patch of the landing stage below that should have been empty stone and was instead occupied — a dark shape at the foot of the stairs, beached and bumping gently against the rock with each swell, patient as a debt collector.

"Pip," I said, keeping my voice pleasant, "go down and tell your grandfather I need him."

"Is it pirates?"

"No."

"Is it the man from the boat?"

"Pip."

He went. I stayed at the rail and watched the shape below and thought about how, two weeks ago, the worst thing in my life had been my father's debts and the smell of the rooms we rented above the chandler's in Porthallow. I had been a girl with problems. Now I was a woman standing in a tower over a beached rowboat, and the difference between those two conditions is the entire distance between a story and its consequences.

* * *

They brought the boat's cargo up to the kitchen rather than open it on the landing stage, which tells you something about how all four of us were thinking by then. Nobody wanted to touch it. Everybody wanted to know what was in it. These are the two great engines of family life, in my experience, running always in opposite directions.

It was a crate. Small, plain, unmarked — the kind of box that has carried a hundred ordinary things into a hundred ordinary houses. Nailed to its lid was a folded paper, and nailed through the paper was a boat nail, driven clean, by someone with strong arms and all the time in the world.

Coral pried it off with the flat of a knife. He read it first. Of course he read it first; he reads everything first, letters and tides and people, and only then decides what the rest of us are permitted to know. But this time he read it and then, without a word, handed it across the table to me, and I understood that gesture too — understood that he was treating me as the second adult in the room, or possibly as the second suspect, and that with Coral Amberdale these may amount to the same thing.

The paper said:

*One berth aboard the supply vessel has been purchased. Paid in full. The holder will decide who travels. When the boat comes, ask for Corven Hale's compliments.*

No signature. No threat. That was the elegant part, the part I turned over and over in my mind for days afterward like a stone with something underneath it. There was no threat because there didn't need to be one. The threat was the arithmetic. Three berths, four souls — we had known that since the letter washed ashore, had carried that number around with us like a stone in the shoe. And now someone had reached across the water and bought the choice itself.

"He's not trying to kill us," my father said. His voice had gone thin. "You see that, don't you? If he wanted us dead he'd — he wouldn't bother with tickets."

"He's not trying to kill us," I agreed. "He's trying to make us do it ourselves. Or worse—" and here I looked at Coral, because I had already done the sum and I knew he had too, "—he's trying to make *you* do it. To us."

The kitchen got very quiet. Pip was in the corner with the cat — some half-wild thing that had adopted the rock and tolerated us as staff — and I watched my father register the boy's presence and lower his voice by half, which is Aldous Verrin's entire moral vocabulary: quieter, not better.

"She informed on Hale," my father said. "Maren. Whatever else is true, that's true, and Hale doesn't forget. This—" he tapped the paper, "—this is him being *civilized*. This is mercy, by his standards."

"Then his standards are a swamp," I said, "and I notice you know them awfully well."

"Sable—"

"How does a man buy a berth on a supply boat, Father? From Porthallow? In advance? Who does he know at the shipping office? Who does he know everywhere?" I heard my own voice climbing and made myself stop, because the truth under my anger was worse than the anger: I wasn't really accusing him of complicity. I was accusing him of being the sort of man whose old acquaintances might do this, and I couldn't prove him innocent, and neither, I suspected, could he.

Aldous sat down. He sat down the way he does everything lately, heavily, apologetically, a man perpetually arriving late to his own defense. "I owe Hale money," he said. "That's the whole of it. That's been the whole of it for years. Not this — I never saw this coming. But I'm the reason the door was ever open to him."

And Coral, who had stood through all of it with the patience of furniture, finally spoke.

"Nobody's deciding anything today," he said. "The boat doesn't come for weeks. The fog lifts or it doesn't. We keep the light, we eat our rations, and we don't let a piece of paper do our thinking for us." He picked up the note, folded it once, and put it in his breast pocket — next, I noticed, to where he kept the letter that started all this. He was building a collection. A dossier of the sea's correspondence. "Whoever holds that berth," he added, almost as an afterthought, "it won't be decided by Corven Hale."

But he said it looking at the window, at the fog, and I saw his hand press flat against his chest for a moment, over the pocket, the way a man touches a wound he's pretending isn't there.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I did what I always do with sleeplessness: I worked. I had taken to keeping my own ledger of the station — stores, oil, wicks, the slow arithmetic of winter — partly because it was useful and partly because numbers are the only conversation that never lies to me. I sat in the kitchen with a candle stub and my book, and the foghorn boomed its interval, and the house ticked and settled around me like a large animal dreaming.

Around midnight I heard Coral's door open and his feet on the stairs — up, not down. Toward the lamp room? No. Toward the room where he kept Maren's trunk, the locked one, the one he had opened once and relocked and not spoken of since. I gave him ten minutes and then I followed, because I am my mother's daughter and my mother's rule was simple: if a door closes quietly in this family, something behind it needs witnessing.

I didn't knock. Knocking is for houses with honest doors.

He was sitting on the floor with the trunk open and papers spread around him — not many, just a few, held close — and in the candlelight his face was doing something I had never seen it do. Not grief exactly. Grief I could have managed. It was more like a man reading a map of a country he'd lived in all his life and discovering the cartographer had lied about the rivers.

"You're burning them," I said. Because among the papers was a dish, and in the dish were ashes, and the ashes were recent.

"Some of them," he said, without turning. "Some aren't mine to keep."

"Whose are they?"

"They're hers." He looked up at me then, and there was something almost like a dare in it, or maybe just exhaustion wearing a dare's coat. "Everything in this tower is hers, one way or another. I married a woman and got a lighthouse thrown in, and twenty years later I'm still finding out which was the bride."

I crouched down across from him. The foghorn went off, long and mournful, and I felt it in the floorboards, in my teeth.

"My father says she betrayed Hale," I said. "That she informed on the ring. Is that what's in those letters?"

Coral was quiet for a long time. Long enough for the horn to sound again. Then he gathered the remaining papers together, squared their edges with a care that told me everything about how carefully he'd been handling the truth, and put them back in the trunk.

"What's in those letters," he said, "is between my wife and me. What's between you and me, Sable Verrin, is this: whoever bought that berth wants this family at each other's throats before the boat arrives, because a family at each other's throats will hand him the win without his lifting a finger. So we can tear each other apart down here in the dark, or we can wait for daylight. I recommend daylight."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It's a schedule."

I should have pushed. I tell myself I should have pushed. But there was something in the set of his shoulders — the particular stubbornness of a man guarding a grave — and I had learned enough about Coral Amberdale by then to know that you cannot dig truth out of him; you can only wait for the tide to leave it exposed. So I stood, and I left him there with his candle and his ashes and his wife's locked heart, and I climbed back down through the fog-haunted stairwells to my bunk.

And it was only lying there in the dark, listening to the horn count out the hours, that the horror of the day finally caught me — not the rowboat, not the nail, not even the note itself, but what it meant. Corven Hale had never needed to storm this rock. He had bought a ticket. He had turned our rescue into a raffle and our family into contestants, and whichever way Coral chose — whichever of us boarded that boat and whichever of us stayed behind on this stone with the storms coming — Hale would win. Escape itself had been weaponized. Even the leaving was his now.

We were four people in a tower, and the sea had sent us a message written in arithmetic: *one of you is mine, and you must choose which, and I will watch.*

The foghorn boomed. Somewhere out in all that white, the sloop waited with its sails furled and its patience endless.

And downstairs, or upstairs, or wherever he kept himself in the dark hours, Coral Amberdale was folding other people's letters into a locked trunk — and I began, that night, to wonder which of us he was really protecting, and from whom.
