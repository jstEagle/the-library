## 16. Three Berths, Four Souls

The arithmetic came to me the way most unwelcome things come to Gannet Rock: on the tide, without knocking, and refusing to leave until acknowledged.

I was in the oil store, counting tins by lamplight because counting them by daylight would have been honest, and honesty about one's stores is a kind of despair. Forty-one tins of colza. I had counted forty-two in October. I counted again. Forty-one. Either I had miscounted in October or a tin had walked off on its own legs, and since the only other legs on this rock belonged to three Verrins, I did not pursue the inquiry to its natural conclusion.

It was while crouched there among the tins, doing sums I didn't want to do, that the other sum arrived — the one with people instead of oil.

The supply boat comes at month's end. That is her habit, and habits are the only scripture a lighthouse keeper keeps faithfully. She is the *Merry Alice*, forty feet of stubborn oak skippered by a man named Tregowan who has not smiled since the death of Queen Victoria and shows no sign of intending to resume. The *Merry Alice* has a deck, a hold full of coal and flour, and room below for exactly three passengers. Three berths. I know this because I once asked Tregowan, idly, the way a man asks about things that will never concern him, and he held up three fingers and then went back to his pipe as though the conversation had cost him something.

Three berths.

Four souls.

I sat down on an upturned crate in the oil store and let that sit in my chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

You would think a man who lives alone with a clockwork mechanism would be good at arithmetic. The clockwork is nothing but arithmetic — escapements and gear ratios, weights and falls, all of it patient, all of it exact. If you tell a lie to a clockwork it stops telling you the time. I had spent twenty years trusting that kind of honesty, the kind that doesn't flatter, doesn't soften, doesn't pretend four goes into three.

And yet when the sum finished itself, I stood there feeling like a man who has been cheated at cards by his own hand.

Because here is what the letter had never said, and what I now understood it had been saying all along, in the way of letters written by frightened people: *come to the rock*. Not *come to the rock and be safe*. Come to the rock, where the supply boat calls once more before winter closes the channel, and after that no boat calls at all until spring. The rock was never the refuge. The rock was the hourglass. Every day we spent behind these walls was sand falling, and at the bottom of the glass were three berths on the *Merry Alice*, and above the glass were four of us, watching.

Someone stays.

I climbed the stairs to the lamp room without deciding to. My legs have their own opinions about stairs; twenty years of climbing them twice nightly has made the climb automatic, a thing my body does while my mind does whatever it likes elsewhere. That evening my mind was doing arithmetic with faces instead of numbers.

* * *

Aldous first. Maren's brother, sixty if he was a day, with the look of a man who has spent his life standing slightly behind himself. He ate his supper like a guest at a funeral — grateful for the food, apologetic for requiring it. When he spoke it was mostly about weather, and even the weather he spoke about apologetically, as though the fog were somehow his fault and he'd write to someone about it.

Could Aldous stay? A man alone on a rock through a winter of storms, keeping a light he'd never kept, in a tower belonging to a dead sister's husband who barely tolerated him? The ring wanted him — or believed they wanted him; I was no longer certain which Verrin the letter's author feared for. Leave Aldous here and I might as well nail a placard to the door: *One Marked Man, Slightly Damp, Free to a Good Storm.*

No. Whatever else Aldous was, he was Maren's brother, and there are debts that outlive the creditor.

Sable, then. His daughter. Now there was a sum that refused to balance. Sable Verrin looked at me the way I imagined auditors look at ledgers — patiently, thoroughly, expecting fraud. She had been on my rock nine days and she had already, I was nearly sure, counted my tins. She found things out. She had found out about the money I sent to the orphanage in Penzance — I don't know how, unless she went through my papers, which of course she had, because she was a Verrin and going through papers was evidently a family accomplishment, like perfect pitch or gout. She hadn't confronted me about it. She had simply begun looking at me differently, the way you look at a map when you discover the cartographer lied about the coastline.

Could Sable stay? She was thirty-odd, strong, clear-eyed, and quite possibly the only one of the four of us who could run this light through a winter without dying of it. Logically — and I am a logical man, God help me, logic is the only family I chose — Sable should be the one left behind. She and I together could keep the station. Two keepers are better than one. The Board itself would approve.

But Pip.

* * *

Pip was seven years old and had the sea-legs of a drunkard and the stomach of a poet, which is to say both betrayed him hourly. He had been seasick from the mainland to the rock and had spent his first two days among us lying in the bunkroom making small sounds of philosophical regret. On the third day he discovered the lamp room, and on the third day everything changed, though neither of us said so.

I caught him up there with his fingers on the Fresnel lens, and every rule I owned rose in my throat — and then I saw his face, which wore the expression I myself wear when I put my hands on that glass. Wonder, checked by guilt. I knew that face intimately. I shave above it every morning.

So instead of scolding him I handed him the chamois and showed him how to polish. You work in circles, always circles, following the curve of the prism, because the light travels in curves too and the glass remembers. He asked questions. Children ask questions the way gulls beg chips — constantly, shamelessly, with total confidence in the world's supply. Why does the light turn. What happens if it stops. Has anyone ever sailed into the rock. Have you ever saved anyone. Do you get lonely.

I answered all but the last. Some questions you answer with silence, and some silences you answer with a chamois and another circle.

He called me Mr. Amberdale for six days. Then, on the seventh, polishing his circle, he said "Coral," tried it out loud like a word in a foreign language, blushed ferociously, and pretended he hadn't. I pretended he hadn't either. We polished the lens and the pretense between us got thinner and warmer, like a fire being fed.

Now do the arithmetic again. Four souls. Three berths. And one of those souls is seven years old and calls me by my Christian name by accident and means it on purpose.

There is no version of the sum where Pip stays. Let the mathematicians object. Let Corven Hale and his smugglers and the whole cold machinery of consequence file their complaints. A child does not winter on a rock in the path of men who nail messages to doors. Any sum ending otherwise is not arithmetic; it is murder with better handwriting.

Which meant the choice lived between Aldous, Sable, and me.

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what I felt next, because this logbook — if it ever becomes a logbook, if I ever stop talking to gulls and start talking to paper — deserves honesty even when the honest thing is ugly.

What I felt was relief. A thin, shameful thread of it, down in the cellar of the feeling-house, where nobody visits. Because if the choice was between the three of us, then it was possible — just possible — that it need not be me. Twenty years I had arranged my life so that no one depended on me and I depended on no one, and I had called that peace, and now the sea had delivered me three people to be responsible for, and some cowardly clerk in the back office of my soul was already drafting excuses. *The keeper must remain with his light. Duty. The Board requires it.* All true. All convenient. The most dangerous sentences in any language are the true ones that arrive exactly when you want them.

I took that relief upstairs and made it look at the lamp. The lamp doesn't care about your feelings. The lamp burns or it doesn't, and if it doesn't, ships die, and your feelings about whose fault it is will be of great comfort to the fish. I wound the clockwork. I trimmed the wick. I lit up at sunset and sent my beam out over water the color of hammered pewter, and I thought: this light has kept me honest for twenty years. Don't let it stop now, just because the honesty hurts.

Then I went down and told them.

* * *

We gathered in the kitchen, which is the only room in the tower where sitting down is permitted without irony. Rain on the glass. The barometer steady, for once, as though the sky were giving us a moment's privacy.

"The *Merry Alice* comes at month's end," I said. "She takes three passengers. No more. Tregowan won't bend it — he's bent nothing since 1887."

Silence. The rain filled it.

"Three berths," Sable said. Her voice was flat in the way that flat voices are loudest. "Four of us."

"Yes."

"And the channel closes after?"

"Until spring. April, if the storms are ordinary. They haven't been ordinary in years."

Aldous set down his tea with exaggerated care, the way men do when they're afraid their hands will say something their mouths haven't approved. "Then one of us—" He stopped. Started again. "There must be another boat. A fishing vessel, a—"

"There isn't. There's Hale's sloop, and I don't recommend applying to it." I looked around the table. "I'm not telling you this to frighten you. I'm telling you because you deserve to hear it from me and not from the sound of an engine passing us by."

Pip was at the table too. I had debated sending him to bed and decided against it — children smell omissions the way sharks smell blood, and a child told nothing will imagine worse than a child told something. He had gone very still, the way he did when the polishing was delicate, and he was looking at me with those grave eyes that missed nothing, absolutely nothing, and I understood suddenly that he had already done the sum. Children do arithmetic faster than adults. Adults have practice at not finishing the problem.

"Who stays?" Pip asked.

And the kitchen went quiet enough that I could hear the wick in the paraffin stove breathing.

"Nobody's decided," Sable said quickly.

"It's decided," Pip said. "Somebody has to. It's just maths." He said *maths* the way you'd say a swear word you'd learned from sailors. "Is it Papa? Is it Grandfather? Is it—" and here his eyes came back to me, and I watched him decide not to finish the sentence, and I have been shot at by no one and threatened by no one in my entire careful life, but I tell you that unfinished sentence did more damage than a broadside.

"It will not be you," I said. "That much is decided, and I've decided it, and I don't unmake decisions."

"You can't promise that," Sable said. "You don't control the boat."

"I control this rock. While you're on this rock, no harm comes to that boy. After the boat takes him, his harm is Tregowan's problem, and Tregowan has survived worse than smugglers. He's survived the Admiralty."

That earned half a smile from Sable, gone as fast as it came. Aldous was staring into his tea as though the leaves might arrange themselves into an answer. And I sat there under Pip's gaze, wearing my promise like a coat that fit in the shoulders and nowhere else, because I had promised the boy safety and had promised no such thing to anyone else, and everyone at that table knew it, including the boy, especially the boy.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, after the middle watch — I stood in the lantern and let myself think the thought I'd been refusing all day.

The letter washed ashore addressed to *the keeper*. Written by a hand that was not Aldous's — I had compared them, night after night, the way you compare a suspect's boots to a footprint. Someone outside our walls knew the Verrins would come here. Someone knew the schedule of the supply boat, or guessed it, or built the trap around it deliberately. Three berths, four souls: perhaps that wasn't the sea's cruelty at all. Perhaps it was the design. Perhaps whoever wrote that letter wanted exactly this — a family reduced to arithmetic, turning on itself, each member weighing the others in the dark. Divide and the rock divides itself.

Or perhaps I was flattering my troubles with intelligence they didn't possess, and it was only bad luck, and bad luck needs no author.

I honestly couldn't tell. That was the worst of it. I stood in the rotating beam with its long breath of light and dark, light and dark, and I could not tell whether I was a keeper or a piece on somebody's board.

Below me the tower slept: Aldous snoring with the guilty regularity of a man avoiding dreams, Sable awake — I could tell by the absence of sound; she slept like a cat, in sudden installments — and Pip dreaming whatever seven-year-olds dream when the adults have started doing sums over their heads.

Month's end. Eleven days.

Eleven days to learn which of us the sea intended to keep.

I wound the clockwork, and the gears took up their patient counting, and for once I envied them: they never have to choose. They only have to go on.
