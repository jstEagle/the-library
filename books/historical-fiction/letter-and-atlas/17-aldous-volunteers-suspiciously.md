## 17. Aldous Volunteers, Suspiciously

The announcement came at breakfast, which was the first suspicious thing about it. Aldous Verrin had not volunteered for anything in the eleven days he'd been under my roof — not dishwater, not lamp-trimming, not even the carrying of his own grandson up stairs the boy could have climbed himself if left to his pride. He was a man who treated labor the way other men treat weather: something that happened nearby and ought to be waited out.

Yet there he stood at my table, holding a cup of tea he'd let go cold out of habit rather than any respect for the leaf, and said, with the air of a man laying down a trump card he'd been sitting on since the deal began:

"I'll stay."

Sable's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. Pip looked up from the biscuit he was dismantling with forensic patience.

"Stay," I said.

"When the boat comes. The three berths. You take Sable and the boy, and I stay on the rock." He delivered it as though the plan had been obvious all along, and perhaps in his head it had been — heads like his have a way of furnishing themselves with noble furniture only after the guests arrive. "It's the sensible arithmetic. I'm the one Hale wants. The old score is mine."

"The old score," I said, "is Maren's."

Something crossed his face then — quick, like a fish under ice — and I would have given a month's oil ration to have netted it and held it to the light.

"All the same," he said. "I'm the Verrin. It should be me."

* * *

Now, I want to be fair to the man, because fairness has become a kind of discipline with me this winter, the way winding the clockwork used to be. There is a version of this story in which Aldous Verrin behaves nobly. A man offers his life for his daughter and grandson; the arithmetic of sacrifice comes out clean; the sea, or God, or the reader nods approval. I have read that story. I may even have believed in it once, back when I believed history happened to other people.

But I had spent twenty years alone with a clockwork that taught me one lesson above all others: when a mechanism starts doing something it has never done before, you don't applaud it. You look for what's jamming it.

Aldous had been afraid from the day he landed. Afraid of the fog, afraid of the sloop we'd seen circling, afraid of the dark stretch between supper and sleep — I'd hear him turn over and over in the bunk below mine, a man trying to outrun his own heartbeat by changing positions. Fear like that doesn't convert to courage in a fortnight. Fear like that converts to strategy.

And there was the matter of the letter. I had told no one of my suspicion about the handwriting — that the letter naming the Verrins, the letter that sent them fleeing to my rock in the first place, was written in a hand too careful, too schooled, to be Aldous's hurried scrawl. Whose hand it was I still didn't know. But a man who volunteers to stay behind on a rock where a smuggler-king's revenge is scheduled to arrive is either a saint or a correspondent, and I had shared a table with Aldous Verrin long enough to rule out sainthood.

So I did what any keeper does with an anomaly in the works. I watched it.

* * *

He made his preparations the way a guilty man does everything: thoroughly, and in plain sight, so that no one thinks to look closer. He mended the old oilskins hanging by the door — actually mended them, with stitches that would hold, though he'd claimed rheumatism in his fingers whenever a pot needed scrubbing. He asked me, casually, over the washing-up, how long the rowing distance was from the rock to the mainland headland in calm water. Casually, mind you, the way a man asks about the price of a horse he has no intention of buying.

"Three hours, if the water's kind," I said. "Four if it isn't. Five if it kills you."

"And the boat — the supply boat — she puts in at month's end regardless?"

"Regardless of anything short of a gale. Captain Pell keeps his schedule the way monks keep their hours."

He nodded at that, slowly, filing it away somewhere behind his eyes, and I understood that whatever Aldous was planning, it had a tide table attached to it.

I might have let it lie. I want that recorded too — that part of me, a large part, would have been perfectly content to let the man row off into his own consequences, provided he did it without taking my ledger of Maren's letters along, or leaving my granddaughter-in-law to learn of it by his absence. But the tower is small. Small towers make small secrets, and small secrets make loud noises when they break.

Besides — and here is the thing I keep coming back to, the splinter in the whole business — he'd said *the old score is mine.* And every night since the sloop appeared, Aldous had gone out onto the gallery deck after the others were abed. I knew because I counted footsteps the way I count everything else: his boots on iron, four turns, a stop at the north rail, four turns back. Every night. A man saying goodnight to the sea does not keep such regular office hours.

On the twelfth night, I followed him.

* * *

There is no art to shadowing a man inside your own house. You simply know the boards that speak and step where they don't — knowledge I'd acquired over years of not disturbing my own solitude, which turned out to be the exact same skill as spying on family. I gave him his ten minutes' start and came up the stairwell after him, keeping to the wall where the iron is riveted tightest.

The gallery door stood open to the night. Cold came through it like a debt collector.

Aldous stood at the north rail, exactly where the arithmetic of his habits predicted, but he was not looking at the sea. He was looking at his hands. In one of them was a folded paper, and in the other a matchbox, and between those two objects hung a hesitation so complete it seemed almost devotional.

Well, I thought. Either he's about to burn evidence, or he can't bring himself to light his pipe in this wind, and both readings tell me plenty.

I stepped out onto the gallery, and the door's hinges announced me the way honest servants do, and Aldous started so badly the matchbox went over the rail and into the dark, where the sea accepted it without comment.

"Coral." His voice climbed half an octave and tried to pretend otherwise. "Couldn't sleep."

"Neither could I," I said, which was true in the sense that I hadn't intended to. "Cold night to be reading."

"It's nothing. A letter. Old."

"Maren's hand?" I said, and watched him.

And there it was again — that fish under the ice. Quick, silver, gone. "Why would you say that?"

"Because everything else in this tower is hers eventually. The cold gets into her things. The damp finds her books. It stands to reason her brother would carry her around too." I kept my voice level, keeper's voice, the voice I use on gulls and machinery. "What does it say, Aldous?"

"Nothing that concerns you." He folded the paper away into his coat with a motion that was meant to look careless and managed only to look protective. "It's private correspondence. Between family."

"You've been standing at this rail every night for a week reading private correspondence by moonlight. Forgive me if I've developed opinions."

He turned to face me then, and the moon gave me his face in full, and I will say this for him: he did not look like a schemer. He looked like a tired man of sixty-odd years whose face had been organized entirely around apology — brows built for wringing, mouth built for the sentence beginning *if only*. That face had fooled better observers than me, presumably. It had certainly fooled Maren, once upon a time, or she'd never have taken the blame he now claimed was hers.

"I mean to settle it," he said quietly. "That's all. Whatever Hale believes, whatever he's owed — I mean to go out to him myself, man to man, and tell him the truth of it."

"The truth being?"

The pause lasted three waves. I counted them against the rocks below.

"The truth being what needs telling," he said, which is the answer of a man who has rehearsed several truths and not yet chosen among them.

"Aldous." I came to stand beside him at the rail, close enough that courtesy required he look at me. Below us the sea worked its endless ledger, black entries on blacker pages. "If you row out to that sloop, you are not settling anything. You are delivering yourself. Hale bought a berth on the supply boat to show us he controls our leaving. Do you imagine he'll be gentler about controlling your arriving? A man who engineers a choice like that doesn't want conversation. He wants custody."

"He wants the traitor."

"He has one already," I said. "She's buried on the mainland under a stone with my name carved next to hers, and she informed on him twenty years ago, and if you believe otherwise then you're either lying to me or lying to yourself, and frankly the second frightens me more."

His jaw worked. The wind pulled at his collar. And for one moment — one — I thought he might say it, whatever *it* was, the whole of it, unburden himself on this freezing gallery the way men do when the night is dark enough and the listener patient enough. His mouth opened. The fish rose toward the surface of the ice.

Then he shut it, and said, "You don't know what she was to him," and walked past me into the tower, and took his guilt to bed with the rest of us.

* * *

I stayed at the rail a while after. This is the trouble with suspicion: it is a lens that magnifies everything and resolves nothing. I had seen a man hesitate over a match. I had heard a man decline to finish a sentence. Neither of these is a crime, and both of them, committed by a different man, might have been grief. Grief hesitates over matches too. Grief reads dead women's letters by moonlight because the daylight versions of them hurt worse.

But grief does not mend oilskins. Grief does not ask the rowing distance to the headland. And grief, above all, does not volunteer for the one berth that keeps it closest to the danger it claims to fear — unless the danger is not what it fears at all, but what it wants.

I ran it through again, the way I run the clockwork, gear by gear. Suppose Aldous meant to stay behind genuinely. Then the plan was simple and stupid: wait out winter on a rock within reach of a vengeful smuggler, armed with mended oilskins and good intentions. I could prevent that stupidity by force if I had to; I had carried drunker men off this rock than Aldous Verrin.

Suppose instead he meant to leave early — before the boat, on some raft of his own arrangement — and meet Hale on terms he fancied favorable. Terms that involved, perhaps, handing over something. A name. A paper. An explanation shaped to save his own skin at whatever cost to the memory of his sister, or the safety of whoever remained.

Or suppose — and this thought arrived last and sat down heaviest — suppose the letter that brought them all here was his writing after all, disguised by care, and the whole flight to Gannet Rock had been a maneuver. Get to the rock. Get to whatever Hale believed Maren had hidden here. Use the keeper, use the child, use the very storm season itself as cover for a search he couldn't conduct on the mainland with Hale's eyes everywhere.

I had no proof of any of it. That was the devil of the thing. I had a fish-shaped flicker of expression and a matchbox at the bottom of the Atlantic and a man who slept too regularly for someone carrying his conscience.

Below me, faint through the iron of the deck, I heard the sounds of the sleeping tower reorganize itself: Pip's small cough, Sable's silence — that eloquent, feline silence of hers, which I had learned to read as either sleep or surveillance — and Aldous turning over, once, twice, and then settling into that guilty snore.

Eleven days until the boat. Ten, now, by the clock downstairs, which had ticked past midnight while I stood there learning nothing except how much I didn't trust my wife's brother, and how much it cost me to admit it, because distrusting him felt like a second betrayal of Maren — as if loving her obliged me to love the wreckage she'd come from.

I went down the stairs quietly, stepping where the boards don't speak, and paused outside the room where the children slept — where Pip slept, and Sable pretended to. Through the door, thin as thread, came a sound I hadn't heard before: Sable's voice, very low, talking to the boy. Not a lullaby. Something quieter and more serious. I caught only the tail of it, five words through the seam of the door:

"—and if Papa goes first—"

I did not stay to hear the rest. Some sentences you shouldn't overhear, because once heard, you can't unknow what the family has already begun to prepare itself for.

I wound the clockwork, though it wanted nothing, and stood a while listening to the gears count down hours I no longer trusted anyone in this tower to spend honestly.

Ten days. And now, apparently, two plans for staying behind: mine, which was to protect them all —

and somebody else's, forming in the dark, in a handwriting I was beginning to think I'd seen before.
