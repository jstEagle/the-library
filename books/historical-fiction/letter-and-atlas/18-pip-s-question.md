## 18. Pip's Question

Children are like gulls in one respect only, and it is not the flattering respect. A gull does not care what you say. A gull watches what you do, and where your hands go, and whether your pockets hold bread. I had spent three weeks believing that Pip Verrin, aged nine and mostly elbows, absorbed nothing from the conversations conducted over his head — that he was a small piece of furniture with a runny nose, present in the room the way a stool is present, useful for sitting and otherwise invisible.

I should have known better. Invisibility was my trade. I knew precisely how much a quiet person in a corner sees.

It happened on a Tuesday, though at Gannet Rock the days had long ago surrendered their names to the weather, so perhaps it happened on a Windy. The barometer had been sulking all morning — not falling, exactly, but hovering in that indecisive way that made me trust it less than if it had committed to something dramatic. Aldous was in the oil store doing his slow inventory of things that did not need inventing, which he performed daily as a form of penance or hiding, I had not yet decided which. Sable was above, in the lamp room, cleaning the lens with a devotion that bordered on usurpation. And Pip was supposed to be mending a net.

The net lay abandoned on the gallery floor, looking like something a very large fish had rejected.

I found the boy in the log room, sitting on my own stool, reading my own logbook. Not the official one — the private ledger where I kept accounts of stores, of coal, of oil, and, since the letter came ashore, of other arithmetic altogether. He heard my boots before he saw me, which was more than could be said for his father, and he snapped the book shut with the guilty speed of a boy who has not yet learned that being caught is survivable.

"That's mine," I said.

"I wasn't reading it."

"You were holding it open with your thumb to keep your place." I held out my hand. He gave it back, and I noticed his fingers were cold, and I noticed that I noticed, which was becoming an inconvenient habit where this child was concerned.

He didn't leave. That was the thing about Pip. Other people, when caught near my things, retreated with murmured apologies. This boy stood his ground on the flagstones with his chin up and his eyes wet, and looked at me the way Sable sometimes did — like a puzzle whose pieces kept refusing the obvious picture.

"You can read?" I asked, because silence between us had never once survived longer than a minute, and because I am weak.

"Father taught me. Before." *Before* was the word the Verrins used for everything that had gone wrong, deployed like a tarpaulin over wreckage. "Mostly. Some words I make up."

"Some words aren't worth getting right," I said. It was meant kindly. It came out like a sermon. I was, I reflected, getting worse at conversation the longer I lived alone, like a blade left unsharpened until it would no longer cut butter.

Pip scuffed his boot against the flagstone. Then, without any warning at all, in the way children deploy their most dangerous ordnance:

"Are you going to be the one who stays?"

* * *

I want it recorded — in some log, somewhere, in a hand steadier than mine — that I did consider telling him the truth. There was a moment, maybe two seconds wide, where I stood there with the ledger under my arm and the whole ugly arithmetic laid out between us, three berths and four souls, and thought: *he already knows. The worst is done. Give him the dignity of the plain answer.*

And then I opened my mouth, and out came the lie.

"Nobody's decided anything yet," I said. "These things take time."

Which was false on every axis available to me. Somebody had decided — me, alone, at two in the morning, standing in the lamp room watching the beam swing its patient circle while the arithmetic turned itself over and over like a stone in surf. Pip and Sable on the boat. Aldous, God help us all, wherever Aldous went. And myself, staying, because the light needed keeping through the winter storms and because I had begun to suspect, in the wordless way one suspects a change in wind, that staying was the first honest thing I'd done in twenty years.

But I said *nobody's decided*, and Pip looked at me, and I watched him decide not to believe me.

"Sable cries at night," he said. "In the little room. She thinks I'm asleep."

"I expect she's tired."

"She's not tired-tired. She's scared-tired." He considered his own taxonomy and found it sound. "And Father keeps saying *it will be arranged*, which is what he says when he doesn't want to arrange anything." The boy's voice did something terrible then — it dropped into an imitation of Aldous's cadence, precise and merciless, the way children imitate the adults they love most and forgive least. "*It will be arranged, Pip. Sit down, Pip.*"

"Pip—"

"There's four of us," he said. "I counted the berths on the supply boat when we came. There's a bench along each side and the skipper said six men work her, so if she carries passengers she carries them on those benches, and I sat on one and it fits three. Three and three." He held up fingers, as though I might dispute the mathematics. "Three benches. Four of us."

I have faced squalls that took the paint off the tower in sheets. I have stood on the gallery in February with spray freezing on my beard and felt nothing worse than cold. Nothing in nineteen years of keeping Gannet Rock had prepared me for a nine-year-old explaining boat seating to me with wet eyes and absolute clarity.

"Boats carry more than they're rated for," I managed. "In calm weather."

"The channel isn't calm in winter. You said so. You told Father the channel closes after the equinox gales and won't open till spring, and he went white as the foam, and you pretended not to see him go white, but I saw." His chin trembled once and he mastered it, visibly, with effort, and I understood that this child had been mastering himself for months now, alone, in rooms full of adults who believed he was furniture. "So whoever stays has to stay a long time. Till spring. Alone."

"Yes," I said. Because I could lie about decisions and dates and arithmetic, but apparently not about that. Not about the length of a winter on a rock.

"Alone how long? Since—" He stopped. Recalculated. Children recalculate constantly; it is why they exhaust us. "How long have you been here?"

"Nineteen years come spring."

His face did something I will be turning over for the rest of my life. It wasn't pity, exactly, and it wasn't horror. It was the look of a boy doing sums beyond his years and arriving at an answer he wished were wrong. Nineteen years, his face said. Alone, his face said. *Then it should be you who knows how,* his face did not quite say, but hovered near saying, and then flinched away from, because even Pip had a floor past which he would not tread.

"Is it very bad," he asked instead, "being alone that long?"

And there it was. The question underneath the question, the one he'd actually climbed down from the gallery to ask, the one he'd carried around all day like a hot stone. Not *who stays*. But *what does staying cost, and can a person pay it.*

I thought about lying again. I want that recorded too. I thought about it and found my stock of lies — never plentiful, never good — entirely spent. So I gave him the truth, or the portion of truth a child can carry without dropping it.

"It's bad in ways you don't expect," I said. "And fine in ways nobody believes. The bad isn't the dark or the storms. Storms are just work. The bad is that you stop talking out loud, and then when you do talk, your voice sounds like a stranger's. And one day you realize you've started arranging the day so there'll be something to tell the gulls, because the gulls are the only ones listening, and then—" I stopped myself. I was describing my own life to a nine-year-old with a runny nose, and somewhere in it there was a confession I hadn't intended to make.

"And then?" Pip prompted, relentless.

"And then either you come down off the rock," I said, "or the rock comes up inside you, and you can't always tell which has happened."

He nodded slowly, absorbing this, filing it among whatever strange cargo children carry. Then he said, "Sable says you chose it."

"Did she."

"She says nobody ends up on a rock by accident. She says even shipwrecks are choices somebody made earlier." He looked up at me, guileless and devastating. "Did you choose it?"

* * *

I sent him up to help Sable with the lens, on the pretext that brass does not polish itself, which is true, and that I needed to check the oil store, which was not. He went willingly enough — the lamp room had become his kingdom since I'd first put a cloth in his hand, and I had watched, these past weeks, with a wariness I mistook for prudence, as the boy learned the names of the prisms and the order of the watch and the particular song the clockwork makes when the weights need winding. I had taught him without deciding to teach him. That is how it goes, I think, with the important things.

In the oil store I found Aldous counting tins he had counted yesterday, moving each one two inches to the left and back, a man performing motion in place of decision. He glanced up with the expression he wore whenever I entered a room — half apology, half assessment, the look of a man perpetually calculating whether today was the day I'd throw him off the rock.

"Barometer's sulking," he offered.

"It does that."

"Coral." He set down a tin. "About the boat. When it comes. I've been thinking—"

"Don't," I said. "Whatever arrangement you're building in there, don't build it out loud yet. Not with the boy hearing everything."

Aldous blinked. "Pip? He doesn't—"

"He counted the berths," I said, "the day you arrived. He's been carrying the sum ever since, waiting for one of us to be honest enough to do it with him. We're the only ones who haven't noticed, Aldous. Him and the gulls."

My brother-in-law went the color of the fog outside. He opened his mouth, closed it, and returned to his tins, and I left him there among the oil and the cowardice, both of which he hoarded against winter.

That evening the four of us ate barley and salt cod in the kitchen, and Pip sat across from me and did not eat, and watched me over the rim of his bowl with the patience of a creature that has learned all the waiting there is. Sable talked logistics at her father — water casks, wool blankets, whether the supply boat could be signaled early — and Aldous answered in his *it will be arranged* voice, and I kept my eyes on my plate like a man with nothing on his conscience, and under the table, unnoticed by everyone except the person who noticed everything, Pip's small cold hand found the hem of my sleeve and held on.

* * *

I write this now at the end of that same day, because the keeper's discipline is to log events while the ink of them is still wet, and because my hand wants something to do besides shake.

Here is what I did not tell the boy, and what I will set down here where no living soul reads it: I have decided. The arithmetic finished itself days ago, in the dark, in the lamproom, and the answer is that Pip and Sable take the boat, and Aldous takes whichever berth his conscience can squeeze into, and I stay. I stay because the light must be kept, and because a man who spent nineteen years learning to be alone can spend one winter more, and because — though I would have lied to Pip about this too, badly, the way I lie about everything that matters — someone in this family has to be the one who stands still when the sea comes for it.

Maren stood still once, in her way. Took the blame and the danger onto herself and let the rest of them sail clear. I spent twenty years not knowing that about her. Now that I know it, I find I cannot despise the choice. Only the silence that surrounded it.

So: Pip and Sable aboard. The rest arranged in its season.

I had just blown out the lamp and got one boot off when I heard the gallery door, and then small feet on the iron stairs, and Pip appeared in my doorway in his nightshirt with his arms wrapped round himself, and I braced for the nightmare, the thunder, the ordinary machinery of childhood at night.

Instead he said, "You lied to me today."

I sat back down on the bed with both boots on. "Yes," I said. "I did."

"In the log room. About the deciding." His voice didn't accuse. It stated, the way the barometer states, without malice and without mercy. "Sable says you lie like a man who's never practiced."

Out of the mouths of babes and sucklings, and also out of the mouth of this particular babe, who had clearly inherited the family talent for evisceration. I rubbed my face. "What do you want me to say, Pip?"

"I want you to say it proper." His chin was going again, and his eyes were bright, and he planted his feet on my floorboards like a man taking a sounding. "If it's you that stays, say it proper, so I know. Because Father lies and Mother—" he stumbled, corrected, "—because everybody lies soft, and then the boat comes and nobody said anything true and it's too late. Say it proper. Please."

And I sat there, an old man in one boot, undone completely by a child asking for the one currency I had hoarded all my life and never spent: the plain truth, spoken aloud, on time.

I drew breath to give it to him — God help me, I was finally going to give it to him — and that was when we both heard it: boots on the gravel path below the tower, quick ones, and then Sable's voice calling up through the stairwell, tight and strange, that a supply crate had come ashore on the morning tide, unscheduled, unmarked, addressed in a hand neither she nor I had seen before.

Addressed, she said, to me. From an orphanage on the mainland.
