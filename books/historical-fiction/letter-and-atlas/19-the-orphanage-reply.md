## 19. The Orphanage Reply

The supply crate came ashore the way all good things come to Gannet Rock: badly, and with a certain amount of swearing.

I had signaled the *Merry Alice* with two flags and a prayer, as I did every third week, and she had answered with a crane, a rope, and a crate that swung over the landing stage like a condemned man hesitating at the gallows before dropping the last six inches onto the stone with a crack that I felt in my teeth. The boatman, a fellow named Tregaskin who communicated exclusively in gestures and weather complaints, shouted something that the wind ate whole, and then they were gone again, and the rock was ours.

Ours. I had begun saying that word without noticing, which is the sort of thing a man ought to notice.

"Mind your fingers," I said, because Pip had already pried up one corner of the lid with a chisel he had no business holding. "There's nails in there with ambitions."

"There's always nails," Pip said. He was eleven going on foreman. He had a way of looking at crates the way other boys looked at fairgrounds. "Uncle Aldous says supply days are the only days worth getting up for."

"Your Uncle Aldous says a great many things." I said it mildly enough. Mildness had become my specialty these past weeks, a kind of mortar between stones that didn't otherwise fit together.

Aldous himself appeared at the tower door with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, wearing the expression of a man perpetually surprised to find himself somewhere and displeased by the finding. Sable came behind him carrying the inventory ledger — her ledger now, though she kept it in my bookkeeping hand's territory out of some courtesy I hadn't asked for and couldn't quite refuse.

"Flour?" she said.

"Flour, salt pork, lamp oil if they remembered, paraffin if they didn't."

"And?"

"And whatever else the Admiralty saw fit to remember a lighthouse exists." I handed down the crowbar. "Which is to say: open it and we'll both be disappointed together."

* * *

We unpacked on the oil store floor because the wind had turned and the landing stage was no place for paper. This had become our ritual, the four of us kneeling around a crate like parishioners around a font, passing things hand to hand. Aldous took the flour sacks and stacked them against the wall with a care that would have surprised anyone who'd met him on dry land. Sable read the manifest aloud in a flat voice, ticking items against her list. Pip claimed anything with rope attached.

It was Sable who found the letter.

She'd have missed it herself, probably, or passed it off as packing material — but Pip shook out a sack of oatmeal and the envelope slid from between the folded layers like something that had been waiting its turn. It was addressed in a round schoolroom hand:

*To the Gentleman Donor, c/o The Harbourside Home for Fatherless Children, Wexmouth.*

And beneath that, smaller, added by someone else's pen: *Via Merry Alice, Gannet Rock run.*

The store went quiet the way a room goes quiet when a clock stops.

"That's not mine," Pip said, reaching.

"It's mine," I said, and heard how quickly I said it, and knew from the look Sable gave me that quickness was itself an admission.

Because here is what I will set down plainly, since this is my own hand writing it and no one else need ever see it: I had been sending money to that orphanage for nine years. Ten pounds a quarter, sent through Tregaskin's cousin, who ran a shipping office in Wexmouth and asked no questions so long as the questions stayed bought. It was not charity, or not only charity. It was Maren's money — money from the sale of her mother's jewelry, which she'd left to me with instructions I never fully understood and never dared ask twice about. *See it goes where it's wanted,* she'd said, in the last winter, her voice already thin as lamp glass. She hadn't said where. I had chosen the orphanage myself, after some searching, because it stood near the harbor and because its windows were painted green, and Maren had liked green.

Ten years of anonymous ten-pound notes. And in all that time, in nine years of quarterly donations, I had received exactly nothing back — which was the entire point of anonymity, and the arrangement suited me the way my solitude suits me, which is to say completely and without examination.

So the letter could not be a reply. There was nothing to reply *to.* A donation is a stone dropped in deep water; you don't expect the water to write back.

Unless, said a small cold voice in me, unless the water has been corresponding with someone else.

* * *

I waited until evening to open it, which is to say I carried it around with me all day like a coal in my pocket, feeling it through the wool, opening my hand to check it was still there the way a man checks a tooth that has begun to ache. I trimmed the wicks. I wound the clockwork — one hundred and forty turns, always, the mechanism groaning up through the tower like something waking reluctantly. I spoke to the gulls, who ignored me with their usual dignity. And all the while the letter sat against my leg and did its quiet work.

At half past eight, with the lamp lit and turning above me and the Verrins below — Aldous reading, Sable mending, Pip asleep in the curve of the spiral stair where he'd wedged himself with a blanket and refused all relocation — I sat in the watch room with the letter under the small shaded lamp I keep for paperwork, and I opened it.

*Dear Sir,*

*Forgive the liberty of this reply. Mrs. Penhale says donors do not wish to be troubled with thanks, and mostly we obey her, but Mr. Tregaskin's man mentioned when he brought the last quarter's sum that the gentleman donor had been sending since before the Home moved to its new premises on Fore Street, and it seemed wrong that in all those years no one should tell him what his money has done.*

*You will know better than we, perhaps, being a gentleman, but we thought you might like to know that there are eleven children sleeping warm tonight who would not be, and that three of them are apprenticed to honest trades through your kindness, and that the little garden behind the washhouse — the one with the green gate — was built entirely on your generosity.*

*I hope you will forgive us also one confusion. When the first sum came, fourteen years ago now, it came with a note that we were told to burn, and Mrs. Penhale being Mrs. Penhale, did not burn it but kept it in the strongbox, and when we looked it out just now to compare the handwriting (for yours and hers differ, sir, we are not fools), we found the old note signed with a woman's name, and we wondered —*

*Maren Verrin.*

*We wondered if perhaps the lady and yourself were known to one another, as the sums continued regular after her note ceased, in the same amount, to the same account, and Mrs. Penhale says that is either a remarkable coincidence or a remarkable devotion, and she has known many donors and thinks it the latter.*

*Please do not feel obliged to answer. We ask only your forgiveness for the liberty, and your assurance that you remain well.*

*Yours obediently,*
*T. Cade, Secretary*

Fourteen years.

I read it four times. The lamp turned overhead; I heard the clockwork tick through its revolution, the great lens sweeping its beam across the water and dragging it back like a man raking the same field forever. Fourteen years ago. Maren and I had been married five years by then. Pip wasn't born; Sable was a child herself; Aldous was — whatever Aldous had been, wherever Aldous had been, in whichever of the ports and parlors he drifted through in those days.

Fourteen years ago, Maren Amberdale, née Verrin, wife of nobody in particular, keeper of nobody's light, had walked into a shipping office in Wexmouth — or written to one, or sent someone — and given money to an orphanage under her maiden name. Her maiden name. Not Maren Amberdale. *Verrin.* The name she'd left behind, the name she never spoke in our house, the name that arrived on my rock twenty years late in an oilskin packet and turned my life into a siege.

She'd hidden it. That was the part my mind kept circling like a gull over a fish head. She had hidden money from her own family. Money from her mother's jewels — or was it? Was that even true? Had I invented the provenance, filled in the gaps of her instructions with a story I could live inside? *See it goes where it's wanted.* Perhaps she'd meant more than I heard. Perhaps she'd meant less. Perhaps the jewelry was a cover story of her own manufacture, and the money came from somewhere else entirely, somewhere with a name like Corven Hale attached to it—

No. I stopped that thought the way you stop a runaway winch: bodily, and at cost.

But here was the arithmetic that wouldn't be stopped: fourteen years of donations, begun under her name. Nine years continuing under mine. Same sum, same account. Either I had, by pure chance, chosen the same orphanage she had — and the odds of that, in a coast full of hospitals and missions and seamen's homes, were the odds of throwing two sixes blindfolded in a gale — or I had, without knowing it, spent nearly a decade walking a road she had already walked, following her footsteps at ten years' distance, calling it my own charity.

Mrs. Penhale, the secretary wrote, called it remarkable devotion.

Mrs. Penhale had no idea how right she was, or how wrong, or how the two had stopped being different things where Maren was concerned.

* * *

I went down to the kitchen, because the watch room felt too full of her suddenly, and kitchens are honest places. Sable was still up, sitting at the table with a candle and a book she wasn't reading. She looked up when I came in, and I saw her take in my face the way she takes in everything — cataloguing, filing, that girl with her ledgers and her lists, measuring the world for discrepancies.

"You've gone grey," she said. "Greyer."

"A letter came in the crate."

"Donors don't get letters."

"No," I said. "They don't."

I put it on the table between us. I want it recorded that I did this deliberately, though I told myself at the time it was carelessness, fatigue, any excuse a man reaches for when he's about to do something his gut has voted against. But the truth is I wanted a witness. Or I wanted a second pair of eyes on the arithmetic. Or — and this is likeliest — I wanted someone else to carry a piece of it, because I had been carrying Maren alone for twenty years, dead and alive, and the weight had just changed shape again and I felt my knees go.

Sable read it once, fast, the way she reads everything, and then again, slow, and when she looked up her face had done something complicated.

"She funded it," she said. "Before you did."

"So it appears."

"With whose money? Grandmother's jewels went to Aldous when Mother—" She stopped. Recalculated. I watched her do it, watched the ledger behind her eyes flip pages. "Grandmother's jewels went to Aldous. Mother got almost nothing. She used to say—" Another stop. Sable was not accustomed to stops. "Mother used to joke that her inheritance was her name and she'd sooner throw it in the sea than spend it."

"And yet."

"And yet." She put the letter down and squared its edges with the table edge, precisely, twice. "Coral. Fourteen years ago. Where was Mother living then?"

"I don't know."

"Where were *you* living?"

"Here. Where I've always—" And then the question landed, the way her questions landed, sideways and below the waterline. Because Sable hadn't asked where I was living. She'd asked where I was living *when her mother was doing this*, and the difference between those questions was the width of an ocean. "What are you asking me?"

"Nothing." Too fast. "I'm asking whether you knew."

"I've told you. No."

"Yes. You've told me." She picked the letter up again, held it to the candle as if the flame might develop the hidden writing between its lines. "It's only — you chose this place. Out of all the charities on the coast, you chose the one she'd already chosen. People say coincidence. In my experience people say coincidence when they mean they haven't finished counting."

"And what does your count say?"

"My count says my mother had accounts I never saw, in places I never heard of, doing things I never imagined, for longer than any of us knew her at all." She set the letter down and looked at me across the candle, and for a moment neither of us was suspicious of the other, which was new, and worse. We were simply two people staring into the same fog. "Do you know what I keep thinking? Everyone talks about Mother like a portrait. Fixed. Finished. Even Father. Even Hale, whoever Hale is — they talk about her like a door that closed. But she was fourteen years of donations and green gates and a name she wouldn't spend. She was *doing things*, Coral. Right up until she died. Possibly right up until—" She stopped herself a third time, which for Sable constituted a confession.

"Until what?"

"Nothing." She gathered her book and stood. "Until recently. Goodnight."

She paused at the stair door, and I thought she was going to say something else — something about the trunk, maybe, or about letters, or about the way I'd flinched at the word *Hale*, which I had, I'd felt my own face do it. But she only said, "The garden had a green gate. You told them to build it green."

"I like green," I said.

"Yes," said Sable. "So did she."

* * *

I did not sleep well, which was becoming less an event than a condition. I climbed to the gallery at three and stood in the wind and let it sandblast the thoughts out of me, and when that failed I went back down and sat with the letter again under the little lamp, and I did the thing I had sworn off doing, which was comparing hands.

The secretary had been thorough — Mrs. Penhale's kept note reproduced in careful copy at the bottom of the page, the original words rendered in a fair imitation of the original script. *To the Harbourside Home. For the children. See it goes where it's wanted. M.V.*

I knew Maren's hand the way I knew my own heartbeat. Twenty years of shopping lists and logbook entries and one letter a year to her sister-in-law that she wrote grudgingly and burned the drafts of. The hand in the copied note was hers. Older — younger, rather; the letters stood straighter, less worn — but hers beyond argument. *For the children.* As if she'd known exactly which children, exactly why, exactly what debt was being serviced. As if the orphanage weren't a choice but a destination.

And the date. Mrs. Penhale's strongbox apparently kept dates the way I keep wicks. Fourteen years ago. Spring.

Spring, fourteen years ago. I sat very still and let my memory walk back down that particular year, because there was a thing about that year, a thing I had filed away long ago under *sea stories* and *Aldous being Aldous*: that was the spring Aldous had come to us for three months, uninvited, unexplained, thinner than a gull's leg, sleeping twelve hours a day and jumping at knocks. He'd left as abruptly as he'd come, owing me forty pounds he never repaid and mentioning, at the door, with a laugh he hadn't quite assembled, that his debts were "sorted, brother, sorted and sunk."

Sorted and sunk. I had thought he meant gambling debts paid off by some Verrin cousin. I had thought a great many things, it was becoming clear. I had built a whole marriage out of thinking, a comfortable house of assumptions with Maren's silence for rafters, and now the tide was pulling one plank loose and the entire structure creaked.

She had paid Aldous's debts. Or she had paid *something* — and Aldous's debts were the only currency of catastrophe that family produced. And she had done it secretly, with money whose source she concealed even from the orphanage, signing only initials, instructing the destruction of the evidence, hiding her charity from the very people she was saving.

Why hide it? That was the question that finally got me out of the chair and pacing. You can hide a gift from pride, yes — she was proud, proud as a mast. But you don't sign your *maiden name* to hide a gift from your husband. You'd sign no name at all. She signed Verrin because the name mattered to the transaction somehow. Because someone at the other end needed to know a Verrin was paying. Because it wasn't only charity.

It was a message. Or a marker. Or a payment on an old account, made in a name that meant something to whoever was keeping the books.

Or — and here the cold came up through the floor — it was the first stone in a wall she was building between her old life and her new one, and she was paying the mason in advance, year after year, quarter after quarter, buying her way out of the Verrins one anonymous ten-pound note at a time, and I had spent nine years unknowingly holding the door of her cage shut with my own hand.

I stood at the window with the letter in my fist and the beam swinging its patient arc over black water, and I understood that I had known this woman for twenty years and married her for nineteen and buried her for three, and I could not say with certainty that I had ever once seen her whole face. Every time I uncovered a layer, there was another layer under it, and another, going down past the foundation stone into dark water. The orphanage. The green gate. The jewels that may never have existed. The name she wouldn't spend and then spent, once, in secret, on children who weren't hers.

*Who were you?* I said it aloud to the glass, and the glass, being glass, said nothing back, and the gulls outside had the decency to be asleep.

Below me, faintly, through the iron stairs, I heard a sound I had learned to dread in recent weeks: the scrape of the sea chest lid. Soft. Careful. Someone in the bedroom below was going through Maren's trunk at three in the morning, and there was only one person in this tower who moved like that — quiet as ballast shifting, guilty as a Sunday.

I blew out the lamp. I put the letter inside my shirt, against my skin, where it lay like a second heartbeat.

Then I started down the stairs to see what Aldous Verrin thought he was looking for — and what he thought *she* had left behind.
