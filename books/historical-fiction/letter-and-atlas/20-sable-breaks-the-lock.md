## 20. Sable Breaks the Lock

My father taught me two things before he taught me anything else: how to read a tide table, and how to lie with a straight face. He always said the second skill was for emergencies. I have since learned that in the Verrin family, everything is an emergency, and so we lie constantly, and well, and with great conviction.

The lock on Maren's trunk was not a good lock. That is the first thing you should know. It was a cheap brass thing of the sort sold to girls going away to school, the kind that keeps out a curious sister and no one else. Whoever had chosen it had chosen it twenty-five years ago, when Maren Verrin was still a girl packing a sea chest her family would never be invited to look inside again.

I had been watching it for six days.

Six days of watching Coral Amberdale carry scraps of paper down to the stove and feed them into the fire with the tenderness other men reserve for love letters. Six days of watching him stand over the flames while they curled, his face lit orange from below like a man being judged by a jury of coals. He burned three letters on Tuesday. Two on Thursday. This morning, one, and he held that one over the stove a long time before letting it go, as though it might change its mind, or he might.

I am my father's daughter. I know what a man looks like when he is destroying evidence, and I know what it means when he does it alone, at dawn, believing everyone else asleep. It means the evidence matters. And if it mattered enough for Coral Amberdale—a man who alphabetizes his tinned goods and once spent forty minutes deciding whether a gull was "impertinent" or merely "hungry"—then it mattered enormously.

The trunk sat in the corner of our shared room, under Pip's cot, which was convenient because Pip sleeps like a dropped anchor. Coral slept upstairs by the lamp, as keepers do, rising every four hours to wind the clockwork. I had mapped his schedule the way I map everything now: light at dusk, wick trimmed at nine, clockwork at midnight, four, and eight, tea at seven if the weather permitted and even if it didn't, because Coral's relationship with tea was the closest thing he had to a vice.

Midnight came. The clockwork wound. His boots went up the iron stairs—clank, clank, clank, each step announcing itself like a witness for the prosecution—and then the slow creak of the lamp room door, and then nothing but the wind and the sea doing their eternal argument about who owned the horizon.

I got up.

* * *

You should understand something about me before you judge what I did next. I grew up in a house where the truth was kept the way other houses keep silver: locked away, brought out only on occasions, polished until it bore no resemblance to anything useful. My father told me my aunt Maren died of a fever. Then, later, that she died of sadness. Then, when I was old enough to notice these stories didn't agree, that she died of distance—"some people drift," he said, "like boats without moorings"—as if she were a dinghy and not a woman who had written him forty-one letters over nineteen years, all unanswered, all kept in a box under his bed, all of which I found when I was fifteen and read by candlelight while he snored one room away.

Forty-one letters. She asked after his health. She asked after me, whom she had never met, calling me "the little niece with the sharp eyes." She sent sketches of Gannet Rock. She described a keeper she called "the quietest man alive, who speaks to gulls because they don't interrupt."

And my father wrote back—once. One letter, in nineteen years. I found the draft of it too, unsent, folded small: *Maren, it is better this way. Don't ask again.*

Better this way. Better for whom? That was the question I had carried across two seas and one very unpleasant crossing in a fishing boat, and it was the question I intended to answer tonight, with a hairpin and a bad lock and whatever was left of the letters Coral hadn't yet fed to the stove.

* * *

The hairpin worked the way hairpins do on cheap locks, which is to say it worked after eleven minutes of swearing I did entirely in my head, because there are limits to the noise one makes even in righteous fury. The brass gave a click like a knuckle cracking, and the lid lifted on a breath of air that smelled of cedar and salt and something older underneath—camphor, maybe, and paper, and the particular dust of a life closed up mid-sentence.

She had packed like someone expecting to come back. That was my first thought, and it hit me harder than I expected. Dresses folded with lavender between them. A brush with long brown hairs still caught in it. A pair of boots worn down on the outer heel—the left more than the right, a woman who walked leaning into the wind, into weather, into things.

At the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string gone brittle as biscuit: papers.

I untied them over the bed so nothing could fall and hide itself. There were perhaps thirty sheets. Some were letters, some were pages torn from a ledger, some were drafts—she drafted, I saw immediately; she crossed out and rewrote the way people do when what they have to say frightens them—and at the top of the stack, in her hand, which I knew from forty-one letters the way you know a voice through a wall:

*For whoever finds this. If it is Coral, forgive me. If it is anyone else, mind your own affairs.*

I laughed. Out loud, at midnight, in a dead woman's trunk. It was exactly what I'd have written. It was, in fact, almost word for word what I had written in the front of my own notebook at fourteen, when my father confiscated it the first time.

* * *

Here is what the papers said, and I will set it down plainly because I read it three times to be sure my eyes weren't doing what frightened eyes do:

My aunt Maren Amberdale, born Verrin, informed on Corven Hale's ring. Not once. Repeatedly, over eighteen months, through a magistrate in Penzance, passing dates, cargo, names of ships, names of men. And she did it for one reason, stated in her own hand with a clarity that made my chest hurt: to buy my father out of ruin. Aldous had borrowed from Hale—of course he had, Aldous borrowing money is less a decision than a weather pattern—and the debt had grown teeth, and the teeth had reached his family, and Maren had looked at her brother's children—you and Pip unborn but already counted, Sable, already counted, already *counted*, she wrote, *I have never met the girl and already I would burn a kingdom for her*—and she had made a trade. Her silence for his freedom. Her name for his safety. Information given quietly, in exchange for the debt torn up and the Verrins struck from Hale's list.

It worked. That was the worst part. It worked completely. Hale lost two cargoes and a ship and half his crew to the magistrates, and he spent years learning who had done it, and when he finally learned—

She wrote it herself, near the end of the stack, in a draft of a letter to my father that was never sent, because none of her letters to my father were ever sent, because my father never answered, because answering would have meant acknowledging:

*Aldous knows what I did. He has always known. He was there the night I told him the debt was cleared, and he did not ask how, because he did not want to know, and I loved him enough to let him not know. But Corven Hale will not stay ignorant forever, and when he comes—and he will come—it will be my door he breaks, not yours. I have made sure of that. I have made sure of everything. That is what women of our family do, little brother. We make sure. You make sure of nothing. You are very good at making sure of nothing.*

There it was. In ink. In her hand. Dated fourteen years ago.

Aldous knew.

He knew, and he let her carry it, and when the danger ripened and the letter washed up on this rock naming the whole family for Hale's revenge, he packed his daughter and his grandson into a boat and rowed straight to the one man whose roof Hale would not dare touch—and he never said a word. Not *thank you*. Not *my sister died of a fever she caught running messages for my sake*. Not even, God help us, *I'm sorry.* Just: here is the lighthouse, here is the ration book, here is my silence, I have practiced it for decades and I am very good.

* * *

I sat on the floor of that cold room with the papers spread around me like fallen leaves, and I did not cry. I want that recorded. Whatever else happened that night, I did not cry, because crying is a currency my father taught us to hoard, and also because I was too furious for it. Fury fills up all the rooms where grief might otherwise lodge. It is a terrible landlord but an effective one.

I thought about Coral burning those letters at dawn.

I had watched him do it with something close to contempt, if I'm honest. Contempt, and a little pity—the lonely man destroying the last voices of his dead wife, unable to bear hearing her speak. I had built a whole story around it in six days, the way I build stories around everything: the coward keeper, hiding from history, incinerating his own past because looking at it hurt. Pip had asked me why Mr. Amberdale burned paper, and I'd said, *because some people would rather have ashes than answers,* and felt very clever saying it.

But ashes preserve what they burn. That's the thing about fire nobody remembers. What goes into a stove as ink comes out as carbon, and carbon keeps its shape, its weight, its testimony. You can read ash if you're patient. Coral wasn't erasing those letters.

He was keeping them the only way he could bear to.

And what he'd been reading, night after dawn after night—what he'd been feeding piece by piece to the fire with that tender, terrible care—was this. Was her. Was the truth that his wife had been braver than his entire family put together, and that we had arrived on his rock carrying the bill for her courage, and that my father had known, had *always* known, and had let Coral believe—what? What had we let him believe? That Maren had simply married out and drifted off? That the estrangement was temper, or pride, or distance? That her death was a fever?

We had let him believe nothing, which is worse. We had let him believe the absence was natural. Weather. A boat without moorings.

* * *

The lamp room door creaked above me. Boots on iron stairs—clank, clank, clank, descending, the four-hour watch ended, Coral coming down for his tea at the wrong hour because the storm glass had gone cloudy and he always checked the barometer twice when it did.

I had perhaps ninety seconds.

I should have put the papers back. I knew that even then, some sensible clerk in the back office of my mind filing the recommendation: return documents, replace string, relock brass, resume innocence. Ninety seconds was enough.

I didn't.

Instead I took the one letter—the draft, the one addressed to *little brother*—and I folded it into my sleeve, and I rewrapped the rest, and I closed the lid, and the lock clicked shut with the same cheap little sound, and then I sat back down on my cot and arranged myself into the posture of a sleeping woman just as Coral's boots reached the landing.

He paused in the doorway. He had a way of pausing in doorways, Coral, like a man checking whether a room still contained what he'd left in it. The lamplight from the stairwell fell across the floor, across the trunk, across me.

"Sable," he said. Not a question. Coral doesn't ask questions; he issues observations and lets you decide whether to treat them as accusations.

"Mm," I said, with tremendous skill.

"The barometer's falling fast." A pause. "Storm coming early this year."

"Then we'll want the windows battened," I said, from inside the fiction of a woman who had been asleep and cared about barometers.

Another pause. Longer. I kept my breathing slow and even, the way Pip breathes, the way honest people breathe, and I felt the letter against my wrist like a coal wrapped in cloth.

"You sleep with your boots on," he observed.

I looked down. I was, in fact, wearing my boots. There is no recovering from a thing like that. There is only commitment.

"I was going to check the hens," I said.

"We haven't any hens."

"No," I agreed. "We don't."

He stood there a moment longer, and I swear—I would swear it on the trunk itself—he almost smiled. Just at the edge of his mouth, where smiles go to die on that face. Then he turned and climbed back toward the lamp room, and the door creaked, and the wind took up its argument again, and I lay in the dark with my father's sin folded against my arm.

* * *

Here is what I decided, lying awake while the barometer fell and fell: I had spent my whole life managing my father. Managing his debts, his silences, his moods, his talent for being elsewhere when consequence arrived. I had done it the way you bail a leaky boat—constantly, competently, without ever asking why the hull was rotten. Because he was my father. Because that's the job.

But Maren had bailed for him too. She'd bailed with eighteen months of her life and eventually with everything she had, and he had watched her do it and said nothing, and then he had watched her sail away to a rock in the sea to wait out the storm *he* had earned, and he had said nothing, and now he had brought that storm to the door of her widower and set it down in his kitchen and called it shelter.

No.

I am done being the bailer. Let the water come. Let it find him standing in it, and let him explain to both his children—Pip counts, Pip always counts—why the boat sank.

Dawn would come in three hours. Coral would go down to the stove. And I would be waiting for him, letter in hand, and I would ask him—not kindly, I had used up my kindness somewhere around the second reading—exactly how much of the truth he had taken from those ashes, and what he meant to do about the man who had caused all of it.

Because upstairs, in the room above the lamp, my father was sleeping the sleep of a man who believes his secrets are safe in a locked trunk on a rock at the edge of the world.

He had forgotten, as he forgets everything, that he raised a daughter with sharp eyes and a hairpin.
