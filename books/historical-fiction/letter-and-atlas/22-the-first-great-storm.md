## 22. The First Great Storm

### Coral

The barometer had been falling for two days, and by the evening of the third it read so low that Coral tapped the glass twice, as though the instrument might be persuaded to reconsider. It did not. The needle lay against the left of the dial like a fainted woman.

He had kept Gannet Rock through eleven winters. He knew the grammar of storms the way a clergyman knows scripture — which gusts were merely punctuation, which ones meant a change of subject entirely. This one opened with a wind from the southeast that arrived not as a sound but as a pressure, a hand laid flat against the tower's flank, and Coral stood at the gallery rail with his coat buttoned to the throat and thought: *well. Here it is, early.*

The great storms were supposed to wait until the month's end. The supply boat was supposed to come before them. Neither had consulted him.

Inside, the Verrins were arranged around the kitchen stove in the particular silence of people who have been told what is coming and are trying to look unbothered by it. Aldous sat with his hands flat on his knees like a man being measured for a coffin. Sable was mending a sock she did not need mended. Pip watched the window, where the first rain came down sideways and glittered in the lamplight like thrown gravel.

"Rules," Coral said, because rules were the only sermon he knew how to preach. "When the wind turns to the north-northeast, the tower will sing. That's normal. It's not falling down; it's complaining. Everything on this rock complains. You'll want to sleep in the kitchen tonight, not upstairs — the upper rooms move more, and there's no dignity in being rocked to sleep by weather."

"And you?" Sable asked, without looking up from her sock. "Where will you be?"

"Where I always am." He nodded upward, toward the lamp room and its hundred and eighty steps. "The light doesn't keep itself."

"The light," Aldous said quietly, "doesn't have a family in the cellar of it either."

Coral looked at his brother-in-law — Maren's brother, the man he'd spent twenty years not thinking about — and found he had no answer that wasn't an argument, and no time for arguments. The wind chose that moment to shift, exactly as forecast, and the tower began to sing: a low, keening note through the stonework, rising and falling, like something enormous mourning something enormous.

Pip's face went white. Sable put down the sock.

"That's the singing," Coral said. "Told you." And he took up the oil can and went up into the dark, and — this was the part he would remember later, turning it over like a stone found in a pocket — three pairs of footsteps followed him.

* * *

### Sable

Sable had been raised on stories of storms the way other children were raised on Bible verses. Her father told them at every opportunity: the gale that took the roof off the Penzance house, the squall that swamped his first command, the hurricane he'd once ridden out lashed to a mast while God, he implied, held his hand personally over Aldous Verrin. She had always suspected the stories grew in the telling. Standing now in the lamp room of Gannet Rock, with the whole Atlantic throwing itself bodily against the glass, she understood that her father's stories had, if anything, been understated.

The light turned above them — the great lens revolving in its bath of mercury, steady as a heartbeat — and between each sweep the darkness outside was total, and then the beam swung round and showed her the sea standing up in walls, black water with white manes, and then darkness again, and the wind hit the lantern glass so hard she flinched though she'd have died before admitting it.

"It's two inches thick," Coral said beside her. He didn't raise his voice; the room made shouting pointless, and he seemed to know that. "The glass. It's taken worse than this."

"You've had worse than this?"

"Twice." He handed her a cloth without ceremony. "Wipe the salt spray where it creeps in at the lower panes. Left side. If it builds up, the light dims on that arc, and a dimmed arc is how ships convince themselves they're somewhere else."

She took the cloth. It was such an ordinary instruction, such a small domestic thing — wipe here, mind the drip — issued at the center of what sounded like the end of the world, and it steadied her more than any prayer would have. There was no drama in the man. There was only the work, and the work was infinite, and somehow that was better than courage. Courage ran out. Work didn't.

Her father was below, in the watch room, doing whatever Coral had assigned him — coiling rope, checking the fog signal, staying out from underfoot, which for Aldous Verrin was a full occupation. And Pip —

Pip was sitting on the iron floor with his back against the pedestal, wrapped in a blanket, watching the lens turn with his mouth open. Every few seconds the beam swept across the windows and lit his face gold, and then he was gone into the dark, and then gold again. A boy made into a lighthouse signal himself.

"He's not frightened?" she asked.

"He was. First hour." Coral knelt to check the mercury bath, tilting the little gauge. "Then I gave him a job."

"What job?"

"Counting the flashes. Out loud. He loses count when he gets distracted, and starting over keeps him busy." A pause, and then, in the same flat tone: "It also tells me he hasn't fallen asleep or fallen ill, without me having to stop working to check. Two birds."

Sable wiped salt spray off cold glass and studied the keeper sidelong. *Coward*, her father had called him, years ago, in the way you mention a debt you don't intend to pay — *Maren could have done better than that grey little man on his rock.* She had built a portrait from that sentence and carried it across an ocean. The portrait did not match. The original was grey, yes, and little in the sense of undemonstrative, but no coward had ever sounded this calm inside a storm, and no man indifferent to family would have given a seasick nine-year-old a counting job to keep his fear occupied.

The tower shuddered — a long groan through the ironwork, the whole structure leaning into the wind and leaning back — and Pip's count faltered, wobbled, resumed. Coral's hand moved to the boy's shoulder for one second, no more, and returned to the gauge.

Sable polished the glass and said nothing, and filed it away with everything else.

* * *

### Aldous

Aldous Verrin had commanded ships. He had faced mutiny in the Java Sea and a court of inquiry in London and his own brother-in-law's silence for twenty years, and he considered himself a man who knew fear intimately, the way one knows a business partner — respectably, at arm's length, with contracts in place.

This storm did not contract.

It came at the tower like a creditor. All night it threw things: water, spray, a fish — an actual fish, which struck the lantern glass above his head with a crack like a pistol and slid away into the dark — and once, impossibly, a piece of planking that might have come off a boat or a house or a church, he couldn't tell and didn't want to. The tower sang and shuddered and held, and Aldous coiled rope in the watch room because Coral had told him to, and discovered that obedience, in his sixth decade, was the only thing keeping his hands from shaking.

He was not afraid of dying. He had made his peace with that years ago, in a different way than most men, having earned it honestly. What he feared was smaller and worse: that the storm would tear the roof off and the sea would come in and Pip would see his grandfather's face and read it correctly. That the boy would learn, in one night, what kind of man held the family name.

"Mister Amberdale." He raised his voice against the wind's percussion on the stairwell. "The fog signal — should we test it in this? Won't the wind carry it wrong?"

"Wind's from the southeast. Carries it right out along the shipping lane, which is where I want it." Coral appeared at the top of the stairs, oil can in hand, hair plastered flat, looking like a man who had been interrupted during a chore rather than during an apocalypse. "You can sound it if you like. Ten seconds every minute. It gives the hands something to do."

Aldous sounded the fog signal. Ten seconds of compressed air bellowing out into the gale, a sound like the world's grief given a horn, and he found — this shamed him even in the moment — that he loved it. That making noise back at the storm was the first honest relief he'd felt in weeks. He sounded it again and again, ten seconds every minute, and the rhythm organized him, and somewhere in the second hour he understood that Coral knew exactly what he was doing: giving the frightened man a lever to pull. A job. The same trick he'd played on Pip.

The old keeper came and went between the lamp room and the watch room and the oil store, checking, trimming, adjusting, a circuit as regular as the light itself, and Aldous watched him and thought about Maren. She had married this. Had chosen this grey deliberate man and this singing tower over the loud bright dangerous world of the Verrins, and everyone had said she'd buried herself alive, and Aldous had said it loudest.

Standing in the shaking watch room, he began to suspect she had known something none of them had. That there was a difference between hiding and anchoring, and that he had spent his whole life confusing them.

At some hour past midnight, Coral paused on the landing, halfway up the stairs, and looked down at him.

"You're doing fine," the keeper said. Not warmly. As a report of fact. Then he climbed on up toward the light, and Aldous pulled the fog signal cord, and for one full minute nobody in the tower was afraid.

### Pip

The flashes were four hundred and eleven, then four hundred and twelve, then the wave hit the rock and the whole tower went *boom* like a giant knocking, and Pip lost count completely and started over at one, which Uncle Coral had said was allowed, starting over was always allowed, the number didn't matter, the counting mattered.

That didn't make sense and also made perfect sense, which Pip was learning was most of what grown-ups said.

He was not scared anymore. Mostly. His stomach still rolled when the tower leaned, but the leaning always came back, like the light coming back, and Uncle Coral had explained it: *the tower isn't fighting the wind, lad, it's bending with it. Things that fight break. Things that bend stand.* Pip had decided this was probably true about people too, though he hadn't worked out yet whether he was a bender or a fighter.

His mother was up on a ladder wiping the big curved windows, moving whenever the light came round so she wouldn't block it, quick and sure, and his grandda was down the stairs booming the fog horn, and Uncle Coral moved among all of them like the middle of a clock, the part everything else turned around. Once, when a wave came clear over the gallery and the whole lamp room went white and roaring, Pip grabbed Uncle Coral's sleeve without meaning to, just grabbed it, and Uncle Coral didn't pull away or tell him to let go. He just kept working with a boy attached to his arm, and after a while Pip let go on his own, because he didn't need to anymore.

"Uncle Coral," he said, during a quieter stretch, "did you ever keep the light alone in a storm this big?"

"Eleven times."

"Weren't you scared?"

The old man considered this seriously, the way he considered everything, like the answer might be different if he checked properly.

"Yes," he said. "But scared is a passenger, not a driver. It rides along. It doesn't get to steer." He nodded at the lens, turning, turning. "Same as the tower. Now — where were we?"

"Four hundred and sixty-one."

"Carry on, then."

Pip counted. Outside, the storm threw the whole sea at them, and the tower bent and stood, and the light went round, and round, and round.

* * *

### Coral

Dawn, when it finally came, was less a sunrise than a surrender — the black going to grey going to a bruised, wrung-out silver, the wind dropping from a scream to a snarl to something almost conversational. The sea remained monstrous, hills of it marching in from the southeast, but the air had cleared enough to see a mile or more, and Coral stood at the gallery rail with a mug of tea going cold in his hand and counted the cost the way he counted everything: methodically.

One pane cracked in the lower gallery, patchable. Rail ladder gone — again; it went every winter, he'd stopped replacing it properly. Stores dry, thank God; the oil store had taken no water. And four people alive and unhurt in his tower, which was not a line item he'd ever carried before, and which weighed more than all the rest.

They had kept the light together. Eleven hours of it. The girl had worked like she'd been born to it; the boy had counted four thousand-odd flashes and fallen asleep at last against the pedestal, mid-number; Aldous had sounded the fog signal through the worst hours without once needing to be told. Whatever else was rotten in the Verrins — and Coral had begun to suspect the rot went deeper than any letter had said — last night the machinery had run true.

He heard footsteps on the stair and didn't turn. He knew the tread by now; light and quick, always slightly too curious for comfort.

"The fishermen use this channel," Sable said, coming to stand beside him at the rail. She nodded south, toward the open water. "Don't they? In weather like this, running for harbor."

"Some do. Fools mostly, and men with no choice. The harbor's six miles north."

"Then why—" She stopped. Her hand came up to the rail. "There."

He saw it a half-second after she did. Far out, at the edge of the grey, where the sea stood highest: a small shape, dark against the white, a fishing smack under bare poles, driven hard down on the outer shoals. Even at that distance he could see it was wallowing — broaching, taking water, its crew indistinguishable specks clinging to something.

And behind it, holding off with the easy confidence of a spectator, a darker shape with too much canvas for honesty.

A sloop.

Coral set down his tea untasted, and reached for the breeches buoy gear, and said the only thing there was to say.

"Get your father. Wake the boy. There's drowning men on my shoals."
