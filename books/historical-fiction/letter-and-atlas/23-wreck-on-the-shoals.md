## 23. Wreck on the Shoals

The gear came out of the oil store in the order I had drilled it into my own bones for twenty years and had never once expected to use. Line box. The whip line with its double breeches block. Rocket apparatus, powder charges dry as a sermon. Fowey gun. I had bought half of it at government auction for pennies on the pound from a station being decommissioned on the mainland, and the auctioneer's clerk had looked at me with something like pity — *a lighthouse keeper buying rescue gear, that's a man who doesn't trust his own light* — and I had said nothing, because the truth was worse than his guess. The truth was that I liked owning it. It was the heaviest argument I possessed against my own uselessness, and I kept it oiled.

Aldous came down the stairwell still buttoning his coat, Sable behind him with Pip by the hand, the boy's face white as the foam below us.

"Is it sinking?" Pip asked.

"Everything sinks eventually," Aldous said, which was not an answer, and Sable gave him a look that could have stripped varnish. "Yes," she told the boy. "That's why we're going to be quick."

I was already counting heads on the smack through the glass. Four specks when the squall lifted, then three, then — God forgive me for the arithmetic of it — two, clinging to the wrecked hull where it ground itself along the outer bar like a cork being peeled. The sea out there stood in grey hills with white combed backs, and every one of them picked the smack up and slammed it down as though the ocean were a child losing patience with a toy.

"Sable." I turned from the glass. "The lamp is yours tonight. Full burn, and keep it turning no matter what you hear or what comes through that door. If we go into the water, that beam is the only thing on this rock that knows what it's doing."

She didn't argue, which told me she understood. She took the stairs two at a time, dragging Pip with her, and a minute later the great lens woke above our heads and began its slow wheeling, throwing its blade of light across the water once every few seconds like a lighthouse-sized finger pointing: *there. There. There.*

"The sloop," Aldous said quietly. He'd seen it too. It lay off to the eastward, hove-to, riding the same seas that were murdering the smack with an ease that made my teeth ache. "They're just watching."

"Yes."

"They could stand in closer. They could—"

"They could do a great many things," I said, "if they were men instead of vultures. Get the fowey gun down to the landing stage. We'll shoot a line across if the wind gives us half a chance, and then we'll pray their crew can hold a tail-block better than they held a tiller."

He went. And here is the thing I want set down honestly, because this book will be honest if it kills me: as I watched Aldous Verrin stumble down the wet iron stairs with forty pounds of rescue gun in his arms, I felt — beneath the fear, beneath the cold — a small hot coal of something shameful. Because if those men drowned, Hale would have lost witnesses. And if they lived, they might name names. And either way, some part of me that I did not care for understood that the sea was about to do my interrogating for me.

I put the coal out. Mostly.

* * *

The landing stage at Gannet Rock is a shelf of dressed granite with an iron stanchion, built by men who assumed any keeper stationed here would be too sensible to use it in weather. The spray was coming over the top rail in solid green fists. I got the gun bedded and braced while Aldous held the rocket case under his coat like a stolen loaf, shouting questions at me that the wind ate whole.

"WHAT?" I bellowed back.

"I SAID — HAVE YOU DONE THIS BEFORE?"

"NO!" And then, because lying to a Verrin had become a kind of hobby lately: "BUT THE MANUAL IS VERY THOROUGH AND I'VE READ IT SEVENTEEN TIMES!"

Something crossed his face that might have been a laugh trying to be born in the wrong country. He braced himself against the stanchion beside me, and together we slewed the gun toward the outer shoals, and waited for the sea to give us a gap between waves, which is like waiting for a wolf to yawn.

The smack struck again — properly struck this time, a sound that reached us even over the gale, timber parting with a groan like a living thing. One figure went into the water. I saw the white splash, saw the dark head surface twice, three times, and then the sea folded over it and did not unfold. Two left. They had gotten themselves up onto the highest broken section of the hull, and one of them was waving something — an arm, a rag, a lantern that guttered and died.

"Line away!" I fired the rocket into the gap the wave left, and the line screamed out over the water trailing its fire-tail, and fell — short. Of course short. First shot always short; the manual says so, in the smug way manuals have. We reloaded with hands that the cold had borrowed and were refusing to return, and fired again, and the second rocket carried true and dropped its line across the wreck's shoulders like a benediction.

"Now they have to help us help them," Aldous shouted. "Do you see them moving?"

I did. Slowly, terribly slowly, one of the figures was hauling the line in hand over hand, and every time the sea hit him he stopped and clung and started again. The other had gone still in a way that stillness should never go.

It took eleven minutes. I know because I counted, because counting was the only prayer I had. Eleven minutes watching a man pull a rope through freezing water with the patience of a saint or the stupidity of one, while the sloop sat off eastward with its lights burning comfortable and warm, watching the same show and doing precisely nothing about it.

Then the tail-block came back to us, and we bent on the hawser and hauled it taut, and rigged the breeches buoy, and sent it out empty into the dark.

* * *

The first man came across the wire like a sack of wet laundry with opinions. The buoy swung and spun and dipped into the crest of a wave that soaked him a second time for spite, but he held on, and when we dragged him onto the granite he was coughing seawater and swearing with a fluency that suggested long practice at both.

"Get him inside," I said, and Aldous hauled the man bodily up the steps while I sent the buoy back out for the last one.

The second crossing was worse. The buoy jammed halfway — fouled block, salt ice in the sheave, the manual's seventeenth chapter come home to roost — and hung there over the troughs while the man inside swung like a bell. I worked the haul line until my palms burned, cursing the engineering of better men, and Aldous came pounding back down and threw his whole weight on the downhaul with me, and between us we walked the buoy off its snag and brought it grinding home.

The man inside was older, grey-bearded, half-frozen, and alive. Alive enough to grip my sleeve with fingers like iron hooks and say, before anything else, before thanks, before his own name:

"She went down with four aboard. Did you see — Tam? Young fellow, red hair?"

I looked at him, and he read my face the way sailors read weather, and he closed his eyes and let go of my sleeve.

We got them both into the tower. Into *my* tower, which had been, twenty-four hours ago, the quietest building on the coast of England.

* * *

Sable had done miracles in our absence. There was a fire going in the stove — the good coal, I noticed, not the dross, and I found I didn't mind — blankets heated, tea brewed strong enough to trot a horse. She'd put Pip to work mopping the floor, which was inspired; the boy needed a job the way a lamp needs oil, and he attacked the puddles of seawater with the fury of someone avenging a personal insult.

The younger fisherman sat wrapped in wool, shivering so hard his teeth clicked like a telegraph key. Red-haired, I noted, and my stomach turned over. The old one sat nearer the fire and stared into it as though it owed him money.

"You'll be the keeper, then," the old man said. His voice was rough gravel. "This is your rock?"

"It is."

"Hobbs," he said. "Silas Hobbs. That's my nephew Danny." A jerk of the chin at the shivering boy. "Smack's the *Petrel*, out of Porthallow. We were running crab pots down the coast when the sky turned ugly faster than a parson's promise." He said it flatly, rehearsed, the way men tell the story of their own disasters when the disaster is still too fresh to feel. Then his eyes came up and fixed on mine, and the flatness cracked. "Keeper. There was another vessel out there. Dark sloop, black hull. She stood off the whole time."

"She did."

"We signaled her. Burned flares. She had the room and the canvas to stand in, and she—" Hobbs stopped. His jaw worked. "Danny, don't listen."

"I'm listening," Danny said, teeth chattering.

"Then hear it," his uncle said, "because you'll meet that boat again if you live long enough, and you'd best know her." He leaned forward into the firelight, and I saw the shape of the thing he was carrying before he said a word of it. "That was Corven Hale's sloop. The *Magpie*. I'd know her lines anywhere — I crewed on her myself, six years back, before I found Jesus or he found me, whichever it was."

The room went very quiet. Even Pip stopped mopping.

"And the two of you," Aldous said carefully, from the shadows by the stair. "You were Hale's crew."

"Were." Hobbs spat into the fire, which seemed to settle something in him. "Half the boats between here and the Sound have carried his crates at one time or another, mister, whatever they say ashore. But I'll tell you what nobody ashore knows, because the men who know it are mostly at the bottom of the channel now." He held up his hands and counted on frozen fingers. "There were nine of us signed on the *Petrel*'s sister run this autumn. Nine. Tonight makes seven drowned since October, and every one of them drowned within sight of that sloop, and every one of them a man who'd carried for Hale and might talk." He looked around the room, at each of us in turn, and his voice dropped. "He's not hunting revenge, mister. Revenge is cheap. He's tidying. Every soul who knows the shape of his business goes into the sea sooner or later, and the sea asks no questions and writes no letters."

My eyes went, without my permission, to Aldous. Aldous was looking at the floor with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"And you," I said to Hobbs. "Why aren't you at the bottom of it?"

"Because I'm careful, and because I got old, and because—" He hesitated, and the hesitation was longer than the rest of his speech put together. "Because there's a reason Hale's cleaning house now, after all these years quiet. Something's missing from his books. Something paper. And he thinks somebody's taken it, and he's burning through everyone who might've taken it, one boat at a time, hoping the thief drowns with the rest of us."

The fire ticked. Above us, faint through the floors, the great lens wheeled on its bath of mercury, steady as a heartbeat.

Paper, I thought. Maren had been dead five years, and still, somehow, everything came back to paper. To letters. To the things she wrote and sealed and hid and never sent, and the things other men would drown the coast of England to find.

"Well," said Aldous Verrin, into the silence, in a voice pitched carefully halfway between innocence and indifference. "That sounds like none of our affair whatsoever."

Hobbs looked at him for a long moment. Then at me. Then at Sable, standing very straight by the stove with her arms folded, wearing the exact face her aunt must have worn at that age, the face I had spent a marriage learning to read and had apparently only ever skimmed.

"No," Hobbs agreed mildly. "None of your affair at all." He turned back to the fire, and added, almost to himself: "Funny thing, though. Before she struck, our skipper got a signal off the *Magpie*. Lantern work. Hale's man asking our position, and asking it particular." He rubbed his beard. "And the message ended with a word I haven't heard in six years. Skipper went white as sailcloth when he read it."

"What word?" Sable asked.

"*Verrin*," said Silas Hobbs, and warmed his hands at the fire, and let the name sit in the middle of my kitchen like a body washed up on the sand.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the fishermen slept stacked like cordwood in the storeroom and Pip had finally been argued into his bunk — I climbed to the lamp room. Sable was there, of course. She had been there all night, trimming wicks that needed no trimming, polishing glass that was already bright, keeping her hands busy so her mind couldn't ambush her.

"He knew," she said, without turning around. "Hobbs. He saw Dad flinch at that name, and he said it anyway. He wanted to watch."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because drowning men float," I said, "but suspicion floats better. He wants allies. Or he wants us frightened enough to run, and running men are easier to rob." I took the logbook and entered the hour, the weather, the lamp's condition, the loss of the *Petrel* with two souls saved and two souls given to the channel. My handwriting came out steadier than I felt, which is the entire art of keeping a log.

"Coral." She used my name rarely enough that it landed like a dropped tool. "If Hale is killing everyone who knows his business — and Dad carried for him once, we both know it, whatever he calls it — then Dad isn't hiding from Hale's revenge. He's next on the list. He's been next on the list since October, and he's known it, and he let us sail to this rock anyway."

"Aye."

"So either he's a fool—"

"He's not a fool," I said. "Fools are innocent. Your father is something more expensive than that."

The lens wheeled. Out beyond the glass, the channel lay black and heaving, and somewhere out in that blackness the *Magpie* rode at her ease, waiting for daylight, waiting for news, waiting — I had no doubt — for whatever it was that Maren Amberdale had written down on paper and hidden somewhere between this lamp and the foundation stone, trusting, in her maddening, unforgivable, magnificent way, that I would find it.

Or perhaps not trusting. Perhaps simply knowing me better than I knew myself.

"Keep the light," I told Sable. "I'm going down to check the stores."

"At three in the morning?"

"There's a crate down there," I said, "that came on the last supply boat, and I've been putting off opening it, and I find I'm not putting it off anymore."

She looked at me the way she had looked at me the first day, like a puzzle — but a puzzle, now, that she intended to solve alongside rather than against. "What's in it?"

"I don't know yet," I said, and started down the stairs. "But the sea's been delivering mail all season, and I've learned not to keep it waiting."
