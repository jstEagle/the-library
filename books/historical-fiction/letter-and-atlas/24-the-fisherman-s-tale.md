## 24. The Fisherman's Tale

The fishermen were laid out in the oil store like two crates of herring that had survived the crossing by accident. One of them—old, grey-bearded, with hands like knotted rope—had not stopped shivering since we'd hauled him over the sill. The other was younger, maybe thirty, with a gash across his brow that Coral had cleaned and bound with a strip of sheet, and he had not stopped talking.

That was how I learned his name before anyone thought to ask it: Tam Breech, master and only crew of the *Kestrel*, out of Pellow Harbour, which I gathered was the sort of place where a man named Breech might reasonably expect to die at sea and be considered lucky for the variety.

"He talks too much," my father said. He said it quietly, in the doorway, with the particular flatness he used when he wanted to be overheard by everyone except the person he was describing.

"He's warm," I said. "Warm people talk. It's the cold ones you want to worry about."

My father looked at me then—the look he'd been giving me since the wreck, since he'd watched Uncle Coral go down the rock face into the surf with a line around his waist and come back up with two strangers in his arms—and something in it had shifted. Not softened, exactly. Recalibrated. He was doing sums about this man, the same way I had been doing them since the day we landed, and he was arriving at answers that did not match the ones he'd brought with him.

I knew the feeling. I'd spent three weeks on this rock revising my arithmetic.

* * *

Here is what I had written in my head, in the ledger I kept where no one could read it, on the first night: *Coral Amberdale. Coward. Hides on a rock because the world frightens him. Married Aunt Maren and never asked her a single question, or asked all the wrong ones.*

Here is what the ledger said now: *Coral Amberdale. Sends money to an orphanage under a false name so no one can thank him. Teaches children to polish lenses instead of shouting at them. Rations his own supper thinner than ours and calls it appetite. Went down a cliff in a storm for men he owes nothing to, one of whom is almost certainly connected to the man hunting us.*

I still didn't understand him. But I had stopped filing him under coward, and starting a new page in a ledger is its own small confession.

Tam Breech talked because he was warm, and because he was frightened, and because fear in some men comes out as silence and in others as a flood. He sat propped against the oil drums with a blanket around his shoulders and a mug of broth going cold in his hands, and he told us about the storm from the inside of it, and then—he told us other things.

It started, as these things do, with a name.

"Corven Hale," he said, and the room changed temperature. My father went very still by the door. Pip, who had been allowed up from the kitchen on the condition that he touch nothing and speak less, went stiller than either of us, which for Pip meant he stopped fidgeting entirely, and I put my hand on the back of his neck without looking at him.

"What about him?" my father asked. Too fast. Too interested. A man who has spent his life lying badly does not improve under pressure; he simply lies with more sweat in it.

Breech shrugged, which set off a fresh round of shivering. "Nothing. Only—" and here he glanced at Coral, who stood apart by the lamp locker with his arms folded, wearing the expression of a man listening to weather reports, "—only every man on that coast knows Hale's boats. You learn 'em the way you learn weather. That smack that foundered on the shoal ahead of us—she wasn't fishing."

"No?" Coral said.

"She was running dark. No lights, no markers, sitting off the point like she was waiting on someone. We gave her a wide berth, same as you would." He drank his broth, grimaced at the temperature, drank again anyway. "Then the squall came down and she was caught between the shoal and the wind with no room to turn. Same as us, near enough. Sea doesn't care whose side you're on."

"Who was aboard?" my father asked.

Breech looked at him for a long moment. I watched the fisherman do his own arithmetic—watched him weigh the fine lady's clothes gone salt-stiff, the gentleman's soft hands, the boy, the keeper standing silent as furniture—and I saw him decide that whatever he knew, we already had a stake in it, because everyone on this rock had a stake in everything now. There were no passengers here anymore. Only crew.

"Hale's men," he said. "Two of the drowned. I knew their faces. Joss Merren, who used to run brandy up the estuary before the revenue boys got religion. And a young one, Denny Foye. He'd have been twenty if the sea let him." He crossed himself, awkwardly, one-handed, the blanket sliding. "Hale'll feel that loss. Two good men, and a boat, in one night, chasing—" He stopped.

"Chasing," Coral said. It was not a question. It was a hand extended across the table, palm open, saying: *go on, I will not grab your wrist.*

Breech's eyes came around to Coral, and there was something in his face—not fear exactly, but the wariness of a man deciding how much truth a stranger can carry without dropping it.

"You're the keeper," he said. "You keep things. Lights, mostly. Maybe other things."

"I keep the light," Coral said. "The rest washes up on its own."

I nearly laughed. Three weeks ago I'd have called that evasion. Now I recognized it as precision: he was telling Breech, in nine words, that he would neither lie nor volunteer, and that the choice of cargo was Breech's own.

Breech chose.

"There's talk," he said, and lowered his voice even though the storm outside was loud enough to bury a scream, "talk along the coast, in the taverns where the wrong kind of men drink, that Hale's not after blood. Not mainly. Blood's cheap. What Hale wants is paper."

"Paper," my father repeated.

"A book." Breech set down the mug. His hands were steadier now, or he was making them be. "A ledger. Names in it. Every customs man, every magistrate, every harbourmaster from here to the capital who ever took ring money and signed his name for it. Twenty years of names, they say. Hale built his whole trade on that book—not the smuggling, mind, the *book*. Because a man who knows which officials are bought knows which doors are painted shut and which ones swing open. Lose the book, lose the map of your own kingdom."

"And Hale's lost it?" I asked.

Breech turned to me, and I held his gaze, because I had learned on this rock that looking away is a kind of permission.

"Lost it, hid it, or had it took from him—depends who tells it. But it's gone, and it's been gone years, and he's been turning over stones ever since. Quiet-like. He's not a man who rages. He's a man who asks questions until the answers start costing people." Breech rubbed his face. "And then, this autumn, the talk changed. The talk got specific. Somebody told him where the book went—or who took it, or who's been sitting on it. And the somebody's name was Verrin."

Pip made a small sound. My hand tightened on his neck, gentle, holding.

"That's why the letter came," my father said. His voice had gone thin. "That's why we—that's why the warning—"

"I don't know anything about letters," Breech said, with the finality of a man who had learned on the water that knowing things gets you drowned. "I know a smack running dark off your point, and I know whose men were on her, and I know they weren't here for the fishing. Whatever Hale wants, keeper, he believes it's on this rock, or it's coming to this rock, or it's already inside these walls drinking your tea." He looked around the oil store—at the drums, the coils of rope, the two of us Verrins, the boy, the tall quiet man by the lamp locker—and I watched him arrive at the same conclusion I had arrived at weeks ago and been resisting ever since:

*One of us is the reason.*

* * *

Afterward, Coral fed them—fisherman's food, bread and salt pork and tea strong enough to stand a spoon in—and installed Breech and the older man, whose name was Hosken and who had said perhaps forty words total, in the storeroom below with blankets enough to see them through the night. Then he climbed the tower stairs without a word to any of us, because the light does not wait for grief or revelation, and I followed him.

I don't fully know why I followed. Habit, maybe. Or the particular hunger of a person who has just heard her family's name spoken like a curse in an oil store and needs to watch someone do something simple and necessary with their hands.

He was trimming the wick when I came into the lamp room, and polishing the lens afterward, cloth moving in slow circles, and he let me stand there a full five minutes before he spoke.

"You knew," I said.

"Some of it."

"How much is some?"

He considered the lens. The beam swept out over the black water and came around again, patient as a lighthouse keeper. "I knew Hale wanted something more than revenge. Revenge doesn't buy berths on supply boats. Revenge doesn't post watchers at dusk and row away. Revenge burns a thing down. This—" he tapped the brass rail, once, softly, "—this is a man protecting an investment. You don't burn down a house you think has your deed inside it."

"So you've known we're not fleeing a grudge. We're sitting on treasure."

"We're sitting on a question," he said. "Whether there's a ledger on this rock is another matter entirely."

"Do you believe there is?"

The cloth stopped moving. For a moment he was perfectly still, the way he went still sometimes—over Maren's trunk, over the burned letters, over the word *Verrin* spoken aloud—and I understood that stillness was not emptiness in this man. It was pressure. A whole sea held behind a wall, and the wall was habit.

"My wife," he said at last, "was the most careful person I have ever known. Careful the way sailors are careful—checking lines that were already checked. She hid things from me for twenty years. Small things, then larger ones. If such a book existed, and if it touched her family, she would not have left it where the family could lose it." He resumed polishing. "She would have left it where *she* could find it again. And she married a man who lives on a rock."

I felt the meaning land in my chest like a dropped anchor. "You think she hid it *here*."

"I think," Coral Amberdale said, "that I have lived in her hiding place for nineteen years without knowing it, and that tomorrow we are going to search this tower from the foundation stone to the ventilator cap, and that you—" he finally looked at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before, something almost like deference, "—you are better at finding what people hide than anyone I have ever met. You found my orphanage letters in four days."

"It took me six."

"Six, then. I'm hiring you anyway."

It was the closest thing to a joke I had ever heard him make, and I laughed—a short, cracked sound, inappropriate to the hour and the news, the kind of laugh that is really just fear changing shape. He almost smiled. On Coral Amberdale's face, an almost-smile is a festival.

Below us, faint through the floor, I could hear my father's footsteps pacing the kitchen, back and forth, back and forth, the rhythm of a man rehearsing. Pip's voice rose once, asking something, and fell silent at whatever answer he received.

And I stood in the lamp room with the beam wheeling overhead and understood that everything had changed while remaining exactly the same. An hour ago we were refugees—people to be smuggled out, bargained over, a berth to be won or lost. Now we were custodians. If the ledger was real, and if it was here, then Corven Hale did not hold all the cards. He held a deck with half its face cards missing, and he knew it, and that was why he negotiated instead of burning.

Leverage. The word sat in my mouth like a coin.

"They'll come again," I said. "Sooner now. He lost two men and a boat on our shoals. He can't leave that unanswered—it looks like weakness."

"Yes," Coral said. "So we'd best find it first."

"*We*," I repeated.

He wound the clockwork, thirty turns, unhurried. "You're crew," he said, as though it were the most obvious fact on the coast, as though he had not spent three weeks keeping us at arm's length like paying guests. "Crew searches together."

I went down the stairs to tell my father, and to decide—with great care, and alone, and before dawn—how much of what I'd learned in Maren's trunk I was finally ready to say out loud.
