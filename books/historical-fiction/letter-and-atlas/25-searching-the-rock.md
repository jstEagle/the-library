## 25. Searching the Rock

The fisherman's tale sat in the kitchen like a third man at the table, eating nothing and taking up all the room. *She hid something. Hale wants it. A ledger.* Two sentences, and suddenly the tower I had lived in for nineteen years was no longer a tower. It was a locked box, and I had been sleeping inside the lock.

"We tear it apart," Sable said. She said it the way she did everything—like a door being kicked open. "Every room. Every stone."

"It's my lighthouse," I said, because someone had to say something foolish.

"It's a rock with a lamp on it," she said. "And somewhere on this rock is the reason a man with a sloop has been circling us like a gull over a fish head. I'd rather find it before he does."

Aldous, who had spent the morning being carefully useless in the way of men rehearsing innocence, cleared his throat. "Maren wouldn't have hidden anything here. She never came back after—"

"After what, Father?" Sable asked pleasantly.

"After the marriage," Aldous finished, and looked at the window instead of at me.

I let that lie where it fell. I had gotten good at letting things lie where they fell. It is the principal skill of a lighthouse keeper, after all—letting things lie where they fall, and sweeping up the gulls.

Pip, who had been feeding marmalade to the cat under the table, looked up. "Are we digging for treasure?"

"Of a sort," Sable said.

"Is it cursed?"

"Almost certainly," she said, and Pip grinned like she'd promised him a pony.

* * *

We began, sensibly, with the rooms. The tower has fewer of them than you'd think: the kitchen, the oil store, my sleeping room, the room the Verrins had taken over with their damp wool and their whispered conferences, the watch room, the lamp room. Nineteen years of my life, and it took Sable forty minutes to map it on the back of a supply list.

"Storage first," she said. "People hide things where nobody looks."

"Nobody looks in the storage because there's nothing in it," I said.

"Then this will go quickly, won't it."

There is no winning an argument with Sable Verrin. There is only the dignity of losing it slowly.

So we pulled the storerooms apart. We took down the crates of lamp oil and checked beneath the false bottoms, of which there were none. We emptied the flour tins, the salt pork casks, the barrel of fresh water. We unspooled rope coils that had hung untouched since before Maren died, and I confess I flinched when Sable's hands went through them—those were Maren's coils, or they had been, in the first year, when she still climbed the stairs with me and learned the knots and laughed at how seriously I took them. A rope is a rope, she'd said. You name your boats and your storms, Coral, not your rope.

I had named the rope. I had never told anyone that. And now a girl of twenty-two was shaking it out looking for a smuggler's accounts book, and I could not even be angry, because she was right to.

"Nothing," Sable said, coiling it back up. She looked at me. "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you go somewhere else. Your wife's been dead—what, nineteen years?—and you still flinch like she's going to catch us at it."

"She would have," I said. "Caught us at it. She had opinions about my rope."

Something moved across Sable's face—not a smile, but the shadow where a smile keeps its coat. "Tell me about her. While we work. It goes faster if you talk."

It did not go faster. But I talked.

* * *

We went through the kitchen by lamplight that evening, because the days were shortening and the great storms were gathering their breath out past the shoals. Sable pried up the floorboards one by one, and I held the lamp, and Pip crawled into the cavity beneath with a candle stub and reported, with the gravity of a mine inspector, "Spiders. Big ones. One of them has a hat."

"Spiders don't have hats," Sable said.

"This one does."

"Nothing," she concluded, replacing the boards. "Next."

We did the sleeping rooms. We did the oil store, which Aldous insisted on searching himself, alone, for a suspicious length of time, and which yielded nothing but the smell of kerosene and one of Aldous's coughs. We did the watch room, going through the logbooks—my logbooks, twenty years of weather and lamp-hours and the small notations of a small life. Sable turned the pages with strange gentleness.

"You write like a printer," she said.

"Nothing else to do with the evenings."

"You could have written anything. Letters. A book. People do."

"People who want to be read," I said.

She stopped turning pages. "And you didn't."

It wasn't a question, but it deserved an answer, and I found I didn't have one that would hold weight. I had wanted to be unread. I had built a life the way you build a wall, and I had called the wall a vocation. Maren had understood that about me before I did. Maybe that was why she'd chosen me—or why, in the end, she hadn't told me things. You don't post letters through a wall.

"Nothing in the logs," Sable said at last, closing the last book. "Unless it's in cipher."

"It's not in cipher. It's in weather."

"Everything's in something, Uncle Coral." She said *Uncle Coral* the way she'd started saying it a week ago—testing it, like a plank over water. "The question is whether she trusted you with the key."

I did not answer that either, and she let me not answer, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

It was on the third day, going over the ground a second time because first times are for amateurs, that the thought arrived—the one that changed the shape of everything.

We were in the watch room. Rain had come in the night and stood on the glass in beads. Sable was going through the chart drawer, and I was sitting with the barometer and not looking at it, and the thought came to me the way weather comes: not all at once but as a change in the pressure of the air.

Maren had hidden the ledger *here*. If the fisherman was right—if Hale wanted a thing Maren had taken from him, and if that thing was not in Penzance, not in her sister's attic, not buried in some churchyard—then it was here, on Gannet Rock, in the one place on earth that belonged to me.

Which meant she had brought it here.

Which meant she had come here—before the marriage, or after it, or in one of those years I had folded away and labeled *visiting her people*—and she had stood on this rock, in this tower, and hidden a book that could hang a dozen men, while I was winding the clock or trimming the wick or speaking to the gulls. She had done it under my nose. Or—worse—she had done it with my nose as the plan. I was not a husband she deceived. I was a hiding place she furnished.

And then she had died, and left me here, guarding her secret the way a dog guards a house it doesn't know it's guarding.

"Coral." Sable was watching me again. She was always watching me. "You've gone somewhere."

"Everywhere she went," I said, "she was already planning the way back. That's what I'm realizing. I thought she married me and left her old life behind like a coat. But she kept the coat. She kept it *here*."

"You think she meant to come back."

"I think—" I stopped. The truth of it was larger than the sentence I could build for it. "I think I was never the destination, Sable. I was the anchor. And an anchor doesn't get told where the ship goes."

Sable was quiet a moment. Then, not unkindly: "Or she was keeping the worst of it away from you. There's a difference between an anchor and a harbor, and you might be the second one."

I had not considered that. I found I could not consider it yet, either. It was too warm a thought to hold in a cold room, and I had spent nineteen years practicing cold.

"Keep searching," I said.

* * *

We searched the stairs—every tread lifted, every newel post drilled and sounded. We searched the gallery rail, the ventilator balls, the rain hood. We went over the lamp room itself, though I drew the line at dismantling the lens; Sable looked at me a long moment and then, to her credit, drew the line with me. Some things are not hiding places. Some things are just the light.

By the fifth day the tower looked like a shipwreck of its own. Drawers on the floor. Mattresses standing on end. The cat, who had watched the whole enterprise with the contempt of the professionally unbothered, now had nowhere to sit that wasn't evidence.

"We've missed it," Aldous said, wringing his hands by the stove. "Or it isn't here. Or the fisherman was wrong. Or—"

"Or we're thinking like searchers," Sable said, "and we should think like Maren."

"And how did Maren think?" I asked.

Sable looked at me. "Like you," she said. "That's the thing nobody in this family ever understood. She married a keeper because she thought like one. She didn't hide things where nobody looks. She hid them where everybody looks, every single day, and sees nothing."

I sat with that. Where everybody looks and sees nothing.

The lamp. The stairs. The clock. The foundation—

But I'm ahead of myself. That evening we were tired, and the barometer had begun, very slowly, like a man changing his mind, to fall, and Pip had gone quiet in the corner with the cat, doing whatever a boy does with a cat and a candle stub and a secret. Aldous had gone to bed early, or to his scheming, which from the outside look identical. Sable stood in the middle of the wrecked kitchen with her hands on her hips, surveying the ruin of my orderly life, and said:

"Tomorrow we start on the structure itself. Walls, foundations. The places the tower and the rock meet."

"Those stones haven't moved in sixty years," I said.

"Neither had your rope," said Sable Verrin, and went up to bed.

I sat a while longer with the lamp turned low, listening to the sea work the rock below, and I thought about my wife walking these stairs with a book against her heart, past me, around me, through me—me, the man who prided himself on seeing everything that came near his light. She had watched me watch the water for nineteen years, and the whole time the thing worth watching had been standing in the room, wearing an apron, humming a tune I never asked her to explain.

The cat jumped down from Pip's corner and trotted past me toward the stairs, and behind it came Pip himself, candle in hand, moving with the exaggerated quiet of a boy who believes quiet is a costume. He paused at the foot of the tower stair. He looked back at me. He looked, I noticed, at the floor—specifically at the great foundation stone at the base of the stair, the first stone laid, the one every keeper touches for luck on his first watch.

Then he looked at me again, with an expression I had last seen on his aunt's face, and said, "Uncle Coral? Where does the cat *go*?"

I did not have an answer. The cat, I realized, had always had an answer. And the barometer, all that day, had been falling like a held breath about to be let out.
