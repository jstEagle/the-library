## 28. The Sloop Returns

I did not sleep. I want that recorded plainly, because there is a kind of vanity in pretending otherwise—men like to say they "rested with one eye open," as though vigilance were a muscle they'd been training for all their lives. The truth is uglier and simpler: I lay on my cot in the room beneath the lamp and listened to the sea make its small noises against the rock, the way it has every night of my keeping, and every noise sounded rehearsed.

The sloop came in at half past nine by my watch, which is to say she came in at the hour when honest vessels are either anchored or ashore, and dishonest ones are deciding what they'd like to be mistaken for. She stood into the roadstead on the last of the ebb, took her sails down without fuss—no scrambling, no shouted orders—and dropped anchor with the neat economy of a ship that has done this before, here or somewhere very much like it. Her riding light went up. One lantern, swinging gently, orange as a coal.

"She's anchored proper," Aldous said from the doorway. He had not slept either; none of us had, though Pip had gone down eventually, curled on Sable's coat with the cat across his feet, because children are built with a valve the rest of us lose, and theirs closes when the day is done whether we approve or not.

"Anchoring proper is the whole point of her," I said. "A man who means harm comes in fast and loud. A man who wants something comes in polite."

"You've met his sort before, then."

"I've kept a light for thirty years, Aldous. Every sort has passed this rock." I said it more sharply than the sentence deserved, but the night had earned some sharpness. "Get below. If he's come to talk, he'll want an audience of one, and if he's come to do worse, he'll find the door barred and the family out of sight."

"And you'll bar it?"

"I'll decide what's true when he says it. That's harder than barring a door."

He went, grumbling in the low way he grumbles, like a kettle deciding against boiling. Sable stayed. She had taken up a post at the gallery rail with the telescope and the particular stillness she does—that ledger-keeper's stillness, everything counted, nothing given away. I have begun to think of her face as a page I can read only upside down.

"He's lowering a boat," she said.

* * *

I dressed for the landing stage the way I would dress for church, which is to say I put on the good coat Maren once mended at the elbow with thread two shades too dark, and then I stood before the little mirror in the washroom and asked myself why in God's name a man polishes buttons to meet a smuggler. The answer, when it came, was not flattering: because civility is armor too, and I intended to wear mine better than he wore his.

The white rag was already flying when I reached the stage—a strip of canvas, bleached and salt-stiffened, lashed to the boat's short mast. It moved in the windless grey morning with the limp sincerity of all flags that mean nothing except *wait*. Behind it sat four men at the oars and, in the stern sheets, one man sitting as though the boat were a parlor and he were expecting tea.

Corven Hale.

I had never seen him. That must be understood—I had no portrait, no description, nothing but twenty years of Aldous's silences and Maren's absences to build a shape from. And yet I knew him before the boat was thirty yards off, the way you know which knock at your door is the tax man's. He was a broad, quiet-looking man in a plain dark coat, hair going grey at the temples in an orderly fashion, hands folded over a stick across his knees. He looked like a shipping agent. He looked like a man whose name is on a subscription list at the chapel. He looked like every respectable evil I had read about in newspapers and never quite believed in, because the papers always give them too many teeth.

The boat came alongside without a scrape. A hand in a jersey threw a line, and I caught it, and I will confess that catching it felt like signing something.

"Keeper Amberdale." Hale's voice matched the rest of him: level, unhurried, pitched for drawing rooms rather than decks. "I'm obliged to you for coming down. I know how early it is."

"It isn't early," I said. "It's precisely when you meant it to be."

Something moved at the corners of his mouth—not a smile; a bookkeeping entry. "So I'm told you're a punctual household. May I come up? I won't keep you above a quarter hour, and I'll leave my men in the boat. You may count them as they sit."

"I can see them."

"Then you may also see I've left the muskets wrapped in oilcloth under the thwart, where they'll stay." He said it lightly, the way a man mentions where he's left his umbrella. "I'd take it as a courtesy, keeper. This is a call, not an action."

There are moments when a man learns what his spine is made of, and I am pleased—no, let me be accurate—let me be *relieved*, which is a smaller and more honest word, to report that mine held. I stepped back and gestured to the stair.

* * *

We did not go up to the tower. I would not bring him past my threshold, and he, to his credit—or whatever passes for credit in his ledger—did not ask. We walked instead along the shelf of rock below the station, the tide low and the weed shining, gulls complaining overhead about the intrusion the way gulls complain about weather, which is to say on principle and without hope of remedy.

For perhaps forty yards neither of us spoke. He walked well on wet stone, I noticed. A careful man even with his boots.

"You'll wonder how I knew your name," he said at last.

"The letter told me already. It seems everyone connected to my wife knows me and I know none of them. There's a symmetry there I don't care for."

"Maren spoke of you." He said the name carefully, setting it down between us like a coin he wasn't sure I'd accept at face value. "More than you'd guess. More than she ever spoke of us, I imagine."

"You imagine correctly. She spoke of you not at all."

"Ah." He received that the way a banker receives news of a bad harvest in a province he doesn't hold. "Well. Twenty-two years is long enough for a great many things to be not-spoken."

We stopped at the flat slab where I land the supply crates. The morning was the color of unpolished pewter, and the sloop rode at her moorings beyond us, patient as a debt.

"You've come for the ledger," I said.

His eyebrows rose a fraction—an actor's touch, and a good one. "You know about the ledger."

"I've had a fisherman's account of it, a letter's threats about it, and a dead woman's silence concerning it. Between the three, I've assembled most of a picture. You want what she hid. You believe it's here."

"And is it?"

"That," I said, "is the question I've been turning over since before dawn, Mr. Hale, and I'll thank you to notice I haven't answered it yet, because answering it is the only card I own and I don't intend to slap it down on wet rock."

He laughed. It was a real laugh, short and warm at the edges, and it frightened me more than any threat could have, because it was the laugh of a man entirely comfortable, and comforted men negotiate from heights.

"There it is," he said. "That's the voice. Maren had it too—the same stubborn arithmetic. She'd count a thing three ways before she'd spend a word on it." He turned to look out at the water, giving me his profile, which was a kindness or a tactic. "Let me be plain with you, keeper, because I respect plainness and I suspect you've had a season of everything else. I don't want revenge. Revenge is for young men and novelists. I want paper."

"Paper."

"A ledger in Maren's hand. Names, sums, dates. Officials from here to the customs house at Wexmouth who took our money for a decade and wrote their receipts in her bookkeeping. If it surfaces—if a magistrate ever reads it—half the coast's respectable men go down, and they'll drag the rest of us with them, guilty and innocent alike, and there will be no sorting done politely." He paused. "Your wife understood that better than anyone alive, once. She made it herself. Every entry."

"And now she's dead, and you've come to collect from her widower."

"Now she's dead," he agreed, "and I've come to negotiate with the man who keeps the only light between Herring Ness and the shoals, and who has, I'm told, a family sheltering upstairs that the winter channel will cut off within the fortnight." He looked at me then, fully, and his eyes were pale grey and perfectly dry. "I mention the family because I'd rather we both admit what's on the table. I've bought the fourth berth on the *Kittiwake*, keeper. The one that was going to force your arithmetic. I bought it weeks ago through a man in Penzance who never knew my name. So the choice you've been losing sleep over—three berths, four souls—was never going to be yours to make. Unless we make it together."

The sea filled the pause. It is good at that; it has practice.

"You're saying," I said slowly, "that you hold the escape hatch."

"I'm saying I hold a key to it. Whether it opens depends entirely on the next quarter hour." He set the stick he carried down on the slab, deliberately, within view, and folded his hands. "Here are terms. Not final ones—I'm not so foolish as to present final ones to a man holding the only copy of his leverage. But a shape of them, so you can sleep tonight, or fail to, with better information."

I said nothing. The gulls said plenty.

"One: the ledger comes to me intact, uncopied, unread by any magistrate. Two: in exchange, the *Kittiwake* takes four passengers when she calls—yourself included if you choose it, though I notice you haven't mentioned yourself in any accounting yet, which tells me something. Three: the Verrin name goes back in my book under *closed*, not *owed*. No man of mine touches them, follows them, or writes to them, so long as no page of that ledger reaches a magistrate's desk. Four—" and here he hesitated, the first true hesitation, and I marked it the way you mark a loose plank, "—four, I'd have your assurance that Maren's reasons stay buried with her. Whatever you found in her trunk. Whatever you think of me. Some things are kinder drowned."

"You speak of her," I said, "as though you loved her."

The silence that followed had texture. I have heard silence in churches and silence in courtrooms—in the one, reverence; in the other, dread—but this was neither. It was the silence of a man deciding how much truth a negotiation can bear.

"I loved her the way you love a lighthouse," he said at last. "From the water. Knowing she wasn't for me. Knowing she'd mark the rocks I'd strike on if I came too close." He picked up his stick. "She informed on us to save her brother, and I hanged two good men for it before I learned whose hand wrote the letter, and by then she was married to a keeper on a rock, and chasing her would have been the one cruelty even I balked at. So I waited. And now I'm old, and tired, and I want the paper and the ending, in that order."

"You'll forgive me," I said, "if the confession of a man who hanged two people doesn't soften the terms."

"I wouldn't respect you if it did."

* * *

He did not press for an answer. That was the worst of it. He gathered his men with a nod, stepped down into the boat with the ease of a man stepping into his own garden, and only then, one hand on the gunwale, looked back up at me.

"You'll want time. Take today and tomorrow. Send nothing out; I'll see anything you send. When you've decided, hang a signal from the gallery—a white cloth if the answer's yes, and if it's no..." He smiled, and for one instant the drawing-room fell away and I saw the water underneath. "If it's no, fly nothing at all, and I'll understand you've chosen to keep your paper and your pride, and I'll act accordingly. I'm told the great storms come early this year."

"They always say that," I said. "They're usually wrong."

"This year," said Corven Hale, "they aren't."

The boat pulled away under the white rag, which flapped once, twice, and hung slack, and the sloop weighed anchor within the hour and stood off toward the horizon without hurry, the way a creditor leaves a parlor—slowly, so the leaving itself is a reminder.

* * *

Sable was waiting at the top of the stair. She had Pip by the shoulder, and Aldous stood behind them both wearing the face of a man who has heard every word through a grate and wishes he hadn't. I climbed up into their silence.

"Well?" Aldous said. "What did he offer?"

"He offered everything," I said. "That's how I know it costs everything. Get inside, all of you. Boil water. We're going to do what families do."

"We're not a family," Aldous said, before he could stop himself, and then had the grace to look stricken.

"No," I agreed, hanging my mended coat on its peg, feeling the dark thread at the elbow like a scar. "Not yet. But we've got a ledger, a deadline, and a storm coming, and in my experience those three things build one faster than blood does."

Pip tugged my sleeve. His face was grave with a child's terrible arithmetic.

"If you say yes," he asked, "does everybody get to go?"

And I looked down at him—at the one soul on this rock whose safety I had already decided outranked my own, my name, and every page Maren ever wrote—and found, for the first time in my life, that I could not make my mouth form even a bad lie.

"Come inside, lad," I said. "We need to talk about what yes costs."
