## 32. Sabotage at Dawn

I have kept enough watches to know that a man's character shows itself at three in the morning. That is when the lies we tell by daylight get tired and lie down, and whatever is underneath them stands up in its nightshirt and looks around. I had spent twenty years at Gannet Rock learning what was underneath me. It turned out to be mostly lamp oil, arithmetic, and a stubbornness my mother would have recognized.

What was underneath Aldous Verrin, I learned on the fourth of November, an hour before dawn, when I woke to the sound of a drawer closing that should not have been closed by anyone but me.

I sleep with one ear open. This is not a figure of speech; it is a professional requirement. A keeper who sleeps like the dead is a keeper whose light goes dark while some smack full of herring men drives onto the Teeth. So I heard it—the soft wooden knock of the logbook drawer in the desk, the one where I had put Maren's ledger after Pip found it under the foundation stone—and I heard something else besides: bare feet on iron stairs, going down, not up.

Nobody goes down at that hour except to the water.

I did not light a lamp. I know those stairs the way you know your own teeth—by count and by ache—and I went down them in the dark with the particular silence available only to men who have lived alone so long that silence is their native language. The tower was cold in the way towers are cold, cold from the inside out, and through the oil store window there was a sky the color of wet slate, with a thin gruel of light beginning at the east.

The oil store door stood open six inches.

Now, I want to be fair to Aldous, because I have since had occasion to be unfair to him in every other direction and balance matters. He was not a thief by trade. He was a man who had spent forty years being small in rooms built for larger men—a younger brother, a failed partner, a widower's poor relation—and he had come to believe, the way water comes to believe it flows downhill, that survival meant moving before anyone could see you. I understood this. Understanding it did not make me any less furious. Fury and understanding are not enemies; they are bunkmates, and they snore all night.

I came through the oil store door without touching it and found him crouched over the canvas sack where I kept the ledger wrapped in oilcloth, his back to me, his hands working at the knot with fingers that shook so badly he might as well have been trying to pick up fishhooks.

"Aldous," I said.

He made a sound I will charitably call a word and dropped the sack entirely. The ledger hit the flagstones with a thud that went through my boots and up my spine, because that book was Maren, or what was left of her that wasn't letters, and I felt the impact somewhere below the ribs.

"You'll wake the boy," I said.

That stopped him better than a pistol would have. He looked at Pip's door the way a drowning man looks at shore, and then he looked at me, and I saw the whole plan laid out on his face like washing on a line: take the ledger, take the skiff, row out to the sloop before first light, hand Corven Hale what he wanted, and buy his family's lives with his sister's last possession. Simple. Clean. The arithmetic of a coward, which is always correct and always worthless.

"Give it here," Aldous whispered. "Coral. Give it to me and I'll go and you'll never—I'll tell him the ledger was mine, that Maren took nothing, that—"

"That you let her hang the blame on herself for twenty years," I said, "and now you'd like to complete the transaction."

His face did something complicated. I watched him try on indignation, found it didn't fit, tried on grief, which fit better but was secondhand, and settled at last on the plain naked fear that had been underneath all along.

"She was my sister," he said.

"Yes," I said. "And she knew that better than anyone. It's why she never wrote to me about you until the end."

I picked up the ledger. I did not hand it over. I held it against my chest the way you hold a child you are about to carry out of a burning room, and I stepped between Aldous and the door.

* * *

We stood there perhaps two minutes, two grown men in an oil store smelling of sperm oil and hemp, and I confess that part of me—the part that had buried Maren and her whole treacherous, magnificent past in one grave and poured concrete over it—wanted simply to let him go. Row out. Give Hale his prize. Let the sloop weigh anchor and be gone, and let the rest of us keep our three berths and our quiet rock.

But I had read Maren's letter. I had read it eleven times by then, once for each year she had lied to me and once more for good measure, and I knew what she had asked. *Protect them, Coral. They are foolish and frightened and they are the family you didn't know you had.* She had known even then—had known it years ago, sitting at this very desk, hiding a ledger under my floor—that Aldous would run. You do not write instructions for a fire unless you can smell the smoke.

"The tide's wrong," I told him. "You'd be rowing against the ebb with a following wind gone foul. You'd be halfway to the sloop by noon, if Hale's men didn't pluck you out of the water like a cork first."

"They won't hurt me," he said. "I'm bringing them what they want."

"Hale bought a berth on the supply boat, Aldous. A berth. Do you think a man who buys a seat at a table does it to feed you? He wants the ledger and he wants no witnesses, and you're proposing to hand him both in one boat."

"He gave his word about the others."

"He gave terms," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole width of the sea."

He sat down then, abruptly, on the coil of spare hawser, the way old men sit when their legs vote independently of their pride, and put his face in his hands. And here is the thing I could not have predicted, though perhaps I should have: looking down at him, all my anger went strange on me. It didn't leave. Anger doesn't leave a man like me; it just changes quarters. But standing over Aldous Verrin crumpled on a rope coil, shaking like a man with ague, I felt something arrive beside the anger that I liked even less.

Pity.

Because I saw it plainly, the way you see the bottom in shallow water: Aldous was a small man in a large storm. Not wicked—not in the way Hale was wicked, which was a clean, patient, well-lit wickedness—but weak in the load-bearing places. He had let Maren carry his debt and his danger because she offered and because accepting was easier than refusing, and he had spent twenty years telling himself the story that made him smallest in it, and now the story had caught up with him wearing Corven Hale's face, and his only remaining move was to run toward the thing that terrified him and beg.

I had spent twenty years being invisible myself. The difference between us was that I had chosen my invisibility, and he had been assigned his, and neither of us had ever once been brave at the moment it counted.

"Get up," I said.

"I can't go back to the mainland. If I'm there when Hale's people come asking—"

"You're not going anywhere." I set the ledger on the shelf above the lamp stores, deliberately, where he could see me do it. "And neither is this. Sit there."

"Coral—"

"Sit *there*, Aldous, or I'll wake Sable, and you may explain to your daughter why her father was robbing a dead woman's grave in the dark."

That got through. Whatever else Aldous feared—Hale, the sea, poverty, the truth—it was plain that Sable's opinion of him was the deepest water in his chart. He stayed on the hawser. I lit the small storm lantern, the one with the ruby pane, so as not to blind us both, and I sat down across from him on the powder keg I used for a stool, and for a while neither of us said anything, and the wind worked at the shutters like a bill collector.

* * *

"Why her?" I asked finally.

He looked up. His eyes in the red lantern-light were wet and ordinary, a clerk's eyes, a bookkeeper's eyes. "Why did she do it, you mean."

"No. I've read her letter. I know why she did it." I kept my voice level, though it cost something. "I mean why her. Why did it fall to her? You were the man. The brother. The head of the family, such as it was. Why did a woman of four-and-twenty pay your debts to a smuggler with her own neck?"

"It wasn't debts," Aldous said. "Not exactly."

"Not exactly," I repeated. The phrase hung between us like a bad smell.

"It was surety," he said. "I'd signed paper for Corven Hale. Not smuggling—not at first. Freight. Storage. A warehouse interest in Penzance that I thought was honest, Coral, I swear I thought—" He stopped, heard himself, and had the grace to look ashamed. "No. By the third signature I knew. I knew and I signed anyway, because the interest was ruinous and the alternative was debtor's gaol, and once your name is in Hale's book you don't take it out. You can't. The book keeps you."

"So when the revenue men started asking questions—"

"When they came to the house," he said, "asking after the Verrin interest, I was in bed with a fever, and Maren answered the door, and she looked at these two officers with their notebooks, and I heard her say, from upstairs, clear as a bell: *the Verrin interest is my brother's warehouse, gentlemen, and I keep his books.*" He laughed, a horrible little sound with no humor in it whatsoever. "She volunteered. Before I could speak. She walked into that interview and gave them names and dates and landings, everything from the ledgers I'd been keeping, and she told them it was all hers. Her information. Her risk. And afterward she said to me—"

He stopped.

"Say it," I said. "You've carried it this far; it can't weigh more than it does."

"She said, 'You have children to think of.'" His voice cracked on *children*. "Sable was nine. And I let her believe I believed it. I let everyone believe it. When the ring marked the family, when the threats began, I moved my household twice and said nothing, and when Papa disowned her over the scandal—over the *scandal*, Coral, not the informing, the shame of it—I said nothing then either. And when she married you and cut us off, I told myself she'd chosen her path, that a woman who informed has made her bed, and I slept at night, do you understand? I slept. Twenty years of sleeping."

The lantern flame bent in a draft. Somewhere above us Pip turned over in his bunk; we both heard the creak and both froze, absurdly, two conspirators in an oil store, and then the house settled again.

"And now," Aldous said, "now she's dead, and Hale thinks a Verrin betrayed him, and he's right, only it isn't the Verrin he thinks, and the one person who could say so is under the foundation stone of this lighthouse." He raised his head and looked at me, and for one moment there was something in his face that wasn't fear or shame but a terrible plain honesty, and it was worse than all the rest. "I was going to give him the ledger and tell him it was me. All of it me. Let him do what he liked with me, if Sable and the boy were safe. That's not nothing, Coral. Tell me that's not nothing."

It wasn't nothing. That was the damnable part. It was the first brave thing Aldous Verrin had done in twenty years, and it was stupid, and suicidal, and three weeks too late, and built entirely on handing a murderer his own acquittal in writing. But it wasn't nothing. Courage is like that. It arrives late, dressed badly, and you can't send it away just because you're ashamed of it.

"No," I said. "It's not nothing. It's also not happening."

* * *

I explained it to him the way I'd explain a lamp mechanism: slowly, with the parts named. If he rowed out and confessed, Hale had the ledger and the confessor and no reason left to bargain. If Hale burned the ledger, every bribed magistrate and customs man on the coast breathed easy, and the corruption Maren had died fighting stayed fat and safe forever. If Hale kept it, he owned half the coast's officialdom, and a man who owns the law doesn't need to honor it. Either way, Aldous's sacrifice bought nothing but his own disappearance and our eventual one.

"What then?" Aldous whispered. "What do we do?"

"We wait for the supply boat. We put three people on it. And whoever stays holds the ledger and holds the light, and makes sure Corven Hale understands that this rock is defended by something older than his threats." I stood. My knees announced their opinions; I ignored them. "Maren trusted this coast with her secrets for twenty years. The coast kept them. It can be relied upon."

"You mean you'll stay."

"I mean the arithmetic hasn't changed. Three berths, four souls. It was always going to come to this."

"But it shouldn't be *you*—" He was on his feet now, and God help me, there were tears on his face, real ones. "You've done nothing wrong. You're innocent in all of it. You're the only innocent in all of it!"

That landed harder than any blow Hale could have struck, because it wasn't true, and because he believed it, and because for a moment I wanted very much to believe it too—to be the man who'd merely married into the wreckage instead of helping build it. But I had chosen blindness for twenty years. Blindness is a decision. I had decided, over and over, not to ask Maren about the sealed drawers and the unexplained money and the way she watched the horizon at dusk, because asking risked answers, and answers risked losing her. Invisibility was always a choice. Mine had cost less than hers, but it hadn't been free.

"There's no such thing as innocent on this rock anymore," I said. "Go up to bed, Aldous."

He went. At the foot of the stairs he stopped, one hand on the rail, and said, without turning around: "She loved you, you know. In her letters home—all those years—every page had you in it. Even when she wouldn't write to us directly, she wrote about you to the orphanage, and the matron read them to us after." A pause. "I never told you that. I should have told you that a long time ago."

Then he climbed the stairs, and I heard his door close, and I stood alone in the oil store with the ruby lantern making everything look like the inside of an ember.

* * *

I did not go back to bed. I am fifty-eight years old and I have stopped pretending I can sleep after a thing like that. Instead I unwrapped the ledger and sat with it on my knees, reading the columns of names by red light—aldermen, customs officers, one name so high I made myself stop reading and put the book away—and I thought about Maren writing about me to strangers, year after year, while her own family pretended she was dead.

The east window had gone from slate to pewter to a thin, hard silver. Dawn was coming up over the water, and the sea lay flat and oiled, deceptively calm, the way it goes calm before it decides otherwise. Far out in the roadstead, the sloop rode at anchor, black-hulled, patient, her riding light still burning in the brightening air like a star that hadn't gotten the news.

And as I watched, the barometer in the passage—Maren's barometer, brass-cased, brought from her father's house—gave a single slow shudder, and the needle, which had been falling for two days, dropped like a bird shot off a wire.

By noon the glass stood lower than I had seen it in thirty winters on this rock. The wind had backed to the southeast and begun to build in long, deep pulses, like something enormous turning over in its sleep far out beyond the horizon. The gulls went inland. Every keeper knows what that means. Every keeper on this coast has said it to himself in the night, hoping to be wrong:

The second storm is coming. The great one. And it would reach us before the supply boat ever could.
