## 33. The Second Great Storm

### CORAL

The barometer had been falling since noon, and by four o'clock it fell the way a man falls when he stops pretending he isn't drunk—suddenly, and all at once, and with a kind of dignity that fools no one.

I stood in the watch room with the instrument's glass face against my thumb and read the number twice, as though it might be embarrassed into improving itself. It did not. Nine hundred and ninety-two millibars, and dropping. I have seen storms come to Gannet Rock for thirty years, and they come the way debt comes to a spendthrift: you can mark the signs for weeks, but the reckoning itself always arrives sooner than the arithmetic promised.

"The sloop," Sable said from the window.

I went to her. The *Kestrel's Fancy*—Hale's sloop, though I refuse on principle to call any vessel fancy—had anchored in the roadstead two days past, riding out the weather like a dog told to wait outside a shop. Now she was dragging. Her anchor cable came up taut, slacked, taut again, and each time she slacked she had traveled. Not much. A boat's length, perhaps two. But between her stern and the Outer Shoals lay perhaps six hundred yards of water that was getting shallower by the hour, and the sea had begun to break white over the shoal itself, which is the ocean's way of licking its lips.

"She'll fetch up or she'll founder," I said.

"Which would you prefer?"

It was an honest question and deserved an honest answer, and the honest answer was one I didn't care to say aloud with Pip in the room, mending a gull's wing with thread he'd stolen from my sewing kit and patience he'd inherited from nobody I could name. So I said, "I'd prefer she anchor properly," which was true, and also a lie, and Sable looked at me the way she looks at everything—like a clerk auditing a ledger—and let it go.

The wind arrived at dusk. Not gradually. Dusk came gray and then darker gray, and somewhere inside that darkening the wind simply *was*, full-grown, howling down out of the northeast with the smell of ice in it even though it was only October. The tower has stood on this rock through forty years of such nights, and it hums. Most people think a lighthouse is silent in a storm. It is not. It sings one long low note through its stones, like a hymn hummed by something very large and very patient, and after enough years you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as company.

"Watch rotation," I said, because routine is a rope and we were all holding onto whatever ropes we had. "Sable, you take first lamp watch with me. Aldous, second. Pip sleeps if he can."

"I can hear better than either of you," Pip said, not looking up from his gull. "And I don't sleep in storms anyway."

This was unfortunately accurate. The boy heard everything. He had been hearing everything for weeks—the arguments, the bargains, the arithmetic of berths—and carrying it around behind those solemn eyes like a coal he was keeping warm for someone. I thought about telling him it would be fine. I am a bad liar at the best of times, and to a boy who collects lies the way other boys collect marbles, it would have been an insult besides. I ruffled his hair instead, which he permitted, and went down to light the lamp while there was still enough daylight to see what I was doing.

By full dark the sea was coming over the rock itself. Not spray—*water*, whole green tons of it, slamming the lower gallery and running off the lantern like sweat off a laborer. The great storm of '89 had done this, and the one before it in '84, and each time I had stood in the lamp room feeling like a man inside a struck bell, and each time I had thought: *this is the one that takes the tower.* And each time the tower sang its note and held.

I trimmed the wick. I polished the lens, though it needed nothing. I did these things because they are the things, and because a keeper who does them badly in fair weather does them unforgivably in foul.

Through the salt-smeared glass, far out, the *Kestrel's Fancy* showed her riding light—one small flame pitching in the blackness, going up and down, up and down, and each time it came up it seemed slightly nearer the white water.

"Hold," I said to her. I don't know why I spoke. The lamp doesn't listen. "Just hold till morning."

The sea, indifferent, did not answer.

### SABLE

My father vomited into the scuttle bucket twice during his watch and blamed the stew, which was cowardice of a culinary kind, since he'd eaten three bowls of it. Coral pretended not to notice. Coral pretends not to notice a great many things, and I have spent seven weeks in his tower learning the difference between a man who doesn't see and a man who sees everything and files it away where no one can reach it.

He is not what I expected. That sentence has been forming in me since the day we landed here soaked and half-dead of fright, and every week it has grown more complicated rather than less. I expected a coward. My father called him a coward for twenty years—*the lighthouse man, the man who hid*—and I built my picture of Coral Amberdale out of that word the way you build anything from secondhand timber: crookedly, and with gaps.

But I have watched him now. I watched him teach my son to polish a lens worth more than our house. I watched him burn my aunt Maren's letters with hands that shook, which is not the act of a man without feeling; it is the act of a man with too much of it and nowhere lawful to put it. I watched him stand on the landing stage and negotiate with Corven Hale as though Hale were a fishmonger short-changing him on herring, and I saw something in Hale's face that I have never seen there before, which was uncertainty.

And tonight I watched him keep the light.

You must understand what that means in weather like this. The tower moves. Not visibly—it is stone, it is bedded into the living rock—but you feel it, a slow lean and recover, lean and recover, like a ship that has forgotten it isn't a ship. The stairs groan. The windows bleed wind. And through all of it, every two hours, the mechanism must be wound, the wick trimmed, the mercury bath checked, the lens turned by hand if the clockwork falters, because out there in the dark are boats with fathers and sons in them, and the light is the only word anyone speaks to them.

Coral does this work the way other men breathe. Without ceremony. Without, so far as I can tell, any awareness that it is remarkable.

At midnight I relieved him, or joined him—he never quite lets you relieve him alone—and we stood together in the drum of the lantern while the storm tried its key in every lock of the tower.

"My father says you hid," I said, over the wind. "All those years."

"I did hide." He didn't look at me. He was watching the sloop's light, or the place where the sloop's light should be. "Hiding is easy work. You'd be surprised how few people ever check whether you're doing anything else."

"And were you?"

"Doing anything else?" For the first time he looked at me, and in the lamplight his face was old and young at once, seamed like a chart but lit from underneath by something stubborn. "Yes. I just did it slowly."

The sloop's riding light went out.

Not dimmed—out. One moment a flame, the next a hole in the darkness, and both of us moved to the glass at once, and below us, faint under the storm's voice, came the sound of the surf changing its mind. When a sea breaks on a shoal it has a different voice than when it breaks on rock—a longer, deeper boom, a swallowed sound—and I had lived on this coast long enough, in these seven weeks, to know it.

"They're on the shoal," Coral said. His voice was very quiet and very level, the voice of a man setting down something heavy before picking up something heavier. "Or nearly. God help them, they're nearly on it."

And then, thin across the water, threading through the wind like wire through cloth, we heard it: a whistle. Three blasts, a pause, three blasts. The signal of a vessel in distress, blown by men who knew that the only ears in the world that might hear them belonged to a lighthouse they had come to destroy.

Coral closed his eyes for exactly one breath. I watched him do it. Whatever war was fought behind those eyelids, it was fought fast, and I already knew which side won, because I had known for weeks, and so had everyone in this tower except perhaps the cat.

"Get your father," he said. "Get the oilskins. Wake Pip—no. Leave Pip sleeping. Wake Aldous and tell him to ready the breeches buoy gear, and tell him—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell him I'm lighting the flare."

"You're going out."

"I'm going out."

"In *that*."

"Sable." He put his hand briefly, awkwardly, on my shoulder—a gesture so unfamiliar to him that it looked borrowed, like a coat worn wrong. "Out there right now are men drowning. Some of them deserve it. That's not mine to sort out in the dark. The sorting comes later, if there is a later. Tonight there's only the light and the line."

He turned to the lamp and began, with steady hands, to raise the flare.

### ALDOUS

I will set down plainly what I felt, because if this account survives me someone ought to know that I am capable of honesty, even if it arrives late and ugly and smelling of vomit.

When the whistle sounded across the water, my first feeling was hope.

There. I've written it. My first feeling, God forgive me, was that Corven Hale and his men were being taken by the same sea that had taken my fortune, my wife's health, and twenty years of my nerve—and that the ledger, the accursed ledger sleeping under this rock, would drown with them and free us all. I stood in the oil store with the buoy gear in my arms and I hoped for drowned men, and I am fifty-four years old and I hoped for drowned men.

Then Coral fired the flare.

I saw it from the gallery—a red star climbing into the black, opening like a wound, lighting the whole roadstead for one impossible second. And in that second I saw the shoal, white and boiling, and I saw the sloop on it, broadside, her mast going by degrees, and I saw—this is the part I cannot get out from behind my eyes—I saw figures. Small dark figures clinging to her hull, to her broken deck, to the rocks beyond her, waving.

Waving at the light.

They were waving at the light like children waving at a window, and Coral had made the light, and Coral was already moving down the stairs with the lifeline coiled on his shoulder, and I understood suddenly, completely, in the way you understand a wave has caught you only when it's too late to swim: that this man whom I had called a coward for twenty years was braver than I have ever been on my best day, and that he was going out into that sea to save the men who had hunted my family, and that the difference between us was not courage, exactly. It was that Coral had spent thirty years learning what a man owes to strangers, and I had spent thirty years learning what a man can talk himself out of.

"Aldous!" His voice through the speaking tube, torn by wind. "The mortar! Bring the mortar to the landing stage!"

I brought it. Of course I brought it. My legs carried me because my legs have always been more honest than the rest of me.

The rock was underwater in places, the sea sweeping the lower stage clean every third wave, and Coral stood at its edge in the roar with the mortar's breech braced against a granite shoulder of the island, working by flare-light, calm as Sunday. Water hit him bodily once, took him off his feet, threw him ten feet along the rock. I screamed something—I don't know what. He got up. He got up the way the tower gets up, the way the light gets up, lean and recover, lean and recover, and went back to the mortar.

"Line's dry?" he shouted.

"Dry!"

"Pray, then!"

And he loaded the rocket, and aimed it into the dark above the wreck where the figures waved, and fired.

The line went out trailing fire like something alive, arcing over the boiling shoal, and I lost it in the black, and then—faint, absurd, miraculous—we heard cheering. Cheering, from drowning men, carried to us on the wind like a gift wrapped in wreckage.

Coral sagged against the mortar, breathing hard, and looked back at me, and rain and seawater ran off his face, and he smiled. Actually smiled.

"Tell Sable," he shouted, "we'll want hot water. And tell her—"

The rest was eaten by the sea. A wave larger than its sisters came up the rock, and I saw him turn toward it too late, and I saw the light go out of the flare, and I saw the place where he had been standing hold nothing at all.

* * *

### PIP

I was supposed to be asleep. Nobody sleeps. The tower was singing its big low song and the whole world was trying to knock the door down, and Mama-Sable had gone down the stairs two at a time, and Grandfather had run past my door without seeing me, which is how I knew it was bad, because Grandfather sees everything except himself.

So I went to the window in the little room and pressed my nose to the glass, and I watched the red flare go up and fall down, and I watched the line of fire arc out over the water, and I cheered when the cheering came back across the sea, because even though those men were wicked—or some of them, or maybe all of them, nobody will say straight—they were cheering, and cheering is catching.

Then the big wave came, and the flare died, and the place where Mr. Amberdale had been standing was empty.

Empty like a missing tooth.

I did not decide to do what I did next. My legs decided, and my heart went along to carry the lantern. I was down the stairs before I knew I was moving, past Mama-Sable shouting my name, out onto the wet rock in my nightshirt with the wind trying to peel me off the earth like a stamp, and I ran to the edge where the line stretched away into the dark, and I grabbed it with both hands.

The line pulled. Once. Twice.

Something at the far end of it was pulling *back*.

"MAMA-SABLE!" I screamed, and the wind took most of it, but she was already there, already falling on the line beside me, her hands over mine, both of us hauling, and from the black water at the rock's foot came a sound that I will hear until I die and be glad every time:

A cough. Then a voice, rough as gravel, gasping between waves:

"PULL, damn you—PULL—"
