## 34. Light for Enemies

The barometer had been falling since noon, and by the time the light came around to its first full turn of the evening watch, it had fallen so far that I half expected the needle to bore through the glass and keep going, down through the table, down through the floorboards, all the way to the rock itself. I have read that a barometer is only a lie detector for the sky. If so, this one was telling the truth with a vengeance.

I trimmed the wicks. I wound the clockwork. I said nothing to the gulls, because there were no gulls; even they had better sense than to be out.

The wind arrived before the rain did, which is the sea's usual courtesy—warning you like a gentleman before it knocks you down like a drunkard. It came out of the southwest in long, deliberate shoves, each one leaning against the tower as though testing whether we'd tip. The Gannet Rock tower has stood through forty years of such testing and has never once tipped, though it groans about it afterward like an old man descending stairs.

Below me I could hear the household at its work: Sable in the oil store, checking and re-checking the reserve tanks, her footsteps brisk and exact, a woman making order against chaos the only way she knew how, which was to count things. Pip was supposed to be asleep but was audibly not. Aldous was pacing the length of the kitchen in a rhythm I could set my own pulse by, four steps up, four steps back, the walk of a man rehearsing arguments he would never win.

And out beyond the shoals, dragging anchor in the rising dark, lay the *Corvina*.

Hale's sloop.

I want to be honest here, because if a man can't be honest in his own logbook of the soul, where can he be: when I first saw her lights go out and heard her cable start to sing, some small mean part of me—the part I keep locked in the same trunk as Maren's burned letters—whispered, *let her drag.*

Let the storm do what you haven't the stomach to do yourself. Let the sea settle accounts. You won't even have to dirty your hands. The rock will take her the way it took the *Maiden's Choice* in '81, the way it takes anything foolish enough to trust its luck near these shoals, and come morning there will be no sloop, no Hale, no ledger to trade, no bargain to strike. Just wreckage and quiet and the Verrins safe, and Coral Amberdale free to go back to being nobody, which is the profession he has practiced longest and best.

That voice is a liar, but it lies fluently, and I listened longer than I should have.

Then I went up to the lamp room and turned the burner to full.

* * *

You should understand what "full" means on a night like that. Full means every wick trimmed to a hair, every valve opened wide, oil feeding flame at a rate that eats our winter reserve like a hungry boy eats bread. Full means the lens turning its great patient wheel above a fire hot enough to blister paint at arm's length. Full means a beam thrown twenty miles over water that wants to kill everything on it—and among the things it wants to kill, tonight, are the men who came to destroy us.

A lighthouse does not distinguish. That is the whole creed, the entire catechism, the beginning and end of it. A light shines for the honest master and the smuggler alike, for the fishing smack and the gun-runner, for the fool and the villain and the child asleep below decks who never chose any of it. The Trinity House men write it in their regulations in dry official language, but it comes down to something simpler, something Maren might have said if Maren had ever kept a light: *you do not get to decide who deserves to see.*

I had spent three days deciding whether Corven Hale deserved to drown. The lamp decided in three seconds. That is why we build machines to do our mercy—it spares us the temptation of arithmetic.

Though I will confess to one moment of weakness. Around ten o'clock, between turns, I stood at the lantern glass with my hand on the ventilator lever, and I thought: *one hour dark. One hour while she drags. No one will ever know but the gulls.* And then I looked down at my own hand, this weathered keeper's hand that had wound this clockwork six thousand times without missing a night, and I understood that I would know. That I would carry it the way Aldous carries his cowardice, quietly, everywhere, forever, until it fit me as badly as his fits him.

I turned the lever the wrong direction from the one the weakness wanted. Full burn. Let Hale see us coming, if seeing was what saved him.

* * *

The rain hit at half past ten, horizontal, hard as gravel. By eleven the sea had organized itself into something less like waves and more like opinions—enormous, conflicting, and delivered at volume. The tower shook on every third blow. In the lamp room I could feel the vibration in my teeth.

Sable came up the stairs just after eleven, oilskin streaming, and had to shout to be heard over the ventilation.

"Reserve's good till morning if we don't waste it!"

"We're burning full!" I shouted back.

"I know! I checked your valves twice!" She braced herself against the frame as the tower took a blow that made the whole structure ring. "Pip's awake. He felt the tower move."

"Did you tell him it always moves?"

"He told *me*. Said you told him." She wiped rain off her face with a hand already soaked. "He said, 'Uncle Coral says towers are just rocks that learned to stand up.' Is that what you've been teaching him?"

"It's true, isn't it?"

She almost smiled. On another night, in another life, she might have finished the smile. Then she looked past me, out the seaward panes, and the smile died somewhere in transit.

"There." Her finger cut the rain. "Off the shoal buoy. Did you see—"

I had seen. A single flare, red, brief, swallowed by the rain—a distress signal, or a piece of lightning playing games, or a man on a doomed deck striking a match against his own ending. Thirty seconds later the wind brought us a sound underneath the storm, a sound I had heard twice before in my keeping and hoped never to hear again: timber working loose of timber, the particular scream a hull makes when the sea begins arguing it apart.

"She's dragging," I said. "Onto the Outer Shoal."

"The Outer—" Sable did the chart in her head; she'd studied it more carefully than any of us, because studying things was how she loved them. "That's the worst ground on this coast. She'll break up in twenty minutes."

"Fifteen," I said, having done the same arithmetic years ago and never forgotten the answer.

We looked at each other in the swaying light. Everything that needed saying got said without a word, which is the economy of people who have shared a small tower through a hard season: *Do we try? Can we? Who goes?*

"Get the breeches buoy gear to the lower gallery," I said. "Wake Aldous. Keep Pip below and tell him—I don't know. Tell him whatever you tell him. Tell him the truth, he hears worse from the walls anyway."

She was already moving. At the stairhead she stopped, rain running off her chin, and shouted one thing more:

"Coral! Whatever's out there on those rocks—if it's them—"

"I know," I said.

"No," she shouted, and the wind nearly ate it, and she fought it back. "I mean—you could let them drown and sleep fine after. Anyone would understand. Pip would never know. *I'd* never tell him."

"That's rather the problem with mercy, isn't it," I called back. "It only counts if someone could know and doesn't."

I think she repeated it under her breath on the way down. I think she'll keep it. Sable keeps things.

* * *

By midnight the *Corvina* was dying on the Outer Shoal in plain view of my window, and it is one thing to know a ship is breaking and another to stand at the glass and watch it happen, wave by wave, plank by plank, the sea taking the vessel apart the way a patient child dismantles a clock—with total attention and no malice whatsoever.

Her mainmast went first, a black diagonal against the white of the surf, and then the stern rose once, absurdly, like a beast rearing at the inevitable, and came down crooked and stayed crooked, and after that it was no longer a ship at all but a shape that used to be one.

The flares had stopped. Either they were out of flares or out of hope, and I didn't want to guess the ratio.

But the light did its work. I'll say that for the machine I serve: with the beam sweeping full over that stretch of broken water, we could see them—dark figures, four, maybe five, moving along the reef ridge where the shoal broke surface at low tide, clinging to rock, crawling whenever a sea came over them. Alive, then. Alive the way a candle is alive, briefly and against advice.

Aldous appeared beside me at the glass, gray-faced, still buttoning his coat wrong. He looked out at the wreck of the man who had hunted his family across two counties, and I watched him look, and I watched what crossed his face, and it was not triumph. It was recognition. He saw himself out there—small, wet, clinging to whatever the current had left him—and for once in his life Aldous Verrin did not look away from it.

"They'll freeze before dawn," he said hoarsely. "Even if the sea doesn't finish it. Water temperature alone—"

"I know the numbers."

"Can the buoy reach that far? The line, the rocket—"

"At low tide, with this wind quartering right, maybe. Maybe not. We'll find out by trying, because the alternative to finding out is watching, and I've watched enough for one lifetime."

He swallowed. "Coral. Those men came to take my daughter's son."

"Yes."

"And you're going out to save them."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was a fair question and deserved better than the answer I gave, which was: "Because the light's burning anyway. Might as well be consistent." But the true answer, which I kept below my breath like a coal carried in a cup, was this: because twenty years ago a woman informed on a smuggling ring to save her brother, and the brother let her carry the blame alone, and the blame grew teeth and chased her family to my door, and if I let the sea eat the consequence while I stood warm behind glass, then I would be Aldous. I would be the man who lets others pay for his comfort. I had been that man for twenty years without knowing it—invisibility, I was learning, is mostly just cowardice wearing a keeper's uniform. I was done with the uniform.

So we worked. Rocket apparatus to the lower gallery, hawser coiled and faked down, the Lyle gun's charge dry under oilcloth. Sable ran the powder and lines with steady hands; her father hauled where she pointed; and Pip—because children hear worse from walls than from mothers, and because Sable had apparently decided truth was the lesser poison—Pip stood at the foot of the stairs holding the spare lantern, solemn as a curate, refusing all orders to bed.

"Somebody has to hold the light for the stairs," he said, "or you'll fall down and then who saves everybody?"

There was no answering that. I let him hold the lantern.

* * *

At half past one, at the bottom of the tide, with the wind backed a point and given us the barest quarter we needed, I fired the first rocket.

I will not pretend to skill I don't have; the shot fell short, and the second went wild, and the third—fired with Sable counting the wind-gusts aloud like a woman timing waves for a jump, because that is exactly what she was doing—the third carried its line out over the reef and dropped across the rocks not thirty feet from the nearest figure, who proved too frozen to notice it.

What happened next happened in pieces, and I record the pieces because the whole is too large for one memory: Aldous wading into the surf to haul the slack shoreward, screaming instructions in a voice I hadn't known he owned; Sable on the winch, taking up strain with the patience of someone who has inventoried every inch of rope on this rock and trusts her arithmetic absolutely; myself at the whip line, hauling the first man off the rocks and through the water and up onto our landing stage, where he arrived less a person than a cargo of cold, coughing, astonishingly heavy meat.

He was young. Twenty, perhaps. One of Hale's crew, by the cut of the coat remnants they still wore. He gripped my sleeve with fingers that couldn't feel they were gripping and said, through chattering teeth, "There's more—there's five of us—Cap'n's still out there—"

Cap'n. Out there.

I fired again. We worked again. The sea permitted it the way a landlord permits tenants—temporarily, at rates designed to ruin you.

By three o'clock we had four of them on the rock, wrapped in every blanket the tower owned, packed into the oil store with the stove going, Pip ferrying cocoa with the gravity of a field medic. Four living men who had sailed to this rock to break a family, warmed by that family's blankets, fed by its stores, saved by its keeper's light.

The fifth was Corven Hale.

I stood at the gallery rail with the rain needling my face and swept the beam across the reef one more time, and there—at the far spine of the shoal, where the rock shoulders above the flood tide—two shapes. One flat and still, which my heart reported with shameful speed as *good news* before my creed corrected it. And one kneeling over the still one, working at it, shaking it, a big man performing rescue on the very man whose death he had perhaps been praying for an hour earlier.

Or perhaps not. The sea flattens all distinctions at this range. I couldn't tell which figure knelt and which lay still. I couldn't even be sure both were ours to claim.

"Coral!" Sable, at my shoulder, soaked through a second time, shouting into the wind. "Tide's turning! In an hour that spine goes under—both of them with it! Can we reach them?"

I measured the distance with a keeper's eye and a keeper's honesty. The rocket line wouldn't carry. The seas between us were running eight feet over the reef. The only boat on this rock worth the name was the little punt, and putting the punt into *that* was a wager even the sea would call reckless.

But the tide was turning, and the light was burning, and somewhere out on that black spine a man I had every reason to hate was either drowning or trying not to, and the difference between those two conditions had somehow become my business.

"Sable," I said, "get me Aldous. And get my oilskins."

She stared at me. "You can't be serious. Not in that sea—not at your age—"

"My age has kept every watch this tower ever asked of it," I said. "It can manage one more. Get Aldous. And Sable—" I caught her arm before she turned. "Keep the lamp at full. Whatever happens out there, however long I'm gone, that light does not falter. Not for me, not for anyone. Promise me."

For a moment she was Maren's face at twenty, furious and frightened and magnificent, and then she was Sable, and she said, "Full burn. I promise. Come back or I'll inventory everything you own and find it wanting."

I was already climbing down toward the water, toward the punt, toward the last and worst argument of the year, thinking: *the sea gives with one hand and takes with the other, and tonight it means to make me bid for everything.*

The first wave hit the landing stage as I untied the painter, and it hit like a verdict.
