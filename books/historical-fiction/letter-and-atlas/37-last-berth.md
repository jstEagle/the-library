## 37. Last Berth

The supply boat came at half past nine in the morning, which was indecent. Boats that carry salvation ought to arrive in dramatic weather, at dusk, with a bell tolling somewhere. This one arrived through clearing swells with gulls screaming over her wake and the sun out like it had nothing to answer for.

I saw her first from the lamp room while I was cleaning the glass for no reason except that my hands wanted work. A smudge on the horizon where no smudge belonged. By the time she rounded the northern point and showed me her funnel smoke, Sable was already on the gallery calling down to Pip, and Pip was already halfway up the stairs, and Aldous was standing in the doorway of the oil store looking like a man who has been told a verdict and is still deciding whether to hear it.

"Is she the one?" Pip shouted at me, as if I were deaf as well as old.

"She's the one."

Three berths. I had known it since Hale's men nailed the message to the door like an invoice, and I had done my arithmetic in the dark hours since, the way you do sums you already know the answer to because you're hoping the candle will show you something different. Four souls. Three berths. The sea had posed its question months ago; I had simply been slow to write the answer.

I went down to make tea, because in my experience every terrible decision goes down easier with tea.

* * *

*Sable*

He was making tea. That was the thing about Coral Amberdale — when the world ended, he put the kettle on. I had watched him do it after the storm took the sloop, after we hauled Corven Hale half-drowned onto his floor and the man vomited seawater onto boards Coral had polished himself. Kettle on. Tea made. The universe restored to order by force of habit.

Now he stood at the little stove with his back to all of us, and I watched his shoulders, because shoulders tell you what faces won't. They were set the way they'd been set the night he doused every light but the lawful beam — the night he chose, deliberately, to be exactly what he appeared to be: a keeper, keeping.

"Pip," he said without turning around. "Get your things together. Only what you can carry. The rest comes in spring."

"In spring *I'll* come back," Pip said. "You said."

"I said what I said."

"You did say." Pip looked at me for confirmation. Children always look for confirmation, and adults are so rarely brave enough to give it.

"He said," I confirmed.

Coral poured four cups of tea, which I noted, and then gave three of them away, which I noted harder. He handed the fourth cup to Aldous instead of drinking it himself, and Aldous took it with both hands like a man accepting communion or a bribe.

"The boat will stand off the landing stage," Coral said. "Swells are still running six feet in the channel, so we go light and quick. Pip first, then you, Sable. Your father last, so he can hand things down."

"And you?" I said.

"I'll be here."

"Coral."

"There's a lamp to light tonight," he said, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather, "and there's weather coming to light it in. Somebody has to stay who knows the clockwork. You've had three weeks of lessons. In another three years you might be trusted with the wicks."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have." He finally turned and looked at me, and I understood that this man had spent twenty years perfecting the art of seeming invisible, and that he had now chosen, with the same care he gave the lens, to be seen. It was the most frightening thing I'd witnessed on that rock, and I had watched my father confess to letting my dead aunt take the blame for treason.

* * *

*Coral*

Packing Pip took eleven minutes, because the boy owned almost nothing and insisted on debating the fate of each item individually.

"The cat stays," I told him.

"The cat could come."

"The cat is employed here. She has mice under contract."

"She'd rather come with me."

"She'd rather you believed that." I folded his spare shirt the way Maren used to fold shirts — sleeves in, roll from the bottom — and then stood holding it a moment too long, because my hands remembered being taught by her and had apparently decided to grieve about it now, mid-morning, with a boat coming.

Pip watched me fold it. He was sharp enough to notice and kind enough not to say anything, which is more than I could claim for any adult in that tower, myself included.

"Mr. Amberdale," he said. "Are you staying because of the lamp?"

"Aye."

"Or because there's only room for three?"

The boy had heard everything for weeks. Of course he had. Children are the best listeners on earth precisely because everyone assumes they aren't listening.

"Both," I said, because I had promised myself I would stop lying badly. "But mostly the lamp. And partly because your Aunt Maren asked me — in a letter I found — to keep you all safe, and I find I'm not finished obeying her. Took me twenty years to start. I don't intend to rush the finish."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then: "She would have liked you to come too."

"She'd have liked me to keep the light. That was the whole trouble between us, more or less." I put the rolled shirt in his satchel and ruffled his hair, which he permitted. "Mind your mother. Mind your grandfather even when he doesn't deserve it — that's the hard arithmetic of family. And mind the sea, always, because she minds nothing."

"Yes sir." Then, at the door, turning back: "Will you write? Sable says you have paper."

"Sable says correctly. Go on now. Boat's in the roadstead."

* * *

*Aldous*

I had prepared a speech. God help me, I had been drafting it since dawn, standing in the oil store among the tins, rehearsing gratitude into something worthy of the man I'd wronged twice — once by living, once by letting my sister die carrying my sins.

The speech died in my throat the moment Coral handed me my coat and said, "Sleeves in, roll from the bottom. Maren showed me how. Apparently it saves space, though I never saw her save a single inch of anything else."

That undid me more thoroughly than any speech of mine could have survived. I stood in the middle of the kitchen of Gannet Rock lighthouse, sixty-one years old, and wept like a bilge pump working backwards, and Coral pretended to study the barometer until I was fit for company.

"Aldous." He spoke quietly. "We're not friends. I want that understood before you go. Twenty years of lies isn't mended by one storm."

"No," I managed.

"But Maren loved you, fool that she must have been about it, and she asked me to protect you, and I am a man who finishes what he starts. So you'll live, and you'll live *better*, and each year I expect a letter telling me how, and each year the letter had better be longer than the last."

"You'll get your letters."

"And Sable reads everything you send, so write as though she's reading it. Because she is."

I laughed — actually laughed, wet and broken — because he was right, she always had been. I put out my hand. He looked at it for a long moment, this man whose hand I had never earned the right to shake, and then he took it, briefly, firmly, and let go, and that brief grip contained more judgment and more mercy than any sermon I'd ever slept through.

"Goodbye, Coral."

"Not goodbye. You'll be back in spring if you're worth the berth." He turned to the window, where the supply boat was warping in toward the stage. "Go on. Your children are waiting, and I find I want the tower quiet, so I can hear myself think."

* * *

*Sable*

The landing stage was slick and the swells made the boat rise and fall against it like a breathing animal. Coral went down first and rigged the lines himself, refusing Aldous's help with a jerk of the chin, and I stood at the top of the iron stair with Pip's satchel and understood that I was watching the last ordinary minutes of something I didn't yet have a name for.

Pip went down the stair clutching Coral's hand, and at the bottom Coral swung him across the gap onto the deck with an ease that startled me — the solitary man, the invisible man, lifting a child over open water like he'd done it a hundred times. Maybe in some life he had. Maybe in the life he'd been denied.

Then my father crossed, gray-faced, gripping the rail, and did not look back at the rock. He never would, I thought. Some men survive by never looking back.

Which left me.

Coral stood at the foot of the stair, holding the line steady, salt wind pulling at his white hair, and waited for me with the patience of a man who had waited twenty years for worse things.

"You could still come," I said. "We could crowd a fourth aboard. People have crowded worse."

"People have drowned worse, too. The master of that boat takes three souls, no more, because that's what the insurance allows, and I have spent my life respecting lawful limits. It's rather the whole of my character." He almost smiled. "Go, Sable. Take your brother ashore. Get him fed. Get yourself somewhere the fog comes in politely, through a window, where you can shut it out."

"And what do I do with what I know?" I said — meaning the trunk, the letters, my father's rot, Hale's bargain, all the machinery of truth we'd assembled on that rock. "What do I do with all of it, on shore, where nobody keeps lamps?"

"You do what keepers do," he said. "You write it down. All of it. Every log requires an honest hand, and yours is the honestest I've met since—" He stopped. Started again. "Since hers."

I came down the stair. At the bottom, instead of taking his hand to swing across, I took his face in both my hands — he stiffened like a man touched by lightning, twenty years unaccustomed — and I kissed his cheek, salt and stubble and all.

"Uncle Coral," I said.

He closed his eyes. For a moment he was utterly silent, and I thought perhaps I'd broken him, and then he said, rough as gravel: "Say it again. Slowly. I want to get the spelling right for the logbook."

"*Uncle Coral.*"

"Aye," he said. "That'll hold."

Then he lifted me across the water like a sack of flour, entirely without ceremony, because he was Coral Amberdale and ceremony was for other men, and I landed on the deck laughing and crying at once, which the sailors pretended not to see, sailors being the only gentlemen left in England.

* * *

*Coral*

They cast off at ten minutes past ten. I stood on the landing stage and watched them go — Aldous at the rail not looking back, Sable at the stern looking back the whole way, and Pip hanging off the bow waving one arm in great sweeping arcs as though the entire coast needed signaling.

I raised my hand. Held it up until the boat was a smudge, until the smudge was a suggestion, until the sea took them the way the sea takes everything, temporarily.

Then I climbed the stair, wound the clockwork, trimmed the wicks, and put the kettle on.

The tower was very quiet. I noticed, with some surprise, that the quiet had changed its character. For twenty years it had been the sound of hiding. Now it was the sound of a house between visits — the silence of a place expecting footsteps. Somewhere below, the cat was complaining about her abandoned mouse contracts. Somewhere in my chest pocket, Maren's letter rode against my heart like a second pulse.

I opened the logbook to the day's page and wrote, in a fair hand:

*"10:14 a.m. Supply boat departed with three passengers: Aldous Verrin, Sable Verrin, Pip Verrin. Weather clearing. Swells moderating. All souls accounted for."*

Then, beneath, after a moment's thought:

*"Keeper remains at station. By choice."*

Outside, far off to the southwest, the horizon had gone the particular bruised color that means the season's last storms are lining up to have their say. I checked the barometer, and it agreed with the sky, and I felt — I confess it — glad. Let it come. I had a light to keep, and for the first time in twenty years, people who knew it was burning, and expected it, and would measure their safety by it across all that dark water.

I was reaching for the oil tin when I heard it: a knock at the tower door. Three knocks, deliberate, unhurried.

No boat stood in the roadstead. No rowboat sat at the stage. And yet someone — or something — had knocked.

I picked up the lantern, and went to see.
