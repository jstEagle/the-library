## 38. Alone with the Light

The supply boat took them out on a Tuesday, which I have always considered an insult. Tuesdays are for errands—for returning borrowed tools and settling accounts at the chandlery—not for carrying away every soul I had learned to love inside of four months. The weather that morning was obscenely kind. Blue sky, gentle swell, the channel open like a door someone had thoughtfully left ajar. If it had been foul, if the sea had raged and refused them passage, I might have been spared the sight of Pip's face pressed to the stern rail, growing smaller by degrees until he was only a coat, then a cap, then nothing at all.

Sable had held my hand on the landing stage. She did not say anything when she did it, which was correct; we were neither of us people who said things when saying them would ruin the thing itself. She squeezed once, hard, the way you'd grip a rope in weather, and then she let go and stepped aboard, and that single squeeze has been doing the work of a hundred sentences ever since. I keep it in the same pocket where I kept Maren's ring for twenty years. A man learns to carry his valuables close and his grief closer.

Aldous shook my hand like a man shaking hands with a judge who has just shown him unexpected mercy. He said, "Coral," which was more than he'd managed in most of our acquaintance, and then he said, "I'll write," which we both knew might be a lie but had the decency to sound like a hope.

And then they were gone, around the shoulder of the rock, into the channel, out of my life and—this was the new part—out of my danger too.

I climbed back up the hundred and eight steps to the lamp room and lit the lamp, because it was Tuesday, and Tuesdays do not care what else they carry.

* * *

The storm season's tail is an honest thing, unlike most tails. The great gales of January had come early and spent themselves extravagantly, like guests who eat everything in the pantry and break a chair on their way out. What remained through February and into March was smaller weather—squalls, fog banks, the long grey swells that come from no particular storm and seem to be the sea simply clearing its throat. Dangerous enough. A keeper who relaxes in the tail of the season is a keeper the Board reads about afterward in the casualty reports.

I kept the watch as I had always kept it, but I will confess—and here, in this logbook that only the gulls and possibly God will read—that I kept it differently too.

For twenty years I had kept this light the way a man tends a grave: faithfully, punctually, with love, but with the understanding that the tending was between him and the dead. The lamp did not know me. The rock did not know me. I had made a philosophy of it. *History happens elsewhere,* I used to tell myself, winding the clockwork at dawn. *To other men. In other places.* It was a comfortable creed. It fit me the way an old coat fits—loose everywhere except at the shoulders, where a man carries what he actually believes.

But the Verrins had lived under my roof for a winter, and now the tower remembered them. Every room had been rearranged by their passing. The kitchen still smelled faintly of Sable's terrible coffee—she boiled it the way her father argued, long past the point of sense. There was a notch cut in the third stair from the top where Pip had tested his knife, badly, and been caught, and taught properly, and had cut a much better notch higher up to prove it. The oil store door stuck in its frame because Aldous had leaned against it during the confrontation, leaning the way guilty men lean, as though gravity itself might absolve him.

I could have sanded the stair smooth. I could have planed the door. I left them. A house that remembers is not a haunted house. I had finally learned the difference, and it had cost me a wife, a war, a smuggler, and one very nearly drowned man to learn it.

Corven Hale had sailed east two days after the bargain, his sloop patched and limping, his pride presumably lashed to the mast alongside everything else he'd lost to my shoals. He had left with the ledger's silence and my word, and I had left with his—which of us got the better trade I leave to the accountants. He'd shaken my hand at the waterline, actually shaken it, a man whose hands had lately been around my throat's ambitions, and said, "You're a strange one, Keeper," and I said, "Aye," and that was the whole of the treaty. Some documents don't need ink. They need only two men standing in cold water, both of them surprised to be alive.

* * *

The letter I read every night.

Maren's last letter—the unmailed one Pip's cat had led us all to, curled in its cavity beneath the foundation stone with the ledger that had nearly gotten us all killed—I read it the way she had written it: slowly, and knowing more each time than the time before.

*I never lied to you about anything that mattered,* she had written, and I had wanted to throw the page across the lamp room when I first read that line, because surely the twenty years of silence mattered, surely the name she hadn't given me, the brother she hadn't mentioned, the money moving through orphanages like blood through a hidden vein—all of it mattered. But I had read the letter forty times now, and I had come to understand what she meant, or what I believed she meant, which in a marriage is often the same thing. She had lied to me about Aldous, about Hale, about the ledger. She had never lied about herself. She had simply shown me the self she thought I could bear, and loved me within those borders, and mourned the rest alone. That is not innocence. But it is not betrayal either. It is something sadder and more common, something most marriages are built on without either party admitting it: two people showing each other their best rooms and keeping the cellar locked.

She had asked me to protect them. *The family you didn't know you had.* And I had protected them, after a fashion, and they had gone off across the channel in a supply boat, and now the tower was mine again, and the strange discovery of this tail-end winter was this:

I was not lonely.

This required some adjustment. For twenty years loneliness had been my constant companion, the way a limp is a man's companion—we don't call it company, but we arrange our lives around it, we make our peace, we stop noticing until someone asks why we walk that way. The Verrins had arrived and I had noticed the limp. And now they had gone and the limp was gone too, and I stood in my own house feeling strangely balanced, and did not know what to do with the balance.

Here is what I found instead, in the long quiet weeks of February: solitude. Which sounds like the same animal wearing a different coat, but is in fact a different animal entirely. Loneliness is a room you're locked in. Solitude is a room you've chosen, with the key in your pocket, and the difference between them is the difference between drowning and swimming, though both involve being covered in water.

I chose the light. That was the revelation of that winter's end. I had spent twenty years telling myself the light had chosen me—cast ashore by circumstance, stranded by grief, keeper by accident. Nonsense. I had wound that clockwork every morning of my own free will. I had trimmed every wick, polished every pane of the great lens, climbed every one of those hundred and eight steps because somewhere in the wreckage of my marriage I had decided that this—this exact work, on this exact rock—was how I would stand against the dark. I had called it hiding. It had looked like hiding. But a man can hide in a cathedral too, and the cathedral is no less holy for it.

* * *

The nights in the tail of the season had their own rhythm, and I fell into it gladly.

At dusk I climbed and lit the lamp. At midnight I made the first inspection round—oil level, wick trim, the rotation of the clockwork that turned the lens, gathering up its thousand prisms and letting them go, gathering and letting go, the most patient machine ever built by impatient men. At three I made the second round, and at dawn I put the lamp to bed and stood a while at the gallery rail watching whatever the night had left behind: wrack on the rocks, sometimes; a dead gull, sometimes; once, memorably, half a rowboat with the name *Dulcibella* painted on it in cheerful yellow, owner unknown, fate unrecorded.

And then, before sleep, the letter.

I had taken to reading it in the lamp room rather than below. There was something right about reading it there, in the place where she had never stood, telling me things she had never said, while the lens turned above my head scattering her words in every direction at once. North, south, east, west. *Protect them,* she wrote. *Forgive him—he was never strong, but he was never wicked, and the difference matters even when it hurts.* West, north, east. *You were the truest thing I ever did, Coral Amberdale, and I hid you the way I hid everything, because I learned young that what is loved gets taken.*

That last line undid me for a week the first time I read it. I had to set the letter down and stand at the rail and look at the black water and breathe like a man remembering how. Because I knew where she'd learned it. I knew about the childhood she'd skimmed over in our courting years—the father lost to the ring's violence, the family scattered, the girl who watched love become a target and drew the obvious lesson. She had hidden me from Hale and hidden Hale from me and hidden her own heart from us both, and underneath all that hiding, all those years, she had loved me with the whole of what remained. Which was, as it turns out, considerable.

I am not a man who cries easily. I report it as a meteorological fact only: there was weather in the lamp room that night, and for several nights after.

* * *

February gave way to March the way it does—reluctantly, with false starts. The barometer climbed and fell and climbed again. I logged the weather twice daily in the official log, in the small neat hand the Board requires, and then, in a separate book—a plain one I had bought from the last crate of supplies, with no crest and no purpose printed on its cover—I began to log other things.

I wrote down the notch in the stair and where Pip had cut it. I wrote down Sable's coffee and the exact shade of disapproval she brought to my cooking. I wrote down the squeeze of her hand on the landing stage, so I would not lose the pressure of it to memory's erosion. I wrote down Aldous at the waterline saying "Coral" like a man testing whether the word would hold his weight. I wrote down the fisherman's tale, and Hale's civility, and the arithmetic of berths, and the night all four of us had worked the great storm together, passing oil and shouting over wind, a crew that had assembled itself out of wreckage and grievance and somehow, in the working, become a crew.

It occurred to me, somewhere in the middle weeks, that I was writing a history. Not the Board's history of weather and oil consumption, but a real one—the kind I had spent twenty years claiming happened elsewhere. It had happened here. To me. On this rock, in this tower, among these people. The scandals and storms I'd imagined passed me by had come ashore wrapped in oilskin, addressed to *the keeper*, and I had opened them, and they had remade me.

Maren would have laughed at the lateness of my education. Twenty years to learn what she'd known at fifteen: that invisibility is a choice, and choosing to be seen costs exactly as much as it saves, and is worth it anyway.

* * *

In late March the first seabirds came back to the rock, and I greeted them like old acquaintances, which after twenty years they were. The gulls resumed their parliament on the north ledge. A pair of gannets—my namesakes, or I theirs, we'd never settled the question—took up residence on the old crane platform and began the loud domestic business of another season.

I talked to them. I had always talked to them; a man alone develops the habit, and I had stopped apologizing for it sometime in my forties. But the conversations had changed. Where once I'd told them nothing—where once my speech to the gulls had been a series of remarks about weather, designed to say nothing to anyone including myself—now I told them things. I told them about Maren's letter. I told them about the boy who'd cut notches in my stairs and the woman who'd picked a lock I'd pretended not to hear being picked. I told them I intended to write to the orphanage in the spring, openly this time, under my own name, and see what Maren's charity had grown into, and whether it wanted a patron or merely a friend.

The gulls were unmoved. Gulls hold their opinions close. But I felt better, and that is the entire function of conversation, as far as I can determine, whether the listener has wings or not.

* * *

On the last day of March, the ice went out of the channel for good, and the horizon filled with sails—the fishing fleet coming back to its grounds, the coast waking up after its long sleep. From the gallery rail I counted eleven sails between the rock and the mainland, and every one of them passing safely wide of my shoals, and I stood there in the wind with my hands on the rail like a landlord surveying his estate, except that what I owned was nothing and what I kept was everyone.

That evening, making the dusk climb, I paused at the third stair—the notched one—and put my thumb in the groove Pip's knife had left. Then I climbed the remaining hundred and five steps, lit the lamp, and sat down with Maren's letter and my plain notebook, and began a fresh page.

I had decided, you see, on a project. All winter the idea had been assembling itself the way weather assembles, quietly, off past the horizon, until one day it fills the sky. Sable had once told me—in the oil store, in the worst of it, when we were circling each other with our secrets—"You're not what they say you are." And I had asked who *they* were, and she'd said, "Everyone who isn't here," and I had understood that I had a reputation, out there in the world, among people I'd never met. *The coward. The hermit. The man who buried himself alive.*

Very well. If I had a reputation, I could have a correspondence. If the world had stories about me, I could send the world some corrections. In the spring, boats came to the rock—supply boats, fishing boats, the occasional revenue cutter—and every boat carried letters. I would write to Sable. I would write to Pip, who was learning his letters and would need the practice deciphering mine. I would write to the orphanage. I might even, God forgive me, write to Aldous first, since he'd promised to write and would probably need the example.

But before any of that, I would finish the page I'd started, which was a list. A list of everything in this tower that the Verrins had touched, so that when they came—if they came, when they came, and I had begun to suspect that *when* was the honest word—I could show them what they'd left behind. Not the furniture. The marks. The notch, the sticking door, the smell in the kitchen. The evidence that a house remembers its people, and that a man who keeps a house like that is not keeping a lighthouse at all.

He is keeping a home, and waiting for it to fill.

The lamp turned overhead, gathering and letting go. Out past the shoals, far off in the blue dusk, a sail appeared—small, single-masted, standing in toward the channel with the tide. Too early for the supply boat. Wrong rig for the fishermen.

I put down my pen and picked up the glass.
