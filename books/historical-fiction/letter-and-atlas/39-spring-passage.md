## 39. Spring Passage

The ice went out of the channel in the third week of March, and I will confess to you — since you are only this logbook, and logbooks keep secrets better than gulls do — that I stood at the gallery rail every morning from the first of February onward, watching for it. Not watching for spring itself. Spring is a general thing, like weather reports, and means nothing to a man on a rock. I was watching for that particular brown ribbon of open water that runs down the middle of the strait when the current wins its annual argument with winter.

When it came, I did not shout about it. I made tea instead, and drank it too fast, and burned my tongue, which is the traditional Gannet Rock celebration.

The truth is I had been counting days in a way I hadn't permitted myself since Maren died. There is a kind of hope that is embarrassing to carry openly, so I carried it the way you carry anything fragile aboard a boat — low, close to the hull, where the motion is smallest. Every morning I told myself: *they may come, or they may not, and either way there is wick-trimming to do.* Every morning I trimmed the wicks with slightly more attention than they required.

Sable had written twice over the winter. The letters came by the supply boat, when the supply boat could come, which in January meant once and in February meant never. Her handwriting had changed between the first letter and the second. In December she wrote like a woman drafting a legal document — careful, upright, every sentence a signed confession of her own uncertainty. By February she wrote like herself, whatever that was: slanted, quick, impatient with margins. *Pip asks daily whether the cat remembers him,* she wrote. *I have told him cats remember no one, but I suspect I am wrong about this, as I have been wrong about most things regarding you.*

I read that line four times and then put the letter in the drawer with Maren's last one, which is becoming a crowded drawer.

* * *

They came on a Thursday. I know because Thursdays were Maren's baking days, and I had baked bread that morning out of habit, out of some superstition I won't examine too closely, and the loaf was still warm when Pip's voice came across the water ahead of the boat itself — the way his voice always did, carrying like a fog signal with opinions.

"UNCLE CORAL!"

He was standing in the bow before any sensible person would stand in the bow of a small craft in a spring chop, one hand on the painter, the other waving with the whole arm, shoulder and all, as though he might flag me down from two hundred yards. Sable sat at the tiller wearing oilskins two sizes too large — mine, I realized; I'd sent them out with the boat in autumn — and she wore them the way she wore everything, as if she'd chosen them deliberately and would thank no one for noticing.

And beside her, wrapped in canvas against the spray, sat a crate that mewed.

"You brought the cat," I said, when they'd bumped alongside the landing stage and Pip had leapt ashore before the boat finished arriving, which is the Pip way.

"He refused to be left," Sable said. "We tried. He filed a formal complaint with the shipping company."

The cat in question emerged from his crate looking affronted and victorious in equal measure, took one look at the rock he had apparently claimed as territory, and began washing himself as though the crossing had been nothing, as though he owned the sea as well. Gannet Rock's previous cat had been dead eleven years. I had not thought of him in ten of those. Animals have a way of reoccupying a house like heirs.

"Where's your father?" Pip asked, and then caught something in my face or Sable's, and amended: "Is he coming later?"

"In summer, maybe," Sable said. "He's working the herring boats out of Penlow. Saving." She said it evenly, but I heard what was underneath it, the same way you hear a rock under water — not visible, but the swell breaks differently above it. Aldous was earning his amends slowly, the way he did everything: late, reluctantly, and with an eye always cocked toward the easier exit. But he was earning them. His letter, which arrived separately a week after they did, said as much in a roundabout Aldous fashion:

*Dear Coral,* it began, which was more direct than he had managed in thirty years. *I am told by my daughter that gratitude unspoken is worthless, and she is generally right about things now, which I attribute entirely to her mother's side. So: thank you. For the light. For all of it. I know what I let Maren carry and I know what you carried without knowing you carried it, and I intend to spend the rest of my working life making the arithmetic less shameful. Enclosed is twelve pounds. It is not enough. It is a beginning.*

Twelve pounds. It went into the lamp fund, because that seemed the only honest place for it. Amends should buy light, I thought. Light is the one commodity that can't be hoarded.

* * *

The tower filled with footsteps that same afternoon, and I learned within hours how much silence I had actually been living in.

You don't hear your own silence, you understand. Silence is like the smell of your own house — invisible until someone else's life moves through it. By evening the place sounded like a harbor town. Pip's boots on the iron stairs, three at a time going up, one at a time coming down because he'd been warned exactly once. Sable's voice asking questions — she asked questions the way other people breathed, continuously and without apparent effort. Where the coal store was. How the clockwork escapement worked. Why the fog bell rang seven times and not six. Whether I'd ever been married. That last one she asked lightly, drying dishes, not looking at me, and I answered lightly too, and we both knew what we were doing, and neither of us minded. We had become, somewhere across the winter letters, two people who could hold a hard thing between us without dropping it.

"The lamp room," Pip announced at dinner, "is the best room in any building I have ever been in, including the cathedral at Torquay, which Papa says has the best room in England."

"The cathedral doesn't float its room forty feet above the sea," I said.

"No," Pip agreed gravely. "That's its weakness."

After dinner he fell asleep at the table, mid-sentence, his cheek on his forearm, and Sable looked at him and then at me with an expression I couldn't parse and didn't try to. She carried him up the stairs — he was nine and mostly legs, but she was stronger than she looked, having spent a winter hauling nets, she said — and when she came back down she stood in the kitchen doorway a moment, just stood there, taking inventory of the room as though seeing it for the first time.

"It's strange," she said. "I've read about this kitchen in your letters all winter. It's odd how a place can be familiar before you've stood in it."

"That's what letters are for."

"Yes." She hung her oilskins on the peg by the door — my pegs, now half hers — and added, quietly, not quite to me: "It's good to be here, Uncle Coral."

She had called me that once before, at the rail, in the storm, in the worst hour of all our lives. Hearing it again in peacetime was a different thing entirely. A name given freely is worth a hundred given under fire.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about solitude ending: it doesn't end all at once. It ends in stages, the way ice goes out of a channel, and some mornings the old floes still come drifting back through.

For eleven years I had spoken aloud perhaps twenty words a day, and most of those to the clock. Now there were voices in every room, arguments about rationing (Sable), debates about lens-polishing technique (Pip, who had appointed himself assistant keeper within the first week and taken the title seriously), and long stretches where both of them were quiet at once and I found the quiet had changed character entirely — it was no longer an absence of voices but a presence of two people choosing not to speak, which is a warmer animal altogether.

I also learned that I had habits I hadn't known were habits. Sable noticed them faster than I did.

"You set two places at breakfast," she observed the second morning, watching me correct the table. "Every morning since we arrived. You set two, and then add a third."

"I lived alone a long time. Two plates is what the shelf holds naturally."

"Mm," she said, in the tone that meant she had filed the information away and would be returning to it later, probably at an inconvenient moment. She was her aunt Maren's niece in ways that grew more obvious weekly. The tilt of the head. The refusal to let a loose thread lie. I had loved Maren for that quality and been destroyed by it in equal measure, and here it was again, younger, sharper, aimed at me with better intentions.

On the third week, she returned to it. We were re-tarring the gallery rail, Pip below us painting the lower balustrade in strokes of increasing chaos, and Sable said, without preamble:

"When you sent the money to the orphanage. All those years. Was that for her, or was that for you?"

I considered lying, mostly out of professional habit. "Both, I think. At the start, for her. She used to say money was just love with the poetry boiled out of it. After she died—" I ran the brush along a seam. "After, I suppose I kept paying because stopping would have meant admitting the marriage was over, and I wasn't done with it yet."

"And now?"

"Now I'm done being done with it." I looked out at the water, brown-ribbed and running free. "Your aunt was a complicated ledger, Sable. I've spent a year reading her. I expect I'll be reading her the rest of my life. But the entries balance better than I thought they did."

She nodded slowly, and painted, and after a while she said, "Papa's letter came. I read it."

"I know. You cried in the coal store."

"How—"

"You came out with coal dust on your sleeve and red eyes and blamed the anthracite. Anthracite doesn't do that to people. I checked."

She laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her — and for a while we tarred rail in peace, three figures on an iron gallery above a freed sea, and I thought: *this is what the tower was for.* All those years I'd assumed it was for ships. Perhaps it had been waiting for a family the whole time, the way an empty coat waits, holding its shape, for shoulders.

* * *

In April the first proper spring gale came through, and I'll admit I watched the barometer with something other than dread. A gale in April is a test. A gale in April tells you whether the winter taught anybody anything.

It did. When the glass began its dive, Sable appeared at the watch room door with the storm checklist already in hand — she had copied it out in her slanted, margin-impatient hand and pinned it inside the cupboard door — and Pip was already on the stairs with the brass polishing cloths, because the boy understood, with the instinct children have for what matters, that the lens comes first, always first, everything else is furniture.

We ran the light through that blow together, the three of us, and it was the finest watch I ever kept. Not because the sea was worst — it wasn't, not by a tenth of what November gave us — but because when I climbed the stairs at midnight to relieve the middle watch, I found Sable in the lamp room with Pip asleep against her shoulder on the watch bench, and she had the logbook open, writing the entry in her own hand beneath my instructions, and she had recorded the weather, the wind, the state of the glass, all correct, all precise —

—and then, in the remarks column, in small letters: *All well. All three well.*

I stood at the rail and let her think I was checking the horizon. The horizon needed no checking. The beam swung its slow white arm out over the black water, around and around, patient as forgiveness, and below me slept a boy who polished lenses, and beside me kept watch a woman who had come back across a cold sea to a rock she owed nothing to, and somewhere south a man was hauling herring nets and mailing twelve pounds at a time toward his own redemption, slow as spring ice going out.

Maren, I thought — and I say it to you now, logbook, as I said it to the dark then — Maren, you hid things from me in cupboards and cavities and twenty years of silence, but you also hid a family in my future, and I have decided to forgive the first on account of the second.

The glass bottomed out at dawn and began its climb. The sea lay down by noon.

* * *

It was Pip who found the envelope, because it was Pip's week to fetch the mail crate from the landing stage, and because Pip opened everything addressed to anyone, on the theory — argued earnestly and at length — that important news shouldn't have to wait for grown-ups' schedules.

"This one's for you, Uncle Coral," he said, appearing at my elbow in the lamp room while I trimmed wicks. "But it's funny. Look."

The envelope was heavy paper, good paper, the kind that costs more than it looks like it should. My name was on it in a fine, controlled hand I did not recognize. No return address. Postmarked Penlow — Aldous's port, I noted, though the hand was nothing like Aldous's sprawling scrawl.

"Funny how?" I said.

"There's no stamp," Pip said. "Postman wouldn't take it without a stamp. Da told me that when I tried to mail a crab shell to Sable once." He handed it over. "So somebody must have paid the postman private, to bring it anyway. Or put it on a boat themselves."

I turned the envelope over. Sealed with wax — actual wax, dark green, pressed with no crest at all, just a plain blank seal, deliberate as a closed door.

Some men would have torn it open right there. I had spent a lifetime learning patience from clockwork, and one terrible season learning that sealed things arrive at Gannet Rock for reasons. I put it in my pocket, and I felt its weight there through the rest of the wick-trimming, through supper, through the lighting of the lamp, a small rectangular gravity pulling at the hem of the ordinary evening.

Sable saw it in my pocket at supper. She saw everything. She raised one eyebrow, a perfect Maren eyebrow, and asked no questions, and I loved her for that too.

Later, alone in the watch room, with the light turning above me and the footsteps of two sleeping people still somehow audible in the bones of the tower, I broke the green wax.

Inside was a single sheet, that same fine hand, and it said only this:

*Mr. Amberdale — I believe you have something that belongs to me, and I believe you know by now what it cost to keep it hidden. I make no threats. Threats are for men who haven't read ledgers. I will call at Gannet Rock when the herring fleet passes north, and I will come alone, and I will come as a friend, if friendship can be arranged. Ask your brother-in-law about the winter of '71. He knows my handwriting. — C.H.*

Corven Hale. Alive, out of the sea that should have kept him, writing fine letters on expensive paper, calling himself a friend the way a fog calls itself weather.

I sat with it a long time. Below me, the tower held its family. Above me, the lamp turned, and turned, and turned.

Then I went and woke Sable, because some letters are not for reading alone — and because the herring fleet, I happened to know, passed north in nine days.
