## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The coach let me off at the foot of Aldenmoor's hill with my tool case and one small trunk, and the driver asked me, as drivers always do, whether I had family waiting at the top.

"No one," I said, which was true, and "I'm expected," which was also true, and between those two truths a person can build an entire life.

He nodded and didn't look at me again. That is the arrangement I have made with the world for twenty years now: I answer exactly as much as is asked, I give away nothing that wasn't purchased, and in return people's eyes slide off me like rain off oiled brass. A woman alone who travels for trade invites questions. An apprentice clockmaker — apprentice being a generous word for what I am, journeyman being a word no one will say aloud about a woman with ink-stained fingers and no master's name to drop — invites more. So I have learned to be dull. Dullness is armor. I recommend it.

The hill road up to the observatory was cobbled and slick with October drizzle, and the town spread out below me in the gray light: slate roofs, brick fronts, the river running high and brown past the warehouse district where men were already stacking sandbags with the brisk urgency of people who had done this every autumn of their lives. Bunting was going up along the main street, red and cream, in preparation for some anniversary or another. I would learn later it was the Founders' Jubilee — sixty years since the letter, though I did not yet know there was a letter, only that this town loved its own history with the loud, anxious devotion of a man repeating a story he has told so often he can no longer hear himself.

The observatory sat at the top of the hill like a stone chess piece, white dome catching what little light the sky offered. Below it, attached by a covered walkway, squatted the clock tower itself — square, sensible, crowned with a face visible from every street in town. The master clock lived inside. It governed the church bells, the mill shifts, the ferry schedules, the whole agreed-upon fiction of shared time that lets ten thousand people believe they are living the same day.

I knocked. No one came. I knocked harder, and eventually the door opened on a man leaning heavily against its frame as though the frame were doing most of the work of keeping him upright.

"You're late," said Elias Thorn.

"I'm not," I said. "Your letter said Tuesday."

"It's Tuesday." He considered me with pale, hooded eyes that had the disconcerting quality of eyes belonging to someone who reads fine print for pleasure. "You're Vane?"

"Gideon Vane."

"Odd name."

"It's the one I have." This was a lie delivered with such fluency that even I half believed it, and I noted, with the mild self-disgust of long practice, how easily the lie sat in my mouth. Vane was a weathervane name, a made-up name, a name chosen from a catalog of plausible surnames twenty years ago by a frightened girl who understood that the safest person in any room is the one nobody bothers to remember.

"Come in, then," Thorn said. "Mind the third stair. It complains."

* * *

The interior smelled of machine oil and lamp black and, beneath both, something faintly sweetish and wrong that I recognized before I wanted to: the smell of a body quietly failing. I had repaired clocks in the houses of dying men before. The clocks always seemed to know.

"The strike train's been losing time," Thorn said, leading me down a corridor lined with star charts yellowed to the color of old teeth. "Three minutes a week, then five, then nine. Last Sunday it struck eleven at noon and the whole town heard it."

"And no one thought to send for someone sooner?"

"They sent for me sooner. I've been keeping it true by hand." He stopped at a door, hand on the iron ring, and looked back at me with that unsettling attention again — not the way clients look at tradespeople, checking for competence, but the way a man looks at a lock he suspects someone else once picked. "You come recommended by Halloran, in Corvistone."

"Halloran is kind."

"He said you were the best pair of hands he'd trained in thirty years and that you'd deny it if quoted."

"I deny it," I said.

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. More like the acknowledgment of a move in a game neither of us had named yet. "Third stair," he reminded me, and pushed open the door.

The master clock filled the room the way weather fills a valley. It stood two stories tall in its own timber skeleton, weights hanging like slow bronze thoughts, the great pendulum breathing its patient breath — swing, tick, swing — and above it all the escapement ticking out the seconds that the entire town below borrowed without ever wondering where they came from. I have loved clocks since I was small enough to hide inside the cases, and I will tell you plainly: standing in that room, I felt the closest thing to home I have felt in twenty years, which is a sad sentence, but an honest one.

I set down my case and opened it. "Who services it normally?"

"I do."

"With your hands?" I glanced at them where they rested on his cane — swollen knuckles, a tremor he was trying to still through sheer will, which works about as well as arguing with a pendulum. "No offense meant, Mr. Thorn."

"None taken. Offense requires believing you're wrong." He lowered himself into a chair near the case, positioned precisely where he could watch me work, and did not pretend otherwise. "Go on. Don't mind me."

I minded him. I minded him considerably. Clients who watch are common; clients who watch like scholars reading a difficult text are rare, and rarer still is the client whose watching makes the hair rise on your arms. But the clock was sick and I was hired, and there is a mercy in work — it gives you somewhere to put yourself when you'd rather be nowhere.

I opened the case panels and began my examination. The strike train was fouled; a pivot in the fourth wheel had worn an egg-shaped hole in its bearing, letting the wheel wander just enough to lose lock with each cycle. Someone had been shimming it with folded paper — good paper, good folding, desperate measure — and the paper was damp and compressing, which explained the accelerating drift.

"This shim isn't yours," I said. "Different hand."

"My predecessor's. Or mine. Depends which month." His voice had gone strange. "You read a mechanism the way some men read faces, don't you, Miss Vane. You look at wear and see the years of it. See who touched it."

"I see what's there."

"Do you." Not a question. He watched me fit my smallest screwdriver to the retaining screws, watched me ease the fourth wheel free, and after a long while he said, conversationally, as though remarking on the weather: "You have calluses in the wrong places for someone who's only been at the bench since Halloran took you up. Bench calluses take ten years. You have fifteen. And you flinched — barely, a hair — when I said your name this morning. Gideon Vane. As if it were a coat that didn't quite fit."

My hands did not stop moving. That is the first discipline of my life and I owe it everything: keep the hands moving, keep the voice level, and the silence will hold. "I'm careful with tools," I said. "It makes me attentive."

"Mm," said Elias Thorn, and watched me for another hour, saying nothing further, which was worse.

* * *

By lamplight that evening I had the fourth wheel trued and re-seated with a proper bronze bushing, and the strike train was falling back into rhythm, and Thorn brought tea he did not drink and sat in the shadow of the great case like a crow on a fence rail.

"The town will want to meet you," he said. "Jubilee's in three weeks. Sixty years since the founding charter. There'll be speeches."

"I repair clocks, Mr. Thorn. Speeches aren't in my rate."

"Smart." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Keep it that way. Keep your head down, sign nothing you don't have to, and when the historians come asking questions — and they will come, they always come — be furniture. Furniture survives."

There was a weight under the words I couldn't yet name, a gravity like water finding a crack. "That's unusual advice from a client."

"That's advice from a keeper." He looked up at me then, and in the lamplight his hooded eyes were very clear and very tired, and what I saw in them frightened me more than any scrutiny had all day, because it wasn't suspicion. It was recognition. It was the look of a man seeing his own armor on someone else and knowing exactly where all the straps buckle.

"Miss Vane," he said quietly, as the pendulum swung its patient count behind us, "how long has it been since anyone called you by your real name?"

The tick of the escapement filled the room, one second, another second, the whole honest machinery of time going on regardless. My hands, wrapped around cooling tea, had gone perfectly still — and he saw that too, saw it and nodded slowly, like a man confirming a measurement he had already taken.

"I thought so," said Elias Thorn. "When you're finished with the clock, there's a drawer upstairs I need to show you. Bring your steady hands."
