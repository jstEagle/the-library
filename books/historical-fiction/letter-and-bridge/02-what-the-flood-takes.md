## 2. What the Flood Takes

The river took the bridge on a Tuesday.

I learned this the way I learned most things in Aldenmoor — secondhand, through a boy who arrived at the observatory door soaked to the ribs and breathless with news that was not really news. The water had been climbing for three days. It had swallowed the ferry landing on Monday, the low pasture by Monday night, and by Tuesday morning it had put its whole grey shoulder against the bridge pilings until the town council, in its wisdom, ordered the span closed "for the season."

For the season. As though the flood were a houseguest one merely declined to feed.

"The last cart goes across at noon," the boy said, wringing his cap into a puddle on Elias Thorn's clean floor. "After that, nothing crosses till the water drops. You'd want to be on it, miss, if you're going."

I looked at the master clock behind me, its movement spread across three cloths like a patient opened on a table, half its escapement in my hand.

"I'm not finished," I said.

The boy looked at me as if I'd volunteered to drown. Perhaps he thought I simply hadn't understood. People often assume a woman alone is operating from ignorance rather than choice. I let him think it. It was easier than explaining that I had spent twenty years being exactly where I chose to be, and that the choosing rarely looked like freedom from the outside.

He left at half past eleven. I heard the cart rattle down the hill road, and then I heard the rain come back, and then I heard nothing but the river, which had stopped sounding like a river and started sounding like something with intentions.

Elias Thorn watched me from his chair by the window, the way he had watched me for six days now — with an attention that sat somewhere between admiration and appetite. He was a thin man gone thinner, the kind of ill that hollows a face from inside out, and he held his blanket around his shoulders like a scholar's robe.

"You could have gone," he said.

"I don't leave work unfinished."

"No." He almost smiled. "You don't. I watched you re-cut that third wheel twice because the tooth spacing displeased you. Nobody asked you to. Nobody would ever know."

"Everyone would know. A clock that runs rough tells everyone."

"Mm." He turned back to the window, where the rain was stitching itself into the river. "You keep accounts of yourself very carefully, Miss Vane. Even your pride is filed under necessity."

I went back to the movement because my hands wanted employment and my face wanted somewhere to hide. There is a particular discomfort in being seen by a dying man. They have nothing left to gain from flattery and no time to waste on the polite fictions the rest of us trade in, so what they say tends to land like a dropped wrench — precisely, and on the foot.

* * *

That night the storm arrived properly, as storms do when they have been rehearsing for days. Wind found every gap in the observatory's old stone and played them like a bad organist. The tower above us groaned. Somewhere below, the river made a sound I had only read about in engineers' reports — a deep, continuous tearing, like cloth giving way.

I climbed to the clock room at midnight to wind the strike train and found Elias already there, standing before the great dial with one hand flat against the case as if taking its pulse. By lamplight his face was the color of the paper on his desk.

"It shouldn't be up here," I said. "In this weather. In your condition."

"My condition," he said mildly, "is the one thing about me that has finally become honest."

"Sir—"

"Gideon." He had never used my name before. It went through me like cold water through a seam. "Come downstairs. Bring the lamp. There is something I should have shown you on your first day, and I kept telling myself there would be a better time, and I find at my age that 'better' is a currency I've stopped carrying."

The drawer was in his study, beneath the writing desk — not the desk drawers themselves, which held ink and blotting sand and forty years of correspondence, but a shallow compartment built into the frame underneath, locked with a key he wore on a cord around his neck. He knelt to open it, and I knelt too, unasked, to steady him, and felt how little of him remained. He weighed no more than a clock case.

Inside lay papers. And on top of the papers, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, lay a letter.

It was not remarkable to look at. That is the first lie such documents tell. It was a single folded sheet, browned at the edges, written in a copperplate hand that any archivist would call period-correct. The date at its head was 1741. The signature at its foot belonged to Josiah Alden himself — the founder, the name on the bridge, the statue in the square, the reason two hundred people would gather in eleven days to celebrate their town's founding with speeches and bunting and a bell rung from this very tower.

"This is—" I began.

"Yes," Elias said. "What they say it is. That's rather the point of it."

He unwrapped it fully and set it in my hands, and I did what twenty years of apprenticeship had trained me to do: I looked at it as material. Not history. Material.

The paper was right. The fold lines were right, softened by handling, or by someone's careful simulation of handling. But the ink —

I tilted it toward the lamp. Iron-gall ink browns with age; it sinks into the fiber and blooms at the edges of each stroke, halos of decay around every letter. This ink had browned. It had browned beautifully, evenly, everywhere — and nowhere had it sunk. It sat upon the paper like paint upon glass. Whatever had aged this letter had aged it from outside, all at once, the way a coat weathers rather than the way a wound heals.

"When was it written?" I asked. My voice came out level. I was proud of that, later.

"Spring of 1849." He lowered himself into his chair, breathing hard from the kneeling. "Forty-four years ago. I was nineteen, and I could draw a fair copperplate, and I needed nine pounds, and Mr. Harrowgate needed a founding document."

"Harrowgate." The name was carved over the bank. And the school. And, I suspected, if one checked, several other things.

"And Judge Merrick. And Deacon Pell. All dead now, all honored now, all named in the anniversary program now." He said it without bitterness, which frightened me more than rage would have. Rage burns out. This had settled into him like sediment. "The original letter burned in the Pell warehouse fire — that part is true, that much really did happen. What survived the fire was a gap. And gaps, Miss Vane, are the most profitable material a town can own. Fill one correctly and you may build statues on it forever."

I set the letter down. My hands were steady. I noted that too, the way one notes a tremor in a movement — clinically, and without comfort.

"Why show me this?"

"Because the historians arrive in ten days. Two men from the state society, coming to collect Aldenmoor's papers for the anniversary archive. Officially sanctioned. Everything in this house that touches the founding will go into a sealed collection, catalogued, authenticated, celebrated." He looked at the letter the way a clockmaker looks at a beautiful, wrong mechanism. "And this will be its crown jewel."

"So burn it."

"Do you imagine I haven't considered it? I've had years to consider it. Fire is what started the lie; it seems fitting it might end it. But burning it proves nothing. It becomes a rumor. Rumors attach to the teller, not the truth." His eyes came up to mine, and they were terribly clear. "No. A forgery dies of evidence, not flame. Someone must write down what happened, when, by whose hand, for whose purse. In full. On paper that will last. And it must be somewhere they will look — truly look — after they've taken everything else."

"Then write it."

"I am dying faster than I can hold a pen. I've tried. Look at the desk."

I looked. There were three sheets there, covered in a hand that deteriorated line by line until the words drowned entirely. He had been trying for some time.

"I'm not asking you to decide tonight," he said gently. "I'm asking you to know. That's the cruelty of knowledge, isn't it? You can't unknow it, and you can't be innocent of it again. I've carried this since 1849 because I told myself silence was safety. Silence was never safety. It was just the dark."

Outside, the river rose. I heard it against the tower stones, patient as interest accruing.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat in the clock room with the lamp turned low and listened to the master clock tick through the small hours, that sound I had trusted all my life — the sound of things proceeding as designed, of mechanisms keeping faith with their purpose.

But I kept seeing the ink sitting on top of the paper instead of in it. And I kept thinking: a clock that runs rough tells everyone.

At dawn the storm broke apart, and the light that came through the east windows was the pale, washed-out gold of a town about to celebrate a lie. Below, faintly, I could hear men shouting measurements at the river, and hammers — the anniversary preparations, going forward regardless, bunting going up on a bridge no one could cross.

Elias appeared in the doorway with a fresh sheet of ledger paper and a pen, and set both on the table before me, and said nothing at all.

That was the trap, I understood then. Not the flood, not the stranded weeks, not even the letter. The trap was that he had waited until I was the only witness left, and that he knew — the way I knew gear teeth, the way I knew load and wear — exactly what kind of person keeps quiet, and what it costs her, and what she'll do when silence finally weighs more than the truth.

He sat down across from me, folded his ruined hands, and waited for me to pick up the pen.
