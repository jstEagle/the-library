## 3. Ledger of Hours

The letter from the Historical Society arrived on a Tuesday, which I have always considered an insult. Mondays are for disasters; Tuesdays ought to be safe.

It came up the hill in the postman's saddlebag, and it sat on the observatory's front table like a summons, because that is precisely what it was. *Dear Keeper Thorn,* it began, in a hand so confident it might have been printed, *in anticipation of the town's sesquicentennial celebrations, a committee of historians will call upon you within the fortnight to collect and catalogue all documents pertaining to Aldenmoor's founding.*

Elias read it twice. His hands shook — they had been shaking for weeks, but now they shook with purpose, which is a different animal entirely.

"Within the fortnight," he said. "They'll want everything. The letter especially. They'll put it under glass." He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing went on long enough that I fetched him the laudanum without being asked. "Under glass," he repeated, when he could speak. "Like a saint's finger bone."

"Or a forged one," I said.

He looked at me then with something I hadn't seen in his face before. Not gratitude, exactly. Recognition, maybe — the look of a man who has been shouting into a well for forty years and finally hears an echo come back.

"Yes," he said. "Or that."

I should explain what I understood at that moment, because I did not understand it gracefully. I understood that I was going to help him. I understood this the way one understands a tooth is going to ache: not as a decision but as a fact arriving on schedule. Twenty years of keeping my head down, of answering every question about myself with weather and clockwork, of being nobody in particular in a series of towns that never asked twice — twenty years of that, and here was an old dying man handing me a reason to be somebody, and my body accepted it before my caution could file an objection.

"There's a ledger," Elias said. "The maintenance ledger. It lives in the cabinet beneath the master clock. Every repair, every adjustment, going back to 'forty-one. Nobody reads it but clockmakers. Historians don't want to know how a thing works. They only want to know what it proves."

"You want me to write your confession," I said, "in a book about oiling gears."

"I want you to write it where it will survive me." He pressed his palm flat against the table, steadying it by force of will. "Paper in a drawer can be burned. Paper in a drawer can be *sorted*. But a ledger that travels with the clock — Gideon, that book will outlive every man on that committee."

*Gideon.* He'd started using my name that week, and I still hadn't decided how I felt about it. A name is a handle; handles let people pick you up.

"When do we start?" I asked, and heard my own voice say it as if someone else had rented my throat for the evening.

* * *

We worked at night. This was partly necessity — Elias slept in fragments through the day, waking confused and angry, his mind a clock whose escapement had begun to slip — and partly strategy. The town below kept its eyes on the observatory tower, and a lit window at midnight was a story they'd tell each other. So we drew the curtains and burned candles down to stubs, and I learned that the master clock, ticking above us through the floorboards, was the best accomplice either of us had.

Here is a thing I know that most people don't: a great clock is never silent even when it isn't striking. The train wheels turn, the pendulum breathes, the whole mechanism murmurs to itself like an old man reading aloud. From the town below, that murmur is reassurance. *All is well at the observatory. The keeper keeps his hours.* And while the clock murmured, two voices could speak underneath it unheard.

Elias dictated. I wrote.

The maintenance ledger was not built for confession. Its columns were ruled for dates and services rendered — *Escapement cleaned; pallets re-polished; oil applied to third-wheel pivot* — and its margins were narrow and its paper was good rag stock that took ink like a grudge. At first I tried to fit the truth into the format, writing small and neat in the service column:

*October 12th, 1893. Regulator adjusted. Also: the founding letter is false.*

Elias made me strike that out. "No," he said. "If you write it like a footnote, they'll read it like a footnote. Write it like a man dying. Write it like it cost something."

So I did. Night after night, I filled the blank pages at the back of the ledger — the ones no clockmaker had needed yet, the ones waiting like unlit rooms — with Elias Thorn's voice in my hand. How in 1861 three men had come to him, young and ambitious, needing a founding document older than the town actually was, because land grants and charters turned on dates and the dates were against them. How he had aged the paper with tea and smoke. How he had matched the ink to a period formula and practiced the dead clerk's hand for four months until his own mother couldn't have told the difference. How the letter had grown the town a decade of legitimacy, and the decade had grown into a century, and the men who commissioned the forgery were now the names on the schoolhouse, the bridge, the park.

"Put down their names," Elias said. "All of them. Don't soften it."

"They'll come after the ledger if—"

"They won't find it. That's the beauty of it, girl. Nobody audits a clock." He settled back against his pillows, gray as candle wax. "And if they do find it — let them. Let them spend the rest of their comfortable lives explaining why a maintenance ledger knows more history than they do."

I wrote until my wrist ached. I wrote while the flood rose another foot against the bridge pilings below. I wrote while the anniversary banners went up in the town square, visible from the tower windows as smears of red and gold, and I thought: *they are sewing bunting for a lie, and I am copying out the lie's deathbed confession by candlelight, and somewhere a committee of respectable men is packing carpetbags to come collect the evidence.* There were moments — I will admit this — when the whole enterprise seemed insane. When the sensible thing seemed obvious: let the old man die, let the letter go under glass, keep my head down, keep moving, stay nobody.

But every time I reached for sensible, I found I'd already written another page instead. My hands had made a decision my head was still litigating. Hands are like that. They're closer to the truth than heads are.

* * *

On the ninth night, Elias could no longer sit up, so I brought pen and ledger to his bedside and took dictation lying-down, which slowed us both. His voice had gone thin, a wire drawn too fine, and I had to lean close to catch it, close enough to smell the illness coming off him, sweetish and wrong.

"Tell them the date it was finished," he whispered. "June 3rd, 1861. Tell them I signed my own name in the margin of the draft before I burned it, because I wanted one true thing in the whole document, even if no one ever saw it."

"Is that true?"

"It is now," he said, and smiled, and the smile was the old mischief and something newer and sadder underneath it. "That's the trouble with confessions, Gideon. You get to compose them."

"That's not how confession works."

"No? Then how does it work?"

I dipped the pen. "You tell the truth and lose something."

"And what are you losing, writing this?"

I didn't answer. I wrote. What would I have said? That I'd spent twenty years being a shadow with excellent references, and that transcribing a dying liar's honesty into a gear-oil ledger was the most real work I'd ever done, and that frightened me more than any historian's committee ever could?

Near dawn he slept, really slept, and I sat in the chair beside him with the ledger open on my knees, finishing the last page. Outside, the flood had crested — I could tell by the sound, water finding its level, the roar flattening into a broad brown hush. The sky over the river had gone the color of pewter, and the first light came up gray and cold across the water.

At some point I noticed the room had gone quiet in the wrong way.

Not the sleeping quiet. The other kind — the kind where the silence itself seems to be holding its breath, waiting for you to notice. I set down the pen. I listened for his breathing and heard the clock instead, steady above us, marking the hour it always marked, indifferent and faithful as ever.

Elias Thorn had died somewhere between one line of ink and the next, without fanfare, without my noticing, the way a clock stops between ticks.

I sat there a long time. Then I finished the sentence I'd been writing, dated it, and did not sign it — not yet, not with any name. Below the tower, faint on the wind off the river, I heard the town waking: carts, bells, the hammering of the last anniversary preparations. And on the road from the east, right where the floodwater had begun its slow retreat, a carriage — early, eager, dusted with mud to the windows.

The historians had not waited for the bridge to open properly.

They were already crossing.
