## 1. The Unfinished Coastline

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Lena finally dipped his pen.

He had been holding it for some minutes without writing—just holding it, the way a man holds a key to a door he is not yet ready to open. The drafting table before him was a country unto itself: the master's atlas, open to plate forty-one, the coastline of the southern provinces laid out in the old man's hand, confident and fine as a hair pulled taut. Everything south of Cape Meren remained unfinished. A ghost of pencil. A promise no one would keep now except him.

Lena set the pen down and pressed his fingertips into his eyes until the dark bloomed orange behind them.

Three days since they'd carried Master Ilyes up from the harbor in a box too short for him—they'd had to fold his arms, Lena had heard the men say it carelessly in the yard below, as though folding a dead cartographer's arms were no different from folding a sheet—and already the workshop had begun its slow reversion to silence. The clock in the tower across the canal struck two, and Lena counted the strokes out of habit, because Master Ilyes had taught him that a mapmaker who lost track of time lost track of everything. *Time is the fourth coordinate,* the old man used to say, tapping the brass instruments on their stand. *Any fool can fix a point in space. The art is fixing it in time.*

The candle guttered. Lena trimmed the wick with his thumbnail and bent back over the plate.

Ink first—the good iron-gall, mixed fresh because the old batch had gone thick and brown as dried blood. He charged the nib, touched off the excess against the bottle's lip, and drew the first true line of the evening: a stretch of coast running southwest from the cape, following the penciled soundings the master had made on his last survey, the one he had returned from with salt still crusted in his beard and a look in his eyes that Lena had been too young and too busy to read properly.

*He knew,* Lena thought, not for the first time. *He came back knowing something.*

He put the thought down the way you put down a heavy tool—carefully, in its place, to be picked up again later. There was work. There was always work. That was the mercy of the craft: grief could not survive at the scale of one-to-five-thousand. Grief had no place on a graticule. When Lena's hand moved, when the line came off the nib clean and unbroken, he was nobody's orphan apprentice, nobody's half-caste boy from the river district whom respectable clerks pretended not to see. He was simply the line itself, and the line was true.

He worked the coast for an hour before allowing himself the soundings. Depth figures went along the channel in the master's notation—fathoms, small and neat, tucked against the shore like secrets. Lena filled them in from the field book that lay open beside the atlas, the leather cracked, the pages soft as cloth from handling. On the last page, in the master's cramped hand, was a note Lena had read perhaps forty times since the funeral:

*South of Meren unreliable after '44. Do not trust the Admiralty sheets. Survey again if ever there is time.*

There was no time. There was never any time. But Lena wrote *unreliable* in the margin of the proof copy, in small letters, because a map that lied by omission was worse than no map at all—that was the second law of the house, carved into the lintel above the door in letters worn nearly smooth: **THE MAP MUST TELL THE TRUTH OR THE MAP IS A WEAPON.**

Lena had walked under those words every day of his apprenticeship. Only lately had he begun to wonder whether they were a warning or a confession.

*

The sky over the canal had gone from black to the grey of dirty pewter when he heard the boots.

Not one pair. Many. The particular cadence of soldiers on cobbles—heavy heels, unsynchronized but unified, the rhythm of men who did not need to be quiet because nothing in Vellmark dared challenge them. Lena's pen stopped mid-stroke, a bead of ink swelling at the tip. He let it fall onto blotting paper rather than the plate, and stood, and crossed the dark workshop to the window in three steps that made no sound. He had learned to move silently years ago, in this same building, so that the master would forget he was there and speak freely on the occasions when visitors came—visitors who looked at Lena the way men look at furniture with an unusual grain.

Below, in the blue-grey light, six soldiers of the Imperial garrison stood before the workshop door. A seventh held a bucket of paste. An eighth held papers.

Lena watched a sergeant smooth a broadsheet against the door, working from the center outward with the flat of his hand, thorough as any tradesman. Then another broadsheet beside it. Then another, on the window frame, on the post beside the steps. They were papering the whole frontage, the way you paper a condemned house.

He should have felt fear. What he felt instead, in the first instant, was professional offense. Whatever those notices were, they had been pasted crooked—one corner lifting already in the canal damp—and some traitor part of his mind itched to go down and straighten them.

Then he read the nearest one through the glass, upside down and backwards, the way he read everything, because seven years of drafting had trained his eye to reverse text without effort.

*By order of the Magistracy of Vellmark, acting under warrant of the Imperial Census and Security Act: the premises herein described are declared sequestered to the Crown. All documents, charts, maps, plates, engravings, and records of whatsoever kind found upon said premises are subject to seizure and confiscation...*

*All maps.* Not the workshop's debts, not the unpaid taxes, not some quarrel with a neighbor. Maps. Plates. Records. The words were general—warrants always were—but the soldiers had come to this door, this morning after the third day, and Lena felt the meaning assemble itself behind the general language like a coastline assembling itself out of fog.

They wanted what was inside. What was inside was the atlas.

He stayed at the window while the sergeant stamped his seal—Lena saw the wax, red even in the grey light, like a wound—and while the squad formed up and marched away eastward toward the customs house, leaving the notices glistening wet on the door. A cat picked its way along the opposite quay, paused to regard the papers with the contempt of all cats, and moved on. Somewhere a bell began the early shift at the ropewalk. Vellmark was waking, and waking exactly as if nothing had happened, which was how Vellmark woke every day.

Lena turned back to the drafting table. The candle had died while he watched; the room was lit only by the window's pewter glow, and the atlas lay open on it, plate forty-one, the unfinished coast, the pencil promise running south toward Cape Meren and stopping.

*All maps, plates, and records of whatsoever kind.*

His hands, when he looked at them, were steady. He noted that the way he'd note a sounding—as data, to be dealt with later. Underneath the steadiness something else was moving, slow and cold, like water finding its level through the bilges of a hull. Because here was the arithmetic he could not avoid: Master Ilyes had spent eleven years on this atlas. Eleven years of surveys the Admiralty hadn't commissioned, of coastal soundings the harbor office had no record of, of inland routes drawn in a detail no commercial atlas required—roads through the hill passes, ford points on the border rivers, the exact depths of channels that appeared on no other chart. And eleven days ago, on his last walk home from the harbor, the master had gone into the water between the pilings of the coal quay where the night watch swore no lamp had been lit, and the magistrate had ruled it misadventure within a week, within *days*, with the efficiency of a man clearing paperwork off a desk before a holiday.

*A drunkard's fall,* the coroner's clerk had said, not unkindly, because everyone knew Master Ilyes drank, and everyone knew a mapmaker's trade took him to docks at strange hours, and everyone knew these things the way everyone knows weather—without needing anyone to make them true.

But the master had not drunk in two years. He had told Lena so himself, the last autumn, gripping the boy's shoulder with a hand like a drafting clamp: *Clear head, clear lines. No more of it. A man making enemies cannot afford blurred edges.* Making enemies. He had said it plainly, the way he said *reef* or *shoal*, marking the hazard on his own private chart.

And now the soldiers had come with paste and broadsheets, three days after the burial, to seize all maps.

Lena sat down at the table. He picked up the pen. He finished the stroke he had abandoned, a clean line running south, and then he worked for another hour in the grey light without candles, because his eyes were good and the lines were simple, and because working was the only prayer he knew.

When the coast of the southern provinces was inked as far as the master's pencil ran, Lena sanded it, blotted it, and closed the atlas.

It was not finished. Forty-one plates done, and the final plate—the forty-second, the one the master had locked in the flat cabinet beneath the floor and never spoken of beyond a single instruction, given weeks ago in that same clamped voice: *If anything happens to me, the atlas goes to print complete. Complete means complete, boy. You'll know the plate when you find it.* Lena had searched once, carefully, the night after the funeral, and found nothing, and told himself he would search again with a clearer head, and had not yet dared.

Now he would dare tonight. Now there was no choice in it at all, because the atlas was contraband. The truth he had spent seven years learning to draw was, as of this morning, property the Crown meant to bury. Every hour it sat in this room was an hour it could be found and burned, and with it the master's eleven years, and whatever it was the master had died rather than surrender.

Lena rose and went to the shutters and looked out at the street one more time. The notices hung on the door, five of them now, redundant and official, their paste still shining. In the strengthening light he could make out the seal at the bottom of each one—not the city's common seal, but something finer, a personal device, a magisterial signet.

He knew that seal. He had seen it pressed into the warrants that authorized the master's surveys, back when the surveys were licensed; he had inked facsimiles of it for practice as a first-year apprentice, copying documents as calligraphy exercises. It was a stag's head between two stars.

The seal of Magistrate Corvin.

The man who had ruled the death an accident. The man whose warrant now sealed the dead man's life's work inside a shuttered house.

Lena stood a long moment with his forehead nearly touching the cold glass, watching the ordinary morning brighten over the canal, over the masts, over the crooked notices, and understood—with the terrible clarity that comes only at the end of a sleepless night—that he was not grieving anymore. Grief required the luxury of standing still.

Somewhere in this room was a plate worth killing for. He intended to find it before the soldiers came back with a key.

* * *

He waited out the daylight hours the way a hunted animal waits—not sleeping, not eating more than bread and hard cheese at the bench, listening to the street. Twice, clerks from the magistrate's office walked past and paused at the notices, copying the description of the premises into ledgers. Once, a boy in livery knocked, received no answer, and left a printed summons weighted with a stone against the door. Lena read it through the glass at dusk: *the responsible party of the premises to present himself to the Office of the Magistrate within three days for inventory and disposition.* Three days. They were giving the dead man's household three days to produce itself for counting.

Let them count the empty rooms. Let them find the hearth swept and the bed cold and no responsible party anywhere, only a half-caste apprentice from the river district who had never existed in any ledger worth searching. For seven years Lena had made himself invisible—no surname spoken aloud in the city, his mother's Okonkwo folded away like a letter from a disapproved correspondent, his father's absence explained as orphanhood, plain and simple, because orphanhood asked no questions. Invisible as a watermark. He had hated it, sometimes, quietly, the way you hate a chronic ache. Tonight, for the first time, he blessed it. No one would look for a person who was not on any list.

Darkness came down over Vellmark like a dropped cloth. Lena lit no candle until the tower clock had struck midnight and the last lamplighter had passed, and then he worked by a single shielded flame to open the floor.

The cabinet was beneath the flagstone by the hearth, exactly where he had guessed—the stone sat a finger's width proud of its neighbors, and the mortar around it was newer, whiter, the mason's secret betrayed by color the way all secrets are. The iron handle turned stiffly, complaining. Below, wrapped in oilcloth and smelling of linseed and camphor, lay the master's hidden things: a strongbox of coin he had clearly expected someone to need; a bundle of survey notebooks Lena had never seen; and, flat and large as a coffin lid, a wooden case banded in brass.

He lifted it onto the drafting table. His heart was going hard enough that he could feel it in his wrists. He cut the bands, raised the lid.

Inside, packed in wool, lay a single copper engraving plate—and Lena knew at once, the way he had known the flagstone, that this was the plate. The forty-second. The final one. Its face was polished mirror-bright, and etched upon it, in the master's finest work, was not a coastline at all.

He tilted it toward the candlelight, and the image swam up out of the metal: the title composition of the atlas—or what should have been the title composition—except the title was wrong. Where the words *A General Atlas of the Vellmark Territories* should have stood, the master had engraved, in letters small enough to pass at a glance but deep enough to print forever:

**THE TRUE COASTS: BEING AN ACCOUNT OF WHAT THE CROWN HAS ORDERED UNDRAWN.**

And around the border of the plate, woven into the decorative scrollwork where no censor would think to read, ran a continuous line of text, tiny as a signature, dense as scripture. Lena fetched the loupe from its drawer with a hand that had begun, at last, to tremble, and brought the flame close, and read.

It was a list. Names. Dates. Sums of money paid. Ships' names, and the names of the officials who had signed their clearances. Deportation quotas, written as calmly as tide tables. And recurring through it all, anchoring it like a compass rose, the stag's head between two stars—Corvin's seal—appearing again and again beside the word *approved*.

The atlas was not an atlas. It had never been only an atlas. Eleven years of surveys, of roads and fords and depths no commercial map needed—it had been evidence, gathered plate by plate, the master drawing his indictment in the only language the Crown could not forbid him: geography.

At the bottom right of the plate, in the position where an engraver signs his work, the master had left the metal blank. Unfinished. Waiting.

Lena set down the loupe very gently, as if it might go off.

Then he saw the rest of it—a thin ridge at the plate's edge, a seam where no seam should be. The backing board, he realized. The plate was mounted on a board, and the board was thicker than it ought to be, and along one edge, almost invisible, ran a line of stitches. Thread. Someone had sewn something flat into the backing of the final plate of the atlas, the way you might sew a letter into a coat lining before a long journey.

Lena took up his smallest knife.

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, the tower clock gathered itself and began to strike three.
