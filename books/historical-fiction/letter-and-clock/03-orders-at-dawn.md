## 3. Orders at Dawn

The knock came before the lamps were even lit.

Lena had been awake since the third bell, as he was every night now, working by the light of two candles and the small brass lamp Master Aldric had kept for fine engraving work. The atlas lay open across the drafting table, its finished plates fanned out like the wings of a great sleeping bird—coastlines, river deltas, the mountain passes of the northern provinces, all of it drawn in Aldric's hand or in Lena's careful imitation of it. He had been filling in the shading on the Vellmark harbor chart when he heard boots on the cobblestones outside. Not one pair of boots. Many.

He put down his pen.

In the weeks since the master's death, Lena had learned to listen the way soldiers listened—for weight, for number, for intention. These were heavy boots, deliberate, spaced too evenly apart to be drunkards or late-night laborers. They stopped at the workshop door. A fist struck the wood, three times, hard enough that the hanging plates rattled on their wires above him.

"By order of the Imperial Magistracy of Vellmark," a voice announced through the door, flat as an official stamp, "this premises is seized effective immediately. Open in the name of the Crown."

Lena did not move. His heart was moving fast enough for both of them.

The notices had appeared on the door only yesterday afternoon—a broadsheet nailed up at eye level, printed in that particular bureaucratic typeface that made cruelty look like clerical error. *By order of His Honor Magistrate Corvin, the workshop of the late cartographer Aldric Fen is hereby declared subject to imperial seizure, its contents impounded for examination, its occupants remanded for questioning.* He had read it three times while pretending to sweep the step, and each reading had told him something worse than the last. Not the workshop's contents. Its *occupants*. The word had been chosen carefully. Magistrates chose words the way mapmakers chose coastlines—every curve deliberate, nothing left to chance.

Aldric had died eleven days ago at the foot of the harbor stairs, his skull broken against the stone in what the coroner had called, with impressive speed, an accident. Eleven days, and already the magistrate's hand had closed around everything the old man had built. Everything he had died protecting.

The fist struck the door again. "Open, or we break it."

Lena looked at the table. At the atlas. Forty-one plates, each one the size of a large book, each one inked on paper so fine it felt like cloth. Thirty-nine were finished. The fortieth—the final plate, the one Aldric had hidden somewhere in this room during his last week alive, the one whose absence had been gnawing at Lena like a toothache—was still unaccounted for. And the forty-first, the harbor chart under his hands, was half-shaded, incomplete, the water around the eastern mole still waiting for its soundings.

They would take all of it. Or they would burn all of it. He had seen what happened to documents the magistracy examined: they went into a warehouse on Cordwainer Street and never came out again. Aldric had spent six years on this atlas. Six years of surveying trips Lena hadn't been allowed to join, of locked drawers and whispered measurements, of a paranoia the apprentice had once mistaken for eccentricity and now understood as prophecy.

"I said open the door!"

There was no back way out of the workshop. There never had been; Aldric had designed the place like a ship, everything visible, nothing wasted. But there was the coal chute.

It opened into the cellar, a cast-iron hatch set into the alley-side wall, used twice a year when the coal merchant came with his sacks. It was narrow—narrower than Lena liked, and Lena was not a large man—and it let out into the coal yard behind the chandler's shop two doors down. Aldric had shown it to him in his first month, laughing: *"Every building has a mouth it doesn't want you to know about, boy. Learn where the mouths are."*

Lena took the lamp and moved through the workshop with the terrible efficiency of someone who has already grieved and cannot afford to do it twice. The plates first. He slid them from their wires, stacked them, wrapped the stack in the oilcloth groundsheet from the survey kit. The harbor chart he lifted wet-inked and all, pressing it flat between two boards from the drying rack. The atlas was not a thing anymore. It was cargo.

Then the cellar, and the chute.

The iron hatch screamed when he turned it—he'd known it would, had oiled the hinge two nights ago precisely because he'd known it would, and the oil had done nothing against cold and rust and fate. Boots thundered overhead. Wood splintered somewhere above him, the front door surrendering, and voices poured into the workshop like water into a hull.

"In there! The drafting room—careful with the tables, you animals, those are evidence—"

*Evidence.* The word almost made him laugh. Almost.

Lena fed the oilcloth bundle through the chute first, then the boarded chart, then himself, shoulders first, arms raised, the way one dives into dark water. The iron scraped his chest through his coat. For one horrible moment he stuck, wedged at the hips, hearing his own blood in his ears and the soldiers' voices growing louder directly above him, and then the chute did what chutes do, which is deliver you whether you will it or not, and he dropped into the coal yard in a black avalanche, rolling, coming up coughing with soot in his throat and his eyes streaming.

He gathered the bundles. He ran.

* * *

He did not stop until he reached the salt warehouses at the river's edge, where the fish-oil stink covered all other smells and the night watch never bothered patrolling because there was nothing worth stealing. Only then did he allow himself to turn around.

From between the warehouses he could see the hill above the harbor district, and on it, the workshop. Windows he had washed every Saturday for four years glowed orange. Smoke stood up from the roofline in a straight grey column, unhurried, as if the fire had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

They had not even waited to remove the furniture.

Lena sat down on a coil of rope with the atlas across his knees and watched his whole life burn. Four years of apprenticeship. The smell of sizing and lamp-black. Aldric's voice correcting his lettering—*"Your R's are lazy, boy; an R should stand up like a soldier"*—the voice that was gone forever and that he could still hear perfectly, which was somehow the cruelest part. The kettle on the iron stove. The way the master hummed off-key when he worked on mountains, because mountains, he claimed, deserved music.

An accident, the coroner had ruled. Broken neck at the harbor stairs. Case closed within a day, faster than any case Lena had ever heard of, faster than decency. And now the workshop seized and burned in the same week, and the atlas—the atlas Aldric had guarded like a second heartbeat—would have followed if a boy with soot on his face hadn't crawled out through the building's secret mouth.

They wanted it destroyed. Which meant it was dangerous. Which meant it was true.

Lena pressed his palms flat against the oilcloth wrapping. Under his hands, through canvas and paper and dried ink, he could feel the shape of every plate: the coastline of the western provinces that official maps showed three miles further north than it actually was; the roads marked with weights and bridges that the army's own charts omitted; the census districts Aldric had drawn with such strange precision, noting not just boundaries but populations, densities, the locations of families the government had stopped counting publicly years ago. It wasn't an atlas. It was testimony. It was a witness that couldn't be drowned at the bottom of a staircase.

And somewhere inside it—or meant to be inside it—was the fortieth plate. The final plate. The one Aldric had hidden.

*I'll find you,* Lena promised the fire. *Whatever he left in you, I'll find it, and I'll finish what he started.*

Above the harbor, the column of smoke bent eastward in the morning wind, toward the open sea.

* * *

Dawn found him in the loft above the cooperage on Tanner's Row, a space he rented under a name that wasn't quite his—Lena Okon, dropping the syllables that marked him as foreign, as mixed, as anything worth a second glance. Invisibility was a craft too, and he had practiced it longer than cartography. A soot-dark boy of uncertain origin carrying a large wrapped bundle attracted no attention in Vellmark at dawn; the city was full of such boys, all of them tired, none of them interesting.

He unwrapped the atlas on the floorboards and counted the plates again. Thirty-nine finished. One in progress. One missing.

Then he saw it.

The harbor chart—the one he'd carried out wedged between its drying boards—was heavier than it should have been. He had assumed the weight was the boards. But when he lifted the chart away from them and held it up to the grey light coming through the loft's single window, he noticed the backing. Every plate in the atlas was mounted on a linen-backed card, standard practice, Aldric's own specification. But the linen on this one was thicker. Stitched, along one edge, with thread so fine it might have been mistaken for a manufacturing seam.

Lena fetched Aldric's smallest knife—the one the master had used for trimming quills—and cut the stitches.

Inside the backing, folded into eighths, was a document. Not a map. Paper of a different stock entirely, heavier, cream-colored, sealed with wax that had cracked along one fold but held intact at its center. And stamped into that wax, sharp and unmistakable even in the bad light, was a seal Lena knew better than he wanted to.

Not the imperial crest. Not the magistracy's public arms.

The private signet of Magistrate Corvin himself—the coiled serpent device that appeared on exactly none of his official correspondence, the seal Aldric had once pointed out to Lena on a dinner invitation and said, quietly, *remember that mark, boy; remember it the way you'd remember the mark of a wolf you've seen near the sheep pen.*

Lena's hands were shaking. He unfolded the letter.

It was addressed—not signed, addressed—in a hand he did not recognize, cramped and hurried, to a name he did not know: *To the House of Alvar, Salt Lane, Vellmark. To be delivered by hand only. Burn before reading if delivery proves impossible.* Below the direction, in smaller script, as though the writer had feared running out of courage: *He knows what we know. He killed to keep it. This is the proof. God forgive us all.*

He read it five times. The words did not become clearer; they became heavier. Somewhere in those cramped lines was a list of names—names of families, dates of shipments, sums of money, and at the center of it all, written plainly, without euphemism: the arrangement by which certain persons were to be *removed* from Vellmark province under cover of the new imperial census, and the price Magistrate Corvin had accepted for arranging it.

And beneath that, in Aldric's own hand—a postscript, added later, in the careful lettering of a man who knew this document might be the last thing he ever wrote:

*If I am dead, trust the bearer. He has my eyes and my training. Finish the atlas. Print them together.*

Lena sat very still for a long time. The loft was cold. Down in the street, the city was waking up around the smoking ruin on the hill, vendors crying their wares, carts rattling, ten thousand people beginning a day in which nothing had changed for any of them.

But everything had changed. The atlas was contraband now, and so was he. The master was murdered, and here, sewn into a map of the harbor where he'd died, was the reason why—and the name of the man who'd done it, stamped in his own private wax, arrogant as a signature on a confession.

Corvin had burned the workshop thinking the atlas was ash. Corvin did not know that a boy had crawled out through the coal chute with the world strapped to his chest.

Lena rewrapped the letter, stitched the linen closed again with thread from his repair kit, using stitches finer than the original ones, and strapped the plates to his body with the survey harness—oilcloth against his sternum, boards against his spine, buckles drawn tight. When he stood, he looked like any courier bent slightly forward under his load. When he breathed, he felt the atlas press back against every breath, a second ribcage, a second heartbeat.

*Aldric,* he thought. *Your R's stand up like soldiers. Mine will too.*

Outside, the clock tower over the customs house began to strike seven, each note falling on the city like a slow hammer. Nine days—that was what the notice nailed to the tribunal house had said, before the soldiers tore it down: the magistrate's commission would convene its special session nine days from yesterday. Whatever Corvin intended to bury, he intended to bury it legally, quickly, and permanently.

Which gave Lena nine days to find a house called Alvar on a street called Salt Lane, in a city where soldiers now had his description and his master's killers had his scent.

He pulled his cap low, settled the straps, and went out into the dawn.

At the corner of Tanner's Row, a posted notice fluttered on the wall, fresh paste still gleaming: *SEIZURE ORDER — FEN CARTOGRAPHIC WORKSHOP — OCCUPANTS TO BE REMANDED.* Beneath it, someone had scrawled in chalk, hastily, in a hand Lena didn't recognize: *Salt Lane remembers.*

He memorized the chalk the way he memorized coastlines, and walked toward the river gate.
