## 5. A Name Not His Own

The river gate smelled of tar and wet rope, and by the third hour of his shift Tomasz Varek had stopped noticing either. He stood at the wooden barrier where the ferry road met the customs house, stamping manifests, waving through carts of herring and barrels of pitch, and thinking—not for the first time that morning—about the sum of four hundred crowns.

It was not a number he could hold in his head all at once. Four hundred was a house. Four hundred was his mother's apothecary debt paid twice over, with enough left to buy back her father's silver watch from the pawnbroker on Cordwainer Street, who kept it behind glass like a relic. Four hundred was what Petrov the moneylender said he owed now, after two years of interest compounding like mold in a damp cellar, and Petrov had a way of saying it slowly, smiling, tapping the ledger with one blunt finger, as if the number were a guest they were both entertaining.

Tomasz's wage was eleven crowns a month. The imperial army did not pretend this was generosity; it pretended instead that a man should be grateful to carry a musket and a uniform for the privilege of standing in the rain at a checkpoint while merchants who earned his yearly pay before midsummer cursed him for slowness.

"Move along," he said to a fishwife whose cart he'd already waved past ten seconds earlier. She gave him a look of pure contempt, which was fair. He deserved contempt today. He deserved it most days.

The morning fog was burning off the river in long gray ribbons. Beyond the barrier, the ferry landing creaked, and the water ran brown-green with spring melt, carrying sticks and petals and once—Tomasz had seen it clearly—a drowned cat, bloated and dignified, sailing downstream like a small admiral. He thought: even the cat is going somewhere.

That was when he noticed the boy.

He came down the ferry road on foot, alone, which was itself unusual—a young man traveling without a caravan or a master was either very poor or very suspicious, and the boy's coat was too good for poor. Dark wool, city cut, salt-stained at the hem but recently brushed. He carried a leather document case slung across his chest the way soldiers carried ammunition satchels, close and protective, and under his arm was a flat bundle wrapped in oilcloth that Tomasz recognized, from years of customs work, as map plates. Survey plates. The kind that went to the Hydrographic Office, the kind that were worth more than the boat they traveled on.

And the boy walked like a man walking into a wall he had decided not to see.

Tomasz had watched thousands of people approach this barrier. Fear had shapes. The smugglers wore their fear loose, in darting eyes and over-casual hands. The guilty wore it stiff, chins up, performing innocence so hard it squeaked. But this boy's fear was quiet and total, like a man who had already fallen and was simply waiting to hit the ground. He didn't look at the guards. He looked at the papers in his own hand, reading them again, memorizing them, and his lips moved slightly as he walked.

*Here we go*, Tomasz thought, and felt something in his chest lift, just slightly—the small ugly lift of a drowning man seeing another head go under near his own.

"Halt." He put out his hand. "Papers."

The boy stopped. Up close he was younger than he'd seemed at distance—seventeen, maybe eighteen—with skin a few shades darker than anyone else in the queue, hair curling tight against his skull, and eyes that were doing a remarkable job of being calm and a terrible job of everything beneath the calm. His accent, when he spoke, was Vellmark harbor-flat, flawless, learned somewhere sharp.

"Survey courier," the boy said. "Plates for the Hydrographic annex, priority seal. I have my passage chit here, and—" he produced a folded paper with a flourish that was almost convincing, "—the requisition docket."

Tomasz took the papers. He was not an intelligent man, and he knew it, but twelve years at gates and barriers had given him a kind of animal literacy in documents, the way a shepherd can read weather in a sheep's coat without knowing why. The chit looked right. The stamps sat in the right corners. The ink was the correct violet-brown of the Office presses.

But there was no requisition docket. There was a second paper where the docket should be, folded to look thicker than it was, and when Tomasz unfolded it he found a surveyor's field authorization signed by someone named H. Merck, Master Cartographer—and the signature was beautiful, confident, a signature belonging to a man who had signed ten thousand documents and never doubted one of them.

Except that Master Cartographer H. Merck had been dead for three weeks. Tomasz knew this because everyone in Vellmark knew this; it had been in the *Gazette* between a notice about cholera quarantines and an advertisement for whale-oil lamps. Drowned, the Gazette said. An accident at the tidewater survey. And then, two days later, a shorter notice: workshop sealed by order of the magistrate pending inventory.

So the dead man's signature sat in Tomasz's hand, attached to a living boy, and the boy was watching him read it with the stillness of a rabbit watching a hawk decide.

"The docket," Tomasz said slowly. "This is a field authorization. Where's your cargo manifest?"

"They don't issue manifests for sealed plates." The boy's voice didn't waver. That was the impressive part. "Chain-of-custody travels inside the case. If you open it, you void the seal, and the Office bills the delay to whatever garrison unit caused it. Section nine." He recited it like scripture. "I can wait while you send for a clerk who knows section nine."

It was a gamble dressed as courtesy. Tomasz had been on the other end of this trick a hundred times—clerks loved nothing better than making a soldier feel stupid with a regulation number—but the boy delivered it perfectly, wearily, the tone of a courier who had done this eleven times already today and would do it eleven more, bored down to the bone.

There was only one problem. Tomasz *had* sent for a clerk once, over a sealed case, eight months ago. The clerk had come, had opened the case, had found forty pounds of untaxed Virginia tobacco sewn into a false bottom, and Tomasz had received a commendation in the form of a paragraph in the garrison log and no money whatsoever. Meanwhile the smuggler's cousin had found Tomasz outside his lodgings three nights later and explained, politely, with a knife resting against his ribs like a cold finger, exactly how much a commendation was worth in the markets of Salt Lane.

Nothing. It was worth nothing. Commendations weighed less than air.

"I'll need to verify the signature," Tomasz said.

"Of course." The boy waited.

The fog had burned off entirely now. Sunlight lay hard on the river, and hard on the queue backing up behind the boy: a wool merchant drumming his fingers, two nuns, a family with a goat that bleated like a saw going through tin. Every minute cost the garrison goodwill it did not have. Every minute also gave Tomasz time to think, and thinking was the enemy.

Because here was the shape of it, plain as the ledger he owed his life against: a dead cartographer's signature, sealed survey plates, a boy with no visible means and no master, three days after the magistrate sealed the Merck workshop and posted seizure notices on every customs board in the district. Notices Tomasz himself had helped paste up Tuesday, his boots splashed with paste-water, while the sergeant joked about how the old mapmaker must have been spying for somebody, because why else would the magistrate want his papers burned before inventory?

Burned. The notices hadn't said burned. But the sergeant had said it, laughing, and sergeants at checkpoints know things the way dogs know storms.

If Tomasz opened the case and reported the forged signature, the boy went to the magistrate's men, and whatever was in those plates went with him, and Tomasz got another paragraph in the log. Perhaps a half-crown reward. Petrov's interest alone ate half a crown before breakfast.

Or—

"You're a long way from the Office district," Tomasz said, buying time, testing. "Courier work usually runs from the annex on Candlemaker Row."

"The annex moved its dispatch point last week. New security protocols after the Merck affair." The boy said *the Merck affair* the way a stranger would, clean and distant, and Tomasz thought: he has rehearsed this sentence in front of a mirror, or in front of someone, and rehearsed it until the grief drained out of the name. Because there had been something under it. Something he'd stepped over like a root in a dark path.

A cart driver behind them shouted, "Are we crossing the river today or next Lent?"

Tomasz held the papers a moment longer. Then, quietly, without looking up, he said: "Section nine is real. You know that. You learned that somewhere."

The boy said nothing. The silence was the most honest thing he'd offered yet.

"And the signature is good," Tomasz went on, almost to himself. "Better than good. Dead men don't sign, though. So either you're robbing the estate—and thieves don't dress their cargo in government seals—or you're carrying something out of that workshop that somebody very high up wants carried, or wants very much *not* to be carried." He finally looked up. "Which is it?"

For a moment—one long breath of river wind—the mask slipped. What showed underneath wasn't guilt or cunning. It was something older and heavier, a weariness that had no business on a face that young, the look of a person carrying weight strapped to his chest who had passed the point where putting it down was possible. Tomasz recognized it. He saw it in his own reflection some mornings, leaning over Petrov's ledger.

"My grandmother used to say," the boy said at last, carefully, "that the river doesn't ask the ferry why it's crossing."

"Your grandmother sounds like a woman who paid bribes."

"She sounds like a woman who survived," the boy said, "which costs about the same."

Something in Tomasz's chest turned over. He thought of his mother, who had survived her husband, her debts, and two imperial censuses, and who now faced the third census with a cough that wouldn't leave and an apothecary's license that Petrov held as collateral. Who would be deported—there were rumors, always rumors, of new removals after the census—to the eastern districts, where the air was bad and the work was worse, unless four hundred crowns appeared by midsummer like a miracle with interest.

He looked at the queue. He looked at the river. He looked at the boy, who stood very straight now, having spent whatever honesty he'd allowed himself, waiting for the axe or the pass with equal readiness.

Tomasz had never taken a bribe. He wanted that recorded somewhere, in some invisible ledger of the self: *twelve years at gates and barriers and never once*. Other men took them constantly—it was practically a toll—and told themselves stories about necessity, and Tomasz had despised those stories with the special fury of a man who was poor and honest and proud of it, because pride was the only property poverty couldn't repossess.

But pride did not pay Petrov. Pride did not buy back the watch. Pride would stand at his mother's bedside in some eastern quarantine town and say *at least I never compromised myself*, and she would look at him with love and something else underneath the love, something like pity, and he would deserve the pity more than the love.

"How much is the passage chit worth to you," Tomasz said, very quietly, "if it doesn't clear this morning?"

The boy understood instantly. That was the worst part—how instantly he understood, as if he'd been carrying the coin ready since the coal-dark hour before dawn, as if he'd known that every gate in the empire had a Tomasz standing at it. His hand went into his coat and came out with a small purse, and from the purse a coin, and he set the coin on top of the barrier post between them, covering it with two fingers, casual as a man adjusting his cuff.

One crown. Silver, heavy, minted this decade. A fortune by Tomasz's standards and an insult by any smuggler's, which meant it wasn't a smuggler's coin. It was everything the boy had, or nearly.

"That's not enough to buy anything," Tomasz said.

"It's not meant to buy anything." The boy's voice dropped to match his. "It's meant to say I know what you're risking, and I'm sorry, and I'd give more if I had it, because what I'm carrying matters more than I do. That's what it says. Read it however you need to."

Behind them the goat screamed. The wool merchant swore. Somewhere on the river a ferry bell rang the quarter-hour, bright and idiotic, marking time for people who believed time belonged to them.

Tomasz picked up the coin. It vanished into his glove the way such coins do, between one heartbeat and the next, a sleight of hand performed a thousand times daily across the empire by men who would each deny it forever.

"Chit's in order," he said loudly, in his gate voice, flat and official. "Field authorization verified against registry. Plates are sealed chain-of-custody, section nine, no opening authorized at secondary posts." He stamped the chit—*thunk*, the sound of a door closing on his old life—and handed the papers back. "Courier proceeds. Next!"

The boy took the papers. For a moment his fingers stayed on them, on the far side of the transaction, and he looked at Tomasz with an expression that was not gratitude exactly. Gratitude would have been easier. It was recognition—the look of one carrier acknowledging another, two men hauling different weights past the same barrier.

"What's your name?" the boy asked, low.

"No," Tomasz said. "You don't get that. Nobody gets that. In a year, if anyone asks, I never waved you through, I never spoke to you, and you never existed. Same as me. Understood?"

"Understood." A pause. Then, so quietly the river nearly took it: "Thank you anyway. Both ways."

He walked on. Down the plank ramp, onto the ferry deck, into the crowd of deck passengers and stacked crates, and the oilcloth bundle never left the crook of his arm and the leather case never left his chest, and Tomasz watched him go the way you watch a lit fuse travel away from the powder store—relief and dread arriving in the same pulse, inseparable.

The ferry pulled out. The current took it. The boy stood at the rail and did not look back, which Tomasz respected enormously, because looking back was how men got caught.

* * *

The rest of the shift passed in stamps and herring and the goat's ongoing grievances. At noon the sergeant ambled over, chewing, and leaned on the barrier beside him.

"Quiet morning."

"Quiet," Tomasz agreed.

"Heard you had a survey courier through early. Merck office plates." The sergeant's voice was pleasant, which was how his voice always sounded when it was dangerous. "Reznik's boys flagged the manifest copy at the harbor annex. Magistrate's got an interest in anything that floated out of that workshop. Sealed orders." He shrugged. "Probably nothing. Old man's papers. Still—if another one comes through, flag it up the line first, eh? Before the stamping. There's a word going around about rewards for diligence."

Tomasz felt the crown in his glove like a coal. "Understood, Sergeant."

"Mm." The sergeant pushed off the barrier and wandered away, and Tomasz stared at the river and did the arithmetic he would be doing for the rest of his life, probably: one crown against four hundred; one boy against one mother; a paragraph in a log against a name nobody would ever speak.

He had waved through a dead man's signature. He had waved through sealed plates that the magistrate himself wanted stopped. Whatever that boy carried, it was heavy enough that Corvin's men were watching the ferries within a day of the workshop sealing—and light enough to be carried on one stubborn young chest through the front door of the empire.

Tomasz wondered what he had just let pass. He wondered whether, in a month or a year, soldiers would come to his lodgings with questions, or whether history would simply flow over him like the brown-green river over its sticks and petals and drowned admirals, indifferent, carrying everything downstream.

At dusk he walked home the long way, past Cordwainer Street, and stopped in front of the pawnbroker's window. Behind the glass, on black velvet, his grandfather's silver watch caught the lamplight—still there, still waiting, patient as debt itself.

Four hundred crowns to midsummer.

He had one.

He walked on toward home through the gathering dark, denying it already, and behind him the river kept moving, taking the boy and the letter and the whole buried truth of Vellmark toward the second city, toward the tribunal, toward everything Tomasz would swear forever he had never touched.
