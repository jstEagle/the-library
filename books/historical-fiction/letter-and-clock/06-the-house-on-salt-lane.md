## 6. The House on Salt Lane

Salt Lane smelled of tallow and wet stone, the way poor streets do when the sea has given up trying to reach them. Lena had walked past it a hundred times in his years of errands for the master, always at a mapmaker's pace, eyes down, counting steps without meaning to. Now he counted again—forty-one doors from the corner of Rope Walk—and stopped before the one with the brass plate worn nearly blank.

*ALVAR.*

Only the *A* and half the *L* still held their polish. The rest had been rubbed away by weather or by hands, he couldn't tell which, and the omission felt deliberate, like a name being unlearned on purpose. Above the plate, the shutters were closed though it was not yet noon. Smoke curled thin from a vent near the eaves—not chimney smoke, kitchen smoke, but something else beneath it, an oil-and-solvent smell that any apprentice of six years would know anywhere.

A press was running somewhere inside.

He stood in the street with the atlas plates strapped against his chest beneath his coat, the letter sewn flat between canvas and skin, and understood that his heart was making more noise than the press. He had imagined this moment differently on the road out of Vellmark—imagined knocking, speaking the master's name, being believed. Now, with the workshop burned to its foundations behind him and three checkpoints ahead of him, belief seemed like a currency he had no papers to prove.

He knocked anyway.

The sound went into the house and did not come back out. He waited long enough to count sixty heartbeats. Then the bolt drew back a hand's width and one eye appeared in the gap, dark-ringed, appraising him the way a weighmaster appraises a coin suspected of clipping.

"You're bleeding," the eye said.

"I'm not."

"Your lip is." The door opened further. A man stood there, sixty perhaps, built like a barrel stave, with ink stains worked so deep into his knuckles they looked like bruising. "Whatever you're selling, we don't buy. Whatever you're preaching, we don't listen. Good day."

"My name is Lena Okonkwo," he said, and then, because the lie of the last two days had taught him how much truth costs and how much it buys: "I was apprentice to Aldous Wren, the cartographer. He was killed four months ago. I have something that belonged to him. Something meant for this house."

The man—Alvar, it could only be Alvar—did not move. Behind him, from the depths of the hall, came the rhythmic sigh of the press, patient as tide. "Wren," Alvar repeated. "The map man. They said his ladder slipped."

"They said wrong."

For the first time the eye in the doorway widened slightly. Alvar looked past Lena's shoulder at the empty lane, then stepped back and jerked his chin inward—an invitation grudging as a tax. "Quickly. And if anyone asks, you're my sister's boy from upcountry, slow since the fever."

* * *

The room they gave him was behind the printing floor, low-ceilinged, lit by one window set high enough to show only sky. A woman sat at a table sorting metal type into wooden cases by candlelight despite the hour, her fingers moving over the letters the way Lena's moved over coastlines—by touch, by memory, by love too practiced to call itself love. She looked up when they entered and did not smile.

"This is my daughter Mirela," Alvar said. "Mirela, the boy claims to carry something from Aldous Wren."

"Claims." She went back to her type.

"The magistrate's men burned our workshop yesterday morning," Lena said. "They posted seizure notices first, then they came early, before the notice said they would. I got out through the coal chute with what I could carry. Master Wren's atlas. His final work. There's a letter hidden in the backing of the last plate, and it names this family."

The word *names* landed in the room like a dropped weight. Mirela's hands stopped. Her father's jaw tightened.

"That's a strange thing to say to strangers," Alvar said quietly.

"It's a stranger thing to be true."

"Then prove it's true." Mirela turned fully now, and Lena saw she was younger than her gravity suggested—mid-twenties, perhaps, with her father's dark eyes and none of his caution. "Anyone can walk in off the street claiming anything. We've had informers before. We've had worse. My brother spent two years in the Marsh Prison because a man we fed told a lieutenant what books we kept." Her voice did not rise; it sharpened. "So recite it. If you read this letter, recite it."

"I didn't read all of it," Lena admitted. "I read enough. When I found it, I—" He hesitated, then chose truth again; it was becoming a habit, dangerous and clean. "I memorized what I saw. It's what I'm trained to do. A mapmaker holds coastlines in his head; holding sentences isn't so different."

"Then hold them now."

Lena closed his eyes. He had gone over the fragments a hundred times since dawn, walking, hiding, waiting out patrols—turning them like plates under lamplight until every line sat where it belonged.

"The seal is black wax," he began. "Not the magistrate's public seal—the civic one with the scales—but his private signet. A tower with a broken clock face, the hands frozen at the fourth hour. I know it because Master Wren kept a rubbing of it hidden in his strongbox. He'd been collecting evidence against Magistrate Corvin for years. That's why they killed him. That's why they burned everything today."

Mirela's father made a small sound, quickly swallowed.

"The letter opens with a date—the ninth of March—and it's addressed to *the household of Alvar, printers, Salt Lane.* It says—" Lena slowed, letting each sentence come back to him whole, exact. "*What follows was taken from Corvin's own desk by one who owes your father a debt older than fear. The census rolls are not for counting. Names marked with the black cross will be summoned in the spring and deported north under writ of quarantine. Among the first marked: this household, the family Ferenc on Cordwainer Row, and the widow Sable and her sons. The second list names the payments—Corvin has sold these deportations to the northern labor contractors at nine crowns a head, receipted in his private ledger, the same ledger that records the sum of forty crowns paid to one D. Havel for 'quieting' a certain mapmaker whose survey showed too much.*"

He opened his eyes.

The silence in the room had changed texture. Before, it had been the silence of suspicion. Now it was the silence of people hearing their own death warrant read aloud in a stranger's voice.

"There's more," Lena said softly. "It ends with instructions. Deliver this to the judge Halloran in the second city before the tribunal sits. Nine days. It says the master trusted your press because your press had already been silenced once and survived it. It's signed with a single initial—*V.*"

"*Viktor,*" Alvar whispered. He had gone gray. He lowered himself onto a stool as though the stool might otherwise escape him. "Viktor Sable's son. The widow Sable's boy. He clerked in Corvin's office two winters. We thought—he wrote once that he'd found steady work. Steady." He laughed, and there was nothing in the laugh. "He stole from the magistrate's own desk and sent the proof through a dead man's atlas."

"He sent it through a living man's atlas," Lena corrected, "until four months ago."

Mirela had risen during the recitation. She stood at the high window now, arms crossed, looking at the square of sky as though answers might be printed there. "Say the part about us again. The census part."

"Names marked with the black cross will be summoned in the spring and deported north under writ of quarantine. This household is among the first marked."

"And the price?"

"Nine crowns a head. Receipted."

She turned around. Something had settled in her face—not peace, nothing like peace, but the terrible calm of a decision already finished. "Then we're dead either way. If the letter stays hidden, we're dead slowly, in the spring, politely, with receipts. If it comes out, we're dead fast, but—" she glanced at her father— "but other people might not be."

"If it comes out, we can run," Alvar said. "There are ships out of Vellmark. Cousin Petra has rooms above her shop in—"

"Papa." Mirela crossed the room and took his ink-stained hands in hers. "They burned Wren's workshop yesterday. Do you think running will be slower next spring? The letter is the only reason we have any warning at all. And the letter is worthless unless someone with power reads it before Corvin's tribunal makes everything legal. Nine days. One judge. One press." She looked at Lena. "You brought it here. Why here? You could have gone to Halloran yourself."

"I tried the river gate. I don't have papers anymore—they burned with everything else. And the letter says your press survived being silenced once. Master Wren trusted this house. I trust what he trusted." He paused. "Also, I have nowhere else."

That last honesty seemed to move the old man more than all the recitation had. Alvar studied Lena for a long moment—at the coat too fine to be a beggar's and too scorched to be respectable, at the soot still shadowed in the creases of his neck, at the way the boy held himself like someone carrying a full cup across a crowded room.

"You're the apprentice," Alvar said. "The one who used to deliver our paper stock. The quiet one who never spoke Vellmark dialect right."

"You knew me?"

"I knew a boy who watched everything and said nothing. I thought you were a spy for the guild." He smiled without humor. "Instead you're a spy for the dead. Worse trade."

* * *

They fed him bread and fish soup at the sorting table while the press ran on—it was printing hymnals now, Alvar explained, harmless as milk, the kind of work that keeps soldiers uninterested. Mirela ate standing, restless, and twice caught herself looking at the ceiling beam as though measuring it for rope.

"My mother was deported," she said abruptly, the third time she spoke. "Before any of this. Twelve years ago, in the last census, before we understood what the marks meant. She had family in the northern provinces they claimed was seditious. We never got a letter. We never got anything." She set down her spoon. "So when you say nine crowns a head, boy, understand that I already know exactly what a head costs. I've been priced my whole life."

Lena said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a lie, and lying to her suddenly felt obscene.

"What I don't understand," Alvar said, "is why Wren hid it with us. Viktor could have sent the letter anywhere. To Halloran direct. To a newspaper in the capital."

"Because mail gets searched," Mirela answered for him. "And because Halloran reads everything that crosses his bench—but Corvin controls what crosses it. No. Viktor needed the letter carried by hand, hidden in something no soldier would bother to open. And he needed it placed with people who'd know what to do when it arrived." She looked at her father. "People who'd been robbed before and learned from it."

"A press," Alvar murmured. "He wanted a press to see it."

"He wanted witnesses," Mirela said. "Halloran alone can be bought, or promoted, or buried. But a press means copies. Copies mean it can't be unread."

The old man was silent a long time. Outside, the light through the high window had begun to fail, and someone in the lane called a child home to supper, the voice ordinary as bread, belonging to a city that did not yet know what had been burned in it.

Finally Alvar pushed back from the table. "Show me the plate."

Lena unstrapped the bundle from his chest. His shoulders ached with the relief of setting it down, and he hated how good it felt to be rid of even a little of the weight. He unwrapped the canvas layer by layer and laid the copperplate engraving on the sorting table between the type cases: the final plate of the atlas, the unfinished coastline, and beneath the paper backing—if you knew where to press—a small hard ridge the length of a letter.

Mirela ran her fingers along it and nodded once, professionally, the way she'd nod at a well-set line of type. "Sewn in with silk thread, doubled. Whoever did this has done it before." She looked up. "We cut it out tonight. Then we decide."

"Decide what?" Alvar asked, though his voice said he knew.

"Whether we're a family that runs," Mirela said, "or a family that prints."

The press sighed in the next room, patient as tide, waiting to find out which.

And beneath the floorboards, faint but unmistakable, Lena heard—or thought he heard—boots. Two pairs. Passing. Slowing.

Stopping, somewhere on Salt Lane, just out of sight of the door.
