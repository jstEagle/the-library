## 7. Reading by Candlelight

The letter smelled of the sea.

Mirela Alvar noticed it before she noticed anything else—the brine-and-iron tang that clung to the paper the way it clung to everything in Vellmark in winter, rising off the harbor in grey sheets and settling into cloth, into wood, into lungs. She had grown up with that smell pressed into her father's aprons, into the felt blankets of the press room, into her own hair on mornings when she'd slept badly. It was the smell of the city that had made them, and now it rose from a dead man's correspondence like a witness taking its seat.

"Hold the candle lower," her father said.

She held the candle lower.

They had carried the boy up from the cellar an hour ago, half-frozen, his lips gone the color of candle wax, and her mother had put him by the stove with broth he was too proud to admit he needed. Now the workshop above the house on Salt Lane was shuttered, barred, dark except for this one flame, and the four of them—Mirela, her father Dario, her mother, and the boy who called himself no name at all—stood around a table like conspirators in a play they had not auditioned for.

The letter lay flat between them, cut free at last from the backing of the atlas plate where some careful dead hand had stitched it. The stitches had been fine work. Mirela had done the cutting herself because her father's hands shook too much, and she had thought, as her blade slid beneath the thread: *whoever you were, master cartographer, you sewed like a printer.* The seam had been set under the paper facing of the plate, invisible unless one knew to look for the slight ridge, the whisper of thickness where there should have been none. It was the oldest trick they had. Her grandfather had hidden pamphlets that way when the last emperor's men came through Vellmark burning presses. Some inheritance passed down through fingers even when the tongue that explained it was lost.

"Read it," her father said again, and his voice was not steady.

Mirela broke the wax.

The seal crumbled along its fault line, and she caught her breath—not at the letter but at what had sealed it. A black boar's head, tusks forward, crowned with a baron's band. She knew that crest. Every soul in Vellmark knew it. It hung over the customs house; it was stamped on the deportation writs that had begun arriving that autumn; it was carved into the magistrate's bench in the Hall of Assizes, gilded so that it gleamed when Magistrate Corvin looked down at the accused.

"That is—" her mother began.

"I know whose it is," Mirela said quietly. "That's rather the point."

* * *

She read aloud because her father asked her to, and because reading aloud is how printers test a text—how it sits in the mouth, whether it trips or runs clean—and because if she did not give the words a voice she feared they would stay only shapes on paper, deniable, dreamlike, safe to disbelieve.

*"To the House of Alvar, printers, Salt Lane."*

Her father flinched at the name. The boy by the stove watched her with eyes that did not blink enough.

*"If you are reading this, then I did not live to deliver it myself, and I must ask you to do what your family has always done, which is to put truth into ink and ink into hands. Forgive me for binding you to my death. I had no one else. Everyone else I trusted is dead or bought.*

*I was the imperial cartographer, Aurel Vess. You will not know the name. That was the design. For eleven years I made maps of this coast for men who intended to use them, and I told myself a map is innocent—a map does not choose what is done upon it. I no longer believe this. I write to unburden myself of it.*

*In March of this year I was summoned to the residence of Magistrate Corvin and shown a document under imperial seal: the schedule of the new census. You will have heard it praised as an instrument of order. It is an instrument of removal. Corvin has drawn up lists—not of households, as the census proclaims, but of bloodlines. Families to be enumerated, catalogued, and deported north to the labor settlements before the ice breaks in spring. The Alvars are on the list. So are forty-one other families in Vellmark and the river towns. I saw the lists. I copied the names.*

*When I refused to survey the routes for the transport barges—refused to draw the maps that would make the deportations efficient—I was told I would be given a second chance to reconsider. I did not reconsider. On the night of the ninth of October, two men came to my workshop. I knew one of them. He wore the magistrate's livery under a plain coat. They did not expect me to survive the night, and they were right, though not in the way they intended. What follows is what I arranged before they returned to finish it.*

*Enclosed with this letter, in the plates of my atlas, you will find the evidence: the census schedules in Corvin's own annotation; the transport manifests bearing his private countersign; and the deposition of the man who drove the cart from my workshop on the ninth of October, taken down and witnessed, naming his paymaster. Corvin ordered my murder because I would not make the machinery of his cruelty run smoothly. He rules the tribunal that will try the families on his lists. There is no court in this province that will hear you. There is only the press.*

*Print it. Print all of it. Print it fast, and print it everywhere, and sign nothing, and may whatever watches over printers watch over you.*

*A.V."*

The silence afterward had weight, like snow on a roof.

Mirela set the letter down on the table and discovered her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the wood to stop them, the way she'd been taught to steady a sheet of type before locking the chase.

"He named us," her mother said. Her voice came out very small. "He wrote our name down in a dead man's letter, and now we're in it. We're in it forever."

"Forty-one other families too," Mirela said.

"Forty-one families I cannot feed or shelter," her father said. "I can barely feed the three under this roof."

Dario Alvar had not sat down since the boy arrived. He stood now at the window with its shutters closed, and in the candlelight Mirela could see how old he had gotten without anyone telling him—the stoop of his shoulders, the liver-spotted backs of his hands, the way his jaw worked around words he couldn't afford to say. He had run the press for thirty years, mostly broadsheets and almanacs and hymnals, honest gray work, and once, long ago, before Mirela could read, something else—the family trade, the dangerous inheritance—which he spoke of the way other men spoke of a war wound. Reluctantly. In low tones. As something that had cost more than it bought.

"We burn it," he said finally. "We melt the wax back together, we stitch the letter into another plate, and we take the atlas and the boy and we go. Tonight. Upriver to my cousin's mill at Brezany, and from Brezany over the mountains in the spring, and we never—" His voice cracked and he hated it, she could see him hating it. "We never speak of any of this again."

"Papa." 

"Don't." He turned from the window. "Don't *Papa* me, Mira. Do you know what Corvin does to printers? Not to pamphleteers—to *printers*? They took the Ostrava man last summer. They didn't hang him. They broke his hands first, both of them, with the frame of his own press, in the square, so everyone would understand what a press is worth. Then they sent him north anyway. His wife got one letter. Half of it was written by someone else, guiding his hand."

"And what happens to the forty-two families if we run?" Mirela asked. "What happens in spring when the barges come?"

"They come for someone else's daughter instead of mine."

The words fell into the room and lay there, ugly and true, and her mother put her face in her hands, and Mirela understood—with a clarity that frightened her—that her father was not a coward. Cowards lie to themselves. Dario Alvar was doing the opposite: he was looking directly at the arithmetic of love, which has always favored the few over the many, and finding it correct. That was the terrible thing about him. He was being reasonable. Reasonable men had kept this family alive through three reigns and two purges, and reasonable men would keep them alive through this, if alive was all they wanted to be.

By the stove, the boy shifted. "The master sewed it to reach you," he said. His voice was rough, unused. "Not to be found after. To be *delivered*. He died arranging that. And the magistrate burned his workshop tonight to bury the atlas. I carried the plates out through a coal chute while the roof came down." He touched his chest, where the strap-marks still showed above his collar like a harness worn too long. "I can't make your choice for you. But I know what it cost to get that letter to this table. And I know the names on those lists are still in Corvin's keeping, and every day they sit there, the ink dries a little harder."

"You're what—seventeen?" her father said.

"Sixteen. Nearly."

"*Sixteen*, and lecturing me about ink."

"He's lecturing you about time," Mirela said.

* * *

Afterward she could never reconstruct exactly how the argument went—only its weather, which moved like the harbor fog, thickening, thinning, closing in. Her mother wept and then stopped weeping and started counting: flour, coin, cousins, roads, the practical liturgy of flight. Her father paced the length of the workshop, twelve steps each way, the same track he walked when a job was going wrong on the press. The boy said little, which somehow counted for much; he had the particular authority of someone who had already lost everything being argued over.

And Mirela stood at the type case with her back to all of it and ran her fingers across the compartments, feeling the faces of the letters in their beds—she'd known the case blind since she was seven, could set a line of type in the dark, and she did it now, slowly, without paper or purpose, just to feel what her hands wanted.

Her hands wanted to set the letter.

That was the whole of it. That was the discovery underneath the fear, plain as a forme locked in its chase. Somewhere between the second reading of *the deposition of the man who drove the cart* and her father's voice breaking on *someone else's daughter*, the fear had stopped growing and something else had begun, and now it filled her chest with a heat that had nothing gentle in it. Fury, she supposed. People wrote songs about fury as if it were grief's loud cousin, but nobody told you it was quiet, that it was precise, that it sat down at the type case and began choosing fonts.

"Mira." Her father had stopped pacing. "You haven't said what you think. You've said plenty about everything else, but not that."

She turned around.

"I think we are already in it," she said. "I think we were in it the moment Corvin put our bloodline on a list, and the letter changed nothing except that now we know. Burning it doesn't take our names off his schedule. It only takes the names off *everyone's* knowledge. It makes us safe and makes us accomplices, and I don't want to be either." She lifted the letter, careful of the torn wax. "Look at what this is, Papa. Schedules in his own hand. Manifests with his countersign. A witnessed deposition naming his paymaster. This isn't a grievance. This isn't a pamphlet. This is a *case*. It's the kind of thing tribunals pretend they've never seen because they've never been handed it whole. If we print it—"

"If we print it, we die."

"If we print it, we might die. If we run, we die slower, somewhere colder, having saved ourselves and no one. And if we hide it—if we stitch it into another plate and wait for better years—we become the drawer it was sewn into, Papa. We become furniture. He counts on furniture." She heard her own voice rise and forced it down. "The master spent eleven years telling himself maps were innocent. He wasn't wrong to believe it. He was wrong to stop at believing it. We have a press. We are not innocent, and we are not furniture, and I am not going north in a barge with my name on his list and my family's skill still warm in my hands."

The candle guttered in a draft from the stairwell and steadied.

Her mother looked up. Her father looked at his daughter for a long moment, and Mirela watched him search her face for the child she'd been and fail to find her, and something in her both broke and finished at the sight.

"You sound like your grandmother," he said at last, hoarse.

"She sounds right, then."

"The Ostrava man—"

"—didn't have a deposition. Didn't have manifests. Didn't have a magistrate's private seal on a murdered man's letter. Papa, we've printed ten thousand hymnals. One true thing, once, with proof behind it—there is nothing in this city harder to confiscate than that. Burn a copy, there are twenty more. Hang a printer, there's a widow with a story. Corvin's power lives in the gap between what he does and what people know he does. A press is a bridge across that gap. That's why they break printers' hands. Because they're afraid of exactly this." She set the letter down between them, gently, like laying a card. "Nine days. The boy says the tribunal convenes in nine days. After that, the lists activate, and it won't matter what we print."

Her father closed his eyes. Twelve steps one way. Twelve steps back. When he opened them, he was not defeated—Mirela would remember this, would cling to it in the hard months coming—he was simply a man setting down a weight he had carried for thirty years and finding his shoulders still shaped to it.

"My cousin's mill at Brezany," he said, "will be very disappointed."

"Papa—"

"We'll need the good paper," he said, walking toward the press, suddenly all craftsman, brisk and unbearable. "Not the gray stock. The rag paper, the watermarked. If this document is going to hang a magistrate, it will outlive us all and it will be examined by experts, and I will not have it fall apart in some archive because my daughter was impatient." He threw back the canvas cover from the press with a snap, and dust went up gold in the candlelight. "Mira. Compose the masthead. No imprint, no address—but a device. Something they'll remember. Your grandmother used a lark. I think it's time the lark flew again."

Her mother made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and went to bank the stove.

Mirela turned to the type case, and her hands, which had not shaken since the reading ended, went to the twenty-six beds of letters and found their places as easily as breathing.

It was the boy who stopped them all—lifted one finger toward the shuttered window, head tilted, listening. Down the lane, faint but unmistakable, came the measured tread of boots, several pairs, and the creak of a lantern bracket, and a voice giving orders in the flat tone of soldiers who have an address and a warrant and all night.

"They've found the workshop burned," the boy breathed. "Now they'll look for what walked out of it."
