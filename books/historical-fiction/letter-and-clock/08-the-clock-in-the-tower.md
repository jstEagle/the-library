## 8. The Clock in the Tower

The clock in Vellmark's harbor tower had governed Lena Okonkwo's life for eleven years without his ever once thinking about it.

It governed the workshop. That was how he would have put it, if anyone had asked—which no one had, because apprentices were not asked things, they were told them. The master rose when the seven o'clock bells shook frost from the eaves of Salt Street; he ate his bread when the quarter-hour struck; he sent Lena to bed at ten with a hand on the boy's shoulder and the words *the coast will still be there tomorrow, but your eyes will not*. The clock was the metronome under every line Lena had ever drawn, and he had never thought about it at all until now, standing at the Alvars' window with ink on his fingers that would not scrub out, counting chimes like a man counting coins he could not spend.

Seven o'clock. One day gone since the workshop burned. Eight left.

Behind him, in the long room that smelled of lead type and lamp oil, the Alvar family was deciding whether to save their own lives or throw them away, and they were doing it loudly.

"We go north," old Alvar was saying. He stood by the cold hearth with his back to the room as though the argument were happening somewhere behind him and he wanted no part of its geography. "Tonight. There are boats out of Grelsk—"

"Grelsk is four days' walk and the roads have soldiers on them." Mirela sat at the worktable with the letter open before her, her hands flat on either side of it as though she were pinning a living thing down. She had not touched it again since she finished reading it aloud an hour ago. Lena suspected she never would touch it casually again. Some documents change weight when you understand them. "Four days there, however long to find a boat, however long to cross. And where? North is the Empire too, Father. It's only colder."

"Then east."

"East is Corvin's family. East is worse."

"There must be *somewhere*—"

"There isn't." Mirela said it without cruelty, which made it worse than cruelty would have been. "That's what the letter means. You keep hearing it as evidence against one magistrate. Listen to it again. It's evidence against the whole arrangement. Corvin didn't invent the deportations. He's just the one whose name got written down." She looked up at Lena then—not accusingly, he noted, though he felt accused anyway, simply because he had carried the thing here, because his hands had been the last hands on it before hers. "You read it. Tell him I'm wrong."

Lena had read it four times now: twice on the road from the burning workshop, once by candlelight while the Alvars watched him like men watching a surgeon open a body, and once more alone in the corner after midnight because he did not trust his memory and his memory was all he had left of anything. He knew it the way he knew coastlines. That was the trouble with training. Everything you learned became a place you could be lost in.

"I can't tell you you're wrong," he said. "The census schedules are dated. They're real dates. The deportation manifests use shipping tonnage that matches what the harbor office requisitioned in spring. Whoever wrote this wasn't guessing." He hesitated, then gave them the part that mattered most, the part that had kept him awake until the tower struck three. "And the handwriting on the marginal notes is Corvin's. I've seen it. The master kept copies of every official order that crossed the workshop—the survey orders especially—and half of those came signed from the magistrate's office. It's the same hand. Same pressure, same way he makes his f's."

Old Alvar turned from the hearth at last. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with printer's shoulders that had spent thirty years hunching over a composing stick, and his face had the gray, washed-out look of someone who had already lived through one confiscation and had hoped never to see the inside of another. "Then we run," he said. "You don't fight a thing like this. You survive it. That's how we survived last time. We printed what we could, we buried the type, we ran, and twenty years later we came back and set up shop again under a new name. Running works."

"Running worked when it was pamphlets," Mirela said. "This is different. This is—" She stopped herself. Lena watched her decide whether to say the next thing, and watched her say it anyway. "This is Master Halden's death. If we run, whoever killed him wins quietly, and nobody will ever even know there was a contest."

The name landed in the room and stayed there. Halden. Even now, even saying it silently to himself, Lena heard it the way the master had insisted it be said—not *the master*, not *sir*, but Halden, a man's name, a name that belonged to a specific stubborn person who had died face-down among his own maps with the back of his skull broken by something that the coroner, in a ruling signed by Magistrate Corvin, had called *a fall against the corner of the drafting table*.

Lena had believed it. That was the worst part, the part he carried like a stone sewn into his coat along with everything else. He had come home and found the workshop door sealed with wax and official ribbon, and he had believed it, because believing it meant the world still worked, meant that men fell against table corners, meant that grief had a shape and the shape had paperwork. Three days later he had found the letter stitched into the backing of the atlas's final plate—stitched with the same thread the master used for everything, hidden so well that Lena had only found it because he knew the stitching pattern of every map in that atlas better than he knew his own heartbeat—and the world had stopped working forever.

Eight days. Seven now, almost. The tribunal convened on the morning after the ninth chime-count, and Judge Halloran's circuit court sat in Tarnholt, the second city, two hundred miles inland across the marsh country, and whatever was going to happen would happen between here and there.

"The judge," Lena said, and the room quieted around his voice the way water quiets around a dropped stone. "Halloran. That's the whole question, isn't it. Whether Halloran is what people say."

"What do people say?" Old Alvar asked.

"That he took the circuit assignment nobody wanted because it keeps him away from the capital. That he threw out a requisition order last year that Corvin personally endorsed. That he's honest." Lena shrugged, uncomfortable with hearsay dressed up as knowledge. "People also say the marshes are haunted. But enough of it fits together that it's worth more than nothing."

"It's worth exactly nothing," Alvar said flatly, "if the letter doesn't reach him. And it won't reach him, because the roads are closed, because the checkpoints started going up the day after the fire—I have customers, boy, printers talk to each other—and because anyone carrying a document like that is carrying his own death warrant with the seal showing."

"So we don't carry it with the seal showing." Mirela's eyes had gone bright in the lamplight, and Lena recognized the look because he saw it in mirrors sometimes: the look of a craftsperson who has just spotted the flaw in a problem and cannot rest until she has fixed it. "Father. You're talking about smuggling a document. I'm telling you we should stop thinking about it as a document at all."

They argued until the eight o'clock bells, and past them, and the argument changed shape three times without ever resolving, because arguments about survival never resolve—they just exhaust themselves into decisions. Lena mostly listened. He had learned listening in the workshop, where the master held a rule that an apprentice who talked over a correction had not understood it. But underneath the listening he was doing arithmetic, and the arithmetic kept coming out the same way no matter how he arranged the numbers.

Two hundred miles. Nine days minus one already burned. Checkpoints at the river gate, at the marsh causeway, at the Tarnholt bridge—the master had mapped them all, years ago, in a private folio of "administrative friction points" that was itself probably worth hanging for, and Lena had memorized that folio the way he memorized everything, helplessly, completely. Three checkpoints. Three chances to be searched, questioned, broken. And a letter that named the Alvar family in its opening lines—a letter that, if found on any carrier, would hang that carrier and destroy the family it was trying to save.

He could see the shape of the solution before he could bear to look at it directly. That happened with maps too. You'd see the coastline first, whole and inevitable, and only afterward would you understand what drawing it would cost.

At half past nine, when old Alvar had retreated to the loft to argue with his wife in low voices and Mirela was pulling specimen papers from a drawer—laying them side by side like a physician comparing symptoms—Lena finally spoke the arithmetic out loud.

"If someone from this house carries it, they die, and the house dies with them. Everyone in Vellmark knows what an Alvar looks like. Your press has a reputation. Corvin's men will be watching this street within the week, if they aren't already."

"They are," Mirela said. "There was a man outside since dusk. Gray coat, bad posture, pretending to read a broadsheet upside down." She said it lightly, but her hand had gone still over the papers.

"Then it can't be anyone from this house. And it can't be hired, because hired men sell you. And it can't be delayed, because the tribunal convenes in eight days and Halloran needs time to act *before* it convenes, not after." Lena heard his own voice steadying, the way it steadied when he drew a difficult curve, and hated it a little, and went on anyway. "So it has to be someone no one knows. Someone who can pass as what he claims to be. Someone who has spent his whole life being invisible and is very, very good at it."

Mirela looked at him. She was quick—he'd known that since the first hour, when she'd caught the seam in his sleeve where the plates were strapped—but she was quick the way printers are quick, seeing the whole page at once, and he watched her assemble the meaning of what he'd said faster than he wanted her to.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the offer yet."

"I don't need to. You're about to volunteer to carry it yourself. Two hundred miles through three checkpoints with a document that names my family, wearing a dead man's apprentice coat." Her voice had gone flat and hard, the voice of someone building a wall out of words. "No."

"With respect," Lena said, "it isn't yours to refuse."

The silence that followed had teeth in it. Down in the street, the gray-coated man turned his broadsheet right-side up, discovered his error, and folded it away in embarrassment.

"Mirela." Old Alvar had come back down the loft stair without anyone hearing him; thirty years around presses taught a man to move quietly. He stood at the foot of the stairs looking older than he had at supper. "Let him finish."

"There's nothing to finish. He's a child."

"He's an apprentice of Aurel Halden," Alvar said, "which means he's been trained by the best forger of administrative documents this coast ever produced, for eleven years, for exactly this kind of work, whether Halden ever intended it or not." He came forward into the lamplight and studied Lena with the frank, weighing gaze of a man estimating how much type a page would take. "How old are you, boy?"

"Nineteen."

"Hm. You look sixteen and you sound thirty. That's a useful combination." He pulled out a chair and sat, heavily, the way tired men sit when they've decided something they hate. "Tell me your plan. All of it. Not the brave parts. The practical parts."

So Lena laid it out, and laying it out made it real, and making it real made his hands cold.

He would leave before dawn. Not tonight—tonight the watcher in the street would mark anyone leaving—but tomorrow, at the changing of the watch when the river gate was busiest and clerks were too harried to study faces. His papers were good. That was the one inheritance the fire hadn't taken: the master's folio of blank forms, counterfeited seals, and half-completed credentials for surveyors' couriers, the kind of men everyone ignored because they carried nothing but measurements. He would travel as a courier delivering duplicate plates from a decommissioned coastal survey—the story explained the atlas plates strapped to his chest, explained the route, explained why a boy traveled alone, explained everything except the truth, which would stay sewn into his coat lining where no clerk had ever yet thought to look.

He would avoid the main coach road. The marsh track added a day but subtracted the patrols. He knew the marsh because the master had surveyed it, and knowing the marsh was the difference between walking two hundred miles and drowning in the first six.

"And if you're taken?" Mirela asked. She had not sat down. She stood with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and Lena understood suddenly that she wasn't fighting the plan anymore; she was fighting what the plan cost. "If they search you at some checkpoint and find the lining?"

"Then they find a boy carrying stolen survey materials, and I'm hanged for theft, and the letter stays sewn shut unless they cut the coat apart stitch by stitch, which takes time, and time is the one thing Corvin can't buy either." He paused. He had decided not to say the next part, and then heard himself say it. "And I'll destroy it first. There's a way to tear it free that drops it into the lamp oil I'll be carrying for my light. Four seconds. I've practiced the motion."

"When?" Mirela's voice cracked slightly on the word. "When have you practiced tearing your own death off your chest?"

"Last night. In the corner. While you slept." He met her eyes and did not look away, because looking away would have been a kind of lying and he had decided, standing at the window counting chimes, that he was done with certain kinds of lying even if the plan required others. "I'm not brave, Mirela. I want to be clear about that. I'm not doing this because I'm fearless. I'm doing it because the arithmetic doesn't work any other way. I've done it every direction. This is the only line that closes."

The clock tower began to strike ten.

They all stopped and counted, the way the city had taught them all to count, and Lena counted hardest of all. Ten strokes, bronze and unhurried, rolling out over the rooftops and down into the harbor where the seized ships rode at anchor. Ten o'clock of the second day. Eight days remaining, and each stroke of that bell from now on was a piece of the distance he hadn't covered, a checkpoint he hadn't passed, a chance for Corvin's men to grow more certain, more thorough, more close.

When the last stroke faded, old Alvar nodded slowly, like a man accepting a proof.

"All right," he said. "All right. But you don't go empty-handed and you don't go unprepared. My daughter is the best hand with paper I have ever known—better than me, and I'll deny saying that—and if anyone alive can make that letter disappear into something innocent, it's her. You'll give her tonight and tomorrow to prepare it properly. You'll eat what my wife puts in front of you. And you'll come back, boy, because if you don't, she'll—" he glanced at Mirela, and something passed between father and daughter that Lena couldn't read, some old grief or old agreement, "—she'll never forgive either of us. Go on. Sleep. There's a cot behind the paper stock. It smells of size and glue, but so does everything in this house."

Lena thanked him. His voice came out smaller than he intended.

Mirela walked him to the cot herself, past the sleeping press with its type locked in the chase like a message waiting decades to be read, and at the curtain she stopped.

"Why?" she asked. Quietly, so her father wouldn't hear. "You owe my family nothing. You met us yesterday. You could take the plates and the letter and vanish north and probably live."

Lena considered the true answer, which had several layers, like the letter itself.

"He drew my name into a map once," he said finally. "Halden. On the survey of the northern coast, in the cartouche where the draftsman signs—he put 'and L. Okonkwo, apprentice' in letters so small you'd need a lens to read them. I found it when I was fourteen. I never told him I found it. But I've drawn every map since then as if someday someone might hold a lens up to mine." He looked at his hands, at the ink in the creases that wouldn't scrub out. "His name is on that atlas. If I run, his name dies in a fire with everything else, and Corvin writes the ending. I can't allow that. It's the only thing I can't allow."

Mirela was silent a moment. Then she said, "Tomorrow I'll show you how a printer hides a confession inside an apology," and pulled the curtain closed, and her footsteps went away toward the worktable where the lamp still burned.

Lena lay on the cot smelling of glue and did not sleep. Through the wall he could hear her working: the whisper of paper, the small precise sounds of blade against rule. Above it all, faint through the roof, the tower prepared itself for the next hour, and he lay there counting ahead instead of counting behind—eleven, twelve, thirteen, all the way to the ninth day, all the way to Tarnholt, all the way to the moment when a judge's hands would open what his hands had carried—and somewhere past midnight he understood that he had stopped being afraid in the ordinary way. What he felt now was larger and quieter, the feeling of a coastline committing itself to a particular shape. Once drawn, it could not be undrawn. Only followed.

At five o'clock the tower struck the hour, and Mirela pulled the curtain open, holding a sheet of paper up to the lamp.

"Come and see what we're going to make them believe," she said.
