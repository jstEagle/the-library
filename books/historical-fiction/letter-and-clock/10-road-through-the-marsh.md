## 10. Road Through the Marsh

The marsh began where the road forgot itself.

Lena had walked since before dawn, and by midmorning the hard-packed track that left Vellmark's eastern gate had dissolved into a ribbon of mud and standing water, stitched across with plank bridges so old their wood had gone silver and soft as bread. Fog lay over everything in long gray bands, and through it came sounds he could not place at first—creaking, clinking, a low murmur of many voices—before he understood what he was hearing: carts. Many carts, ahead of him on the road.

He had not expected company. The surveyor's route his master had drawn for this leg ran along the old salt causeway precisely because no one used it anymore; a courier with nothing to declare traveled best where there was no one to declare to. But when he crested the low rise above the first drainage ditch, the fog thinned enough to show him the truth: a line of people and wagons stretching ahead as far as he could see, moving south through the wet ground like a slow dark vein.

Displaced families. He knew them by sight even before he could name the signs—the mattresses lashed to cart roofs, the goose cages, the grandmother seated atop a wardrobe strapped sideways to a handcart, riding her own furniture out of the province. The census edicts had begun emptying the eastern parishes in the spring, and Vellmark's wharves had filled all summer with families waiting for ships that never came. Now they were walking instead.

Lena slowed. Every instinct trained into him said *separate yourself*—a lone courier moved fast, drew no attention, answered no questions. But the road itself decided. The causeway narrowed at the next bridge to a single plank width, and behind him came more carts, more voices, and a soldier of the provincial guard on a mud-splashed pony who waved everyone forward with the bored authority of a man herding geese. There was no leaving the caravan. There was only being in it.

He fell in beside a cart piled with looms—disassembled weaving frames, their wooden arms wrapped in sacking—and a woman walking at its shoulder with a sleeping child against her hip and another child, maybe six years old, holding the hem of her skirt with one fist and a carved wooden horse with the other.

"You'll want to walk on the planks," she said, without looking at him. "The ditch water's got a fever in it. My mother took ill from less."

"Thank you." He stepped up onto the bridge beside her. The plank flexed; below, black water held the sky's gray reflection and nothing else.

"Where are you bound?" she asked.

"The second city," he said. "On business. Papers for a firm."

"Everyone says papers." She smiled without much humor. "I'm Halina Brandt. This is Marta"—the sleeping child—"and this is Jozef, who has not put down that horse in three days, so if we drown, the horse drowns with us."

Jozef looked up at Lena with the flat, serious appraisal children give strangers, then offered the wooden horse for inspection. It was good work, worn smooth at the neck by years of small hands.

"It's a strong horse," Lena said gravely.

"He knows," Jozef said, and took it back.

* * *

By noon Lena understood the shape of the caravan: some forty households strung along two miles of causeway, bound for the second city or the towns beyond it, carrying whatever the census commissioners had let them keep. They had organized themselves the way water organizes—a head, a middle, a straggling tail—and they had done it without anyone appointing anything, which struck Lena as its own kind of mapmaking: reading terrain, finding the safe line, marking it with feet.

Halina's household turned out to be four carts and eleven souls, three generations of weavers from a parish called Ostrava Bend whose looms were worth more than their house had been. Her father, old Tomas Brandt, rode the lead cart and directed traffic with a stick like an admiral on his quarterdeck. Her brother Piotr walked the wheels with grease. And at the rear rolled the family's true fortune, wrapped in oilcloth under sacking: forty bolts of unfinished cloth, dyed but uncut, smuggled past the commissioners because the census forms had asked about persons and livestock and had no column for cloth.

"Everything has a column now," Halina said, when she told him this. "You're either in one or you're between them. Between the columns is where they take you."

Lena kept his face still and thought of the letter strapped flat against his chest beneath his shirt, sewn there by Mirela's needle into the lining of his coat—no, not the coat; the coat was the decoy story, the atlas plate in his satchel was the decoy object, and the letter itself rode against his skin where no clerk would look unless he made them. He thought of columns. He was between every column there was: no travel writ under his own name, no surname on his forged pass that any registrar would believe, a face the empire filed nowhere at all.

And yet he was walking freely. That was the thing that sat in his stomach like swallowed stone as the afternoon wore on.

At the second major crossing, where the causeway met the toll bridge over the Slowen channel, the caravan stopped and queued, and Lena saw what he had somehow not let himself see all morning: the checkpoint.

It was nothing. A plank hut, a brazier, two guards and a table, a clerk with a ledger and a stamp. Compared to the river gate at Vellmark—compared to what waited for him further south—it was a child's drawing of a checkpoint. But the queue before it moved like a queue moves when fear is in it: slowly, quietly, each family stepping forward as though approaching a surgeon.

He watched the Brandts go through. Old Tomas presented the household register, which the clerk read twice, frowning. Halina presented her marriage lines and the children's baptismal records. Piotr presented his guild card, and the clerk held it up to the light as though checking for counterfeits, and Piotr stood very straight and sweated through his cap while the guards looked into the cart with their sticks, prodding the sacking over the cloth bolts, and found nothing, and stamped the register, and waved them on. Eleven minutes. Lena counted. Eleven minutes for a family to prove it existed.

Then it was his turn, and he stepped up with Mirela's forgery in his hand and felt the shame arrive before the clerk even spoke.

The papers were beautiful. That was the whole problem. Mirela had made them on the Alvar press from plates she'd cut herself, and they were better than real—not just accurate but *confident*, the watermark woven in, the registrar's signature aged with tea and iron-gall, the route stamps layered exactly as a genuine courier's would be after weeks of legitimate travel. Beside them, the Brandts' documents were parish originals: handwritten, water-spotted, corrected in margins, the honest messy papers of people who had lived in one place their whole lives until the empire decided their lives needed proving.

The clerk glanced at Lena's pass for three seconds. Three seconds against eleven minutes. He didn't check the watermark. He didn't look up. He stamped the margin and said, "Courier, is it? Step through," and Lena walked past the brazier onto the far side of the channel feeling as though he'd stolen something from the queue behind him—which, he supposed, in a way he had.

* * *

That evening the caravan camped on the raised ground of an old salt-works, a shelf of dry land above the marsh with ruined evaporation pans terracing down toward the water. Fires bloomed among the carts. Someone had brought a fiddle. The smell of onions and smoke replaced the smell of sulfur and rot, and for an hour the marsh almost forgave itself for being a marsh.

Lena ate at the Brandt fire because Halina refused to let him eat alone. "A man who walks alone eats alone and thinks too much," she said, handing him a bowl of barley and onion, "and thinking too much on this road gets you lost." Old Tomas interrogated him mildly—what firm, what master, what goods—and Lena gave the answers he'd rehearsed, the master's jargon flowing off his tongue: bearing surveys, plate corrections, the courier rates per sealed folio. The old man nodded along, satisfied, and Lena hated how easily it worked. Hated that the same fluency that had carried him past the clerk at the bridge had been given to him by a dead man who'd trusted him with truth, and here it was, spending smoothly on lies.

Afterward Jozef fell asleep against Lena's arm without ceremony, mid-sentence, the wooden horse still in his fist, and Halina lifted the boy away and said, "He doesn't do that for everyone," and Lena felt something crack open in his chest that he had no time for.

He walked out to the edge of the salt-works after the fires burned low, where the terraces stepped down into black water and the fog had lifted enough to show stars doubled in the flooded pans—one sky above, one below, the whole world mapped twice. He checked the letter's stitches by feel, an habit now, fingertips reading the seam of his coat lining like braille. Still there. Still flat. Still a family's life folded against his ribs.

Behind him, voices. Halina came out with a shawl over her head and stood beside him looking at the doubled stars.

"You watched us at the bridge," she said.

"I'm sorry?"

"At the checkpoint. You counted something. I saw your lips move." She wasn't accusing him; she was simply a woman who noticed things, which was worse. "Eleven minutes for my father's register. How long for yours?"

"Less."

"How much less?"

"A few seconds."

She nodded slowly, and the wind moved the reeds below them, and she said, "My husband had papers once. Real ones. Guild papers, second class. The commissioner in Ostrava Bend took them last winter because our name was on a list he wouldn't show us. He held them in his hand and said, 'These are irregular,' and kept them, and wrote us a new register with a red mark in the corner. You know what the red mark means?"

"No."

"Neither do we. Nobody will say. That's the trick of it—you can be condemned by a color." She pulled the shawl tighter. "So when I see a young man whose papers open gates in three seconds, I don't think, *there goes a lucky one*. I think, *someone made those papers*. And whoever makes papers that good must have a reason, and reasons like that are heavy things to carry."

Lena said nothing. The letter lay against his chest like a second heartbeat.

"I'm not asking," Halina said. "On this road you don't ask. You share fire, you share water, you don't ask." She turned to go back to the fires, then paused. "But if you're carrying something heavy, walk in the middle of the caravan tomorrow. Not the front—the front meets the soldiers first, and not the tail—the tail gets picked off by the scavengers and the suspicion. The middle. That's where families put the things they mean to survive."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn in the gray half-light, and Lena took Halina's advice without admitting he was taking it: he walked in the middle, between the loom cart and a family of fisherfolk whose boat, disassembled, rode stacked on a wagon like the skeleton of some great animal. The road bent south along the channel, and the second city was two days away, and the tribunal convened in six, and every chime of every village church tower they passed seemed to fall on his spine like a small hammer.

Near midday the caravan halted at the crest of a long embankment, and word passed forward from the tail, cart to cart, mouth to mouth, the way bad news travels when it outruns its messengers: soldiers on the road ahead. Not provincial guards—imperial infantry, a picket, turning back travelers and inspecting carts with a thoroughness the toll bridge hadn't attempted.

Old Tomas came puffing along the line of wagons, stick in hand, and stopped at the loom cart, and his eyes went around the family circle and landed on Lena last, and stayed.

"They're checking for a courier," the old man said quietly. "A boy traveling alone with documents. There's a description going down the road ahead of us—somebody sold it at the last town, or somebody wants a promotion. Either way." He looked at Lena with the frank, tired gaze of a man who had buried a wife and outlived an empire's patience. "You've been good to my daughter's boy. So either you tell me what you're carrying, or you tell me nothing and take the middle position and let my grandsons walk on either side of you like they're part of our household. Those are the choices I see."

Lena looked down the embankment at the picket, at the bayonets catching the flat marsh light, at the travelers being turned around in the mud with their lives on their carts. He thought of Mirela saying, *they'll search the satchel, so the satchel lies*. He thought of the river gate, of Tomasz the debt-drowned guard waving him through, of complicity as survival. He thought of Jozef asleep against his arm.

"The satchel," he said. "There's an atlas plate in it. Survey work. If they open it, it should satisfy them—it's meant to satisfy them. What matters isn't in the satchel." He touched his chest, once, lightly. "What matters is here. And it stays here no matter what happens to me. If they take me, walk on. Don't stop. Don't look back. Promise me that, at least."

Old Tomas studied him for a long moment. Then he spat thoughtfully into the ditch.

"My grandsons will walk beside you," he said. "And nobody looks back. That's not a promise, boy—that's just how this family travels."
