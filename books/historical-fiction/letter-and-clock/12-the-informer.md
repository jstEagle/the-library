## 12. The Informer

The boy ate the way soldiers eat—fast, without ceremony, eyes moving between bites to count the exits. Reznik noticed that first, before anything else, and it troubled him more than all the rest.

He sat across the table in his own quarters at the river fort of Kessel's Landing, watching the courier work through a plate of salt pork and black bread, and told himself he was being hospitable. That was the official story he was building, brick by brick, in case it ever needed to hold weight: *I questioned the traveler over dinner because it was late and the road was bad and I am a man who does things properly.* The unofficial story—the one that lived behind his sternum like a swallowed coal—was different. The unofficial story was that he had known within four minutes of the boy walking through his gate that something was wrong, and that he intended to find out exactly what, and then decide what it was worth.

Reznik had been a captain for six years. Six years of garrison duty at a crossing nobody important crossed, of inspecting grain carts and stamping manifests and saluting men younger and better connected than himself as they passed up the ladder he could see from every window of this fort. He was forty-one years old. He had a wife in Vellmark who wrote him letters full of small grievances and no affection, and debts incurred by her brother that were now, by the arithmetic of marriage, his own. He had a commendation from eleven years ago that had grown yellow in its frame. And he had, tonight, sitting across from him eating his pork with suspicious competence, a courier who did not exist.

"You've been on the roads long?" Reznik asked, in the tone of a man making conversation.

"Three years." The boy—Lena, the papers said, a surveyor's courier out of Vellmark, twenty years old if the ink was honest—did not look up from the plate. "Mostly coastal runs."

"Coastal." Reznik poured wine into his own cup and not the boy's; a small discourtesy that would read as carelessness but wasn't. "Then you'd have worked out of the Vosk survey office. The old cartographer's shop."

Something happened in the boy's face. It was nothing—a stillness, a fractional pause, the kind of thing you'd miss if you hadn't spent six years watching smugglers and debt-runners and deserters try to be boring. But Reznik had spent those six years doing exactly that, and so he saw it: the half-second where the chewing stopped and resumed, where the eyes came up a shade too deliberately, where the whole animal recalibrated.

"I passed through there," the boy said. "Picked up work where I could."

"Shame, what happened to him. The old master." Reznik leaned back, letting the chair creak. "Drowned, they say. Fell off the breakwater drunk. A man who drew coastlines for thirty years, drowned in two feet of harbor water." He shook his head slowly. "The magistrate called it an accident. Very quick about it, I heard."

"Was he?" The boy's voice was flat, careful. "Quick?"

"Corvin? Corvin is always quick. It's his gift." Reznik smiled, showing teeth without warmth. "You knew the old man, though. Your hands say so."

Now the boy did look up fully, and Reznik got what he'd been fishing for: the direct gaze of someone deciding whether to fight or lie, and choosing neither yet. The hands themselves were ink-stained—not the casual smudge of a clerk but the deep, ground-in staining along the right index finger and thumb, the callus ridge of a man who held a pen the way other men held knives. Surveyors' couriers carried finished documents. They did not make them.

"My father was a draftsman," the boy said. "I picked up the habits."

"In Vellmark."

"In Vellmark."

"And your papers are drawn up under whose authority? Because I'll tell you frankly—" Reznik tapped the travel warrant lying between them on the table, the one bearing the impress of the Vellmark survey office seal— "this is good work. Very good. The paper stock is right, the registration number follows the current sequence. Almost too current. That sequence changed eleven days ago, when the office was seized by imperial order, and the new warrants went out under military countersign." He let that sit. "Yours doesn't have one. Which means either it was issued before the eleventh—which would make you very fast on the road—or it was never issued at all."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the shuttered window. Outside, the river moved in the dark with its low endless mutter, and somewhere on the water a barge bell rang twice, lazy and far. Reznik watched the boy across the table and felt the familiar cold pleasure rising in him, the one that came whenever he held a thread that, pulled correctly, might unravel into something worth money or rank. He had felt it perhaps five times in his career. Four times the thread had broken. The fifth had been a grain-smuggler, and the reward for turning him in had been forty crowns and a letter that no one above major ever read.

But this thread felt different. This thread had weight.

"I'm a courier," the boy said quietly. "I carry maps. If my warrant's out of date, that's the survey office's failure, not mine. You can send word to Vellmark and confirm me."

"Oh, I could." Reznik stood, collected the wine bottle, walked with it to the window as if considering the night. Through the shutters' gap he could see the checkpoint yard below, torchlit, the sentries stamping against the cold, the queue of tomorrow morning already forming—a caravan of displaced families camped beyond the gate, waiting for dawn inspection. He could also see, if he chose to look inward, the shape of the decision forming in him, and he looked at it the way a man looks at a card he hasn't turned over yet.

"Here's my difficulty," he said, conversational. "Tomorrow morning I'm due to forward a report to the third checkpoint at Greyford. Standard traffic—caravan counts, manifest summaries, weather. One line more or less, nobody reads those reports closely." He turned. "And here's *your* difficulty. Because if I write down that a young courier with ink-stained hands and a too-perfect warrant passed through Kessel's Landing claiming association with a murdered mapmaker whose workshop burned three days ago—if I write that, and it turns out to be nothing, I've wasted a colonel's attention and made myself a fool. But if I don't write it, and it turns out to be *something*..." He let the sentence die the way officers do, letting silence do the arithmetic.

The boy had gone very still. Not frightened-still—Reznik had seen frightened often enough, and this was its opposite. This was the stillness of a man measuring distances.

"What do you want," the boy said. Not *what will you do*. What do you want. As if they were merchants over a scale.

And Reznik, who had prepared three versions of this conversation and discarded all of them in the last minute because none of them accounted for being *seen* this clearly, felt something shift in his chest—discomfort, almost shame, quickly buried. "Nothing tonight," he said. "Eat. Sleep in the barracks room. In the morning you'll show me your cargo—all of it, every plate—and if it's maps, it's maps, and you'll pass through with my compliments. If it isn't..." He set the bottle down. "Then we'll discuss what a captain's discretion costs these days."

* * *

He did not sleep well. That surprised him.

He had expected to sleep the fat, satisfied sleep of a man holding leverage. Instead he lay in his narrow bed listening to the river and turning the evening over, and the more he turned it, the less he liked what his own hands had been doing.

It wasn't the extortion itself that bothered him—he'd long since stopped pretending the army ran on anything cleaner than favors and fear. It was the boy's face when Reznik mentioned the master's drowning. Reznik had spent twenty years learning to read faces across interrogation tables, and what he'd seen there was not the guilt of a conspirator. It was grief. Fresh, badly bandaged, bleeding through at the seams. Grief and fury packed down under discipline like powder under a ramrod, and Reznik knew—with the certainty that comes from having once been young and furious himself—that he was not looking at a smuggler. Smugglers ran from trouble. This boy was carrying it toward somewhere, deliberately, the way a man carries fire in a wind.

Which meant the boy might be exactly what he appeared: a loyal servant of a dead man, hauling legitimate survey work to some provincial client. Or he might be carrying the thing Corvin wanted buried. The workshop seizure order had come through Kessel's Landing ten days ago with an unusual annotation—*all materials and persons*—and unusual annotations meant someone powerful wanted something gone thoroughly. Reznik had filed the order and thought no more about it. Now he thought about it constantly.

If the boy was innocent, reporting him was cruelty and risked nothing. If the boy was guilty, reporting him might be the single most valuable intelligence Reznik had ever handled—and handled wrongly, it would be the last intelligence he ever handled, because men like Corvin did not reward the people who found their buried things. They rewarded the people who helped keep them buried, and they silenced the rest.

There was the coal at the center of it, the thing he kept circling: information about corruption was only safe currency if you sold it *to* the corrupt. To sell it against them you needed protection, and protection was bought with proof, and proof was back in Vellmark, in ashes, in the mouth of a dead man.

So: the third checkpoint. Greyford. The garrison commander there was Major Havel, a careerist of the worst and most useful kind—vain, cautious, hungry for notice from above. If Reznik sent Havel a quiet description of the courier, flagged but unconfirmed, he accomplished three things at once. First, he established priority: if the boy proved to be something, Reznik had been first, and the record would show it. Second, he bought insurance: if the boy was dangerous and slipped past, it would be Havel's failure, not his own. Third—and this was the move he admired most in himself, in the grim way one admires a scar—it cost him nothing. He reported suspicion, not fact. Suspicion was free. Only facts had prices.

He rose at the fourth bell, dressed, and sat at his desk with the lamp turned low. The report form was standard: *Traffic summary, Kessel's Landing, for onward relay.* He wrote the caravan numbers, the grain counts, the note about flooded fords below the mill. Then he paused, pen lifted, and wrote:

*One item of possible interest. Single male traveler, approx. 20 years, passing as survey courier out of Vellmark, name given as Lena. Papers competent but lacking current military countersign. Manner and hands suggest training in drafting rather than conveyance. Possible connection to the Vosk survey office matter (see seizure order ref. 44-C). Have detained overnight pending inspection of cargo; traveler proceeds east on the Greyford road at first light. Recommend routine but thorough examination. No grounds for detention beyond transit—advisory only.*

*Advisory only.* Three words that would let Havel feel clever for acting on them, and let Reznik deny any instruction if it all went wrong. He read it twice. Every clause was true. That was the craft of it—that was the whole art of surviving inside an empire: never write a false word, only arrange true ones into a shape that kills.

He sealed it, stamped it with his own hand rather than waking the clerk, and took it down to the signal post himself, where the relay rider stood saddled in the grey pre-dawn. "Greyford," Reznik said. "Direct to the major. Don't stop at the relay office."

The rider took the packet, saluted, and was gone, hoofbeats fading down the river road into the mist. Reznik stood a moment in the cold, watching the place where the road disappeared, and felt the coal behind his sternum settle—not extinguished, just banked, waiting.

* * *

At first light he inspected the boy's cargo himself, alone, in the guardroom.

The atlas plates were beautiful. Reznik was no artist, but he knew enough to know that what lay spread on the trestle table was not the work of a provincial hack—the coastlines breathed, the hachured hills had shadow in them, the lettering was done with a steadiness Reznik had only seen in church manuscripts. He turned each plate over with deliberate slowness, feeling the backing cloth, pressing the margins, looking for the thickness of hidden paper, the lump of folded vellum, the seam that didn't belong. The boy watched him do it with his arms crossed and his face arranged into boredom, and Reznik gave him credit: it was excellent boredom, nearly perfect, marred only by the pulse visible at the throat.

The plates were clean. Of course they were clean. Reznik had understood by midnight that they would be.

"Well," he said, stacking them. "Maps."

"Maps," the boy agreed.

"You'll want to make Greyford by dark, if the fords are passable. There's a caravan leaving within the hour—displaced families, going your direction. You might fall in with them." He said it mildly, watching. "Company makes the road shorter. Also makes a lone traveler less interesting to bored soldiers."

For the first time, something flickered across the boy's face that wasn't performance—something quick and complicated, guilt maybe, at the idea of traveling comfortably among people who couldn't. Reznik catalogued it and added it to the file building in his head. Innocent men didn't look guilty around refugees. Guilty men didn't look guilty at all; that was the point of them.

"I'll manage," the boy said.

"I'm sure you will." Reznik handed back the warrant, and their fingers met briefly on the paper, and he held it one second longer than necessary—a touch, a message, a hook set gently in flesh. "Kessel's Landing remembers its travelers, courier. Safe roads."

He watched from the gate as the boy shouldered his satchel and joined the stream of the departing caravan, a slight figure with ink-stained hands folding into a crowd of the dispossessed, moving east toward Greyford and whatever waited there. Toward the net Reznik had cast ahead of him, thin and deniable and patient.

His clerk brought the morning dispatches. Reznik signed them without reading. In his quarters, above the desk, the yellowed commendation caught the early light, and he looked at it for a long moment—eleven years old, worthless, honest—before drawing the curtain against it.

One line in his head kept repeating as he worked, unwelcome as a stone in a boot: *he looked at you the way you look at the ladder.* Like a man counting rungs. Like a man who believed, despite everything, that climbing was still possible.

Reznik crushed the thought, stamped the last document, and began composing, in his mind, the second letter he would send to Greyford—the one he'd write the moment he learned what the boy really carried, the letter that would be worth a major's star, a colonel's favor, maybe more.

All he needed now was for the boy to be guilty.

He told himself he didn't care which way it fell. He was still telling himself that when the caravan passed out of sight down the eastern road, and the river went on saying what rivers say, which is that everything carried downstream arrives somewhere eventually.
