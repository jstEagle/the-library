## 13. Stolen Hours

The chapel had no roof worth the name. Rain came through the broken vault in threads, silver in the last light, and pooled in the hollows of a floor where pilgrims had once knelt and soldiers now slept on their way to nowhere. Lena chose the corner behind the collapsed altar, where the stones of the apse still stood to shoulder height and the dark was deep enough to be trusted.

He had walked out of the marsh that afternoon with wet boots and a dry heart, which was more than he'd expected of either. The caravan had gone on toward the ferry crossing without him—he'd told them he was meeting an uncle at the salt works, another lie laid down like a paving stone—and now the silence was so complete he could hear his own blood moving.

He built no fire. Fire was a confession. Instead he ate the rest of the bread Mirela had pressed into his satchel at Salt Lane, hard as slate now, softened by chewing, and unrolled the oilcloth from his back.

The plates caught what little light remained through the broken clerestory: forty-one sheets of heavy paper, each one a region of the coast rendered in the master's hand—Aurel Vess's hand, steady even in its last months, even when the tremor had begun to show in the fine hatching of the northern cliffs. Lena knew every plate by touch by now. He could have found the Bay of Serrels blindfolded by the way the ink sat slightly heavier where Vess had corrected himself, scraping away a wrong river and drawing the true one beside it, leaving both ghosts beneath the surface like drowned men.

Plate forty-two was blank.

It had been blank since Vess died. The atlas's final plate—the southern reach, the coastline from Cape Merren down to the border marshes—was the one the master had never lived to finish. And it was the one whose backing had held the letter, sewn flat against the paper with surgeon's thread until Lena's fingers found the ridge of it three nights after the funeral, while imperial notices bloomed on the workshop door like white flowers.

Lena had copied the letter into memory, then given the original to the Alvars. The plate itself had come south with him stripped bare, its map-face empty, waiting for a hand that wasn't there anymore.

He set it on his knees and looked at it for a long time.

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere far off, a dog barked twice and stopped, and the silence after it felt like a held breath.

*You could leave it blank,* he thought. *Blank plates pass inspection. Blank plates don't hang anyone.*

But the atlas would be incomplete. Worse—it would be a lie. An atlas that stopped at Cape Merren told the world the coast simply ceased, that the marsh country and the fishing villages beyond it did not exist, that the people who dried their nets there and buried their dead above the tideline were not worth the ink. Vess had spent eleven years proving that every mile of shoreline deserved to exist on paper. Leaving the last plate empty would undo him more thoroughly than the magistrate's fire.

And there was another thing. A smaller, harder thing.

Lena had not drawn a line of his own since he was twelve years old. Everything since—every river, every contour, every careful lettered name—had been made under the master's eye, corrected by the master's pen, signed at the bottom margin with the master's initials and the workshop mark, the small crowned compass rose that said *Vess & Apprentice* without ever saying whose apprentice. Passing invisible meant passing unsigned. For nine years his work had existed only inside another man's name, the way his face existed only in the space between other men's glances, noticed by no one, claimed by no one, safe because it was nothing.

He took out his pens. He took out the ink, wrapped in wool against the cold, and the straightedge, and the dividers whose brass legs Vess had worn smooth with thirty years of thumb.

The rain gave him light enough to fail by, if not enough to succeed. That was all right. Vess used to say a surveyor who needed perfect light had never learned to see.

* * *

He began where the master would have begun: not with the line but with the soundings.

From memory. That was the whole audacity of it—from memory alone, because he had never surveyed the southern reach, had only read the master's field notes, studied his rough sketches, listened to him talk through the coast in the evenings the way some men recite scripture. *The marsh gives you nothing straight,* Vess had said once, drawing smoke from his pipe and a line in the air with it. *Everything bends around the tide. So you don't fight the bend. You let the pen go where the water goes, and you put your faith in the soundings, not the shore.*

Lena closed his eyes and brought up the numbers. He had copied them all once, in the margins of his own practice sheets, the way a boy copies anything he loves: depth figures off the master's notes, fathoms at half-mile stations, the shoal off Merren village marked with a cautionary dot-and-circle, the channel through the marsh that shifted every spring and that Vess had measured three times across two years because he refused to print a lie just because it was a convenient one.

He opened his eyes and began to plot.

The first hour was fear. His hand shook; his points wandered; he scraped away two false starts before they were old enough to embarrass him. The chapel dark pressed close. Twice he heard something move in the nave—a bird resettling in the rafters, nothing more—but both times his heart slammed as though the magistrate himself had stepped over the fallen altar with a lantern in his fist.

The second hour was different. Somewhere in the plotting of the third station, the fear burned off like morning fog, and what replaced it was the oldest feeling he knew: the feeling of the world agreeing to hold still.

That was the secret no one outside the trade understood. Drawing a map was not making something up. It was asking the world to be exactly what it was, in one place, for one moment—to consent, just this once, to being known—and then catching it. Every line was an act of attention so complete that the self dissolved inside it. For years Lena had hidden inside that dissolution. Tonight, for the first time, he understood there was a difference between hiding and dissolving, and that he had been doing the first and calling it the second.

The coastline came. Not Vess's coastline—Lena could feel that, could feel where his own hand chose differently. Where the master's line had been economical, almost austere, cutting a cliff down to its essential gesture, Lena found himself lingering on detail: the little cove below Merren where the boats wintered, the split rock at the harbor mouth that Vess's notes called *the teeth*, the slow accordion fold of the marsh channels opening toward the sea. He drew them all. He let the pen go where the water went.

When he reached the teeth, his eyes stung, and he understood it was not grief, or not only grief. It was the strange pain of becoming visible to yourself.

* * *

Near midnight the rain stopped. Clouds tore open in the east and dropped a wedge of moonlight through the broken vault, and it fell—Lena looked up and could hardly breathe—directly onto the plate on his knees, as if the ruined chapel had been designed four hundred years ago for precisely this one purpose.

He laughed, once, quietly, at the extravagance of it. Then he bent back to work, because the light was good and good light was a debt you paid immediately.

The moon moved. He worked in its path, tilting the plate, chasing the silver across the stone floor like a man following a candle down a corridor. By the time the clouds closed again he had the whole outer coast committed: headlands, soundings, the cautionary marks, the channel rendered in the dashed convention Vess had invented himself and that no other cartographer in the empire used yet, because no other cartographer had bothered to map shifting ground honestly.

What remained was the naming.

Vess had named features sparingly, in a small italic hand, always the local names when he could learn them—*the fishermen call it this*, he'd write in the margin notes, *and the fishermen are the only ones who sail it*. Lena had spent years learning that hand, copying it, being corrected in it. Now he wrote the labels in it again, and they came out right, and that frightened him more than any checkpoint had. Because the hand was right but the man writing it was not Vess, and somewhere in the last few miles of line the distinction had become undeniable. This was apprenticeship perfected into authorship. The style had passed to him the way a lamp passes flame: same fire, new wick.

At the bottom of the plate, below the scale bar, below the date, there was the place where the signature went.

Every finished plate bore it: *A. Vess*, and beneath it the crowned compass rose, and beneath that the words *Vellmark Survey, by commission*. Forty-one times. On the forty-second plate the space waited, blank as everything else had been blank, patient as everything else had been patient.

Lena set his pen down and picked it up three times.

If he signed Vess's name—if he completed the master's signature the way he'd completed the master's lines—it would be safer. It would make the atlas seamless, whole, one continuous act by one continuous hand. It might even honor the dead. And if the atlas were ever seized, a forgery of a dead man's signature would hang no one, because the dead man's enemies already believed the atlas was his.

But that was the arithmetic of hiding, and he was done with that arithmetic. Hiding had kept him alive for nine years and had cost him nine years. The magistrate had killed Vess to bury the truth, and had buried along with it every name connected to it, and every day Lena spent unnamed was a day the magistrate won by default.

There was also the letter to think of. The letter that named the Alvars. The letter that would, if everything went rightly in the next eight days, put their fate and his in the hands of Judge Halloran. If the atlas surfaced alongside it—as Mirela intended, as the press intended—an anonymous atlas was contraband. But a *signed* atlas was testimony. Signed work declared itself. Signed work could stand in court.

His hand was steady. That surprised him. His heart was going like a drum at a hanging and his hand lay on the plate calm as a sleeping animal.

He wrote the *L* first.

Then the rest: **Lena Okonkwo**. The letters of his mother's language sitting inside an imperial cartouche, the name he had not written anywhere since the day he arrived at the workshop at twelve years old and Vess had looked at him and said, *In this room you will be called the apprentice, and that is all anyone needs to know, and one day, if we do our work well enough, everyone will need to know more.*

Below the name, he did not copy the crowned compass. He drew a new mark instead: a plain compass rose, uncrowned, its needle pointing not north but east—toward the sea road, toward the marsh country, toward the coast that had never been mapped by anyone official because no one official cared who lived there. Beneath it, in the small italic hand he had inherited: *Completed from the field notes of Aurel Vess, and from memory, by his apprentice.*

Not *by commission*. There was no commission left. The commission had been seized and burned with the workshop.

*By memory.* He looked at those two words for a long time. They were the humblest words on the plate and the most dangerous. Memory was not admissible evidence; memory proved nothing to a tribunal; and yet memory was the only archive the empire could not confiscate. They had burned the workshop. They had not burned him.

Rain started again, softer, a drizzle sifting through the broken vault like flour through a sieve. Lena packed the ink, wrapped the pens, rolled the forty-two plates back into the oilcloth with the care of a priest handling relics—which was, he supposed, exactly what they were. The letter's twin rode against his chest, sewn into his coat lining where Mirela's needle had hidden it, and the decoy atlas in its false binding lay at the bottom of his satchel, ready to be surrendered at the third checkpoint if it came to that. If it came to that. Reznik's dinner questions had followed him out of the captain's camp like a smell he couldn't wash off. The man had known Vess. The man had watched Lena's hands while he talked, the way a man watches hands that might be lying.

Let them watch. The hands had a name in them now.

He settled into the corner behind the altar, the oilcloth under his head, his coat buttoned over the letter, and let the exhaustion arrive all at once, the way it does when the body finally believes the mind has stopped. Sleep came fast and dreamless, and if a dream had come, he thought in the last grey moment before it took him, it would have shown the southern coast, complete at last, every mile of it existing openly on paper, and a boy standing on the shore of it with his name in his mouth like a lit lantern.

* * *

He woke to hoofbeats.

Not near—half a mile off, maybe less, coming along the marsh track from the west, the direction of the ferry landing. Two horses, maybe three, moving at a walk, the deliberate unhurried pace of men searching rather than riding.

Lena was on his feet with the oilcloth over his shoulder before his mind fully assembled the thought. Through the empty window arches the dawn showed grey and featureless; the fog off the marsh had come inland overnight and swallowed the world past twenty paces. Good cover. Bad omen. Soldiers loved fog the way fishermen loved high tide.

He gathered the satchel, checked the coat lining with two fingers—the letter's small rectangular stiffness, reassuring as a heartbeat—and slipped out through the collapsed eastern wall, low and quiet, into the churchyard where the leaning grave markers wore collars of moss.

The hoofbeats stopped.

Somewhere behind the fog, a voice—too far to catch the words, close enough to catch the tone: the flat, procedural boredom of a checkpoint patrol working a list. Checking the chapel. Of course checking the chapel. The ruin was the only shelter on six miles of marsh track, and patrols knew it, and travelers knew they knew it, which meant the patrol would find cold ash if he'd lit a fire, or worse, nothing at all if he'd been careful—and careful, today, was its own kind of signature.

Lena moved along the churchyard wall, bent double, boots finding the tussocks without sound. At the corner where the wall met the drainage ditch, he paused and looked back once at the chapel he'd slept in, at the broken vault where the moonlight had come through, at the floor where a coastline now existed that had not existed yesterday, signed by a man the empire did not know it had lost yet.

They would search the ruin and find nothing, and ride on. Or they would find the faint ghost-lines of scraped corrections on the flagstones—no, he'd worked on his knees, the plate had never touched the floor. Nothing. They would find nothing.

Except the patrol was not riding on. The voices had stopped moving entirely, and now, threading through the fog, he heard the one sound he had dreaded since Salt Lane, the sound that meant the net had tightened another knot:

A horse whickering, and a man saying, clearly, in the bored voice of routine: "Fresh tracks. In the mud, here—someone's gone along the ditch."

Lena pulled his collar up, turned east into the fog, and ran.
