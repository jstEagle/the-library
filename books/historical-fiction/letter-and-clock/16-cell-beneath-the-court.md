## 16. Cell Beneath the Court

They took the atlas from him at the door.

Lena had expected it—had counted on it—and still his hands shook when the sergeant pried the boards loose and stacked them on a trestle table like firewood, like nothing, like twenty years of a dead man's life reduced to kindling. The decoy plate went with them: the coastline he'd copied in Mirela's workshop, the false annotations in her careful hand, all of it built to be found and to satisfy whoever found it. A soldier flipped through it with a thumb greasy from his supper.

"Surveys," the sergeant said, not asking.

"Imperial coastal surveys," Lena said. "Courier for the Office of Works."

"Office of Works doesn't send boys."

"The Office of Works sends whoever can carry forty pounds of plates through marsh without dropping them." He kept his voice flat, bored, the voice of a man whose only fear was of being delayed. "I have a delivery schedule. If I miss the morning tide at Halden—"

"You're not catching any tide." The sergeant jerked his chin toward the stair. "Down. Magistrate wants everyone who came off that ferry held until tribunal convenes. You'll get your papers back if you're clean. If you're not—" He shrugged, and the shrug said everything Vellmark's courts said these days: *we will decide what you are, and then we will punish you for it.*

The cell was beneath the courthouse, which meant it had been a wine cellar once, before some magistrate three generations back decided that good stone vaults were wasted on bottles. The racks were still bolted to the walls; prisoners slept where casks had lain, in rows, on straw gone gray and wet. There were nine others down there when they put him in—a fishmonger with bound wrists, two brothers from the salt flats who spoke to no one, an old woman who sang hymns under her breath in a language Lena didn't know, a boy about his own age who wept steadily and silently, the way one weeps when one has learned that noise brings soldiers.

Lena took the far corner, under the smallest window—a slot of glass near the ceiling, thick as a fist, glowing with the last gray light of evening. He sat with his back to the wall and did what he had trained himself to do since the night the seizure notices went up on the workshop door: he took inventory.

Coat: present. They hadn't searched him properly—the sergeant had been interested in the atlas, and men interested in one thing are blind to everything else. Inside the coat lining, sewn flat against his left ribs by Mirela's own needle, lay the letter. Folded to the size of a prayer card. The magistrate Corvin's private seal pressed into wax no thicker than a fingernail. The names of an entire family. Nine days until tribunal; eight now, or seven by whatever counting the magistrates used, because their clocks never ran honest.

He breathed. In through the nose, out slow, the way Master Aldric had taught him—not for meditation, the old man had said, but for drafting hands. *A hand that trembles draws a line that lies.* His hand did not tremble. That was the one thing he could still trust.

* * *

The first night passed in fragments. He dozed against the wall and woke each time someone shifted on the straw, each time boots crossed the floor above—muffled thuds through the vaulting, the courthouse breathing its weight down onto them. Sometime past midnight the boy stopped weeping and began instead to whisper numbers, over and over, and Lena realized after a while that he was reciting debts. Sums owed. Interest accrued. A ledger learned by heart because paper got confiscated.

Toward dawn, the door at the top of the stairs opened.

Lena had expected soldiers. What came down instead was a woman carrying a lamp.

She was perhaps thirty, plainly dressed in the dark wool of court clerks, her hair pinned back with the severity of someone who had decided long ago not to be noticed. She moved along the row of prisoners with a slate in one hand, taking names, and the guards upstairs let her—called something down after her that might have been *don't be long*—and she answered with a nod that managed to be both obedient and entirely dismissive.

At the fishmonger she paused longer than the others. She wrote something on the slate, then tore a corner from a scrap of paper she drew from her sleeve, wrote again, folded it small, and pressed it into the man's bound hands with a motion so quick and so practiced it looked like nothing at all—a clerk adjusting a prisoner's grip while recording his name.

Lena watched her work down the row. By the time she reached him he had arranged himself into what he hoped read as exhaustion rather than calculation.

"Name?" she said.

"Lena." He gave no surname. "Courier."

"Courier for whom?"

"A surveyor. Dead now." He let that sit. "The Office of Works can confirm my papers were seized at the door. Along with an atlas."

Her pen didn't pause. "The atlas is already upstairs. I catalogued it myself this afternoon." Now she looked at him, and her eyes in the lamplight were the color of tea left too long—flat, patient, missing nothing. "Interesting piece. Coastlines mostly unfinished. Except one plate."

His heart kept its pace. He made it keep its pace. "Plates get damaged in transit."

"This one wasn't damaged. It was annotated in a hand that isn't yours, with survey marks around a village called Brast Hollow, where—I checked, I'm thorough—there has never been a survey." She closed the slate. "There is also a seam in the backing board that someone opened and restitched with thread a shade too fine for map cloth. Printer's thread. I grew up above a print shop; I know printer's thread."

The cellar was very quiet. The old woman's hymn had stopped. Even the boy's numbers had faltered.

"I don't know what you mean," Lena said.

"No," she agreed. "You wouldn't. Nobody ever does, at first." She glanced up at the door, then back, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into a register meant only for him. "My name is Sefa Halloran. I am clerk to Judge Andras Halloran, who sits the third bench of this court and who, unlike most of the men upstairs, reads what I put in front of him. Twice a week I come down here to record names for the tribunal rolls. Twice a week, messages leave this cellar inside those rolls. Fourteen prisoners' letters to family in the last year. Three petitions that reached the judge's desk. One confession of a poisoning that would otherwise have died in here with the man who made it." Her face did not change at all. "You are holding something that belongs in that count. Aren't you."

It was not a question, and Lena understood suddenly that refusing it would cost him more than answering—that she had already catalogued him the way she catalogued everything, and the only choice left was whether to be filed under *lost* or *delivered.*

Still, he made himself ask the only question that mattered. "Why? Why risk it for strangers?"

"Because I sat in this cellar for six days myself, four years ago, when Corvin's men arrested me for copying a petition they didn't like." She said it the way she might report a weather observation. "Someone carried word out for me. Someone I never met, whose name I never learned. I owe a debt to a stranger, and I pay my debts forward." She held out her hand, palm up. "Well, courier? Your surveyor is dead. Your atlas is upstairs. Whatever you've been keeping against your ribs since you walked through that door—you cannot carry it to the tribunal yourself. They'll search you before they seat you. They'll take it, and they'll burn it, and no one will ever know there was anything to burn."

Every argument he had prepared dissolved. She had simply walked straight through them, the way water finds the crack in a wall.

"How do I know it reaches him?" he asked. "Your judge."

"You don't." For the first time something moved behind her eyes—not warmth exactly, but an honesty that was its own kind of respect. "That's the whole of it. That's what trust costs down here. You give it away and you never learn whether it landed. I've sent fourteen messages and gotten confirmation of eleven. The other three I think about every night. If you need certainty, keep your letter and lose it tomorrow instead. If you need it *read*—" she turned her palm a little, waiting— "I'm the best chance it has. Better than you are. Better than anyone else in this building."

Lena looked at his hands. They were steady. Master Aldric's voice, somewhere under his ribs next to the letter itself: *a line is a promise, boy; draw it true.*

He worked the stitching at his lining with his thumbnail—Mirela had taught him that too, *always know how to undo your own hiding places*—and drew the letter out. Small. Warm from his body. The wax seal caught the lamplight, dark red, stamped with the crest Lena had memorized on the second night of his flight: a stag's head quartered with scales. The magistrate's private mark, pressed by the magistrate's own ring, on a document naming the magistrate's own crimes.

Sefa looked at it for a long moment. Then she did something unexpected: she did not take it.

"Recite it for me," she said. "Not the whole text. The seal, the address, the first line, and the last. So that if this letter disappears between us, I can testify to its contents from memory. Corvin can burn paper. He cannot burn what's in my head, and neither can you."

So Lena closed his eyes and gave it back the way he had carried it across eighty miles of marsh road, the way he had repeated it to himself in ruined chapels and ferry landings and the dark behind his eyelids: the stag quartered with scales; *To the House of Alvar, printers, Salt Lane, Vellmark, under the hand of the late Aldric Vesely, cartographer*; and the first line, in the master's formal, unhesitating script—*What follows was set down because the census lists are being written not to count the living but to mark the ones to be removed*—and the last line, the one he knew by heart the way he knew the shape of the northern coast: *Let the reader weigh this against the finding of accident upon my death, and act accordingly.*

When he opened his eyes, Sefa Halloran had gone very still.

"*Late* Aldric Vesely," she repeated softly. "So that's whose atlas it is." Something passed across her face—grief, recognition, anger, all compressed into a single blink. "Judge Halloran signed the accident ruling. Under protest—he told Corvin's clerk the evidence was thin, and was overruled. He has wondered for two months what he wasn't shown." She finally extended her hand again, and this time Lena laid the letter in it. "Now he'll be shown."

She folded the letter into a fold of her sleeve, into a pocket sewn where no pocket should be, and Lena understood he was looking at the machinery of a year's quiet smuggling—a craft as deliberate as mapmaking, hidden in plain wool.

"When does he see it?"

"Tribunal convenes in seven days. He reviews the rolls the night before. This goes in the rolls." She lifted the lamp and studied Lena's face as though adding him to some internal catalogue. "You understand what happens next, courier. If it works, the tribunal becomes something Corvin never planned: an inquiry. You'll be brought before it. They'll ask you what you know. Everything depends on whether you tell the truth in front of men paid to make truth expensive."

"And if it doesn't work?"

"Then in seven days I'm in a cell beside you, and the letter dies with both of us." She said it without drama, the way she'd said everything, and somehow that was the most frightening and most steadying thing she could have done. "Hope isn't a guarantee. It's a delivery. Sometimes it arrives. That's all."

She rose, smoothed her skirt, became again the unnoticed clerk. At the foot of the stairs she paused.

"One more thing. The name you withheld—give it to me. Not for the rolls. For the record I keep in my head, alongside the ones I owe."

Lena hesitated. He had given up his name once already, on the marsh road, trading *Okonkwo* for *Vesely's courier* because a name was a hook and hooks got pulled. But that had been concealment. This, he sensed, was something else—an entry in a ledger that might someday be read aloud.

"Okonkwo," he said. "Lena Okonkwo."

"Apprentice to Aldric Vesely," she confirmed, half to herself. "Good. Then the master's work has an heir, and heirs can inherit trials as easily as estates. Be ready, Okonkwo. Seven days."

The lamp climbed the stairs and shrank to a coin, a thread, a darkness. Above, the courthouse settled into its night sounds. Beside him the boy resumed his whispered sums. Out in the city, faint through the slot of glass, the clock tower struck three—all that remained of the nine days, falling away like coast eroding into sea.

Lena leaned his head back against cold stone and discovered that his body wanted, absurdly, to sleep—to actually sleep, deep and defenseless, for the first time since the workshop burned. The letter was gone from his ribs. The absence felt like a wound and like a splint at once. He had carried it so long that its weight had become part of his posture, and now that weight belonged to a stranger climbing stairs toward a judge's desk, and there was nothing left to do with his hands.

*A line is a promise,* he thought. *Somewhere tonight, another hand is drawing mine.*

He slept.

* * *

He woke to boots.

Not the measured tread of the night clerk—this was many boots, descending fast, and lantern light swung wild across the vaulting ahead of the men who carried it. Prisoners stirred and flinched along the straw. The sergeant from the door stood at the bottom of the stairs with a list in his fist and an expression that had curdled overnight from boredom into alarm.

"Roll call," he barked. "Everyone on your feet. Names out loud."

Lena rose with the others, heart hammering against the place where the letter used to be, mind running the same circuit: *discovered, traced, burned, burned.* But the sergeant was reading from a different list than the one Sefa had carried—fresh ink, official crests—and when he reached the bottom of it he looked up, scanning faces, and his voice dropped as if the walls themselves might repeat it.

"They're asking for the courier. The one with the atlas." His eyes found Lena and stayed there. "Magistrate Corvin's own clerk came before dawn with questions about you. Where you slept. Who you spoke to. Who came down these stairs last night."

Behind the sergeant, at the top of the steps, a figure in magistrate's colors was already descending—slowly, deliberately, into the lamplight—carrying in one gloved hand a single sheet of paper that Lena, even at this distance, recognized by its fold.

The size of a prayer card.
