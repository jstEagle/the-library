## 17. The Magistrate's Dinner

The candles were beeswax, imported, and Corvin had ordered them lit an hour early so the dining room would smell of money before his guests arrived. He stood at the window with a glass of Tokay he was not drinking, watching the last light fail over Vellmark's harbor, and permitted himself—for the first time in eleven days—the luxury of satisfaction.

The workshop on Rope Street was ash. He had seen the report twice: once as a summary dictated by the fire captain, once as a list of salvaged items that amounted to three scorched drafting stools, half a chest of tools, and nothing else worth the ink used to record it. The plates of Aldous Verrek's great atlas—the atlas that had taken the old man twenty-two years and would have shown the world precisely where the imperial surveys had been quietly falsified—were gone. Burned, or scattered, or sunk in the river by a panicking apprentice; it made no difference. A map that does not exist cannot contradict a census.

And the Alvars. Corvin turned from the window and crossed to the sideboard, where a silver tray held his correspondence in neat descending order of importance. At its bottom lay the tribunal schedule for Thursday, drafted in his own hand, reviewed by his own clerk. Mirela Alvar, her father Tomas, her brother Petre—all three named in the seizure order, all three charged under the Sedition Statute with possession of proscribed printing materials. The press itself had been confiscated from their cellar on Salt Lane two weeks ago, broken into components so thoroughly that even if some sentimental judge returned it, it would never run again.

Thursday they would stand before the tribunal. Thursday evening they would be sentenced. Deportation east, most likely—he had argued privately against hanging, not from mercy but from arithmetic. A hanged printer becomes a martyr with a name on every wall; a deported printer becomes a rumor that fades by spring. The Alvars would be put on a barge, the barge would be put on the river, and Vellmark would forget them the way it forgot everything, efficiently, within a season.

He heard the carriages in the square. Guests.

Corvin straightened his collar in the mirror above the mantel—a vain gesture, he knew, but one he allowed himself because he had earned the right to be vain. Forty-one years old, magistrate of the second-largest port in the province, invited next month to the capital to advise on the new census implementation. There had been a time when men like him were kept out of rooms like the ones he now hosted. His father had been a customs clerk who died owing money. Corvin had built himself with paper: petitions, reports, recommendations, each document a brick laid so smoothly that no one could point to the wall and say when it had been built. That was his genius, if he had one. He did not break laws. He wrote the versions of events that made laws unnecessary.

Tonight's guests were three. Colonel Brandt, imperial survey corps, passing through on inspection. Herr Vogel, the census commissioner's financial attaché, soft-spoken and dangerous, who wore his wealth the way other men wore uniforms. And Father Illes, not clergy at all despite the title, but the capital's quiet man—the one whose letters arrived without seals and whose visits were never recorded in any official log. It was Illes whose approval mattered. Brandt and Vogel were the empire's hands. Illes was closer to its memory.

They came in together, stamping off the harbor damp, and Corvin greeted each with the correct degree of warmth: handshake for Brandt, both hands for Vogel, a small bow for Illes, who acknowledged it with the faintest tilt of his head, as a man acknowledges a debt he intends to repay at his own convenience.

Dinner was pike from the mountain lakes, venison, a soup of white beans and smoked ham that Corvin's cook had learned from a tavern keeper in the marsh districts—an affectation of local flavor that Corvin included deliberately, so the imperials could feel they were tasting the province while being served above it. The wine was Hungarian. The conversation began, as it always began, with roads.

"The eastern marsh causeway," Brandt said, sawing at his venison. "Your surveyors have it marked passable year-round. I rode it Tuesday. In October. My horse went to the chest in muck."

"Then your horse read the wrong map, Colonel." Polite laughter around the table. "I'll have the section corrected."

But Corvin felt a small cold thread pull through him as he said it, because *corrected* was the whole of his life's method, and tonight he wanted nothing corrected, nothing moved, nothing amended. Tonight he wanted the world to hold still for nine more days until the tribunal was finished and the last loose names were tied off.

Vogel set down his spoon. "You've received the final figures for the district census?"

"Preliminary only. Full rolls by month's end."

"Commissioner Varga wants the port numbers first. There is talk—" Vogel paused, selecting the phrase the way a man selects a key, "—of accelerated scheduling. For certain populations. The marsh settlements, the river quarters. Persons of mixed registration."

*There it was.* The word beneath the word, served between courses. Corvin met Vogel's pale eyes and inclined his head fractionally, neither agreeing nor refusing, keeping his face the smooth surface it had always been. But inside, the cold thread pulled tighter. Accelerated scheduling. He had seen the draft orders six weeks ago and had signed the routing slips that made them administratively possible, and he had done it the way he did everything—cleanly, deniably, one paper removed from consequence. Deportations under cover of census. The master cartographer Aldous Verrek had found the falsifications in the boundary surveys, the ones that would let whole parishes be redrawn out of legal existence, and Verrek had died for it—had fallen, officially, from his own roof ladder on a wet night, an accident ruled upon by Magistrate Corvin himself, in his own courtroom, with his own signature.

Illes had said almost nothing all evening. He ate little, drank less, and listened with the particular attention of a man who has already read the ending of the book everyone else is discussing. Now he set down his glass and asked, mildly, about the atlas.

"The Verrek atlas. The provincial survey. I understand it was near completion?"

"It was destroyed," Corvin said. "An accident of fire. Unfortunate. The old man's workshop was a tinderbox—oil lamps, solvents, paper everywhere. An accident waiting decades to happen."

"Destroyed entirely?" Illes's voice remained pleasant, unhurried. "All the plates?"

"Everything of value was lost." Corvin smiled. "Why do you ask, Father? Surely the empire doesn't mourn one mapmaker's hobby."

"The empire mourns nothing." Illes turned his glass slowly by its stem. "It counts. A completed atlas of this coastline would be worth more than this entire dinner service. If even fragments survive—if a single plate reached the market in the south, or the archives of some foreign navy—it would be counted. Counted, and priced, and traced." He looked up. "Traced back to whoever failed to destroy it completely."

The candle flames leaned, though there was no draft. Somewhere below, in the kitchen, a pot lid clattered and was silenced.

"Nothing survived," Corvin said again, and heard, in his own ears, how the repetition sounded. Like a man insisting. He covered it with motion, refilling Illes's glass himself, leaning close enough to smell the man's plain soap. "I had the ruins sifted personally. I can show you the report."

"No need." Illes lifted the refilled glass but did not drink. "Reports are how mistakes become permanent, Magistrate. I prefer to hear things twice—from different mouths." He smiled then, a thin, courteous expression with nothing behind it. "Tell me about the apprentice."

The question landed like a dropped fork. Corvin kept his hands steady on the decanter. "Apprentice? Verrek kept a boy, a mixed-blood foundling, unregistered. He ran, presumably, when the fire started. Or burned with the building—we haven't recovered a body, but the river takes things." He shrugged. "A nonentity. Unrecorded, therefore invisible. The boy doesn't exist on any roll in this province, which means legally he never existed at all."

"How convenient," Illes said softly, "to work beside a man for years and remain invisible. One wonders what such a person sees. What such a person carries." He raised the glass at last and drank, and Corvin understood suddenly that the toast was not to anything at the table. "Well. No matter. Thursday will tidy matters, won't it? The printers. And after Thursday, Vellmark will be a clean page."

"Cleaner than clean," Corvin said.

Brandt launched into an anecdote about a garrison mutiny in the north; Vogel laughed at the correct moments; the meal resumed its rhythm. But Corvin had stopped tasting his food. *One wonders what such a person carries.* Illes knew something, or suspected something, or simply enjoyed loosening the ground beneath powerful men to watch which way they leaned. Corvin could not tell which, and not knowing was a sensation he had not experienced in fifteen years, and he found he despised it with a completeness that frightened him slightly.

The guests left at half past ten. Brandt first, loud and satisfied; Vogel second, pressing Corvin's hand a moment too long and murmuring, "Month's end, Magistrate. The Commissioner is watching this district specifically"; Illes last, declining the offered carriage, walking out into the drizzle alone like a man with nowhere in particular to be and every intention of arriving there.

Corvin dismissed his household staff and poured himself brandy in the study, standing rather than sitting, because sitting felt too much like settling. Through the tall window he could see the clock tower above the customs house, its face lit faintly by the moon behind cloud, and beneath it the dark bulk of the courthouse where Judge Halloran's chambers occupied the second floor, windows dark, the old man long since asleep or pretending to be.

Halloran. The one judge in Vellmark who had never taken Corvin's hospitality, never accepted his gifts, never laughed at his jokes in quite the right way. Old, ill, stubborn, harmless—so Corvin had catalogued him years ago and filed him away. Harmless men were useful; they gave the appearance of justice without the inconvenience of it. Halloran would preside at nothing important ever again, and everyone understood this, including Halloran. That was the arrangement. That was how cities worked.

The brandy was good. Outside, the rain thickened. Corvin let himself imagine Thursday: the courtroom, the Alvar family standing in their borrowed clothes, the reading of charges, the verdict already decided in the language of procedure. Then the barge. Then silence. Then the census rolls in November, and the accelerated schedules moving through the marsh quarters like water finding its level, and by spring Vellmark would be exactly what he had spent twelve years making it: a city with no contradictions in it. A city that matched its maps.

He was reaching for the bell to call for a second glass when the knock came.

His house steward, gray-faced, holding a lantern. "Beg pardon, Magistrate. There's a courier at the servants' door. From the courthouse. Says it's urgent—says Judge Halloran's clerk sent him personal."

"At this hour?"

"He wouldn't leave the message with me, sir. Said he was instructed to place it in your hand only."

Corvin took the folded slip of paper. Cheap stock, cheap ink, a single line in a clerk's careful hand:

*A document was delivered to Judge Halloran's chambers this evening. Sealed. The Judge has requested the tribunal be postponed pending his review. He reads tomorrow at noon.*

Corvin read it twice. The words did not change. Behind him the fire settled; outside, the clock tower began striking eleven, each note falling into the wet streets like stones into deep water.

Postponed. *Pending review.* Halloran requesting anything was unusual; Halloran requesting delay over a sealed document delivered by night, by hand, past every ordinary channel—that was not procedure. That was alarm. Someone had given the old man something worth alarming over, and someone had carried it through a city Corvin believed he had sewn shut.

The atlas. A plate. A letter—something from Verrek, something the old man had hidden before the fire, something that had traveled tonight across checkpoints and gates while Corvin dined on imported wine and called the world clean.

He thought of the apprentice. Unregistered. Invisible. *What such a person carries.*

"Magistrate?" the steward whispered. "Shall I send a reply?"

Corvin looked at the slip of paper in his hand, and for the first time since his father's funeral, since the years of hunger and paper and climbing, Magistrate Corvin felt the specific, physical sensation of a crack running through load-bearing stone—invisible from the street, perhaps, silent, but running, and running, and not stopping.

"No reply," he said quietly. "Wake my clerk. Wake Captain Reznik's garrison office. And find out"—he folded the message closed with deliberate care—"exactly who walked into the courthouse tonight, and what they looked like."

The clock tower struck the last of eleven, and the sound seemed very far away, as if the night itself were already withdrawing from him, carrying something toward noon.
