## 18. Tribunal

They came for him before the bells finished counting six.

Lena had slept perhaps two hours on the stone bench of the holding cell, his coat folded beneath his head—the coat with its sewn lining empty now, the letter gone into the hands of a clerk named Ilse who had promised nothing and looked at everything. When the keys rattled he was already awake, already standing, because a body that has spent three weeks learning to be seized learns to rise before the door opens.

Two guards. Not the river gate men, not checkpoint soldiers with their bored eyes. These wore the magistrate's livery, deep blue with silver thread, and they handled him like something already condemned.

"Name," said the first.

"You'll hear it in the hall," Lena said, and paid for it with a cuff across the ear that rang like a struck plate all the way up the stairs.

The tribunal met not in the courthouse but in the Assize Hall of the Customs House, where the ceiling was painted with the imperial arms and the windows were tall enough to remind everyone inside that the world outside still existed, though not for them. Lena had drawn this building once. Master Aldous Vey had made him measure its façade from a rowboat in the harbor because no surveyor's chain could be carried openly through the customs yard. He remembered the proportions even now—remembered that the pediment was four inches out of true on the eastern side—and the memory steadied him in the way that only numbers ever had.

The hall was fuller than he expected. Not a crowd—a selection. Magistrate Corvin sat elevated on the dais beneath the painted eagle, flanked by two assessors in gray and, further down, a row of clerks with pens already moving. Along one wall sat officers; along the other, a scattering of civilians whose presence had the quality of arrangement rather than accident. Witnesses, or the appearance of witnesses. A theater requires an audience, and Corvin had always understood theater. Lena had never seen the man except in engravings, and the engraving had softened him. In person his face was longer, colder, the mouth a thin instrument for pronouncing.

A table had been set in the center of the floor. One chair behind it. No counsel's chair beside it, because counsel implied a defense, and a defense implied doubt.

They put Lena in the chair and stepped back, and for a moment the whole room simply looked at him.

He knew what they saw. He had spent his life knowing what people saw: a young man of indeterminate origin, brown-skinned enough to draw second glances in Vellmark's better quarters, dressed in a dead man's coat too big at the shoulders, hands ink-stained past the wrist. A nobody. A courier, a spy, an instrument—anything but a person with a name worth speaking. He had built that invisibility plank by plank over twenty years, and now, sitting in the light of forty candles under a painted eagle, he felt it come apart around him like wet paper.

Good, he thought, surprising himself. Let it come apart.

"The prisoner will state his name," said the assessor on Corvin's left, a dry man with a voice like sand poured slowly.

Lena opened his mouth to lie. It was reflex, the oldest habit he owned—*Tomas Ferren, courier's apprentice, papers lost at the ferry.* The words were right there, worn smooth as river stones from use.

Instead he heard himself say: "My name is Lena Okonkwo."

The pen-scratch of the clerks stopped. Somewhere in the gallery a woman breathed in sharply. Corvin did not move, but something behind his eyes recalculated, the way a surveyor's needle swings when a mass of iron passes near it.

"That name appears on no document in our possession," the assessor said.

"No," Lena agreed. "It wouldn't."

"You are charged with espionage against the Crown, theft of sealed instruments, forgery of transit papers, and conspiracy with persons unknown to disseminate seditious material." The assessor read it without inflection, a list recited over a grave. "You may respond."

"I stole nothing," Lena said. "I forged nothing that wasn't already forged for me. And I conspired with no one except a murdered man."

The word *murdered* went through the hall like wind through wheat. Corvin's assessors exchanged a glance. One of the clerks began writing faster.

"The death of Aldous Vey," the assessor said carefully, "was ruled accidental."

"By you," Lena said, turning—not to the assessor but to the dais itself, to Corvin. He had planned this in the cell, in the dark, with his back against cold stone. Not the exact words; the direction. *Speak the master's name. Speak your own. Let them hear both.* "By this court. By this magistrate. Drowned at his own mooring on a night with no wind, with his ledger missing and his workshop watched for a week beforehand. Ruled accidental within two days, before the tide gave back half of what it took. I read the ruling. It is four sentences long. I have written longer descriptions of mudflats."

"Prisoner will address the bench—"

"He *is* addressing the bench," Lena said, and his voice cracked upward and he let it, let them hear that it was a young man speaking, a frightened one, and speaking anyway. "For eleven years I worked in that workshop. For eleven years I ground the ink and cut the plates and checked every sounding twice because Aldous Vey believed a map was a promise—that what you drew, you owed to whoever used it. He died protecting a set of copper plates, and your soldiers burned the building they were kept in, and now you sit under a painted eagle asking me my name as if names were contraband."

Corvin lifted one hand. The motion was small, almost gentle, and the hall quieted as if a valve had closed.

"Young man." The magistrate's voice was low and unhurried, the voice of a man who had never needed to raise it. "No one here doubts your grief. Grief makes men do reckless things—carry documents they don't understand, repeat accusations they've been fed. Tell us plainly: do you claim to possess a certain letter?"

And there it was. The trap laid flat in the open, so obvious it was nearly respectful. If Lena denied the letter, he was a harmless madman, easily disposed of, and whatever Ilse carried toward Halloran's chambers traveled without a witness to confirm it. If he admitted it, he confessed to the charge as written, and the tribunal could close around him like water.

But there was a third answer, and Mirela would have seen it in a heartbeat. She had taught him that ink hides best not in blankness but in abundance.

"I claim it exists," Lena said.

Silence. Even the clerks' pens paused.

"I have never seen it," he continued, and let a slow smile move across his face, because he could feel the room leaning toward him now, the officers and the clerks and the arranged audience, all of them wanting the next sentence. "Neither have you. That is precisely what it was designed to ensure. But I can tell you this much: it bears a seal this court will recognize. It names names. And when it is read—and it will be read, Magistrate, I would stake what remains of my life on that verb—no one in this hall will be able to pretend they were never told."

"You are confessing to possession of seditious correspondence."

"I am confessing that I know it exists. Whether possession can survive its own distribution—" he shrugged, the dead man's coat shifting on his shoulders— "is a question for a better lawyer than this room intends to provide me."

One of the military officers along the wall laughed, short and involuntary, and turned it into a cough. Corvin's gaze flicked to him and back with the smooth economy of a man moving a piece on a board.

"Then you leave us no choice but to treat you as what you appear to be," Corvin said. "An agent of unknown principals, carrying unknown instruments, found in possession of forged identity papers and stolen cartographic materials. The atlas plates recovered from your effects—"

"Recovered," Lena repeated. "Is that the word for what your men did at Salt Lane? Or at the ferry landing? I ask as a mapmaker. Precision matters. A coast is either surveyed or it isn't. A thing is either taken lawfully or it isn't. You should choose your verbs more carefully than your victims."

"Prisoner!" The assessor's sand-pour voice cracked at last.

But Lena had found the strange clear air above fear, the place he had touched once before, lying his way through the river gate with his heart hammering and his tongue steady. It was not courage exactly. It was the discovery that terror, fully inhabited, becomes a kind of fuel. Every eye in the hall was on him. For the first time in his life, being visible did not feel like danger.

It felt like testimony.

"Aldous Vey," he said, and said it loudly, deliberately, so that the clerks would have to write it. "Surveyor-General of the Eastern Coast, Fellow of the Cartographic Society, maker of the finest charts this empire has printed in thirty years. He took in an orphan with no papers and taught him that accuracy is a moral act. He measured every river between here and the marsh road, and he is buried in an unmarked grave because this magistrate ruled his murder an accident in four sentences." He stood—the guards moved, then didn't, because the room's attention held them as surely as any order. "My master's name is Aldous Vey. My name is Lena Okonkwo. My mother was born in Onitsha and my father in the Vellmark dockyards, and neither of their names appears in your census, which I suspect is why you are building one. Write all of it down. It's the most honest record anyone will make in this hall today."

The silence afterward had texture. He could feel it pressing on the room like weather.

Corvin studied him for a long moment, and what moved behind the magistrate's face was not anger. It was arithmetic. Lena recognized it because it was his own kind of thinking—weights, distances, tolerances. Corvin was calculating what a dead boy was worth against what a silenced boy was worth, and finding the columns uncomfortably close.

"Remove the prisoner," Corvin said at last, mildly. "We will reconvene when the bench has reviewed the recovered materials."

Guards took his arms. Lena let them. As they walked him toward the doors he counted, out of lifelong habit, the paces—forty-one to the threshold—and noted, also out of lifelong habit, that the eastern window faced the harbor, and that beyond the customs yard, beyond the roofs, the clock tower stood above the city with its face lit by morning, and that Judge Halloran's chambers lay four streets east of it.

Ilse had left the cell before dawn. Four streets. Perhaps she had already crossed them.

Perhaps, by now, someone else was reading.

* * *

They put him in a different cell after the hearing—deeper, damper, with a door instead of bars, which he understood perfectly. Doors meant they intended to keep him past the day. Bars meant they hadn't decided. He lay on the bench with his hands behind his head and listened to the building breathe: boots overhead, the rattle of the records room, distant bells marking the hours he could no longer see.

Nine days, Mirela had said, until the tribunal convened. Then the count had shrunk to three, then to this—one day, one hearing, held early and quietly, with an audience arranged like stage dressing. Corvin had accelerated because Corvin was afraid. Fear was a clock too, and it ran faster than brass.

He thought about the master. Not the dignified version the hall had demanded—Vey the Surveyor-General, Vey the martyr—but the real one, swearing at damp paper, burning the porridge, falling asleep over proofs with his spectacles pushed up into his white hair. *A map is a promise,* the old man had said a hundred times, and the hundredth time it had sounded like a platitude, and now, in the dark under the Customs House, it sounded like the entire law.

Lena had spoken the promise aloud today, in front of the eagle and the clerks and the thin-lipped man on the dais. Whatever happened next, it was spoken. It could not be unsaid. Somewhere in the record room a clerk's copy existed with two names on it that Corvin wanted erased, and paper, Lena knew better than almost anyone alive, was patient. Paper waited. Paper outlived magistrates.

Hours passed, or what he took for hours. His ear throbbed where the guard had struck him. He catalogued his injuries the way he'd catalogue errors on a proof—minor, correctable, survivable—and was drifting toward sleep when the key turned again.

Not the guards. A small woman in clerk's gray, carrying a ledger, with ink on her fingers and Ilse's exact steadiness in her eyes.

"You're either very brave or very stupid," she said quietly, setting the ledger on the floor, "and the judge's chambers received a document tonight, so it hardly matters which."

Lena sat up so fast the bench scraped stone.

"What did it say?" he asked. "The letter—what did the judge say?"

The woman glanced at the door, then leaned closer, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, but her eyes were bright with something that in a courtroom would have been called contempt and in a cellar could finally be called hope.

"He hasn't read it yet," she said. "The magistrate's men are watching his chambers. But the tribunal reconvenes at nine tomorrow—and Judge Halloran has requested the honor of reading the recovered evidence *aloud*, before the full bench, as is his ancient right." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Corvin cannot refuse without telling the whole hall there's something in it worth refusing for."

Lena pressed his back against the wall and laughed—a raw, disbelieving sound, the first laugh since the workshop burned.

Tomorrow at nine, the chimes would sound over Vellmark, and in the Assize Hall, under the painted eagle, a dead man's letter would be opened in front of the man who had killed him.

"Then God help us all," Lena said, "if the seal holds."
