## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The frost on the ministry windows had grown so thick by midwinter that the light coming through it arrived already defeated—gray, diffused, like something that had given up before reaching the room. Elodie Blackwood stood in the corridor outside the Office of Correspondence and watched her own breath hang in the air a moment longer than breath should. The stoves had not been lit since Tuesday. No one had said why. No one said anything anymore about the small abandonments; they simply stepped around them the way one steps around a beggar in a doorway, with practiced eyes that refuse to confirm what they see.

She was early. She was always early. In eleven years as a courier she had learned that punctuality was not a virtue but an armor: the courier who arrives first has time to look at the room, count the exits, note who is absent. The courier who arrives late is remembered.

The corridor smelled of cold stone and old paper and, faintly, of lamp oil that someone was rationing without admitting it. At the far end, a clerk named Pellier was stacking files into crates—not filing them, Elodie noticed, but *stacking* them, spine-out, in the careless way of a man building a fire he hadn't yet been ordered to build. He saw her looking and turned his back with elaborate indifference.

Elodie adjusted her collar and did not ask. That was the first rule, learned before she could legally carry documents at all: *the courier does not ask.* Not what's in the pouch. Not where the office is going. Not why there are three desks empty in the Records Hall with their chairs still pushed in neatly, as if the occupants meant to return after lunch and simply never did. Questions were how people disappeared here—not dramatically, not with boots in the night, but slowly, by becoming the sort of person others stopped greeting in stairwells.

She knocked twice on the door of the Registrar's antechamber, waited the regulation interval, and entered.

* * *

The pouch lay on the desk like something sleeping. It was a diplomatic case, oilcloth over stiffened leather, sealed with wax at three points—one for the Chancellery, one for the Northern Bureau, and a third seal Elodie didn't recognize. She catalogued that absence of recognition and set it aside, deliberately, the way she'd been taught to set aside everything that wasn't her business. The seal was dark green wax pressed with a device she couldn't quite resolve from this distance: not the republic's crossed quills, not the river-crown of the old provinces. Something else. She would have to look at it again, and then make herself stop looking.

Vasseur sat behind the desk, or rather perched above it, a thin man built of angles and gray wool, his hands folded as though in prayer or apology. He was perhaps fifty-five and looked older this winter, the way all the buildings looked older—the frost getting into the mortar of him. His office had once held four clerks. Now it held Vasseur, a stove with a dead heart, and a wall map of the river territories whose pins had been quietly removed, all of them, leaving only the small brown holes like a constellation nobody believed in anymore.

"Blackwood." He said her name the way he always did, flatly, as if reading it off a manifest. "You're well?"

"I'm well, sir."

"The roads?"

"Ice holds north of Verenne. South of Verenne the thaw's started under the bridges—there'll be black ice at the crossings come evening. I'd cross at dawn if I were crossing."

"You're not crossing today." He unfolded his hands, folded them again. "Today you take the route memorization. Tomorrow, before light, you go north."

She nodded. This too was ritual: no courier ever received a route in writing. The written route could be found on her body; the memorized route existed only in her skull, and skulls, unlike satchels, could be counted on to burn with their owners. Vasseur rose and went to the map, and together they performed the ceremony they had performed a dozen times, except that this time his finger trembled slightly against the painted paper, and she pretended very hard not to see it.

"Out through the Salt Gate. You do not use the Salt Gate road past the tanneries; you take the cart lane behind the aqueduct, even though it adds forty minutes." His finger moved. "Forty minutes buys you a road with no garrison view. At Mireval there is a checkpoint—new since autumn. They will want your papers, your manifest, and your name spoken aloud. You give all three. You are carrying agricultural dispatches for the Northern Bureau, seed allotments and grain tallies. If asked why a diplomatic seal, you say the Bureau's own seals went into storage during the reorganization and no one has issued new ones. Say it bored. Boredom is believable; indignation is a flag."

"Bored," Elodie repeated, tasting it.

"At Mireval they may hold you while they copy your papers. Let them copy. A courier who argues about being delayed is a courier carrying something worth delaying for. You will be delayed two hours, possibly four. Use the time to sleep, because—" the finger slid north along the blue thread of the river, "—the second checkpoint is at Ferrier Bridge, and Ferrier Bridge is different."

"Different how?"

"It's run out of the old tollhouse, and it's run by Madame Oryl."

The name landed in the room like a coin dropped in a church. Elodie knew it the way everyone in the service knew it—a name passed in low voices, attached to stories that always ended the same way: *she let them through, and they were never seen again,* or, worse, *she let them through, smiling.* Oryl interviewed couriers. Not searched—interviewed. Sat them down in a warm room with tea and asked questions for an hour or three, gentle questions, kind questions, and remembered every answer forever, and compared them later, across months, against answers other couriers gave, until the lies snagged on each other like burrs.

"I know," Elodie said. "I've heard."

"Heard is not prepared." Vasseur came back around the desk and sat, and for a moment he looked at her with an expression she had never seen on him—something almost like reluctance. "At Oryl's checkpoint you will volunteer truths. Small ones. Your mother died when you were young—you may offer this yourself if she asks about family; she will ask. Tell her you're tired. Tell her your feet hurt. Tell her the truth about anything except the pouch, and bury the pouch so deep under harmless honesty that she tires of digging before she reaches it. She is not lazy. But she is thorough, and thoroughness takes time, and time is the only thing we have less of than money."

"And the third checkpoint?" Elodie asked, though she had already begun assembling it in her mind, the whole architecture of the journey rising like a house built of rules. "You've only given me two."

Vasseur's hands went still.

"The third is not on any map I can show you," he said finally. "It sits on the river itself, below the old ferry landing at Coursaint. An ice road. There's a hut, a brazier, and officers of the Third Commission—and Blackwood, I will tell you plainly, because you deserve plainness once in this career: the Third Commission does not interview. They do not copy papers. They weigh people, and what doesn't balance goes into the water when the ice opens." He paused. "You'll have new instructions for Coursaint tomorrow. Tonight, learn the first two legs until you can walk them blindfolded, and forget nothing, because forgetting, this season, is indistinguishable from betrayal."

* * *

She spent the afternoon in the map room, which was really just a corner of the Records Hall now, since the eastern half had been closed "for repairs" in November and had remained, like everything else, unrepaired. She drew the route from memory onto scrap paper—drew it three times, burned each drawing in the cold stove's dead grate (there was no fire; she simply tore the papers small), and walked the distances in her head. Salt Gate to the aqueduct lane: forty minutes added, no garrison sightlines. Aqueduct to Mireval: six hours on the cart road if the snow held firm, seven if it drifted. Mireval to Ferrier Bridge: a day and part of another, following the river north through country where the farms had gone quiet—not abandoned exactly, but quiet, the shutters closed in the daytime, smoke rising thin and guilty from chimneys that used to burn generously.

This was how she carried routes: not as lines on paper but as bodies of habit, as hours and smells and the particular way a village dog barks when soldiers are billeted versus when they are merely passing. Her mother had taught her none of this. Her mother had vanished before any of it, and yet Elodie sometimes felt that the woman had taught her everything anyway, in the negative space she left behind—in the shape of the hole, which told you precisely what had stood there.

By four o'clock the light was dying and Pellier had finished his crates. There were nine of them now, stacked by the loading door, and a carter had come for six of them, and no one had said where the carter was taking them, and everyone had watched the carter leave with the particular studied blindness of people watching a coffin go by.

Elodie packed up her slate and her chalk and, on her way out, allowed herself one glance at the third seal on the pouch, which Vasseur had left on his desk while he went to speak with someone in the Chancellery wing. Green wax. And pressed into it—not a state device at all, she saw now, but something older: a compass rose, eight-pointed, worn almost smooth, the kind of mark a smuggler might burn into a crate to claim it, or a family might press into wax a hundred years ago when families still had such things.

She looked at it for exactly as long as it took to memorize it, and then made herself look away.

That night, in her rented room above the chandler's shop, Elodie laid out her coat on the narrow bed to pack it for the morning—oilskin packet, spare stockings, bread wrapped in cloth, the forged manifest with its agricultural fictions. And as her fingers ran along the inside lining, checking the seams out of eleven years' habit, they found something that had not been there yesterday.

A small hard shape, sewn flat against the lower hem, beneath fabric that had been restitched with tiny, expert, unfamiliar stitches.

Her heart did not pound. Eleven years had trained even her heart to be discreet. But her hands went very quiet on the bedspread, and the room seemed to lean in around her, frost ticking softly in the window glass, the chandler's dog stirring below, and Elodie Blackwood sat alone in the capital of a dying republic, holding a coat that someone had entered in the night—or long ago—and wondering, with the first true fear of the winter, what her mother had hidden in the seams twelve years before she disappeared, and why it had chosen tonight to find her.
