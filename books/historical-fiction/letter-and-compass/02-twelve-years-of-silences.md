## 2. Twelve Years of Silences

The night before a mission, Elodie Blackwood slept badly, and she had learned to consider this a professional advantage.

She lay now in her narrow room above the chandler's shop on Rue Verrerie, listening to the building settle its bones against the cold. The plaster ticked. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog turned twice and sighed. In three hours she would rise, dress in the grey wool that made her look like every other woman hurrying through a hungry city, eat bread she wouldn't taste, and walk north out of the capital carrying words that could kill her if she ever let them into her mouth.

But for now she lay still and did what she always did the night before: she went back over the rules. Not the ones written in the office handbook—those were almost useless, being written by men who believed paperwork was a kind of armor—but the real ones, the ones no one had taught her because there was no one left to teach them.

Rule one: never learn more than you must carry.

Rule two: never be interesting.

Rule three—and this was the one that had cost her the most to learn—never answer a question with another question. Curiosity was a scent, and the dogs of the republic had been bred for it.

She recited them the way other people prayed, and when she reached the end of the list she began again from the top, because sleep was a country she had stopped trying to enter years ago, and the rules were at least a road she knew.

Outside, snow came down without wind, straight and silent, erasing the city one street at a time. Elodie watched it through the frost-feathered glass and thought, as she always did on the last night, about her mother's hands.

* * *

Twelve years ago, in the spring the old government fell for the first time—not the final fall, but the first crack that told everyone with ears that the wall was coming down eventually—a girl of eleven stood in a kitchen on the edge of the Marais district and watched her mother sew a compass into the lining of a coat.

Not hide it. Sew it. With small, even stitches, using thread pulled from the hem of a curtain, working by the light of an oil lamp turned low. The compass was brass, smaller than a plum, and it had no markings on its case except a worn smoothness where a thumb had rubbed it for years.

"Mama," the girl said, "why don't you just put it in your pocket?"

Her mother didn't look up. "Because pockets get searched, little wren, and seams only get searched if they already suspect you. Remember that. Suspicion is a door you open yourself, nine times out of ten."

It was not an unusual conversation in their house. Her mother, Maren Blackwood, was a widow who dealt in textiles—so said the license framed above the stove, so said the bolts of cloth stacked in the front room—and like everything else in their house, the license and the cloth were true and also not the whole truth. Elodie had understood this the way children understand weather: not as information but as atmosphere. There were men who came after closing hours and spoke softly. There were packages wrapped in linen instead of paper. There were nights her mother went out and came back before dawn smelling faintly of river water, and on those mornings she made eggs with extra butter and hummed while she cooked, and Elodie learned to read the humming as a barometer.

Then came the morning the humming stopped.

Elodie was eleven. She woke to a house with the fire dead and her mother's coat gone from its hook—the coat with the seam, though Elodie would not understand the significance of that for years—and a neighbor, Madame Ferrand, standing in the doorway with a face arranged into something between sympathy and caution.

"There's been an accident on the river crossing," Madame Ferrand said. "They think."

They think. Two words, and between them a chasm wide enough to swallow a woman whole.

What happened next happened quickly, the way catastrophes do, and also slowly, the way grief does. The textile shop was sealed by an office whose name changed twice in a month. A man with a clipboard inventoried bolts of wool as though counting the spoils of a war. Questions were asked of Elodie by people whose kindness had a texture she couldn't name yet but would learn to recognize everywhere afterward: the texture of an instrument.

Where did your mother go? Who did she see? What did she carry?

And Elodie, eleven years old and already learning, said: I don't know. She sold cloth.

It was the first lie of her life that mattered, and it came out of her smooth and easy, and afterward she sat alone in Madame Ferrand's spare room—her mother having owned nothing so useful as relatives—and felt the strange cold pride of it, mixed underneath with a terror that never entirely left.

I don't know. She sold cloth.

Three sentences, and a life built on them.

* * *

The neighbors were the next lesson.

Madame Ferrand meant well; Elodie understood that even then. But Madame Ferrand also talked, and her talk was the innocent kind, which is the most dangerous kind, because it has no reason to stop. Within two weeks of the disappearance, half the street knew that the Blackwood girl was staying with the Ferrands, that she barely ate, that she hadn't cried once, which they found unsettling.

Within a month, someone had asked whether Maren Blackwood had kept a ledger. Whether she'd had business partners. Whether the girl remembered any names.

So Elodie added to the rules:

Greet the baker. Greet the fishwife. Do not greet the couple on the second floor of the corner building who asked too gently how she was sleeping, because gentle questions from strangers are not questions; they are nets.

Do not cry in public. Tears invite comfort, and comfort invites conversation, and conversation is where people hand you their suspicions disguised as concern and wait for you to hand back something true.

When officials come—and they came four times in that first year—answer slowly. Not reluctantly; slow. Slow answers read as honest answers, because liars rehearse speed.

She was very good at all of it by twelve. By thirteen she had made herself into a creature the street barely saw: quiet, tidy, forgettable, a girl who ran errands for Madame Ferrand and studied her letters and wanted, everyone agreed, to become a clerk someday. A clerk. The word had a lovely grayness to it. Clerks were furniture. Furniture was safe.

What none of them saw—what she scarcely admitted to herself—was that every rule she followed was a letter to her mother. If I am invisible enough, the silence said, whatever took you won't find me, and I will be here when you come back.

Because she did believe her mother would come back. For the first two years she believed it with the whole furnace of her heart. Every knock was possibly her. Every figure at the end of the lane, walking toward the house through rain or dusk. She trained herself out of running to the door—running attracted attention—but she never trained herself out of listening for footsteps that walked like her mother's, quick and light, with a little drag on the left side from an old injury she'd never explained.

The drag on the left side. That was the detail Elodie guarded most jealously, the one she never gave up even in her own thoughts, because details were the currency of the offices that asked questions, and some part of her understood that as long as she kept that one detail private, her mother remained hers—not a file, not a name on a list, not a case number, but a woman with a limp who sewed compasses into coats and hummed when the work was good.

* * *

The office found her at fourteen, or rather she found it, though the finding was arranged so smoothly she never learned whose hand moved which piece.

A clerk position, advertised through the church charity that administered her stipend. An office of the diplomatic service, in a ministry building whose corridors smelled of cold stone and lamp oil and, increasingly in those years, of dust, because rooms were being closed off one by one as budgets failed and faith failed faster. The pay was small. The work was sorting.

But Elodie sorted well, and quietly, and one afternoon in her second year a man named Vasseur appeared at her desk with a misdelivered pouch and watched, without appearing to watch, how she handled it. She logged it. She sealed it. She asked nothing about it—not its weight class, not its origin, not why the seal had been broken and resealed with wax a shade too red. She simply put it where it belonged and went back to her inventory.

Vasseur came back the next week with another pouch. And the week after that.

"You don't ask questions," he said finally, the third time, standing at her desk with his hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting a horse he intended to buy.

"No, sir."

"Most people ask. Even the frightened ones. Especially the frightened ones—they want to know if the thing they're afraid of is in the box." He set a third pouch down, lighter than the others. "Your file says your mother disappeared during the river crossings. Eleven years ago. I'm sorry."

"Thank you, sir."

"That's all you have to say?"

"It was a long time ago, sir."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she looked at the desk, because looking back would have been interesting, and interesting was against the rules. When she glanced up at last—he had waited until she did; she understood later that he'd engineered even that—he wore an expression she couldn't parse then and could only partially parse now: something between pity and recognition, the look of a man seeing a younger version of something he himself had lost.

"Couriers earn triple," he said. "And couriers are never asked what they carry. It protects them. Ignorance is armor." He paused. "You'll be tested first. Everyone is. Most fail."

"What happens to the ones who fail?"

"They go back to sorting." His voice was mild. His eyes were not. "That's the mercy of this office, Miss Blackwood. We're not the sort who make failures disappear."

She signed the paper. She told herself it was for the money—for Madame Ferrand's rent, for coal in winter—and this was true, and it was also, even then, not the whole truth. Because somewhere beneath the practical reasons lay the thing she never said aloud, not to Vasseur, not to the priest, not to the dark ceiling of her room: the routes her mother had worked were river routes. Northern routes. And couriers were never asked what they carried, but nobody had ever written a rule against a courier noticing which roads she traveled, and which roads she was never assigned, and what lived at the far end of the roads she wasn't given.

If her mother had been taken by the work, the work might show her where.

It was not a hope, exactly. Hopes were warm. This was colder and harder, more like the brass compass itself: a small weight she carried, pointing nowhere she could prove, humming faintly whenever she held it up to her life.

* * *

Now, twelve years later, she lay in her room above the chandler's shop and finished the third recitation of the rules, and permitted herself—as a last ritual, the way other people crossed themselves—one minute of remembering.

Her mother's hands, sewing. The smell of lamp oil. *Suspicion is a door you open yourself.*

And then the morning of the humming stopping, which she visited less often these days, because she had learned that grief, unattended, grows quieter but never smaller; it just moves deeper into the walls of you, like water into plaster, and you live in the house anyway.

They never found a body. They never found anything. The river crossing had been closed for repairs that entire month—she'd checked, at sixteen, risking a question in a records office, her heart going like a trapped bird—and so whatever "accident" had been reported to Madame Ferrand was a fiction, a soft word handed to a child to keep her from asking the hard ones. Which meant her mother had not drowned. Which meant she had been taken, or fled, or chosen, or been chosen for—and each possibility was a different wound, and Elodie had spent twelve years refusing to pick just one, because picking one would mean knowing, and knowing was the one thing her entire life was built to avoid.

She sold cloth. I don't know. It was a long time ago.

Tomorrow she would carry a diplomatic pouch north along the river road, through checkpoints staffed by men and women whose salaries depended on making people answer questions, and she would be forgettable, and slow, and safe, because she had made herself into a fortress of not-knowing, brick by brick, silence by silence, since she was eleven years old.

She turned onto her side. The needle of her mind, as always on these nights, swung between its two directions: the daughter who wanted the truth about Maren Blackwood more than she wanted air, and the courier who understood that truth, once loose, could not be sewn back into a lining.

Sleep came late and thin. She dreamed of a coat with a seam in it, hanging on a hook, and in the dream she reached for the hem and—

The bell of the chandler's shop below rang once. Then again. Someone at the door at half past midnight, insistent, muffled by snow.

Elodie was out of bed and dressed before the third ring, her body moving on rules older than thought: candle unlit, shoes on, bag packed by the door exactly where it always stood. Through the frost-furred window she could see nothing but white falling white. But the knocking continued, patient and rhythmic, three strikes and a pause, three strikes and a pause—and she knew, with a certainty that settled over her like the coat itself settling onto her shoulders, that whoever stood below was not there for the chandler.

She picked up her bag, blew out the stub of candle she hadn't lit, and went down the stairs into the dark.
