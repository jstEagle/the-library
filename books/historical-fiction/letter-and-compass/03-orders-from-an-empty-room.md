## 3. Orders from an Empty Room

The Ministry of Correspondence had once required forty clerks to process the morning dispatches. On the morning Elodie Blackwood climbed its eastern stair, she counted four coats on the rack by the door, and one of those had been hanging there since autumn, abandoned by a clerk who had presumably gone home one evening and simply never returned. The coat had gathered dust the way things do in rooms where no one is left to be embarrassed by it.

She did not let herself think about that too long. Thinking too long was the first symptom.

The corridors smelled of cold ash and lamp oil and, beneath it, the particular staleness of rooms heated irregularly—the smell of an institution forgetting itself from the inside out. Her boots rang against marble that had been polished daily by a staff of six; now a single old woman with a push-broom worked the far end of the hall, moving at the pace of someone who had concluded, correctly, that no one was measuring her effort anymore. Elodie nodded to her as she passed. The old woman did not nod back. Perhaps she hadn't seen her. Perhaps she had decided that seeing people was a habit better surrendered early.

Vasseur's office was at the end of the corridor, behind a door with frosted glass that still read DEPUTY DIRECTOR, SECOND BUREAU in gold letters gone brown at the edges. Someone had once tried to scrape the title off—Elodie could see the crescents of a blade's work under the gilt—but had stopped halfway through *Deputy*, as if the arm had tired or the conscience had intervened. She knocked twice, softly, the way she always did. Loud knocking invited witnesses.

"Enter," said Vasseur, and then, before she had finished turning the handle, "Shut it behind you."

The room was dim. He kept the shutters half closed now, a habit that had crept up on him over the winter the way frost creeps over glass—from the edges inward, quietly, until the middle goes too. He sat behind a desk that had been magnificent once, walnut and leather, and was now distinguished mainly by the absence of anything on it. No papers stacked for signature. No inkwell with its blotter squared beside it. Just a single leather pouch, a folded map, and a cup of coffee he plainly was not drinking. Steam still rose off it. He had poured it to have something to do with his hands, she thought, and then forgotten to perform the drinking.

"You're punctual," he said.

"The roads were empty."

"That's not why roads are empty." He said it without looking up, and there was something almost gentle in the way he said it, the way a man might tell a child that the dead bird is not sleeping. "Sit down, Blackwood."

She sat. She had learned long ago not to sit until invited; Vasseur noticed such things, catalogued them, and never mentioned them, which was worse than mentioning them.

He was not an impressive man to look at, and he had perhaps once been proud of that—the idea that the Second Bureau's best handler should resemble a provincial tax assessor, gray at the temples, soft around the jaw, forgettable in a crowd of three. His spectacles sat low on his nose. His coat, she noticed, was buttoned wrong. One button. A small derangement, the kind that meant a man had dressed in the dark or in haste or in some third state she didn't have a name for.

"You know the route," he began.

"I know the route."

"Tell me anyway."

This was ritual. She understood ritual; ritual was what remained when trust became too expensive. "North along the river road as far as Verrey Bridge. Then east through the forest road to the ferry crossing at Saint-Aubin. Then north again on the ice road to the last station before the marsh country." She recited it flatly, the way one recites a prayer one has stopped believing in but still finds comfort in the shape of. "Three days if the weather holds. Five if it doesn't. Six is failure."

"And the checkpoints?"

"Verrey Bridge first. Military, junior officers, paperwork. They're slow but they're honest—honest in the way of men who want to go home." She held up two fingers. "Saint-Aubin second. That one isn't military anymore, or doesn't pretend to be. They say an interrogator runs it now. Madame Oryl." She watched his face when she said the name. Nothing moved in it, but something *arranged* itself, the way furniture arranges when someone leans against it. "They interview couriers at length. They remember everything you say, and they ask about it again later to see if you change your story."

"And you won't."

"No."

"Because?"

"Because I only ever tell the truth except when I lie, and I lie so rarely that it comes out sounding like truth."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile; more the memory of one, preserved like a flower in a book whose pages hadn't been opened in years. "And the third?"

"The ice road checkpoint. It moves with the season. In deep winter it sits on the river itself—a hut, a stove, two bored soldiers and a sergeant who takes bribes in brandy." She paused. "In thaw it becomes a boat patrol, and boats can stop anything."

"So you understand the calendar."

"The thaw is my deadline. Yes."

Vasseur nodded slowly. Then he reached across the desk—not for the pouch, as she expected, but for the map—and unfolded it with both hands, and it was then, watching his hands, that she saw it.

They were shaking.

Not dramatically. Not the tremor of drink or palsy. It was finer than that, a vibration at rest, the kind of shaking that a man can hide while moving and cannot hide while still. He flattened the map with his palms and the trembling transmitted itself down into the paper, so that the river line drawn in blue ink seemed to quiver, seemed almost to flow, and Elodie felt a sensation move through her chest that she recognized with dislike: fear arriving ahead of its own reasons.

*Everyone pretends not to notice,* she thought. *That is the arrangement. That has always been the arrangement.*

She looked at the map instead of his hands.

The river ran diagonally across it like a crack in porcelain, and along its length, in Vasseur's small precise hand, someone had marked the three checkpoints with red circles. But there were other marks too—pencil this time, fainter, erasable: little crosses scattered along the eastern bank, clustered near the crossings, near the fords, near the places where the road came closest to the water. She counted eleven of them before she made herself stop counting.

"What are the crosses?" she asked.

"Nothing that concerns your mission."

"Everything on this map concerns my mission. That's what a map is."

He looked at her over the rims of his spectacles, and for a moment—one unguarded moment, gone almost before she registered it—he looked *tired* in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "The crosses are places where the ice will fail first," he said. "Springs underneath. Current channels. Old knowledge, from men who used to run cargo before any of us had opinions worth arresting." He straightened. "You won't need them if you keep to schedule. Keep to schedule, Blackwood. Schedules are how ordinary couriers survive extraordinary winters."

*And how do extraordinary ones survive?* she did not ask. There was a whole category of questions now that lived permanently in that condition—asked and answered only inside her own skull, where no interrogator could reach.

He slid the map aside. The pouch remained.

It was standard issue, diplomatic gray, wax-sealed at the throat, and she had carried fifty of them exactly like it. But the seal itself—

She leaned forward despite herself. She had been trained out of curiosity the way one trains a horse out of shying, patiently and repeatedly and never entirely, and what remained of it stirred now like an animal smelling weather.

The wax was black. Not the dark red of the Foreign Office, nor the green of Interior, nor the plain uncolored wax of internal circulation, which she knew better than her own handwriting. Black wax existed officially for exactly one purpose: communications of state so classified that even acknowledging their classification was forbidden. She had seen black wax twice in twelve years. Both times, other people had carried the letter, and both times, she had later heard, those people were no longer employed by anyone, anywhere, in any capacity anyone would name aloud.

But it wasn't the color that stopped her. It was the impression.

A seal she had never seen. And Elodie Blackwood knew seals the way organists know keyboards. The republic's arms—a tower above three waves—she could have pressed into wax from memory. The Bureau's cipher wheel. The Director's personal signet with its worn lion. This was none of these. It showed a tree, bare-branched, and beneath the tree a small object she couldn't resolve at this angle, roundish, maybe a stone, maybe fruit, maybe—her mind supplied the word unwillingly—a compass rose. Around the rim ran letters too fine and too worn to read, the script of some authority older than the republic, older possibly than the kingdom the republic had replaced.

"Whose seal is this?" she asked. The question left her mouth before discipline caught it, and she heard her own voice come out lower than intended, almost reverent, almost afraid.

"It's better that you don't recognize it," Vasseur said. "Recognizing a thing gives it weight. Weight slows a courier."

"Everything weighs something. That's why you hire couriers instead of posting notices."

For a long moment he said nothing. The coffee steamed between them, going bitter in its cup. Somewhere down the corridor the old woman's broom whispered against the marble, a sound like the building breathing in its sleep, and Elodie found herself listening to it the way one listens to a clock in an otherwise silent room—needing to confirm that something, somewhere, was still keeping time.

"This may be the office's last mission," Vasseur said at last.

She had expected it the way one expects winter—knowing it was coming, having prepared for it, and yet feeling the actual cold arrive as a surprise anyway. She kept her face still. Twelve years of practice went into keeping her face still, and they served.

"The northern stations are being consolidated. Or dissolved. The word depends on who is using it and what they're hoping you'll hear." He removed his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and without the glass his eyes looked suddenly naked, older, ringed with a fatigue that spectacles had been politely concealing. "There will be no resupply. There will be no second courier sent after you if you don't arrive. If you're taken at a checkpoint, the Bureau's position—" he almost smiled, "—is that you acted alone, without authorization, for private reasons we decline to speculate upon."

"In other words I'm already disowned."

"In other words you are *protected*. Disavowal is protection, Blackwood. It's the only kind we have left to give." He put the spectacles back on, and the man reassembled himself around them, the handler again, the gray forgettable functionary. "Deliver the letter to the stationmaster at the last office on the map. A man called Ferriol. You'll know him by his hands—he lost two fingers to a printing press and is vain enough about the remaining eight that he counts them in company. Give him the pouch. Take nothing back. Come home by a different road than the one you travel north."

"Why a different road?"

"Because the road you take north will be remembered. By everyone. Including the people who wish you had never walked it."

She looked at the pouch. Black wax. A tree without leaves. Something round beneath it. Eleven pencil crosses marking where the ice would fail.

"Vasseur," she said, and used his name deliberately, which she almost never permitted herself, "you know what's in this letter."

It was not a question, and he did not treat it as one. What he did was worse. He picked up the coffee cup—as if the gesture could restore the fiction of an ordinary morning, two colleagues, a routine run—and his hand shook so badly coming off the desk that the surface of the liquid shivered, rings spreading from the rim, and he set it down again untasted, and neither of them remarked upon it.

"I know that it must reach Ferriol before the thaw," he said. "I know that you are the only courier I have left whom I trust to carry a thing without opening it, and the only courier I have left whom I trust to open it and still carry it, depending on what the road demands. Both of those sentences are true, and I am aware that they shouldn't both be true, and I have decided to stop being troubled by that." He drew a breath. "The office is emptying because the republic is finishing, Elodie. Not ending—finishing, the way a sentence finishes. What remains is only to choose how the last words are delivered. I have chosen you to deliver them. Whatever else happens, whatever you decide on the road, alone, with the wind in your face and no one watching—" his voice dropped, and for a moment the shaking in his hands seemed to have traveled up into it, "—I chose you *deliberately*. Remember that. Whether it comforts you or damns me, remember it."

The room was very quiet. Through the shutter slats a bar of pale light lay across the desk like a blade laid down, and in it the black seal glistened faintly, wet-looking, the leafless tree casting no shadow at all.

Elodie stood. She took the pouch. It weighed almost nothing—that was the obscenity of it, that a thing capable of whatever this letter was capable should weigh less than a loaf of bread—and she slipped it inside her coat, against her breast, where it would ride warm and secret over the next hundred miles.

"Keep to schedule," she said.

"Keep to schedule," he agreed, and turned away to the window as if there were something in the frozen street worth watching, and she understood that she had been dismissed, and that he could not watch her walk out of the room, and that both of these facts were the same fact.

The corridor beyond was colder than the office. The broom-woman had vanished; the marble stretched away empty in both directions, and her footsteps went ahead of her like a messenger announcing her to no one. At the head of the stairs she paused—only a moment—and looked back down the hall at the door with its half-scraped gold letters, and thought: *the last words of a sentence.* Then she took the pouch from her coat just long enough to press her palm flat against the black wax, sealing it to her the way wax seals paper, and put it away, and descended into the morning.

She did not yet know what the letter said. But she carried, all the way down those stairs and out into the white silence of the capital, the certainty that Vasseur knew—and that his shaking hands had told her everything the sealed tree would not: that whatever waited inside the wax was heavy enough to make even him tremble, and that he had handed the whole of its weight to her, deliberately, and let her walk out the door.

* * *

At the corner of the Rue aux Chevaux, where the frozen fountain wore its hood of ice like a mourner's veil, Elodie stopped to check her bootlaces—an old habit, a courier's pause, giving herself ten seconds of stillness in which to feel the street around her. A fish cart rattled past. Two women argued cheerfully about the price of salt. A boy swept snow from a doorstep that would need sweeping again within the hour.

An ordinary street. An ordinary morning. Everyone pretending, beautifully, fluently, that the world was still nailed down at its corners.

Only when she raised her head did she see it: chalked small and low on the fountain's stone base, at the height of a child's hand, a single mark. A circle, bisected by a line, the two halves shaded differently—one dark, one light.

A needle between two directions.

She stared at it for the space of three heartbeats. Then the fish cart's driver glanced her way, and she bent again to her laces, and when she looked up a second time she was only a woman tying her boots on an ordinary street, and the mark was only a mark, and the morning went on pretending, and so did she.
