## 7. The Smuggler at the Ferry

Tomas Reyes knew every sound the river made in winter, and the one he heard at dusk on the third of Thaw-Month was not one of them.

It was a footstep. Deliberate, unhurried, but wrong in its rhythm—too even for a drunk, too light for a soldier, too confident for a fool on ice that had begun to sing. He set down the net he was mending and did not reach for the knife under the seat plank. Reaching was a confession. He simply sat in the doorway of his shack and waited, letting the lamplight fall across him like an accusation he'd already answered a hundred times.

The figure came along the frozen margin where reeds stood dead and brittle as wire, following the river's edge the way river people do—without looking at it, the way you don't stare at a scarred face. A small figure wrapped in a gray traveling coat, hood back, breath steaming. A woman, young, with the particular posture of someone carrying weight that wasn't visible: shoulders squared to distribute it, chin level, eyes doing the work of hands.

She stopped ten paces out, at the exact distance where courtesy begins and suspicion hasn't yet ended.

"I'm looking for the ferryman," she said.

"The ferry's closed." He let his accent thicken, the river drawl he could put on and take off like a coat. "Ice is rotten past the bend. Come spring, maybe."

"It's not spring yet."

"Then you've answered yourself."

She didn't move. The lamp threw her shadow long across the ice, and Tomas studied it the way he studied everything—the shape told truths the face withheld. Her shadow was compact, controlled. No sword. The bulge at her left hip was a satchel, flat, strapped tight against the body the way documents are carried, not bread.

"Reyes," she said. Not a question.

His hands went still in his lap. Outside the capital, almost nobody used the name. On the river he was Tomaz, or the old man at the ferry, or nothing at all. "You have me confused with someone."

"I don't think so." She came two steps closer, boots crunching, and stopped again. "I think you're the man who runs cargo past the second checkpoint for people who can't afford to be seen running it. And I think you owe a debt."

The wind moved through the dead reeds with a sound like paper tearing. Somewhere under their feet, deep in the black water beneath the ice, the river shifted its shoulders and groaned—a long tectonic complaint that would grow louder every night now until the ice gave way. Early thaw this year. Everyone on the river said so. Everyone on the river was already counting what they'd lose when the crossing closed.

"Debts," Tomas said, "are between me and the dead."

"That's why I'm here instead of her."

He looked at her then, properly, and the lamp did him no favors because it showed him what he had been trying not to see since she spoke. The set of the mouth. The way she held stillness—not the stillness of fear but of training, of a person who had learned that motion attracts the eye. He had known another woman who held herself exactly that way, twenty years ago, on a night much colder than this one, when she'd appeared out of the dark with a child bundled against her chest and a lie about her husband that Tomas had never believed and never challenged.

"You'd better come inside," he said, "before the ice hears you."

* * *

The shack smelled of pitch, fish oil, and woodsmoke, and it was warm in the way that only small rooms over stoves can be warm—aggressively, personally warm, warmth as a form of argument. She sat where he pointed, on the bench by the wall, and set her satchel on her knees and kept it there, arms around it, the way a child holds a thing it has been told not to lose.

"Tomas Reyes," she said again, testing it.

"And you're nobody," he said. "That's how these conversations go. You're nobody, going nowhere, carrying nothing, and I'm a poor fisherman who's never moved anything heavier than pike in his life. We agree on all of it, and then we talk about weather until one of us names a price."

A flicker crossed her face—almost a smile, quickly disciplined. "Weather first, then. They say the thaw is early."

"They say right. Ice'll break inside three weeks. Maybe two if the south wind holds. After that there's no crossing here till summer, and no road either, because the mud takes the north road first and keeps it." He poured tea from the blackened pot without asking whether she wanted any; she took the tin cup in both hands and drank, which told him she'd been on the road since morning at least, maybe longer. "So whatever you're moving, you're moving it soon, or not at all."

"I need to pass the second checkpoint."

Everyone needed to pass the second checkpoint. It sat at the narrows where the river bent toward the hills, in a converted customs house with new shutters and old stones, and it was not like the first checkpoint, which was bureaucracy wearing a uniform. The second checkpoint asked questions. The second checkpoint remembered. Couriers went in with clean papers and came out with their lives catalogued—their mothers' maiden names, their childhood illnesses, the color of the door on a house they'd sworn they'd never lived in—and the ones who came out changed came out quieter, and some didn't come out at all but were recorded as having traveled on, destinations various, signatures neat.

"They say a woman runs it now," she said carefully.

"They say right. Oryl." He said the name the way you name a current that has drowned good swimmers. "She'll talk to you for an hour about your grandmother and mean none of it, and then ask you what your father kept in his barn, and mean all of it. Papers don't get you past Oryl. Papers just get you into the room."

"So I've heard." She turned the cup in her hands. "I'm told you know the water route. Under the bluff, where the checkpoint can't see."

"I know a lot of routes."

"I'm told you owe a debt to a woman who once carried you across this river when the whole republic wanted you dead, and that she never named a price, and that you swore—" She stopped. Recalibrated. He watched her decide how much of the blade to show. "I'm told you're a man who pays what he owes."

The stove ticked. Outside, the ice groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a door opening very slowly in a house everyone thought was empty.

Tomas set down his own cup. "Who told you all this?"

"A friend of my mother's."

"Your mother." He let the words sit. "And what does your mother say about me?"

The pause was half a heartbeat too long, and in that half-heartbeat Tomas Reyes learned more than any interrogation would have given him. She looked at the tea. She looked at the stove. Everything about her said *my mother sent me*, and nothing about her said it truthfully, and the grief underneath the performance was not performed at all.

"She says you're honest," Elodie Blackwood said—for of course he had already guessed, had guessed from the moment she said *debt*, because there was only one woman on either bank whose debts were spoken of in that tone, only one woman who had vanished twelve years ago leaving behind a daughter with her jaw and her stillness and her terrible competence at being unseen. "She says you keep your word even when it costs."

"Your mother," Tomas said slowly, "is a careful liar. It's why she's alive. Or was." He watched the flinch land and hated himself for aiming it, and hated himself more for needing to. Because that was the other thing he'd learned in the half-heartbeat: the girl didn't know where her mother was either. Twelve years, and the daughter was still walking around inside the silence the mother had built, treating its walls as shelter.

He should send her away. That was the sensible thing, the surviving thing. A courier off the diplomatic pouches, in the last winter of a dying republic, asking a smuggler to move something past Madame Oryl—there was no version of that story that ended with Tomas Reyes dying warm in his bed at a great age. Every instinct he owned, instincts refined by two decades of living precisely because he trusted them, said: give her tea, give her lies, give her the road and a blessing.

But he had stood on this bank twenty years ago with river water in his lungs and a price on his head, and a woman had knelt in a leaking boat and held his head above the waterline and said, *I don't need to know what you did. I just need you to breathe.* And she had never asked. In all the years after, in the letters that came unsigned, in the single meeting at a market stall where they spoke of fish and nothing else, she had never once asked what the republic had wanted him for. She had carried him and asked for nothing, which meant the debt had no bottom, which meant it could be paid only forward, to whoever came carrying her stillness and her jaw.

"Show me what you're moving," he said.

Her arms tightened around the satchel. "It's sealed."

"Everything's sealed. Sealed is how the powerful talk to each other. Unsealed is how the dead talk to the living." He held out his hand, palm up, an old gesture from an older trade. "I don't read it. I look at the size of it. Size tells me rope, tells me oilcloth, tells me whether we swim or walk. That's all."

For a long moment she weighed it—he could see the weighing, the scales behind her eyes—and then she loosened the strap just enough to tilt the bag toward him, showing the outline of a letter case, flat, perhaps the length of a forearm, wrapped in oilskin already.

"Light," he said. "Good. Light means fast." He stood, went to the corner, and began pulling out the things he'd hoped not to need this year: the long pole with the iron shoe, the second lantern with the shuttered slit, the cork vest that smelled of twenty years of river. "We go tomorrow night, not tonight. Tonight the moon sets late and Oryl's night watch drinks early but not early enough. Tomorrow there's cloud coming up from the south, and the ice near the bluff will hold one more week. Barely. We walk the shelf under the bluff where the checkpoint's lamps don't reach, and we come up past the alder stand, and from there you're a traveler on the north road with clean papers and a boring face, and I was never born."

"And you'll do this," she said. Not quite a question. Checking the arithmetic of it, the same way he would have.

"I'll do this." He didn't turn around. "Because I owed your mother more than I can say, and because she'd have done it for you without blinking, and because—" He stopped himself before the third reason could get out. The third reason was that the girl looked like a person standing on ice that was already singing, and he had spent his whole life hearing that sound before anyone else did.

When he turned back she was watching him with those level eyes, and he understood suddenly that she was doing to him what he had done to her: reading the shadow, weighing the load, deciding how much of the blade to show. Whatever she carried in that oilskin, it wasn't routine dispatches. Routine didn't make hands tighten like that. Routine didn't make a trained courier flinch at a mother's name.

He didn't ask. That was the arrangement, the ancient one, the only one that kept smugglers and couriers both alive: *I don't need to know what you did. I just need you to breathe.*

"One condition," he said.

"Name it."

"When we're on the ice, you do exactly what I say, when I say it, even if it sounds mad. Especially then. The river doesn't argue and neither will you." He picked up the cork vest and tossed it to her; she caught it against the satchel, staggering slightly, the first ungraceful thing he'd seen her do. "Try that for size. And eat something—you look like a heron."

She almost smiled again, and this time it got further before discipline caught it. "Thank you," she said, and the words came out rough, as if they'd been stored a long time in a dry place.

"Don't thank me. Thank a woman who isn't here." He busied himself with the stove, with the pot, with anything that wasn't her face. "Sleep by the fire. There's a blanket that smells like fish. All my blankets smell like fish. It's a professional hazard."

She settled onto the bench, satchel still in her lap, and within minutes—trained, that too, sleeping on command the way soldiers and couriers do—her breathing slowed and deepened.

Tomas sat up alone for a long while, listening to the river groan beneath the ice, and thought about a woman in a leaking boat twenty years gone, and about daughters who inherit silences like furniture, and about the letter-shaped weight in the oilskin that the girl guarded like a wound.

Whatever it was, it was heavy in every way but the one he'd measured. And tomorrow night he would carry it under the bluff, past the house of questions, and he would still not know what it was.

He only knew, with the certainty of a man who had heard the ice singing since childhood, that before the thaw came, that letter was going to break something—and that he had just agreed to be standing nearby when it did.
