## 8. Second Checkpoint: The House of Questions

The second checkpoint was not a barrier but a house.

That was the first thing wrong with it. Checkpoints were supposed to be barriers—timber gates and sandbags and bored soldiers with chapped hands, like the one at Marrow Ford where Brannig had kept her waiting through the whole gray length of an afternoon. Barriers you could understand. A barrier wanted only your papers and your patience. But this building sat at the bend of the river road like something that had once been loved: a two-story farmhouse with a slate roof going green at the eaves, geraniums dead in their window boxes, a sign over the door that read *CUSTOMS AND TRANSIT — SECTOR NINE* in letters so official they looked embarrassed to be there. Smoke rose from the chimney in a thin, disciplined line. Someone was keeping order even here, in the middle of a country coming apart like wet paper.

Elodie stopped on the road and let herself feel what she felt, briefly, privately, the way she allowed herself all luxuries: three seconds of dread, no more.

She had heard about this place. Every courier had. They called it the House of Questions, and they said its name the way sailors said the name of a reef—not because saying it would help, but because not saying it felt like tempting something.

*Madame Oryl*, the whispers went. *She remembers everything.*

Elodie adjusted the strap of her satchel, touched the letter where it lay flat against her ribs inside her coat—Vasseur's letter, sealed with the unknown crest, warm now from her own body as though it were a living thing she carried—and walked up the path between the frozen flowerbeds.

A soldier took her papers at the door without looking at her face, which she understood immediately to be theater. The real examination would happen elsewhere, by someone else, in a room designed for it.

"Wait," he said, and she waited.

* * *

The room had been a parlor once. She could tell by the proportions, by the ghost-marks on the wallpaper where a mirror and two framed pictures had hung until someone decided sentiment was contraband. Now there was a plain table, two chairs, a stove ticking quietly to itself, and a lamp turned up brighter than any parlor needed to be. Brightness was a technique. Elodie knew that much. Brightness made people squint, and squinting made faces look guilty.

Madame Oryl sat behind the table writing in a ledger, and did not look up for a full minute.

Elodie used the minute. Oryl was perhaps fifty-five, dressed in civilian gray with a military greatcoat over it, buttoned to the throat, as if she could not decide which government she belonged to and had chosen to belong to both. Her hair was pinned with mathematical precision. Her hands were remarkable—long, unhurried, the hands of a woman who played an instrument or had once intended to—and they moved across the ledger's columns with the calm of someone copying out scripture. On the table beside the ledger lay a small dish of candied ginger and a glass of water, untouched. Everything about her said: *I have done this ten thousand times and I will do it ten thousand more, and I am in no hurry whatsoever, because hurry is a weakness and I do not have weaknesses.*

"Sit down, please," Oryl said, still writing.

Elodie sat.

"You are Courier Blackwood." It was not a question, which meant somewhere ahead of her, her arrival had been announced—or her file had traveled faster than she had. "E. Blackwood. Diplomatic service, Office of Correspondence." Now Oryl looked up, and her eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, the eyes of winter water over stone. "You came through Marrow Ford yesterday."

"I did."

"And before that?"

"The capital."

"Yes. Obviously." A faint movement at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile; a smile's blueprint. "I mean to say, you departed the capital on the fourteenth. You reached Marrow Ford on the sixteenth. Two days for a distance that a fit courier covers in one." She turned the ledger around so Elodie could see the column of figures, neat as arithmetic homework. "You lost a day somewhere, Courier Blackwood. Where did you lose it?"

There it was. Not the letter, not yet—the letter was the treasure at the center of the maze, and Oryl was too good to go straight at a treasure. She would start with time, with weather, with the texture of a road. She would build a floor of small verifiable facts and then see whether everything else stood on it.

"A young officer named Brannig held me at Marrow Ford," Elodie said. "Questions about my transit seal. There was a discrepancy in the date stamp—he thought it had been altered. It hadn't. He copied my papers twice and sent a runner to verify the office registration number. I waited most of the afternoon."

"All true," Oryl said mildly. "I have Brannig's report here. He describes you as—" she consulted a card, though Elodie would have bet her life she needed no consultation— "'cooperative to the point of tedium.' His word. Tedium." She set the card down. "He also writes that you volunteered information he did not ask for. The names of the ministries that signed your pouch. The route you intended to take north. The fact that you had not eaten since morning."

"He seemed nervous," Elodie said. "New at the work. I find that when people are new and nervous, giving them facts calms them. Facts are like stones—they can hold on to them."

"Mm." Oryl poured water into a second glass and pushed it across the table. An offering, or a test—did the courier drink from a stranger's glass? Elodie drank. Refusal would have been a data point, and she refused to be one. "And are you nervous, Courier Blackwood?"

"No, madame."

"Liar," Oryl said pleasantly, and wrote something down.

* * *

The interview went on for two hours.

It went on through the afternoon light failing in the frost-ferned windows, through the soldier bringing in a lamp and another log for the stove, through a plate of bread and hard cheese that Oryl pushed toward her with the same gesture she'd used for the water—*eat, drink, relax, show me who you are when you think you're comfortable*. Elodie ate. She answered questions about her childhood in the northern valleys, about her schooling, about the year the river froze early and the ferries stopped running. She answered questions about Vasseur—his habits, his voice, whether he drank, whether his hands shook.

That last one she felt like a hook going in under the skin.

"His hands?" she repeated.

"When he handles documents," Oryl said, watching her with those winter-water eyes. "Do they shake? We hear things, in my profession. Rumors travel faster than couriers. One hears that the old man's nerves are failing. That the Office of Correspondence is a shell with one lamp lit in it. Is that your experience?"

"It's cold in the capital," Elodie said. "Everyone's hands shake in the capital. Mine do, when I've been riding all day. You hold the reins and then you can't hold a spoon."

Oryl wrote that down too. Elodie understood, with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, that everything she said was being written down, and that the writing was not the point—the pauses were the point, the hesitations, the places where a sentence changed direction halfway through. Oryl was not recording answers. She was recording the shape of a mind under pressure, building a model of it, and someday—perhaps today, perhaps next month—she would compare the model against a single sentence spoken out of turn, and the difference between them would be the truth.

So Elodie gave her a flood of harmless truths.

This was the old discipline, the first thing she had ever learned, back when she was eleven years old and learning why her mother had left before dawn and come home after dark and never explained either: *if you hide one thing, hide it inside twenty.* A person guarding a single secret holds it visibly, the way a fist holds a coin. But a person full of small open truths—who offers them freely, who volunteers the names of ministries and the state of the roads and the quality of the cheese—is a person whose silences have nowhere to stand out. Bury the dangerous thing under ordinary things. Make yourself forgettable. Make yourself tedious.

Brannig had written *tedious*. She decided to earn the word again.

She told Oryl about the inn at Marrow Ford, how the taproom smelled of tallow and wet wool, how the innkeeper's wife had argued politics with a carter until both of them were shouting and neither was listening. She told her about a lame mule on the river road, about a shrine at a crossroads where travelers had left ribbon offerings until the ribbons froze into a single stiff bouquet. None of it mattered. All of it was true. Truth was the cheapest commodity she owned and she spent it like a drunkard spends wages.

Oryl listened to all of it, wrote down a fraction of it, and asked, at intervals, with the gentleness of a physician:

"And your family, Courier Blackwood? You come from the north. Do you expect to visit them, on this trip?"

"My family is scattered," Elodie said, and this too was true, and the truth of it hurt in a way she hoped did not reach her face. "An uncle farming outside Verchères. An aunt in the capital we don't speak of. My mother—" she paused, precisely, the pause of a woman deciding how much grief to spend— "died some years ago. Or vanished. In the north those words are sometimes the same word."

"I'm sorry," Oryl said, and Elodie believed she meant it, which was somehow worse.

"And your father?"

"Never known." True. "My mother never spoke of him." Also true. Also a wall built of true stones.

Oryl set down her pen. For the first time she leaned back in her chair, and the lamplight moved across her face and made her look, briefly, tired—a woman in a dead parlor in a dying sector, keeping ledgers for a republic that had already stopped answering its own letters.

"Do you know what I collect, Courier Blackwood?" she asked.

"Candied ginger," Elodie said, nodding at the untouched dish.

Something flickered in the gray eyes—surprise, quickly filed away, possibly approval. "Ginger. Yes. And also mistakes." She folded her hands. "In twelve years at this table I have interviewed nine hundred and forty couriers. I remember every one. Not their names—I forget names deliberately, names are noise—but their mistakes. The man who said he'd ridden from the capital in one day when the bridge at Saint-Aubin was out, and no rider alive could have made the ferry crossing. The woman whose boots were caked with red clay that exists nowhere between here and the border except a single valley she claimed never to have entered. Small things. Stones out of place." She tilted her head. "Your stones are all in place, Courier Blackwood. Your roads, your days, your innkeepers, your mule. It is the most orderly account I have heard in years."

"Thank you, madame."

"That," said Oryl, "is what troubles me."

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked, a soldier shifting his weight on watch. Elodie kept her face still and her breathing slow and thought: *she knows. She doesn't know what, but she knows that something is buried, because nothing is buried deep enough for a woman who has dug nine hundred and forty graves.*

"In my experience," Oryl went on, conversationally, "only two kinds of traveler give an account with no loose stones. The first kind has nothing to hide, and they are rare, because everyone hides something—shame, fear, a love affair, a debt. Everyone. The second kind has practiced." She picked up her pen again, examined the nib as though it interested her. "Practiced accounts have a particular music. Too smooth. Like a road graded by engineers. You hear it, and you think: someone built this road on purpose. Someone wants me to travel it quickly and not look at the ditches."

"What would you like me to say, madame?"

"Nothing." Oryl smiled, fully this time, and the smile was the coldest thing in the room. "I would like you to say nothing at all, forever, and I would like to know what the nothing contains. But the republic is short of everything now, including time, and my orders are to move diplomatic pouches north without obstruction." She opened the ledger, drew a line beneath the day's entries, and stamped the page with a seal that thumped like a heartbeat. "Your papers are in order. Your account is in order. You may continue north in the morning—the ferry won't run before daylight, and I don't send travelers onto the ice after dark, whatever their business."

Elodie rose. Her legs were stiffer than she'd realized; two hours of stillness had turned her muscles to rope.

"Courier Blackwood." She was at the door when Oryl spoke again, and she did not turn around, because turning around would have been a stone out of place. "One question more. Off the record—it will not appear in any ledger." A pause long enough to cross a frozen river. "When you steamed open your letter at the Ferryman's Rest last night—and do note that I said *when*, not *whether*—what did it tell you?"

The cold went through Elodie like the ice going out in spring, all at once, everywhere.

She had been careful. She had boiled the water in a covered pot, worked the blade flat along the seam, dried the paper flat under her mattress, resealed it with wax bought from the innkeeper and blended with ash to match. She had checked the seal against the lamplight three times. No one had seen. No one could have seen.

But Madame Oryl collected mistakes, and Elodie understood suddenly that the mistake might not have been hers. Wax was sold in every market town. Ash was in every grate. A woman who remembered everything would know exactly what a resealed diplomatic letter looked like, because she had probably reseal
ed a few herself—or watched others try—and the knowledge lived in her the way riverbeds live under ice, invisible and exact.

Every possible answer stood in Elodie's mouth and none of them were safe. Denial was a stone out of place—Oryl had said *when*, and denying the *when* confirmed everything around it. Confession was death by degrees. So Elodie chose the third road, the oldest one she knew, and let silence do the work while her mind ran the ridgeline of options—and found, at the end of it, only one answer that a courier trained never to read what she carries could give without breaking character or breaking herself.

She turned around slowly, and let confusion into her face—not performed innocence, which Oryl would smell in a heartbeat, but genuine bewilderment, which required no performance at all, because Elodie genuinely did not know how the woman across the table could possibly know what she knew.

"Madame," she said, "I delivered a sealed pouch through your checkpoint with its seal intact. If someone along this road has been opening the republic's letters, I'd want to know who, before I carry another one."

Oryl regarded her for a long moment. Outside, the wind had come up off the river, and the dead geraniums rattled in their boxes like small dry bones.

"There she is," Oryl said softly, almost to herself. Almost with admiration. "Nine hundred and forty-one." She waved one long hand toward the stairs. "Go and sleep, Courier Blackwood. The ferry leaves at first light. And when you reach the third checkpoint—" the gray eyes held hers, mild and bottomless— "give Captain Verel my regards. Tell him Madame Oryl says the ice is thinner than it looks this year. He'll know what I mean."

Elodie climbed the stairs to the bare room they gave her, lay down on the narrow bed without undressing, and put her hand flat against her coat, against the letter sleeping over her heart.

The thaw was early. Oryl had said so without saying it. *The ice is thinner than it looks*—a warning, or a threat, or simply a fact offered the way she offered everything: to see what Elodie would do with it.

Through the frost-ferned window, the river lay black and white under the rising moon, and somewhere far downstream, past the dark miles and the third checkpoint, spring was already moving underneath it, patient as an interrogator, waiting for the surface to give way.
