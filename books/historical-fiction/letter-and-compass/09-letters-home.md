## 9. Letters Home

Tomas Reyes had carried a great many things across the river in his life—brandy in fish barrels, rifles under false floors, a bishop once, though that was a story for warmer weather—and he had learned early that cargo told you what it was by how it behaved. Bricks sat still. Fugitives breathed too carefully. And couriers, in his experience, watched everything and admitted nothing, which was precisely what the woman calling herself Madame Lefevre was doing now, three days out from Oryl's checkpoint, sitting in the stern of his boat with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral.

Except they weren't on water yet. The river was still ice, hard enough to walk, not yet soft enough to trust, which was why he was running this route at all: the smugglers' season, the narrow weeks when the ice held and the patrols grew lazy because nothing moved in winter that couldn't be shot where it stood. He poled the sledge along the eastern channel where his father had run it before him, and behind him the courier sat wrapped in gray wool, being forgettable.

She was bad at it, was the thing. She thought she was good—she had the flat voice, the unremarkable face she wore like a coat—but Tomas had grown up reading people the way other men read weather, and there were tells. She counted things. Bridge arches, chimney smoke, soldiers at the second checkpoint's far gate. Her lips barely moved when she did it, but they moved. A woman memorizing routes wasn't carrying letters; a woman carrying letters didn't need to count anything.

And she'd looked at him, back at the ferry landing, one heartbeat too long, when he'd mentioned—in passing, carelessly, the way you drop bait through a hole in the ice—that he ran cargo upriver every winter, same as always.

*Same as always.* As if she knew what always meant. As if someone had told her.

He kept his eyes on the channel and said nothing, because asking questions on the river was like drilling holes in the hull: each one seemed small until the water came in.

* * *

They made camp that night in a stand of black pines above an abandoned lime kiln, one of a hundred ruins the war had salted along the banks and nobody had bothered to reclaim. Tomas got a fire going small and low, the way you do when smoke is a confession, and shared out bread and hard sausage and a bottle of plum brandy that had crossed this river four times before it reached his hands.

The courier ate neatly, thanked him politely, and then did something no real courier would ever do.

She took out paper.

He watched from the corner of his eye while pretending to study the fire. She'd found a pencil stub somewhere—he hadn't seen her buy so much as a match—and she sat with the paper on her knee, writing by firelight, stopping, crossing out, beginning again. Not dispatches. Dispatches were copied clean, no crossings-out, no second thoughts. This was something else. This was a letter, and she was writing it the way people write to people they love and have failed: slowly, badly, with long pauses in which she stared into the flames like a woman looking down a well.

Twice she glanced at him to see if he was watching. Both times he was watching the fire with the studied innocence of a man who had never noticed another human being in his life. Both times, when her gaze slid away, he looked straight back at her.

On the third sheet—the first two had gone into the fire already, curling and flashing and gone—she finally wrote something she didn't cross out. He saw her fold it, saw her hesitate over the fold, saw her look around for a way to send it, and find none, because of course there was none. There was no post on the river road in winter. There was no post anywhere worth trusting. That was half the reason people like him existed.

She sat holding the letter for a long moment. Then she fed it to the fire.

It went up fast, the way paper does, a bright bloom and a scatter of ash, and she watched it burn with an expression Tomas recognized because he had worn it himself, once, on this same river, watching a different fire a long time ago. It was the expression of a person mailing something by flame because there was no address left they dared write down.

"You burned it," he said, before he could decide not to.

"It was nothing."

"Nothing doesn't take three drafts." He passed her the brandy. She took it—good instinct, refusing would have been worse—and drank a little. "Nothing doesn't get written at all, usually. People who have nothing to say stay quiet. You've been quiet all day like a kettle building up to whistle."

"And you've been talkative all day," she said, "like a man filling silence so nobody notices what he's listening for."

He laughed despite himself—a short bark of a laugh, surprised out of him. Fair. Fair enough. The wind moved through the pines and the kiln behind them stood against the stars like a broken tooth, and for a while neither of them said anything, and it was almost companionable, the silence of two people who had both decided the other was dangerous and interesting in about equal measure.

"Madame Lefevre," he said at last, tasting the name. "That's a river-town name. Lefevres used to keep the ferry at Vasseau Mill, before the tolls." He turned to look at her properly. "You're not from there."

"No."

"No," he agreed. "And you're not a courier, or not only one. Couriers don't write letters home. Couriers *are* letters. You—" he gestured at the ash, the little gray ghosts of three drafts stirring in the heat "—you're somebody's daughter is what you are. Somebody who taught you to be forgettable, and taught you well, and isn't here anymore to see how well."

The fire popped. Somewhere downstream the ice groaned, a long whale-song moan that traveled through the ground and up through the bones, the sound the river made when it dreamed of spring. He saw her hand go, briefly, unconsciously, to the breast of her coat—to whatever slept there—and come away again.

"You know who I am," she said quietly. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know who your mother was," Tomas said.

That landed. He watched it land—watched the stillness come over her, the particular stillness of a hunted animal deciding whether the sound it heard was wind or wire. Twelve years, he thought. Twelve years since a woman with a canvas satchel and a laugh like a struck bell had stood on his father's deck, and the girl in front of him had that same stillness, that same way of going absolutely quiet when the truth got close, as if stillness were armor.

"How?" she said. Just that. One syllable, spent like the last coin in a purse.

"Because I owed her my life," Tomas said, "and debts are a kind of memory that doesn't fade."

* * *

He hadn't meant to tell her. He had a rule about it, actually, one of his few rules: never tell anyone what their people did for you, because gratitude makes people careless, and careless people drown, and sometimes they drag you under with them. But the fire was warm and the night was long and she had burned a letter home in front of him, which was its own kind of confession, and rules, Tomas had learned, were mostly just stories you told yourself about the man you intended to be.

"She came through in the autumn of the year the bridges burned," he said. "You'd have been—what? Six? Seven? Too young to remember, or maybe you remember and pretend you don't. Doesn't matter. I was nineteen and stupid and I'd done something brave and idiotic for the union men in the mill towns—carried names, same trade as yours, except I was bad at it. They took my contact. They didn't get me, but they got his ledger, and my name was in it, and by morning there were two men asking after me at every landing from the weir to the sea."

He turned the brandy bottle in his hands, watching the firelight swim in the dark glass.

"My father wouldn't run me. Not because he was a coward—my father feared God and the customs men in roughly equal measure, and neither of them much—but because running me meant losing the boat if we were taken, and the boat was the family. So I went to the one person everyone said could move a man north through three checkpoints without a paper to her name. A woman. Alone. With a canvas satchel and a smile like she knew something funny about the whole republic."

"What did she do?"

"What any good courier does. She made me luggage." He smiled at the old absurdity of it, the smile arriving unbidden across thirty years and a frozen river. "I spent nine days dressed as her late husband's grieving brother, traveling north for the burial, and I grieved, let me tell you—I wept at the right moments, I stared at middle distances, I developed a limp. She coached me in it like a director. 'Again,' she'd say, 'but this time sadder.' By the fourth checkpoint I wasn't acting. She'd talked to me so long about grief—about how it moves in a body, how it takes a man's voice down into his chest—that I'd dug up real ones to work with. My mother, dead two years. She built my mourning out of true things. That was her gift. She never lied, exactly. She just arranged the truth until it pointed where she needed."

The courier was very still. In the firelight her face had the quality of a portrait—someone trying to hold an expression steady while it changed underneath.

"That sounds like her," she said at last, and her voice was careful the way ice is careful.

"She got me to the northern office. She got me past a captain who was later shot for letting her through, though not before she'd charmed the boots off him. And when we parted she refused payment." He shook his head at it even now, the sheer insolent arithmetic of it. "Refused. I had money sewn in my coat, real money, and she looked at it like I'd offered her a dead fish. 'You'll pay me,' she said, 'by doing for somebody else what I did for you, someday, when it costs you something.' Those words. I've had thirty years to forget them and I can't."

The wind shifted. Sparks lifted off the fire and climbed, red points ascending into the black between the pines, like sparks always did, like prayers or embers or letters with nowhere to go.

"So when a gray little courier hired my boat last week," Tomas said, "and flinched—flinched, mind you, just the smallest flinch, a hair's width—when I said *same route as always*, I started wondering whose daughter rode my deck wearing her mother's stillness. And then you wrote a letter home and burned it, and there aren't many families on this river a courier would need to warn and couldn't." He met her eyes. "The Blackwoods. North fork. The farmhouse with the crooked barn. Am I close?"

For a long moment she said nothing, and the river groaned again beneath them, nearer now, or maybe just more insistent, the thaw talking in its sleep. When she finally spoke, it was barely above the fire's murmur.

"If you know that," Elodie Blackwood said, "then you know why you should put me off at the next landing and forget my face."

"I know why I *could*," Tomas said. "I've been knowing it for three days. Put you off, paddle home, tell myself the republic's affairs are the republic's affair." He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "But I owe your mother a debt, madame, and I've discovered something about debts in my old age: you don't get to choose what form they come due in. She told me to help people when it costs something. Well." He spread his hands, taking in the frozen river, the ruined kiln, the thin gray light of a country coming apart at its seams. "Here I am. It costs something. Consider it paid."

She looked at him for a long time—not the courier's flickering assessment, but something older and rawer, the look of a woman standing in a room she thought was empty and finding it full. Then she reached into her coat, and his hand moved toward the knife in his boot before his mind caught up, but what she drew out was not a weapon. It was a brass compass, old, dented, its glass clouded, its needle trembling inside like a captive bird.

"This was hers," Elodie said.

Tomas felt something turn over in his chest, slow and heavy, like ice shifting on the river.

"I know," he said. "She showed it to me. Nine days on a boat, you learn a person's pockets. She said it didn't point north anymore—said it pointed wherever it damn well pleased, and that she trusted it anyway." He looked at the small tarnished thing in the woman's palm, the needle wavering between directions, settling on neither. "It's still broken?"

"It was never broken," Elodie said quietly. "It's just honest."

The fire burned down between them. Neither of them moved to feed it. And it was there, in that failing orange light, with the ash of three burned letters still stirring and the river muttering underneath the ice like something counting the days, that Elodie Blackwood opened her mouth to tell him what she carried—Tomas could see it gathering in her face like weather—and stopped.

Because downriver, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that froze them both where they sat.

Hooves. Many of them. Coming fast along the western bank, toward the third checkpoint, in the dead of night, in the wrong season, in a hurry.
