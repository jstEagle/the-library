## 12. The Forgery

The printer's shop smelled of ink and cold iron, the way a church smells of wax and old prayer—like a place where words had been made holy for so long that the holiness had soaked into the walls. Elodie stood in the doorway while Tomas worked the lock on the back door with a pick he kept rolled in his sleeve, and she tried to remember when she had become a person who stood watch outside dark buildings.

Twelve days ago she had been a courier who never read what she carried. Now she was a forger. The distance between those two women was not twelve days; it was her whole life, collapsed into a single terrible step she could not unstep.

"In," Tomas said softly. "Before the lamplighter makes his rounds."

* * *

The shop belonged to a man named Pellin, or had belonged to him; the press sat under a sheet of sacking like a sleeping animal, and dust lay on the composing stone thick enough to write in. Tomas had found it three nights earlier while scouting the river road—a village called Saint-Aubin-sur-Glace, half its houses shuttered, its printer fled south with the last of the republic's money and none of his type. The letters were still here, sorted into their cases, waiting for hands that would never come back for them.

Tomas lit a single lamp and turned the wick low. "We have until the moon comes up over the mill roof. After that, anyone on the road can see light in these windows."

Elodie set her satchel on the composing stone and unwrapped the letter for the fourth time since she had read it in the inn at Varennes. She knew its contents now the way she knew her own hands—the names, the dates, the order of arrest to be executed *when the ice breaks*, as if the river itself were an accomplice, as if spring were a legal instrument. Her uncle Aldric. Her aunt Maren. Her cousin Ivet, who was nineteen and had never in her life finished a sentence without apologizing for it.

"You've read it how many times?" Tomas asked.

"Enough."

"Enough to recite it?"

"Enough to know which parts matter." She smoothed the page flat. "The seal is a double eagle under a crown of wheat—I've never seen it before, but whoever receives this will have. The hand is formal, ministerial, someone writing in haste but trained in patience. The date is written out in words, not numerals. And there—" she touched the bottom edge, where a faint diagonal crease crossed the paper, "—it was folded in thirds and carried in a coat lining. Someone else brought it partway before Vasseur gave it to me. It has already been on one journey."

Tomas studied her across the lamplight. He had a way of looking at her that made her feel mapped, charted, every coastline named. "You notice everything," he said. "That's what frightens you about this, isn't it? Not the lying. That you'll be good at it."

She did not answer, because the answer was yes.

* * *

They began with the paper. The original was a heavy stock with a faint watermark—a wheat sheaf visible only when held against flame—and Tomas had spent two evenings of their journey pressing sheets between boards with paste and pulp scraped from the flyleaves of ruined books, until he produced something close enough that close would have to do. "Paper forgery is a trade," he said, working the pulp with his thumbs like a man kneading bread. "My mother did it during the border wars. She said the secret isn't making it perfect. It's making it imperfect in the right places."

"Imperfect in the right places," Elodie repeated.

"Every honest document has flaws. A too-perfect letter screams louder than a bad one. So we leave a smudge. We let the ink pool once in a descender. We make something a tired clerk in a northern office would believe because it looks exactly like everything else he's ever believed."

The type cases were organized by face, and Elodie ran her fingers along the rows of brass letters, each one tiny and cold, each one carrying its own small history of things printed and sent into the world. Somewhere in these cases were the letters that had composed proclamations, death notices, wedding announcements, lies. Type did not judge. That was its mercy and its horror.

"We'll need to match the hand," she said. "But we're printing, not writing. The original is manuscript."

"I know." Tomas pulled a stool up beside her. "So here's what we do instead. We print the body of the letter—we compose it fresh, same wording, same order of arrest—but we print it as a copy. Look." He turned the original toward the lamp and pointed to a line near the top: *By authority vested under seal of the Provisional Directorate.* "A copyist reproducing an order for transmission north would add a line like this. *True copy, taken at such-and-such office.* It explains any small differences. It explains the different paper. It gives your northern recipient a reason not to compare it against anything."

"And the signature?"

"That we trace. Tonight, by lamplight, with your hand. You're the one who's memorized it."

Elodie looked down at the original signature—a cramped, forceful name, *Corvel*, with a flourish that cut through the final consonant like a blade through rope. She thought of the man whose hand it was. She would never meet him. He had signed an order for the destruction of her family with the casual violence of all men who sign things, and now she would hold his pen-name in her fingers and make it say something else entirely.

"It feels like witchcraft," she said quietly. "Taking a man's name and making it mean the opposite of what he meant."

"It isn't witchcraft," Tomas said. "It's politics. Half the world runs on it. The other half just hasn't figured that out yet."

* * *

The composing took four hours. Elodie read the original aloud, phrase by phrase, while Tomas set type in the stick, and she discovered that speaking the letter's contents was worse than reading them. In her mouth, the sentences became orders given. *It is further directed that the subjects be apprehended without public process, the better to avoid sympathy among the rural districts.* Her voice cracked on *rural*. Tomas glanced up. "Say it flat," he told her. "Words are just shapes until someone puts blood in them. Don't put blood in them yet."

So she said them flat. She became, for those hours, a machine for transferring language from one surface to another, and she understood with a cold clarity that this was precisely what the diplomatic office had trained her to be—except that now she was turning the training inside out, using the discipline of the faithful courier to build a faithful lie.

The changes themselves were few, and they were surgical. Where the original named *the Blackwood family of Ferme des Saules, Aldric Blackwood principal conspirator*, the new text read *the persons resident at the property designated in Annex C, identities confirmed by the field officer*. Where the original listed three names with dates of birth and descriptions, the new text referred to a list *to follow under separate cover, delayed by weather*. Every reference to family, to blood, to the particular farmhouse where Ivet fed chickens every morning of her life—all of it dissolved into bureaucratic mist, pointing everywhere and therefore nowhere. The order still existed. The arrest would still be authorized. But the warrant would name shadows, and shadows could not be arrested, and by the time anyone in the north discovered that Annex C contained nothing but fog, the thaw would have come and gone and the roads would be closed forever.

"It protects everyone and convicts no one," Elodie said, reading the composed proof by candlelight. "Is that mercy or cowardice?"

"Yes," said Tomas.

* * *

They broke at some black hour before dawn to eat bread and hard cheese, sitting on the floor of the shop with their backs against the press, and Elodie found herself laughing—quietly, helplessly—at nothing, at the absurdity of two criminals picnicking in a dead man's print shop while they committed treason against a government that no longer existed well enough to prosecute them.

"What?" Tomas asked, though he was almost smiling himself.

"I used to think the hardest thing in the world was not knowing. Carrying sealed letters through checkpoints, never asking, never reading. I built my whole self around not-knowing. It felt like virtue." She turned the cheese rind in her fingers. "And it turns out the hardest thing is knowing, and choosing anyway. Every choice costs somebody. There's no clean hand in any of it."

"No," Tomas agreed. "There's only the question of whose dirt you're willing to wear." He was quiet a moment. "When my mother forged papers, she had a saying. *A lie is a bridge. You cross it, and then you burn it behind you, or it becomes the road you live on.*"

"What does that mean, for us?"

"It means you'd better decide, before this is done, whether this lie is a bridge or a road." He looked at her steadily. "Because the woman who walks into that northern office with a forged letter in her coat—that woman doesn't get to go back to being the courier who never reads what she carries. Whatever you're building yourself into, you'll still be her in the morning."

Elodie looked at her hands in the lamplight. They did not look different. They should have looked different. "I keep thinking about Vasseur," she said. "He gave me this letter knowing what it was. Knowing whose niece I am—or guessing. Maybe he wanted me to destroy it. Maybe he wanted me to deliver it and prove the office could still trust its people even at the end. Maybe he wanted me to do exactly this." She shook her head. "I can't tell if I'm disobeying him or completing his instructions, and it doesn't matter, because either way I'm the one holding the pen."

"That's the whole trick of power," Tomas said. "Making other people hold the pen."

"Then let me hold it," she said, and heard in her own voice something new—not the flat courier voice, not the frightened niece voice, but something harder and stranger, a voice she did not fully recognize as hers. "Let me hold it and write with it."

* * *

The tracing took longest of all. By the grey hour when the frost began to climb the windows, Elodie sat with the original signature before her and a printed copy beneath a sheet of thin paper, following the strokes of Corvel's name with a fine brush dipped in iron-gall ink, learning the rhythm of a stranger's hand. First pass: hesitant, wrong. Second pass: better. Fifth pass: she stopped watching the model and let the motion live in her wrist, the way her mother must once have let compass-bearing live in hers, knowledge passed from hand to hand down a chain of women and men who moved secretly through dangerous country.

On the eleventh attempt she signed the copy, and when she laid the two signatures side by side under the lamp, she could not tell which hand was which. Neither could Tomas. Neither, she thought, would God.

"There," Tomas said softly. "Now it's a true copy of a false letter, or a false copy of a true one, depending on which side of the river you're standing on." He blew out the lamp as the first light came up over the mill roof, and in the darkness the wet ink gleamed faintly, drying into permanence.

They cleaned the shop as best they could, restored the type to its cases, folded the false letter into thirds along the same diagonal crease as the original. The real letter—the true one, the murderous one—Elodie wrapped again in oilcloth and sewed into the lining of her coat beside the compass, where it lay against her ribs like a second heart, heavier than a heart ought to be. She could not burn it yet. Burning it required a stove, a reason, a moment alone; burning it required her to be certain, and certainty, she was learning, was not a thing you possessed but a thing you walked toward through the dark.

At the back door Tomas paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at the press under its shroud. "Pellin the printer," he said. "I left coin under the sacking. Enough to replace the paper and the ink. I'm a smuggler, not a thief."

"Does it help?" Elodie asked. "Leaving the coin?"

"It helps me sleep. Which is the only currency that matters anymore."

They stepped out into the blue cold of the false dawn. The village lay silent under frost, chimneys beginning to smoke, ordinary lives resuming around the extraordinary thing they had made in the night. Elodie buttoned her coat over both letters—the lie against her breastbone, the truth against her spine—and felt them there, and understood that from this moment forward she would always carry two versions of everything: what happened, and what needed to have happened.

They were a mile down the river road when Elodie saw the rider coming toward them through the frost-mist, sitting her horse with the perfect stillness of someone who had been waiting, and recognized the grey coat and patient eyes of Madame Oryl.
