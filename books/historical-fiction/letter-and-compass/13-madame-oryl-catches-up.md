## 13. Madame Oryl Catches Up

The road out of the valley climbed through pine country where the snow lay blue in the shadows and the morning smelled of resin and cold iron. Elodie walked with her hood down because a hooded figure invited questions and an uncovered one invited none—this was the first lesson of invisibility, learned at eleven years old from a woman who had then vanished into it herself. Beside her, Tomas Reyes led the cart horse by its bridle and said nothing, which was his way of saying many things.

They had been on the road two days since the storm broke and gave them back to the world. Two days since she had sat up half the night with printer's type pressed into warm wax and ink, building a letter that named no one, accused no one, condemned no one—a letter that was, if you held it to the light just so, a perfect instrument of mercy built entirely out of lies. It rode against her breastbone now in the diplomatic pouch, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom of her satchel, rode the original: the true letter with the seal she had never seen before this winter, the letter that ordered the arrest of her uncle and aunt and cousins when the river opened.

She had not yet decided what she would do with the original. She told herself she had decided—that she would burn it at the first safe stove—but the truth was that it sat in her satchel like a second heartbeat, and she could not make herself let it go. Destroying it felt like destroying evidence of a crime, and she could not yet name whose crime.

Tomas stopped walking. He did it without any drama, simply ceased moving, his hand tightening on the bridle until the horse snorted and stamped. Ahead, where the road narrowed between a granite outcrop and a stand of black firs, a small gray carriage stood at an angle, one wheel slightly off the crown of the road, unhurried, deliberate, as though it had been parked there since dawn by someone who knew exactly what time they would come.

A woman stood beside the carriage with her hands folded at her waist.

Elodie knew the silhouette before she knew the face. The upright stillness of it. The way the woman's coat was buttoned to the throat despite the climb, as if cold were something that happened to other people.

"Madame Oryl," Elodie said quietly.

"I see her." Tomas's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used for rivers in thaw. "Elodie. There are trees behind us. We could be gone before she—"

"No." Elodie heard how steady her own voice came out, and marveled at it, the way one marvels at a stranger's composure overheard through a wall. "If she wanted us taken, there'd be soldiers. She doesn't travel with soldiers unless she means to use them."

"How do you know?"

"Because she told me so herself. At the checkpoint. She said—" Elodie closed her eyes briefly, retrieving it exactly, because Oryl's words deserved exactness; everything about the woman did. "She said, *I never bring force to a conversation I intend to win.*"

Tomas looked at her. In the pale light his face showed every year of the river in it, every winter crossing, every debt. "And did she win?"

"I don't know," Elodie said. "That's what frightens me. I passed her interrogation. I've been telling myself I won it ever since."

* * *

Madame Oryl did not come toward them. That was the first thing. She remained beside her carriage with her hands folded while they approached, letting them close the distance themselves, making their approach a choice—and therefore something they would afterward have to account for. Elodie understood the technique. She had been inside the House of Questions once already; she recognized the architecture even standing outdoors, even with sky overhead. Oryl built rooms wherever she went.

"You're traveling lighter than you were," Oryl said when Elodie stopped an arm's length away. Her voice was mild, almost pleasant, the voice of a woman discussing weather or the price of oats. Her eyes moved past Elodie to Tomas, to the horse, to the cart, cataloguing, and then returned. "Two days ago you had a certain set to your shoulders. A weight. Now—" she tilted her head, birdlike, considering. "Now the weight has moved somewhere I can't see it. Which means it hasn't left you at all, has it? You've only hidden it better."

"Madame Oryl." Elodie inclined her head the precise degree protocol required. "You're far from your checkpoint."

"My checkpoint is a building. My work is wherever couriers are." Oryl unfolded her hands and gestured, almost graciously, toward a fallen log laid along the roadside like a bench, placed there years ago by some forester or pilgrim. "Sit. Please. This will take some time, and I dislike conducting long conversations with people who are calculating escape routes. Sitting makes people honest. It's why I keep chairs in my office."

"I'll stand."

"Yes," Oryl said, with something that might have been approval. "I thought you might. Very well. Then I'll stand too, and we shall both be dishonest together."

Tomas had drifted to the horse's head, close enough to intervene, far enough to seem uninvolved. Oryl glanced at him once—the glance of a woman confirming an entry already made in a ledger—and dismissed him. Elodie saw it land on Tomas like a blow anyway. Being dismissed by Madame Oryl, she suspected, felt worse than being noticed.

"The young man at the ferry," Oryl began, conversationally, "the one who took your cargo across the lake road—do you know he lied to me twice? Once about your destination and once about the weather. The weather, courier Blackwood. As though I cannot read a barometer." She sighed, genuinely, softly. "People lie to me constantly. Do you know what most of them don't understand? I don't need truth. Truth is merely the fastest route. I can arrive at anything by the slow roads too—it simply takes longer, and leaves more wreckage."

"What do you want from me?"

"Ah. Directness. Good. Direct couriers save everyone time." Oryl stepped closer—not threateningly; she never did anything that could be called threatening; she simply occupied space with the confidence of a person who had never once been struck. "Here is what I want. On the fourteenth of December, a diplomatic pouch passed my station bearing a seal I did not recognize. I have spent nineteen years learning the seals of this republic, mademoiselle—all of them, including the ones that pretend not to exist. This seal was new. New seals mean new committees, and new committees in this season of our history mean one thing only: men arranging the furniture before the house falls down."

She paused, giving Elodie room to speak. Elodie kept her face smooth and said nothing, and Oryl nodded as though the silence were itself a satisfactory answer.

"The pouch carried a letter. The courier who carried it—you—was interviewed by me for six hours and released. And here is what has occupied me since." Oryl's voice dropped, became almost intimate. "You answered forty-one questions. You volunteered nine truths I never asked for. Your pulse, when I mentioned the word *family*, rose by a margin I could feel through the table. And yet you passed, because your answers were all true. Every one. Harmless truths, but true. You buried the dangerous answer under honest ones, which is more sophisticated than anything my own interrogators manage, and I remember thinking at the time: *this girl was trained by someone excellent, or by grief.*"

The wind moved through the firs above them with a sound like slow water. Somewhere down-valley, faint as memory, came the crack of ice shifting on the river.

"Now," Oryl continued, "a week later, the same courier passes north again, carrying—so the northern office reports—a letter bearing the same seal. But courier, I have correspondents everywhere, and mine tell me the northern office received a document that orders no arrests. No names. An administrative circular, essentially. A letter about grain requisitions." She smiled then, and it was the worst smile Elodie had ever seen, because it held no cruelty whatsoever, only certainty, vast and patient as winter itself. "And I think: either the committee changed its mind between the fourteenth and the twenty-second—which committees never do—or the letter was replaced. And if it was replaced, then someone read it. And if someone read it, someone chose. And if someone chose—" she spread her hands, gently, offering the conclusion like a gift, "—then that someone is standing in front of me on a frozen road, refusing to sit down."

* * *

The silence stretched. Elodie was aware of everything with terrible clarity: the horse breathing, the creak of Tomas's glove on the bridle leather, a bead of meltwater gathering at the tip of a fir bough above Oryl's shoulder, trembling, refusing to fall.

Deny it. That was the discipline. Twelve years of silences said deny it, say the letter was always a circular, say the seal meant nothing, be forgettable, be gray, be the road itself. And she opened her mouth to do it—to build the wall of harmless truths one brick higher—

—and found she could not.

Perhaps it was the way Oryl waited, without triumph, without pressure, as though she had all the patience of the state and none of its appetite. Perhaps it was the compass against Elodie's ribs, sewn into her coat lining these twelve years, its needle lying still now but present, reminding her there were two directions. Or perhaps it was simpler and worse: perhaps she was tired, bone-tired, of being a corridor through which other people's secrets passed untouched. Perhaps some part of her had been waiting since the inn at Marrow Ford, since the steaming kettle and the curling seal, for someone to catch her—not to punish her. Just to catch her. To make it real.

"I read it," Elodie said.

Beside the horse, Tomas made a small sound, quickly silenced.

Oryl exhaled slowly, and something in her posture eased—not relaxation exactly, but the settling of a scale reaching balance. "Thank you," she said, and meant it, which was somehow more frightening than menace would have been. "Do you know, in nineteen years, fewer than ten people have said those words to me first? Everyone else requires extraction. Extraction damages the goods." She clasped her hands again. "Now. Tell me about the forgery. Where did you get type?"

"A printer in Vellacourt who owed Tomas money."

"And the wax?"

"Same candles as the original. I melted the old impression down and recast it."

"Clever. Imperfect—the eagle's wing is a hairline wrong, but no one at the northern office knows the seal well enough to notice, so it hardly matters." Oryl said this the way a teacher corrects arithmetic, without judgment. "And the original?"

Elodie's hand moved, without her permission, halfway toward her satchel strap. She caught herself. Oryl watched the motion with interest but no surprise.

"Still with you," Oryl observed. "Of course. Because destroying it would be a decision, and you haven't decided. You're waiting for the river to decide for you." She turned her face briefly toward the south, toward the water they had crossed and the capital beyond it, invisible under the hills. "It will be early this year, you know. The thaw. My correspondents say the ice is rotten at the bends already. Whatever you intend to do, you have days, not weeks."

"Why are you telling me this?" The question came out harder than Elodie intended, edged with something close to anger. "Why stop us on the road? Why not report the discrepancy, send riders, take the pouch at the third checkpoint? You could end this in an afternoon."

"Because," Oryl said, "of what the letter contains."

The wind dropped. The forest went very quiet, the particular hush of extreme cold.

"You haven't asked me what's in it," Elodie said carefully.

"I know what's in it. I've known since January, when a clerk in the Ministry of Interior drank too much at the Winter Ball and wept into his wine about *lists going north*. Names, courier. Arrests scheduled for the opening of navigation. Families." Oryl's voice stayed level, but something moved behind her eyes, deep and old, like current under ice. "I pulled the thread quietly. Quietly matters, now more than ever, because the men who wrote that letter are not the government—they are what remains when the government stops believing in itself, and they will not survive the spring, and neither, if we are careless, will anyone whose name appears on their paper."

"So arrest them. Confiscate it. You have the authority."

"I have a stamp," Oryl said dryly, "and a building full of clerks who update their wills monthly. Authority is a rumor this winter. No—I am here alone, with one proposition, and I will make it once, plainly, and then never mention it again." She stepped closer, near enough now that Elodie could see the fine lines around her eyes, the gray at her temples, the utter absence of threat in her stance. "Give me the names. Not the letter—the names. Tell me whom it condemns. I will see that the order is intercepted, misfiled, lost in the spring dissolution with ten thousand other papers. The family goes free. No one is arrested. No one disappears. The original comes to me and dies a bureaucratic death, which is the gentlest death a paper can have."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange for nothing." Oryl said it simply. "That is what you don't understand, courier. I am not cruel. Cruelty is inefficient. I have spent my life extracting truth from people because I believed—I still believe—that truth is the only mercy. Lies protect no one; they only postpone the cost and compound the interest. Your forgery is kind, and it is doomed. Sooner or later someone in the north compares dates, or finds the clerk's drunken ghost, or notices a circular about grain where an arrest order should be, and then the investigation begins backward, and backward leads here, to you, and through you to them—and by then it won't be me asking, and I promise you the ones who ask next do not offer bargains." She paused. "Truth, given freely now, while I am the one asking—that saves them. That is the entire argument. I offer it because it is correct, not because I enjoy it."

Elodie's heart was beating hard, and she hated it. Because the argument was good. That was the horror of it—Oryl's logic was clean and load-bearing, and every beam of it pointed at the same conclusion: give her the names, save the family, pay the smallest possible price. It was the same conclusion the needle of her mother's compass kept trembling between. Deliver and betray. Destroy and betray. And now a third direction, offered by a woman who genuinely believed she was holding out mercy.

"You want me to trust the republic," Elodie said quietly. "A republic that emptied its ministries, that loses people at checkpoints, that taught me at eleven years old which neighbors not to greet. You want me to hand you the only thing keeping my family alive and trust that your filing system outlasts the collapse."

"I want you to trust *me*," Oryl said. "Which is different, and I understand why you hesitate. I am not the republic. I am what happens to a person who serves it too long." For a moment—one moment, brief as the flash of sun off the carriage glass—she looked tired in a way that reached all the way down. "My daughter lives in Vellacourt, courier. I have not spoken her name aloud in six years because speaking it invites questions I cannot afford. I know precisely what silences cost. I am offering you a cheaper one."

Behind Elodie, Tomas spoke for the first time. "And if she refuses?"

Oryl looked at him, then back to Elodie, and her expression did not change at all, which was its own answer.

"If she refuses," Oryl said, "then I return to my carriage, and I drive south, and I file my report of this conversation—which will say that I questioned a courier on the river road and found nothing. Nothing, courier. I will not hunt you. I have neither the time nor, frankly, the heart. But I will not lie to you about what happens next either: the thaw comes, the letter sits in your satchel rotting your sleep, and the men who wrote it go on existing, and one day—perhaps not this spring, perhaps not for years—someone less patient than I am asks the question you refused to answer me today." She straightened her coat with a small precise motion, closing the conversation the way she opened doors: deliberately. "That is the future I can see from where I stand. I dislike it. But it is yours, and I will not pretend otherwise. Pretense is the one cruelty I refuse."

She turned toward the carriage. Stopped. Looked back once.

"For what it is worth—and I know it is worth little coming from me—you read the letter and you did not run. You did not abandon it and you did not obey it. You tried to build a third thing. Most people, given your choices, deliver or flee. You tried to forge mercy out of printer's type in a barn." Something almost soft crossed her face, and was gone. "Be careful, courier. People who can lie that well usually learn it by having been lied to magnificently."

The carriage door shut. The wheel crunched back onto the crown of the road. The gray carriage rolled south, unhurried, and dwindled between the firs, and was gone.

* * *

For a long time neither of them moved. The meltwater bead finally fell from the fir bough and struck the snow with a sound Elodie heard distinctly, absurdly, as though the world had turned up its volume to fill the silence Oryl left behind.

"She knew everything," Tomas said at last. His voice was rough. "The type. The wax. The clerk. Everything. And she just—drove away."

"She got what she came for."

"What, the names? You didn't give her—"

"Not the names." Elodie's hands had gone cold inside her gloves, and she realized she had been gripping the satchel strap hard enough to ache. "Me. She came to find out whether I'd break, and how, and whether I'd break alone. That's what she files away. That's what she trades in." She looked south, down the empty road. "And the worst part, Tomas—the part I'll be chewing for the rest of my life—is that I believe her. I believe she'd do it. I believe she'd lose that paper and save them. She's not lying. That's what makes it a temptation instead of a trap."

"So what do we do?" Tomas came to stand beside her, and his shoulder nearly touched hers, and she was grateful for the warmth of it. "We've got two days to the third checkpoint, maybe three. The ice at the crossings is going soft. And now we've got a state interrogator driving the other direction knowing exactly what we're carrying and choosing, for reasons I'll never fully understand, to let us carry it."

Elodie reached into the satchel and drew out the oilcloth bundle. She did not unwrap it. She only held it, feeling through the fabric the crisp weight of the true letter, the real names, the order that would turn her family's farmhouse into an empty place by summer.

"She said the thaw would be early," Elodie murmured. "She said we have days."

Down in the valley, faint and far, the river groaned—a long, deep sound, like something enormous turning over in its sleep, beginning to wake.

"We walk tonight," Elodie said. "While it's still frozen. We cross the ice road in the dark, before it decides for us."
