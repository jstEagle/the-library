## 14. What Loyalty Costs

Tomas Reyes had learned long ago that the worst arguments were not the loud ones. Loud arguments burned out like straw fires; you could stand in their ashes afterward and still recognize each other. The dangerous ones were quiet—the kind that started over a cup of coffee gone cold and ended with two people discovering they had been traveling with strangers all along.

This one began over coffee.

They had taken a room above a drover's tavern three miles short of the third checkpoint, a low-beamed place that smelled of tallow and wet wool, where the landlord asked no questions because asking questions was bad for business in a winter like this one. Outside, the storm had passed but its aftermath remained: a sky scoured to the color of pewter, drifts shouldering against the fences, and beneath everything, faint but unmistakable now, the sound of the river working at its chains.

Elodie sat across the table from him, mending a tear in her glove with a patience Tomas found almost offensive. That was the thing about her—she could sit with her hands busy while the world came apart, as if competence were a form of prayer. He had known smugglers, soldiers, priests of three different faiths, and none of them had her talent for stillness.

"You're going to say it," she said, without looking up. "So say it."

He set his cup down. "Oryl's offer stands until the thaw. You heard her. Give her the names, and your family walks free. No arrest. No lists. She keeps her word—she's not a woman who spends words cheaply."

"She's a woman who buys them," Elodie said. "There's a difference."

"Is there? From where I'm sitting, the difference is your uncle's neck."

The needle paused in the glove. Then it went on moving, steady as a clock. Tomas felt something twist in his chest—not anger exactly, though anger was in it somewhere. It was closer to the feeling of watching someone walk calmly toward ice he knew was thin.

"You think I haven't considered it," she said quietly.

"I think you've considered everything except surviving it." He leaned forward. "Listen to me. I've run this river for nineteen years. I've watched good people die for principles, and do you know what they bought with those principles? Nothing. A headstone with the wrong date on it, if they were lucky. Oryl is offering you the only currency left in this country—a guarantee. Names for lives. It's arithmetic, Elodie. Cold, ugly arithmetic, but arithmetic saves people. Principles just bury them."

"And what did arithmetic get you?"

The question landed harder than she probably intended—or perhaps she intended it fully. He couldn't tell anymore. Her face had that courier's smoothness, the one he'd spent two weeks on the road learning to read underneath.

"You know what it got me," he said.

"I know what you told me. Your battalion surrendered at Vellane. The republic promised amnesty for officers who gave up their men's escape routes. You gave them up. The routes were already blown. Six men died anyway, and the republic put *your* name on a list within the year." She said it flatly, without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "That wasn't arithmetic, Tomas. That was a lie wearing arithmetic's coat. They never meant to keep it. Oryl might be honest—but she isn't the republic. She serves it. And when the regime that owns her changes—and it will change, it's changing already—her guarantees go into the same drawer as yours did."

"The alternative is your family dies when the ice breaks."

"The alternative," Elodie said, laying the mended glove down with terrible care, "is that my family lives free instead of living pardoned. There's a difference. Pardons can be revoked. Names don't come off lists—they just move to new ones."

He pushed back from the table and stood, because sitting still had become impossible. The room was small; three steps took him to the window, where the gray light lay on the snow like tarnish. Somewhere downriver a branch cracked under the weight of ice shifting against the bank.

"Do you know what I see when I look at you?" he said, not turning around. "I see a woman carrying a letter that could kill everyone she loves, and she won't put it down, and she won't open her hand either. You're going to walk onto that river road carrying both versions—the true one and the false one—and you're going to pray the ice holds under you and Oryl's patience holds behind you. That's not principle. That's pride dressed up in mourning clothes."

"Now you sound like her."

He turned. "Like who?"

"My mother." Something moved behind her eyes then, quick and submerged, like a fish under winter ice. "She believed in silences. In carrying things alone so other people wouldn't have to. She thought she was protecting me. Twelve years, Tomas—I didn't even know enough to mourn properly, because I didn't know if she was dead or gone or simply done with us. Do you understand what that does to a child? Every silence has an echo. I grew up inside hers." She picked up the glove again, put it down again. "And now you're asking me to buy my family's safety by handing their names to the same machine that made her disappear. You're asking me to become the echo."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed softly. "It isn't. Very little of this is."

* * *

He walked out because staying was worse.

The cold hit him like a hand pressed flat against his chest. The yard behind the tavern was empty except for his sledge and the drover's oxen stamping steam in their pen, and beyond the fence the fields ran white and featureless down toward the river, which lay somewhere below the tree line—you couldn't see it, but you could feel it, a low seismic mutter under the snow, the sound of a thing too large to be contained deciding whether to wake.

His mother used to say the river talked in February. His mother had said a great many things before the fever took her, most of them about weather and none of them useful, and Tomas had built a whole life out of preferring useful things. Useful: the debt he owed Marguerite Blackwood, the woman who had hidden him in a coffin-load of salted fish and poled him across this same river while a patrol burned the far dock behind them. Useless: the promise he'd made her afterward, drunk on relief and cheap brandy, that he'd watch over whatever she loved.

She'd laughed at him. *I don't need watching, boy. I need forgetting.* And then she'd gone north one last time, and no one had ever told him why, and he hadn't asked, because asking questions of that family was like knocking on doors in a house where everyone was pretending to sleep.

Now here was the daughter, standing in a tavern room with her mother's silence wrapped around her shoulders like the coat she wore, and Tomas understood—with the miserable clarity that arrives only after you've already said the unforgivable thing—that he had not been arguing about Oryl at all.

He had been arguing with a ghost. With Vellane. With the officer who'd signed the amnesty paper and looked through him like glass while six of his men were marched off to a prison camp and four of them into the ground. *Give up the routes and live,* they'd told him. Truth for survival. Arithmetic. He'd done the sum, and the sum had been wrong, and he had spent nineteen years telling himself the error was theirs—that they'd lied, that the bargain was rotten from the start—because the alternative was admitting the bargain had always been rotten, that any truth sold under threat purchases nothing, that the man who buys his life with another man's name doesn't get to keep either.

And here he was, selling it again. *Names for lives.* Handing Elodie the same coin the republic had once handed him, polished up, weighted right, and calling it mercy.

He stood in the cold until his beard stiffened and his hands ached, and then he went back inside.

* * *

She hadn't moved. The glove was finished, laid beside her pack; she sat with both hands wrapped around the cold coffee, staring at nothing, and when the door opened she didn't startle, which told him she'd heard every one of his steps in the yard.

"I owe you an apology," he said.

Her eyes came up. Wary. He deserved wary.

"Not for arguing. For how." He sat down across from her, heavily, the way his knees insisted on these days. "You're right about the echo. I've been one myself for a long time and I hate the sound of it. When I told you to take Oryl's bargain—" He stopped, started again. "At Vellane, I gave them the truth because they frightened me, and I called it reason. Six men paid for my reason. I have spent nineteen years telling myself the republic broke the deal, because the other version—that no deal like that was ever worth making—means I didn't lose them to treachery. I lost them to myself."

The silence stretched. The river muttered under the snow, patient as a creditor.

"So yes," he said. "Take the bargain, save your family, live with the arithmetic. That's what I'd have said yesterday. But I watched what it cost, Elodie. From the inside. And I watched you burn a letter to your family rather than let one word of it be read by strangers, and I watched you sew a forgery together letter by letter like it was surgery, and I realized something I should have realized on the ferry." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "You're not protecting a secret. You're protecting a choice. Theirs. The plot, the plan, the whole mad restoration—it was theirs to make and theirs to answer for. If Oryl takes their names out of your mouth, she takes the choice too. They become prisoners of your confession instead of authors of their own risk. And people who are saved that way—" his mouth twisted, "—they don't thank you. They resent you. Every morning of their pardoned lives."

"They might die instead."

"Yes."

"That's your argument. *Yes.*"

"It's the only honest word I have left today." He looked at her straight on. "But hear the rest of it: I'm not leaving. Whatever you choose—bargain, forgery, the ice road at midnight like a madwoman—I'm not leaving. Not because of the debt. The debt was paid the day your mother's boat touched the far bank. I stay because—" and here the words caught, because Tomas Reyes had not said a sentence like this aloud since his mother died, "—because I have spent my whole life paying for one truth given badly, and I would rather freeze on that river than watch you give one away the same way."

Elodie was very still. Then, slowly, she reached into her coat—not the inner pocket where the letters rode, but the lining seam, the one he'd seen her touch a hundred times without thinking—and drew out the brass compass.

It sat small and dented between them on the scarred table, the needle trembling in its housing, swinging, settling, swinging again, unable to commit.

"She carried this," Elodie said. "Across this river. The night she carried you."

"So I've gathered."

"When I found it, the needle was doing this. It hasn't stopped." Her finger hovered above the glass without touching it. "I used to think it was broken. Now I think it's the only honest object I own. It doesn't refuse to choose because it's cowardly. It refuses because it's pointing at two true things at once, and it won't lie about either of them." She looked up at him, and for the first time since he'd known her, the courier's smoothness was gone entirely, and what was underneath was young, and tired, and unbearably human. "My mother pointed at two true things her whole life. Family and duty. Silence and love. She chose silence, and I lost her, and I swore I would never choose a half-truth when a whole one was possible—even a whole one that burns."

"And the forgery?" he asked gently. "Is that a whole truth?"

"No." A ghost of a smile, bleak as the light outside. "It's a lie. A good one, maybe. But I know what it is, and I'll carry knowing it, and someday—if we live—I'll tell my family exactly what I did and let them weigh it. Freely. That's the whole difference, Tomas. Oryl wants truth extracted. I want truth offered. Extraction breaks the vessel. Offering—" she closed her hand around the compass, "—offering is the only way anything survives being true."

Outside, the light was failing. Down in the dark under the snow, the river groaned—a long, deep note, like a door opening somewhere in the earth.

Tomas exhaled, and some knot that had lived in his chest since Vellane loosened, not all the way, but enough to breathe around.

"All right," he said. "Then we do it your way. But hear my condition, and it's not negotiable." He leaned forward. "The thaw is early. Oryl knows it, we know it, and the third checkpoint sits on the ice itself. If we're crossing that river, we cross tonight, in the dark, before the ice knows it's supposed to break. Both letters. All the way."

Elodie looked at the compass in her fist, then at the blackening window, and slid it back into the lining over her heart.

"Tonight, then," she said. "God help us, before the river opens."

Above the tavern, the wind changed direction—coming off the water now instead of onto it, warm, wrong, smelling of mud and spring—and they both heard it, and neither said what it meant.
