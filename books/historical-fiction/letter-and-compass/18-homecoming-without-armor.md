## 18. Homecoming Without Armor

The farmhouse showed itself to her the way old griefs do—suddenly, and all at once, without any kindness of warning.

Elodie crested the last hill above the Merrow valley an hour past dawn, when the light was still gray and thin as skimmed milk, and there it stood: the low stone house with its sagging eastern porch, its slate roof furred with frost, the black skeleton of the orchard behind it where her mother had once hung wash in every weather. Smoke rose from the chimney. Someone was awake. Someone had always been awake here, even in the worst years, because a farm did not forgive sleep and neither did the land.

She had told herself she would not go down.

That was the plan—the whole of it, rehearsed through three hundred miles of thawing road and two nights sleeping in hay barns with her boots on. She would come within sight of the house, the way a pilgrim comes within sight of a shrine he does not dare touch. She would look at it. She would let herself have that much. And then she would turn east on the lower road, toward the ferry crossing and the new life she had not yet invented, and her family would never know she had stood on this hill at all.

It was a coward's plan, and she knew it was a coward's plan, and she had built it anyway with great care, the way she built everything now—sealed, weighted against discovery, addressed to no one.

Because what would she say? That she had carried their names in her coat like a stone? That she had opened a letter she was trained never to open and read, in cold ministerial ink, the sentence that had been written for them: *to be taken into custody upon the breaking of the ice*? That she had sat in a smuggler's cabin with stolen printer's type under her fingers and forged her way out of betraying them, and that the woman who came down off this hill was therefore not the niece they remembered but something newer and stranger—a person who could lie beautifully, who had learned to make paper tell whatever truth kept people alive?

No. You did not hand people that. You carried it for them. Twelve years of training said so. Twelve years of watching Vasseur seal things and saying nothing, twelve years of being forgettable on purpose, twelve years of loving her family from the outside of a locked room—all of it said: *you do not speak this. You carry it, and you keep walking.*

The compass was in her pocket. She took it out without deciding to.

Her mother's brass compass, sewn for years into a coat lining until Elodie had cut it free like a surgeon removing something the body had grown around. The needle trembled in its glass as it always did now, swinging between north-northeast and a direction that was not quite anything, never settling, never pointing home or away or anywhere a person could commit to. She had stopped trusting it weeks ago. She had also stopped being able to leave it behind.

"Point," she murmured to it. "Just once. Just point."

The needle shivered and lay still between two truths, and Elodie laughed—a short, cracked sound with nothing in it—and put it back in her pocket.

Then she saw the figure by the gate.

* * *

He was standing at the foot of the lane where it met the road, hands folded over the head of a walking stick, breath steaming, and he had been standing there long enough that the frost had melted in a half-circle around his boots. Her uncle Aldous. He was smaller than she remembered—or rather, he was the same size and she had become someone who measured men differently now, who weighed them by how they held themselves at checkpoints and whether their eyes went to her face or her papers. He held himself like a man who had been waiting a long time and intended to wait longer if necessary. His coat was the brown one with the mended elbow. It had been the brown one with the mended elbow for twenty years.

There was no way he could have known she was coming today. There was no way at all.

Except that there was, she realized, as her feet started down the hill without consulting her. Because he had known about the plot. He must have known—he had been named in the letter, after all; you could not be named in a thing like that innocently. And a man who conspires knows what conspiracies cost, and what they summon, and which roads couriers ride in the last week before the ice goes out of the river. He had not waited at the door, where waiting would look like fear. He had walked down to the gate, where a man might simply be taking his morning air.

They faced each other across ten yards of frozen mud.

"Elodie." Not a question. He said her name the way he had said it when she was nine and came home with a split lip and refused to say who had done it—gently, and with the terrible patience of a man prepared to stand there until spring.

"Uncle Aldous."

"You're thin." He looked her up and down, cataloguing her the way farmers catalogue livestock and weather: not cruelly, just thoroughly. "You've been eating other people's bread too long. Courier's diet." He knew what she was. Of course he knew what she was. He had known since the year her mother vanished, probably; the whole family had known and had agreed, wordlessly, in the way of families, never to say it, because saying it made it real and real things got people taken.

"I wasn't going to stop," Elodie heard herself say. "I want you to know that. I was going to walk past on the lower road. I only wanted—" She stopped. The honest end of that sentence was *I only wanted to see the house,* and it sounded so small, so nakedly childish, that for a moment she couldn't push it out of her mouth.

"To see the house," her uncle finished for her.

"Yes."

"Well." He turned and started up the lane, not looking back to see if she followed, because he had never once in his life needed to look back. "It's standing. Come and see that it's standing properly. Your aunt won't believe me otherwise."

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of woodsmoke and yeast and the particular mineral smell of stone walls in winter, and it undid her more thoroughly than any interrogation ever had.

Madame Oryl had undone her with questions; Brannig had undone her with hours; the ice road had nearly undone her with terror. This undid her with a kettle. With her aunt Marta's shawl draped over the chair where it had always draped. With the notch her cousin Pieter had cut in the doorframe at age seven and never been punished for. Everything was exactly where twelve years ago had left it, as though the house had been holding its breath the entire time she was gone, keeping her place.

Marta cried a little, quietly, into the bread dough, and then pretended she hadn't, and set an extra place without being asked. Pieter—sixteen now, tall and sullen and beautiful in the way of boys who do not know it yet—stared at Elodie as though she were a ghost, and then, when she caught him staring, stared harder out of sheer adolescent defiance, and she wanted to laugh and weep at once.

But Aldous drew her into the back parlor, the small cold room where the serious conversations of three generations had happened, and closed the door, and Elodie understood that the hour she had been fleeing for three hundred miles had finally arrived.

"The river will break tonight," he said. "Or tomorrow. The ice is talking."

"It's talking," she agreed. "I heard it all night at the crossing."

"So." He lowered himself into the chair by the window, and for the first time she saw how much the waiting at the gate had cost him—how carefully he arranged his bad knee, how his hands were not quite steady. "You'll tell me why my niece rode through a war's ending to look at our roof from a hilltop and then run away again. Or you'll tell me nothing, and I'll die curious, which is a poor trade but one I can afford."

And there it was. The door, open. The choice, laid out on the table between them like the compass itself.

Every instinct she owned screamed at her to give him the courier's answer: *Nothing, uncle. Routine run. Bad roads.* Every instinct, and twelve years of discipline, and the memory of her mother's face teaching her, without ever saying a word aloud, that the safest thing a Blackwood woman could be was unreadable.

But the instincts had gotten her across three checkpoints and a frozen river, and they were spent. And sitting in this room, in this chair, smelling this smoke, she found that she was tired—tired in a place beneath exhaustion, in the marrow—in a way that lying could not reach anymore.

"I read the letter," Elodie said.

Aldous went very still. Outside, a crow called twice and fell silent, as though it too were listening.

"There was a diplomatic pouch. Sealed. My handler gave it to me himself—Vasseur, his name is Vasseur—and told me to take it north past the third checkpoint before the thaw. And I opened it." Her voice came out level, which surprised her; she had expected it to break. "I steamed the seal at an inn outside Ferrenholt, and I read it, and Uncle—it was about you. About all of you. It named this house. It named the restoration—the old government, the loyalist cells in the valley, everything. It ordered arrests to be made when the ice broke. When the ice breaks, Uncle. Which is tonight."

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere in the walls, the house settled, patient.

"And I burned it," she said. "The original. I burned it in a stove at the northern office, and I delivered a forgery instead—one I wrote myself, with type we stole from a printer in Marrow's Bend. A letter that names nobody. That convicts nobody. As far as the ministry knows, their evidence went north and their arrests are coming, and the arrests will find nothing, because there is nothing left to find." She looked up at him. "So that's why I came to look at the house from the hill. Because I thought—I thought if I came inside, I'd have to tell you, and if I told you, then it would be real, and if it was real, then I'd have to admit what I am now. What I became to do it."

"And what are you now?" Aldous asked. There was no judgment in it. Only the question, offered freely, which was more than any interrogator had given her.

"A person who lies well," Elodie said. "A person who is very good at making paper say what keeps people alive." Her voice did break then, finally, on the next words, and she let it. "And I don't know, Uncle, I honestly don't know, whether that makes me my mother's daughter or whether it means I've become exactly the kind of person she spent her life running from. Because she never told me anything either. She loved me in silence. She disappeared in silence. She left me a compass that points at nothing, and a profession built on not-asking, and I carried all of it like it was an inheritance instead of a wound, and I am so tired, Uncle. I am so tired of carrying sealed things."

The silence after that was long, and warm, and full of woodsmoke.

Then Aldous leaned forward, took both her hands in his—farmer's hands, cracked and cold—and said:

"We knew."

Elodie blinked. "What?"

"About the plot. About the cells. About all of it." His eyes were steady and sad and entirely unhidden, and she realized she was looking at a man setting down a weight he had carried for years. "Your mother helped build it, before you were born. After she vanished, some of them wanted to pull your family deeper in—use the grief, use the name. I refused. But I didn't report anyone either. I've spent fifteen years knowing everything and saying nothing, girl. Sitting at this hearth, passing the salt to men who were planning a war, saying nothing." He squeezed her hands. "You think you became someone who lies well? Where do you imagine you learned it?"

The room tilted slightly. Elodie gripped his hands back.

"Why didn't you ever tell me?" she whispered. "Any of you. Why the silence?"

"Because silence was the only armor we had left," Aldous said simply. "And because we hoped you'd find a better one." He smiled then, a crooked, tired, unmistakably familial smile. "It seems you did. Forged paper. Well. Your mother would have laughed herself sick with pride."

Outside, far off across the fields, came a sound like a giant clearing its throat—the deep groan of the river ice beginning to move. Both of them turned toward the window.

"The thaw," Elodie said softly.

"The thaw," Aldous agreed. "Which means, if I understand my niece correctly, that somewhere north of here, a ministry is expecting arrests that will never come—and when they learn that, they may come looking for whoever made their evidence disappear." He stood, wincing at his knee, and there was something in his posture now that had not been there at the gate: decision, the farmer's kind, plain and immovable. "So you'll not be running off down the lower road after all. You'll stay, and eat, and sleep behind a real door for the first time in months. And tomorrow—" he paused at the door, hand on the latch, and glanced back at her with one eyebrow raised, "—tomorrow there's a man from the ferry crossing asking after you at the inn in town. River man. Says he owes your mother money. I told him where the farm was, because I assumed you'd want to know your debts were finding you."

Tomas. Here. Already.

Elodie reached into her pocket and felt the compass, its needle trembling between directions, and for the first time in twelve years she found she did not mind the trembling at all.
