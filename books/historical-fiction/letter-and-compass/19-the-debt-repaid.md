## 19. The Debt Repaid

The river was still holding when Tomas Reyes walked back into the town of Verenchal, and he noticed it the way a man notices a debt coming due: with a certain grim respect. The ice had gone the color of old pewter along its edges, and beneath the surface he could hear the first mutterings of water finding its voice again after four months of silence. Two more weeks, maybe three, and the crossing would be impossible for anyone without wings.

He had left Elodie at the crossroads outside her uncle's farm an hour past dawn. She had not wanted him to walk the last mile with her—said it would look wrong, a stranger's tracks in the yard where nobody strangers came—and he had understood that too well to argue. There were doors you knocked on in this country and doors you let close behind other people. He had watched her go up the frozen lane with her collar up and her shoulders set like a woman walking into weather, and he had thought: *Mirelle's girl. All this time, Mirelle's girl.* Then he had turned south, because he still had one account to settle, and accounts were the only scripture Tomas Reyes had ever kept faithfully.

Madame Oryl was drinking tea in the back room of the customs house, alone, as if she had been waiting for him since morning. Perhaps she had. She was the sort of woman who arranged coincidences in advance and then enjoyed watching people believe in them.

"Master Reyes." She did not rise. Her teacup was porcelain, thin enough to see light through, and she held it the way other men held knives. "You've come back across my river. That took either courage or stupidity."

"The ferryman said the same thing," Tomas said. "I told him courage. He laughed."

"He would." She gestured to the chair across from her. It was the chair interrogators put people in—straight-backed, slightly lower than her own, angled so that no one could sit comfortably and no one could sit defensively. He sat anyway, and made a show of being comfortable, because the only way to play that table was to refuse the game. "You understand," Oryl continued, "that I let you pass through my checkpoint twice now. Once going north with the courier, once coming back alone. A man might wonder why I permitted such a thing."

"A man might," Tomas agreed. "I stopped wondering about officials years ago. Bad for the digestion."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile; Oryl did not smile so much as permit small breaches in her own composure. "Your file is thin," she said. "Thinner than it should be. A smuggler with twelve years on this river should have a thicker file. Either you are very good, or someone has been pruning your record. I dislike both possibilities."

"I can't help what's in your files, madame. Paper lies. That's most of what paper is for."

"Is it?" She set down the cup. The sound it made against the saucer was very small and very final. "Then tell me something true, Master Reyes, since we agree paper is worthless. Tell me what you carried north."

And there it was—the question, the whole question, dressed in plain clothes. Tomas looked around the room. Bare plaster walls, a stove ticking as it cooled, a single window frosted over except for one clear patch near the sill where someone had scraped it to watch the road. He thought about Elodie walking up that lane. He thought about a woman named Mirelle standing on a dock fourteen years ago with a boy half-drowned beside her and soldiers' lanterns sweeping the reeds, saying *run* in a voice that brooked no discussion, and how he had run, and how he had never even learned until later whose life he owed his own to.

He thought about what truth was worth, and who paid its price.

"I'll tell you exactly what I carried," he said. "A courier and a sealed diplomatic pouch, both under legitimate papers, which you already know because you read those papers yourself and let us through. What was in the pouch, I don't know. Couriers like that one—" he paused, choosing, "—couriers like that one don't open their letters. It's the whole religion of them. You know that better than anyone at that checkpoint of yours."

"You're telling me you helped move cargo you never inspected."

"I'm telling you I'm a ferryman, madame, not a priest. People bring things to my boat. I take them across. What they carry in their hearts stays theirs."

Oryl studied him for a long moment. Outside, the wind moved along the eaves of the customs house with a sound like a hand drawn slowly over wood. When she spoke again her voice had changed—lost its edge of performance, gained something wearier and more human underneath. "Do you know what I am, Master Reyes? Everyone assumes I enjoy this work. They think I collect secrets the way misers collect coin, for the pleasure of counting them. But I will tell you what I actually believe. I believe that every lie this republic has told itself for twenty years eventually arrives somewhere as a body. In a cell. In a ditch. On a list of names copied out by a clerk with cold hands. Lies are just murders that haven't finished traveling yet. And my job—my actual job—is to stand at the border of that journey and stop as many of them as I can."

"That's a fine creed," Tomas said quietly. "Does it have room in it for the ones who lie to survive? Because from where I sit, madame, most of the lying in this country isn't murder. It's shelter. It's a roof people build over the people they love while the weather does what weather does."

"And sometimes," she said, "the roof hides exactly the people the storm was sent to find. You knew what that girl carried. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise. I watched her face while I questioned her. I've questioned nine hundred couriers; I know what guilt looks like when it's been rehearsed for years and what it looks like when it arrived last Tuesday. Hers arrived last Tuesday." She leaned forward. "Give me the names, Reyes. Not for arrest—for protection. The thaw is coming. When the new commissions arrive from the capital, every name on that restoration list becomes a target for whoever holds it. If I have the names first, I can lose them. Do you understand? I can misfile them. I have misfiled things before. But I cannot protect names I do not possess, and I cannot possess them without someone giving them to me."

Tomas sat with that. It was, he had to admit, the best trap he'd ever been caught in—a bargain built entirely out of mercy, which was the most dangerous kind, because you couldn't hate your way out of it. She was offering him the chance to save everyone by surrendering everything, and the terrible thing was that she probably meant it. He believed she meant it. He also believed that papers got copied, that files traveled, that misfiled documents had a way of resurfacing during purges like corpses surfacing in thaws—and he had seen, with his own eyes, what happened to families whose names appeared on lists, even lists held by decent people. Decency was not waterproof. Nothing was waterproof. The whole country was learning that lesson at the speed of melting ice.

So instead of the truth, he gave her the next thing to it—a partial truth, shaped like a key but opening nothing.

"There were no names," he said.

Her eyes narrowed.

"On the list I saw—and yes, madame, I saw a list, at Brannig's checkpoint, being copied—I saw no names that mattered. Clerks copying clerks' work. Half of them were dead men already, or men who'd fled south years ago. Whatever plot your office fears, whoever wrote that letter padded it with ghosts. That's how these things get written. Fear inflates the numbers. Someone wanted the conspiracy to look bigger than it was, because a big conspiracy justifies a budget and a small one justifies nothing." He spread his hands. "You want names to protect. I'm telling you the names on that document aren't protecting anything. They're ballast. Throw them overboard and the letter floats just as high."

For a long time Madame Oryl said nothing. She poured herself more tea, though he noticed she didn't drink it, only held the warmth through the porcelain. And Tomas understood, with the particular clarity available to liars in the presence of honest women, that she did not entirely believe him—that she saw the shape of the omission the way you see the shape of furniture under a sheet. But a shape under a sheet is not proof, and Oryl, whatever else she was, dealt in proof. That was her weakness, if you could call it that: she would not act on certainty alone. Certainty was what the fanatics used. She wanted evidence, and evidence was the one thing he would never give her.

"The courier," she said at last. "Elodie Vasseur, according to her papers."

"Is that what they said?" Tomas kept his face still, and thanked whatever gods watched over smugglers that he had asked her name only once, on the third night, and that she had given him only the first one.

"That is what her travel authorization said." Oryl's gaze was a long, cold instrument. "It is remarkable how many couriers on that road travel under names that dissolve the moment you reach for them. Vasseur is the surname of the deputy director of the diplomatic office, incidentally. A man named Corin Vasseur. Did you know that?"

"I didn't know there was a diplomatic office anymore," Tomas said. "Half the capital says it emptied out in January."

"It did." She rose, finally, and went to the frosted window, and stood looking at the road through the cleared patch of glass. From the back he could see her reflection faintly, doubled in the pane, so that there seemed to be two of her—one facing the winter, one facing him. "Here is what I have decided, Master Reyes, and you may report it to whoever sent you, though I suspect that circle is smaller than you'd like me to believe. I am going to write in my report that the courier passed all three checkpoints with valid documentation and that the pouch was delivered to the northern office. Which will be true. I am going to note that the alleged restoration conspiracy appears, upon review, to consist largely of outdated intelligence and inflated names. Which may be true. And I am going to recommend that further surveillance of the river road be suspended after the thaw, since the roads will close and the office will be dissolved and there will be, administratively speaking, nothing left to guard."

Tomas felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight for days. "That's generous, madame."

"It is not generosity." She turned, and her face was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "It is arithmetic. Every arrest I make this spring buys the new regime nothing and costs this river everything it has left. I have spent twenty years believing truth was the only mercy. I am beginning to suspect that mercy is the only mercy, and that truth is merely one of its instruments—instruments, Master Reyes, are supposed to serve the surgeon, not the other way around." She picked up a bell on the desk and rang it once. Somewhere in the front rooms a clerk's chair scraped. "You may go. Your papers will be found in order. If you ever come back across my river, knock at the front door like a respectable man. The tea is better in the front room."

He was nearly to the door when she spoke again, without turning from the window.

"The debt you're carrying, Reyes. Whoever it's to. Dead debts weigh the most." A pause, the frost breathing against the glass. "Pay them while you can."

* * *

He paid them that evening, or tried to, and discovered that the living are harder to settle with than the dead.

He found Elodie not at the farmhouse—she had been right about that—but in Verenchal itself, at the inn near the ferry landing, sitting in the corner of the common room with a bowl of soup she was mostly not eating, watching the door the way people watch doors when they've spent a month being hunted. She looked up when he came in, and something crossed her face that was almost relief before discipline folded it away again.

"They gave you a room?" he asked, dropping onto the bench across from her.

"For the night. I told them I'm waiting for the ferry south." She pushed the soup aside. "Are you?"

"No." He set his pack down. "I'm waiting for you to eat that soup, and then I'm walking you back to your uncle's lane, and then I'm getting on the ferry alone."

"Tomas—"

"Don't argue yet. Let me finish, because I've been composing this speech for two days and it's a bad speech and I want to get it over with." He counted coins out onto the table between them—not many, he was not a rich man, but real ones, worn smooth, earned on this river the honest dishonest way. "Fourteen years ago a woman pulled me out of the shallows below the second bend with two soldiers' dogs already on the bank above us. I was nineteen and stupid and carrying other people's letters, and she hid me under netting in the bottom of her boat and rowed me downstream past a checkpoint with a lantern hanging off her bow like she owned the night. She fed me for two days in a fish cellar. She never asked my name and never told me hers, and I found it out a year later from a man in Sarnholt who owed her money too—everybody owed Mirelle Blackwood something; that was just how she operated, she collected debts like a banker and forgave them like a fool. I swore I'd repay her. Fourteen years I've carried that. And then a girl walks onto my dock with her mother's jaw and her mother's way of not answering questions, and I finally understand that the debt was never money." He slid the coins across. "It's yours. All of it. For the cellar and the boat and the two days, with interest, compounded annually, at rates that would embarrass a moneylender."

Elodie looked at the coins for a long moment. Then she picked them up—all of them, every one, and his heart sank—and pressed them back into his palm, folding his fingers closed over them with both of her hands. Her hands were cold. Everything about her was cold; she had crossed a river in February and it was still in her bones.

"No," she said.

"Elodie—"

"No. Listen to me, because I'm bad at speeches too, so we'll suffer together." Her grip tightened. "My mother is dead. Has been for twelve years, though nobody ever told me how or why or even confirmed it until I started asking questions I wasn't supposed to ask. I grew up on her silences. I learned to read which neighbors not to greet, which roads not to take, which questions curdle a room. I thought that was love, for a long time—being kept safe by not knowing things. Then a letter came through my hands naming everyone I loved, and I burned it, and I forged another, and I lied to an interrogator and to a young officer and to myself for three hundred miles, and do you know what I learned?" Her voice dropped low, steady, the voice of someone setting down something heavy very carefully. "Debts you pay in secret keep you in chains just as surely as debts you owe. My mother chained me with silence because she thought it was protection. I won't do that to you, and I won't let you do it to me, and I won't let you do it to her memory either. She didn't save you so you could spend your life balancing her ledger. She saved you because that's what she did—it was just who she was, the way the river is wet. You don't owe a river."

"But—"

"If you need to give the money to someone," she said, "give it to the ferryman's widow, or the fisherman whose nets burned at Brannig's checkpoint, or the first scared nineteen-year-old with a bag full of letters who needs two days in a cellar. Make her habit yours. That's the payment. That's the only payment she'd have accepted, and it's the only one I'll accept, and if you push those coins at me again I'll throw them in the river, and we'll both feel foolish, and the fish will be richer than either of us."

Tomas sat there with his fist full of coins and a feeling moving through him that he hadn't let himself feel in years—something between grief and unburdening, the specific ache of a weight being set down after so long you'd forgotten it wasn't part of your spine. Fourteen years he had structured himself around that unpaid debt, the way a tree grows around a fence wire. And this exhausted, frost-bitten young woman, who had every right to take everything from everyone, was refusing the one payment that mattered, and handing him back his freedom like it was a thing he'd misplaced.

"She talked about you," he said quietly. "In the cellar. Second night. She didn't say your name—she never said anything plainly—but she had this little brass compass she'd turn over and over in her hands, needle spinning, wouldn't settle anywhere, and she said, 'Someday I'll give this to my daughter, and she'll hate it, because it only shows you the choices, never the answer.' And I remember thinking, what a strange thing to promise a child." He looked up. "She was right about you, you know. You hate it. And you used it anyway."

Elodie's eyes were bright and furious with tears she refused to shed in a public room. "Did she really say that?"

"Word for word. Smugglers have good memories. It's most of our equipment."

She laughed—a broken, wet sound, half a sob wearing a laugh's clothing—and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, like a woman doing chores. "All right," she said. "All right. Give the coins to whoever needs them. But you'll come to the farm tomorrow before you sail. My uncle will want to meet you. He'll feed you until you're sick, it's how our family says thank you—we inherited that much from her at least. And—" she hesitated, and for a moment she was sixteen again, or twelve, some age before the silences set, "—and there's a letter I've been writing in my head for twelve years. To her. You're the only person alive who heard her voice. Maybe... maybe you could listen to it once. Before I decide whether to burn it or keep it."

Tomas thought of Madame Oryl at her window, *dead debts weigh the most*, and understood that the woman had known about this too, somehow, the way she knew everything—had known that his account would only truly close here, in a shabby common room, over cold soup and refused coins.

"Tomorrow," he said. "But first, finish the soup. Your mother would never forgive me if I let Mirelle Blackwood's daughter cross her own threshold hungry."

Outside, far down the valley, the river groaned—a long, tectonic complaint, the sound of winter losing its argument. Spring was coming for all of it: the ice, the checkpoints, the republic, the silences. And three days later, when Tomas boarded the southbound ferry with empty pockets and a lighter heart, the ferryman handed him a letter that had come up from the capital with the last official post—addressed in a careful, fading hand, not to him, but to *the courier, wherever the thaw finds her.*

He tucked it inside his coat, next to his heart, and began rowing north.
