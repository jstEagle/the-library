## 20. Vasseur's Last Letter

The letter came on a morning when the mud had finally begun to give way to green.

Elodie found it wedged into the doorframe of the farmhouse, folded twice and sealed with the plain gray wax of a government that no longer existed—a wax she knew by touch before she saw it, the way one knows a dead friend's knock. The courier who had left it was long gone; there were no hoofprints in the soft earth, only the faint impression of boots, already filling with water. Whoever had carried it had not wanted to be remembered, and Elodie understood that instinct better than she understood most things.

She stood in the doorway for a long moment with the letter in her hand, letting the cold of it seep through her fingers. It was heavier than paper should be. She recognized Vasseur's handwriting even through the fold—the small, precise capitals, the letters that leaned slightly left as if the man writing them were always about to turn away.

Her uncle was in the kitchen, and he looked up when she came in but asked nothing. That was the new language of the house: questions offered like cups of tea, accepted or refused without comment. Elodie took her coat back off the peg and walked out past the orchard, past the stone wall where the apple trees gave up their argument with the wind, and sat down on the flat rock where her mother used to sit when she thought no one was watching.

She broke the seal.

*Mademoiselle Blackwood—or perhaps I should say Courier Blackwood, though I suspect neither title fits you anymore, and I confess I take some satisfaction in that.*

*By the time this reaches you, the Office of Correspondence will have been dissolved. The order came down eleven days ago. There was no ceremony. A clerk from the Ministry arrived with a manifest, and we surrendered our seals, our ledgers, our letter knives. I watched a boy of nineteen carry the office's great stamp out to a cart like a piece of furniture being repossessed. Forty years of sealed words, hauled away in an afternoon. The building is to become a tax house. I am told the vaults make excellent storage for grain records.*

*I remained until the end because someone had to sign the final inventory, and because I did not trust anyone else to burn what needed burning. There is a stove in my office—you will remember it, the one with the cracked door—and for three days I fed it everything that could hang a person or save one, whichever way history decided to bend. Some of those papers were yours. You know which ones. They are ash now, and I find I feel nothing about it at all, which is either peace or exhaustion, and at my age the two are indistinguishable.*

*I am leaving the country. There is a ship out of Maraval at the end of the month, and I have a cousin in the southern provinces who has offered me a room and, more generously, has offered to pretend I was never anything but a minor functionary with a talent for filing. I intend to let him. I have spent thirty years being exactly where I was supposed to be, precisely when I was supposed to be there. I find I would like, for whatever years remain to me, to be somewhere unremarkable and slightly late.*

*You will notice I have written all of this first—office business, dissolution, departure—because I have been composing this letter in my head since the thaw began, and every version of it circled the same thing the way your mother's compass must have circled north, and I have learned from watching you that one should get the difficult sentence over with rather than approach it by degrees as though it were a checkpoint.*

*So: I knew.*

*I knew what the letter said before I ever put it in your hands. I chose you knowing. I want you to understand that completely, without any softening, because you have earned the right to an unsentimental account. There were four couriers available that week. Two were loyal to the men named in that letter—I could not risk them. One was brave, and brave people do unpredictable things at checkpoints. And then there was you: careful, invisible, trained by absence to ask nothing. On paper, you were the safest pair of hands in the capital.*

*That is what I told myself, in the beginning. But you will remember that my hands shook, and they did not shake from fear of the checkpoints. They shook because I had already made my decision and could not bear the shape of it. I sent you north carrying your family's death warrant because I believed—I hoped—that you would open it. I could not order you to open it. I could not tell you to destroy it. The law required its delivery; my conscience required its destruction; and so I did the coward's arithmetic and placed both requirements in the hands of a woman I trusted to choose correctly, and then I sat in an empty office and waited to learn whether I had spent my last act as a servant of the republic or as something better than one.*

*You delivered a letter. I have read the copy that reached the northern office—it passed through channels, as such things do, and eventually it passed through mine. It is flawless work. The seal is perfect. The hand is very nearly mine. If I did not know better, I would believe it myself, and I am the man who wrote the original.*

*And yet I am proud of you, and here is the part I have rewritten nine times, because every earlier draft got it wrong:*

*I am not proud of your obedience. Obedience I could have bought from any of them. I am proud of your disobedience—the specific, deliberate, costly disobedience of a woman who understood exactly what she was betraying and whom, and did it anyway, and then stood in a farmhouse kitchen and said the truth aloud to the people she had saved, which is more than I have managed in thirty years of service. You burned twelve years of silence in a stove in the north, and I burned forty years of files in a stove in the capital, and between us we have cleared away enough ash to plant something, if anyone is foolish enough to try.*

*Your mother once told me—this was before you were born, or when you were very small; she was applying to be licensed, and I interviewed her, and I denied her, and she came back, and I approved her, and I have never told anyone that story—she told me that a courier's job was not to protect secrets. Anyone can protect a secret; you simply bury it deep enough and stand on the grave. A courier's job, she said, was to make sure the right words reached the right people, and to accept that sometimes the right words are terrible ones. I thought she was being philosophical. She was being prophetic, and she was describing you, twenty-five years early.*

*I owe her a debt I cannot repay, and I owed you an apology I cannot deliver in person, because if I stood in front of you I would prevaricate, and you have had enough of men who prevaricate. So I will put it here, in ink, where it cannot be softened by a smile: I used you. I chose you because of who your mother was and because of what her disappearance had made you, and I told myself it was strategy, and it was also penance. If the republic had survived, I would have gone to my grave with that. Since it has not survived, I am telling you instead, from a desk in an empty room, with a stove still warm behind me.*

*Do not write back. The mail is unreliable in ways that flatter neither of us, and besides, there is nothing you could say that I need to hear. If you wish to honor this letter, live in a country worth living in, or failing that, help build one. I am told the new government is recruiting clerks. I am also told they are recruiting informants, and that the two job postings look remarkably similar in print. Be careful which list your name ends up on. You have a talent for lists; I mean that as a warning and not a compliment.*

*The compass needle does not choose for you. Your mother said that too, once, when I asked her how she decided which side of a river to land on. I did not understand her then. I think I understand her now, and I think you understood it before either of us.*

*With respect, and with regret, and with something I will call affection because no other word quite holds the weight—*

*V.*

*P.S. — The stove with the cracked door. When they dismantled the office, I took the handle. It is in my pocket. I intend to throw it into the sea at Maraval, which is a sentimental gesture entirely unworthy of a man of my training, and I find I do not care.*

* * *

Elodie read the letter twice.

The first time she read it quickly, the way she had been trained—as cargo, as information, as a thing to be gotten safely across the distance between one person and another. The second time she read it slowly, sitting on the flat rock while the sun climbed and the orchard dripped and somewhere down the valley a cart creaked along the road that had been closed all winter and was open now, open forever, the ice gone as though it had never been.

When she finished the second reading, she did the thing she had done with every important document of her life: she held the corner of the page near the flame of the match she'd struck against the rock.

She held it there for a long time. Long enough for the heat to crawl up the paper and begin to brown the edge of Vasseur's name.

Then she cupped her hand around the match and blew it out.

Her uncle found her there an hour later, when the sun was high enough to warm the stone. He came down through the orchard with his hands in his pockets and his gait arranged into the elaborate casualness of a man who has been waiting for permission to approach. He sat down beside her without asking, which was itself new—he had spent the winter asking permission for everything, as though the family's survival had taught him that nothing could be assumed, not even a place on a rock.

"Well?" he said.

"He's leaving the country." She folded the letter along its original creases, precise as a courier, precise as her mother. "Maraval. A ship at the end of the month."

"And the rest?"

Elodie looked out at the valley. The river was visible from here, a bright thread through the brown fields, running fast and full with snowmelt, carrying everything downstream without judgment. All winter it had been a road of ice, and men had walked it in the dark with their hearts in their throats, and now it was simply water again, doing what water does.

"He knew," she said. "About the letter. About what it said. He chose me on purpose."

Her uncle was quiet for a moment. Then: "Did he apologize?"

"In his way. Which is to say, in ink, from a safe distance, after the fact." She almost smiled. "He said he was proud of me. Not of my obedience. Of my disobedience."

"Is that enough?"

It was a real question. Her uncle had spent the winter learning to ask real questions, and Elodie had spent the winter learning to answer them, and the two educations had proceeded together like horses in harness, unevenly, pulling the same load.

"I don't know," she said, and found that saying it did not hurt the way she had expected. "I think maybe it doesn't have to be. I think maybe that's what I'm learning—that things don't have to be enough. They just have to be true."

He nodded slowly, the way he nodded at weather reports and price lists, accepting the world as it presented itself. "There's a man coming tomorrow," he said. "From the provincial office. The new one. They're going farm to farm, taking names, offering positions. Clerks, surveyors, registrars." He paused. "They asked specifically whether anyone in the household had administrative experience."

Elodie felt the old reflex rise in her—the flattening, the forgettability, the instinct to become a gray figure at the edge of a room. She let it rise, examined it, and set it down again like a tool she no longer needed.

"They asked about me?"

"They asked about everyone. But yes. Someone gave them your name." He glanced at her sideways. "I didn't. I want that on the record. Though I'll admit I couldn't decide, these past weeks, whether I'd warn you off or push you toward it, if it came to that."

"What did you decide?"

"I decided it wasn't mine to decide." He stood, brushing the damp from his trousers, and looked down at her with an expression she was only now learning to read without armor: plain, tired, fond. "That seems to be the whole lesson of this year, as far as I can tell. Deciding what isn't yours to decide."

He walked back up through the orchard. Elodie stayed on the rock a while longer, the letter in her lap, the sun on her shoulders, the river running loud and free below.

Tomorrow a man from the new government would come to the farmhouse, and she would have to decide—not whether to serve, she had already decided that in some chamber of herself she hadn't fully inspected—but what to do instead. What a woman became when she stopped carrying sealed things. Whether there was honest work left in a world built on envelopes and locks, or whether she would have to invent it.

In her pocket, her fingers found the compass. She drew it out. The brass was warm from her body, and the needle swung once, twice, and settled—not north, exactly, but toward the river, toward the road, toward whatever came next.

It didn't choose for her. It just reminded her there were two directions.

Elodie closed her hand around it, stood up, and went inside to wait for morning.
