## 21. A New Kind of Courier

Spring came to the river valley the way a rumor comes to a frightened town—first as a whisper at the edges, then all at once, everywhere, undeniable.

Elodie watched it happen from her uncle's kitchen window, a cup of chicory going cold in her hands. The ice on the river had gone gray and rotten weeks ago, then broken apart in groaning plates that rode the current south like slow, exhausted animals. Now the water ran brown and fast and full of itself, carrying branches and fence posts and one entire section of someone's dock, and along the banks the willows had turned overnight from bare whips to a haze of green so tender it looked almost apologetic. The mud smell rose up off the fields, thick and alive. Somewhere down the road a cart wheel squeaked in a rhythm no winter sound had ever managed—loose, easy, unguarded.

The republic was gone. Everyone said it plainly now, in the way people say the name of a dead relative they never liked much: quickly, with a small shrug, already turning to other subjects. The new government had a name that was long and dignified and printed on fresh paper, and it had soldiers who were mostly the same soldiers wearing different armbands, and it had offices—the same offices, swept out, resealed, their signs repainted by men who charged by the letter.

And it had work for her. That was the strange part. That was the part she kept turning over like a stone with something underneath it.

* * *

The offer arrived on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy on a borrowed bicycle who clearly had no idea what he was carrying—a plain envelope, no seal, addressed to *E. Blackwood, c/o the Ferreol farmstead*, which meant someone in the northern office had written down where she'd come from before she ever reached them. She sat on the porch step and opened it with her thumbnail and read it twice.

It was polite. That was what unsettled her most. It was courteous the way Madame Oryl had been courteous—every sentence smooth, every implication sheathed. The Provisional Directorate of Communications, it said, was reorganizing courier services along the river corridor. Her service record, though incomplete, had been noted. A position was available. Steady pay. Official papers. Protection at the checkpoints, which were being streamlined but not abolished. All she needed to do was present herself at the district office before the end of the month and sign the register.

Sign the register. Put her name in a book again. Become, once more, a woman whose value lay in what she could be trusted not to know.

She read it a third time while the boy waited by the gate, shifting his weight, glancing at the farmhouse with the frank curiosity of the young. Then she folded the letter along its original creases, went inside, and set it flat on the kitchen table between the salt cellar and the bread board.

Her uncle looked at it, then at her. He didn't ask. He had gotten very good, these past weeks, at not asking—but he had also gotten good, she was learning, at waiting. There was a difference, she was beginning to understand, between silence as armor and silence as courtesy, and Marten Blackwood practiced the second kind. He poured himself coffee he did not want and sat down across from her and let the room hold its breath around the letter.

"It's a good wage," Elodie said finally, because someone had to say something.

"Probably."

"Steady. Papers. I wouldn't have to—" She stopped. *I wouldn't have to be afraid anymore.* But that wasn't true, and they both knew it. The fear would just change employers. "I wouldn't have to guess anymore," she amended. "About which doors are open."

Her uncle turned his cup slowly on its saucer, once, a full circle, the way he did when he was choosing his words the way a man chooses footing on river ice.

"When your mother left the last time," he said, "she told me she was going to carry letters. That's all she ever said about it. Carry letters." He smiled, faintly, without much humor in it. "I asked whose letters, and she laughed at me. Said it didn't matter whose. Said what mattered was whether they were sealed or open." He looked up. "I thought she was being clever. Took me twenty years to understand she was telling me exactly how to judge the work. Sealed letters are carried for whoever seals them. Open ones are carried for whoever wrote them."

Elodie looked at the envelope on the table—at its clean paper, its considerate phrasing, its absence of any seal at all, because the new regime hadn't yet decided what it needed to hide—and felt the old familiar pull, quiet and reasonable, toward safety. Toward the register and the steady wage and the official papers that would make her untouchable at every checkpoint from here to the capital. Toward becoming, once again, forgettable on purpose.

Then she thought of the stove in the northern office, and the original letter curling into black lace inside it, and how light her satchel had felt afterward. How light, and how strange, like walking after carrying water for miles.

"I'm going to decline," she said.

Her uncle nodded, unsurprised, and got up to fetch more bread, and that was that. In the Blackwood family, apparently, decisions were made quietly, at kitchen tables, over cold coffee, and then life simply rearranged itself around them.

* * *

Declining was easier than deciding. Writing back was harder than either.

She sat up half the night composing her refusal, because she had learned—from Vasseur, from Oryl, from twelve years of watching words do violence—that even a no is a document, and documents are read by people who were not meant to read them. She could not write *I don't trust you*. She could not write *I burned evidence once and would burn it again*. She could not write anything that sounded like grievance or conscience or knowledge. In the end she settled on something short and dull and slightly humble, thanking the Directorate for its consideration, citing family obligations on the farm, expressing regret. She made her handwriting a little worse than it was. She signed her name plainly, because hiding it now would look like guilt, and guilt invited questions.

Then she walked to the crossroads village and mailed it, and stood a moment outside the post office—a converted tavern with the new government's stenciled crest drying on its window—and listened to herself think.

What surprised her was not the fear. She knew the fear; the fear was an old neighbor. What surprised her was the clarity underneath it. For years she had moved through checkpoints and ministries like a woman walking through smoke, holding her breath, keeping her eyes down, and she had called that discipline. It had been necessary, yes. It had kept her alive, and later it had kept her family alive. But standing there in the spring air with nothing sealed against her ribs, no false bottom in her satchel, no route memorized and then deliberately forgotten—she felt something she had almost forgotten the shape of. She felt *legible*. Like handwriting instead of wax. Like a person rather than a container.

She wanted, with a hunger that embarrassed her, to stay that way.

The trouble was money. Honesty, it turned out, paid poorly—or seemed to, until she started asking around.

* * *

The war and the collapse had done many things to the valley, and one of them was this: it had scattered families the way the flood scattered seed. Brothers conscripted south who'd ended up north. Sisters married across the old border and stranded when the crossings closed. Sons in the capital who hadn't heard in eight months whether their mother's cough had killed her. The roads were open again, technically. The river ran free again, technically. But postage was ruinous, the new postal service lost more than it delivered, and half the villages on the far bank still had no service at all—and no one trusted an official envelope anyway. People had spent too many years learning what happened to you when your words traveled in government hands. They had letters written and letters unsent, sitting in drawers, held hostage by distrust.

Elodie heard about the first one by accident, in her aunt's kitchen, from a neighbor named Perrin whose son had been a gunner on the river barges and was now, as far as anyone knew, working the docks in Vasseau-les-Bains two days' travel east. The neighbor mentioned—casually, the way people mention wounds—that she'd written him eleven times since autumn and received nothing, and that she'd stopped writing because she couldn't bear to keep letters in the drawer that might be reaching him and might not.

"You could take it yourself," Elodie's aunt said, meaning it as a joke, a small kindness shaped like humor. "You used to travel."

And Elodie, before she could weigh it, heard herself say: "I could."

Perrin looked at her the way people look at a door they'd assumed was painted on the wall. "You'd do that? Truly?"

"For the cost of my lodging on the road," Elodie said. "That's all. And—" she hesitated, and then decided to practice, right there, the thing she was trying to become. "And you should know I'll read nothing. Not because I'm sworn to secrecy. Because you don't need me to keep your secret. You can seal it if you want. Or you can leave it open and tell me it's about his daughter's teeth coming in. Whatever you want. Your letter belongs to you. I'm just the road."

There was a silence in the kitchen. Elodie realized, with a small internal start, that she had just said aloud—in front of witnesses—the opposite of everything she had been trained to say. *Sealed if you want. Open if you want. Your letter is yours.* Perrin's eyes had gone bright, and she pressed her palm flat on the table as if steadying herself against weather.

"He'd want to know about the teeth," she said. "He'd want to know about the teeth and the roof and whether his father's knee gave out again. He'd want—" Her voice caught. "You'd tell him we're alive?"

"I'd deliver the proof myself," Elodie said. "In your own words."

* * *

She made a sign before she made a plan, which her uncle found hilarious and her aunt found typical. It was a shingle of planed pine, and she painted it herself at the kitchen table with the good brushes, letter by careful letter:

**LETTERS CARRIED. SEALED OR OPEN. FAIR RATES. THE NORTH ROAD AND THE RIVER TOWNS.**

She hung it first at the crossroads, then took it with her on her first circuit and hung copies in three villages, in two market towns, outside a chapel and a mill and the ferry house where Tomas Reyes kept his boat. Tomas studied the sign for a long moment, arms crossed, reading it twice.

"Open correspondence," he said. "Between separated families." He glanced at her sideways, and there was something in his face—not mockery, never quite mockery, but the memory of mockery, worn smooth by a hard winter. "You know what you've invented, don't you? You've invented honest work."

"Don't say it too loud," Elodie said. "It won't catch on."

But it caught on.

It caught on the way dry ground catches rain. By mid-spring she was making the circuit every ten days—crossroads village, the mill town, the ferry crossing, the two settlements on the north bank that had seen no post since the autumn before the thaw—and her satchel, which had once carried state secrets in a false bottom, now carried forty and fifty and sometimes seventy letters in a plain canvas roll, tied with string, each one labeled in its sender's own hand. Some were sealed. Most were not. The open ones she was welcome to hear read aloud by senders who had no one else to read to—old men dictating accounts of harvests and hip ailments, young women writing to fiancés with a bluntness that made Elodie look firmly at the horizon—and she learned the valley's whole heart that way, family by family, in fragments: who had survived, who had not, who was owed apology, who was owed money, whose grave needed tending by someone with stronger arms.

She charged what the road cost and a little more, enough to live on, and no more than that, and people paid it gratefully and often overpaid and she learned to accept eggs and cheese and mended socks without protest, because refusing gifts, she discovered, was its own kind of sealing.

And the strangest thing—the thing she kept noticing with the wonder of a swimmer surfacing—was how little danger there was in it. Checkpoints waved her through. Officers glanced at the open letters, saw nothing worth taking, saw only ordinary human noise, and grew bored. One of them, at the second checkpoint, actually smiled at her and asked if she had anything from his own home region, and she dug through the roll and found him a letter from a village four miles from where he'd been born, and he read the sender's name and went quiet, and thanked her twice. Boredom, she thought, walking away with her papers stamped. Boredom was what honesty bought you at checkpoints. It was the cheapest and rarest commodity on the road.

* * *

One evening in late spring, on the porch of the ferry house waiting for Tomas to finish securing his boat, she took out the compass—habit now, ritual, the way other people touched lintels or kissed their fingers to saints—and set it on her knee.

The needle swung and settled. North, more or less. The needle had grown steadier all spring, or perhaps she had; it was hard to separate the instrument from the hand holding it. She thought about her mother sewing it into a coat lining, all those years ago, and wondered if she had known—even then, even leaving—that her daughter would need a way to remember that directions could be multiple. That a person could go north and south at once. That loyalty was not a single fixed point but a field of forces, and the art of a life was learning to read it without pretending it was simple.

"You're smiling at that thing again," Tomas said, dropping onto the bench beside her. He handed her a tin cup of something hot and terrible and restorative. "Same face you made the day you lied to me about your cargo."

"I didn't lie."

"You lied beautifully. I've respected you for it ever since." He drank, wincing with pleasure. "Word from the capital, by the way. Ferry gossip travels faster than you do, and that's saying something. They're saying the Directorate's courier corps has doubled in size and half the new hires are women who used to carry sealed things and are very sorry about it now." He shrugged. "They offered you a place, didn't they. Before you put up that ridiculous sign."

"They did."

"And?" He already knew. He was giving her the pleasure of saying it.

"And I told them I had family obligations." She turned the compass over in her palm. "Which was true. Just not the obligation they'd have guessed. I owe some people the truth now. I spent twelve years owing everyone silence. It doesn't transfer well between governments."

Tomas nodded slowly, looking out at the river, where the evening light lay along the water in long copper bars. "Your mother crossed this river twice with sealed orders sewn into her coat," he said. "I helped her both times and never asked what they were. I used to think that was loyalty." He shook his head. "Now I think it was just laziness dressed up as honor. Asking costs something. Silence is free, right up until the bill arrives."

"That's the most honest thing you've ever said."

"Don't tell anyone. I'll deny it." He stood, stretched, and jerked his chin toward the boat. "Cargo's aboard. Sixty-one letters, give or take, and a crate of candle wax for the Widow Amelin, who pays in stories, so don't rush her. You want to run the north bank with me tomorrow, or are you walking?"

"Walking," Elodie said. "I like seeing the farms. People flag me down. There's a boy in Harrow's Bend who writes to his grandmother every week now—same letter, mostly. 'Still alive. Still working. Still sorry.' She says it's enough." She tucked the compass away and shouldered the satchel, heavy with other people's open hearts. "It turns out that's the whole trade, Tomas. *Still alive. Still here.* Everything else is commentary."

Tomas laughed, a real laugh, unguarded, and the sound followed her down the darkening road.

* * *

She walked the last mile in full dusk, the willows black ribbons against the last gray light, the frogs starting up in the ditches in their enormous spring chorus. At the top of the rise above her uncle's farm she paused, as she always did, and looked down at the lamplit windows, and felt the particular gravity of a place that expected nothing from her but her presence.

A light was moving in the kitchen. Someone was writing, probably—her aunt answered letters the way other women knitted, steadily, compulsively, three finished that week for the roll. Tomorrow they'd go into the satchel with all the others. Tomorrow she'd carry them north again, unsealed, unhurried, honest.

At the gate she stopped. Something was pinned to the post where she usually hung the satchel—an envelope, weighted under a stone, her name on it in a handwriting she knew better than her own.

Not her uncle's. Not her aunt's. Not Oryl's careful clerk's script, not Brannig's blocky soldier's print.

Vasseur's.

She stood very still in the dark, the frogs loud around her, the compass a small warm weight against her ribs, and reached for the letter.
