## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The knock came at half past four, in the hour when the harbor belongs to no one — not the fishermen who would not wake for another two hours, not the gulls who had gone quiet against the cold, not even the tide itself, which seemed to pause and gather its breath before turning.

Emrys Thackeray heard it from the dispensary, where she was grinding chalk into powder by lamplight. Three knocks, spaced too carefully to be accidental. Not the frantic hammering of a man bleeding out on her step, which she knew well enough by now, but something measured. Almost apologetic.

She set down the mortar and stood very still, listening. The clinic had taught her that sounds were a language of their own: the wet rattle of a lung filling, the particular silence of a room where someone has just stopped breathing. A knock like this one meant hesitation, and hesitation meant either shame or fear, and both arrived at her door more often than bread did.

When she opened it, there was no one.

Only the fog, thick as wool, pressing in from the water, and the smell of coal smoke and salt, and at her feet — a child.

He stood perhaps three feet high, six years old if that, dressed in a coat far too large for him with the sleeves rolled twice at the wrists and still swallowing his hands. His boots were wrong; they'd been cut down from a man's pair, the leather hacked rather than stitched, and one lace had been replaced with twine. He was looking at the ground as though the cobblestones might open and offer instruction.

Behind him, footsteps retreated down the lane — quick, deliberate, already fading. Emrys caught only the impression of a figure: tall, collar up, moving with the purposeful stride of someone carrying a weight they intended never to set down again.

"Hello," she said.

The boy did not look up.

She crouched, keeping her voice low, the way one speaks to injured animals or shell-shocked men — she had done plenty of both. "Are you lost?"

Nothing. The fog moved between them like a curtain being drawn.

There was paper pinned to his coat. She saw it then, a square of cheap note-paper fastened with a straight pin through the wool, positioned over his heart as though whoever had placed it had considered for a moment where such things ought to go. She unpinned it with fingers stiff from the cold.

*Forget the face.*

Four words. No name, no date, no explanation. The handwriting was hurried but educated — a schoolmaster's hand undone by haste, the letters leaning as though running. And beneath the words, pressed hard enough to tear the page, a single ink blot where the pen had been held too long in one place, as if the writer had stopped, mid-sentence, unable to decide whether to say more.

Emrys looked from the note to the boy. He was shivering now that he was standing still, the tremor working through him in waves, and she noticed his lips had gone the color of slate.

"You'll freeze out here," she said. It wasn't an invitation so much as a fact, delivered to the fog and the lane and anyone listening. "I'm going inside. There's a fire."

She left the door open behind her and walked to the hearth, and after a long moment — long enough that she had begun counting, an old habit, counting the seconds between lightning and thunder, between a wound and the bleeding's stop — she heard small boots on her floorboards.

* * *

He would not sit until she sat first.

She learned this by experiment, offering the chair near the fire and watching him stand rigid beside it, hands buried in his stolen sleeves, staring at the flames with an expression she could not read. Only when she pulled her own rocker close and settled into it with a sigh did he fold himself onto the edge of the other chair, perched like a bird ready to flee, knees together, spine straight, a soldier at attention in a child's body.

"Are you hungry?"

No answer. But when she rose and put bread and dripping on the table, and turned her back to fetch the tea, the bread vanished. She heard it — the desperate, ungraceful sounds of a child eating too fast, the way they'd eaten in the field hospitals, men who'd learned that food could be the last thing you ever got. When she turned around, his plate was clean and he was watching her with wide dark eyes, and his mouth was smeared with fat, and he looked, for one unguarded moment, like any child in any kitchen in England.

"There's more," she said gently.

He shook his head once, a small precise motion. Then his eyes went past her shoulder, to the window, and everything in his face closed like a shop pulling down its shutters.

She followed his gaze. Nothing there but fog and the gray suggestion of the harbor wall.

It was later — after she had bathed his feet and found them blistered, after she had wrapped him in a blanket that smelled of camphor and carbolic, after she had made up the cot in the back room, the one she kept for patients who couldn't be moved — that she understood what she had seen in his face at the window.

Fear. But not of what was outside.

Of what *might* come to the window. Of uniforms.

* * *

She discovered this the next afternoon, when Sergeant Pellow of the coastal constabulary came by on his rounds.

Pellow was a good enough man, slow and kind, with a mustache like a scrubbing brush and a habit of removing his helmet when he entered a lady's establishment, which he always called an establishment regardless of what it was. Emrys liked him moderately. He brought her news of the town, looked the other way at her more unconventional patients, and had once, without comment, carried a dying smuggler up her stairs on his own back when no cart could be found.

The boy was sitting in the window seat with a cup of milk when Pellow's knock came.

Emrys answered the door, and before she could speak, she heard it behind her: a sound she knew from France, from the tent at Étaples, from men three years dead whose faces she still could not fully recall though their sounds she never forgot. A breath drawn in and locked. The sound of a body preparing to be struck.

She turned. The boy had flattened himself into the corner of the window seat, the milk untouched, his arms crossed over his face, making himself small — smaller than small, folding inward like paper in flame.

"Sparrow," she said — the word leaving her mouth before she decided on it, a name for a creature that flinches — "it's all right."

Pellow stood in the doorway with his helmet in his hands, looking past her at the child with honest bewilderment. "Beg pardon, miss. Didn't know you had—"

"He's a patient," Emrys said. Her voice came out flat and final, a door closing.

"Right you are." Pellow put his helmet back on, which somehow made it worse, and the boy pressed harder into the corner, and Emrys stepped sideways to break the line of sight between them.

"The fever ward," she said. "Scarletina. You'll want to keep your distance, Sergeant."

It was a lie. It was the first lie she had told a uniform since the war ended, and it tasted of iodine and old smoke, and she found, to her surprise, that it went down easily.

Pellow touched his brim and went about his round. The boy stayed folded in the corner for twenty minutes after the door closed, and when Emrys finally coaxed his arms down, his face was gray and slick with sweat, and his pulse — she took it without asking, nurse's prerogative — hammered like a fist on a door.

*Forget the face.*

She thought of that note all evening, turning it over while she boiled linens and counted stock and wrote the day's entries in her official register, which recorded the town's sick and hurt in a hand so neat it might have been typed. Mrs. Calloway, bronchitis. The Tregenna boy, broken wrist, mended well. One dose laudanum administered to J. Penhale, fisherman, for pain of the teeth.

And in the other ledger — the one that lived under a loose board beneath her bed, the one with no lock because locks invite questions, the one no clerk would ever see — she wrote nothing yet. But she opened it, and ran her thumb along the pages of names, and thought about what it meant that somewhere in this country a child had been taught, thoroughly and recently, to fear a uniform more than hunger, more than cold, more than the dark.

Someone had walked away from him. Someone tall, with a collar up, walking fast toward the fog. Someone who could write *forget the face* in a schoolmaster's hand and pin it over a child's heart and then do the hardest thing, which was keep walking.

What face? Whose?

* * *

On the third night, she woke to silence — the specific silence of a house where something is awake.

Nurses learn to sleep in slices, surfacing at intervals to check the wards, and Emrys surfaced now with the instinct of six years' night shifts and lay listening. Rain on the roof. Wind off the water. The tick of the stove cooling. And beneath it, faint, from the back room: the small rhythmic sound of a child rocking.

She found him sitting upright on the cot, knees drawn to his chest, rocking forward and back, forward and back, eyes open and fixed on the wall. His lips were moving. No sound came out.

She did not turn on the lamp. She sat on the floor beside the cot, her back against the frame, the way she used to sit with the men who couldn't bear a light, and waited. The rocking slowed. Stopped. Started again.

"You're safe here," she said softly. "Whatever it was — you're safe now."

His lips kept moving. She leaned closer, straining, and caught the shape of it, repeated over and over like a prayer worn smooth: numbers. He was saying numbers. Or trying to. Counting something, perhaps, the way she herself counted seconds between lightning and thunder, between disaster and its measure.

"How many?" she whispered.

He stopped rocking. In the darkness his head turned toward her, slowly, and for the first time since the doorstep he looked directly at her — looked *through* her, at something far beyond the walls, beyond the rain, at some burning elsewhere — and his mouth worked once, twice, shaping a word that had no sound.

Then he raised one small hand and held up his fingers.

All ten. Then all ten again. Then again, faster, the fingers flying, until the counting broke apart into shaking and he curled down into the blanket and Emrys gathered him up — he let her, this time, let her hold him, his head fitting beneath her chin, his heart going like a bird against a window — and she rocked him as the rain came harder and the tide turned below in the dark.

Thirty. Thirty of something. Thirty names, thirty bodies, thirty faces that someone wanted forgotten.

She did not sleep again that night. Near dawn, with the boy finally heavy against her and the sky graying at the edges, she slid the loose board aside and drew out the ledger of small names, and opened it to the first blank page, and dipped her pen.

She wrote: *A boy, age uncertain, arrived at my door before dawn, 14th December. Left by person unknown. Cannot or will not speak. Counts to thirty.*

Below that, after a long moment, she added the line that changed everything, though she didn't know it yet — the line she would one day read aloud in a courtroom with her voice breaking and her hands steady:

*Whoever he is, somebody went to great trouble to make sure no record of him exists. Which means somewhere there is a record that somebody else is equally determined I should never see.*

She blotted the page, returned the ledger to its hiding place, and lay back as the harbor came slowly awake below — the first carts, the first gulls, the first smoke rising crooked from the chimneys along the quay.

At her window, the fog was lifting off the water. And through the thinning gray, far out past the harbor wall where the fishing boats were beginning to stir, she watched a naval launch cutting a white line toward the pier — moving fast, at an hour when no official vessel had business moving fast, flying a pennant she did not recognize.

The boy stirred in her arms and, without waking, began again to count.
