## 3. The Boy Who Would Not Speak

The boy ate like a bird that had learned hunger in a hard winter — small quick bites, eyes always moving, one hand curled near the bread as though the rest of the loaf might be confiscated at any moment. Emrys watched him from the doorway of the clinic's back room with a cup of tea going cold in her own hands and made a private resolution not to stare.

Three days now. Three days since she'd found him on her step in the blue hour before dawn, his bare feet blue as well, a note pinned to the collar of a coat two sizes too big for him: *forget the face.* No name. No village. Nothing else.

She had named him herself, quietly, because a child without a name was a wound without a bandage. *Sparrow,* she'd called him, for the way he perched on the edge of chairs and windowsills and never quite settled into any of them. He hadn't objected. He hadn't done anything so ambitious as object. He simply looked at her with those gray, patient eyes and waited, as if speech were a currency he no longer carried.

"More bread?" she asked now.

He shook his head once — a precise, economical motion — then pushed the last crust toward the center of the table. An offering. She understood it as such and took it, chewing slowly while he watched her with something that might, in a less guarded face, have been amusement.

"There's a good lad," she said, and immediately regretted the condescension of it. He was perhaps eight. Perhaps ten. Malnutrition flattened age the way tide flattens sand; she had seen soldiers of twenty-five look like this boy, hollowed and watchful, their eyes older than their grandfathers'. Whatever he had come from, it was not a place where children were allowed to remain children long.

The clinic was quiet in the mornings before the fishing boats came back. Emrys used the hour for dressings and ledgers — both kinds. The official register lay open on the front desk, its columns neat as drill lines: names reported, names confirmed, names filed away by men in offices who would never smell gangrene or hear the particular silence of a ward after lights-out. And beneath it, in the drawer with her spare spectacles and a tin of peppermints she kept for shock cases, the other ledger. Her ledger. Small black book, cheap paper, her own cramped hand filling page after page with names the reports did not carry: the drowned trawlerman logged as *lost overboard, cause unknown* when every soul in the harbor knew his boat had been run down by a patrol vessel that never stopped; the fevered soldier discharged from a hospital train at midnight and dead by morning, unrecorded anywhere; the boy from the mine village, sixteen, conscripted off the books because his father had made trouble at the pithead.

*History happens elsewhere,* she had written once, early, in a fit of bitterness she now half-ashamed of. *To other people. By other people. We are only its debris.*

But debris washed up on harborsides. Debris arrived on doorsteps before dawn with notes pinned to their coats.

Sparrow finished his milk and sat very still, hands flat on the table, waiting to be told what came next. That was the thing about him that turned her heart over sideways: he didn't fidget, didn't wander, didn't touch anything. He waited for instructions like a man awaiting sentence.

"You needn't wait on me," she said gently. "This is your house too, for now."

Something flickered across his face — not understanding withheld but understanding *refused*, as though accepting comfort might cost him something he couldn't afford. Then he slid from the chair and went to the window, the one that faced the harbor road, and stood on tiptoe to look out.

Emrys followed his gaze. Empty cobbles. Gulls arguing over fish heads. Mrs. Penhale's washing snapping in the wind like signal flags.

And then the boy saw them — two soldiers in field-gray walking the far side of the road, ordinary enough, likely delivering requisition papers to the customs house — and Emrys felt the change come through the floorboards before she saw it. A stillness. Not calm. The stillness of a hare that has caught the scent of the dog and knows that movement is death. His hands found the sill and gripped it. His breath went shallow and quick, and when the soldiers paused outside the chandler's shop to light cigarettes, the boy dropped below the window line so fast and so silently that Emrys's nurse's mind registered it as trained — drilled, even — before her heart caught up and broke a little.

They moved on. The boy rose, resumed his perch, and looked out again as though nothing had happened.

Emrys said nothing. But she wrote in her head, in the margin of herself, the first entry of a different kind of ledger: *Flinches at uniforms. Drops from sight without sound. Waits like a man under orders.*

Whatever this child had witnessed, it had worn a uniform too.

* * *

The drawings began on the fourth day.

She had given him paper and pencil partly out of desperation — the clinic's few toys had gone to the evacuee families in the spring, and a silent child with nothing to do was a silence that grew teeth — and partly out of professional habit. In the wards at Rouen she had learned that what patients could not say, their bodies said for them: in the twitch of a hand at night, the flinch at a slammed door, the drawings some of the shell-shocked lads made obsessively, over and over, trench parapets and wire and faces with no mouths. Sister Vance had called it *the spill.* "It comes out somewhere," she'd said. "Better on paper than in the marrow."

Sparrow took the pencil the way he took everything offered — carefully, as though testing whether it might be withdrawn. He drew all morning at the little table by the stove while Emrys dressed Mrs. Coad's ulcerated leg and splinted a fisherman's crushed finger and argued down the price of iodine with the supplier's man. She glanced at him between patients, the way one glances at a kettle one expects to boil over.

By noon there were eleven sheets of paper on the table, and every one of them held the same drawing.

Not similar. *The same.* She laid them out in a row on the examining table and stood over them with her arms folded, and the hair rose along her forearms in the cold clinical way it did when the body knew a fact before the mind consented to it.

A building. Long, low, stone, with a steep roof and three windows across the front — drawn with an accuracy that unsettled her, each window the same distance apart on every sheet, the chimney always at the left-hand end. It was clearly a place he knew. And it was burning. Flames rendered not in childish scribbles but in fine, controlled strokes, rising from the roofline, pouring from the middle window. At the bottom corner of each sheet, tiny figures. Always the same number. She counted twice to be sure. Seven figures, small, running — no. Not running. Standing. Seven small standing figures facing the burning building, drawn with a deliberateness that chilled her more than flames ever could, because running meant escape and standing meant—

Meant watching.

In four of the eleven drawings, among the seven figures, one wore a shape on its head. Not a cap. A peaked silhouette, brimmed and stiff, drawn with two or three economical strokes but unmistakable to anyone who had spent five years within sight of officers: a service cap. An officer.

On those four sheets, one of the seven figures was lying down.

Emrys straightened. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were not entirely steady, and she pressed them flat against the table until they remembered their discipline.

"Sparrow," she said, keeping her voice level and warm, the voice she used for breaking bad news. "Is this your house?"

He had stopped drawing. He watched her arrange the sheets, and his face did the terrible thing it did sometimes — emptied itself, shuttered, became a wall with no door. He looked at the drawings the way a man looks at a cell he has already served time in.

"It's very well drawn," she tried. "You've a good eye for buildings. Was it a church? A school?"

Nothing. He picked up the pencil, turned it over, set it down precisely parallel to the paper's edge. A soldier's habit of order. Or a clerk's. Where had a child learned to align objects like kit?

She crouched beside the chair, bringing her eyes level with his. Up close she could see the faint old bruise-shadow fading along his jaw, yellowing now, days old. She could see the pulse beating fast in his throat.

"No one will hurt you here," she said. "I don't say things I don't mean. Ask anyone in this harbor — they'll tell you Emrys Thackeray is stubborn past all sense and honest past all patience. If you can't speak it, you needn't. But I'll tell you what I see, and you may correct me however you like." She touched the row of drawings, lightly, not taking them. "This house burned. You saw it burn. There were people outside, and one of them fell, and someone wearing a cap was there when it happened. And it isn't behind you yet, because you draw it every day until your pencil goes blunt, and you still see it when you sleep."

His breathing had gone ragged. She wanted to stop — every instinct of care told her to stop, to fold it away, to let the wound close over whatever foreign matter lay inside it — and she made herself go on, softly, because she had spent five years learning that wounds closed over shrapnel festered.

"I'm not asking you to forget the face," she said. The note's words, and she saw them land: his whole body flinched, a single convulsive jerk, as if she'd struck the paper itself. "Whoever wrote that to me — whoever left you — they got it wrong. Faces are my trade, Sparrow. I keep them. All of them." She thought of the black ledger in the drawer, and did not know why she thought of it, except that somehow she already knew the two things belonged together — the boy and the book, the small names and this small witness. "When you're ready. Not before."

He looked at her for a long moment. And then, very slowly, he reached out and turned the topmost drawing face-down, and beneath it he wrote — printed, laborious, in a child's uneven capitals — a single word:

*NO.*

Not *no, I won't speak.* She read it again and understood it differently, and the understanding settled into her chest like cold iron. Not refusal. Correction. As if she had asked the wrong question entirely. As if *your house* were not merely inaccurate but obscene.

Then he gathered the drawings into a stack, squared their edges with the same precise motions, and carried them to the stove, and fed them into the fire one by one, watching each sheet curl and blacken until the building and its seven figures and its officer were ash.

Emrys stood in the heat of the open stove door and felt the protective thing in her chest — which had been, these four days, a vague maternal ache, soft-edged, negotiable — harden into something with structure and weight and edges. Something like a keel. Something that would keep a vessel steady in weather.

Someone had burned a building with a child watching. Someone had taught a child to stand at attention while it happened. And someone — the same someone or another — had carried this boy through the dark to a stranger's door and begged her, in a hand she almost recognized, to *forget the face.*

She was not in the habit of doing what she was told.

* * *

The officer came on the sixth day, in the middle of the afternoon, when the clinic was full of the usual traffic — Berriman's infected hand, two children with whooping cough, old Tregose sleeping off his bottle in the corner chair as he did most Tuesdays. Emrys heard the motorcar before she saw it, which was itself remarkable; motorcars were rare enough in Porthollow that children ran to the road to watch them pass. This one stopped outside her door and sat ticking.

The man who stepped out of it wore a captain's uniform fitted by a tailor rather than a quartermaster, and a smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of his face. He was perhaps forty, fair, clean-shaven, with the tanned competence of a man who had spent the war well behind the lines and intended to spend the peace the same way.

"Mrs. Thackeray?" He removed his cap. "Captain Hale. Adjutant to Colonel Merrick, Southern District. Forgive the intrusion — I'm making a round of the coastal villages. Welfare survey." He produced a leather folder and tapped it pleasantly against his palm. "May we speak?"

"We may," Emrys said, wiping her hands. "Mind the bucket by the door. And it's *Miss* Thackeray."

"My apologies." He stepped inside, and she watched his eyes do the room in one sweep, quick and professional: the patients, the corners, the curtain across the back room where the cot stood. Trained sweep. She had seen it on intelligence men in Boulogne. "A busy establishment. The harbor keeps you well employed?"

"The sea does that." She positioned herself between him and the back-room curtain with the ease of long practice, leaning against the dispensary counter as though idly. "What can I do for the Southern District?"

"A small matter." He consulted the folder without opening it. "We've had a report of a stray — a child, seen in the vicinity. Perhaps eight or nine years of age, unschooled, no papers. The district has a duty of care, you understand. Children adrift after the war, it's a sad business. If such a child presented himself here, we'd want to see him placed properly. Institutional provision. Education. Medical attention." Another pleasant tap of the folder. "There may even be funding attached. The army looks after its obligations."

"Does it," Emrys said.

"Where there are records to justify it, certainly." The smile widened a fraction. He had good teeth. She catalogued them automatically, the way she catalogued symptoms. "So. Have you seen such a child?"

Every patient in the room had gone quiet in the way rooms go quiet when authority enters — Tregose feigning deeper sleep, Berriman staring at his bandaged hand, the coughing children stilled by their mother's grip. They were listening. Harbor people listened to everything, and they would remember every word exchanged in this room tonight, and by tomorrow it would be all over Porthollow, reshaped and improved.

Emrys considered the lie. It sat right there, available, easy: *No child, Captain.* One sentence and the machine of his attention rolled elsewhere.

And then she looked past his shoulder, through the gap in the curtain, where a small gray eye had appeared — the boy awake, watching, motionless behind the canvas — and something in her refused. Not nobility. Something colder and more practical. A man who lied this smoothly about welfare surveys would check the school rolls, the parish registers, the neighbors' testimony, and a discovered lie would bring him back with powers she preferred he never learned he needed.

Besides. She was tired of being managed by men with folders.

"I've a boy in the back," she said. "Came to me six days ago, sick with fever and half-starved. He's recovering. He doesn't speak — hasn't said a word since he arrived, and the doctor at Falmouth will confirm that mutism following fever is well documented. When he's fit to travel, I'll notify the parish council myself, and they can determine his disposition according to the law. That is the proper channel, Captain. I assume the army approves of proper channels."

Something moved behind Hale's pleasantness — not anger, nothing so useful as anger. A recalculation. "Of course. Though the parish can be slow. And a child in a sickroom is a child at risk." He let that sit, then inclined his head graciously. "You'll understand, Miss Thackeray, that we must verify. May I see him? Merely to confirm identity — height, coloring, distinguishing marks. There's a family in the next county searching for a lost nephew."

*There's a family searching.* She heard the boilerplate for exactly what it was. "He's asleep," she said. "Waking him for inspection would set his recovery back a day at least, and I'll thank you not to prescribe medicine in my own dispensary. I'll send word to the district office when he's fit, with a full description. You have my word on it."

"And your word is reliable?"

"My word is famous," Emrys said, "for being worth precisely what it costs me."

For the first time the smile slipped its moorings — just slightly, just at the edge, like a picture hung crooked. He replaced it, wrote something in the folder — she noted that he wrote something, and hated how much the noting of it pleased her, and hated more what it implied — and put on his cap.

"You're a woman of principle, Miss Thackeray. The district values that." He moved to the door, then paused, half-turned, casual as a man remarking on weather. "One hears strange stories on the coast these days. Evacuations, relocations — administrative matters, mostly dull. But occasionally a civilian takes an interest beyond her station. Writes things down. Keeps accounts." He smiled at her fully now, and the warmth in it was the warmth of a lamp over an examination table. "I'd advise against it. History is written by professionals, after all. Amateurs only get hurt trying."

"I'm a nurse, Captain," Emrys said. "Amateurs get hurt around me. Professionals generally leave alone."

He laughed — genuinely, she thought, which was worse — tipped his cap to the room, and was gone. The motorcar pulled away. Through the window she watched it take the coast road north, toward the next village, the next survey, the next stray.

The curtain moved and Sparrow came out. He crossed the room without a glance at anyone, took her hand — the first time he had touched her by choice — and gripped it with a strength that surprised her, small fingers pressing hard, once, and letting go.

Tregose opened one eye from his chair. "Thoroughbred officer, that'un," he said to the ceiling. "Boot-polish and lies. My lads used to say you could tell 'em by how clean the boots stayed." He closed the eye again. "Yours never stayed clean, miss. Remember that."

She remembered it. She went to the drawer, took out the black ledger, and turned to a fresh page. Above the column of names she wrote the date, and beneath it:

*Officer, Capt. Hale, adj. to Col. Merrick. Asking after the boy. Knows things he should not. "Evacuations." Watched me write.*

Then she looked up at the boy, who was watching her write, and whose face for the first time in six days held something other than emptiness.

Fear — yes. But beneath it, unmistakably, hope. Terrible, dangerous hope, the kind that gets people killed, the kind she had sworn off years ago at a dressing station in Flanders when hope proved heavier to carry than grief.

He pointed one small finger at the ledger. Then at himself. Then he looked at her, and though he made no sound at all, she heard the question as plainly as if he'd shouted it:

*Will you write me down? Will I be kept?*

"Yes," Emrys Thackeray said aloud, to the boy, to the room, to the closed door through which the motorcar had gone. "You'll be kept."

Outside, the gulls wheeled over the harbor, and far up the coast road, dust hung in the air where a car had passed — northward, she noted, toward the villages, toward whatever it was that a child drew in flames eleven times over.

She would find out. That much, history could rely upon.
