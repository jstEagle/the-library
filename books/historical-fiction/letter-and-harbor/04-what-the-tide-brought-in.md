## 4. What the Tide Brought In

The ferry from the mainland came in on the morning tide, gray water heaving under a gray sky, and Aldous Wren stood at the rail with his one good coat buttoned to the throat and watched the harbor town assemble itself out of the mist — the crooked breakwater, the masts like winter trees, the smoke from chimneys standing up straight in the cold air as if nothing in the world had ever gone wrong anywhere.

He had been sick over the rail twice crossing. Not seasickness, though he would have preferred to claim it. It was something else, something that rose in him whenever land appeared — that particular nausea of a man returning to a country he had lied about.

Three years ago he had filed dispatches from the Western Front that made him famous. *The boys are in fine spirits.* *Our losses are light and the enemy's are ruinous.* He could still recite them, every polished sentence, the way other men remembered prayers or sins. The censors had cut worse and kept worse; he had learned to write what the War Office wanted and call it patriotism, and the papers had printed him under a bold byline, and mothers had clipped his columns and pasted them into scrapbooks beside photographs of sons who were already dead when the words reached them.

Then, eighteen months ago, he had written one true paragraph. Just one. About a push that failed, about men drowning in a flooded crater while the artillery fired on schedule because nobody upstairs would amend the timetable. One true paragraph out of three years of careful falsehoods, and it had cost him everything: the paper, the press card, the byline, the flat in London, the friends who suddenly had other engagements. A man could lie for a nation for years and be decorated for it; tell one truth and you were finished before luncheon.

So now he was here, in a harbor town at the edge of the map, chasing a rumor the way starving men chase smells through a shuttered street.

* * *

He found the clinic by asking, which took longer than it should have, because half the people he asked looked at him with the flat suspicion harbors reserve for strangers and the other half simply walked away. Finally a fishmonger's boy, bribed with a shilling, jerked his thumb toward the sea wall.

"Old Miss Thackeray's place. Past the salt house. She'll not thank you for coming, mind."

"Why not?"

The boy shrugged with the whole economy of someone who has been paid enough for directions and not a penny more. "She doesn't like questions."

*No more do I*, Aldous thought. *I only ask them for a living. Or I did.*

The clinic sat where the town ran out of land and the tide began — a low stone building that must once have been a net loft, its windows fogged with salt, a painted board above the door with letters worn nearly to ghosts: *THACKERAY — SICK AND HURT TAKEN IN*. Below it, smaller and newer, someone had added in careful white paint: *ALL WELCOME. NO QUESTIONS ASKED AT THE DOOR.*

He raised his hand to knock, hesitated, and was still hesitating when the door opened from within.

She was smaller than he expected, and older — silver hair pinned back without ceremony, sleeves rolled over forearms that had plainly hauled and lifted and held things heavier than most men ever touched. Her eyes went over him in a single practiced pass: coat, boots, hands, face. Triage eyes. He had seen that look before, in field hospitals, and knew exactly what she was assessing — whether he was dying, drunk, dangerous, or merely tiresome.

"You're not hurt," she said.

"No." He removed his hat. "Miss Thackeray? My name is Aldous Wren. I'm a correspondent — or I was. I've come about—"

"I know who you are." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "You wrote the piece about the Fifth Division holding the ridge. My brother died on that ridge. Your article said the position was secured with negligible loss."

The wind came off the harbor and pushed between them into the doorway, smelling of salt and tar and old rope. Aldous felt the words go in like a splinter driven deep, precisely, by someone who knew anatomy.

"The position was taken," he said quietly. "That much was true."

"And the rest?"

"The rest was written for me."

Something moved behind her eyes — not forgiveness, nothing so generous, but perhaps recognition. The look of a woman deciding whether a creature at her door was predator or merely wounded animal, and finding the question genuinely open.

"We're busy," she said. "Whatever story you're hunting, it isn't here."

"May I say what it is first? Then turn me away if you like."

She didn't move aside, but she didn't close the door either, and he took that for leave.

"There's a coastal village," he said. "South of here. Small. Fishing, mostly, a few farms. In October of last year it appears in the record as evacuated — orderly evacuation, civilians relocated, no casualties. That is what the report says. But a man I knew in Flanders — a signals man, demobilized, drinking himself dead in Portsmouth — told me before the drink finished him that the village wasn't evacuated. That it was burned. After the armistice. With people still in it." He heard his own voice go thin and hurried, the voice of a man handing over evidence against himself. "He wouldn't say how he knew. He died before I could press him. And every editor in London has shown me the door for even asking."

Emrys Thackeray regarded him for a long moment. Somewhere inside the clinic, faintly, a child coughed.

"You'd better come in," she said. "It's freezing on that step, and I don't nurse corpses."

* * *

Inside smelled of carbolic and woodsmoke and boiled linen. Two cots along the far wall held sleeping fishermen, their chests rising under blankets with the slow rhythm of men whose lungs had been ruined by salt spray and would be ruined a little further by brandy tonight. A kettle ticked on the stove. And at the table by the window, a boy of perhaps eight or nine sat bent over a sheet of paper, drawing.

Aldous stopped just inside the door.

He had interviewed hundreds of soldiers. He had learned to read faces the way sailors read weather — the set of a mouth, the stillness around an eye. And the boy's face, turned briefly toward the stranger, wore an expression Aldous had seen only once or twice in three years of war and never in a civilian, never in a child: the look of someone who has been somewhere the rest of the world cannot see and is not sure yet whether they have truly come back.

"He won't speak," Emrys said, watching him watch the boy. "Found at my door six days ago, before dawn, with a note pinned to his coat. *Forget the face.* Those are the words. Whoever left him wanted him erased, and wanted whoever found him complicit in the erasing."

"Forget the face," Aldous repeated. His mouth had gone dry. "Whose face? The boy's own? Or a face the boy saw?"

"That," said Emrys, "is rather the question, isn't it."

The boy had returned to his drawing. Aldous crossed the room slowly, keeping his hands visible the way one does with frightened animals, and stopped a respectful distance from the table. Then he looked down at the paper, and the floor seemed to tilt beneath him, gently, the way a ship moves when it leaves the shelter of the harbor wall.

The boy drew well — better than well, with the fierce accuracy children keep until adults teach them to blur. And he drew the same thing. Sheet after sheet lay stacked at his elbow, and every one of them showed the same scene rendered again and again with small variations, the way a dreamer returns to the same dream hoping to change its ending: a village street. Stone houses. A church tower. Smoke — not chimney smoke but the rolling black billows of a burning building, drawn with such pressure that the pencil had torn the paper in places. Figures running. And in the corner of each drawing, always, small and precise and terrible, soldiers. Soldiers with rifles, standing not fighting but watching, faces blank ovals, watching houses burn.

Aldous picked up one sheet with fingers that trembled slightly and did not trust themselves to hide it.

"Where did you get these?" he asked Emrys, though he already knew.

"He draws them. Every day. Dozens. I've given him all the paper in the house and he wears pencils down to stubs. Same picture. Always the same picture."

"May I?" He indicated the stack.

She hesitated, then nodded once.

He went through them the way he had once gone through casualty lists — methodically, dreading what he would find and unable to stop looking. The church tower had a distinctive squat spire, capped with what might have been a birdhouse or a beacon. There was a well-head in the square, drawn from memory at every angle. And on the third sheet from the bottom he stopped altogether, because there, scratched into the corner in a child's crude letters, was a name: *MERRIVALE*.

His breath went out of him slowly.

"Merrivale," he said aloud. "There's a village called Merrivale. Or was. South of here, on the coast road. I passed the turning two days ago and thought nothing of it. It's in the gazetteer. Evacuated October 1918, the record says. Civilians relocated. No casualties."

Emrys had gone very still. "And the boy draws it burning."

"With soldiers watching. Not fighting — watching. Standing guard around a fire." Aldous set the sheet down as if it were hot iron. "Miss Thackeray, I spent three years learning the difference between what happens and what gets written down. This—" he touched the paper, barely, "—this is what happened. I'd stake whatever's left of my name on it."

The boy was watching them. Not the words — he was too young, or too far away, for the words — but the paper, the sheets spread across the table, his work laid bare before strangers. His small hands had curled closed on the tabletop. And then, slowly, he did a thing that made Aldous's chest tighten: he pulled the topmost drawing back toward himself and covered it with both arms, the way children cover food, or cover a wound.

"He thinks you're going to take them," Emrys said softly. "Or him."

"I'm not going to take anything." Aldous crouched, deliberately, so that his eyes were level with the boy's. Up close he could see the details the triage look missed: chapped lips, a healing scrape along one jaw, the shadow of sleeplessness in a face too young for shadows. "Listen to me," he said, gentler than he had spoken to anyone in years. "I'm a writer. That's all — a man who writes things down so they aren't lost. What you draw — I want to help make certain the whole world sees it. That's all I want. Nothing taken. Nothing hidden."

The boy stared at him. Something worked behind those dark eyes — assessment, suspicion, and underneath it, terribly, a flicker of hope, quickly smothered like a match cupped too long in the wind.

Then he looked down at the drawing under his arms, and after a moment he turned it around and slid it, an inch, toward Aldous. An offering measured in inches. On this sheet the soldier figures were drawn larger than in any of the others, and one of them — just one — had a face. Not blank. Drawn with a few hard strokes: heavy brows, a mouth like a gash, a peaked cap with insignia the child had copied carefully, wrong in its proportions but unmistakable in its intent.

An officer. The boy had given one officer a face.

Aldous looked at it for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice came out rough.

"Miss Thackeray. Who else has seen these drawings?"

"No one. Only me. Until you."

"Keep it that way." He straightened, and the old hunger was awake in him now, the reporter's hunger he had thought burned out of him, except it had only been sleeping, and it woke ravenous. "Because if that village burned the way your ledger—"

"My what?"

He turned. Emrys was looking at him with an expression he couldn't read, somewhere between warning and invitation.

"You keep names," he said. "The fishmonger's boy told me you take in anyone and ask nothing. But I saw the book on your desk when I came in — the black one, the one you keep separate from the dispensary records. You think I don't know a private ledger when I see one? I've spent three years reading official reports and learning what they leave out. Men like us—" he caught himself, corrected, "—women like you and men like me, we keep the other list. The list of everyone the reports forgot."

For a moment he thought she would order him out. Then she crossed to the desk, took the black ledger, and set it on the table beside the drawings — two kinds of evidence, side by side, the dead and the burning.

"October," she said. "I nursed at the aid station nearest the coast that autumn. These are men brought in from the transports, and men the villages buried, and men nobody claimed. Officially, most of them never existed."

"Officially," Aldous said, "neither did the fire."

Outside, the tide was turning; they could hear it, a long low exhale against the sea wall. The boy watched them both, his officer-faced drawing between them on the table like a coal that refused to cool.

"Aldous Wren," Emrys said at last, and there was no warmth in it, but there was, at last, no door closing either. "If you print lies again — if you use that child's grief to sell newspapers—"

"If I print anything again," he said, "it will be true. I've lost everything worth keeping except that, and I mean to spend what's left of me on it. One true story. That's all I'm asking the world for. One, before they seal the doors."

She studied him a moment longer, then nodded toward the chair opposite hers.

"Then sit down," she said, "and tell me everything you know about Merrivale. Because three days ago, a captain of the Royal Army Medical Corps came to my door asking pointed questions about 'a stray' — and he smiled the entire time."

Aldous sat. And in the fogged window, faint and wavering, the reflection of the boy's drawing seemed to burn all over again.
