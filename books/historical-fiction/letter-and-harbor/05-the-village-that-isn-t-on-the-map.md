## 5. The Village That Isn't on the Map

The rain came in off the harbor that morning the way it always did in February — sideways, patient, as if it had all winter to wear the town down and intended to use every hour of it. Emrys Thackeray stood at the clinic window with a cup of tea gone cold in her hand, watching Aldous Wren stand in her doorway looking like something the tide had refused.

"You're soaked," she said.

"Your harbor's wet," he said. "I'd assumed that was common knowledge."

She let him in because he had come back — that was reason enough, and more than most men earned. The night before he had sat in her kitchen until midnight turning Sparrow's drawings over and over with hands that shook slightly, not from drink, though she suspected there was some of that too, but from something closer to recognition. He had not explained himself. He had only said, when he finally left into the dark: *Tomorrow. I need to show you what I carry.*

Now he stood dripping on her clean floorboards, and from inside his coat he drew a oilcloth packet tied with string, and set it on the table between them as carefully as a man setting down something that might still go off.

"What is it?" Emrys asked.

"My war," Aldous Wren said. "Or the version of it they let me print."

* * *

Sparrow was asleep in the back room, curled small under three blankets, one fist closed around a stub of pencil even in sleep. Emrys had learned to work quietly in the mornings, moving through the clinic on socked feet, and now she pulled two chairs to the table and untied Wren's packet while he watched her with the wary attention of a man handing over contraband.

Inside were papers. Dozens of them. Dispatches, carbons, telegraph flimsies, and clippings — clippings from half a dozen newspapers, each one bearing his byline or fragments of it. She recognized his name; everyone who read the papers during the war had. *Aldous Wren, Special Correspondent.* He had been famous for exactly eleven months, she remembered, and then he had simply stopped appearing, and the world had moved on without asking why.

"This one first." He laid a clipping flat. It was dated fourteen months after the armistice — after, she noted, the guns had stopped, after the peace bells, after the boys had come home to parades and bad poetry. The headline read: **COASTAL EVACUATION COMPLETED WITHOUT INCIDENT, SAYS ARMY**.

She read it twice. "Without incident."

"That's what it says."

"But it isn't true."

"No," Wren said quietly. "It isn't. And I knew it wasn't when I wrote it."

The rain ticked against the glass. In the back room, Sparrow turned over in his sleep, and both of them went still until the small breathing evened out again.

Emrys smoothed the clipping with her palm, as though the paper might confess under pressure. "Tell me properly. Start at the beginning."

"The village is called Porthellick." He said the name the way one says a name at a graveside. "Cornish coast, south of here. Fishing hamlet, maybe sixty souls, a chapel, a pilchard cellar older than anyone living. In the last autumn of the war the Admiralty decided it was a nuisance — too close to a new coastal battery, too many lights at night, boats going where boats shouldn't. So they evacuated it. Or that was the order. Evacuation completed without incident." He tapped the headline. "That was my dispatch. I wrote it sitting in an officers' mess drinking their whisky, having spent four hours on the road being shown nothing but empty cottages and told everything was fine."

"And what did you see?"

He looked at her for a long moment. She had seen men make this calculation before — in field hospitals, over dying soldiers deciding whether to tell the truth about how much it hurt. Whether she could bear it, whether she needed to.

"I saw a chapel with scorch marks on the stone," he said. "I saw a boatyard burned to its slips. I saw a woman's boot in the tideline, and no woman anywhere to be missing it. And I saw Captain Hale, who was then a major, standing very straight and very polite, telling me that any discrepancy between what I observed and what I reported would be treated as a matter of military discipline." He laughed, a short ugly sound. "And I was thirty-one years old and tired and my editor wanted the story closed and I wrote *evacuation completed without incident* and filed it, and they printed it, and it became true. That's the thing nobody tells you about reports, Miss Thackeray. They don't describe the world. They replace it."

Emrys thought of her ledger upstairs in the locked drawer, the names she kept because no report ever would. "Yes," she said. "I know something about that."

"So do I," said Wren, "now. Too late and from the wrong side of it." He spread the rest of the papers across her table like a man laying out a losing hand. "After the armistice I couldn't leave it alone. I went back twice. Both times I was turned away at the road — sentries, paperwork, a wall of courteous nothing. But I talked to people. A carrier who'd driven supplies through before the evacuation. A chaplain who'd been stationed inland. And every single one of them said the same strange thing: that Porthellick was emptied, yes, but emptied *after* something happened, not before. That the burning came first and the official account came second. That there are families from that village scattered along this coast who will not say the word Porthellick out loud."

"Why not?"

"Because the ones who talk have a way of stopping talking," Wren said. "Or moving. Or drowning. Accidentally."

* * *

She brought out the ledger.

She had never shown it to anyone — not whole, not deliberately. It lived in the drawer beneath her spare linens, wrapped in a flour sack, and she carried it out now with the odd sensation of undressing in public. Wren watched her set it on the table and did not reach for it. He understood, somehow, that it was not a document but a wound.

"What is it?" he asked.

"A record." She opened it to the first page, dated November 1916, her own careful hand marching down the ruled lines. "When I was at the clearing station we were required to report our dead to the adjutant, and the adjutant reported them upward, and somewhere above us the numbers were made tidy. Men who died of wounds were sometimes entered as recovered. Men who were never identified were sometimes entered as returned to duty. Boys with no papers at all — and there were so many, so many who'd enlisted under false names or lost their tags in the mud — they simply ceased to exist. The telegram went to the wrong house or to no house. The mother waited forever for a son the army had already erased." She turned pages. "So I began writing them down. Name if I knew it, description if I didn't. Where they fell. What killed them. Who they called for at the end, if they called for anyone. Forty-one names the first year. There are more than nine hundred now."

Wren put his hand flat on the table beside the ledger, not touching it, the way one rests a hand beside a relic. "Nine hundred," he said.

"It's not history," she said. "I used to think history was what happened elsewhere, to other people, written later by clever men in warm rooms. Now I think history is exactly this — a list of people somebody decided not to count."

They worked through the afternoon. It was Wren who saw the pattern first, because it was his trade to see patterns in paper, and Emrys felt the dread arrive in her body before her mind assembled it — a cold weight behind the sternum, the same feeling she'd had at the clearing station whenever the stretchers started coming faster than the beds could empty.

"Look here." He had laid her ledger open against the *without incident* dispatch, cross-referencing dates. "Your entries for the coastal sector — these twelve names, all fishermen, all recorded dead or missing between October and December of the war's last year. All listed in your book as drowned, lost at sea, washed ashore unidentified." His finger moved down the column. "Porthellick was a fishing village, Miss Thackeray. Sixty souls, most of them men who went to sea. If the village burned — if it burned *after* the armistice — where did the bodies go?"

"The sea takes people," she said. "It always has."

"The sea takes a few. Not twelve from one parish in nine weeks. Not with—" he flipped to another page, another entry, "—this one. *Man, approx. forty, found face-down in the shallows below Harrow Point, hands bound at the wrist with wire.* You wrote that. Bound hands, and your own entry says drowned. Drowned men don't tie themselves up first."

She stared at the line in her own handwriting and felt the ground shift under six years of careful record-keeping. "I wrote drowned because drowned was the word the coroner used. Because drowned was the word I was given."

"Of course you were. That's how it works." Wren's voice was gentle and terrible. "Nobody burns the evidence. They just hand you the wrong word, and you write it down, and the wrong word becomes the record. One omission is an accident. Two is carelessness. But this—" he gestured at the spread of papers, the ledger, the drawings pinned above the stove where Sparrow's charcoal houses burned in charcoal fire over and over, "—this is a system. And systems don't stop on their own. They stop when someone makes noise."

* * *

It was near dusk when Sparrow woke, and Emrys heard him before she saw him — the small deliberate silence of him moving in the doorway, the way he had of standing where he could see both the room and the exit. He was holding his pencil and the latest drawing, and he looked from her face to Wren's and back with the grave assessment of a child who had learned that adults' expressions were weather to be read.

"Sparrow," she said softly. "Come here. We're not going to hurt you. You know that."

He came, but slowly, and stopped at the table's edge. Wren had the sense to sit back and make himself small and unremarkable, hands visible, eyes down — a trick, perhaps, learned from interviewing frightened men.

Emrys took the drawing from the boy's hand gently. It was the same village. She knew it now the way one knows a recurring nightmare: the crooked row of cottages, the chapel with its square tower, the boatyard with the upturned hulls. The flames rising off it in great ragged strokes pressed so hard the paper had torn in one corner. And at the edge of the village, drawn smaller than everything else, a line of figures — small dark shapes with long thin lines for legs, and marks on their shoulders she had taken for haversacks.

"Wren," she said. Her voice came out strange. "Look at the figures."

He leaned in. For a moment he said nothing. Then: "Those aren't packs. Those are rifles slung. Those are soldiers." He looked up at her, and she watched the correspondent and the guilty man contend briefly in his face before the correspondent won. "Miss Thackeray — ask him about the tower. Ask him if there was a bell in the tower."

She crouched to Sparrow's level, the drawing held between them. "There was a church in your village," she said. "With a bell."

Something moved behind the boy's eyes — not speech, never speech, but the flicker of a door rattling on its latch. He nodded. Once.

"And did the bell ring? On the night of the fire?"

His jaw tightened. He looked at the drawing, and then, unbearably, he looked at her, and his small chest rose and fell too fast. Then he took the pencil from his own hand, and on the back of the drawing, pressing hard enough to score the paper, he drew the tower — and beside it, a figure climbing it. A soldier. Climbing the bell tower with something long and dark in his hands, and below, in strokes that tore the paper, he drew the bell fallen in the churchyard grass, cracked in half.

"They took the bell down," Wren said quietly behind her. "So it couldn't ring. So nobody could raise the alarm, or mourn, or — God help us — remember the hour it happened." He passed a hand over his mouth. "You can't evacuate a village without incident, Miss Thackeray. But you can silence one."

Emrys stayed crouched, eye-level with the boy, and made herself keep her voice steady, though her hands were not steady at all. "Sparrow. Listen to me. This village — the one you draw. Does it have a name?"

The boy's lips parted. No sound came. But his finger came up, trembling, and touched the drawing — the chapel, the cottages, the burning — and then he did something he had never done in all the weeks he had been with her. He reached past her, toward the ledger lying open on the table, and laid his small hand flat upon the page of names.

Emrys looked down. Her own handwriting marched up the column under lamplight. Twelve names in the coastal sector. Fishermen. October, November, December. The last name on the page was written in her hand on a night of sleet, a body from the tideline, no papers, no tag:

*Man, approx. 40, fisherman's hands. Called for "Mari."*

Sparrow's finger slid down the column and stopped at that final line. He could not read — she was nearly sure he could not read. But he tapped the entry twice, insistently, and then pointed at himself, and then at the door, toward the harbor, toward the sea.

"He knows those men," Wren said. "Or one of them. Miss Thackeray — he's not just a witness to the burning. He's kin to your ledger."

Outside, the rain thickened, and far down the harbor road Emrys heard — faint, unmistakable, wrong for this hour — the measured sound of boots. Several pairs, keeping step.

She closed the ledger and blew out the lamp.
