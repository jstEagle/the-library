## 6. Captain Hale's Courtesy

He came on a Tuesday, which Emrys would remember afterward as the detail that unsettled her most. Tuesdays were quiet. The fishing boats went out late after a blow, the market stalls were half-stocked, and the harbor town moved through its errands with the unhurried air of people who had survived another winter and intended to enjoy the fact. No one had business at a charity clinic on a Tuesday morning unless they were sick, poor, or both — and Captain Edwin Hale of the Army Records Office was neither.

She saw him first from the dispensary window: a motorcar standing at the foot of the clinic steps, mud-spattered but gleaming, the kind of vehicle that announced itself to a street of carts and gulls. Two soldiers leaned against it with the studied boredom of men pretending not to be guards. And Hale himself stood at her door, hat in hand, arranging his face into courtesy.

He knocked. Of course he knocked. Men like Hale always knocked; it was the knocking that was the threat.

Emrys dried her hands, checked that Sparrow was in the back room — he was, cross-legged on the cot with his slate, drawing for the hundredth time the building with the fire in its windows — and opened the door.

"Mrs. Thackeray." He pronounced it carefully, as though he'd rehearsed it. "Captain Edwin Hale. I hope I'm not intruding."

"You are," she said. "But you've come a long way to do it."

Something flickered behind his eyes — appreciation, perhaps, or recalculation. He was younger than she'd expected from his letters, forty perhaps, with the smooth good health of a man who had spent the war behind desks, and the particular stillness of a man who never needed to raise his voice because other men raised theirs for him. He held up a wicker hamper.

"A courtesy call. We've heard such good things about your work here. The Army takes an interest in civilian medical efforts along the coast — especially those serving returned men." He smiled. "May I come in? It's cold enough out here to shame the Germans."

The joke sat between them like something dead on a beach. Emrys stepped aside anyway. Refusing him at the threshold would be a statement, and statements this early were ammunition she couldn't afford to spend.

* * *

He made a tour of the clinic the way a man tours a house he is considering buying. He admired the rows of cots. He praised the cleanliness of the surgical room. He paused at the ledger stand by the admissions desk — the public one, the official record she kept for the parish — and ran one finger down the page without reading it, though she had the distinct sense he read every word.

"You treat a great many men whose names don't appear anywhere else," he observed.

"I treat whoever comes through the door."

"Yes. So I'm told." He set the hamper on her desk and began unpacking it with the ease of a host in his own parlor: tinned milk, sugar, a jar of quinine tablets, oranges — *oranges*, in February, fruit that cost more per pound than her nurses made in a day — and beneath everything, wrapped in wax paper, a block of proper tea. "From the Officers' Mess. Surplus, really. It seemed wrong for it to sit in a storeroom when there was work like yours being done."

"We manage."

"I can see that. Which is precisely why I'm here." He turned, hands clasped behind his back, and gave her the full weight of his attention, and Emrys understood that everything before this — the hamper, the jokes, the tour — had been the overture. "Your funding situation is precarious. The parish grant ends in March. Your benefactress in London has let two of your last three requests go unanswered — I know because I make it my business to know things. You're running this place on stubbornness and salvaged bandages." He nodded toward the window, toward the town. "And yet you keep it open. Remarkable. The Army admires remarkable women, Mrs. Thackeray. We'd like to be your patron. A formal grant. Five hundred pounds a year, equipment, coal, a telephone line to the garrison surgeon. Enough to make this clinic what it deserves to be."

Five hundred pounds. She kept her face still while the number did its work inside her — new roof, winter fuel, penicillin substitutes for the septic ward, shoes for children who came in with feet wrapped in sacking. He watched her think about it. That was the point of naming the figure aloud; he wanted to watch her want it.

"And in exchange?"

"Nothing onerous." He said it lightly, the way a man says *just a small operation* before cutting. "Administrative cooperation. The Army has been asked to account for certain persons displaced during the coastal actions of last autumn. Strays, vagrants, unaccompanied minors. There's a child in this district — a boy, six or seven years old, found wandering — whom our records suggest may have come into your care."

The room did not change temperature, but Emrys felt it change anyway.

"I take in strays the way I take in everyone," she said. "Whoever comes through the door."

"Naturally. But this boy would be a special case. Ward of the Crown. There's a receiving home in Plymouth — clean, well-run, schooling, proper provision. Better than a clinic cot, surely. And better for you, frankly. An undocumented child is a liability, Mrs. Thackeray. No papers, no next of kin, no record of how he arrived." He let that hang. "Questions get asked about clinics that harbor undocumented children. Funding bodies are sensitive to scandal. Even the Army's generosity has limits."

There it was. Not veiled so much as wearing a veil thin enough to see through: surrender the boy, and be rewarded. Keep him, and be ruined politely, with paperwork.

"The boy you describe," Emrys said slowly, folding her hands so he wouldn't see them tremble — not with fear, she told herself, only with the effort of stillness — "I haven't seen him."

"No?"

"No. Children pass through here. Some stay a night, some a week. I don't catalogue them like inventory, Captain. If a mute child was left on my step with no name and no papers, I'd have fed him, washed him, and asked the parish to find his people. That's all any decent person would do."

Hale regarded her for a long moment. Then he smiled, and the smile was worse than the threat had been, because it acknowledged them both — acknowledged that she had lied to his face and that he knew it, and that he was prepared to let the lie stand for now, the way a chess player lets a bad move stand until it costs more later.

"Just so," he said softly. "A hypothetical child. Well." He began repacking nothing, straightening the hamper's contents that didn't need straightening. "Do consider the offer. The grant stands open through the end of the month. After that—" he shrugged, a small elegant motion "—the budget goes where budgets go. To clinics without complications."

At the door he paused, hat back on, and looked at her with what might almost have been kindness, if kindness could be issued like a requisition form.

"For what it's worth, Mrs. Thackeray — and I mean this sincerely — you strike me as a woman who understands that history is written by careful people. The alternative is that it's written by accident. Nobody wants their name in a book by accident." He tipped the hat. "Good day."

* * *

She watched the motorcar pull away and stood at the window long after it had gone, watching the gulls settle back onto the mudflats as though the street had never been disturbed.

*Careful people.* She turned the phrase over. He meant it as comfort, or as bait, or as both — the same thing, probably, to a man like Hale. Be careful, and history will be kind to you. Be careless, and it will eat you.

Her whole life she had believed the opposite: that history happened elsewhere, to other people, in capitals and conference rooms, and that the best a woman could do was tend the wreckage when it washed ashore. The ledger in the locked drawer upstairs was her private rebellion against that — not history, just names. Names the reports had dropped. Small names. A gunner from Swansea who died asking for his mother. A stretcher-bearer listed as deserter who had bled out carrying someone else. Names she had promised, silently, over and over, would not simply vanish because some staff officer's arithmetic required them to.

Now a man in a good coat had walked into her clinic and offered her five hundred pounds a year to help him make names vanish. And she had lied to protect a child who wasn't hers, whose name she didn't even know, who called himself nothing because he called himself nothing at all.

*Forget the face.* The note still lay folded in her writing desk, soft at the creases from handling. Forget the face. As if a face could be forgotten by instruction. As if the boy's face — sharp little chin, eyes too old, the flinch that ran through his whole body at the sight of khaki — could be unwitnessed by anyone who had seen it.

She climbed the stairs quietly. Sparrow was where she'd left him, slate balanced on his knees. But he wasn't drawing. He was sitting motionless, slate abandoned beside him, staring at the door with an expression Emrys had learned to read in wounded men: the look of someone listening to sounds the rest of the world couldn't hear.

"He's gone," she said gently. "The man with the car. He's gone."

Sparrow's eyes came to her face. He didn't speak — he hadn't spoken since the night he arrived, not a word, not a sound except once a cry in sleep that had woken the whole house — but his mouth worked around something, shaping it, testing it, and failing.

She sat down on the edge of the cot. Up close she could see the drawing he'd finished, the burning building again, but today with a difference: below it, small and precise, he had drawn a row of figures. Soldiers, she thought, by the shapes of their hats. Standing in a line. Watching.

"Sparrow," she said — the name she'd given him for the way he watched from high corners, quick and silent, ready to fly. "Did that man frighten you?"

A pause. Then, slowly, the boy reached out and touched her hand. Not comfort-seeking — the gesture had a strange deliberateness to it, almost adult. He pressed one finger against her knuckles, then drew it away and pointed, very deliberately, toward the window. Toward the road the motorcar had taken.

Then he pointed at himself.

And then — and this was the thing Emrys would carry into the days ahead, the thing that settled her course the way a keel settles a boat — he pointed at the drawing of the burning building, and shook his head, and mouthed something with great care, two syllables, soundless, that she understood anyway because some words are shaped by every tongue:

*Not dead.*

She held him then, and he let her, stiff at first and then not, a small warm weight against her chest while the harbor light shifted across the floorboards. Not dead. Someone else in that village besides him had lived. Someone the Captain's careful records had missed — or was hunting.

Downstairs, the clinic door opened and closed: Mrs. Penhale arriving for her dressing, calling out that there was a gentleman waiting in the lane, said he was a newspaperman, looked half-drowned and wouldn't say who sent him.

Emrys closed her eyes briefly. Fear in one hand, stubbornness in the other, and now, apparently, a reporter at her gate.

"Tell him to wait," she said, rising, "and put the kettle on. It seems we're collecting visitors."
