## 7. Sparrow Speaks

The dream did not come all at once. It never did.

It began the way the fire had begun — small, almost gentle, a warmth at the edge of things that might have been kindness if he had not known better. In the dream he was small again, smaller than he was now, and his mother's hands were warm on either side of his face, and she was saying something he could not hear because the sea was loud, and then the sea was not the sea at all but the sound of many boots, and her hands were gone, and the warmth at the edge of the world turned orange and climbed.

He ran through the village in the dream the way he had run through it awake, past the smithy where the coals glowed like eyes, past the well with its green-slicked stones, past the chapel whose bell had rung for armistice and would never ring again. The smoke bent low over the lanes as though the sky itself were pressing down to smother what remained. And behind him, always behind him, the voice — not shouting, the voice never shouted, that was the worst of it — saying in a tone as level as a ledger line: *No witnesses.*

In the dream he always looked back. That was the part that woke him.

He looked back and saw the faces. Not the burning houses — houses were only houses, and even at seven years old he understood that wood and thatch could be made again — but the faces, standing in doorways, gathered at the crossroads, lined up along the harbor wall where the boats were being pushed out to burn on the water like funeral candles. He saw Mrs. Penhale who sold mackerel and always gave him the tail end of the loaf. He saw old Tam with his white beard and his hands stained blue from nets. He saw his mother turn toward him across the whole width of the burning village, and her mouth shaped his name —

And Sparrow woke.

* * *

He came up out of the blankets with a sound he did not know he could make, a thin tearing noise like cloth ripped slowly, and the clinic room swam around him — the whitewashed walls, the shelf of brown bottles, the moon through the salt-hazed window laying a pale stripe across the floor. For a moment he did not know where he was. For a moment he was still in the village, and the stripe of light was firelight, and someone was coming up the stairs.

Then the smell reached him: carbolic and clean linen and, underneath it, the sea. Her smell. The nurse-woman's smell. Emrys.

He sat in the dark with his knees pulled to his chest and shook. He had learned in the village how to be quiet when fear came — quiet as a gull frozen on a piling, quiet as a boy under a floor — but the shaking was inside him now and it would not obey. His teeth chattered. His hands would not close. And somewhere below him, down the stairs, he heard the small sounds of the clinic at night: the tick of the stove cooling, the sea against the pilings, and then — footsteps.

Soft ones. Deliberate ones. Coming up.

The door opened without a knock. She had learned that knocking frightened him; she had learned it weeks ago, in the first days, when a knock sent him behind the coal scuttle and it took an hour of sitting on the cold floor beside him, talking about nothing, about weather and fish and the price of bread, before he crept out. So the door simply opened, and Emrys Thackeray stood in it with a candle in one hand and a blanket over her arm, and she did not say *what's wrong* or *you're safe now*, because she had learned those words were wind against stone.

She said nothing at all. She crossed the room, set the candle on the table, and sat down on the floor beside his bed, her back against the wall, the way she did sometimes when the night was bad. As though she were keeping watch. As though there were nothing strange in a grown woman sitting on cold boards at three in the morning.

Sparrow looked at her. The candle flame stood very still between them.

He wanted — God, he wanted — to tell her. It rose in him like the tide, like the fire, like everything else in his life that came without asking. He wanted to say *they came after the bells, they came after the war was over, nobody tells you that, everybody thinks the ending is the ending.* He wanted to say *I heard the order given, I was under the boat hull, I was close enough to see the officer's gloves.* He wanted to say *my name is not Sparrow, my name is—*

But his throat closed on it. It had been closed since that night. Words went into it and lay there like stones dropped down a well, and no sound ever came back up. He had tried once, early, in the first week, and the effort had hurt so much — a physical pain, a hand squeezing his neck from the inside — that he had decided the words were lost forever, drowned with everyone else.

So instead of speaking, he did something else. He slid off the bed, took the stub of pencil and the paper she left for him on the table — she always left them, every night, without comment, and this too was a kind of language he had come to understand — and he knelt on the floor beside her and drew.

He drew the village. He had drawn it dozens of times by now; he knew she kept the drawings, he had seen the folder in her desk drawer, and he did not mind. Drawing was easier than breathing. But tonight his hand shook, and the lines came out wrong and jagged, and he found himself drawing not the neat picture of before but the other one, the one he usually stopped himself from making. He drew the harbor wall. He drew the men standing along it. He drew the officer.

He drew the officer carefully, the way a witness draws. The tall cap. The gloves. The mustache trimmed like a hedge. And beside him, because it mattered, because it was the thing the whole night hung upon, he drew the second man — the one with the list, the clerk-shaped man holding the paper, and the officer's gloved finger coming down on the page, once, twice, pointing at names.

Emrys went very still beside him. He felt the stillness more than saw it, the way you feel a change in the wind.

"Pointing," she said quietly. Not a question. "At names."

He nodded.

She was silent a long moment. Then, in a voice gentler than any he had heard from her, she said: "Was it your village? The one in your drawings?"

He nodded again.

"And the men on the wall. Were they—" She stopped herself. Started again. "Were they soldiers? Your soldiers?"

He shook his head. No. They were fishermen. They were bakers. They were Tam and Mr. Penhale and the boy from the next farm over who had a laugh like a donkey. They were nobody's soldiers. That was the whole of it. That was the entire unspeakable thing: they had done nothing, and it had not mattered.

"The fire," Emrys said. "Did it start by accident?"

His whole body answered before his head could. He flinched so hard the pencil skidded from his fingers, and he pressed his palms flat to the floorboards as though the floor might open and take him somewhere the question couldn't follow. *Accident.* The word was obscene. The fires had been set by men walking house to house in a line, methodical as harvesters, each man with his tin of oil, and the officer walking behind them checking them off—

He grabbed the pencil. He drew the line of men. He drew the tins. He drew, with furious jabbing strokes, the flames going up in rows, house after house after house, and then he drew the date — or tried to; numbers were hard — and finally, in desperation, he drew the church bell with the little flag beside it, the flag he had seen everywhere that autumn, the flag of the armistice, the peace. *After,* he tried to make the picture say. *This happened after. After the bells. After the end.*

Emrys stared at the drawing for a long time. When she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, harder, a blade wrapped in wool.

"After the armistice."

He nodded.

"And the officer gave the order. This man." Her finger hovered above the tall cap, the gloves, and did not touch the paper, as though touching it might burn her. "You saw him give it."

He nodded. Then, because nodding was not enough, not nearly enough, he pointed at his own ear. *I heard.* Then at his own eye. *I saw.* Then he held up both hands, fingers spread, and folded them down one by one, and looked at her with all the terrible clarity a child can have, and waited for her to understand what he could not say: *and then he said no witnesses, and I am the witness, and I am still here.*

Emrys Thackeray closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet, but her jaw was set like a keel.

"They missed you," she said softly. "Under the boat hull. Your mother put you under the hull."

He had never told her that. He had never told her anything. But she had read the drawings the way she read everything — closely, completely, the way she read the faces of dying men, he supposed — and somehow she had known. He nodded, and the nod broke something loose in his chest, and the tears came all at once, hot and silent, the way they had refused to come for months. He cried the way he had not let himself cry in the boat, in the water, in the hedgerow, in the freight car, in all the long gray places between there and here.

And Emrys did not hold him — not at first. She knew even that much about him. She simply moved closer, until her shoulder touched his shoulder, and stayed there, a warm solid weight, while the candle burned and the sea knocked patiently at the pilings below.

It was in the middle of the crying — he could never afterward explain why, except that grief is a floodgate and words are water — that it happened.

"My name," said Sparrow.

The sound of his own voice was so foreign that he startled at it. It came out cracked, rusted, half-swallowed — a voice that had been shut in a cellar for a season. But it was his. It was words. They had come up out of the well.

Emrys turned her head very slowly, afraid, he could see, of frightening the sound away, the way one moves near a wild bird.

"My name is—" and here the cellar door stuck, and he coughed, and tried again, and the second time it came: "My name is Tomas. Tomas Vey." He swallowed. His throat ached as though he had shouted for hours. "Mam called me Tom-Tom. Because of the bell. Because I was born the day the new bell went up."

"Tomas," Emrys repeated, and she said it correctly, reverently, the way you handle something breakable. "Tomas Vey."

"I'm from Porthellick." The words were tumbling now, falling over each other, six months of silence pouring through the crack. "It's not on their maps right. It's on the maps but it says evacuated. Nobody evacuated us. Nobody came. We rang the bell for the armistice and they came two days after, and the officer—" his breath hitched — "the officer wore grey gloves and he had a list, and he walked down the line of men on the wall and he pointed, and where he pointed they took them to the boats, and they burned the boats with—" His voice failed. He pressed his fists against his mouth.

"Breathe," Emrys said. "Slowly. There's no hurry now. You've got years' worth of hurry in you and none of it needs to happen tonight."

But he shook his head, because there was a hurry, there had always been a hurry, and the hurry was this: "He said no witnesses. I heard him. Under the hull I heard everything, the water carried the voices, and he said the village was to be entered in the record as evacuated, and anyone found from it was to be—" he made himself say it, made himself push the words up the well — "was to be treated as deserters. Deserters! Tam is seventy years old. Mrs. Penhale fed gulls older than me!"

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, the tide turned against the pilings.

"He counted us," Tomas whispered. "Before. When they first marched in, the clerk wrote down every soul, and the officer checked the count, and I remember thinking it was like school register, like roll call, and Mam squeezed my hand because I almost laughed. He counted us so he'd know exactly how many were missing afterward. One." He touched his own chest. "I'm the one. I'm the number that doesn't add up."

Emrys drew a slow breath. He watched her do it — watched the deliberate steadiness of it, a nurse's trick, borrowing calm for someone else's benefit — and then she said, "Tomas. Listen to me. What you just told me — every word of it — I am going to write down. Not tonight. Tonight you'll sleep, or try to. But tomorrow I'm going to sit with paper and you're going to tell me all of it, in whatever order it comes, and I'm going to write it exactly as you say it, and my hand won't shake even if yours does. Do you know why?"

He shook his head.

"Because there's a book," she said. "A ledger. I keep names in it — people the reports leave out. Soldiers, mostly. Sailors. People who died and were entered nowhere, or entered wrong. I used to think it was a small mad habit of mine." Something crossed her face then, brief and bitter, some memory with edges. "I'm beginning to suspect it isn't mad at all. I'm beginning to suspect it may be the only honest book in England."

She pulled the spare blanket around his shoulders then, finally, and this time she did hold him, briefly, one arm firm across his back, and he let her, and it was the first time since his mother's arms that being held had not felt like a trap.

"You spoke," she said into his hair. "Do you know how long I've wondered if you ever would? I'd begun to make my peace with never hearing it. And the first thing you chose to spend your voice on was their names. Tam. Mrs. Penhale. Porthellick." She pulled back and looked at him, and her eyes were fierce and bright. "That was well spent, Tomas Vey. That was the best use of a voice I've ever heard."

He slept after that — truly slept, dreamless, fathoms deep — and woke late to gull-cries and the smell of bread, and for one golden moment he was only a boy in a warm room above a harbor.

Then he heard the voices downstairs. One was Emrys's, low and controlled. The other was a man's, smooth and unhurried, with the particular polished courtesy of someone accustomed to being obeyed — and beneath the courtesy, faint as oil under water, something that made Tomas's stomach drop through the floorboards.

He crept to the top of the stairs. Through the banister rails he could see into the clinic room below: Emrys standing very straight behind her desk, and facing her, hat in hand, smiling, a man in a uniform with a tall cap — and as the man turned slightly, gesturing graciously at the room, the light caught his hands.

Grey gloves.

Tomas's hand flew to his own mouth to trap the sound rising in his throat, and below, in her steadiest voice, Emrys Thackeray said:

"—and to what do I owe the honor, Captain Hale?"
