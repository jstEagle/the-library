## 8. The Widow Merrow

Bess Merrow smelled the trouble before she saw it, the way she smelled weather — a sourness on the wind off the water that had nothing to do with fish.

She was mending nets on the quay outside the Anchor & Eel when the boy came into view. Small thing, seven or eight perhaps, trailing Emrys Thackeray like a shadow that hadn't yet learned to walk properly. He kept to her left side, always left, and his head turned at every uniform in sight — a customs man here, a coastguard there — with a quickness that Bess recognized from years of watching men who'd been on the wrong end of a revenue cutter's lantern.

That was no stray. That was a child who knew what soldiers did.

"Well," she said to her net, working a knot loose with her thumbnail. "There's a story walking about in short trousers."

She'd heard the talk, of course. Harbor towns ran on two currencies: herring and gossip, and gossip bought more. The clinic woman had taken in an abandoned boy some weeks back, mute as a stone, wouldn't speak nor hardly eat. The constable had come round twice asking questions he didn't seem to want answered. And now there were officers in town — proper officers, not the usual demobbed strays — asking after children.

Bess had buried two sons in France and a husband to the sea, and she had developed over those losses a fine-tuned ear for the sound of officialdom lying. It had a particular music to it. Smooth. Rehearsed. Like a hymn sung by men who'd never prayed.

The boy looked up suddenly and caught her watching him.

Most children would have looked away. This one held her gaze — dark eyes in a thin face, older than they had any right to be — and Bess felt something turn over in her chest, slow and heavy, like ballast shifting in a hold.

*Oh,* she thought. *You've seen it too, haven't you. Whatever it is.*

Emrys Thackeray said something low to the boy and steered him toward the chandlery. Before they reached it, Bess set down her netting needle, stood, and crossed the quay with the rolling gait of forty years on deck plates.

"Mrs. Thackeray." She let her voice carry just enough. "A word, when you've a moment."

Emrys turned. Up close the nurse looked tired in the way that bone-deep tired looks on women who don't sleep so much as wait for sleep to find them. "Mrs. Merrow."

"Bess will do. Mrs. Merrow was my mother-in-law, and God rest her, she was a horror." Bess glanced down at the boy, who had gone very still, one small hand fisted in Emrys's coat. "And this is the famous stray."

"Sparrow," Emrys said. "He's called Sparrow."

"Is he now." Bess crouched, unhurried, bringing herself level with the child. She didn't reach for him — she'd learned from cats and half-wild things that you let them come to you — but she studied his face openly, the way she'd study a chart before running a channel at night. Chapped lips. A scar along the jaw, old enough to have healed clean. Salt-cracked knuckles on a boy who couldn't be more than eight.

"You'll have been to sea," she said quietly. "Or near it. I can tell. You've got the look of a lad who knows which way the wind sits."

The boy's eyes flicked toward the harbor mouth. Toward the open water. Then back to her.

Something passed between them then, brief as a spark off a grinding wheel. Bess had smuggled brandy, tobacco, unregistered immigrants, and once — a story she told no one — a French countess with a price on her head, and in all those years she had learned to read people the way she read tide tables. This child was carrying cargo. Heavy cargo. The kind that sank ships.

She stood, knees cracking, and fixed Emrys with a look. "Come by the boat this evening. The *Kestrel's Fancy*, third berth past the customs shed. Come after dark, mind, and come alone with the lad if you can manage it without being seen doing it. I've a stove that needs lighting and tea that wants drinking, and I'll thank you not to argue, because I'm a widow and widows are allowed to be bossy. It's in the prayer book somewhere."

Emrys's brow creased. "Why?"

"Because," Bess said, turning back toward her nets, "you've got a look of a woman carrying something too heavy for one pair of shoulders, and I've got a boat, and I don't much care for the cut of the officers who've been sniffing round this harbor lately. That's why. Evening, now."

* * *

The *Kestrel's Fancy* was a thirty-foot lugger that Bess had inherited along with the widowhood and the debts, and she loved her the way one loves a temperamental relation — fiercely, exasperatedly, with full knowledge that the affection would never entirely be returned. By the time dusk came down gray and cold over the harbor, Bess had the stove lit, the kettle on, and the cabin lamp turned low so its glow wouldn't show through the portholes.

They came at half past seven, slipping along the quay shadows like a couple of conspirators in a melodrama. Bess watched them from the galley window and permitted herself a grim smile. The nurse moved well for a landswoman. The boy moved better.

"Hatch is open," she called softly. "Mind your heads."

Emrys came down the companionway first, then lifted Sparrow through after her. In the close warmth of the cabin, lit amber by the stove, the boy looked younger and somehow more fragile — all wrists and collarbones under a coat that had been made for someone bigger. He pressed himself against the bulkhead near the ladder, cataloguing the exits. Bess pretended not to notice and poured three cups of tea strong enough to trot a horse across.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. It's not a palace but it floats, which is more than can be said for most of what passes for civilization these days."

Emrys sat on the bench by the table. Sparrow remained standing until Bess shoved the second cup across the wood toward him, and then — slowly, watchfully — he lowered himself onto the opposite bench, cup cradled in both hands, steam rising past his chin.

"Now." Bess settled across from them, elbows on the table. "I'll not waste your evening nor mine with circling about. Something's happened to that boy. Something bad enough that men in good coats are asking questions in my harbor, and bad enough that you're frightened — and don't tell me you're not, Mrs. Thackeray, because I've seen frightened since before you were weaned, and you've got the whole look of it. So. Tell me what I've put my nose into, because I'll tell you straight: I've already put it in."

For a long moment Emrys said nothing. Her hands were wrapped around her cup, and Bess watched her weigh something — not whether to speak, but how much.

"You run contraband," Emrys said finally. Not accusatory. Just stating the shape of the world.

"I run *goods*," Bess corrected. "Some of 'em happen to have escaped the attention of His Majesty's customs service. Difference is mostly paperwork. What's your point?"

"My point is that helping us could cost you."

"Lady, I lost two sons to a war fought by men who lied about why they started it and lied again about how it ended. My Tom went at Loos. My Danny went on the Somme, though it took the army eleven months to admit it, and the letter they sent me got his battalion wrong." Bess's voice stayed flat and even, the way it did when she talked about the boys, because if she let any feeling into it she'd never get the words out at all. "I've already paid everything this country can take from me. There's nothing left to threaten me with except more of the same. So the cost doesn't frighten me. What frightens me is dying comfortable."

The stove ticked. The hull creaked against her mooring lines, that old familiar breathing.

Emrys looked at her for a long moment, and then she began to talk. Low and steady, she laid it out: the note pinned to the boy's coat — *forget the face* — the silence, the flinching at uniforms, the drawings. At the word *drawings* she drew a folded paper from her coat and opened it on the table between them.

Bess looked down at it and felt the cabin go cold around her despite the stove.

It was a house. A cottage, drawn in a child's wobbly hand, with smoke curling from a chimney — except the smoke was drawn in hard black strokes, scribbled over and over until the paper nearly tore, and the windows were flames, and there were small figures in the doorway. Figures with their arms up.

"I've seen that chimney style before," Bess said quietly. "That's a Cornish coast cottage. Or it was." She looked up. "Where?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Emrys's voice was tight. "There's a hamlet south of here — Carreglan, on the old charts. Officially evacuated in the spring of '17. Officially standing empty ever since. Except the boy draws it burning, and he draws it burning the same way every time, and last week a fisherman told me he'd seen army lorries on that road in November. After the armistice, Mrs. Merrow. *After.*"

Bess sat back. Outside, somewhere across the water, a ship's bell rang the hour, and the sound seemed to come from a long way off.

She thought of the officers in town. The polite ones, the well-fed ones, the ones who asked their questions gently and wrote down the answers and thanked everyone for their cooperation. She thought of the constable's second visit, and how he'd avoided her eyes.

"And the men asking after him," she said. "They're not from the welfare society."

"No."

"They're the ones who did it. Or sent the ones who did."

"They're the ones who want it forgotten," Emrys said. "Which may amount to the same thing."

Bess looked at the boy. Sparrow had gone rigid at the mention of the lorries, his knuckles white around his teacup, and when Bess met his eyes she saw it there plain as a lighthouse beam: not fear exactly, or not only fear. A kind of terrible waiting. The look of someone standing at a door, waiting to see if the person on the other side would open it or bar it.

Bess had spent her whole life on the wrong side of official paper. Smugglers, poachers, wreckers' daughters — her family had been outside the law since before the law took much interest in poor folk either way. She knew what it was to be a name in somebody's ledger, a line item, a problem to be managed. And she knew — the way you know a squall is coming, in your teeth and your bad knee — that this child was in danger of becoming exactly that: a line item. An omission. A face to be forgotten.

Well. Bess Merrow had never in her life done what she was told, and she didn't propose to start at her age.

"Right," she said. She stood, went to the locker beneath the starboard bunk, and hauled out a chart case, thumping it onto the table. "Here's what I've got, and you listen close because I'll only say it once. I know every inch of this coast from here to Falmouth — every cove, every channel, every spot where a revenue man can't follow because his boat draws too much water. If that boy needs moving, I can move him. Quietly, at night, by water, past anyone they care to post. I've done it with cargo worth ten times what he weighs and half as precious."

Emrys's breath caught. "You'd do that."

"I'd do more than that." Bess flipped the chart case open and spread the coastal chart across the table, weighting the corners with teacups. Her finger came down on a small indentation in the cliffs, twelve miles south. "Porthollow. Old smuggling hole, deep water right up to the rocks, invisible from the road. If it comes to running, we run from there. And I've friends — fisherfolk, farmers, a vicar in Penzance who owes me his reputation and several other things less repeatable. Word travels my way faster than the telegraph, and none of it shows up in anybody's report." She tapped the chart. "Whatever's happening, whatever they're covering up, this coast remembers. The sea keeps everything, Mrs. Thackeray. It's only the men in offices who forget."

Sparrow had leaned forward. He was looking at the chart — really looking, tracing the coastline with one cracked fingertip, and then his finger stopped. South of the harbor. A small notch in the cliffs.

He tapped it. Twice. Then he looked up at Bess, and his eyes were wet and blazing at once.

Bess felt the hair rise on her arms.

"That's it," Emrys whispered. "That's Carreglan. That's where he's from."

The boy nodded, once, hard. Then he pulled the drawing toward him and set it beside the chart, aligning the little burning cottage with the notch in the coastline, precise as a navigator taking a fix. When he looked up again, he wasn't crying. He was waiting. Watching them both with that door-open-or-bar-it stare.

Bess reached across the table and covered his small hand with her own, rough palm against chilled skin.

"Listen to me, young man," she said, gruff and low. "I don't know what you saw, and I won't ask you to say it. But I know what it is to lose everything to men who sign papers afterward and call it done. So here's my word, and my word's the only thing I own outright: while I've a boat that floats and breath in my body, nobody lays a hand on you. Nobody. They'd best bring their own coffin, because they'll need a place to sleep eventually, and I'll see they get it."

The boy turned his hand over and gripped her thumb. Small fingers, fierce grip. Held on.

Bess cleared her throat and glared at the teapot as though it had said something untoward. "Right. That's settled, then. Now — conditions."

"You're setting conditions?" Emrys said.

"I'm a businesswoman. One condition." Bess held up a single finger. "You tell me nothing you don't have to, and I ask nothing I don't need to know. Cleaner that way, if it goes wrong. But—" and here her voice dropped, and the gruffness fell away from it like a coat taken off, leaving something older and harder underneath, "—if there's truth to be had out of this, if there's names to be said and a reckoning to be had, I want in on it. Not for payment. For Tom and Danny. For every mother who got a printed lie in a buff envelope and was expected to say thank you. That's my price. The truth, when it comes. Nothing else."

Emrys extended her hand across the chart. "When it comes, you'll have it. Every name."

Bess shook it. Her grip was a rope-burn grip; she felt the nurse's answering strength and revised her opinion of the woman upward another notch.

"One more thing," Bess said, gathering the cups. "There's a man been asking after you at the Anchor & Eel. London coat, ink-stained fingers, drinks like a man who's trying to drown something specific. Says he's a writer. Says he's looking for a story." She snorted. "In my experience, Mrs. Thackeray, men like that are either the best friends you'll ever have or the worst trouble you'll ever meet, and it's usually both at once. He asked me about you tonight. Asked if I knew where you'd found the boy."

Alarm sharpened Emrys's face. "What did you tell him?"

"Told him I keep my nose out of other folks' business." Bess grinned, showing the gaps in her teeth. "Which is the biggest lie I've told since I swore to the customs man that the French brandy in my hold was vinegar. But he's not going away, whoever he is, and he's asking his questions in places where answers travel. If he's yours, better you find him before the wrong ears do. If he's theirs—" she shrugged, and the lamplight caught the silver in her cropped gray hair, "—then we'll know soon enough, one way or the other."

She walked them up onto the deck. The night had cleared; stars in the rigging, the harbor black and still, the smell of tar and salt and coming frost. On the quay, a figure in a long coat stood under the lamp by the customs shed, hat brim low, watching the water. Watching, Bess noted, the *Kestrel's Fancy*.

"That'll be him now," she murmured. "London coat. Been there twenty minutes."

Emrys pulled Sparrow close against her side. "Do you know him?"

"Don't know him. Know *of* him — Wren's the name he gave at the pub. Aldous Wren." Bess watched the figure push off from the rail and begin, unhurried, to walk along the quay — not toward them, not away, just walking, the way a man walks when he wants to be seen deciding something. "Correspondent, he says. Says he was at the front till they threw him out. Says the papers won't print what he writes anymore."

"Aldous Wren," Emrys repeated slowly. "I've heard that name. Years ago. He used to be famous."

"Famous men fall farthest," Bess said. "Question is whether this one fell or was pushed — and which side of the wall he landed on." She turned to the boy, touched two fingers briefly to her forehead in a little salute. "Night, Sparrow. Keep your feet dry and your eyes open. Both of you — come by tomorrow, low tide, and I'll show you Porthollow on the big chart. If we're going to be criminals together, we may as well learn the coastline."

They went down the gangway into the shadows, the nurse and the silent boy, and Bess stood at her rail watching them go, feeling something in her chest that she hadn't felt since before the telegrams came — a pulling, a leaning-forward, the old sea-feeling of a wind rising that might carry you anywhere.

Behind her, the cabin lamp still burned over the chart of the coast, and the little notch in the cliffs where a village had been, and was not, waited in the lamplight like an open wound.

On the quay, the man in the London coat stopped walking. Turned. Looked directly at her across the black water.

And raised his hat.
