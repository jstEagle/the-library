## 9. Ledger Against Report

The clinic after midnight had a different pulse than the clinic by day. Emrys knew its rhythms the way she knew her own heartbeat: the tick of the stove cooling in the back room, the creak of the salt-swollen doorframe, and above it all — when the wind came off the water at a certain angle — the long low groan of the harbor chain, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

She sat at the worktable with every lamp in the building lit except Sparrow's room, which she'd left dark on purpose. The boy slept better with darkness. Light made him watchful; watchfulness made him wakeful; and wakefulness brought the nightmares that left him clawing at his own throat as though pulling smoke out of his lungs.

Across from her, Aldous Wren spread the last of his papers flat with both hands, smoothing them the way a priest smooths an altar cloth.

"Well," he said. "I've been waiting three years to do this properly."

"Do what?"

"Build a wall of paper high enough that they can't walk around it." He looked up, and in the lamplight his face was older than it had seemed on the quay that morning, the dissolute charm burned away, leaving something leaner underneath. "They'll call it obsession, you know. When this comes out. *A disgraced correspondent with a grievance.* They'll say I invented half of it."

"They'll say worse about me," Emrys said. "They'll say I invented all of mine."

"Then we'd best make certain we haven't." He tapped the table. "Name by name, Mrs. Thackeray. That's the only way this works. Dates against dates. Names against names. If we're wrong by so much as one man, they'll use him to discredit the other hundred."

She pulled the ledger toward her across the table, and for a moment her hand simply rested on the cover — cracked leather, tide-stained, swollen with three years of entries. She had carried it through Ypres and Étaples and the typhoid wards at Rouen. She had carried it home wrapped in oilcloth inside her kit bag, past the customs officers at Dover who searched everything else and never thought to search a nurse's grief.

It was not a diary. Diaries were for people who believed their own lives mattered.

It was a ledger.

*Private memoranda,* read the first page, in her younger handwriting, *of persons deceased or unaccounted for, whose names do not appear in official returns.*

She opened it now to the winter entries and turned it to face Aldous.

"Read," she said.

* * *

He read aloud, slowly, because she asked him to — not for his benefit but for hers. She wanted to hear the names in someone else's voice. For three years they had lived only in her own, murmured over the stove some nights like prayers, or accusations, whichever the hour demanded.

"*Corporal Daniel Ainsworth, 2nd Battalion, listed missing 14 October, reported dead in regimental gazette of November — but no burial record, no effects returned, mother's inquiry of December unanswered.*"

"*Private Thomas Ellery, wounded, evacuated from the aid station at —*" She stopped him there. "The place names are why I kept them separate. Look."

She drew a second notebook from the drawer — thinner, cheaper paper, the sort sold to schoolchildren — and opened it beside the ledger. This was the map book. Not a proper map at first, just her own hand-drawn coastline, but over the months she had bought Admiralty charts of the whole littoral and marked them herself: villages, depots, aid stations, the small geography of the war's neglected edges.

Aldous leaned over it and went very still.

"You've marked them," he said. "All of them."

"Every village where a name in my ledger came from and could not be confirmed dead. Every hamlet where a returning soldier told me something that didn't match the dispatches." Her finger traced the coast eastward, past the naval town, past the fishing ports, to a stretch of shoreline where her pencil marks clustered thick as bruising. "I told myself it was habit. Bookkeeping. A nurse's tidiness. But look at it, Mr. Wren. Look at the pattern."

He was already looking. His forefinger came down beside hers, stopping on a small penciled cross.

"Here," he said. "That's where your boy's drawings point. And here—" his finger moved to a cluster of names in the ledger's margin, seven of them, each annotated in her hand with a date in late October, "*—these are the men your ledger lists missing from the garrison company stationed near it. Seven men, Mrs. Thackeray. And the official account says—?"

"The official account says the garrison company was rotated south in September." She recited it flatly, having read the dispatch so many times she could have set it in type. "'No engagements. No casualties. Routine redeployment.'"

"Routine redeployment." He said it the way Bess Merrow said a bad tide forecast — with the professional contempt of someone who knew the water personally. "Seven men don't evaporate in a routine redeployment."

"They desert." She said it before he could. "That's the answer they'll give. That's the answer they *have* given — to the mothers who wrote. Desertion, or transfer with clerical error, or lost at sea. Each family got a different story. I know because three of the families wrote to me afterward, asking if I'd heard anything in France, since I'd nursed near their sons' battalion. Different stories, Mr. Wren. Same week. Same clerk, probably, inventing them over strong tea."

Aldous sat back. For a long moment the only sound was the stove ticking down and, faintly, the sea.

"You knew," he said quietly. "You knew before I ever arrived. Before the boy. You had the pattern in your own hand and you called it bookkeeping."

"I called it nothing. That was the trick of it." She heard the bitterness come up her throat like bile and let it. "History happens elsewhere, Mr. Wren. That's what I believed — what we're all taught, we small keepers of small places. Wars are decided in ministries. Truths are established by commissions. My job was to bind wounds and record names, and the recording was—" she touched the ledger, "—sentimental. Private. A woman's keepsake. I told myself the ledger wasn't evidence. It was mourning."

"And now?"

"Now I've held a child while he shook himself apart over a burning house I can find on my own chart." She turned the ledger pages with deliberate care, back and back, to October. "And now it's ammunition. Say it plainly, Mr. Wren. You came here to say it plainly. So will I."

He almost smiled. It was not a happy smile. "Then let's build the case."

* * *

They worked until the lamps burned low and the stove needed feeding twice. It was the strangest night's work Emrys had done since the war — stranger even than the surgery tents, where the work was terrible but simple. This was archaeology. This was raising the drowned.

Aldous produced his materials piece by piece from the oilskin packet he'd carried since London: clippings from provincial papers, a shipping notice, a requisition form he'd photographed before leaving his last post ("I was supposed to burn the negatives; I developed them instead"), and — most damning — the text of two official communiqués concerning the coastal district, printed side by side in his neat journalist's hand.

"Communiqué one, dated the ninth of October," he read. "'Mopping-up operations concluded. Civilian populations of the coastal sector evacuated to interior camps. No significant resistance encountered. Property secured.'"

"Evacuated," Emrys said.

"Evacuated. Now communiqué two, dated the twenty-third — fourteen days later. 'Inspection of the coastal sector confirms successful resettlement. Villages stand ready for demilitarization.' Villages *stand*, Mrs. Thackeray. Present tense. Standing villages, two weeks after everyone supposedly left them." He laid the papers down. "But your chart says—?"

"My chart says a fisherman named Collier — no relation to our lighthouse man, a different Collier entirely — came through my clinic in November with burns on both hands and would not say how he got them. His name went in the ledger under *treated, cause unstated.* And his boat's registration, which I copied because he was too shaken to speak of anything else, was registered out of—" she put her finger on the coast, on the little cluster of crosses, "—there. The village that stands ready for demilitarization."

"That isn't evidence."

"No. It's thread. Pull it."

So they pulled. Through the small hours they built the timeline on the backs of pharmacy wrapping-paper, pinned together into a long scroll across the worktable:

**October 9th** — evacuation declared complete.
**October 11th** — garrison company's last confirmed muster (from a letter a widow had shown Emrys, the envelope preserved).
**October 13th–17th** — the five days covered by the seven missing names in her ledger, men of that same garrison company, none of whom appear on any subsequent roll Aldous could trace.
**October 19th** — a supply barge logged delivering *construction stores* to the sector, per the shipping notice.
**October 23rd** — villages standing ready.
**November** — burned fisherman at her clinic. Burned houses in a child's drawings. A village that appears on the new Admiralty chart as a *ruin, unmarked by name* — Aldous laid the old chart and the new side by side and the omission was so clean it looked surgical.

"They didn't erase the village from the records," Aldous said, staring at the two charts. "They erased it from the *map*. Do you understand the difference? Records can be subpoenaed. Maps get reprinted. Every sailor, every surveyor, every schoolchild gets the new version, and in ten years nobody remembers there was ever a question. That's how they'll win, if they win. Not with lies. With editions."

Emrys looked at the new chart for a long time. Then she reached for her pen.

"What are you doing?"

"Correcting it." She wrote the village's name — the real name, the name Sparrow's drawings had dragged up from wherever drowned things wait — in the margin of the new chart, small and precise, and beneath it the seven garrison names from the ledger, and beneath those: *witnesses exist.* "There. Now there are two editions."

* * *

Near three in the morning, a floorboard sighed in the corridor, and they both froze — pen lifted, papers still — until the sound resolved into the particular shuffle of Mrs. Penhale, the charwoman, making her dawn rounds early because her rheumatism liked her upright before the damp settled. Her footsteps passed Sparrow's door, paused a moment — Emrys heard the soft click of the woman looking in, the way she did every night without being asked — and moved on down toward the kitchen to put the kettle on.

The tension went out of the room by degrees.

"She checks on him," Emrys said. "Every night. Never says a word about it. Half the town does things like that now, quietly. Leaving milk on the step. Mending his coat. Nobody asks me who he is anymore — they've decided he's ours, whoever he is, and that's the end of it."

"Your town has more sense than my newspapers."

"My town has buried enough sons to know what a lie smells like." She gathered the scroll of timeline, rolling it carefully. "Mr. Wren — Aldous. I want to ask you something and I want the ugly answer."

"The ugly answer is usually the useful one."

"When this goes wherever it's going — tribunal, inquiry, commission, whatever shape it takes — what happens to a private ledger? Mine. Kept by a civilian, compiled from soldiers' letters and refugees' talk and one burned fisherman who wouldn't explain himself. Will they even admit it? Or will they laugh it out of the room as gossip in a pretty hand?"

Aldous considered her honestly, and she was grateful to him for not flinching.

"On its own? Laughed out. Hearsay, sentiment, no chain of custody, compiled by an interested party." He held up a finger. "But ledgers don't testify alone. They corroborate. Your October entries match the muster gap I found independently. Your treated-and-unstated fisherman matches the burn pattern in the depot requisitions. Your chart matches the child's drawings drawn by a child who has never seen your chart. Alone, each is a thread. Together—" he gestured at the scroll, the charts, the ledger lying open between them like a witness on a stand, "—together they're a rope. And ropes, Mrs. Thackeray, hold things. Bodies. Boats. Men to account."

"And the child?"

The question came out of her quieter than she intended.

Aldous was quiet too, for a moment. Outside, the tide was turning; the groan of the harbor chain sounded again, long and low.

"The child is the one thing they cannot argue with," he said finally. "Reports can be disputed. Ledgers can be dismissed. Charts can be reprinted. But a living boy who saw it — if it ever comes to that, if it ever comes to him standing up in front of men in uniforms and saying what he saw—" Aldous's jaw tightened. "They will try very hard to make sure it never comes to that. You understand that, don't you? We've spent tonight making ourselves dangerous. From tomorrow, we aren't a nurse and a broken journalist anymore. We're a threat with documentation."

"I've been a threat before. In France they called us *the ones who write things down.*" She closed the ledger and set her hand flat upon it, feeling the warmth the lamp had put into the leather. "Do you know what I've understood tonight, sitting here? All those years I thought I was keeping this out of weakness. Sentiment. Because I couldn't bear to let the names go. But that's exactly backward. The bearing-letting-go was theirs. The War Office let go of these men the moment it was convenient. The clerks let go. The historians will let go. Someone had to be the one who *wouldn't*, and it fell to a harbor nurse because no one else would do it."

She stood, stiff-backed, and began stacking the papers into their careful order — ledger, chart, timeline, clippings — a dossier now, whether she liked the word or not.

"It's ammunition," she said. "You said it yourself, the first hour. Name by name. Well. Then we treat it like ammunition. Dry, counted, and never left where it can be stolen."

As though the words had summoned it, her eye caught on the ledger's spine as she slid it into the locked drawer — caught on something wrong, a small wrongness she'd have missed any other night but couldn't miss tonight, having handled this book nightly for three years until its weight lived in her wrist.

The ribbon marker sat four pages further in than she'd left it.

She froze. Opened the drawer again. Turned to the marked page.

The entry for the eleventh of October was there. The entry for the thirteenth was there.

Between them, where the twelfth should have been —

The stub of a torn page, ragged at the gutter, cut close and quick with a blade.

Emrys heard her own breath go thin. Across the room, Aldous rose slowly to his feet, following her stare, and the lamplight caught his face going gray.

"Mrs. Thackeray," he said softly. "Who else has keys to this room?"
