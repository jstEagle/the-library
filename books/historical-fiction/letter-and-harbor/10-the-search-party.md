## 10. The Search Party

The boots woke her before the gulls did.

Emrys stood at the clinic window with the lamp unlit, watching them come down Harbor Row in the blue hour before dawn — six soldiers in marching order, rifles slung, their breath steaming like small ghosts. They moved with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been made to account for themselves. Behind them, at the corner by the chandlery, stood a staff car with its engine running, and beside the car, a figure she knew by his posture alone: Captain Hale, hands clasped behind his back, watching the harbor like a man inspecting a garden he intended to buy.

Her first thought was not of Sparrow. It was of the ledger, which was foolish and telling — the boy was asleep in the loft above her surgery, wrapped in a blanket that still smelled of camphor, and her mind went to a book of names hidden beneath a loose floorboard, as if the dead could not look after themselves. Perhaps they couldn't. That was rather the point of her.

She dressed quickly, hands steady in the way they had learned to be steady at Passchendaele, when steadiness was the only instrument left to her. Down in the surgery, the morning was grey and salt-smelling. She filled the kettle, set out her instruments as if this were any ordinary Tuesday, and listened to the boots come nearer.

They knocked at the door the way soldiers knock — not asking.

She opened it to a young sergeant with a face still soft with boyhood and a list in his gloved hand. "Madam Thackeray? Captain Hale's compliments. We're conducting a search of the harbor district for a missing person. A child. We have reason to believe he may have been taken in."

"Taken in," Emrys repeated. "By whom?"

"That's what we're establishing, ma'am." His eyes went past her shoulder into the clinic. "May we look about?"

"Have you a warrant?"

Something flickered across his face — surprise, then the careful blankness of a man who had been briefed for exactly this question. "The military doesn't require one to recover a ward of the Crown."

"He is not a ward of the Crown. He is a child abandoned on a public doorstep, which makes him a matter for the civil authorities, the parish, and God — in roughly that order. The last time I checked, Captain Hale held a commission in none of those." She said it evenly, and heard, underneath her own voice, the stranger who had been living in her throat for two weeks now. "You may come in and see an empty surgery, Sergeant, if it helps you fill out your list. You may not open drawers, and you may not go upstairs."

The boy-soldier hesitated. Behind him, down the row, she could see his men fanning out toward Bess Merrow's boathouse, and her heart performed a slow, deliberate drop, like a stone into a well.

"Ma'am," the sergeant said, and there was something almost apologetic in it, "if you're sheltering him, it would go better for you to say so now. The Captain's not a vindictive man."

"No," Emrys agreed. "He's a thorough one. I've found that's worse."

* * *

She had lied to dying men for four years. *You're improving. The letter's on its way. You'll see the spring.* She had told those lies the way one administers morphia — precisely, mercifully, with a clear understanding of the dose. But she had never lied to a healthy man with a rifle, standing in her own doorway, in her own country, in peacetime.

That was the thought that kept returning to her as she led the sergeant through the surgery, narrating its emptiness: *this is what it is to be on the other side of the uniform.* At the front she had been a fixture, a gray angel, untouchable in her usefulness. Here, in her own town, she was a woman with a locked loft and a floorboard full of names, and the law wore the same khaki it had worn at Ypres, and it had come up her walk.

"Who's your usual patient load?" the sergeant asked, trying at professional courtesy.

"Fishermen with ruined hands. Children with coughs. Wives with bruises they won't explain." She watched him write nothing down. "And you, Sergeant? Where were you posted?"

"Flanders. Then the Rhine." He said it without pride.

"Did you burn any villages there?"

The question left her before she could weigh it — a reckless, uncharacteristic thing, and she watched it land. The sergeant stopped at the foot of the stairs and looked at her, and for a moment the boy was gone from his face and the soldier was there instead, and behind the soldier something older and wearier, something that had seen what she had seen and had chosen a different way to carry it.

"Ma'am," he said quietly, "I follow orders. That's all I am. If the child's here, the Captain will find him, with your help or without it. And if he's not—" He glanced up the stairs, just once, and she understood that he was not going to look. He was giving her the stairs. He was buying himself a clean list. "—then he's not."

"Then he's not," Emrys said.

He wrote something. They walked the surgery together like two people at a funeral of someone neither had liked, and he noted the cot, the cabinet, the basin, and left through the front door without once looking up.

She stood in the doorway and watched him rejoin his men, and the exhilaration came on her like a fever — sudden, hot, disgraceful. *He didn't look. He didn't look.* She had done it. She had stood in her own doorway and lied to the King's Army and been believed, and somewhere above her head a child slept on, breathing, alive, unrecorded. She pressed her hand flat against the doorpost to feel the wood, to feel the morning, to feel the fact of it: *justice outside the law begins here, in a lie told kindly to a tired boy in a uniform.*

Then she saw the boathouse, and the fever broke.

* * *

Two soldiers stood at the doors of Merrow's shed, and a third was inside — she could hear him, the clank and scrape of a search conducted without much hope. Bess Merrow stood on her own threshold with her arms crossed, a woman built like the breakwater, watching them go through her nets with an expression of vast, contemptuous patience.

"—told you, son, it's a cargo shed. Fish, rope, and the smell of both. If I'm hiding the Crown's lost property under my herring, you're welcome to the herring."

Emrys crossed the distance at a walk, forcing herself not to run. "Bess. Mrs. Merrow." She put a hand on the older woman's arm — an old patient's privilege — and felt the tension in her like a hawser. "Is everything quite all right here?"

"Fine as paint, doctor. They're just having a look at my winter stock." Bess's eyes, small and bright as a gull's, found Emrys's and held them with a message in them Emrys could not read. *Or could she?* There was something in the boathouse. There had to be. Sparrow had been at the clinic at dawn — she had checked, she had *touched his shoulder* — but the loft had a window, and Bess had a boat, and between the two of them a plan might have been made that Emrys had not been told of, because they had decided she was the one who'd be searched.

The soldier inside emerged, shaking his head. "Nothing, Sergeant. Barrels of salted fish. Some of them reeking enough to be hiding a regiment."

The sergeant — her sergeant, the one who hadn't looked — consulted his list. "Mrs. Merrow. We'll want to come back."

"You'll want to bring a stronger stomach," Bess said.

They moved off down the row, boots ringing, and the staff car at the corner pulled away, and only then did Emrys allow herself to breathe. Bess turned to her, and the gull-bright eyes were laughing now, and furious, both at once.

"You told them he was upstairs," Bess said, low.

"I told them nothing. I stood in the way, which is different."

"It's not different. It's the same thing with better manners." She jerked her chin toward the water. "He's been in my boat since four this morning. Under the tarp, in the fish hold, with a flask of tea and a prayer. Wren rowed him out at first light. I told you I'd keep him safe, and you — you *brave, stupid woman* — you stood in your own doorway and perjured yourself to a search party without so much as a by-your-leave."

"You didn't tell me."

"No. Because you'd have said no, and then said yes, and then lain awake all night deciding, and I haven't got time for your conscience, Emrys. Neither has he." Bess's voice dropped further. "But listen. Listen now. They'll be back, and next time it won't be a sergeant with a list. Hale came down the row himself. He looked at my shed the way a man looks at a woman he means to marry — like he already owns it. We need him out of the harbor, or gone around, and we need it soon."

Emrys looked out at the water, at the grey swell beyond the breakwater, where somewhere a rowboat bobbed with a silent child in its hold, and she felt the two halves of the morning — the fever-heat of the lie, the cold drop of the search — settle into something harder and more durable than either. Not exhilaration. Not guilt. Purpose.

"Then we'd best get him back before dark," she said, "and we'd best decide, all of us, what we do next. Because I have looked at that man's list, Bess, and I know how it ends. It ends with a tribunal and a sealed record and a boy who never existed."

Bess spat, ceremonially, toward the water. "Then we'd best not let him exist quietly."

* * *

They brought Sparrow back at dusk, rowing in with the last of the light, and Emrys stood on the shingle to meet the boat. He climbed out stiff with cold and salt, and when she crouched to take his hands they were freezing, and he let her hold them, which was itself a small revolution. His eyes went past her to the clinic, to the harbor row, to the dark, reading the world the way he read everything — for exits, for uniforms, for fire.

"You're safe," she told him. "You're home. You did well."

He looked at her then, and there was a question in his face she had seen before in the dying, at the very end, when the morphine loosened them: *was it worth it? what did it cost?* She could not answer him yet. She only squeezed his cold hands and said, "Come inside. There's soup."

But at the door of the clinic, Aldous Wren caught her sleeve. He was soaked to the waist and shivering and trying not to show either, and his face had the grey, lit look of a man carrying news he both dreaded and needed.

"Emrys. The sergeant who searched you — I made inquiries this afternoon, quietly." He glanced at Sparrow, who had gone still on the step. "He was at the village. After. He was one of the ones ordered to burn it, and he refused a direct order to shoot the survivors, and he was broken to sergeant for it and shipped home. Hale doesn't know his own search party is full of men who know exactly what happened. And the sergeant knows the boy is here."

Emrys felt the shingle shift under her feet, the whole harbor tilting.

"Then why," she said slowly, "didn't he look upstairs?"

Wren's breath came out white in the cold. "Because, Emrys — I think you've just found your first witness."
