## 11. A Witness in the Lighthouse

Aldous Wren had spent eleven years learning that the truth rarely announced itself. It did not march up to a man and present its credentials. It arrived sideways, in the pause before an answer, in the thing a source almost said, in the smell of paraffin where there should have been none. So when Emrys Thackeray said *the lighthouse*, he understood at once why she'd said it quietly, and why she'd said it at all.

They stood at the end of the north mole with the wind coming straight off the water, hard enough to make speech a negotiation. Below them the harbor lay gray and restless, fishing boats knocking against their moorings like impatient horses. Above them, on the black shoulder of the headland, the old lighthouse stood dark — decommissioned since before the war, its lantern room empty, its paint peeling in long gray ribbons. The Admiralty had listed it for demolition twice. Twice the paperwork had been lost. It was, Aldous thought, exactly the kind of place where forgotten things went to wait.

"The sergeant's name is Callum Reeve," Emrys said, her collar turned up against the spray. "I checked the regimental lists. He was attached to the garrison company that rotated through the coast villages in February. He was there."

"You're certain it's the same man."

"I'm certain of the way he looked at Sparrow when he came through my clinic pretending to count beds." Her jaw was set in the particular way Aldous had learned meant she had already decided something and was merely allowing him to catch up. "And I'm certain of what you told me on the shingle. He refused an order. Men who refuse orders like that don't forget what the order was for."

Aldous watched the lighthouse. Gulls wheeled around its gallery rail, screaming their grievances at the sea. "If he's still in touch with anyone from the village—"

"He's not in touch with anyone. He's hiding from everyone. There's a difference." She pulled her gloves tighter. "Bess says he takes his leave alone. Walks the headland path every evening at dusk, rain or no rain, and comes back down after dark. Bess has watched him do it four nights running."

"Bess has been watching a sergeant major's movements," Aldous said, "with binoculars, presumably, while also running contraband brandy past two customs men. She is wasted on this coastline."

"She'd agree with you. Loudly." But Emrys almost smiled, and then the smile went out like a lamp behind shutters. "Aldous. If we go up that path tonight, and we're wrong about him — if he reports us — Hale doesn't send a polite sergeant next time. He sends a warrant. He sends men who don't pretend to be counting beds."

"Then we shouldn't both go."

She turned to look at him fully, and in the flat winter light he saw again how young the war had left her — not young in years, but young in the way a burned house is young, everything essential exposed and weather-hardened all at once.

"You want to go alone."

"I want you here with Sparrow. If this goes wrong, someone has to be standing between that boy and whatever comes down the hill. And—" He hesitated, because the honest reason was heavier than the tactical one, and honesty was a discipline he'd only recently resumed practicing. "And because I've spent eleven years printing other men's lies. If this goes wrong, I'd rather it cost me than cost you."

"That's not noble, Wren. That's arithmetic."

"Yes," he said. "It's arithmetic. I've done the sums. I come out cheaper."

* * *

He climbed the headland path alone as the light failed, which felt fitting. The path was old fisherman's track, rutted and slick with frost, switchbacking up through gorse the color of rusted wire. Below him the town began to kindle — first the harbor lamps, then windows in the terraced streets, then the clinic's lit square at the water's edge, small as a playing card. He could see the shape of someone on the step. Emrys or the boy. He chose not to look long enough to know which. Looking back too often was how a man talked himself into turning around.

The lighthouse resolved out of the dusk in stages: first a darker darkness against the sky, then the bulk of it, then the door — a low iron door in the base of the tower, hanging open by a hand's width.

Open. Not broken, not forced. Open the way a door is left open by someone who wants to hear footsteps coming.

Aldous stood in the wind a moment and considered, not for the first time, the strange education of his profession. Eleven years as a correspondent taught a man three things: how to read a room, how to read a silence, and how to walk into a building that might contain either salvation or a court-martial. He had walked into such buildings in Antwerp and Galicia and a nameless village outside Ypres whose name he had printed wrong because the censor told him to. He had learned to think of each threshold as a headline waiting to be written correctly or incorrectly.

This one, he decided, would be written correctly or not at all.

He knocked on the open door. It seemed absurd and it seemed necessary, and he heard, faintly, a sharp intake of breath within.

"Mr. Reeve," he said to the gap. "My name is Aldous Wren. I used to write for the *Chronicle*. I'm not here for the army. May I come in?"

Nothing. The wind filled the pause with the sound of the sea working the rocks below, patient as a debt collector.

"I know about February," Aldous said. "I know about the eleventh. I know about the order given on the morning of the twelfth, after the armistice had already been signed, which means every man who followed it committed murder in peacetime and every man who refused it is the only honest soldier in that company. I'm not here to arrest anyone. Nobody's paying me. I'm here because I helped bury the story once and I intend to dig it up with my own hands."

Silence — but a different quality of silence now. The held-breath silence had loosened into something more like listening.

"There's a boy," Aldous said. "Eight years old, perhaps nine. He was left at a clinic in the harbor with a note that said *forget the face*. He hasn't spoken a word since. He draws the same burning house over and over until his pencil goes through the paper. I believe he's from your village. I believe you may be the last living man who can say so out loud."

Then, from inside, low and roughened: "How many drawings?"

Aldous closed his eyes. "Forty-one. Emrys counts them. She keeps a ledger of them, same as she keeps a ledger of everything else the reports leave out."

A long breath. Then the door swung wider, and a match flared, and Aldous stepped down into the dark of the tower.

* * *

Callum Reeve sat on an ammunition crate that predated him by fifty years, in a circle of candlelight at the foot of the spiral stair. He was younger than Aldous expected — thirty at most — with the heavy hands of a laborer and the eyes of a man who had seen something and been ordered to unsee it. His uniform coat hung on a bent nail by the door; he wore a fisherman's sweater beneath it, darned at the elbows. On the floor beside him, wrapped in sacking, a tin cup and a loaf of bread and a folded newspaper dated three weeks ago.

"You were followed?" Reeve asked. His voice had the flattened accent of the northern coast — the same coast, Aldous realized, as the drowned village. He'd come home to a hundred miles of shoreline and couldn't go the last twelve.

"No. Bess Merrow watches the harbor road. Anyone following me would have had to get past her first, and I'd pay money to see them try."

That earned him nothing. Reeve studied him with a soldier's unhurried appraisal, cataloguing hands, boots, the cut of his coat. "You said you buried the story. Say that plainer."

"In March I filed a dispatch from the coastal sector. Two thousand words on conditions among the fishing villages — requisitions, losses, resettlement. The piece mentioned a hamlet called Carreg Du had been evacuated under orders, population relocated inland, no casualties." Aldous crouched down across from him, knees complaining. "I knew it wasn't true before I wrote it. I'd spoken to a transport driver who'd passed through. He said there was nothing left to relocate. My editor killed what was left of the paragraph anyway, and I let him, and I took my byline on the rest, and I told myself the war was over and one more softened sentence wouldn't matter." He made himself hold the other man's gaze. "It mattered. It always matters. That's the whole discovery of my life, Mr. Reeve, and it came too late to be useful to anyone except, possibly, now."

Reeve looked away, out toward the bricked-up arrow slit where the candlelight couldn't reach. For a long time the only sound was the sea, arriving in long slow blows against the cliff below, like something enormous breathing in its sleep.

"They told us it was a security operation," he said finally. "Carreg Du. Smuggling routes, they said. Enemy agents landing at night, they said. We went in on the tenth of February to clear it. There were no agents. There were forty-one people and a church and a smokehouse, and half of them were too old to walk to the end of their own street." His hands opened and closed on his knees. "On the eleventh they marched them onto the quay and put them on two boats and told us they were being taken to resettlement housing at Milford. On the twelfth — the twelfth, Mr. Wren, the day after the guns stopped everywhere in Europe — the captain read out new orders. The boats hadn't gone to Milford. The village was to be burned and the official record amended. Evacuated, not destroyed. And any witnesses found returning were to be treated as — his word was *irregulars*."

Aldous kept very still. In eleven years he had learned that the worst moments of an interview were the ones where his own face might interrupt someone else's memory.

"I refused," Reeve said. "There were four of us who refused. Sergeant-major broke two of us down to private on the spot and told the other two they'd be shot for mutiny if they repeated it. Then he gave the order to a different squad, and they did it, and I stood on the ridge and watched the church go up." His voice didn't break. It did something worse — it went level, the way water goes level, flat and deep and carrying things underneath. "The boy. Was he on the quay?"

"We don't know. He won't tell us. He can't."

"Then how do you know he's from Carreg Du?"

"Because he draws the church," Aldous said. "Every drawing. Same angle — from the water, looking up the slipway. Burning. And there's a figure in the doorway of the smokehouse in some of them, a tall figure, arms out. Emrys thinks—" He stopped, recalibrated. Reeve's face had changed; the levelness had cracked. "You know the smokehouse."

"It's not a smokehouse." Reeve pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "It's the old lifeboat station. They converted it. And in February it was full of people who couldn't fit on the boats — the sick ones, the ones from the almshouse. The order said burn it with the rest." His hands dropped. "Someone got them out. Someone must have, because the boy was looking at it from the water, which means he was on a boat, which means—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "Whoever put that boy on a boat saved his life and then vanished, and the army has been looking for loose ends ever since. That's what the searches are. Not deserters. Loose ends."

"And you're one."

"I'm the loosest end there is." He laughed, briefly, without any humor in it. "I could testify. God knows I've rehearsed it every night since February, standing right here, saying it to the sea. Names, dates, orders given, orders refused. I could stand in front of any tribunal in the country and say it till they carried me out." He leaned forward, and the candlelight caught the wet in his eyes without spilling it. "But hear me, Mr. Wren. I have a wife in Aberdaron and a daughter who'll be six in April, and the army knows their street. The moment my name enters a record, Captain Hale doesn't need to touch me. He needs to do nothing at all except make sure certain men know where they sleep. Do you understand? Silence isn't cowardice for me. It's a wall I built around two people."

Aldous nodded slowly. He had heard this wall described before, by other men, in other wars — always in the same tone, the tone of a man describing a fortress he hated living in.

"I'm not asking you to knock it down tonight," he said.

"Then what are you asking?"

"I'm asking you to help us build a better one." He reached into his coat and brought out a slim oilcloth packet, and unwrapped it on his knee: Emrys's ledger — or rather, the pages Emrys had copied out for him, forty-one names in her small fierce hand, each with a date, a ward, a cause of death where one existed, a blank where the army's records said *no such person*. "This is a list of the dead of Carreg Du and the surrounding coast, compiled by a nurse who treated them before the war and recorded the aftermath after it. Every name on it is contradicted somewhere by an official report. Every blank on it is a person the reports insist never existed. What we need — what the tribunal will need, if we can force one to listen — is a witness who can connect these names to faces, faces to the quay, the quay to the order. You can't testify yet. Fine. But you can sit in this room with me and a pencil and tell me what you remember, name by name, and what I write down tonight becomes testimony the moment you sign it. Sealed, witnessed, dated. It exists whether you ever enter a courtroom or not. And a thing that exists, Mr. Reeve, is a great deal harder to murder than a thing that's only in a man's head."

Reeve stared at the pages. Slowly he reached out and touched the topmost name, the way a man touches a door he's afraid is painted on.

"Maren Pritchard," he read aloud. "Ward: none — delivered at home. Cause: exposure, presumed. Date: unknown."

"Emrys delivered her daughter in 1913. She never learned what became of either of them."

"Both dead." Reeve's voice was very quiet. "Maren Pritchard and the baby were on the first boat. I saw them on the quay. Maren carried the child wrapped in a red shawl." He looked up, and something had shifted in his face — the wall still standing, but a gate opening in it, narrow and deliberate. "I remember the shawl. I've remembered nothing else for ten months but I remembered the shawl. Write that down. Write down that I saw them alive on the eleventh of February, and write down what I saw on the twelfth, and write down every order, every name, every hour, and I'll sign it. And if your tribunal turns out to be honest—" He exhaled, long and shaking. "If it turns out to be honest, and my family can be moved somewhere the army can't find them before my name goes in — then I'll stand up and say it myself."

Aldous took out his notebook, and his hand, he noticed, was not entirely steady. Eleven years of journalism and his hand had never shaken over a story before. He understood why now. This wasn't a story. This was a stone being pried off a grave.

"Tell me about the red shawl," he said, and began to write.

* * *

They worked by candlelight until the candle was a thumb's width of guttering wax. Reeve spoke and Aldous wrote and occasionally Reeve stopped, mid-sentence, staring at the arrow slit, and Aldous waited without prompting because waiting was the oldest tool in his kit and the only one that never failed. By the time they finished, the pages numbered fourteen, and at the bottom of the last page Reeve signed his name in letters that pressed clean through to the wood of the crate beneath.

"Where does this go?" Reeve asked.

"To a solicitor in Cardiff who owes Bess Merrow a favor she has declined to specify. Sealed copy, plus the original ledger pages when we can spare them. If anything happens to any of us, the record survives us."

"Us." Reeve weighed the word. "There's an *us*, then. Nurse Thackeray, the widow, the boy, and you."

"And now you."

Reeve looked at his signature a long moment. "When I refused that order, the sergeant-major told me a man like me would die forgotten, and that forgetting was a mercy." He rolled the signed pages and handed them over like something hot. "Make sure he was wrong about the second part."

Aldous pocketed the roll and stood, joints stiff with cold. At the door he paused. "One more question. The night the boy was left at the clinic — the note said *forget the face*. Whoever wrote it knew the army was hunting survivors. Whoever wrote it wanted one child erased instead of dead." He watched Reeve carefully. "Do you know who put him on that boat?"

For the first time, Callum Reeve smiled, and it was a terrible smile, grief-shaped and furious.

"No," he said. "But I know who *didn't* — and I know the boat that left the quay last, and I know its skipper was a woman, and I know the navy lists her as lost with all hands off Milford Head." He nodded toward the harbor lights far below, where a certain black-hulled ketch rode at her moorings, innocent as Sunday. "Ask your widow Merrow what she was doing on the eleventh of February."

The wind came through the open door and guttered the candle out, and Aldous descended into the dark with fourteen pages of testimony against his ribs and a question in his mouth that he suddenly, badly, needed answered.
