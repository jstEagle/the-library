## 12. The Tribunal Announced

The news came the way winter comes to the harbor—not all at once, but in a slow graying of the light that you only notice when it has already settled over everything.

Emrys heard it first from the fishwives at the market, who had heard it from the customs man, who had read it aloud in the public house from a week-old paper someone had brought up on the mail packet. By the time Aldous Wren came through her clinic door that evening, shaking rain from his coat like a dog coming off the moor, she had already heard it three times and disbelieved it twice.

"They've announced it," he said, without preamble, without taking off his hat. Water pooled on her clean floorboards. She did not have the heart to scold him for it.

"I know."

"You know." He laughed, but there was nothing in it. "I spent the whole walk down from the telegraph office composing how I'd tell you, and you already know. This town is faster than the wires."

"This town listens," Emrys said. She set down the bowl of carbolic she'd been carrying and wiped her hands on her apron. "What does the telegraph say? The official language of it. I want the words as they'll be printed."

Aldous unfolded the flimsy telegram slip he'd been crushing in his fist. He smoothed it against the doorframe, though the creases would not come out. "'His Majesty's Government announces the convening of a tribunal of inquiry into allegations arising from military operations in the coastal districts during the period of demobilization,'" he read. His voice was flat, professional, the voice he must once have used reading casualty lists into a dictaphone. "'The tribunal will sit for a period not exceeding three weeks, at which time all matters under inquiry shall be considered closed, and the resulting record shall be transmitted to the historical section for permanent archiving.'"

"Closed," Emrys repeated.

"Considered closed," Aldous said. "Three weeks, and then the historians take over. And you know which historians. The ones who write what the War Office feeds them. I used to be one of them, God forgive me. There's a man—Brayley—already drafting the narrative in London. I've seen his memoranda. He calls it 'shaping the record for posterity.' Posterity." He spat the word as if it tasted of ash.

Through the open door to the back room, Emrys could see Sparrow sitting on the floor by the stove, drawing. He drew every day now, hours of it, the same burning building rendered with a child's terrible precision—the windows, the door, the small figures outside it—and each day the drawings accumulated in a biscuit tin until Emrys could bear no more and burned them, one by one, in the same stove that warmed him. It felt like a kind of mercy and a kind of complicity, both.

He looked up when Aldous raised his voice, then went back to his pencil. The boy had learned, in the weeks since the knock before dawn, exactly how much noise a room could hold before it became dangerous.

"Three weeks," Emrys said again. She found she was holding the edge of the table. "Aldous, they announced this the same week we found Tregaskis in the lighthouse. The same week. You don't believe in coincidence."

"I stopped believing in coincidence somewhere around Passchendaele." Aldous finally removed his hat and dropped onto the bench by the wall, suddenly looking every one of his forty-odd years, the gray in his beard more pronounced in the lamplight. "Hale pushed for this. Or Hale's superior did—the one whose name we still don't have. They know someone is asking questions. They know a fisherman survived. They know—" he gestured vaguely toward the back room, careful not to look directly at Sparrow, as if looking might mark him, "—they know about the boy. A tribunal isn't an investigation, Emrys. It's a lid. It's a machine for closing things. Three weeks of hearings conducted behind sealed doors, witnesses summoned or not summoned at the discretion of the panel, no civilian testimony accepted without petition, and at the end of it a record so clean you could eat your dinner off it."

"And our testimony?"

"Not wanted. Not permitted, except by their grace." He rubbed his eyes. "I wired three old colleagues today. Men I drank with, men who owed me favors back when my byline meant something. Two didn't answer. The third wired back two words: *Don't bother.*"

Emrys went to the window. Below, the harbor lay in its evening quiet, masts knocking softly against each other, gulls settling on the breakwater like white stones. Somewhere out there Bess Merrow's boat rode at anchor, and somewhere beyond the point the old lighthouse held its witness like a lantern holds a flame. She thought about how much light they had gathered in these few weeks—a ledger, a child's drawings, a fisherman's memory, a correspondent's shame—and how easily light can be shuttered.

"When does it convene?"

"The fourteenth. In the county seat, in the assize courts—they're requisitioning them. Which means soldiers at the doors, Emrys. Soldiers deciding who goes in."

* * *

She took the news to Bess Merrow that same night, because the widow had made it plain weeks ago that anything touching the boy touched her too, and because Emrys had learned that fear carried alone grows heavy, while fear divided grows wings.

Bess received her in the low room above the boathouse, where nets hung from the beams like the skeletons of enormous fish and the smell of tar and salt never quite left the walls. She listened without interrupting, which was unlike her, turning a brass compass over and over in her broad red hands.

"Three weeks," she said at last. "That's nothing. That's less than nothing. That's one good blow of weather and a fortnight after it."

"That's what I thought."

"No, you didn't. You thought something cleverer than that, because you've got a clever face, nurse, and it's working itself right now." Bess set the compass down. "Say it."

Emrys sat on the bench beneath the hanging nets. "The tribunal doesn't need to find us. It only needs to exist. Once it's announced, everyone with something to lose starts calculating whether truth is worth the price. Tregaskis agreed to testify because we promised him his family would be protected. But promises are cheap when the alternative is a tribunal that will call him a liar in a sealed room and then hand him back to the coast guard. Every witness we have is one threat away from silence. One letter. One visit from a uniformed man saying *cooperate and your son keeps his berth.*"

Bess was quiet a long moment. Outside, water slapped the pilings. "You're describing my boys' messmates," she said finally. "Half of them folded inside a week when the press gangs came round. Not because they were cowards. Because the empire knows where a man keeps his bread." She stood abruptly, crossing to the little stove and feeding it a knot of wood harder than the fire wanted. "So we don't wait for the tribunal. We go to it."

"Go to it? They won't let us in the door."

"They won't let us in *their* door. So we make another one." She turned, firelight on her face. "Wren's got wire access. Wren's got old friends abroad who owe him or hate him, either way they'll print. You get that story out of this country before those doors close, and no tribunal in London can unprint it. Sealed rooms seal both ways, nurse. What's done in the dark stays in the dark—unless somebody carries it out into the daylight where everybody's looking."

"Aldous has tried. No paper here will touch it."

"Then it goes further than here. Amsterdam. Geneva. The American papers love nothing better than a British scandal—it lets them feel righteous for free." Bess's mouth twisted. "My husband ran cargo past blockades for six years. There is always a gap, Emrys. There is always a ship. The empire builds walls, and men like me build doors."

Emrys felt something loosen in her chest—not hope exactly, hope had a specific shape she no longer trusted—but something adjacent. Momentum. A direction to move in, which was nearly as good.

"And the witnesses?" she asked. "Tregaskis can't cross the channel in a fishing boat with the coast guard watching every hull."

"Tregaskis's testimony travels on paper. Signed, dated, witnessed. Paper crosses water easier than men do." Bess crouched to stir the stove, and when she spoke again her voice was lower, rougher. "But understand what you're asking. Once that story leaves this harbor in print, there's no calling it back. Hale won't be able to bury it quietly anymore. He'll have to come for it openly. For you. For the boy. For me and mine, if he traces the route. Are you ready to be that visible?"

Emrys thought of the ledger in its oilcloth wrapping beneath her floorboards. Four hundred and eleven names. She had written them believing no one would ever read them, writing them anyway, because the dead deserved ink even if the living refused it. She had told herself for years that history happened elsewhere, to other people, in ministries and staff colleges and marble halls. But history, it turned out, had washed up at her clinic door wrapped in a blanket with a note that said *forget the face.*

"I have been invisible my whole life," she said. "It hasn't saved anyone."

* * *

Sparrow knew something had changed before anyone told him. Children do; they read the weather in adult faces long before they learn to read words. That night, when Emrys checked on him in the narrow cot beside her own, she found him awake, sitting up, the biscuit tin of drawings open on his knees.

He held one up to her silently. Not the burning house this time. A boat. A small boat with a figure at the tiller, and above it a gull drawn in three strokes, and beneath it waves rendered in careful, repetitive lines, like handwriting.

"He's going to take you across," Emrys said gently, sitting on the edge of the cot. "Bess. If it comes to that. She's the best sailor on this coast, and she's fierce as ten dogs, and she'd let the sea itself sink before she let anything happen to you."

Sparrow studied her face with those dark, grave eyes that seemed to catalog everything and spend nothing. Then he pointed at the drawing—at the empty space beside the figure at the tiller—and looked at her.

Emrys's throat tightened. "You want to know if I'm coming too."

He nodded, once, small.

"I don't know yet," she said honestly, because lying to him had become impossible somewhere along the way, and because she suspected he collected lies the way she collected names, storing each one against the teller. "There may be a trial. There may be trouble first. But listen to me, Sparrow." She waited until his eyes met hers and held them. "Whatever happens, whatever they announce or decree or decide behind their sealed doors—you saw what you saw. It was real. It mattered. And it is going on record. I promise you that, and I have kept every promise I ever made to a patient, including the ones I couldn't keep except by writing them down."

For a moment he simply looked at her. Then he set the drawing aside, picked up his pencil, and wrote—or rather printed—in the margin of the paper, in a child's careful capitals: *3 WKS.*

He had seen the newspaper. Of course he had. He missed nothing.

"Yes," Emrys said. "Three weeks."

He considered this. Then he turned the page and began, methodically, to draw again—the burning house, the road, the officer on horseback whose face he always left blank, though lately, in the last few days, the blankness had begun to acquire features, half-formed, like something surfacing through deep water.

Emrys watched him work and understood, with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, that the boy was preparing. That somewhere in his silence he had decided that when the time came—if the tribunal would hear him, if the law demanded his voice—he would give it. That he was rehearsing, in pencil, the thing he feared most.

* * *

Captain Hale came on Thursday.

Not with gifts this time. Not with the oily courtesy of his earlier visits, the tins of tea and the solicitous questions. He came with a corporal and an envelope, and he stood in her clinic among her patients—an old net-mender with a festering hand, a child with croup—and he made no pretense whatsoever of being anything other than what he was.

"Sister Thackeray." He did not use her title kindly. "I'll be brief, as I know you're busy."

"You always were a brief sort of man, Captain."

Something flickered behind his eyes—the first crack in the official mask she'd ever seen, and it frightened her more than the mask had, because cracks mean pressure, and pressure means a man running out of patience. "The tribunal convenes on the fourteenth. Your name appears on a list of persons whose cooperation may prove material to the inquiry. You will receive formal summons within the week. I am authorized to inform you that testimony offered voluntarily, prior to formal proceedings, will be viewed favorably by the convening authority." He laid the envelope on her table. "Unofficial cooperation, Sister. Before the lawyers make everything rigid. A signed statement. Nothing more."

"Signed by whom? About what?"

"You know about what." His gaze moved, deliberately, slowly, to the doorway of the back room, where Sparrow stood frozen with a tin cup in his hands, and Emrys saw the boy's knuckles whiten until the cup trembled. Hale smiled without warmth. "The boy. The fisherman. Whatever papers you imagine you possess. Voluntary disclosure now, and the convening authority will remember it kindly when the final record is drafted. Refuse—" he straightened his cap, "—and the record gets drafted without you. And people who obstruct tribunals have a way of appearing in records they wouldn't choose for themselves."

He left. The corporal left. The clinic exhaled.

Emrys stood holding the envelope a long time before she opened it. Inside was a single sheet, typed, official: summons details, date, hour, court address. And tucked behind it, a second sheet, untyped, handwritten in a clerk's careful copperplate:

*In view of the sensitive nature of certain anticipated disclosures, the Tribunal regrets that testimony from minors and from persons of unsound mind cannot be admitted to the record.*

*Persons of unsound mind.*

They were going to declare him mad. A mute child who had watched soldiers burn his village after the armistice was to be classified as insane, committed, erased—filed away in some asylum under a number, his testimony inadmissible before it was ever spoken. The same trick they'd pulled on shell-shocked men for five years. *Madness* was the empire's favorite filing cabinet.

Emrys's hands shook, and she let them, briefly, alone in her clinic with the lamp turned low and the boy asleep at last in the next room.

Then she went downstairs, pried up the board, and took out the ledger. Four hundred and eleven names in her tight, careful script. She counted them again, though she knew the number the way she knew her own heartbeat, and she thought: *three weeks, and they mean to close the book with him inside it.*

She was still sitting there, the ledger open on her knees, when she heard the soft double knock at the back door—Bess's signal—and opened it to find the widow standing in the sleet with Aldous behind her, both of them wet through and both wearing expressions that told her, before a word was spoken, that the night had brought worse news than hers.

"Don't tell me yet," Emrys said, stepping aside to let them in. "Put the kettle on first. I want one more minute of not knowing."

But even as she said it, her eyes went to Aldous's hands—to the crumpled telegram he was holding—and she knew, with the certainty of a woman who had watched men receive death notices by the hundred, that the clock had just started running faster.
