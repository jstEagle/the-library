## 13. Betrayal at the Boathouse

Bess Merrow had spent thirty years learning to read the harbor, and on the morning the fisherman vanished, she read it wrong.

She knew that later, turning it over like a stone with something dead beneath it. She'd stood at her window above the Salt & Anchor with her tea going cold, watching the gulls wheel over the water in their usual argumentative patterns, and she'd thought: *all's well*. The tide was right. The wind sat easy out of the southwest. Her boat, the *Kindly Ann*, rode at her mooring with the particular contentment of a vessel whose owner knows every plank of her. And somewhere across the bay, in the old lighthouse with its lamp long dark, old Tam Pellowe — the one living man who could say aloud what had happened to the village that wasn't on the map — was eating the bread and salted herring she'd carried him two nights past, keeping his courage fed along with his body.

All's well. That's what the morning told her. Mornings lie.

* * *

She found it first because she was the one who checked.

It was habit older than grief: every third day, weather permitting or not, Bess rowed out to the lighthouse rock herself rather than send one of the boys who ran errands for her. Trust was a currency she kept in small denominations. Tam Pellowe was a secret, and secrets were cargo, and you did not let cargo travel in hands you hadn't weighed yourself.

The sea was calm as poured pewter when she came around the headland, and the lighthouse rose off its black reef the way it always did — leaning, whitewash peeling, its gallery rail furred with salt-bird filth. She ran the dinghy up the old slipway and climbed the worn steps with a basket on her arm, calling ahead the way she always called, low, so as not to startle a man who had learned the hard way what footsteps meant.

"Tam. It's Bess. Brought you marmalade, God help us both."

No answer.

She stood in the doorway of the lamp room and knew before she looked. There is a particular quality to an emptied room, a stillness that isn't peace but absence wearing peace's coat. His blanket was folded on the cot — folded, not thrown. His tin cup sat on the sill, rinsed clean. The bread was gone from the basket she'd left, the crumbs swept into a neat little pile, because Tam Pellowe was a tidy man even hiding from soldiers, and he would never have left without —

Without being made to leave.

Bess set down her own basket very slowly. She walked the room once, twice. On the floor by the stair, half-scuffed away but not scuffed away enough, was a boot-heel mark in the dust. Army issue. Hobnails. She'd patched enough soldier boots during the war to know the tread the way other women knew embroidery patterns.

There'd been no boat seen leaving the bay. Which meant either they'd come by the coast road and gone back by the coast road, walking a half-dazed fisherman between them like a drunk being helped home — or someone had brought them round by water, quiet as sin, and taken him out beyond the point before the gulls had finished their breakfast.

Either way, somebody had known where Tam Pellowe was sleeping.

And Bess could count on one hand the people who knew that number, and she didn't need all five fingers to do it.

* * *

She rowed back hard, arms burning, mind running faster than the oars. By the time she tied up at the clinic steps her jaw ached from clenching it.

Emrys Thackeray met her in the doorway, and one look at Bess's face told her everything before a word passed.

"Who?" Emrys said.

"That's the question, isn't it." Bess pushed past her into the dispensary, where the smell of carbolic and dried lavender hung as it always did, where a kettle sat on the little stove, where Sparrow sat cross-legged on the floor with his pencils and paper, drawing. The boy looked up. He had learned, in the weeks since he'd arrived mute at this door, to read faces faster than most grown men read print, and whatever he saw on hers made him gather his papers against his chest like a shield.

"Not here," Bess said.

They went into Emrys's small back room, the one with the ledger locked in the drawer, and Bess shut the door and said it plain: "Tam's gone. Taken sometime in the night, or the night before — I've been slack, Emrys, I waited too long between visits. Soldiers' boots were in that room. He didn't go willing."

Emrys went white in the particular way she went white — no gasp, no hand to the throat, just a slow draining, like a tide going out of her face. "Taken. Not —"

"He's alive, or they'd have no use for him alive except one use." Bess didn't dress it. She never had. "A witness they can produce when it suits them, a witness who'll say what they want him to say after enough time in whatever room they keep him in. Or a witness who dies of misadventure in custody, convenient-like. Either way, Captain Hale has just closed a hole in his account."

"The drawings," Emrys said. "If they have Tam, they don't need —" She stopped herself, glancing at the wall, at the boy beyond it, and Bess understood. They didn't need Sparrow anymore. Which cut two ways, and one of those ways was almost worse than the first, because a thing that is no longer needed can be disposed of without ceremony.

"Aldous," Bess said. "Where's Wren?"

"Out. Wiring his people in the city, he said."

"He said." Bess heard her own voice come out flat and ugly, and she watched Emrys hear it too.

"You're not suggesting —"

"I'm not suggesting anything yet." Yet. The word sat there between them like a lit fuse. "I'm saying somebody sold Tam Pellowe, Emrys. Sold him cheap, likely, or sold him frightened, and I want to know which. Because the list of folk who knew that lighthouse is short, and every name on it is a name I've broken bread with."

* * *

By evening the whole strange little family had gathered in the boathouse — not the Salt & Anchor, not the clinic, but Bess's boathouse up the creek, where the walls were thick and her boys kept watch out of habit and loyalty both, and where a single lantern burned low over a table scattered with charts nobody was looking at.

Aldous Wren arrived last, smelling of pipe smoke and the cheap ink of the telegraph office, his collar askew, his eyes bright with the feverish energy he got when a story moved under him. "I've filed three hundred words," he announced. "Buried in shipping news, coded, but my editor will dig it out. If the tribunal thinks they can seal this inquiry —"

"They've sealed more than your inquiry." Bess didn't move from her place against the wall. She'd been leaning there since sundown, arms crossed, watching each face as it came through the door the way she used to watch strangers come aboard. "Tam's gone."

Wren stopped mid-stride. "Gone?"

"Taken. Soldiers' boots in the lamp room, blanket folded, cup washed. Somebody walked them straight to him." She let the silence do its work. It did. She watched it land on each of them in turn — on Emrys, steady and pale; on young Peter Lanyon, the clinic assistant, whose ears went red and whose hands began fussing with a coil of rope that needed no fussing; on Aldous Wren, whose bright eyes flickered — flickered, quick as a match strike — toward the door before coming back.

And on Sparrow, sitting in the corner shadow where Emrys had settled him, knees drawn up, watching them all with those dark unblinking eyes that missed nothing and said nothing.

"There's four of us knew," Bess said. "Five counting the boy. Me, Emrys, Wren, Peter. And the lad." She said it evenly. She made herself say it evenly. "Now I know how it sounds, laying it out like that. But I've run cargo through blockades and customs men for twenty years, and the first law of it is you find the leak before it drowns you. So we find the leak."

"This is madness," Wren said. "You're talking about — suspicion. About *him*?" He gestured at the corner, and then, catching himself, softened his voice into something gentle and horrible. "The boy wouldn't. The boy couldn't. He barely speaks."

"He sees everything," Bess said. "That's not the same as nothing, Wren. A man can be turned without knowing he's turned. Questions asked kind-like. A uniform that doesn't threaten. Or they follow him when he wanders the quay, which he does, Emrys, don't look surprised, I've seen him. Half the harbor's seen him. A child alone who never talks draws eyes like blood draws sharks."

"Bess." Emrys's voice was quiet and it cut through the room quieter than shouting would have. "He's eight years old."

"I know how old he is." Bess pushed off the wall. "I buried two sons younger than he is now, so don't stand there and tell me what children are worth. I'm telling you what fear does. Fear makes informers of better folk than him." She looked around the lantern light, at each face again, slower this time. "Peter. You were at the clinic Tuesday when the supply cart came. Who drove it?"

Peter Lanyon looked up from the rope. His ears were still red. "Just — the usual man. Cobbett's fellow."

"And did anyone else come? Anyone ask after the clinic's business?"

"No one. Mrs. Merrow, I've told you everything I — I'd never. My mother's in Truro, my sister's at the school — if Hale's people thought I —" He stopped, swallowed. "I'd die first. I'd want that said plainly. I'd die before I gave up that boy."

And Bess believed him, mostly, the way you believe a man swearing on his family, which is to say you believe the believing and you keep one eye open anyway. Fear made liars of honest men; it also made honest-sounding men of liars. Thirty years of smuggling had taught her the difference was invisible until the moment it wasn't.

"Wren," she said. "You were in the city Monday. Who did you see?"

"My editor. A wire clerk. Two colleagues who think I've gone soft in the head chasing a story about a village nobody admits existed." Wren's chin came up. "You think I'd sell a witness? After what I printed? After what I helped bury in print, you think I'd —" His voice cracked and he hated it, she could see him hate it. "That story cost me everything I had left. This is the only coin I've got to buy it back. I'm not spending it on betrayal."

"Then one of us is lying," Bess said, "or one of us was followed without knowing it, or one of us talked in sleep or drink or confidence, or —" She stopped. The lantern hissed. Rain had started against the boathouse roof, soft as sifted flour. "Or there's a fifth name none of us has thought of yet. But until we know which, we change how we do things. No more visits to anyone. No messages written down. Nothing moves by day. And the boy —"

"Don't finish that sentence." Emrys had risen. She was not a tall woman and she was not a loud one, but there was a steel in her that Bess had respected since the first week of the war, when a shell had come through the ward wall and Emrys Thackeray had kept pouring morphine with glass in her hair. "Whatever you were about to propose about Sparrow, don't."

"I was about to propose we keep him closer, not cast him off," Bess said. "But I'll say this much, and I'll say it to his face, because I won't whisper behind a child." She crouched, slowly, down to the boy's level, and the lantern light caught the fine bones of his face, the wariness that never entirely left his eyes even now, even here. "Sparrow. Did anyone speak to you? On the quay, in the lane, anywhere. A kind voice. A man who wasn't frightening. Did anyone give you anything, or ask you anything, even a small thing — where you slept, who came and went?"

Sparrow looked at her. Then at Emrys. Then, last and longest, at Aldous Wren — and there was something in that look, some flicker of the boy's inner weather, that made Wren go still as a heron.

Then the boy shook his head. Once. Firmly.

And Bess, who had raised two sons and cheated a hundred customs officers and buried a husband and read the harbor's lying mornings all her life, looked at that shake of the head and could not tell — could not *tell*, and it gnawed at her like rust — whether she was looking at truth, or at a child who had learned that the safest answer in an uncertain world was the smallest one.

* * *

They dispersed before midnight, singly, by different doors, as she directed. Emrys took Sparrow home through the rain under an oilskin big enough to swallow them both. Peter Lanyon went off toward the town with his collar up, walking fast, not looking back — and Bess noted that he did not look back, and noted that she had noted it, and despised herself a little for the noting.

Wren lingered.

"Mrs. Merrow." He stood dripping in the boathouse doorway, hat in hand like a man at a graveside. "For what it's worth — I know what suspicion looks like from the inside. I wore it for two years after the war, every time I passed a newsstand. Everyone who'd read what I wrote looking at me like I was something on their shoe." His voice dropped. "So if you need to believe it was me for a while, to keep the rest of you safe while we work it true — I'll wear that too. I've practice."

Bess looked at him a long moment. "Get home, Wren," she said at last, gruff as gravel. "And lock your door. Whoever it is, they've shown they can reach a man in a locked lighthouse on a rock in the sea. Nobody in this harbor is out of reach tonight."

He went. She banked the lantern. She sat alone in the dark boathouse listening to the rain and the water slapping the hulls, and she let herself do the arithmetic she'd been refusing all evening.

Four names. One leak. And a tribunal convening in under a fortnight with a witness in its pocket who could be made to say anything — unless they got to him first, unless they got him *back*, out of whatever coastal depot or guarded room they'd stowed him in, which meant a run by water, which meant speed, which meant trusting somebody with the plan, which meant handing the leak another secret to sell.

Every road forward ran through the traitor's hands.

Bess put her head back against the cold planking and closed her eyes, and it was there in the dark, with the rain easing and the tide turning, that she heard it: footsteps on the shingle outside, soft and deliberate, stopping just short of the boathouse door. Someone standing in the wet dark. Someone who knew exactly where she was, alone, at this hour.

The door handle turned, slow as syrup.

Bess slid the gutting knife from her belt without a sound and waited.
