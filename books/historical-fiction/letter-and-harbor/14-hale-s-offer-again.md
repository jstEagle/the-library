## 14. Hale's Offer, Again

He came at the hour when the clinic was emptyest — between the last of the afternoon dressings and the first of the evening rounds, when the light through the harbor windows went the color of weak tea and even the gulls seemed to lose interest in their quarrels. Emrys had sent Tobbins home early. She would think about that afterward, about how she had emptied the room herself, as if some part of her had known it was coming and wanted no witnesses to what she might say.

Captain Hale did not knock. He never knocked; knocking, she supposed, was for men who expected refusal.

"Mrs. Thackeray." He stood in the doorway with his cap under his arm, which was his way of being courteous, and a wrapped parcel under the other, which was his way of being dangerous. "You look tired."

"You look punctual," she said, not rising from the basin where she was rinsing lint. The water was cold and gray with blood-tinged soap. She kept her hands moving. Hands that kept moving could not be made to tremble. "I'm afraid I can't offer you tea. My assistant has gone home."

"I know."

Two words, mild as milk, and they landed like stones. She rinsed the last of the lint, wrung it out, laid it on the clean towel, and only then turned to face him.

"Then you also know you'll have to pour your own."

He smiled — the smile of a man who had been told all his life that he was reasonable, and who believed it, which was the worst kind of believing. He set the parcel on the table by the door. It sat there with the patience of an unexploded shell.

"That's sugar," he said. "Real sugar, not the rationed stuff. And there's butter underneath, and tinned milk. I noticed the children here go thin in winter. Whatever else you may think of me, Mrs. Thackeray, I don't wish anyone's child to go hungry."

"No," Emrys said. "You only wish certain ones to disappear. Hunger is beneath your dignity."

The smile thinned but held. He took the chair opposite her desk without invitation — another of his courtesies — and folded his gloved hands upon his knee. Up close, in this light, she could see how young he was under the authority. Thirty, perhaps. A smooth jaw and careful eyes and the particular stillness of a man who had learned early that other people's fear was a tool he was permitted to use.

"You've been difficult," he said.

"I've been busy."

"You've been difficult," he repeated, with the patience of a schoolmaster, "since October. Since Wren came sniffing. Since the Merrow woman started asking her questions down at the quay — and she does ask questions, Mrs. Thackeray, whatever she tells herself about asking nothing. And since the boy." He let the word sit there. *The boy.* Not *the stray*, not *your foundling* — he knew now exactly what Sparrow was to her, and he meant her to know he knew. "I'm not here to threaten you."

"You're here to bribe me. It's a different trade but the same market."

"I'm here," Hale said quietly, "to make you an offer that will let everyone sleep at night. Including you."

Emrys dried her hands slowly on her apron. Outside the window a fishing boat was coming in on the last of the tide, its lamp already lit against the early dark, and she watched it because watching it was easier than watching him, and because some part of her mind — the part that had stood in dressing-stations at Ypres and Passchendaele while men screamed themselves into silence — had begun doing what it always did under pressure: counting exits, counting seconds, counting the distance to the drawer where she kept the ledger.

She had moved it two nights ago. After the search party. After the soldier whose face she'd lied to, whose hand she'd shaken over the lie. It lay now inside the false back of the dispensary cabinet, behind the bottles of laudanum, wrapped in oilcloth. She thought of it lying there in the dark among the poisons and thought: *that is where truth lives in this country now. Behind the poison. Wrapped against damp.*

"Say your offer," she said.

Hale leaned forward. When he spoke again his voice dropped into something almost gentle, and that gentleness was more frightening than any threat, because it meant he believed he had already won.

"The War Office is preparing a tribunal. You know this. In three weeks it will convene, take evidence, and close the inquiry into the incident on the coast. The record will state what the record must state: that the village was evacuated in good order pursuant to operational necessity; that casualties were regrettable, minimal, and enemy-inflicted; that the matter is closed. Historians will read that record for a hundred years, madam. Schoolchildren will learn from it. There will be no appeal, because there will be nothing left to appeal *to*. Do you understand? Not a cover-up. A *foundation*. The ground everything else gets built on."

"And the people who were actually there?"

"Were few," Hale said, "and are fewer every week." He said it without cruelty, the way one reports the weather, and somehow that was crueler than anything shouted could have been. "Which brings me to the offer. You surrender the ledger — the private ledger, the one you imagine no one knows about, in which you have been so industriously recording names. You surrender the boy. In exchange: full amnesty. No inquiry into your harboring of a military witness. No charges arising from your obstruction of my men. No examination of your correspondence with Wren, or your association with the Merrow woman, both of which I should tell you are already documented. You keep this clinic. Your license stays clean. Your name stays out of everything. You go on binding wounds and delivering babies until your hands give out, and no one ever troubles you again."

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled. Emrys became aware that her heart was beating very hard and very evenly, the way it did before hemorrhages, and that her mouth had gone dry, and that — God help her, God forgive her — for three whole seconds she *wanted it.*

That was the thing she would remember longest, afterward. Not his words. Her own wanting. Three seconds of a life with the weight lifted: no more looking over her shoulder at the harbor road, no more lying to soldiers, no more waking at four with her heart hammering over what they might do to the boy if they took him. Three seconds of peace, priced at a ledger and a child.

She counted them the way she counted pulse-beats, and when they passed she found her voice was steady.

"You know about the ledger," she said.

"Page by page," said Hale. "Did you think a woman keeps records like that for two years and no one notices? Your assistant is fond of you. Frightened men talk to frightened clerks. There isn't a name in that book I couldn't recite to you right now — though I confess the handwriting defeats me in places. You really ought to have used a copyist."

Every name. Every small name she had gathered like a woman gleaning a field after the reapers — the drowned stoker no ship's manifest admitted losing, the Canadian gunner buried under a false initial, the eleven men from the *Providence Star*, the fisher-folk of the burned village whose deaths existed nowhere but her ink and Sparrow's drawings and the memory of one hiding man. He had them all, or claimed he did, and the claiming itself was a wound: proof that even her defiance had been observed, catalogued, filed.

"Why," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and she hated that, and went on anyway, "why do you want paper? If you already have the names. Burn the book yourself. Why do you need *me*?"

"Because paper in your hands is testimony. Paper surrendered by you is evidence destroyed. There's a difference the tribunal will understand, if it ever comes to that — and it won't, because after tonight there'll be nothing left to come to trial about." He rose, unhurried, and picked up the parcel of sugar and butter and tinned milk, and held it out to her like a man offering flowers at a funeral. "And because I would rather have it done gently. I've seen enough burning for one career."

She looked at the parcel. She looked at his face — that reasonable, decent, damnable face — and she understood suddenly that he was not lying to her, that he genuinely believed this was mercy, that somewhere inside his carefully built soul he had arranged the facts until surrendering the dead's names and handing over a traumatized child looked like *kindness*, like tidiness, like sleep for everyone. That was the empire in miniature. Not sadism. Bookkeeping.

"Captain," she said, "do you know what I wrote in the ledger for a boy called Thomas Renwick?"

Something flickered across his composure — surprise, perhaps, that she would answer an ultimatum with an anecdote. "I don't."

"He was nineteen. Stoker, second class. His ship went down in March of '17 and the Admiralty listed him missing, presumed drowned, and paid his mother six pounds and stopped her pension after eight months on a technicality. He didn't drown. He washed up near here with a broken back and lived four days in this clinic, and he talked the whole time about his mother's garden, and when he died I asked the authorities what I should write on his grave-marker and they told me *unknown*. Unknown. I'd held his hand. I knew his mother grew sweet peas." She took a breath. "So I bought the marker myself and I cut his name into it with a chisel, badly, and I wrote him in my ledger, and I have written in that ledger every man and woman and child this war and its aftermath have decided not to count, and I will tell you what that book is, Captain Hale. It is not evidence. It is not ammunition, whatever Mr. Wren hopes. It is a debt register. Every name in it is money owed by men like you to people who cannot collect. And I do not settle other people's debts by burning the account book."

The parcel was still between them. He lowered it slowly, set it back on the table, and when he looked up at her the courtesy had drained away and left something older and colder, and she thought: *there he is. Under the uniform. There is the man who gave the order, or carried it, or watched it given.*

"You mistake the nature of your position," he said.

"No," said Emrys. "You mistake the nature of mine. You think I'm a woman protecting a child, and you believe every woman protecting a child can be reasoned with, because children are negotiable and women are soft. But I'm not protecting him. I'm *vouching* for him. There's a difference. Protection I could withdraw when it got expensive. Vouching means his name goes next to mine in front of God and the tribunal and history, and if he's lying then I'm lying, and if he dies unheard then it's my failure carved beside his. You cannot buy vouching. Believe me, the Germans tried, at a dressing-station outside Bailleul, and I told a captain very much like you very much the same thing."

"Your clinic," Hale said softly, "operates on a lease renewed at the discretion of the Port Health Authority. Which reports to—"

"To the Admiralty. Yes. I did read a newspaper once."

"—and your license to practice was granted under emergency provisions that lapsed in June and have been continued by—"

"By the same. I follow the arithmetic, Captain. You don't need to finish the sums."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The tide turned below the windows; she heard it, the faint long sigh of the water changing its mind. Hale straightened his gloves, finger by finger, a small meticulous violence.

"I had hoped," he said, "to spare you."

"Spare me." She laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "Captain, I spent three years elbow-deep in boys who were told they'd be spared. Spare me your sparing."

"Then hear the alternative plainly." All the gentleness was gone now; what remained was flat and precise and almost a relief, because at least it was honest. "If the ledger surfaces at that tribunal, you will be charged — concealment of material evidence, obstruction of officers in the execution of duty, harboring a minor without lawful guardianship. If the boy testifies, he will be examined as a witness of unsound mind, and committed to an asylum for care and observation, and you will not be permitted to see him, and within a year no one outside the walls will remember his name — except, I suppose, you, in your little book, which by then will be ash in a stove in Whitehall. And if the Merrow woman's boat is intercepted — and boats are intercepted, Mrs. Thackeray, in weather like this, regularly — then it becomes smuggling of persons, and smuggling of persons off this coast in wartime carries penalties I will not describe to a lady over supper." He put on his cap, settling it with two fingers above the brow. "Three weeks. Think about what the dead would actually want. They're dead. They wanted, most of them, to go home. They wouldn't want a nurse in prison and a child in an asylum on their account. Sentiment is a luxury of survivors. Be sensible. Survive."

At the door he paused, and did not turn fully around, and said, without any expression at all:

"For what it's worth — the note pinned to the boy's coat. *Forget the face.* We never established who wrote it. I'd ask him, but he doesn't speak, does he. Curious, that. Almost as curious as a mute child who can draw a building he claims never to have seen." He tapped the doorframe twice, a strange little tic of finality. "Good evening, Mrs. Thackeray. Mind how you go. These stairs are treacherous in winter."

Then he was gone, and his boots went down the steps evenly, without hurry, a man entirely satisfied with his evening's work, and Emrys stood in the cold he'd left behind him with her arms wrapped around herself and discovered she was shaking after all — not her hands, which had stayed surgeon-steady through all of it, but everything else, a deep interior shudder like the harbor water when a big boat passes.

*Be sensible. Survive.*

She walked to the window. Down on the quay, lamps were coming on one by one along the seawall. The *Merrow* boat rode low and patient at her moorings. And there — small, hooded, hauling a crate of fish up the steps with the grim competence of a much larger person — went Sparrow, helping old Tam with his catch, because Bess had decreed the boy needed occupation and occupation had worked better than any medicine Emrys possessed. He stumbled on the wet stone. Caught himself. Kept going. At the top of the steps he stopped, and turned, as if he'd felt her eyes, and looked up at the clinic window, and raised one hand — not waving, just raising it, the way you'd raise a hand to show someone you were still holding on.

*They're dead,* Hale had said. *They wouldn't want—*

How dare he speak for them. How dare any of them speak for the dead, who had never once in this whole filthy business been consulted. Thomas Renwick had wanted sweet peas and his mother and a grave with his own name on it, and he had gotten a chisel-cut marker bought by a nurse, and that was more honesty than the entire Admiralty had produced in five years of war. The dead wanted what Sparrow wanted: to be counted. To be *somebody's*. To have their faces remembered by somebody who refused, refused, refused to forget.

Emrys pressed her forehead briefly against the cold glass.

Then she turned, crossed the room, knelt at the dispensary cabinet, and drew out the oilcloth bundle from behind the laudanum. The ledger lay heavy in her hands — swollen with damp and years, its spine cracked, its pages crowded edge to edge with the small defiant names. She untied the string and turned, deliberately, to the page she had torn free two nights ago and hidden separately, in case of exactly such an evening as this: the page listing the eleven men of the *Providence Star*, and beneath them, added in fresher ink after Aldous and she had matched the drawings against the charts, the names of the villagers of Porthcarrow as far as the fisherman in the lighthouse had been able to recall them.

The fresh ink was wrong.

Not smudged — *wrong.* The entries ended mid-line. The last name, *Merrin, Owen, fisherman, age 44*, trailed off in a half-formed letter, and below it the paper showed the pale ghost of writing that was no longer there, the indentations of a pen pressed hard and hurried, and where the ghost-lines should have continued onto the next leaf, the next leaf was not the next leaf. The numbering skipped. Forty-one to forty-three.

A page was gone. Cut cleanly, close to the spine, by someone who knew the book well enough to open it flat and fast.

Tobbins. Kind young Tobbins, who brought her tea unprompted, who sang to the children while he bandaged them, whose mother lived alone in a terraced house on a street Captain Hale's men could reach in ten minutes' walk.

Emrys sat back on her heels in the empty clinic, the mutilated ledger open across her knees, and outside the harbor lights burned steady and indifferent, and somewhere in the dark between the lamplight and the headland a boat was loading cargo for a crossing that would now, if the missing page held what she feared it held, sail toward a trap with her whole ragged family aboard.
