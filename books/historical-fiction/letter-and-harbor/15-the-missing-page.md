## 15. The Missing Page

The ledger lived in the third drawer of the dispensary cabinet, beneath the bottles of iodine and the rolled bandages that smelled faintly of camphor and salt. Emrys had kept it there for six years, through the war and after, through the nights when the harbor clinic filled with men the Army had forgotten to count, and she had never once found it out of place.

She found it out of place on a Tuesday morning in February, when the gulls were screaming over the fish market and the light came thin and gray through the clinic's north windows.

She had gone to it for a name — Thomas Ellery, stoker, lost off the *Kestrel Maid* in '17, whose widow had come the day before to ask, again, whether anyone official had ever written him down. Emrys had promised to copy the page for her. She opened the drawer, lifted the ledger out, and knew before her thumb touched the binding.

It was the weight first. A ledger learns its own heft in your hands, the way a sleeping child does, and this one was lighter by a breath. Then the spine — the stitching at the spine had a small slackness to it, a loosening, like a mouth gone slightly wrong.

She set it on the table between the scales and the mortar and pestle, and turned the ribbon marker, and counted.

Pages one through forty: the war years, the field hospital at Ypres, the dressing station at Passchendaele where they had run out of morphine and she had run out of tears and kept writing anyway, because someone had to hold the names or they would dissolve like sugar in rain. Forty-one through sixty: the harbor dead, the drowned, the fevers, the men who came home wrong and died quietly in rented rooms with no one but her to spell their names correctly.

Page fifty-three was gone.

Not blank. Gone. Torn close along the gutter, so carefully that at first glance the ledger seemed only to skip a beat, as if the book itself had flinched. But the stub was there when she tilted it to the window's gray light — a pale thread of paper, ragged-edged, and the ghost of ink where the writing on the reverse had pressed through from the missing side.

Page fifty-three. She did not need to open her memory to know what lived there. She knew every page the way she knew her own hands.

Fifty-three held the autumn of 1918. The last months before the armistice. Eleven names from a coastal patrol, men listed as *transferred* in the regimental record and entered in her ledger as *died of wounds, date uncertain, location withheld*. And below them, in her smaller, harder hand — the hand she used for things she could not bear to write large — four more names. Civilians. A fisherman named Curnow. His wife. Two children. Reported to her by a shell-shocked private who had washed up half-dead on Bess Merrow's beach and raved, before fever took him, about a village burned after the order to stand down.

The private had died. The names had stayed. And now the names were gone.

Emrys stood very still. Outside, a cart rattled past on the cobbles; somewhere a woman called a child home to breakfast; the ordinary machinery of the morning went on turning, indifferent, and she stood in her own clinic with her whole body gone cold and quiet, the way it used to go cold and quiet at Passchendaele when the shelling started — not fear exactly, but the mind clearing everything off its shelves, preparing for what must be done next.

Someone had been in the drawer. Someone had known which page.

Only three people knew the ledger existed. Aldous Wren knew — he had sat across from her at this same table three weeks ago and watched her turn its pages with the expression of a starving man shown bread. Bess knew; Bess had carried Sparrow's blankets up from the boat herself while Emrys wrote. And —

Her eyes went, unwillingly, to the door that led to the back room, where the smell of boiling linens and lye soap drifted through, and where a young man was singing under his breath, tunelessly, as he worked the wringer.

Tobias Quill.

He had come to her in the spring of '18, seventeen years old, knock-kneed, with a stammer he tried to hide behind politeness and hands too gentle for any work heavier than tending. His father had been a fisherman lost with the *Providence*; his mother took in washing until her lungs gave out; his sister, Lily, was nine and bright as a new coin. Emrys had hired him to scrub floors and boil bandages because the clinic needed hands and because his mother had once, years ago, sat with her through a bad night when Emrys was new to the harbor and grieving something she never named.

For two years Tobias had been the clinic's quiet kindness. He learned to anticipate — the bucket placed before she asked, the lamp trimmed before dark, an extra blanket folded at the foot of a dying man's cot without being told. He could sit with the worst patients, the ones who screamed at nothing, and soothe them just by being near, his voice soft and stumbling and patient. When Sparrow had come, mute and wild-eyed, it was Tobias who'd coaxed him to eat, Tobias who'd taught him checkers without ever asking him to speak, Tobias who slept on the clinic floor the first week so the boy would not wake alone in the dark.

Emrys had thought, more than once, that if anything happened to her, the boy would be all right, because Tobias was there.

Now she stood in the doorway of the back room and watched him at the wringer, sleeves rolled, forearms pink from the hot water, singing his broken little tune, and she felt the grief arrive ahead of the certainty — the way you feel the thunder before you let yourself believe it is really the guns.

"Tobias."

He startled, knocked the soap dish, caught it against his chest with both hands. "M-miss Thackeray. Morning. I didn't hear — the machine makes such a noise, I didn't—"

"Come sit with me a moment."

"I've still the second load, and Mr. Wren's shirt has a tear I meant to—"

"Sit with me, Tobias."

Something crossed his face then, quick as a fish under ice, and she saw it and hated herself for seeing it. He dried his hands on his apron, twice, three times, though they were already dry, and followed her to the front room.

She did not put the ledger in front of him. She made tea instead, because her hands wanted work, and because she wanted to watch what his hands did. They trembled around the cup. They had always been gentle hands. Gentle hands can still carry a thing to another person's door.

"Has anyone spoken to you," she said, "about this clinic? About me? About Sparrow?"

"N-no." Too fast. "No, miss. Why would they?"

"Captain Hale's men have been asking questions in the district. If anyone had approached you — offered you money, or frightened you —"

"Frightened me?" He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, a sound with nothing in it. "Who'd frighten me?"

"Tobias." She kept her voice level, nurse-soft, the voice that had talked men through amputations. "Look at me."

He looked at her. He was nineteen now, nearly twenty, but in that moment he was the boy who had walked in off the street two springs ago with his dead father's cap clutched in both hands, and she felt the old tenderness rise in her like flood water, and she made herself speak past it.

"The ledger is missing a page."

The cup rattled against its saucer. He set it down too hard and tea slopped over, spreading brown across the scarred wood, and he stared at the spill as though it might save him from answering.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Fifty-three. Eleven soldiers and four civilians. Names no report carries anywhere but here. You knew where I kept it. You've seen me write in it — you brought me my spectacles once, in December, when I'd left them beside the bed."

"That doesn't mean—" His voice cracked clean through. He pressed the heel of his hand against his mouth. "Miss Thackeray, please, you have to understand, I didn't want to—"

"They have Lily."

It was not a question. It came out of her flat and cold, deduced rather than guessed, and watching his face she knew she had struck true. He folded forward over the table like something collapsing, both arms coming up over his head, and the sound he made was not crying exactly; it was lower than crying, older, the sound of a structure giving way.

"They know where she goes to school," he said, into his arms. "They knew her name. The officer — not Captain Hale, another one, a captain with a gray mustache and a voice like — like a man reading a menu. He sat in our kitchen, miss. In our kitchen, with his gloves on, eating nothing, and he asked me questions about you, and when I said I didn't know anything he smiled and said that was all right, they'd get to know her eventually either way. Lily was at the window. She waved at him. She's nine, she waves at everyone, and he—" His shoulders shook. "He tipped his hat to her. Through the glass. Like a promise."

Emrys sat with her hands folded on the table, on the edge of the spilled tea, and listened, and let him empty himself.

"What did they ask you to do?"

"Tell them things. Whether you had visitors. Whether the boy was still here. Whether — whether you kept writing things down." He lifted his head, and his eyes were swollen and hopeless. "I told them nothing for weeks. I swear it. I told them the clinic was dull and you were strict and I knew nothing worth knowing. And then three days ago the same man came back, and he had Lily's school satchel with him, miss. Just sitting on his knee, holding it, patting it, like it was a dog. And he said, *we'd hate for this to come home empty.*"

"And the page."

"He gave me a razor blade." Tobias's voice dropped to a whisper, as though the walls themselves might inform. "And he told me which page. He knew the number, miss. He knew the number better than I did. How does he know the number?"

Because they had copied it already, Emrys thought. Because somewhere in a staff office a transcription existed, and what they wanted was not information but destruction — the original, the evidence, the ink in her hand. Reports could be forged; her ledger could only be stolen.

"You tore it out yourself?"

"I couldn't — I tried to refuse, I sat up all night, and then in the morning I heard Lily laughing in the kitchen and I—" He covered his face again. "I'm sorry. God forgive me, Miss Thackeray, I'm sorry. Take it out of my wages. Turn me out. I deserve — I'll go, I'll go today, only tell me Lily will be safe, tell me they'll leave her be now I've done it—"

"No," Emrys said.

He looked up, red-eyed, braced for the blow.

"You will not go today. You will finish the second load, because the linen won't boil itself, and Mr. Wren's shirt wants mending, and this clinic runs on you and I'll thank you not to pretend otherwise." She heard her own voice steady and was grateful to some old drilled part of herself that had learned, long ago, how to speak clearly while everything underneath was breaking. "Then you will go home and you will kiss your sister and you will say nothing to her, nothing at all, and tomorrow you will come back and we are going to get that page back together. Do you understand me?"

"Get it — how?"

"How did he take delivery? Did he tell you to bring it to him?"

"A note under the door of the King's Head. Signed R.M. I was to leave it in the taproom, wrapped in oilcloth, with a pint standing on it."

"Then it isn't gone yet," Emrys said, "if you haven't delivered it."

His face went through several weathers at once — hope, terror, disbelief. "It's in my coat pocket. I was going to — I couldn't make myself walk there. I've been carrying it since yesterday evening. It's been burning a hole in me like a coal."

"Tobias." She reached across the table and took his wrist, gently, the way she took the wrists of men in pain. "You carried it a day and a night and you could not deliver it. Do you know what that means? A man who would betray us truly would have been rid of it inside the hour. You are not a traitor, Tobias Quill. You are a frightened boy doing arithmetic with the only currency you have, and I will never ask you to spend your sister. Do you hear me? Never."

"But the page—"

"The page is mine to recover. Your part ends here." She paused, and then, because mercy unaccompanied by truth is only sentiment, she added: "One thing more, and I need it exact. This officer. Gray mustache. Was there anything else about him? Anything he wore, said, any habit?"

Tobias wiped his nose on his sleeve, thinking. "He — he kept clicking his fingers. Soft, like. While he talked. Like he was keeping time to music nobody else could hear. And his boots. Officer's boots, but city-made, polished yellow-brown, not black. And—" He frowned, dredging. "A ring. Signet ring, worn on the wrong hand, on his little finger. It had a bird on it. Some kind of hawk."

Emrys went very still.

She had seen that ring. Three weeks ago, on the hand of the staff major who had sat beside Captain Hale at the tribunal office in the county town, smoothing his gloves, clicking his fingers softly while Hale spoke — a small dry sound she had noticed and dismissed, the way one dismisses a fly in winter.

So it was not Hale alone. It ran above him. Which meant the orders to burn a village and erase it ran above him too, and the page in Tobias's pocket was a thread that, pulled far enough, might unravel a great deal more than one lie.

"Keep the page," she said.

Tobias stared. "*Keep* it?"

"In your coat. Wrapped in oilcloth as instructed. Tomorrow you will walk to the King's Head, exactly as ordered, and you will leave the package on the bar — but the package will hold thirty pages of the clinic's laundry inventory, and the real page will be somewhere Captain Hale's people will never think to look." She allowed herself the smallest smile. "Under the floorboards of a nine-year-old girl's dollhouse, perhaps. No soldier alive searches a dollhouse."

He made a sound halfway between laugh and sob. "They'd never—"

"They'd never. And Tobias — after tomorrow, if they press you again, if they threaten her again, you tell them the truth: that you delivered it, that it's done, that you're finished. Let them believe they've won you. A spy they think is theirs is worth more to me than ten honest men." She squeezed his wrist once and released it. "That's an order, Private Quill."

"I'm not a — I never enlisted, miss."

"You enlisted the day you boiled your first bandage in this house. We simply never issued you a uniform."

He went back to the wringer. After a while, unbelievably, he began to sing again — the same tuneless scrap of song, thinner now, wavering, but going. Emrys stood a moment in the doorway listening to it, and understood that he sang for the same reason soldiers whistled in the trenches: not because things were well, but because the sound proved he was still a living creature and not yet entirely a tool in other men's hands.

She left him to it and climbed the stairs to the attic room where Sparrow kept his drawings pinned to the sloped wall like a fleet at anchor.

The boy was there, cross-legged on the bed, working in charcoal on a board across his knees. He looked up when she came in — he always looked up quickly, always checked, always confirmed her face before returning to whatever held him — and she sat down on the stool by the window.

"Sparrow," she said. "Do you remember the man with the gray mustache? The one who came with Captain Hale?"

The boy's charcoal stopped. His eyes went to the wall of drawings, to the burning village rendered eleven times in a child's heavy strokes, and then back to her, and he nodded once, slow.

"Did he wear a ring? On his little finger?"

Another nod. Then — and her heart turned over — he set down the charcoal, crawled to the edge of the bed, and took her hand. He turned it palm-up, studying it with the fierce concentration he brought to everything he could not say, and with one grimy finger he traced, slowly, deliberately, in the center of her palm:

Three letters. *R.M.*

Emrys looked down at the letters drying on her skin like a brand.

"You know that name," she whispered.

Sparrow nodded. His jaw worked. Nothing came out — not yet, not words, not yet — but his eyes were wet and blazing, and his finger rose again and tapped her palm, twice, hard, right on the invisible letters, and Emrys understood with a cold, rising certainty that the initials under the King's Head door and the ring with the hawk and the officer with the menu-voice were one man, and that this man had stood in a burning village and given the order, and that her silent sparrow had watched him do it.

Below them, faintly, the front door bell rang — two sharp jingles, urgent, the way Bess Merrow rang when she didn't want to be kept waiting on the step.

Emrys closed her fist around the letters and went down to answer it.
