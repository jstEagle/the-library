## 16. Storm Passage

The gale came up out of the southwest like something with a grudge.

Bess Merrow had watched weather her whole life — forty years of it, first as a fisherman's daughter, then a smuggler's wife, now a smuggler widow with nothing left to lose but a boat and a promise. She knew the difference between a blow that wanted to frighten you and a blow that wanted to kill you, and the one building on the horizon that October evening was the second kind. The clouds stacked themselves black over the water in long shelves, and the air went still and warm and wrong, like the held breath of a man about to swing a fist.

"Could wait for morning," said Aldous Wren at her shoulder. He was gripping the rail of *Kittiwake* hard enough that his knuckles showed white through the skin, and he was trying very hard to pretend he wasn't afraid, and failing at it in the way honest men fail — visibly, without shame.

"No," Bess said. "We couldn't."

She didn't explain, because he already knew. Every hour they waited was an hour for Captain Hale's men to move Tom Caddock out of the coastal depot at Penharrow and onto a transport, an hour for the second survivor — the woman, Maren Voss, hiding in the sea caves past the point with two children and no food — to starve or freeze or simply give up. And every hour was another hour of the tribunal grinding toward its verdict in the city, three weeks and counting down, the victors sharpening their pens. Weather waited for nobody, but empire waited even less.

"Besides," she added, uncoiling the mooring line, "the Navy won't be running patrol in this. Even Hale's lot have enough sense to stay ashore."

"And we don't?"

"We don't." She grinned at him, and she knew what the grin looked like — she'd been told often enough, by men who'd underestimated it right up until the moment it cost them. "We've got more sense than they do, Wren. That's different."

He almost smiled back. Almost. Then the first spit of rain came across the harbor, cold as thrown gravel, and there was no more time for talking.

* * *

Bess ran the *Kittiwake* out through the harbor mouth with the last grey light behind her and the black wall of the storm ahead, and within twenty minutes she understood that this crossing would live in her bones until she died.

It was not the worst weather she'd ever sailed. That honor belonged to a night eleven years back, running brandy past the revenue cutters with her husband alive beside her at the wheel, both of them laughing like fools while the sea tried its best to murder them. But that night she'd been young and stupid and rich with luck. Now she was fifty-three, alone at the helm except for Aldous Wren lashed to the mast doing what he could with the sails, and the stakes were not barrels of French brandy but human lives — a fisherman locked in a depot for the crime of surviving, a woman and her children shivering in a cave because their village had been burned off the face of the earth after the armistice, after the guns were supposed to have gone quiet forever.

After. That was the word that kept Bess's hands steady on the wheel when nothing else could. *After.* They'd signed the paper, they'd sung the songs, they'd put the flags out in every window from here to London — and then soldiers in government coats had marched into a fishing village and put it to the torch, and some captain with a soft voice and clean boots was spending his evenings writing reports that said it never happened.

Her boys would have been about Sparrow's age, if they'd lived. She didn't let herself think it often. But out here, with the sea standing up in black mountains around her and spray hitting the deck like gunfire, she let herself think it now: *this is what I owe them. Not tears. Passage.*

The wave came without warning — they always did — a grey-green cliff rearing up off the port bow, higher than the masthead it seemed, and Bess hauled the wheel hard over and drove the *Kittiwake* into its face rather than let it take her beam-on. The boat climbed, and climbed, and for one suspended moment hung at the top with the whole furious world laid out below, and then fell, and the impact went through the hull like a hammer through a coffin lid. Somewhere below decks a crate broke loose and thundered against the bulkhead. Water sheeted across the cockpit, found every gap in her oilskins, and turned her to ice from the collarbones down.

"Wren!" she bellowed into the wind. "The jib! Half of it!"

His answer was lost entirely, but a minute later the sail came down in ragged stages, and the boat stopped trying to fly and settled into the long brutal business of simply enduring — driven on under storm canvas, quartering the seas, shipping water and shrugging it off, again and again and again.

Hours passed. Or minutes. Or years. Time went strange in weather like this; it stopped being a river and became a series of blows, each one followed by the scramble to survive the next. Bess's arms burned. Her hands had gone past pain into numbness where they gripped the wheel, and she had lashed herself to the helm post with a length of rope because she'd learned long ago what happened to helmsmen who trusted their grip.

Once, between waves, she caught sight of Aldous Wren lashed at the mast, drenched to the soul, hair plastered to his skull, and she saw that he was crying — or the sea was running down his face so thickly that it made no difference — and that his mouth was moving. Prayer, maybe. Apology. It hardly mattered which. A man who'd spent four years printing the government's lies in respectable columns was out here paying his debt in salt water, and Bess thought: *good. This is what it costs. Let him learn the price properly.*

She shouted to him anyway, above the wind: "Still with me, correspondent?"

He turned his head, and even through the dark and the driving spray she saw him bare his teeth at her — not quite a smile, not quite a snarl, but something true either way. He raised one fist to the sky.

That would do. That would keep them both.

* * *

They raised the point at Penharrow near midnight, if midnight it was — the compass and the clock disagreed and Bess chose to believe the compass, because the compass had never lied to her and the Admiralty chronometer in her cabin had been built by men who worked for the same government that had erased a village.

The lee side of the headland gave them a few minutes' mercy, the seas flattening from mountains to hills, and Bess used those minutes the way a drowning man uses air. She got the storm jib off, got the anchor gear ready, and brought the *Kittiwake* in close under the cliffs toward the black slot in the rock that the charts called the Gull's Throat and the local smugglers had always called, more honestly, the Back Door.

"There," Aldous shouted, pointing. A thread of light, weak and shuttered, flickered deep in the cave mouth. Someone was signalling. Three flashes, pause, three flashes — the pattern Emrys Thackeray had drilled into all of them before they sailed, in her flat clinic voice that somehow made everything sound like a matter of life and death, because it was.

"That's her," Bess said. "Maren Voss. Hold us steady."

Getting into the cave in that sea was the worst piece of seamanship she'd ever done sober, and she'd done plenty drunk. The entrance was barely twice the *Kittiwake*'s beam, and the swell surged through it like breath through a throat, sucking the boat in and spitting her half-out again before Bess could wrestle her true. Rocks ground somewhere beneath the keel with a sound like teeth. Aldous got a line ashore on the third throw — the correspondent, of all people, with the best arm — and hands hauled from the darkness, and then the cave swallowed them whole and the roar of the open sea dropped away to an echo, and it was done.

A woman stood on the shingle with a lantern held high and a child on each hip, and she was thinner than any living person had a right to be, and she was standing like a queen.

"Maren Voss," Bess said, jumping down into the shallows.

"You're Merrow." The woman's voice was cracked from disuse but level. Her eyes went over the boat, the two of them, the storm outside, cataloguing everything. "The nurse said you'd come. I told her not to waste anyone on me."

"Well, no one asked you." Bess waded forward. "Can your children hold on?"

"They can hold on to anything," Maren said, and something moved in her face that might once have been pride and had survived everything else. "They're fisherfolk."

"Good. Because we're going straight back out, and the sea hasn't improved since we came in."

One of the children began to cry — quietly, the way children cry when they've learned crying gets you nothing — and Maren gathered her closer without looking down, the way other women breathe. Bess felt something twist under her ribs where she kept the things she didn't look at. Eleven years since she'd lost her own, and still.

"We'll get you warm," she said, gruffer than she meant to. "All of you. That's the whole of my job tonight. The rest comes after."

* * *

The rest, as it happened, came sooner than after.

They were loading the last of the provisions Aldous carried ashore from the boat — bread, tinned meat, the good wool blankets from under Bess's own bunk — when Maren spoke again, low, urgent.

"There's a man. In the depot at Penharrow. Tom Caddock — he fished with my husband, before." Before. The word carried villages in it. "Your boy Wren was asking after him in the village, I heard. They took him off the beach ten days ago. There's a man who works the coal store there, a sympathizer — he gets word out when he can. Last word was Sunday." Her jaw tightened. "They mean to move him. The officer in charge has been waiting on orders. If the orders come before you—"

"We know," Aldous said quietly. He'd come to stand beside them, streaming wet, and there was a look on his face that Bess recognized from the mirror on bad nights: the particular hunger of a man who has failed once and cannot bear to fail twice. "We know what happens to witnesses who get moved."

Nobody said the rest aloud. Witnesses who got moved didn't testify. Witnesses who got moved turned up as statistics in somebody's tidy report, or didn't turn up at all.

Bess stood in the lantern light and looked at this woman who had walked out of a burned village with two children and nothing else, who had hidden in a sea cave through two weeks of autumn, who was offering her last coin of information as though it were a gift she was ashamed to give. And Bess thought about the ledger — Emrys's ledger, the little book of names that had started all of this, that sat wrapped in oilcloth in a tin box aboard this very boat because Emrys wouldn't leave it home and Bess hadn't argued. Names. That was the whole war, right there. They wanted the world full of blanks, and Emrys Thackeray kept filling the blanks in.

Tom Caddock was a blank. Not tonight.

"The depot," Bess said. "How many guards?"

"Six, maybe eight. Coastal watch, mostly old men and boys — but there's a sergeant, regular army, and he's not old and he's not kind." Maren hesitated. "And there will be others soon. The coal man says a transport is expected. Any day. Maybe tomorrow."

"Then tomorrow's too late." Bess turned to Aldous. Rain dripped off his chin. "Well, correspondent? You wanted a story no paper would print. How does it feel to be inside one?"

"Like being drafted," he said. But he didn't flinch, and she marked that. Four years ago he'd have flinched. Whatever had broken him on the way here had also, apparently, rebuilt part of him truer than before.

* * *

They put Maren and the children aboard — the little girl asleep before they were clear of the cave mouth, worn down to nothing, the boy staring back at the cliffs as they receded with eyes too old for his face — and Bess took the *Kittiwake* back out through the Back Door and into the teeth of the storm a second time, because there was no shelter on this coast that Hale's patrols didn't know, and the only safe harbor was the one behind them, thirty miles of black hell away.

The second passage was worse than the first. Of course it was. The wind had backed and hardened, and the seas came now out of the exact direction they needed to go, so that every mile was bought twice — once with the engine straining against the swell, once with Bess's arms and spine wrestling the wheel through each mountain of water. She wedged Maren and the children into the leeward corner of the cockpit with blankets and her own body between them and the worst of the spray, and she steered, and she steered, and she steered.

Somewhere in the small hours, Maren appeared beside her, braced against the wheel housing, shouting over the wind.

"Why?" the woman demanded. "You owe me nothing. None of us. Why are you doing this?"

Bess laughed — she heard it come out of her, wild and strange, torn away by the wind almost before it left her mouth. It was a fair question. She'd asked it of herself often enough, lying awake in the loft above the boathouse, listening to the tide.

"Because they burned your village," she shouted back, "and wrote a report saying they didn't. And because somebody has to stand up on the day they read that report out and say: *I was there. These are the names. You lie.*" She tightened her grip on the wheel as the *Kittiwake* plunged. "And because I had two sons, and the war took them both, and the telegram spelled one of their names wrong. Thomas. They wrote *Thos.* Like he was a filing cabinet."

Maren said nothing for a long moment. Then her hand came down over Bess's on the wheel — work-roughened, freezing cold, steadying — and stayed there through three waves and half a mile of black water, and neither of them needed to say anything else.

* * *

They made the outer roads of the harbor an hour before dawn, grey and howling, the storm beginning at last to spend itself in long ragged squalls. Bess brought the *Kittiwake* in past the breakwater on instinct and memory alone, every light in the town doused by order of the coastal defense, and grounded her gently in the mud off Bess's own private jetty, where no official eye ever bothered to look because officially the jetty belonged to a dead woman's worthless enterprise.

Emrys Thackeray was waiting on the jetty with a storm lantern and a blanket over her arm, and behind her, wrapped in a coat three sizes too big, stood the boy they called Sparrow, pale as bone in the lamplight.

Bess handed Maren Voss down into Emrys's reaching arms and watched the two women cling for a moment — the survivor and the keeper of names — and then she looked down into the cockpit where the sleeping child lay, and up at the wrecked, soaked, half-frozen correspondent hauling lines with bleeding hands, and finally at her own boat, listing gently in the mud with a split jib and a strained forestay and seawater in her bilges that would take a week to pump out.

"She'll need repairs," Bess said. Her voice came out sanded raw. "Two days. Three."

Emrys looked up sharply. "What's in two or three days?"

"The transport," Bess said. "Penharrow depot. They're moving Tom Caddock, and they're moving him soon." She swung herself down onto the jetty, legs nearly buckling under her, and stood upright through sheer bloody-mindedness. "So we've got two days to decide how a nurse, a disgraced reporter, a smuggler, and a child who doesn't speak are going to walk into a military depot and take a witness out from under eight rifles."

Behind Emrys, in the circle of lantern light, the boy called Sparrow had gone very still. He was looking east — toward Penharrow, toward the depot, toward whatever he had seen that the reports said never happened — and his small fists were clenched at his sides like knots in wood.

When Emrys knelt to speak to him, low and gentle, he shook his head once. Then he reached out and took hold of her sleeve, deliberately, firmly, the first time Bess had ever seen him reach for anything, and pointed — east, toward Penharrow — and opened his mouth, and made a sound.

Not words. Not yet. But a sound — rough and rusted, a door forced open after years shut — and every one of them heard it, and none of them would ever forget it.

"He wants to come," Emrys whispered.

"No," said Bess. And then, watching the boy's face — that burned-village face, that forget-the-face, that face they had ordered the world to forget — she heard herself say the opposite: "God help me. He's coming, isn't he."

Nobody answered. Nobody had to.
