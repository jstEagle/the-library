## 17. What We Owe the Dead

The storm had spent itself by dusk, and the smuggler's cove lay calm as a held breath. Bess Merrow's fire was built in the lee of a fallen sea wall, ringed with stones she'd carried up from the beach one winter when there was nothing else to do with her hands. The smoke went sideways along the cliff and unraveled into the dark, thin enough that only a gull or someone who knew to look would find them.

They came down from the cottage in single file — Emrys first, her coat still smelling of carbolic and salt; then Aldous Wren with his collar turned up against the damp; then Sparrow, who walked between them now without being asked, as though he'd always belonged to some such procession. Bess brought the kettle and a bottle of rum that had never seen a customs stamp.

The fisherman, Tomas Verrick, sat closest to the flames. He had been out of the depot only two days, and he still flinched at the sound of boots on shingle. His hands were wrapped around a tin cup he did not drink from. He watched the fire the way a man watches a thing that once burned his house down.

Bess poured the rum without ceremony. "Drink," she said. "It's the only honest cargo I ever carried."

Aldous took his cup and held it a long moment before drinking, staring into it as if the liquor might show him something.

* * *

**Aldous**

He had told lies for eleven years, and he could recite them the way other men recited scripture.

Not the small lies — those were the trade's currency, everyone knew that, no one bled from those. The datelines fudged by a hundred miles so the censor wouldn't kill the piece. The adjective softened here, the atrocity moved there, attributed to the enemy because printing it against one's own side meant the wire stopped answering. Every correspondent in France had done it. That was the comfort he'd carried through four years of it: *everyone*. A whole profession walking together into the same moral mud, shoulder to shoulder, boots sucking.

But sitting here, with Tomas Verrick's ruined hands three feet from his own, Aldous found the arithmetic collapsing.

Because there had been one lie that was not like the others. One story, in the autumn of 1918 — after the armistice, God help him, *after the guns had officially gone quiet* — when a corporal had come to his billet smelling of woodsmoke and vomit and said, *there's a village down the coast that isn't on any list, and what's left of it is still smoking.* And Aldous had written it up. Twelve hundred words. He had named the regiment, given the date, described the boy they'd found in the churchyard who hadn't spoken since. He had sent it through channels.

And the story had come back to him in pieces over the following week, dismantled. Killed, first. Then denied. Then — this was the part he had never confessed, not to editors, not to himself — *replaced*. He'd been summoned to a tent with good coffee and a major with kind eyes, and shown an alternative account already typeset, and offered a choice framed so gently it hadn't even felt like a choice. The village had been evacuated, the major said. Regrettable necessity. The residents resettled inland, alive, compensated. Would Mr. Wren prefer to be part of the record, or apart from it?

He had chosen the record. He had signed a retraction saying his source was unreliable. He had watched the corporal — his source, a boy of twenty-two with a stutter and a conscience — be transferred somewhere east and never heard from again.

And now the boy was sitting across the fire from him, seven years old instead of twenty-two, mute instead of stuttering, drawing burning churches on the backs of prescription slips.

"Sparrow." Aldous said it quietly, and the child looked up. In the firelight his face was all shadows and one bright place where the flame caught his eye. "The drawings you make. The building with the tower. Is it a church?"

Sparrow nodded.

"And there were people in it? Before?"

Another nod. Slow. Then the boy reached into his shirt and drew out the folded papers he carried everywhere, and unfolded one, and laid it on the flat stone between them. The church again — he must have drawn it forty times since arriving at the clinic — but this one had figures. Small stick people going in the door. And beside the door, drawn larger than everything else, a man with marks on his shoulders that Sparrow had rendered with furious precision: the chevrons and pips of rank.

Emrys leaned forward. "You know him," she said. Not a question. She had seen the drawings for weeks; Aldous had seen them for days; but the fire and the rum and the darkness made a confessional of the cove, and things that couldn't be said under lamplight came out here.

Tomas Verrick spoke for the first time that evening, his voice like a hull scraping rock. "That'll be the one they call Hale's master. Colonel Ashworth. He stood at the wellhead while it burned." He turned his cup in his ruined hands. "I saw him. He saw me see him. That's why I'm the last."

The fire popped. Out beyond the sea wall, the tide was coming in with that long low sigh the coast made at night, as though the water itself were tired of carrying what it carried.

Aldous set down his cup. "I wrote the truth once," he said, to no one in particular, to the fire, to the dead. "And I un-wrote it. I put my name to a lie and let a man disappear for telling me the truth." He looked at Sparrow. "I don't expect you to understand what that costs. But I want it said aloud, here, where the dead can hear it if they've anywhere to listen. What I print next will be true or I will print nothing ever again. That's my vow. Take it or leave me."

No one answered, which in that company was answer enough. Bess refilled his cup.

* * *

**Bess**

She had buried two sons and gotten drunk exactly once over it, and that once had lasted four months.

Daniel had died at Ypres in the second year, official cause: shellfire. Michael had died in '18, official cause: influenza, though Bess had paid a nurse in Boulogne enough to tell her otherwise — dysentery and neglect in a casualty clearing station where the officers' ward had fresh linen and the orderlies burned the men's letters unread. Two sons, two tidy reports, two crosses in two fields she would never stand in because the crossing cost more than a smuggler widow earned in a year.

Everyone thought Bess Merrow ran contraband for the money. The money was real; she wasn't sentimental about it. But there had been a night in the winter after Michael's death, standing on her boat in the dark with a hold full of untaxed brandy, when she had understood suddenly and completely what she was doing with her life. Moving things past borders. Things the authorities wanted stopped. She had laughed until she cried, alone on the deck, because the whole trade was just grief with a profit margin, and she had finally found work that fit.

So when these three had come to her — the nurse with her ledger, the broken correspondent, the silent child with eyes like a man just back from the front — she had said yes before they finished asking, and they had probably thought her generous. Foolish, maybe. Generous, certainly not.

"You're all looking at me like I'm owed a turn," she said now, because the silence had gotten reverent and reverence choked her. "Fine. You want to know why an old woman runs soldiers through storms for free."

"Only if you want to say it," Emrys said. Always careful, the nurse. Bess liked that about her and distrusted it equally.

"My boys are in the ground under names the War Office spelled wrong," Bess said flatly. "Both of them. Daniel Merrow — they've got him as Merrick. Michael — they've got his death a month early, in a hospital he never entered. I've written to them eleven times. Eleven. I have every reply in a box in my kitchen, and do you know what they amount to?" She held up one hand, fingers spread. "*Clerical error. Regretted. Matter closed.*" She let the hand fall. "My sons were the clerical error. So when your Captain Hale wants a village full of dead people filed as *resettled*, I know exactly how heavy that paper feels, and exactly how little it holds."

Sparrow had shifted closer to her sometime during the speech. Children did that around her — the harbor children, the ones whose fathers didn't come back; they gravitated to her stall, her doorstep, her rough voice, the way cats go to a warm engine. She put her arm around him now, awkwardly, her body remembering motherhood the way a missing limb remembers itching.

"So here's mine," she said. "I'll carry whatever needs carrying. Testimony, witnesses, that ledger of yours, the lot. And when it's done, if any of you are fool enough to still be speaking to me, I'll buy the rum." She raised the bottle. "To the wrongly spelled."

They drank. Even Tomas. Even Sparrow, a sip, wincing, and Bess laughed — a short bark, quickly swallowed — and the sound startled her more than anything else that night.

* * *

**Emrys**

Her turn, and she almost refused it.

All evening she had been the listener — taking notes in her head as she once took pulse and temperature, cataloguing, triaging. It was easier. Confession was a luxury of the guilty, and Emrys Thackeray had never been able to locate herself among the guilty. She had done nothing wrong. She had kept a clinic open through bombardment with half a staff and a quarter of the supplies. She had held men's hands while they died and written their names in her ledger because no one else would, and what sin lived in that?

But the fire was warm and the night was black and Tomas Verrick was watching her with the patience of a man who had waited two years in a lighthouse to give evidence, and she understood that her silence was itself a kind of withholding. That the others had paid in truth tonight, and she was hoarding.

"I had a patient," she began, and her voice came out steadier than she expected. "In the third year. A private named Owen Llewellyn Price. Sixteen years old. He'd lied about his age and the army had known and hadn't cared, because sixteen-year-olds march and shoot as well as nineteen-year-olds until they break, and he broke." The firelight moved on her face. "Shell shock, they called it then. He couldn't stop shaking. They were going to return him to the line — the review board passed him fit in nine minutes, I timed it. He died that night in my ward. Not of wounds. Of—" She stopped. Started again. "Of terror. His heart simply gave out under the weight of being sent back. And when the report came through, his name was misspelled. Price became Pryce. And his mother wrote to me, years later, asking if anyone could confirm which grave was his, because the army's letter gave a plot number that didn't exist."

She looked at the ledger, which lay on her knees. She had brought it down without deciding to. It was a habit of the last weeks — the book went where she went, like a weapon or an organ.

"That's why I keep it. Not defiance. I'm not brave, whatever Aldous keeps writing in his notes." A ghost of a smile crossed his face across the fire. "I keep it because a name is the smallest thing we can give a person and the last thing they lose. Everything else gets taken — the uniform, the health, the life. But the name, properly recorded, properly witnessed — that survives us. That's what history actually is. Not the generals' maps. Names, kept by someone who bothered." She pressed her palm flat against the cover. "There are four hundred and six names in this book. Four hundred and six people the official record has lost, misspelled, or erased. I can't bring back one of them. But I can refuse to let the erasure stand. That's my vow — that every name in here gets read aloud somewhere that counts, or I die trying. And I'd ask—" her voice caught, and she let it, because hiding it felt like another lie— "I'd ask you all to witness that."

"We witness," Bess said simply.

"Aye," said Tomas.

Aldous raised his cup. Sparrow, watching her with those old young eyes, reached over and touched the ledger's edge with one finger, deliberately, the way a man signs a document.

Emrys looked at the boy and felt something rearrange itself in her chest, permanent and irreversible. "And you," she said softly. "What do you owe the dead, Sparrow?"

For a moment she thought she had asked too much. Then the child straightened, and pointed — at the ledger, at the drawing of the church, at each of them in turn, and last at his own mouth, pressing his lips tight shut and then opening them wide.

*Remember,* said the gesture. *And speak.*

* * *

They fed the fire until midnight, and the talk turned practical the way talk among working people does — tides, routes, the layout of the coastal depot where a second survivor was reported held, the timing of the tribunal now barely ten days off. Bess scratched a chart in the sand with a stick. Aldous sketched the depot's watch rotations from memory of a visit two years ago. Emrys marked the pages of her ledger with thread.

It was near the end, as the fire sank to coals, that Sparrow tugged Emrys's sleeve.

She bent to him. He pointed at the chart in the sand — at the depot, at its crude rectangle of walls — then held up his drawing of the officer with the insignia. Then he did something he had never done before: he took her pencil from her hand, pulled a fresh page toward himself, and wrote.

His letters were shaky, schoolboy letters, learned years ago and rusted. It took him a long time. No one breathed.

*HE IS THERE*, the page said. *THE ONE WHO ORDERED IT.*

Emrys read it twice. Around the dying fire, the others leaned in, and the meaning settled over the company like cold air: the colonel himself, Ashworth, was inspecting the depot. The tribunal's architect, standing inside their reach.

Bess looked up from the chart, and there was nothing soft left in her face at all. "Well," she said. "Then we'd best decide quick, loves. Storm's coming back tomorrow — perfect running weather."
