## 18. The Depot Raid

The plan was made in the dark of Bess's kitchen, over a chart of the coast and a stub of pencil, and it was a bad plan. Emrys knew that from the first moment she traced the line of it with her finger — the depot at Greywater Point, its palisade, its guard rotation, the half-mile of exposed road between the tree line and the gate — and she said so.

"It's a bad plan," she said.

"Most good ones start that way." Bess Merrow tapped the pencil against her palm. "Bad plans get better when you've nothing left to lose."

"We have something to lose."

Her eyes went, without her permission, to the corner of the kitchen where Sparrow sat with Aldous's spare coat around his shoulders, drawing. He had been drawing since the fisherman's disappearance — the lighthouse, the depot's silhouette as he'd imagined it, the boat they would need. He did not know yet that he would be left behind. That was the part Emrys could not make herself say aloud, and Bess saw her not saying it and nodded once, grimly, the way women nod across graves.

"He stays here," Bess said. "With Marta's girl watching him. Nobody touches a hair on his head while we're gone."

Emrys looked at the chart instead of at the boy, because if she looked at the boy she would lose her nerve entirely, and there were men in a coastal depot forty miles north who did not have the luxury of her nerve. Tom Cadwell, the fisherman from the lighthouse, taken two nights ago by men who moved like weather coming in. And behind him, somewhere in that same compound or one like it, the second survivor Sparrow's drawings kept circling back to — the man who had carried him out of the burning village, whose face the boy drew over and over without ever quite finishing it.

"You'll be inside the wire," Bess went on. "That's your part. A nurse with papers and a sick man to fetch. They'll let you through the gate because nobody stops a woman carrying a stretcher. It's the oldest trick there is and it works because it's true — you *are* a nurse, you *will* be carrying a stretcher, and the man on it will be sick enough that no sergeant wants him dying on his watch. We're only lying about which sick man."

"And you?"

"I'll be delivering brandy to the officers' mess, like any honest smuggler." Bess smiled with all the warmth of a gutting knife. "Aldous will be my man on the water. When I signal from the jetty, he brings the boat in close, past where any patrol expects a hull to sit, and we put Cadwell aboard before the tide turns."

"And if the tide turns first?"

Bess folded the chart closed. "Then we swim."

* * *

They went north in the grey hour before dawn, when the world is all rumor. Emrys sat in the stern of Bess's boat under an oilskin, holding a medical bag packed with genuine instruments and a stretcher rolled tight as a coffin, and she watched the coast slide past — black rocks, white gulls, the occasional chimney-smoke of a village waking up to a morning that belonged to them and not to her.

She thought about the first time she had lied for a living. Not lied exactly — nurses learn early that truth is a medicine to be dosed, not poured out wholesale. *You're doing beautifully,* she'd told men who were dying. *He'll write to you soon,* she'd told mothers. But this was different. This was lying to armed men about a living man, and the strange thing, the thing she turned over and over in her mind as the boat thudded through the swell, was how easy it felt already. How the guilt she'd braced for hadn't come. Only a cold clarity, like the air off the water.

*This is what history costs,* she thought. *Not grand sacrifice. Just this: a woman in a boat, rehearsing a lie.*

Greywater Point resolved out of the mist near seven o'clock — a low headland, a stone jetty, and behind a palisade of new-cut timber a cluster of sheds with a flagpole and a guard hut. Too new to be on any chart older than the war. A place built quickly for purposes no one wanted written down.

Bess brought the boat around to a cove south of the point and dropped anchor among fishing smacks that had every right to be there. "Two hours," she said. "If you're not back at the jetty by nine, I'm coming in after you, consequences be damned."

"Don't," Emrys said. "If I'm not back, take the boat and go. Get Cadwell another day. Don't trade three of us for one."

Bess looked at her for a long moment, and something passed between them that had no words — the look of two women who both knew what it was to count lives and find the arithmetic obscene.

"You'll be back," Bess said. It wasn't reassurance. It was an order.

* * *

The gate was easier than she'd feared, which frightened her more than difficulty would have.

The sergeant who met her at the palisade was young, red-cheeked, bored. She showed him her papers — real papers, clinic letterhead, a transfer order for a prisoner-patient signed by a doctor whose name she'd borrowed with a clear conscience because the doctor owed her for two years of night shifts — and she watched his eyes do exactly what she'd predicted they would do: skip over the text and land on the Red Cross armband, the stretcher, the bag.

"Prisoner's feverish," she said, in the flat, tired voice she'd used on ten thousand orderlies. "Your commandant wrote asking for assessment. If he's got typhus, your whole garrison wants to know today, not next week."

That word — *typhus* — opened gates faster than money ever had. The sergeant stepped back as if the stretcher itself might breathe on him.

"Quarantine shed's at the far end," he said, waving her through. "Mind you don't touch anything."

Inside, the depot was worse than she'd imagined, because it was so ordinary. Men stacking crates. A cook-fire smell. Laundry strung between posts. An empire doing its housekeeping, and in one of these sheds, behind one of these doors, a fisherman who had seen his village burned was being kept the way inconvenient documents are kept — filed, sealed, forgotten.

She walked the length of the compound slowly, counting sheds, marking guards, letting the stretcher explain her. At the third shed from the seaward wall a guard stood apart from the others, and stood *alertly*, which marked it the way a sore thumb marks a hand. She slowed beside it, adjusted the stretcher on her shoulder, and let her trained eye flick through the gap in the door.

Tom Cadwell lay on a cot inside, wrists bandaged, face the colour of old cheese, but his eyes — his eyes found hers through the gap with a ferocity that nearly stopped her heart. Alive. Furious. Waiting.

She walked on. She did not look back. Somewhere behind her ribs, terror and triumph were braiding themselves together into a single cord, and she understood suddenly why soldiers described battle the way they did — not as horror, though it was that, but as *clarity*. Every step mattered. Nothing was wasted. She had spent six years believing history happened elsewhere, to other people, and now she was walking through it in nurse's shoes, and it fit her like her own hands.

At the quarantine shed she performed her examination — of a private named Willis who had, genuinely, a bad chest and a worse attitude — and wrote her recommendations in a fine round hand while the corporal assigned to her hovered nervously out of contagion range. Then she mentioned, casually, to the corporal, that the prisoner in the third shed ought to be assessed too, given the overcrowding, given the reports, given the risk.

"The one that came in Tuesday? He's not sick. He's just—" The corporal lowered his voice. "He's just to be held. Orders."

"Held *how*?" She let the nurse's indignation rise, the righteous fury of a woman whose wards are being neglected. "Manacles leave wounds, Corporal, and wounds in this season kill. Do you want that death on your ledger? Because I promise you, no officer will carry it. It will be yours."

She watched the word *ledger* land, watched the boy's conscience — conscripted, confused, nineteen — begin to sweat. "I could— I mean, if it's medical, I could have him brought to you. For assessment. Here."

"Yes," Emrys said. "Do that."

* * *

It worked. That was the terrible, wonderful thing — it worked. Twenty minutes later four soldiers carried Tom Cadwell into the quarantine shed on a door taken off its hinges, dumped him on a cot, and withdrew to the doorway to smoke, because fever was catching and cigarettes were important. Emrys knelt over him with her stethoscope and spoke into his chest as if listening to his heart.

"Jetty," she breathed. "Nine o'clock. Can you walk?"

His lips barely moved. "Walk, crawl, or swim, missus. Whichever gets me away from these bastards."

"Good. On my signal, you faint. Convulsions, if you can manage it. You're being transferred for urgent care and no one questions a stretcher leaving a quarantine shed — they'll be relieved to see the back of you."

"And the other one?" His hand found her wrist, gripped with a strength his body didn't match. "The one they took off the boat before me. Older man. Grey beard. Is he alive?"

Her heart stumbled. "I don't know."

"They keep him in the commandant's quarters. Not a cell. *Quarters.* Like a guest who can't leave." His eyes burned up into hers. "Whatever you're doing, missus — do it fast. There's a captain comes Thursdays. Takes men out in a motor car. They don't all come back."

Outside, a whistle blew. Nine o'clock. Somewhere beyond the palisade, Bess would be walking up the jetty with her brandy crates and her gutting-knife smile, and Aldous Wren would be bringing a boat in through water no honest hull should sail.

Emrys straightened, announced that the prisoner required immediate transfer, and signed a form no one read. Two soldiers took up the stretcher ends. Tom Cadwell closed his eyes and became, with a fisherman's instinct for playing dead things, convincingly moribund.

And then, as the party reached the open ground between the sheds, everything happened at once.

A shout from the gate. Running boots. The sergeant from that morning sprinting toward them with a telegram flapping in his fist, shouting for the commandant, shouting about a woman — *a woman at the jetty, sir, says she's a supplier, but the manifest's wrong, sir, the crates are wrong* —

Bess. Caught. Or pretending to be caught; with Bess it was impossible to know. Either way, the compound woke like a kicked hive.

"Keep moving," Emrys said through her teeth. "Fever patient. Out of the sun."

For thirty yards it held — the sheer inertia of bureaucracy, soldiers reluctant to countermand a signed transfer even as their world came apart around them. They reached the seaward gate. The guard there lifted the bar. Beyond it, the path down to the jetty, the grey water, and — God bless her and sink her enemies — the black hull of Bess's boat sliding in impossibly close, Aldous at the tiller, white-faced and steady.

Then the sergeant caught up.

"That's her," he gasped. "That's the one from the lighthouse business — the nurse — there's a warrant —"

"Stand aside," Emrys said, and heard her own voice come out in a register she didn't recognize. "This man has suspected typhus. Unless you want to explain to the commandant why you released a contagious prisoner into his garrison, you will stand aside."

The soldiers wavered. The sergeant did not. He grabbed for her arm — and that was the mistake, because Tom Cadwell, hearing the words *lighthouse*, chose that moment to stop feigning illness and start creating diversion, rolling off the stretcher with a roar and driving his shoulder into the sergeant's knees. Men shouted. Someone fired a shot that went high and wild. Emrys grabbed the stretcher straps and ran, hauling the canvas toward the path, and Cadwell scrambled up and ran beside her, and then they were down the slope and into the shallows and hands — strong hands, Bess's hands, salt-crusted and sure — were hauling them over the gunwale as the boat heeled away and oars bit and the shore fell behind like a bad memory.

They were a quarter mile out before anyone spoke. Cadwell lay gasping in the bilge, laughing and coughing at once. Bess was wringing seawater from her sleeves, having apparently escaped custody by the simple expedient of falling into the harbour. Aldous stared at Emrys across the thwart with an expression she couldn't name.

"You did it," he said. "You actually—"

"Where's Sparrow?" she said.

The question came out of her like something breaking. All the way through it — the gate, the shed, the running, the shot — she had carried one thought beneath all the others, the way a diver carries the rope that leads home: *the boy is safe, the boy is safe, the boy is safe.* And now, surfacing, she reached for the rope and found the boat silent.

"Marta's girl was with him," Bess said slowly. "In the loft above the chandlery. We agreed—"

"Where *is* he?"

Nobody answered. The boat ran on. And Emrys Thackeray, who had walked through a fortified depot under the noses of an empire and never once trembled, felt her hands begin to shake — because in the pocket of the coat she'd left draped over Sparrow's shoulders that morning, folded small, was the drawing he'd finished at last: the burning village, and the grey-bearded man carrying a child, and beneath it, in a child's careful block letters learned only weeks ago, the words *I CAN HELP.*

He hadn't been in the loft since dawn.
