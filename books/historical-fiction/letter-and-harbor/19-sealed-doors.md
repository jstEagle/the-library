## 19. Sealed Doors

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy in a corporal's coat who would not meet her eyes. *His Majesty's Tribunal of Inquiry, convened under authority of the War Office, requests the attendance of Mrs. Emrys Thackeray at the Customs House, Penzance, Thursday next, ten o'clock.*

*Requests.* The word had been chosen carefully. Emrys read the paper twice at her kitchen table while Sparrow slept upstairs, then set it beneath the teapot as though its weight might be contained by crockery.

"They've called us," she told Aldous that evening, when he came with his collar up and his hat low, looking like a man perpetually about to be arrested, which lately he often was.

"Called *you*," Aldous corrected gently. He dripped seawater on her floorboards; he'd come round the headland from Bess's boat rather than walk the high road, where soldiers had taken to loitering outside the bakery like men with nothing better to do and everything to watch. "I'm still a name they'd rather not print."

"Then why am I summoned?"

"To be dismissed." He said it without cruelty, which made it worse. "They'll seat you in a room with mahogany panelling and a man with a very kind voice, and he'll thank you for your service to the wounded, and he'll explain — patiently, as to a child — that civilian testimony is outside the tribunal's remit. That the matters you wish to raise fall under military jurisdiction, which has already concluded its findings. And then you'll be shown the door, and the door will be locked behind you, and that will be that."

"You sound very sure."

"I sat through three of these in Flanders." Aldous turned his hat in his hands. "I watched a mother from Loos try to give evidence about her son's last letter. They wouldn't even let her finish the sentence. *Remit*, Mrs. Thackeray. It's a beautiful word. It means: we have decided what may be said before anyone has said it."

Emrys looked out the window at the harbor, gray under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. The fishing boats were in early; the weather was turning. Somewhere beyond the mole, past the lightship, lay the drowned coast where a village had burned after the guns went quiet, and somewhere in London men were drafting the official account of how it hadn't happened at all.

"Then we're finished," she said. "If they won't hear us, there's nowhere left to stand."

Aldous was quiet a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — lower, rougher, the voice she imagined he used when dictating dispatches at three in the morning with his hands shaking too badly to hold the pen steady.

"I didn't say there was nowhere to stand," he said. "I said their door is sealed. I never much liked doors anyway. I prefer windows. And windows," he added, standing, pulling his hat down over his eyes, "can be broken."

---

The Customs House smelled of floor wax and old rope, an unholy marriage of empire and harbor that Emrys found oddly fitting. She had dressed in her good black — the dress she'd worn to her husband's funeral, and before that to her matron's interview, and before that to nothing at all, because she'd bought it secondhand and it had served every occasion gravity required of her since.

The hearing room was exactly as Aldous had described: mahogany, green baize, a long table behind which sat five men in uniforms and one in civilian wool, all of them middle-aged, all of them comfortable, none of them looking at her when she entered except the youngest, who looked quickly away.

"Mrs. Thackeray." The chairman was a major-general whose name she recognized from the casualty lists — not as a dead man but as a signatory, the tidy signature that had appeared beneath a dozen reports assuring families their sons had fallen honorably in action. He gestured to a single chair set facing the table, arranged like a witness box and yet pointedly not one. "Thank you for coming. We appreciate your service to the wounded of this district."

"Service to the wounded," Emrys repeated. She sat. "Is that what this is?"

"It is an inquiry into irregularities arising during demobilization operations along the southwestern littoral—"

"Burning villages is an irregularity now?" She heard her own voice go flat and hard, the nurse's voice, the one she used for men who screamed and bit and lied about how much it hurt. "Major-General. I kept a clinic at Ypres for two years. I know what irregular means. An irregular heartbeat. An irregular supply line. A village put to the torch six days after the armistice, with its people in it — that is not irregular. That is murder with a typewriter."

A murmur ran down the table. The chairman's face did not change; it was a face built for not changing.

"Mrs. Thackeray. You are here as a private citizen. This tribunal receives testimony from commissioned officers and Crown officials. Civilian statements regarding military conduct fall outside our remit."

"The boy saw it."

"We are aware of certain rumors concerning a child." The word *rumors* landed on the table between them like something dead. "Indeed, that matter is being addressed through other channels."

"What channels?"

He almost smiled. Almost. There was a flicker at the corner of his mouth, the ghost of a courtesy that Captain Hale wore openly and this man kept holstered. "Channels appropriate to the welfare of minors. You needn't concern yourself further."

"I have concerned myself with him since November. He sleeps under my roof. He eats at my table. He—" She stopped, because the next word was *trusts me*, and saying it aloud in this room felt like handing them a knife.

"He is, I believe, without documented parentage or guardianship." The chairman consulted a paper before him, though Emrys would have wagered her clinic he knew its contents by heart. "Which raises questions the tribunal is not equipped to address, but others are. You are free to go, Mrs. Thackeray. Thank you for your time."

"That's it? I've crossed half the county and you won't hear one word?"

"You are free to go." He repeated it kindly, terribly kindly, the way one speaks to the confused elderly or the shell-shocked. "The record will note your attendance."

"The record," Emrys said, rising slowly, her hands gripping the back of the chair, "will note nothing. That is precisely the trouble with your record, Major-General. I have spent nine years writing down the names of men your record loses. Boys who died with no rank but a first name. Lads the stretcher-bearers knew only as *the Welsh one* or *the one with the laugh*. Somebody has to remember them, because you have built an entire architecture of forgetting and called it victory."

For the first time, the chairman looked directly at her. His eyes were pale and patient and utterly without malice, which was the most frightening thing in the room.

"Mrs. Thackeray," he said, "history is written by those entrusted with it. Good day."

She walked out past the soldier posted at the door, past the brass plaque and the marble steps, into a wind that smelled of coming rain. Her hands were shaking so badly she had to lean against the sea wall before she could manage the walk home. Not from fear. From the peculiar, suffocating rage of speaking truth into a room designed to swallow sound.

She had thought the ledger would be enough. Names on paper, dates, wounds witnessed, deaths recorded in her own careful hand. She had thought evidence was a door that opened when you knocked with the truth.

She understood now that some doors only open outward. From the other side. By arrangement.

---

Hale came on Friday, and this time he did not bring gifts.

She found him standing in her dispensary, uninvited, turning a brown glass bottle in his fingers as though appraising its contents. Two of his men waited outside on the step, visible through the frosted pane like shadows on a lantern slide. Upstairs, she could hear Sparrow moving about — the small sounds of a child playing alone, blocks against wood, the particular silence he kept whenever uniformed men were near, as though stillness might make him invisible.

"You should knock," Emrys said.

"A captain doesn't knock in his own district." Hale set the bottle down precisely where it had stood. He was a handsome man in a way that had curdled — good bones gone sharp, blue eyes gone flat. "You attended the tribunal yesterday."

"You keep close count of my movements."

"It's my business to know what happens here." He picked up her mortar and pestle, weighed them in his palm, set them down. He touched things. She noticed it now — he always touched things, establishing ownership by contact, a habit she'd seen in men who had spent the war requisitioning whatever they liked. "And it has been decided that the child's situation requires attention. Formal attention."

"He has attention. Mine."

"An undocumented minor, harbored by an unmarried woman of no fixed medical qualification, in premises adjacent to premises used for smuggling—" Hale let the sentence dangle, unfinished, a hook baited with implication. "The Poor Law authorities take a dim view. So do the military police. A ward of the state, Mrs. Thackeray. There are institutions. Reformatory schools, in the first instance. Very disciplined places. The sort of place where a mute child learns to be very quiet indeed."

The cold that went through her was absolute and physical, a tide reaching her chest. She thought of Sparrow's drawings, the burning building rendered again and again in careful, obsessive strokes; thought of how he flinched at boots on stairs; thought of what such a place — stone walls, strange hands, the smell of other children's fear — would make of him. Or unmake.

"He's a witness," she said quietly. "That's what this is. You can't reach the fisherman in the lighthouse, you can't reach Bess Merrow's boat, so you'll take the child and lose him in the system until the tribunal closes and the ink dries. Then he can be released, or transported, or simply forgotten — another name that never enters any record."

Hale smiled, and it was worse than his flatness. "You make it sound sinister. It's welfare. Everyone wants what's best for the boy."

"Everyone wants what's best for the report."

"There it is." He sighed, almost fondly. "That tongue of yours. You know, I offered you funding once. A proper grant. New equipment, a trained assistant, coal enough to heat the whole building. You could have done such good, Mrs. Thackeray. Instead you chose—" he gestured vaguely at the dispensary, the ledger's hiding place somewhere beneath her floorboards, the whole stubborn architecture of her defiance— "*this*. Association with a disgraced journalist. With Merrow's widow and her boats. With a child who belongs to the Crown whether you like it or not."

"He belongs to himself."

"No one belongs to themselves." Hale said it lightly, a throwaway, and somehow that made it the truest thing he'd said. He moved toward the door, then paused, hand on the frame. "The order comes Monday. Magistrate's signature — there's a magistrate in Truro who owes the War Office several favors. You'll have until Monday evening to surrender him peaceably. Do that, and perhaps nothing else need happen. Keep him, and I'm afraid the smuggling charges against Mrs. Merrow become... unavoidable. And your correspondent friend — well. Sedition is such a flexible word."

"And if I testify publicly? If I tell the whole town what you are?"

Hale looked back at her, and for a moment the handsome mask slipped and she saw the officer underneath, the one who had signed the order that emptied a village into ash, and he was tired. Tired and frightened, in his way. Men who burn witnesses are always, underneath, afraid of fire.

"Tell them," he said. "Who listens to a woman with a notebook?"

He left. The shadows moved off the frosted glass. From upstairs came the small clatter of blocks, oblivious, ordinary, unbearably precious.

Emrys stood in her dispensary among the bottles and the bandages and the ghosts of every patient she had ever failed to save, and felt the last legal avenue swing shut in her face with a sound like a vault door. Tribunal sealed. Testimony refused. Magistrate bought. Monday coming like a wall of water.

There was no lawful road left. Every door was locked, and the men holding the keys were the same men who had struck the match.

---

She went down to the harbor at dusk, because she could not bear the clinic's walls, and found Bess Merrow mending net on the quay with the economy of motion that thirty years of water teaches. Bess took one look at her face and put down the net.

"Out with it."

"Hale's taking him Monday. Ward of the state. And he'll hang charges on you and sedition on Aldous if I don't hand the boy over."

Bess was silent. Gulls argued over the midden heaps. Down the quay, the *Kestrel's Pride* — Bess's boat, named for a son, both sons lost to the war in ways the official record called *honorable* — knocked gently against her mooring timbers.

"Monday," Bess said at last. "Three days."

"Two nights. And I've no idea what comes after, because every road I know is closed."

Bess rolled a cigarette with seaman's fingers, lit it, and stared out at the darkening water for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had the gravel of decision settling into it.

"My lads' names aren't on any memorial. Did you know that? Tom because the Navy lost the paperwork, and Jem because the regiment spelled his name wrong and nobody corrected it. Two boys, lived and died, and the country that spent them can't even spell them right." She drew on the cigarette. "My Tom used to say the sea keeps its own records. Tide tables, currents, what the water carries and what it gives back. Nothing's ever truly lost at sea, he said. It just goes round again."

Emrys waited.

"So the doors are sealed." Bess flicked ash into the water. "Fine. Sealed doors are for people who need doors. We've got a boat." She turned, and in the last light her face was grim as granite and twice as solid. "Your journalist — the windows man. Ask him what happens when a story can't be told *to* a tribunal. Ask him where a story goes when every official ear is shut."

"I don't understand."

"No," Bess said. "But he does. He's been chewing on it for a week, ever since the tribunal date was posted. Why d'you think he's been down at the telegraph office every evening, sweet-talking that stammering clerk? Why d'you think he's been hoarding wire forms like a miser?" She stood, joints cracking, and gripped Emrys's arm with a hand like a mooring cleat. "A story they won't hear in one room can still be shouted from a rooftop. And there's more than one rooftop in this world, love. Some of them are across the Channel."

Emrys stood on the quay as the first rain arrived, thin and cold, stippling the black water. Across the Channel. Newspapers that answered to no War Office, tribunals of public opinion with jurisdiction everywhere, readers in Paris and New York who could not be threatened with magistrates or institutions. Aldous Wren, the disgraced correspondent, the man who had printed lies for four years and hungered for one true thing — he had not been despairing at all. He had been *preparing*.

Behind her, up the hill, a light burned in the clinic window, and behind the glass a small silhouette bent over its drawing, rendering the burning village once more, keeping faith with the dead the only way it knew how.

Monday was coming. But tomorrow night, Emrys decided, walking back up through the rain with her chin lifted against the wind, she and Aldous and a smuggler's widow were going to sit down at her kitchen table with a ledger full of names, a stack of telegraph forms, and a list of foreign newspapers — and they were going to commit the fastest crime against the Empire's memory that any of them had ever contemplated.

The doors were sealed. So be it.

They would go through the wires instead.
