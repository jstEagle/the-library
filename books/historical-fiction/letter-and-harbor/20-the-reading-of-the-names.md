## 20. The Reading of the Names

The morning of the reading, Emrys woke before the gulls and could not tell if her hands were shaking from cold or from what she meant to do with them.

She dressed in the dark of the Merrow boathouse loft, where they had slept in shifts for three nights since Hale's second offer. Bess lay below with one arm flung over Sparrow's shoulders, both of them breathing in the rhythm of people who had learned to sleep through storms. Emrys buttoned her collar slowly. In the pocket of her coat sat the ledger — smaller than she remembered it being when she first stitched its cover from sailcloth, lighter than the four years of names inside it deserved to be.

Aldous was already on the dock when she came down, hatless despite the wind, a telegram blank folded into his breast pocket like a pistol.

"The wires are open," he said. "I've sent word to three foreign desks. If I stand close enough to shout over their heads, they'll hear me in Paris by evening."

"And if they don't print it?"

He looked at her then — that hollowed-out look he wore like a borrowed coat, the look of a man who had spent a war turning other men's deaths into column inches. "Then I'll have written one true thing at last, even if only the sea reads it." He paused. "You can still walk away, Mrs. Thackeray. The tribunal convenes at nine. No one expects a nurse to stand in the square."

"That," Emrys said, "is precisely why they'll listen."

* * *

The tribunal building had been a customs house before the war, gray stone and tall windows, built for the weighing of goods rather than the weighing of guilt. Now its doors were barred by two sentries and a placard: *SITTING IN CAMERA — BY INVITATION ONLY.* Inside, behind those handsome windows, eleven officers and a stenographer were drafting the version of history that would outlive them all.

Emrys stood at the foot of the steps with the crowd beginning to gather around her — fishwives with market baskets, dockhands still smelling of pitch, children hoisted onto shoulders, old men who had lost sons and said nothing about it for years because no one had ever asked them properly. Word had traveled overnight down the harbor lanes the way word always did in a town that lived by rumor as much as tide.

She unfolded the ledger against her forearm the way she had once held casualty lists in a dressing station, and her voice, when it came, surprised her by steadiness.

"My name is Emrys Thackeray. I kept a clinic at this harbor through the war. These are the names of the dead and missing that no official report has recorded. I am going to read them."

A silence, then the shuffling of a crowd settling itself the way congregations settle.

"Abbott, Thomas James. Private. Lost off Point Aldery, March 1917. His mother waited eleven months for a letter."

"Abernathy, William. Stoker. Fever, unreported. Buried at sea without record."

"Acton, Peter. Acton, Samuel. Brothers. Drowned together, August 1918. Their boat was found; their names were not entered anywhere."

She read. The wind took some of the words and gave them back doubled. Her voice did not break — not at the twentieth name, nor the fiftieth, though somewhere past the hundredth her throat began to ache as though the names themselves were passing through it like stones. She had written every entry in ink because pencil fades. She had never imagined the ink would be read aloud on stone steps under a gray winter sky, but she had known, in the way a body knows a wound, that someday someone would have to say these names where the powerful could hear them.

History happened elsewhere, she had believed once. To other people, in ministries and headquarters, in rooms with maps on the walls. Standing there, she understood at last that history happened wherever a name was spoken or withheld — and that she had been making history all along, one ledger line at a time, without knowing it was ammunition.

* * *

By mid-morning the crowd had swollen past two hundred. Shopkeepers closed early. Someone brought a brazier and set it near the steps so the old ones could warm their hands. A woman in a widow's black stood directly below Emrys and mouthed each name after her, as if learning them for a memorial she intended to keep privately forever.

Emrys's voice grew hoarse by the eleventh hour. She stopped, drank water a child pressed into her hands, and began again.

"Hollis, Edmund. Fifteen years old. Lied about his age to serve. Killed in the burning of Carrickmoor — the village the reports call *evacuated.*"

At that name a murmur went through the crowd like wind through barley. *Carrickmoor.* Even spoken aloud, even here, the name had weight enough to bend people toward each other.

On the tribunal steps, the sentries shifted. One of them disappeared inside. Emrys kept reading.

She saw Aldous at the edge of the square, dictating rapid-fire to a telegraph clerk who had come himself, notebook out, face shining with the particular excitement of a man carrying news too big for his salary. Twice Aldous looked up at her, and twice she saw in his expression something she had not seen there before: not hunger, not ambition, but awe — the awe of a man watching the story he had chased across half a Europe finally catch fire without him needing to strike the match.

Then the doors opened.

Captain Hale came down the steps alone, greatcoat buttoned to the throat, his face arranged into the courteous mask Emrys knew better than any other expression he owned. The crowd stiffened. Somewhere behind her, Bess Merrow's hand found her shoulder and stayed there.

"Mrs. Thackeray." Hale's voice carried easily; he'd been trained for parade grounds. "This gathering constitutes an obstruction of the tribunal's proceedings. I must ask you to disperse."

"I am not dispersing, Captain. I am testifying." She held the ledger slightly higher. "Since the tribunal will not receive civilian witnesses, the civilian public will have to do."

"You are inciting disorder."

"I am reading names." Her voice cracked now, finally, and she let it. "Hollis, Edmund. Fifteen years old. You know how he died, Captain. Your report says he was never born."

Something moved behind Hale's eyes — not shame, she suspected, nothing so useful as shame, but calculation meeting an obstacle it had not priced in. Behind him, at the tall windows of the customs house, faces had begun to appear. Officers, clerks, the stenographer with his fingers still ink-stained. They were watching. They could not help it. The spectacle had breached the sealed doors more effectively than any writ.

"Mrs. Thackeray," Hale said, lower now, pitched for her alone, "you are handing me the means to charge you with sedition."

"Then you will have to arrest a woman reading the names of dead boys to their neighbors," Emrys said, "in front of two hundred witnesses and three foreign correspondents, while your own officers watch from windows." She turned back to the crowd before he could answer. "Hollis, Edmund. Private. And with him—"

* * *

They did not arrest her.

Later, Bess claimed she'd seen the exact moment it turned — a big harborman named Coady stepping forward to stand beside the widow in black, then another man, then a dozen, forming a loose and unspoken wall between the steps and the crowd. Whether it was numbers or nerves or the sight of the stenographer's face at the window, hungry for exactly what Emrys was giving him, Hale withdrew up the stairs with a bow that fooled no one, and the doors closed again, and Emrys read on.

She read until the light went amber and the lamps along the quay were lit. She read the last name — *Young, Alfred. Boy, first class. Age unknown. He told the clinic he was sixteen. He was not* — as the sun dropped behind the masts, and when she finished, the crowd did not cheer. They stood in a silence so complete she could hear water moving against the pilings, and then, one by one, the women in widow's black began to say the names back. Quietly at first. Then all together, a low tide of sound rolling up the square: *Abbott. Abernathy. Acton. Acton.*

Grief transfigured. That was what it was, Emrys thought, standing spent against the cold stone with the ledger pressed to her chest. Not victory — the tribunal had not yielded an inch yet — but transfiguration. Two hundred people had come strangers to grief and left carrying it in common, and no committee of eleven men could vote that out of existence.

Aldous reached her first, breathless, waving a telegram confirmation slip like a battle standard. "Filed. Paris, New York, Amsterdam. By tomorrow morning the story has no borders left." His grin was wild and young and briefly stripped of all its guilt. "They can seal whatever doors they like now. The record is loose in the world."

Bess appeared at her other side, Sparrow half-hidden in the shadow of her skirts. The boy looked up at Emrys, and without a word he touched the ledger's cover — the same small gesture he'd made the night he first arrived, when the note pinned to him said *forget the face.* His eyes asked a question he still mostly couldn't speak.

"No one will forget the face," Emrys told him softly. "Or the village. Or you."

It was then that the doors of the tribunal opened a third time — not Hale this time but a pale junior officer, moving quickly, a single sheet of paper in his hand. He crossed the square through the thinning crowd as though walking into weather he didn't trust, and stopped before Emrys, and cleared his throat.

"Mrs. Thackeray." He glanced at the crowd, at the windows, at the telegram slip still in Aldous's fist. "The Tribunal... the Tribunal requests your attendance tomorrow at ten o'clock." A swallow. "As a witness. There will be a hearing — open, sir, madam — open to the public." His voice dropped. "And Captain Hale asks—" he hesitated, visibly choosing between two sentences, and chose honesty, "—Captain Hale wishes you to know he intends to conduct the cross-examination personally."

The paper trembled slightly in Emrys's hand as she took it. Around her the square had gone quiet again, everyone waiting, the widow in black, the boy called Sparrow, the correspondent, the smuggler, the whole improvised family of the harbor.

Ten o'clock. Cross-examination. Hale, with her lawbreaking laid out like exhibits.

"Well then," Bess said into the silence, grinning fierce and salt-hard, "best get some sleep, love. Tomorrow we go to war."
