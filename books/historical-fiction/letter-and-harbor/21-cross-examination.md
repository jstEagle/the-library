## 21. Cross-Examination

The tribunal chamber had been a ballroom once, in the years when the town's shipping families still had money for dancing. Emrys could see the ghost of it in the parquet floor, in the tall windows now draped with grey wool so the light would fall evenly on the witnesses and no one could read a face from the gallery. Someone had decided that justice, like surgery, was best performed under flat, shadowless illumination.

They had seated her at a plain table facing the long bench where five officers sat behind a rail of dark oak. Captain Hale sat to their right, at a smaller table of his own, his uniform pressed into knife-edge creases, a leather folder squared before him like an instrument laid out for an operation. He did not look at her. He did not need to. She felt his attention the way she had once felt the approach of hemorrhage — a pressure in the room, a change in the air.

Aldous Wren sat behind her in the public benches with Bess Merrow, close enough that she could hear the scratch of his pencil even when he wrote nothing at all. He had shaved for the occasion. It made him look younger and more frightened, which she suspected was the truth of him.

The president of the tribunal, a Major-General with a face like a closed ledger, read the charges of inquiry in a voice sanded smooth by decades of drill: unlawful destruction of property, deaths of civilians subsequent to the armistice, falsification of official returns. The words *alleged* and *it is said* appeared so often they became a kind of music, a lullaby the empire sang to itself.

"Mrs. Thackeray." The general looked down at her over spectacles. "You will state your name and occupation for the record."

"Emrys Thackeray. Keeper of the harbor clinic. Formerly sister-in-charge, Number Fourteen Casualty Clearing Station."

"You are not a lawyer. You are not a historian. You are not a serving member of any medical corps."

"No, sir. I am a person who writes things down."

A murmur moved through the benches behind her. The general let it die before he spoke again.

"You have submitted a document. A private ledger of some two hundred and eleven entries. You understand that its provenance is irregular. That it was compiled without authority. That portions of it were obtained under circumstances we will come to."

"I understand," Emrys said, "that every name in it belongs to someone who does not have a grave anyone can find."

The general's pen paused. Somewhere behind the grey drapes, a gull screamed and was silenced by a slammed window.

* * *

Hale rose to cross-examine her in the afternoon, after the clerk had read entries aloud until the numbers blurred. He approached the witness stand unhurried, hands clasped behind him, and stood closer than courtesy required. Up close she could see the fatigue under his polish — three weeks of siege had marked him too, though he wore it better than most men wear anything.

"Mrs. Thackeray." His voice was gentle, almost kind, and she understood at once that this was the weapon. "You've done a great deal of good in this town. Nobody disputes it. The clinic, the children you've fed through two hard winters. I'd be the last man to diminish that."

"Then don't," she said.

Someone laughed — quickly strangled. Hale smiled as if she had pleased him.

"Let us talk about the ledger." He turned to the bench. "May I?" Permission given, he lifted the ledger itself from the evidence table — her ledger, her handwriting, her dead — and held it up as if weighing it. "Two hundred and eleven names. Compiled between November 1918 and this month. Is that correct?"

"Yes."

"Compiled how? From what sources? Let me help you. Soldiers' letters home, copied without leave. Hospital returns taken from offices where you did not work. A page removed — stolen, I believe is the word — from a military depot during what your friend Mrs. Merrow's boat log charmingly calls 'a supply run.' Shall I go on?"

"If it helps you sleep," Emrys said. "Go on."

The laugh this time was louder. The general rapped for order, but something had shifted in the room, and Emrys felt it turn beneath her like tide under a keel.

"You broke into military facilities," Hale continued. "You received stolen documents. You harbored a minor whose custody was formally transferred to the Crown — you knew that, because I told you myself, in this very town, with witnesses. You conspired to move that child out of jurisdiction aboard a smuggler's vessel. You aided the escape of a detainee from a coastal depot." He set the ledger down on the rail before her, open to a page she knew by heart. "Every fact you wish this tribunal to hear arrived in your hands by way of the law being broken. So I ask you plainly, madam: why should the record of this inquiry rest upon evidence gathered by criminals?"

Emrys looked at the open ledger. Entry forty-one. *Pvt. Thomas Ellery, 2nd Btn., listed missing 3rd March; seen alive at Vellacott's Corner, burned. Wife: Ada, Mill Lane.* She had written it in ink gone brown now, in a kitchen that smelled of carbolic and bread.

"Because the alternative," she said, "is that they rest on nothing at all."

"Answer the question."

"I am answering it. Your report says the village of Carreg Ddu was evacuated on the ninth of November with no loss of life. My ledger holds nineteen names from that village alone — nineteen people who were evacuated, as you put it, straight into the ground. If the only way those names could reach this room was through a broken lock, then the fault, Captain, lies with the lock, not with me."

Hale studied her for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice dropped, pitched for her alone, though the stenographer caught every word.

"You're a nurse. You triage. You know that sometimes the wounded are left to save the many. That is all my superiors did — an ugly arithmetic, done in an ugly week, and the war was over, and the country needed to look forward—"

"The war was over," Emrys repeated. "That is the whole of it, isn't it. The killing went on four days past the armistice because no one had yet found the arithmetic ugly enough to stop. You didn't leave the wounded to save the many, Captain. You burned the ward and called it housekeeping."

She heard Aldous's pencil stop. In the silence she saw Hale's jaw tighten — the first crack in the pressed uniform — and she thought, with a strangeness she would remember later, *he is young underneath it all. Someone taught him this was duty, and no one has ever made him say it aloud.*

"No further questions," he said quietly, and sat down.

* * *

They called Sparrow on the third morning.

Emrys had argued against it through the whole of the previous night — with the general's legal advisor, with Aldous, with herself, pacing the clinic while Bess sat sharpening nothing in particular just to have a knife in her hand. He's eight years old. He has said eleven sentences in his life that any of us have heard. But the tribunal's own counsel had insisted: the identification of the officer who gave the order was the spine of the entire inquiry, and there was exactly one living witness to the giving of it.

So on the third morning Bess walked him up the aisle of the old ballroom holding his hand as if they were crossing a street, and Emrys watched him climb into the raised chair built for grown men and disappear into it, his boots swinging above the floor.

He wore the coat Emrys had cut down for him in December, wool gone soft at the cuffs. His hair had been combed — Bess's work, and it showed, one stubborn cowlick rising like a flag. He did not look at the officers. He looked at Emrys, and she nodded once, the way she had nodded across a dressing station a hundred times: *I'm here. Go on.*

"State your name for the record," the general said, almost gently.

Sparrow's throat worked. The chamber leaned toward him.

"Thomas," he said. Then, louder, finding the word like a foothold: "Thomas Carreg. Mam said our name was the village's name, before the soldiers took both."

The stenographer's keys clattered. Emrys pressed her knuckles to her mouth. They had chosen the name together, weeks ago, in the clinic kitchen — but hearing it enter the record, spoken in his own small voice, was a different thing entirely. A boy who had been *forget the face* was now, officially, forever, somebody.

"Do you know why you are here, Thomas?"

"They want to know about the burning."

"Tell it your own way. Take whatever time you need."

He told it his own way. He spoke slowly, in the careful grammar of a child who had rehearsed in dreams, and the room grew so quiet that Emrys could hear the harbor gulls through the stone. The soldiers had come on the twelfth of November — after the church bells, he said, after everyone had rung them — and said the orders had changed. There was a list of men who had been in a certain company, and the list was wrong, or the men on it were, and the officer who came in the motor car was not the captain of the company but someone above him, someone the company officers stood very straight for.

"He had a red tab," Sparrow said. "On his cap. Not red like blood. Red like the postbox. I saw it when he got out of the car and I saw it again when—" His voice snagged. Bess, in the front bench, made a low sound like a dog settling.

"When what, Thomas?"

"When he came back at night. To see it burn. He stood by the well with his hands behind his back. Same as in the picture." Sparrow pointed, suddenly certain, suddenly loud, at the wall where his drawings hung in the evidence exhibit — the same burning building, drawn forty times, the same figure by the well. "That's him. That's the man."

"Can you identify him now? Look carefully. Take your time."

The provost marshal brought forward a board of photographs. Sparrow slid off the chair's edge until only his fingertips gripped it, walked to the board on legs that shook and did not stop, and studied the faces the way he had once studied the sea from the clinic window — patiently, completely, with the whole of himself.

He touched the third photograph.

"Him. The red tab. He told the captain, 'Leave none who can tell it.'" Sparrow's hand stayed on the photograph. "I could tell it. That's why I ran."

The silence afterward lasted four full seconds — Emrys counted them, the way she counted a pulse. In those seconds the general read the name printed beneath the photograph, and said it aloud for the record, and the stenographer typed it, and somewhere beyond the grey drapes a clerk was already reaching for a telephone that would carry the name to London before the chamber emptied.

Brigadier Sir Edmund Vance. Hale's superior. The red tab.

* * *

Hale cross-examined the boy for eleven minutes, and it was the longest eleven minutes of Emrys's life.

He did not raise his voice. He asked about distances, and light, and whether an eight-year-old could truly distinguish one staff officer's insignia from another's in the dark. He asked whether the nice lady at the clinic had helped him practice his answers. Sparrow answered each question with a terrible literal honesty — *yes, ma'am helped me learn big words, but she never told me which man it was; I knew him before I knew her* — and with each answer Hale's questions grew thinner, and the tribunal's patience grew visibly shorter, until the general himself said, "Captain, the witness has answered," and Hale sat down.

But as Sparrow stepped down, Hale rose one last time, not to question but to address the bench, and his voice carried the flat calm of a man reading his own sentence.

"I would remind the tribunal that Brigadier Vance is a decorated officer with thirty years' service, currently commanding in the field, and that the testimony placing him at Carreg Ddu comes from a child who cannot write his own surname." A pause. "Whoever moves against General Vance should understand what such a motion costs — the careers it ends, the government it embarrasses, the allies it unsettles. This inquiry may find that the price of one village's truth is more than the peace of Europe can pay."

He gathered his folder, saluted the bench with parade precision, and walked out through the side door without looking at any of them.

The general adjourned the sitting until the following day. As the benches emptied, Aldous reached the table where Emrys sat with her hands still folded over the ledger, and she saw that his face had gone the color of paper.

"He wasn't threatening the tribunal," Aldous said quietly. "He was warning us. Vance won't wait for a verdict. Men like that act — tonight, before the record reaches London. And there's a copy of these proceedings going north on the evening mail train, in the custody of two corporals and a locked dispatch case." He looked at the door Hale had gone through, then at Emrys, and she watched the old correspondent's hunger and guilt fuse into something new and cold and clear. "If that case doesn't reach the War Office intact, everything your boy said today becomes a rumor told by a smuggler, a drunk, and a woman with a notebook."

Bess appeared at his shoulder, pulling on her gloves finger by finger, her eyes already on the clock above the empty bench.

"Evening mail leaves at six," she said. "It's half past four. Somebody had better decide, right quick, whether we trust the army to deliver its own truth — or whether this family's got one more job in it."

Emrys closed the ledger. Outside, through a gap in the grey drapes, she could see the first lamps coming on along the quay, and the dark water beyond them, waiting.
