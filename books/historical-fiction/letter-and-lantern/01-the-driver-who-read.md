## 1. The Driver Who Read

The kettle in the mews kitchen had a voice like a woman clearing her throat before saying something unpleasant, and Bram Vane had learned to listen for it the way other people listened for church bells. It meant three minutes. Three minutes was what a letter needed — not two, which left the gum stubborn and the paper dry enough to tear at the fold, not four, which curled the pages like autumn leaves and gave the whole game away to anyone with eyes. Three minutes exactly, and then the letter came apart under her fingers as sweetly as a secret told in the dark.

She sat at the deal table with her sleeves pushed back, her driving gloves still on the hook by the door where she'd flung them twenty minutes earlier, and watched the envelope turn soft and translucent in the steam rising from the spout. Outside, October rain came down on Holloway Road with the patience of something that intended to go on forever. The mews smelled of petrol and wet horse from the livery next door, of coal smoke and boiled starch, and underneath it all — though only she could smell it, she was sure — the faint ghost of Edith Marlowe's cologne, which clung to the car seats and to the folded rugs Miss Marlowe kept for her knees and would not wash because washing took the memory out of them, she said.

Bram held the envelope over the steam with a pair of fish tongs, which was undignified but effective, and thought, as she thought every time, that this was not really eavesdropping.

That was the word she'd built for it over eight months, brick by careful brick: *not really*. A woman who drove for Edith Marlowe read the newspapers; that went without saying. And a woman who read the newspapers already knew most of what these letters contained, or near enough. Mrs. Pankhurst's health. The date of a meeting in Croydon. Complaints about a treasurer in Leeds whose arithmetic was as crooked as his politics. The letters were duller than the newspapers, mostly. That was the astonishing thing, the thing that had surprised her in the first weeks and still surprised her now: history, up close, arrived mostly on cheap stationery, in handwriting that slanted left, full of quarrels about train timetables and who owed whom four shillings for printing.

History was something that happened to other people, and Bram Vane was the person who carried their luggage.

The gum released. She set down the tongs, wiped her hands on her apron out of habit though they were dry, and slid the single page free.

*Dear Miss Marlowe* — everyone wrote *dear*, even the ones who despised her — *I write in haste as Frederick may come back early from his club. I have enclosed the five pounds you asked, and I beg you will not use my name again at any public meeting. My husband reads the papers now. He is not a cruel man but he is a proud one, and pride is worse.*

Bram read it twice. Five pounds. A husband who read the papers. Somewhere in the suburbs of London a woman had walked to a pillar box in the rain to post this, looking over her shoulder, and here was Bram holding the whole of her courage in two hands over a kitchen table while the kettle sighed itself quiet.

She read it a third time, because there was nothing else to do with it, and because reading it felt like being trusted, which was absurd, since the trust ran entirely in the wrong direction. Then she laid the page face-down on the clean towel by the stove to dry, and sat back, and looked out the little window at the grey brick wall opposite, and performed the small daily ceremony of telling herself who she was.

She was a driver. She was good at it — better than good; she could feather a cold engine into life on a February morning and parallel the Daimler into a gap on Marylebone High Street that other men wouldn't attempt with a bicycle. She was discreet — that was why Miss Marlowe kept her, that and the fact that a woman driver drew half the attention of a man, and sometimes less than half, because people's eyes simply declined to register her. She was an observer. That was the word. An observer stood at the edge of events the way a photograph stands outside its frame, and what she saw belonged to her, privately, harmlessly, the way weather belongs to anyone standing in it.

She was not a participant. Participants got their names in the papers under unflattering headlines. Participants went through windows at Downing Street and came out on stretchers, or went to prison and came out older, or — Bram's mind touched this and moved off it quickly, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth — participants were the women Miss Marlowe spoke about in the low steady voice she used for casualties. Bram kept her hands on the wheel. That was her contribution. The car arrived; the car departed; no one remembered the car, which was precisely the point of a car.

In the corner by the flour tin lay the week's accumulation: nine envelopes, opened and resealed, waiting to go up to the house on the tray with the rest of the morning mail. Nine letters, none of which would ever know what she had done to them. She liked to think of them that way — innocent, restored, carrying their secrets home intact. It made the whole enterprise feel almost kind.

The page on the towel had dried to a faint ripple, invisible unless you knew. Bram turned it over, breathed on the gum — the heat of the breath woke it just enough — pressed the flap down with her thumb, running it slowly along the seam the way Miss Marlowe ran her thumb along the spine of a new book. Then she slid the letter back into its envelope, squared the corners, and added it to the pile.

Nine. She counted them again, though she'd counted them last night. Nine, and one more expected today — she'd seen the handwriting on the morning delivery, a heavy black italic hand she'd learned meant money or trouble, usually both.

Upstairs, floorboards creaked. Miss Marlowe was awake.

* * *

Edith Marlowe came down to breakfast at seven-forty every morning regardless of what the day held, because she said a woman who let the day arrange her schedule had already surrendered to it, and surrender was a habit like any other. Bram heard her before she saw her: the particular rhythm of her walk, brisk on the heel, and the sound of her speaking to Nellie the house girl in the corridor, asking after Nellie's mother, remembering the mother's name, which was more than Bram had managed in eight months and she wasn't proud of it.

Miss Marlowe appeared in the kitchen doorway in her grey day dress with the collar that made her look, Bram always thought, like a portrait of someone's formidable aunt, and stopped.

"You've been at the kettle."

"The car wants warming anyway. Thought I'd make tea while it did."

"You've been at the kettle for twenty minutes." Miss Marlowe crossed to the range and held her hands over it, though it had gone nearly cold. "And the range is stone cold, so whatever you were doing, you weren't cooking." She said it mildly, almost absently, the way she said everything that mattered, and Bram felt the familiar cold slide down between her shoulder blades and reminded herself that Miss Marlowe said things like that to everyone, that it was a manner, a style of command, that it meant nothing.

"Post's come," Bram said, nodding at the tray. "I sorted it."

"I can see you sorted it. You've alphabetized a man named Ogilvie ahead of a countess." Miss Marlowe lifted the top letter — the black italic hand, arrived early, delivered by a boy running through the rain — and slit it with the bread knife in one motion, and Bram watched the blade go through and thought, not for the first time, that there was a whole education in watching how Edith Marlowe opened things. No hesitation. No ceremony. Whatever was inside, she met it head-on.

Bram filled the teapot and did not look at the letter, and looked at it sideways the entire time.

"It's the Manchester people," Miss Marlowe said at last, folding the page away. "They want me Friday week. There's a meeting — never mind what. Can you drive me to Euston for the ten past nine?"

"Any day you like."

"Don't say that." Miss Marlowe took the cup Bram poured and drank it standing, which scandalized the china, which was probably why she did it. "Never say *any day you like* to anyone, Miss Vane. Days are not all alike and neither are you. Say *Thursday*, and mean Thursday."

"Thursday, then."

"That's better." Miss Marlowe set down the cup and looked at her properly then, the way she occasionally did — a long level look, blue-grey eyes, the gaze of a woman who had been shouted at by cabinet ministers and heckled in eleven cities and had found out exactly what her own face could do. Bram held it. Eight months of practice. If her pulse moved, she hoped it didn't show above her collar.

"You were at the window when I came down," Miss Marlowe said. "Watching the road."

"Watching the weather."

"Mm." And then, mercifully, she turned for the door. "Half past eight. The Daimler, not the little car — I want to arrive looking respectable, it unsettles them far more than arriving looking dangerous." At the door she paused, one hand on the frame. "There's a parcel coming today. A book, from a bookseller in Charing Cross Road. When it comes, bring it straight up. Don't leave it in the hall with the others."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Miss Vane—" She didn't turn around. Her reflection hung faintly in the dark kitchen window, superimposed on the rain. "You're a good driver. I want you to know I know it. Whatever else happens in the next few months — whatever you see, or think you see — remember that I know it."

Then she was gone up the stairs, and Bram stood in the cold kitchen with the teapot going bitter, and heard the sentence repeat itself in her head with a slow ominous music, *whatever you see, or think you see,* and decided, firmly, in the way she decided things, that it meant nothing at all.

* * *

The morning run took them from Holloway to Westminster and back by way of everything Bram loved about London, which was to say everything she could watch from behind a windscreen. Rain silvered the streets; the Daimler's tyres hissed; omnibuses shouldered past with their upper decks streaming, and clerks ran bent double under umbrellas, and a flower seller under the arches at Charing Cross had rigged an oilcloth awning and stood beneath it serene as a duchess, selling wet violets to wet men.

Miss Marlowe worked in the back seat. Bram could hear the scratch of her pen over the engine, and once, when they halted for a dray at the Holborn crossing, she heard her murmur numbers to herself — figures, sums, the movement of money — and once, low and fierce, a fragment: "*...and he can tell Halloran the answer is no, and he can put it in writing.*"

Halloran. Bram filed the name where she filed everything: Undersecretary something-or-other, Home Office, photographed in the illustrated papers at garden parties, a smooth fair man with the smile of a shop window. One of the faces of the government that the movement existed to annoy. She had driven Miss Marlowe past his department twice and both times Miss Marlowe had looked up at the windows with an expression Bram had no name for and hadn't tried to find one.

At Whitehall a policeman waved them down for no reason Bram could see except the reason policemen had, which was to be seen waving. Miss Marlowe lowered the window an inch.

"Good morning, officer. Is there a difficulty?"

"No difficulty, madam." His eyes went over the car, over the driver — paused on the driver, half a second, the flicker of surprise that Bram had stopped finding flattering years ago and now merely noted, like a dent — and returned to Miss Marlowe. "Routine."

"Of course it is." She raised the window. To Bram: "Drive on."

Bram drove on. In the mirror she watched the policeman watch them go, and thought about routine, and about how many kinds of it there were, and how the dangerous kind always introduced itself as the harmless kind.

* * *

The afternoon brought two meetings, a deputation, and a woman who wept in the back seat for eleven minutes without making a sound, which Bram judged afterward to be the hardest thing she had seen anyone do that year. Miss Marlowe sat beside her and held her hand and said nothing at all, and when the woman got out at King's Cross she stood a moment in the rain with her chin up, and Miss Marlowe watched until the crowd took her.

"She lost a son at Tonypandy," Miss Marlowe said, when the door was shut. "A soldier's boot did it. The inquiry called it regrettable."

Bram said nothing. There was nothing in the manual of driving for replies to that.

"They'll call our dead regrettable too, one day, if it comes to it. They'll call us mad first, then criminal, then regrettable. It's a very orderly process, Miss Vane. The English do grief the way they do everything else — by appointment." She settled back against the cushions. "Home, please. By way of the post office in Camden Road. There's a letter to go tonight and I don't trust it to the pillar box outside the house."

Bram drove. At Camden Road Miss Marlowe went in herself and posted the letter and came out pulling her gloves tight, finger by finger, and Bram watched in the mirror and thought: *that one won't be mine.* Some letters Miss Marlowe guarded like that, hand-delivered, never trusted to the general post — and those were the ones, Bram had noticed, that mattered. The rest, the ordinary traffic of a famous woman's correspondence, came and went through the house in envelopes, and a certain number of those envelopes passed through a kitchen in the mews where a kettle had a voice like a disapproving woman.

She was not proud of it. She had made her peace with not being proud of it. That was the arrangement she'd come to, somewhere around midsummer, sitting at the same table with the same fish tongs: she would not pretend it was right, and she would not stop, because stopping would mean giving up the one room in the great house of events where she was allowed to stand. Out there, in the streets and the meeting halls, history happened to other people — to Miss Marlowe, to the women with banners, to ministers and policemen and the weeping mothers of dead miners. In here, over the steam, history passed through Bram Vane's hands, and for eight minutes at a time, it was hers.

It was a small ownership. She knew that. But she'd had smaller.

* * *

The parcel came at dusk, as parcels do — carried up the area steps by a boy soaked to the collar, wrapped in brown paper and string, stamped Charing Cross Road. Bram signed for it, weighed it in her hand — heavier than a book ought to be, or maybe not, maybe her sense of books had gone soft — and carried it through to the hall.

And stopped.

The hall was empty. The house was quiet; Miss Marlowe was dressing for dinner, Nellie laying the dining room, the maid-of-all-work gone to her sister's. Nobody was watching. The parcel sat in Bram's hands with its string tied in a flat knot, and the instruction rang in her ears — *bring it straight up, don't leave it with the others* — and beneath the instruction, patient as the rain, sat the other thing, the habit, the kettle waiting downstairs with its throat cleared.

She stood in the hall for perhaps forty seconds. Later she would take the parcel upstairs and knock and hand it over and say nothing, and that would be the end of it, one more evening in a year of evenings. But in those forty seconds she stood on the checkerboard tiles with the parcel growing warm in her grip, and she examined herself with the cold thoroughness she usually saved for other people's letters, and she asked the question she never allowed at the table: *what am I actually doing?*

Reading. Only reading. Watching the water rise toward the bridge, noting the traffic on it, touching none of it. A driver drives. A reader reads. Neither of them makes anything happen.

The clock in the hall ticked through the silence. Upstairs a door closed. Downstairs the kitchen waited, and the kettle waited inside the kitchen, and the evening waited inside the day like a letter inside an envelope, sealed, addressed to somebody, its contents unknown.

Bram carried the parcel up and knocked, and heard Miss Marlowe's voice say *come*, and went in, and handed it over, and said nothing, and told herself — comfortably, thoroughly, with the ease of long practice — that she was exactly who she believed she was.

It was the last week she would be able to.

Because when she went back down, the boy was still at the area gate, huddled out of the rain, holding a second parcel he'd forgotten he was carrying — no, not a parcel, a bundle of cloth — and behind him, at the mouth of the mews, a hansom cab had drawn up with its lamps lit though the dark was barely worth lighting yet, and a man was getting out of it slowly, the way men get out of cars in photographs of accidents.

"Miss," the boy said, shivering. "There's a kid here. Been standing at your front door since before I come. Won't speak. Won't move. Just holds this bundle and stares at the bell."

Bram looked past him. At the foot of the front steps, small and soaked and utterly still, stood a child with a bundle clutched to her chest, staring up at the door of Edith Marlowe's house as if it were the last lit window in England.

And pinned to the child's coat, flapping in the rain, was a note.
