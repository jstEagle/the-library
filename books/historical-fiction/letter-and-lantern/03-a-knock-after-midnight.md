## 3. A Knock After Midnight

The rain had been coming down since nine o'clock in a fine, needling sort of way that got under collars and into the bones of the house, and by half past midnight I had given up on sleep entirely. The attic room Mrs. Quill let me over the garage was warm enough — the engine's heat lingered in the bricks below like a sleeping animal — but warmth and rest were different animals altogether, and my mind kept circling the day's letters the way a dog circles its own tail.

I had read four of them before supper. That was the habit now, three months old, and it had settled into me so comfortably that I no longer bothered to feel ashamed of it. Miss Marlowe — Edith, though never to her face — wrote her correspondence at the writing desk in the morning room, and she sealed each envelope with a lick of gum and a press of her thumb, and then she left them in the brass tray on the hall table for me to take to the pillar box on the corner come evening. And every evening, without fail, I took those letters up to my room, set the kettle on the spirit stove, and held each envelope over the spout until the gum surrendered.

I told myself it was diligence. A driver ought to know where she is taking her mistress; a driver ought to know which names are dangerous names. If a letter mentioned a meeting at Caxton Hall or a deputation to Downing Street, wasn't it better that I knew, so that I might watch the pavements for men in bowler hats who stood too long in doorways? That was the story I told myself, and it was a good story, well-made, with a moral stitched neatly down one side. It did not mention that I read the letters about her sister's rheumatism, or the ones from the printer about pamphlet orders, or the long affectionate scold from her aunt in Cheltenham. It did not mention that I had begun, somewhere in those three months, to know Edith Marlowe better than anyone alive knew her — better, perhaps, than anyone had ever known her — and that this knowledge felt less like theft than like inheritance. As though I were being left something in a will whose terms I hadn't read.

That night there had been a fifth letter, and the fifth letter was the reason sleep would not come.

It was addressed to a name I didn't know: *Mr. T. Ashworth, care of the Manchester Guardian.* She had written it slowly, I could tell from the hand — not her usual quick slashing script but something more deliberate, more careful, the hand of a woman counting her words as she spent them. I had steamed it open with particular attention, expecting strategy, and found instead something almost like fear. Only two lines.

*What we discussed has arrived early and uninvited. I will write again when I know what it wants. Burn this.*

No signature. No date beyond the postmark she'd add herself. I had sat with it for a long while, the paper trembling faintly in the draught from the window — or perhaps the trembling was mine — and then I had resealed it with the thin glue stick I kept for exactly this purpose and set it back atop the others, and gone down through the rain to the pillar box, and posted it along with the rest, and stood there a moment in the wet dark listening to the letter land among all the other letters of England.

*Arrived early and uninvited.* Arrived where? What had been discussed? She had said nothing at dinner — dinner had been the usual affair, cold mutton, an argument about whether Millicent Fawcett was a saint or merely a very competent administrator, Edith taking neither side with her usual delight in taking both. Nothing in her manner. But then, nothing ever showed in her manner unless she wished it shown, and I had learned that the hard way, watching her smile at policemen who had just broken a friend's wrist.

So: awake at half past midnight, rain on the slates, the fifth letter turning over and over in my head like a coin I couldn't stop flipping. Below me, the house breathed. Edith slept in the second-floor front room — I knew because I had carried her trunk up those stairs often enough to know which door was hers, and because once, coming down late, I had seen the candle gutter behind the frosted glass and heard her voice low and even, reading aloud to herself, though there was no one to read to. She read aloud when she was troubled. I had catalogued that fact with the rest of my stolen inventory and filed it away in the place where I kept everything about her, a place I did not examine too closely by daylight.

At twenty minutes to one, I heard the knock.

*

You will think me fanciful, and perhaps I am, but I swear the knock woke me before I fully registered having heard it — that is, my body knew before my mind did, sitting bolt upright in the dark with my heart already going. It was not a loud knock. That was the strange thing. Three soft raps, spaced carefully, the knock of someone who does not wish to be heard but cannot bring themselves simply to leave. A guilty knock. An apologetic knock. Nothing like the brisk double-rap of Edith's colleagues or the hammering the police used when they used the knocker at all rather than the boot.

I dressed in the dark — skirt, blouse, boots laced crooked, corrected — and went down the back stairs with the candle unlit, feeling my way by memory. The rain had thickened. Through the kitchen window the yard was a black shine, the Daimler a darker shape beneath its tarpaulin, and the whole street beyond the gate seemed emptied of the world, as though the rain had washed everyone else out of London and left only us.

Then I saw that the gate was open.

It hung a few inches off its latch, swinging gently, and beside the front steps stood a figure so small that for one absurd moment I thought of a garden statue, one of the stone children you see on the lawns of better houses than ours. Then the figure moved, hunched against the rain, and I understood: a child. A girl, seven years old perhaps, eight at most, in a coat too big for her and no hat at all, her hair plastered flat to her skull. She stood absolutely still, facing the door, as though she had been placed there like a parcel and instructed to remain until collected.

And there was no one else. I looked up and down the street twice. Nothing. The lamplight made a weak yellow island at the corner and the rain fell through it in silver threads, and beyond that, darkness, and no footsteps going away, no cab rattling off, no door closing anywhere. Whoever had brought her had done it silently and was already gone, and the manner of that going frightened me far more than the child did.

I opened the door. The girl flinched — a full-body flinch, shoulders to heels, the kind of flinch that has been trained into a person rather than born there — and then went still again.

"You'll want to come inside," I said. I kept my voice low, the voice I used with horses and engines and other things that spook easily. "You're soaked through."

She didn't answer. She looked up at me, and the lamplight from the hall caught her face, and I remember thinking, with a strange detached clarity, that hers was not a pretty child's face nor an ugly one but a *watchful* one — a face built for seeing and not being seen. Her eyes moved past me into the hall, cataloguing it: the coat hooks, the umbrella stand, the stairs. Taking inventory. A soldier's eyes in a child's head.

"I'm not going to touch you," I said. "But you can't stand out there."

Nothing. Rain dripped off the end of her nose. She was shivering in a continuous fine tremor, the way a wire trembles.

Somewhere above us a floorboard creaked, and the girl's whole body locked — locked, the way a rabbit locks, the way I have seen a man lock in a courtroom awaiting verdicts. Whatever she was afraid of, she was afraid of it finding her here, at this door, tonight. And whatever it was, it had been worth carrying her across London in the rain and leaving her without a word.

I stepped aside and held the door wide. After a moment — after some private calculation I would have given much to see — she came inside.

*

I will not pretend I handled it well. I had never been alone with a child in my life; I had driven for Edith Marlowe eleven months and before that for a wine merchant whose children were at school in Switzerland, and my entire repertoire of childcare consisted of offering food. So I sat her in the kitchen chair nearest the stove, wrapped her in the blanket Mrs. Quill kept for the cat, put bread and dripping in front of her, and watched her not eat it.

Not-eating turned out to be eloquent. She looked at the bread the way one looks at a letter containing bad news — wanting to know, not wanting to know. Her hands stayed in her lap, both of them, curled around something. I had noticed it at the door: she carried her right arm oddly, pressed close against her ribs, and now, in the stove light, I could see why. Clutched against her side, gripped so tightly her knuckles had gone white, was a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string. It was maybe eight inches square. It was dry. Everything else about her was streaming wet, hair, coat, boots leaving little dark prints on the flagstones, but the packet was bone-dry, and it had clearly been kept dry with great care, sheltered under her coat, against her body, all the way here.

Someone had trusted an eight-year-old with a thing that mattered more than her own warmth.

"Nell," I said gently. It was a guess. There was a tag inside her collar — I'd seen it when she came in, a laundry tag, ink faded — and the letters *NEL* were still legible. "Is your name Nell?"

Her eyes came up to mine. For the first time they fixed on me properly, and I saw that they were grey, and enormous, and ringed underneath with the bruised shadows of a child who has not slept in some time. She nodded. One small, precise nod, as if confirming a delivery.

"Who brought you here, Nell?"

A shake of the head. Quick, hard.

"Did they say anything? When they left you?"

Another shake, harder, and her arms tightened around the oilcloth packet until I feared the string would cut her fingers, and I understood that I had asked the wrong question, that perhaps any question was the wrong question, and that whatever had been said to her — or done — had gone into her somewhere deeper than words could reach and taken the words with it. She was not refusing to speak. I began to suspect she *could not*, or believed she could not, in some way I had no tools to understand. I was twenty-six years old and I had read perhaps four hundred of another woman's letters and I thought myself knowledgeable about the human heart, and I sat there in that kitchen utterly unequipped, watching a mute child shiver by my stove.

The kettle was still warm. I made tea with too much milk and too much sugar, the way one makes it for shock, and this time she drank. Small sips, eyes always moving — the door, the window, the dark square of the yard. Watching for whoever had brought her. Or whoever might follow.

It must have been ten minutes past one when the light came on in the hall.

I heard her before I saw her — the particular unhurried tread on the stair, bare feet on wood, Edith never wore shoes to bed — and I was on my feet with my body angled between the kitchen doorway and the hall before I had decided to be, some instinct posting itself like a sentry. The girl went rigid. Her hands flew to the packet.

Edith appeared in the doorway in her dressing gown, grey wool, candle in hand, and I watched her take in the scene in a single sweep: the girl, the blanket, the untouched bread, the oilcloth bundle, my face. I watched her arrive at conclusions faster than I had managed in half an hour of trying. That was the thing about Edith Marlowe that people who read about her in the papers never understood — the papers had made her a figure, a torch, a slogan, a woman who chained herself to railings in photographs, and none of it captured the speed of her mind, the cold clean economy of it, running underneath all the fire.

"Well," she said quietly. And then, to the girl, kneeling down so that their eyes were level, in a voice I had never heard her use — softer, slower, stripped of all its theatre: "You're safe now. Nobody in this house will hurt you. My name is Edith. This is Bram. Can you tell me yours?"

Silence. But the rigid line of the small shoulders eased by a degree. Something in the child had weighed Edith — the same inventory-taking look, coat hooks and umbrella stands — and rendered a verdict.

"She won't speak," I said. "She's been here since before one. Someone left her at the gate and walked away — I heard nothing, saw nothing, and the gate was swinging. She wouldn't eat. She won't let go of—" 

"The packet." Edith's eyes had not left it. "Yes. I can see the packet, Bram."

There was something in her tone then that stopped me. Not impatience. Recognition. She looked at that dry square of oilcloth the way I imagined a doctor looks at a wound he has seen before on other bodies.

"Nell," Edith said. "Nell, did someone give you that bundle to carry? And tell you to keep it dry?"

The smallest nod.

"And did they give you something else? A note? A message for whoever answered the door?"

The girl's chin lifted. Slowly, with her left hand — her right never loosening its grip on the packet — she reached into the breast of her oversized coat and drew out a folded paper, sodden at the corners, and held it out to Edith with a formal little gesture, a courier completing a commission.

Edith took it. Unfolded it. I leaned in despite myself, shamelessly, and read it upside-down in the candlelight, in handwriting I did not recognize, hurried and ill-formed:

**Forget the face.**

Nothing else. No signature, no address, no explanation. Two words, and a child delivered to our door in the rain at one o'clock in the morning, holding a dry packet like it was the last living thing in the world.

I looked at Edith. I expected questions, anger, the swift mobilization I knew her capable of — telegrams, telephone calls, the machine of her will engaging. Instead I saw something I had never seen on her face in eleven months of watching it across a driving seat and a breakfast table and a hundred committee rooms.

I saw that she was afraid.

She hid it instantly — smoothed it away like a crease in cloth — but I had seen it, and having seen it I could not unsee it, and the kitchen suddenly felt colder than the street outside. Because Edith Marlowe did not frighten, and if a wet scrap of paper with two words on it could frighten her, then the packet in that child's arms contained something that had already changed the shape of all our lives, whether we knew it yet or not.

Edith crouched again before the girl. "Nell," she said, very gently. "We're going to keep your bundle safe tonight. Nobody will touch it. You'll sleep here by the fire, and Bram will sit with you. Will you let us do that?"

A pause. Then the girl looked at me — directly, deliberately, transferring something — and loosened her grip by a single finger, and held the packet out toward Edith.

Edith took it with both hands, the way one takes a sleeping infant, or a live shell.

And upstairs, later, when the girl was asleep wrapped in the cat's blanket and the fire had been banked and Edith had sent me to bed with a look that permitted no argument, I lay in the dark listening to the rain and the small sounds of a child breathing one floor below, and I thought about the fifth letter. *Arrived early and uninvited. I will write again when I know what it wants.*

It wanted nothing, I understood now. It had arrived.

And in the morning, when the light came, Edith Marlowe would untie that string — and whatever the oilcloth held, there would be no more ordinary days for any of us.
