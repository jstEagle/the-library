## 4. What the Packet Held

Edith Marlowe had learned long ago that the truth arrived the way weather did—without permission, without apology, and usually in the small hours when a body was least prepared to meet it.

She sat at her writing desk with the oilcloth packet before her, and for a full minute she did not touch it. The house was quiet around her. Bram had taken the child upstairs an hour past—the girl had finally gone down into sleep in the guest room, still clutching that wretched packet as though it were the last warm thing in England—and now the only sounds were the tick of the mantel clock and the wind working at the loose sash in the parlour, which she had asked Mr. Petch twice to mend and he had twice promised to mend and had never mended.

The lamp threw a yellow circle on the desk. Outside that circle the room went grey and shapeless, and Edith found she was glad of it. She wanted nothing tonight but this circle of light and what lay inside it.

*Forget the face.*

Whoever had written those four words had written them in haste—a blunt carpenter's hand, pencil rather than ink, the letters pressed so hard into the paper that she could feel the grooves of them under her thumb. Someone had wanted the child delivered and forgotten in the same motion. Someone had wanted no name attached, no trail, no thread that could be pulled back toward whoever had set the girl on Edith's doorstep at midnight like a parcel of dynamite.

And yet they had brought her here. That was the part Edith kept turning over, the way one turns a stone to see what shelters underneath. There were a dozen houses between wherever this child had come from and Holloway Road. Any of them would have served if the object had been merely to be rid of her. But someone—someone who knew exactly what Edith Marlowe was and what she would do—had carried the girl through dark streets to *this* door.

Which meant the packet was not an accident. It was a delivery. And deliveries implied senders, and senders implied that somewhere in this city there was a person who trusted Edith Marlowe more than they trusted their own chances of staying alive.

She untied the string.

The oilcloth crackled as it opened—oiled canvas, good quality, army surplus by the smell of it, folded twice and tied with waxed twine in a knot a sailor would have approved of. Inside there were three things: a bundle of photographs held together with a paper clip gone rusty; a slim ledger bound in grey cardboard, the kind stationers sold by the dozen to shopkeepers and clerks; and, tucked beneath both, a man's railway ticket, third class, Crewe to Manchester, punched but unused.

Edith looked at the ticket for a long moment.

Then she put on the spectacles she pretended she did not need, tilted the lamp closer, and began.

* * *

There were eleven photographs.

The first six were of a man. He appeared to have been taken unawares in every one of them—coming out of a doorway, crossing a street, standing at a barrow buying apples. He was perhaps thirty-five, thin, with a weak chin and quick eyes that even in a candid photograph seemed always to be measuring the distance to the nearest exit. On the back of each print, in the same carpenter's pencil, was a date and a place. *W. Tice, Chancery Lane, 3rd Sept.* *W. Tice, Fetter St., 11th Sept.* *W. Tice, outside the Gaiety, 19th Sept.* Six photographs spanning three weeks: a man being followed, though he plainly did not know it.

The seventh photograph was different. It was not candid. It was posed—a group of five men arranged on the steps of some government building, the sort of photograph taken for a departmental record or a newspaper society column. Four of the men wore the bland, well-fed faces of men who had never in their lives been cold or hungry or afraid. Edith's eye passed over them and snagged, as she had known it would, on the fifth.

Undersecretary Halloran stood slightly apart from the others, half a step down, his hands folded in front of him, wearing the modest smile of a man content to be second-in-command forever. She had met him once, two years ago, at some interminable garden party where the Home Office sent its junior ministers to be photographed among the roses while their betters talked money on the terrace. He had complimented her on her "energy" in the tone of a man complimenting a horse. She remembered thinking him forgettable.

He was not forgettable anymore. In the eighth photograph Halloran's face appeared again—this time cropped close, enlarged, grainy—and behind his shoulder, blurred but unmistakable, stood the thin man with the weak chin. W. Tice. Standing where a private secretary might stand. Standing where a man stands when he belongs to somebody.

The ninth and tenth photographs Edith had to look at twice, because her mind refused them at first.

They were of an alley. A service alley behind some warehouse district, wet cobbles, a loading door standing open, the whole scene lit harsh and flat by whatever light the camera's flash powder had thrown. In the first, a figure lay crumpled against the wall halfway down the alley—a man in a dark coat, one arm flung out, and beside him, scattered across the cobbles, a leather satchel with its strap snapped clean through. In the second, taken from nearer—much nearer—the same man from above, and now Edith could see what she had refused to see: the angle of the head against the wall, the dark spread beneath him, the satchel lying open and empty an arm's length from his fingers, which were curled as though they had tried to crawl toward it.

On the back of the ninth photograph, the pencil again: *T. Wrenn, courier, 14th Oct., Rivington St.*

Tom Wrenn. She knew the name. Of course she knew the name—he had carried for the movement off and on for two years, a quiet Manchester man with a wife in Ancoats and a way of arriving precisely when he said he would arrive, which in their trade made him rarer than rubies. He had carried documents between London and Manchester a dozen times without incident, riding third class with his satchel chained to his wrist, unremarkable, unhurried, safe.

*Fourteenth of October.* Three days ago. Three days ago Tom Wrenn had left London with a satchel, and three days ago Tom Wrenn had died in an alley in Shoreditch with the strap cut through, and nobody had told her because nobody knew—or nobody who knew had survived long enough to tell.

Except, apparently, one child.

Edith set the photographs down very carefully, aligning their edges, because her hands wanted to shake and she would not permit it.

* * *

The ledger was worse. The ledger was infinitely worse, because the ledger was orderly.

It was a payments book, that much was clear from its construction—columns ruled for dates, amounts, purposes, initials. But whoever had kept it had used it for a purpose its stationer never intended. The entries ran backwards through October and September, in a neat commercial hand, and each line recorded a sum paid and a purpose rendered, and the initials in the final column were the same three letters over and over: **H.M.**, with occasional countersignatures marked **W.T.**

Edith read by lamplight with the patience of a woman who had spent fifteen years reading hostile newspapers about herself, and the picture assembled itself line by line like a negative developing in acid.

*2nd Sept.—£15—printing, 40,000 leaflets—"Suffrage Agitators Plan Bombs," text approved H.M.*
*9th Sept.—£8—purchase, assorted chemicals & clockwork, via W.T., receipt destroyed*
*21st Sept.—£25—hiring, two men, "demonstration" scheduled 30th, premises secured*
*1st Oct.—£10—press placement, three provincial papers, advance copy of "militant outrage" story*

Page after page. A bombing purchased and scheduled. Leaflets printed in advance announcing the suffragists' guilt. Newspaper stories drafted before the bomb existed, bought and waiting like coffins. And threaded through it all, in entries that grew more frequent as the autumn deepened, a single recurring item:

*Courier intercepted—see W.T.*
*W.T. reports courier route confirmed—weekly, Thursdays, London–Manchester, 3rd class*
*W.T.—final payment re: courier matter—£40—matter closed*

*Matter closed.* As though Tom Wrenn had been an invoice. As though a man with a wife in Ancoats could be settled at forty pounds, receipt destroyed.

Edith took off her spectacles and pressed the bridge of her nose until the ache there became something she could think through instead of merely feel.

She understood it now—all of it, the whole architecture of the thing—with the terrible clarity that sometimes arrives too late to be useful and must therefore be made useful quickly. Halloran's people had not merely planned to frame the movement for a bombing. They had planned the *story* first and the explosion second, which was how such men always worked; the newspapermen would have their headlines ready-set before the smoke cleared, the Home Secretary would stand up in the Commons with manufactured evidence already in his dispatch box, and by Christmas the franchise question would be buried under a national convulsion about sedition and murder, with every militant in the country jailed and the movement's respectable allies scattering like startled birds.

And Tom Wrenn had died because he had been carrying something worth intercepting. Something Halloran's men had needed badly enough to kill for. Something that had been in that satchel, chained to his wrist, on the fourteenth of October—

—and was not in the satchel now, because the satchel in the photograph was empty, and the child on her doorstep had arrived clutching an oilcloth packet as though God Himself had handed it to her.

Edith looked at the eleventh photograph, the one she had saved for last without quite admitting she was saving it.

It was not of a man or an alley. It was of a document—a page photographed flat on a table, edges sharp, every line legible. It was a letter, typed on House of Commons stationery, signed in ink, and the signature was Halloran's. It instructed the recipient—Illingworth, Private Secretary to the Home Secretary—to hold certain materials "in readiness" against a date in November, and it referred, in language of perfect bureaucratic blandness, to "the theatrical element of the operation" and to the necessity that "no paper trail survive beyond the inner circle."

Someone had photographed it. Someone inside the inner circle, or close enough to touch it, had laid this letter on a table under a camera and committed treason against their master. And then they had wrapped it, with the rest, in oilcloth, and given it to a little girl, and told her to run.

*Forget the face,* the note said. But the face was the least of it. What the note really meant was *forget you ever saw us, forget the room, forget the voice, because if you are caught with this you will not be ransomed or reasoned with—you will simply cease, the way Tom Wrenn ceased.*

Edith rose and walked to the window. The street below lay blue and empty under the gaslight, a milk cart two roads off, nothing else moving. Somewhere out there was a person who had done the bravest and most dangerous thing she could imagine, and they had chosen her—chosen Edith Marlowe, whose name was already mud in three newspapers, whose arrest would surprise no one—as the one soul in London they believed would not fail.

She would not fail. That was not resolve speaking; it was simple fact, the way gravity was fact.

But knowing *what* she carried and knowing *how to move it* were different animals entirely, and the second one was already beginning to snarl.

* * *

She worked until nearly four.

First the ledger, copied out entire in her own hand onto loose sheets, in a cipher of her own devising that she had not used since the old days of dodging the police spies in Croydon—numbers for names, positions for words, nonsense to any eye but hers. The original would travel or it would not, but the copy would exist regardless, hidden where not even Inspector Crake's breed of bloodhound would think to sniff for it. She burned nothing. Burning was what frightened people did, and frightened people destroyed the very proof that would later save them.

Then the photographs, studied again one by one. The sixth photograph of Tice—the last chronologically, dated the thirteenth of October, the day before Wrenn died—showed him crossing a street toward a warehouse door, and Edith held it under the lamp until she had memorized the doorway, the signage above it, the position of the drainpipe. If she lived long enough, that address would matter.

Then she sat with a sheet of plain paper and drew the map of the problem, because Edith Marlowe had won battles before by refusing to let them remain feelings, and a feeling was precisely what this was threatening to become.

The evidence had to reach Manchester by Friday. Not should—*had*. She wrote the word and underlined it twice. On Friday morning the *Manchester Dispatch* went to press with whatever its editor had in hand, and the Dispatch's editor, Joseph Callaghan, was the one man in English journalism who possessed both the nerve to print Halloran's letter verbatim and the circulation to make it unstoppable. A story like this lived or died by its first printing. Printed Friday, it would be in a hundred thousand breakfast rooms before Halloran's office finished its Saturday sherry; printed late, printed leaked, printed in fragments, it would die in committee the way such stories always died, passed from editor to lawyer to proprietor to grave.

Friday, then. Today was Tuesday. Which gave her—she counted, and the counting felt like walking out onto ice that held, so far—three days, minus the hours already bleeding away toward dawn.

Three days to move a packet that at least five men would kill to intercept, from a house that might already be watched, along a route she did not yet trust, to a city she could not enter openly, past police checkpoints she could not yet name.

Because there would be checkpoints. She knew Halloran's methods, or she knew enough of them now to predict the shape of his fear. A man who had built a conspiracy this elaborate would not leave the roads open. He would assume the evidence would run for Manchester—Wrenn's route, the obvious route, the route the ledger itself had mapped in cold columns—and he would have set his watchers along it accordingly. Rail stations. Road junctions. The ordinary choke-points of ordinary travel, thickened with men holding descriptions and orders.

Very well. Then the route would have to be found, tested, and beaten, and it would have to be beaten by someone the checkpoints were not looking for—which, she understood with a dull settling in her chest, ruled herself out, and ruled out everyone whose face the newspapers had already sold to the nation.

That thought led where she did not want it to lead, so she set it down on the paper instead, where thoughts could be managed: *Who carries?*

Not herself. Not Petch—too known in Manchester, too old for what was coming. Not any of the women in the inner circle; Halloran's list of faces would include every one of them within a day of the story breaking.

Her pen hovered.

Below the stairs slept a child who had seen a murder, and upstairs in the attic room, in a bed that smelled of engine oil and cheap tobacco because Bram Vane never opened a window, slept her driver—who read every letter Edith posted, believing herself invisible, believing that history was a theatre one watched from the wings, and who had no idea that she had been cast in it months ago.

Edith had known about the letters since spring. She had said nothing, because Bram's furtive, careful, guilty attentiveness had struck her as the most honest thing in the household—the hunger of a woman starving for a life she didn't believe she was allowed to want. One did not punish appetite. One waited for it to become commitment.

She looked at the map, and at the ledger, and at the photograph of Tom Wrenn lying in his own blood on wet cobbles, and she thought: *the driver reads everything; perhaps it is time she carried something.*

No. Too soon. There were other options to price first, other routes to weigh, and besides—she was not yet certain the house was not already ringed. Decisions made in the dark hours were worth exactly their weight in regret.

She folded her ciphered copy into the false bottom of her writing case, returned the originals to the oilcloth, and retied the knot. Then she took the unused railway ticket from the packet—Crewe to Manchester, third class, Tom Wrenn's own ticket, bought for a journey he had never completed—and she held it a moment in her palm.

It was a strange thing to keep. But Edith Marlowe believed in keeping strange things. Somewhere in this business, she suspected, the ticket would tell her something: about the route, about the timing, about the mind of the person who had slipped it into the packet like a signature, or an instruction, or a promise—*he was going where you must go; finish what he started.*

She put it in her breast pocket, over her heart, where she kept the things she meant to remember when remembering became expensive.

The clock said ten past four. In the yard below, first light was still hours off, grey and grudging. Upstairs, two people slept—one who had seen too much, and one who had seen too little and believed that about herself, and both of them, Edith thought, were going to learn things before Friday that could not afterward be unlearned.

She pinched out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, letting her eyes adjust, listening to the house breathe.

Three days. Two rail inspections and whatever the roads held, if her instincts about Halloran were half right—and her instincts, God help her, were rarely less than half right. Evidence that could topple a minister or hang everyone who touched it. And somewhere between here and Manchester, a man named Crake or Crake's equivalent, patient and well-paid, already turning over stones.

Edith Marlowe climbed the stairs past the sleeping child's door and stopped, briefly, at the attic landing, where a strip of lamplight—no, not lamplight; a match-flare, quickly smothered—had just flickered under Bram Vane's door.

So. Not asleep after all.

Edith stood in the darkness of the corridor and smiled without warmth, and made a decision she would neither explain nor apologize for: tomorrow, she would give the driver something real to read.
