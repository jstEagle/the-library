## 5. The Face She Was Told to Forget

Morning came grey through the curtains of the spare room, and Bram Vane stood in the doorway with a cup of milk warming her palm and watched a child sleep the way soldiers sleep — lightly, with her fists curled near her face, ready to run even in dreams.

Nell. That was all they knew. The note pinned to the coat last night had said nothing else, only those five words in a clerk's careful hand: *Forget the face.* No name for the girl. No address for whoever had left her on the Marlowes' step at half past midnight, his boots still audible on the gravel for three strides before the dark took him.

Bram had not slept. She had sat downstairs in the study while Edith read the oilcloth packet by lamplight, and she had heard Edith stop breathing twice, and when the lamp finally went out Edith had said only, "Go up and sit with the child. I have letters to write." Her voice had been the one she used on platforms — level, pitched to carry over jeering — and that frightened Bram more than anything in the packet could have, because she hadn't seen the packet, and Edith's calm was never real calm but a thing built plank by plank under pressure.

Now the child stirred. Her eyes opened — brown, enormous in a small pale face — and fixed on Bram with the flat, evaluating stare of an animal deciding whether the thing in the doorway was weather or wolf.

"I've brought you milk," Bram said. She kept her voice low, unhurried, the voice she used with the motor when it shied at a hill. "There's bread and dripping too, if you want it. And marmalade, though I warn you it's Mrs. Petch's own and it would strip paint."

The child didn't answer. She didn't take the milk either. She sat up against the headboard and looked past Bram, at the window, at the door, cataloguing exits. Seven years old, perhaps eight. Small enough that her feet didn't reach the end of the bed. The coat hung over the chair where Edith had put it last night, a man's coat cut down badly, the sleeves let out with mismatched thread, and Bram found herself looking at it and thinking: someone loved her once with more care than money.

"You're safe here," Bram said. It came out too easily, the way lies come out when you haven't decided yet whether they're lies.

The child looked at her then — really looked, a long slow inventory from Bram's cropped hair to her driving gloves still tucked in her belt — and something in the small face shifted, some weighing done, some verdict reached. She took the milk. She drank half of it in four swallows and set the rest on the sill with the gravity of a general rationing supplies.

"Thank you," she said. Her voice was rusty, like a gate opened after a season.

"What's your name?"

A pause. "Nell."

"Nell what?"

The pause this time went on so long Bram thought the gate had rusted shut again. Then: "Nell," the child said again, firmer, and Bram understood that it wasn't refusal. It was everything the child had left, and she was counting it.

* * *

Downstairs, Edith had gone out — Bram found the note propped against the teapot, three lines in that upright slashing hand, saying only that she'd be back by two and that Bram should on no account leave the house until she returned. On the kitchen table lay the oilcloth packet, open, its contents squared into a neat row as though tidiness could make them less terrible.

Bram told herself she wouldn't look. She was aware, distantly, of how often she told herself things — that she was only reading the letters because history passed through this house and someone ought to know what it said; that she only drove because driving was useful and thinking was dangerous; that she stood apart, always, at the edge of other people's courage, taking notes like a war correspondent who never filed.

She looked.

Photographs first. A man face-down on cobbles, one arm flung out, a spill beneath him that the camera's grain turned almost black. A bicycle, wheel still turning — no, she corrected herself, the shutter had caught it mid-fall, but the mind insisted on the turning, insisted on the courier having been alive seconds before, pedalling somewhere with a leather satchel full of paper. Another photograph: the satchel itself, empty, lying open like a gutted bird. A third showed men — faces angled away or hatted, except one, a heavy jaw and small eyes caught full-on as he glanced toward the lens, and Bram felt her stomach drop through the floor, because she knew that face, had driven past it on a poster outside a ministry office, had heard Edith say its owner's name once in a tone of purest contempt: Halloran's chief man, the one they called Crake.

And under the photographs, a ledger. Columns of figures. Dates. Initials. Payments — she could read enough of it to understand payments — running back eighteen months, tied to entries marked only with a word repeated down the margin in the same hand throughout: *Courier. Courier. Courier.* As if the man on the cobbles had never been a man at all, only a line item, a recurring cost.

Bram set the ledger down with hands that were not quite steady and made herself breathe. Then she picked up the photographs again and forced herself to do the thing she was best at, the thing she did instead of feeling: she observed.

The cobbles were wet. The shadows ran long and eastward, so evening, so autumn or early spring. There was a wall behind the body with a painted sign, mostly out of frame — she could make out *—LEY'S* and below it the curve of a letter that might be a G. A shop. Somewhere specific. Someone had taken these photographs within minutes of the killing, close enough that the blood was still spreading, which meant the photographer had been there before the killing ended or arrived immediately after, which meant —

Which meant whoever took these had wanted proof. Had stood over a dying man and chosen the angle.

She put the packet back exactly as she'd found it, squared corners, folded oilcloth, and went upstairs to the child, because there was nowhere else in the world her feet wanted less to go and nowhere else they could go at all.

* * *

The morning passed in small negotiations. Bread and dripping achieved. Marmalade rejected with a grimace that was the closest thing to a smile Bram had yet seen. Nell spoke maybe twenty words in three hours, and Bram hoarded each one like coins.

It was the coat that broke the silence.

Bram had hung it nearer the fire to dry — the child had been out in drizzle when they found her, the shoulders dark with damp — and Nell watched it swing on its hook with a tension that Bram felt before she understood. When Bram reached for it, the child was off the bed in one motion, quick as a sparrow, and had the sleeve in both fists.

"It's only drying," Bram said. "I won't —"

"He gave it to me." The words came out fast and fierce. "Da gave it me. Cutted down himself. He said —" and then the voice stopped, snagged on something, and the child stood gripping the sleeve with her knuckles white, looking at Bram with an expression that was not fear anymore but grief wearing fear's clothes, and Bram knelt down on the cold linoleum without deciding to, the way you kneel in church.

"He said it would keep the rain off till spring," Nell finished, very quietly. "Till he got paid."

"That sounds like someone who took care of you," Bram said.

Nell nodded. Then, in the same breath, in the flat reciting voice children use for things learned by rote: "He said if ever anyone asked, forget the face. He wrote it down so I'd remember to forget it. He said remembering was the dangerous thing." Her brow furrowed. "That's backwards, isn't it?"

Bram's mouth had gone dry. "Is it," she managed. "What face, Nell?"

But the gate had closed. The child shook her head, climbed back onto the bed, and pulled the blanket to her chin, and for an hour she answered nothing at all, only watched Bram with those brown eyes that held, Bram now understood, a picture worse than any photograph on Edith's table.

* * *

In the afternoon, while the light went thin and gold across the garden, Bram sat on the floor of the spare room with paper and the good pencils she kept for route maps, ostensibly drawing, and let the child drift near. This was a trick she'd learned from her youngest brother decades ago: don't ask children questions, give them tools, and their hands will tell you what their mouths won't.

Nell drew a house. A crooked chimney. A dog, possibly, or a bear.

Then, without any change in her breathing, without looking up, she drew a circle. Inside the circle she drew another shape, slower now, the pencil pressing harder, each stroke deliberate — a bird, wings spread wide, talons out. Beneath it, a ribbon, and on the ribbon squiggles that meant words she couldn't yet write.

Bram's blood went cold so gradually it felt like frost crossing a window.

She knew the crest. Every literate soul in England knew it, stamped in gold on the spines of parliamentary reports, embossed on official envelopes that arrived at this house bearing warrants and refusals in equal measure. The eagle displayed, the motto ribbon. The arms of a government office. Of a department. Of, at the top of that particular pyramid, an Undersecretary whose name Edith had spoken with contempt and whose chief man's face Bram had just that morning seen glancing toward a camera over a dead man's bleeding head.

"That's a fine crest," Bram said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, calm, driver-calm, engine-calm. "Where did you see it?"

"On a door," Nell said, still drawing, shading the eagle's wing with tight little strokes. "Big doors. Green wood and gold knobs. Da knocked and a man opened it and Da went in and I waited on the step with the bike."

"When was that?"

"Daytime. Before." The pencil paused. "After we came out it was getting dark. We went down an alley because Da said quicker that way, and there was a wall with — with LEY'S on it, and then the lanterns came round the corner."

The pencil stopped.

Bram did not move. She was aware of her own heartbeat, absurdly loud, aware of the fire ticking, aware that she was holding her breath like a woman underwater. *Lanterns,* the child had said. Lantern light. Her father's last words — Edith had said the packet implied testimony, and here it was, sitting cross-legged on a bed drawing eagles, seven years old and carrying the whole rotten truth in her head like shrapnel.

"Nell." Very gently. "You can tell me about the lanterns whenever you want. Not now. Whenever. But can you tell me one thing?" She waited until the brown eyes lifted. "The men with the lanterns. Did they see you?"

The child considered the question with a seriousness that broke Bram's heart along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"They saw my shoe," she said. "It stuck out from the crates. The big man with the little eyes looked right at it. Right at it for ages. And then Da's friend shouted from up the street and they all ran off, and Da —"

She stopped. Her lower lip performed the smallest possible tremor and was mastered, with an effort that no child should ever have to make, and Bram understood suddenly and completely that this child had already decided not to cry, had spent whatever remained of last night learning not to cry, and that the note — *forget the face* — had not been cruelty but a father's last, desperate field-dressing for a wound he couldn't stay to bind.

"The big man with the little eyes," Bram said. "Did he pick up your shoe? Did he look at it and keep it?"

"No. He put it back with his boot. Careful-like." Nell frowned. "Like he was putting evidence back."

Bram laughed — one short, awful bark of a laugh that she strangled instantly — and gathered the child against her shoulder, and Nell allowed it, stiff at first, then not stiff, her small fist closing in Bram's collar the way it must have closed in her father's.

*Evidence.* The word a child had learned from listening at doors. The word that meant her shoe could hang her. The word that meant a man with little eyes had stood over a murdered courier, noted a witness the size of a shoe, and filed it away with the same tidy precision he'd bring, sooner or later, to finding her.

*Forget the face.* But the face was drawn on a sheet of paper on Bram's knee, wings spread, talons out. The face lived in a child who remembered everything because her father had ordered her to forget, and children, bless them and damn them, obeyed the wrong instructions.

* * *

Edith came back at ten past two, letting herself in quietly, and Bram met her in the hall with the drawing held in both hands like something rescued from a fire.

Edith read it in four seconds. Bram watched the information land in her — watched it the way you watch weight settle onto a bridge, beam by beam, waiting for the groan. Edith Marlowe's face did not collapse. It did the opposite: it assembled, feature by feature, into the platform face, the barricade face, and only her eyes stayed human, and only for a moment, and only because it was Bram looking.

"She saw it," Edith said. Not a question.

"She saw it, and she can draw it, and she remembers a green door and gold knobs and a wall with LEY'S on it, and a man with little eyes who knows a shoe means a witness." Bram heard her own voice shaking now that the day's work of steadiness was done with her. "Edith — the note said forget the face. Whoever left her here knew what she carries. They didn't kill her because killing a child makes papers, but they'll come back for her the moment it's convenient. And the man in the photograph — Crake — he doesn't file things away. He files them *for later*."

"Yes." Edith took off her gloves finger by finger, unhurried, and Bram recognized the ritual: armor going on. "Then we have a choice to make, and not much time to make it in. Because that packet isn't only photographs, Bram. It's dates and names and sums, and if it reaches the right hands by Friday, Halloran's beautiful little frame collapses before he can build it. And if it doesn't reach them —" She folded the gloves together, once, precisely. "If it doesn't, then a courier stays a line item, a child stays a loose end, and in three weeks' time the papers print a story about a suffragist bomb, and every woman in this country who has ever raised her voice gets to find out what happens next."

Outside, a cart rattled past on the wet road. Nell's voice drifted down the stairs, singing something tuneless and half-remembered to herself, the sound of a survivor making herself ordinary.

"Friday," Bram said. "How far is Friday's hands?"

"Manchester," said Edith, and began, calmly, terribly, to unpin her hat.
