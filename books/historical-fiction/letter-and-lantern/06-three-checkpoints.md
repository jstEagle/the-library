## 6. Three Checkpoints

The atlas lay open on the drawing room floor like a wounded bird, its spine cracked, London spilling northward in a wash of faded red and brown. Edith Marlowe knelt over it in her stocking feet, hair still damp from the basin, because the house was not yet awake and she had learned long ago that the best decisions were made before the world arrived to argue with them.

She had been at it since four o'clock. The candles had burned down to stubs. Three of them stood on the mantelpiece like a jury that had heard everything and would say nothing.

On the carpet beside her, arranged with the precision she brought to everything, were the contents of the oilcloth packet: the photographs, curling slightly at their edges now that they had been freed of their wrapping; the ledger, its columns of figures marching down the pages like soldiers who did not know they were deserting; and the note that had come with the child — *Forget the face* — which she kept separate from the rest, weighted under the brass letter-opener, because it was evidence of a different kind. Evidence of intent. Whoever had written those three words had believed there was something left alive to do the forgetting.

Edith did not intend to forget anything. It was, she reflected, rather the opposite of her life's work.

She took up her pencil and drew a line north out of London. Euston first. Then Crewe. Then the road over the Pennines into Manchester, and somewhere past Stockport, where the hills closed in and the road narrowed and any sensible man building a net would tie off his final knot.

Three places. She was nearly certain it was three. She had spent yesterday evening confirming it, and confirmation had cost her two telephone calls she could ill afford and one favour she would spend years repaying, but certainty in these matters was worth more than comfort. A woman who guessed at checkpoints died at them. A woman who *knew* — well. She might die anyway. But she would die having chosen the ground.

Euston. She wrote the word small and hard, pressing until the pencil point snapped against the paper, and sharpened it again with the little knife she kept for the purpose.

* * *

By seven the kettle had been on and off twice, and Bram Vane appeared in the doorway with the particular careful blankness she wore in the mornings, as though the day were a horse that might startle if approached too quickly.

"Miss Marlowe. You're on the floor."

"I am aware of it."

"There's tea."

"Put it down where I can reach it and don't step on Cheshire." Edith tapped the county without looking up. "Or Lancashire. Or any of them. They've suffered enough this century."

Bram came into the room the way she always did — quietly, though she was a large presence in a room and made no real attempt otherwise; it was simply that she moved like someone accustomed to being the last thing noticed. She set the tea down, looked at the spread of papers, the photographs, the atlas, and Edith watched her take it in: the quick flick of the eyes, left to right, cataloguing, and then the deliberate withdrawal, the shutter coming down behind which Bram Vane stored whatever she saw.

"You've been reading my mail for months," Edith said mildly, not looking up. "You may as well look at this too."

The silence that followed had texture. Edith counted it out — one, two, three seconds of absolute stillness, the kind of silence a person produces when they are deciding between denial and dignity — and then Bram said, "I don't know what you mean," which was, Edith noted with some private satisfaction, neither one nor the other, but a third thing entirely: a door held very slightly ajar.

"Mm." Edith turned the atlas toward her. "Sit down, Miss Vane. I need your eyes and I need your memory, and I haven't time to pretend I don't know about either."

Bram sat. She sat the way she drove — hands loose, attention total. Edith pushed the ledger across the carpet.

"The courier's name was Albert Fenwick. He worked for a printer in Fetter Lane who does our leaflets when he can afford the risk. On the ninth of last month Fenwick carried a parcel north by train — nothing dramatic, accounts and correspondence, the ordinary sediment of an organisation the government has decided to treat as a foreign army. He never arrived. His parcel never arrived. Until Tuesday, when it walked up my front steps wearing a child."

Bram's gaze went, involuntarily, to the ceiling — upward, toward the room where Nell slept. Edith filed that away too. Everything went into the file. That was what forty years of fighting had taught her: that history was not made in parliament or in prison cells but in files, kept faithfully, consulted ruthlessly.

"Fenwick is dead," Edith continued. "Thrown from a train near Wilmslow, or so the coroner will eventually be told. The parcel contained photographs and this ledger, and together they show that men paid out of Undersecretary Halloran's private office have been planning to blow up a building of their own choosing and hang the smoking wreckage round our necks. There are names in here. Dates. Sums of money written in a hand I'd know anywhere because it belongs to a man who once prosecuted three of my girls and enjoyed himself thoroughly doing it."

"And you want it to reach Manchester," Bram said. "Friday's edition."

"I want it to reach Joseph Renwick by Friday morning, yes. Renwick prints what the London papers won't touch. If this appears in the *Manchester Dispatch* on Friday, Halloran's story dies in the womb. If it doesn't—" She let the sentence go unfinished, because the end of it was already sitting in both their minds, wearing its little painted face. A bomb. A dead constable, perhaps, or only broken glass and terror, and then the headlines, and then the Act extended, and then the feeding of women into Holloway until the movement choked on its own martyrs.

Bram picked up the ledger. Her thumb moved along the columns. "How does it travel?"

"That," said Edith, "is the interesting question."

* * *

She stood, joints protesting — she was fifty-one years old and the floor took payment for every hour it was lent her — and fetched the second document, the one she had compiled overnight from memory, from the telephone calls, from a map of England's nervous system drawn in pencil on the back of a laundry list.

"Look here." She laid it flat. "A parcel travels badly now. Since the Fenwick business, Halloran's people will have every parcels office between here and Manchester turned inside out — not officially, you understand. Officially there is no Fenwick business. Unofficially, there are men at every sorting desk who have been shown a description and promised a bonus. So the packet goes as luggage. Luggage means a passenger, and a passenger means inspections, and inspections are where we must be clever."

Her pencil touched Euston.

"One. Euston itself. Every through train to Manchester is watched — not by the railway police, who are honest enough and merely stupid, but by two men in plain clothes who stand at the barrier on busy mornings and ask pleasant questions of anyone travelling alone with heavy bags. The tall one is called Sergeant Dunn and he is genuinely a sergeant, on secondment, which is how they launder respectability. The short one has no name I can learn. He carries a clipboard and writes nothing on it, which tells me everything: the clipboard is theatre. The real record is his memory. He has been posted at Euston since the spring. He was posted there, I am quite sure, because the spring is when this conspiracy was hatched."

Bram studied the page. "And the second?"

"Crewe." The pencil moved north. "Everyone changes at Crewe — or did, until the new carriage workings; half the northern traffic still funnels through it, and it is the natural place to catch anything moving between systems. There is a station inspector there named Prosser. Prosser drinks. Prosser also, since February, has stopped drinking in the public bar and started drinking in the station hotel, where his bill is settled monthly by an account that traces back — I traced it Tuesday night, through a friend at a firm of chartered accountants who owes me more than he wishes he did — to a solicitor's clerk in Westminster who acts for none other than Mr. Halloran's private office." She smiled without warmth. "The Empire runs on such clerks. One day I shall write a pamphlet about them and be sued, and it will be wonderful publicity."

"You're certain of Prosser?"

"Certain enough to plan around him and not certain enough to relax. That is the correct amount of certain. Certainty is a luxury for people who have never been imprisoned."

Edith crouched again over the atlas, ignoring her knees, and drew the road. It was the third line that troubled her most, and she let the trouble show, because Bram needed to see that this was not a game with a guaranteed exit.

"Three. The Stockport road. Here—" the pencil pressed down— "where the A6 comes down off the moors through Hazel Grove, there is a level crossing and beside it, since the middle of September, a permanent police post. Permanent. In a village where nothing has required policing since a man stole a pig in 1908. It exists for one reason: to stop cars. Not carts, not coaches — the coachmen wave through, because coaches carry dozens and searching dozens is work. Cars. Motorcars are still rare enough that every one of them is remembered, described, and reported. And the sergeants who staff that post rotate weekly, but the rotation is signed off in Manchester by a Chief Constable whose brother-in-law holds the lease on a property in Kensington purchased, eighteen months ago, with money I could follow all the way back to the same solicitor's clerk if I cared to waste another week proving it."

She straightened. The candle stubs had gone out sometime during the speech; grey daylight stood at the windows, and the room looked suddenly less like a war council and more like what it was: a drawing room in a terraced house in Camden, littered with the wreckage of a nation's bad conscience.

"So," Edith said. "There it is. Two rail inspections and a road checkpoint, each one salted with Halloran's men, and between them a hundred miles of country full of ordinary policemen who are not corrupt but are obedient, which in practice amounts to the same thing when the orders come dressed as patriotism."

* * *

Bram said nothing for a long moment. She had picked up one of the photographs — Edith saw which one, and did not stop her: the exterior of the warehouse in Limehouse, unremarkable, a wall and a door and a shadowed window, the sort of image that meant nothing until you knew that eleven pounds of gelignite were intended for its cellar.

"If they're watching lone travellers with heavy bags," Bram said slowly, "then the answer isn't disguise. It's ordinariness. Send someone who looks like exactly what the inspectors expect to see and give them nothing to find."

"The hollow book."

"A book they'd expect a woman like you to carry." Bram glanced up, and there was the ghost of something in her face — humour, or its cousin. "You have a reputation for reading."

"I have a reputation for several things. Most of them slanderous, all of them earned." Edith permitted herself the smile, brief as a struck match, and then put it away. "Yes. A volume of essays, something improving and dull — Ruskin, perhaps; no inspector in England will search *Sesame and Lilies*. The ledger pages are thin. They'll fit. The photographs are another matter; paper photographs crackle, they shift, they have edges. The photographs may have to travel separately or not at all. I haven't decided. Deciding is what tonight is for."

"And the third checkpoint? A car can't hide in a book."

"No." Edith looked at the road line, at the crossing she'd marked with a small brutal X. "The third checkpoint is the one that worries me, and I'll tell you why. Railways are anonymous. A hundred faces pass a platform barrier in an hour and Dunn's clipboard man remembers perhaps five. But a car is a *character*. It has a number, a colour, a dented wing, a driver. If we send the packet by car through Hazel Grove, we send a biography of ourselves directly to the men hunting us. And if we send it by rail the whole way, we arrive in Manchester on foot with no way to move once we're there, and Renwick's office will be watched too — you may depend on it; a journalist who prints the truth is watched as a matter of course."

"So the car goes partway."

"The car goes partway, or the car goes nowhere and waits, or the car—" Edith stopped. She had been about to say *the car takes the risk*, and the sentence had a shape she didn't like, because shapes like that usually meant she was planning to fill it with someone else.

She looked at Bram instead, and found Bram looking at the map with an expression Edith recognised from rallies and courtrooms and the worst nights of her life: the expression of a person doing arithmetic in which the sum was themselves.

"It won't be decided this minute," Edith said, more gently than she had intended. "It will be decided by Friday, and not before it must be. That is the whole art of it, Miss Vane. Never decide early what you can decide late. Early decisions calcify. Late decisions stay liquid, and liquid finds the cracks."

"Yes, miss."

"Don't call me miss as though we're strangers. We are not strangers. We are co-conspirators, which is closer than family." Edith gathered the photographs back into their stack, squaring the edges, and paused with her hand on the topmost one. "There's one more thing you should know, and then you may escape to your engine and your gears, which I know you prefer to conversation."

Bram waited.

"Whoever carries this will be carrying it into a trap built for us specifically. Halloran knows the evidence exists — or fears it exists, which comes to the same thing in his arithmetic. He has had five days to position his pieces. Five days is enough. Which means the question is not whether the route is dangerous. I have established that to my satisfaction and yours. The question is *who he expects*, because the man who expects the famous Mrs. Marlowe will look straight past everyone else — and the man who has been watching my household will have seen exactly who drives my car."

She let that sit. Bram's face did not change, which was itself an answer of sorts.

"Think about it," Edith said. "Don't answer me. Answers given too quickly are worth what they cost."

* * *

Bram went out to the garage — Edith heard the doors, the familiar clank and settle of the crank, the cough of the engine finding itself — and Edith remained alone with her maps and her dead courier and her candle stubs.

She sat down at last in the good chair by the cold fireplace and allowed herself, for precisely sixty seconds by the mantel clock, to feel the weight of it. Fenwick dead in a cutting outside Wilmslow, his face forgotten on official instruction. A child who did not speak. A minister in Westminster who would dine tonight among friends and sleep soundly, because his conscience had been professionally removed, like tonsils, years ago. And somewhere between here and Manchester, three small knots in a long rope, each one tied by a man being paid to betray his uniform.

They thought they were setting a trap. Very well. Edith had been caught in traps before — she bore the scars of several, literal and otherwise — and she had learned the one great secret of them: a trap is only a trap if you walk into it believing it is a road. Walk in knowing, and it becomes terrain. Terrain can be fought on.

The clock finished its minute. She rose, rang the bell for Mrs. Pym and hot water, and began, methodically, to choose which book would die for the cause.

Outside, the motorcar pulled away from the kerb with Bram at the wheel, and Edith stood at the window and watched it go, and thought: *she reads my letters. What else does she read? And when will she admit she has been reading her own future?*

Then she sat down again, and drew the map closer, and began to plan for the possibility that every one of her three checkpoints was wrong — and that the true fourth waited somewhere she had not yet thought to look.
