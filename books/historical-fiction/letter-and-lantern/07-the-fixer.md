## 7. The Fixer

The rain had followed Inspector Ambrose Crake from London like a debt collector, and by the time he stepped down onto the platform at Crewe it had settled into a fine grey persistence that soaked through wool and made the whole of Cheshire look as though it were dissolving. He stood for a moment beneath the station clock — four minutes slow, he'd have to remember that, details mattered in this work — and watched the porters scurry with their trolleys while respectable people hurried into cabs and respectable women clutched their hats against the weather.

He did not hurry. Hurrying was for men who feared being seen.

Crake was forty-one years old, narrow through the shoulders and precise through the mind, with a face that witnesses later struggled to describe because there was nothing in it to hold on to. He had spent eleven years in the Metropolitan Police learning two lessons that his superiors had never quite mastered: first, that most crimes were not solved but *arranged*; second, that the men who arranged them required someone honest enough to be believed and flexible enough to be useful. Crake had made himself both. It had cost him certain friendships, a wife who'd tired of waiting up for a man who never brought his work home because his work never stayed anywhere long, and whatever portion of his soul is surrendered when a man stops asking whether a thing is right and asks only whether it will hold.

It would hold. That was all that mattered.

The Undersecretary's telegram had reached him Tuesday evening: *Courier lost. Item presumed recovered. One loose end, female, child or near-child. Find her before anyone else does.* The word *lost* doing its quiet, expensive work where a lesser man might have written *murdered*, and *recovered* standing in for stolen, and *loose end* meaning a girl of perhaps nine or ten years who had seen something she should not have seen on a dark lane outside Northampton.

Crake folded the telegram smaller and smaller until it was a hard pellet in his fist, then dropped it through a grate into the dark water below the platform. He had developed the habit early and never broken it. Paper could be found; paper could be read; paper was the death of careful men.

* * *

The dead courier had been called Albert Fenn, and he had been twenty-six years old, and for three days now he had been nobody at all.

That was the artistry of it, Crake thought, as he sat in the back parlour of the Wheatsheaf Inn in the village of Harpole with his notebook open and a pot of tea going cold beside him. A man dies on a road and within seventy-two hours the world has agreed to forget him. His lodging room emptied. His name struck from the books of the firm that employed him — a shipping concern in Euston whose ledgers showed no record of any Fenn whatsoever, though Crake knew for a fact the man had drawn wages there for fourteen months. No coroner's file. No police report from the local constabulary, which had been told by a telephone call from an unnamed Whitehall number that the matter was in hand. In hand. As though murder were a parcel.

The landlady of the Wheatsheaf, a Mrs. Tibbet, poured herself a small sherry uninvited and sat down across from him with the air of a woman preparing to enjoy herself. She was sixty-odd, sharp-faced, dressed in black that had been black so long it had gone brown at the seams.

"Albert," she said, before he'd asked anything. "You'll be wanting to know about Albert."

"You knew him?"

"Knew him? He lodged here two year." She sniffed. "Polite boy. Quiet. Carried parcels up and down the line and never said what was in them, which I never held against him, because a man who talks about his parcels is a man looking to lose his situation." Her eyes narrowed, and Crake watched the arithmetic happen behind them — the inspector, the questions, the sherry, the shape of the thing taking form. "He went out Thursday week with a case he wouldn't let me touch. Came back Friday in a box they wouldn't let me open. They said train accident. There weren't no train accident, Mr. Inspector, because I've lived by this line thirty years and I know what a train accident sounds like, and it don't sound like nothing, which is what Friday sounded like."

"What did they tell you exactly?"

"A Mr. Crane—" Crake's pen paused. "*Crane,*" she repeated, enjoying it, "from the Ministry. Very smooth gentleman, gloves, never took 'em off. Said Albert had been working private and private work has private ends. Gave me fifty pound for my trouble and told me to forget his face." She laughed, short and hard. "Fifty pound. Like he was buying back a photograph."

Crake wrote the name down. *Crane.* It would be an alias; it always was; but aliases were habits too, and habits left tracks. Halloran's office favoured names beginning with C — he had collected three already over the years, like pressed flowers: Callow, Carter, Crane. Somewhere in Whitehall there was a clerk who chose them, and one day Crake meant to find that clerk and shake his hand.

"And after?" he asked. "Did they ask anything else?"

Mrs. Tibbet looked at him a long moment, and when she answered, her voice had dropped as though the walls themselves were paid informants.

"They asked if Albert had family."

* * *

He had none. That much Crake confirmed by evening: no parents living, no siblings, no sweetheart whose grief might grow loud enough to need silencing. The Undersecretary's people had been thorough, and Crake noted the thoroughness without admiration. Thoroughness in that direction was merely competence. What interested him was thoroughness in the other direction — the direction of the girl.

Because there was a girl. He was sure of it now, though three days ago he had not been.

The certainty had arrived by increments, the way most truths did, each piece worthless alone. A milkman on the Northampton road who remembered a child at a gate at dusk, though he couldn't say whose. A signalman at Harpole Halt who'd mentioned — unprompted, in the way of men who mention things they've been told to forget — that the Friday mail cart had carried an unexpected passenger, "a little maid, no bigger than my Gladys, sat with her knees together like she was frightened of the seat." And then, today, the jeweller.

Crake sat across from the jeweller now, in the man's shop in Northampton, among trays of mourning brooches and lockets, and listened to him describe the customer who had come in on Saturday morning with a child.

"Wanted a locket," the jeweller said. He was a soft man, bald and pink, with the nervous hands of a tradesman who sensed that the conversation was not really about lockets. "For the girl. Said she'd lost someone. Paid in coin, which was unusual, but not unheard of, sir, not unheard of—"

"The child," Crake said quietly. "Describe the child."

"Well — small. Eight, ten years old. Dark hair, cut short, which struck me, because you don't often see it cut short on a little girl unless there's been fever. Grey eyes, I think, or blue-grey. Thin as a sparrow." He hesitated. "She didn't cry. That was the thing, sir. You expect a child in mourning to cry, and she just — stood. Watching everything. Like a little bird watching a cat through glass."

"Watching everything," Crake repeated. He wrote it down, though he would not need the notebook. "And did she speak?"

"Not once. The gentleman answered for her. Bought the locket, put it round her neck himself, very tender, and out they went." The jeweller wrung his hands. "Is — is something wrong, sir? Has the child come to harm?"

"No," Crake said, and was faintly surprised to find the word true as far as it went. Nothing had come to harm yet. Harm was a thing he administered on schedule, like doses. "The child is missing. She may have witnessed an accident. We're trying to return her to her people."

The lie settled over the shop like dust. The jeweller nodded, relieved, grateful, already composing the retelling for his supper table — *a real Scotland Yard man, in my shop, about a poor lost mite* — and Crake let him keep it. Lies were kindest when they made the liar feel important.

But walking back toward the station, collar up against the rain, he turned the description over and felt the cold pleasure of a pattern closing. Dark hair cut short — cut, perhaps, in haste, or to change her, or by someone who understood that a distinctive child was a dangerous child. Grey eyes. Sparrow-thin. Silent, watchful, unspeaking.

A witness. Not merely present at the lane outside Harpole on Thursday night — *attentive*. Children who screamed forgot; children who watched remembered. And somewhere between that lane and the locket, the girl had been passed along, hidden, moved — by whom? The mail cart was a thread. Threads led somewhere. Every hiding place was a house, every house had a door, and every door opened eventually for a man with a warrant or a man with patience, and Crake had learned to do without warrants.

* * *

In London the next morning, he reported to the Undersecretary's private secretary — never to Halloran directly; Crake was a tool, not a guest — in a room off Whitehall that smelled of beeswax and old carpet. The secretary, a colourless man named Petherton whose entire function was to stand between power and consequence, listened with his fingertips steepled.

"You believe she saw it."

"I believe she was close enough to see it, and that your Mr. Crane believed the same, or he wouldn't have bought her a locket and started asking after her family." Crake laid his notebook on the desk, open to the accumulated page. "The question is where she is now. She didn't go home — she hasn't got a home to go to, which is why the locket matters. Someone's keeping her. Someone with a conscience, or an interest, or both."

Petherton's fingers tightened slightly. "Consciences are expensive."

"Yes, sir. But they're also predictable. A conscience wants to give the child to someone safe — clergy, Quakers, some widow with a spare bed. Safe means documented. Documented means findable." He tapped the page once. "Give me a week and a free hand with the railway companies' passenger clerks, and I'll give you a house."

"There's a complication." Petherton rose and went to the window, hands behind his back, watching the rain silver the pavements below. "Marlowe."

Ah, Crake thought. *There* she was. Edith Marlowe had been circling this business from the edge since spring — speeches, subscriptions, a motorcar and a driver and more money than militant suffragism could decently account for. He had a file on her two inches thick, mostly newspaper clippings and the observations of men who watched houses for a living, and none of it proved anything except that she was serious, which in a woman of her class was itself a kind of crime.

"If Marlowe has the girl," Crake said slowly, "then she may also have what the girl saw. Or heard. Or carried."

"Precisely." Petherton turned. "Which is why the item must be considered compromised until proven otherwise, and why your task has broadened. Find the child. Find what she carries. And find it before—" he chose the word with care, "—before it finds a printing press."

Crake closed his notebook. "You understand, sir, that if it comes to searching premises connected to Mrs. Marlowe, I'll want it done properly. Warrant, witnesses, the whole apparatus. A famous name needs famous procedure, or the newspapers eat us alive."

"Procedure," Petherton said, with the ghost of a smile, "is being prepared. Your part is to make certain that when procedure arrives, it arrives *justified*. Give me evidence, Inspector. Evidence is the one thing in this country that cannot be bought back at fifty pounds a time."

* * *

That evening Crake walked from Whitehall to his lodgings in Pimlico because the fog was thick enough to make cabs dangerous and because walking helped him arrange his mind. Lamplighters were working their way up Vauxhall Bridge Road, each flame blooming yellow in the murk, and he found himself thinking — as he sometimes permitted himself to think, in the privacy of his own skull — about the jeweller's phrase. *Like a little bird watching a cat through glass.*

Somewhere in England tonight that bird was sitting in a lit room, breathing, remembering, holding God knew what against her flat chest with her thin arms. And somewhere else a woman named Edith Marlowe — clever, reckless, photographed in every illustrated weekly in the kingdom — was deciding whether to be a custodian or a courier.

Crake did not hate her. Hatred was inefficient. He understood her, which was worse for her. He understood that she believed history was a machine that ran on martyrs, and that she had been feeding herself into it for years with the serene appetite of a communicant at communion. Such women were not stopped by warnings or arrests; they metabolised punishment into publicity the way furnaces metabolised coal.

But everyone, Crake reflected, pausing under a lamp to light a cigarette, everyone had a thing they would not burn. For the married ones it was husbands; for mothers, children. He had built careers out of finding the thing. And if Edith Marlowe's weakness wore a grey-eyed child's face — well.

Then he would simply have to introduce them.

He flicked the match away and walked on into the fog, and behind him the lamp he'd stood under buzzed and steadied, keeping its small flame against all that darkness, for whoever cared to notice such things.

By morning he had wired ahead to Crewe, Stockport, and Manchester: *Watch all northbound traffic. Description follows. She travels small.*
