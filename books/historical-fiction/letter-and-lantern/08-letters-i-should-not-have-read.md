## 8. Letters I Should Not Have Read

The house did not sleep so much as hold its breath. I knew its rhythms the way I knew the gears of the Daimler — the settling of pipes after midnight, the creak of the third stair that Edith always skipped going up and never coming down. By half past one on the morning after the child arrived, even those sounds had stopped. Nell had been given the blue room, the one with the good lock, and Edith had sat outside her door on a straight-backed chair until the girl's breathing went long and even. Then Edith had gone to her study to read what the oilcloth held, and she had not come out.

I should have gone to bed. Instead I went down to the pantry.

It was not a dramatic decision. That was the thing I understood later, standing in the cold with a lamp turned low and the biscuit tin in my hands — that the worst acts are rarely dramatic at all. They are habits. They are a woman who cannot sleep reaching for the one thing that has ever made her feel less like furniture.

I had been steaming open Edith's letters for eleven months.

I told myself it began by accident, and in a way it had. The kettle trick was something my father used — he'd been a railway clerk with a suspicion of everyone, including his own mother — and when I first took the position as Edith's driver and general man-of-all-work, the letters came through me because I answered the door. One evening in February I'd set a sealed envelope near the stove while I warmed milk, and the gum had lifted like a sigh, and I'd seen the edge of Edith's handwriting inside before I could stop myself. *Dear Miss Garrick, the Manchester meeting must move to the twenty-third…*

I sealed it again with the flat of a spoon. No one ever knew. And no one knowing became its own argument: if there were no consequences, perhaps there was no sin. I was not reading to betray her. I told myself I was reading to protect her — that a driver who knew where her employer would be could keep the car gassed, the routes planned, the exits considered. There was enough truth in that to make the lie load-bearing.

So the habit grew. Letters from organizers in Leeds and Glasgow, from printers, from lawyers, from women who signed themselves only with initials because they knew better than most what interception meant. I read them all. I learned the movement's whole nervous system from a kitchen chair, and I called it devotion.

Now, with a sleeping child upstairs who had been left at our door with two words — *forget the face* — I carried the tin to the pantry table and began to take them out.

* * *

I had kept them longer than I admitted even to myself. Not copies; the originals went back into their envelopes, back into the post bag, but I had started keeping a list. A driver's notebook, I called it. Dates, names, places. In case anything happened to Edith, I told myself. In case someone needed to reconstruct her correspondence. It sounded noble when phrased that way. On paper, in my own hand, it looked exactly like what it was: a record of trespass.

I laid the envelopes out in order and untied the string on the notebook, and for the first time since February I read the letters not as a spectator reads a playbill but as a witness reads testimony.

The first one was dated the fourth of March. Miss Garrick in Leeds, about the printing schedule, and then a paragraph that had meant nothing to me in spring:

> You will tell Mr. Ashcombe that the northern route is not safe for parcels just now. There is a man asking questions at Crewe, and Tom says the same carriage has passed him twice between Stockport and the junction. Until we know whose man he is, the papers travel with people, not with post.

*The papers travel with people.* I remembered copying that phrase into the notebook and feeling a small thrill, the thrill of a girl reading a spy novel behind the counter of her father's shop. Now I read it again and felt the thrill curdle. Papers. People. A courier. A route through Crewe and Stockport — the exact geography, I now understood, of whatever had put a silent child on our step with a packet wrapped in oilcloth.

I pulled the next letter. April, from a Mrs. Ollerenshaw in Manchester, mostly about hall rents and the price of leaflets, and then, buried in the middle like a stone in bread:

> Tom Wrenn asks whether the gentleman from the ministry means to keep his appointment or whether we are to be stood up again. He was to bring the schedule of what is being stored at the depot. If he delays past Friday, Tom says he will go to the depot himself and see what can be seen. I have told him he is too well known. He laughs at me. You know how he laughs.

Tom Wrenn. I had written the name down in March without a second thought — *Wrenn, T., Manchester, courier?* — the question mark mine. Now the question mark burned. A courier who laughed at danger. A courier with an appointment with a gentleman from the ministry, a schedule of what was being stored at a depot. I sat very still in the cold pantry and let the arithmetic finish itself, because I could feel it wanting to finish, the way a train wants the end of the line.

A courier carrying proof of something. A ministry that delayed and delayed. A courier too impatient or too brave to wait. And somewhere along the northern route — Crewe, Stockport, the junction — a man asking questions, a carriage passing twice.

I had read all of this months ago. I had held every piece of it in my hands, warm from the kettle, and I had filed it under *color*, under *atmosphere*, under the pleasant fiction that I was a bystander collecting texture for no purpose at all. History was happening to other people. Other people named Tom Wrenn.

There was more. Of course there was more; there always is, once you agree to look. A letter in May from Edith's solicitor noting that a client "connected with a government office" had approached one of the provincial organizers offering money for information about "the movement's methods of transporting documents" — I had skimmed that word *transporting* without once wondering what documents, transported where, by whom. A note in June, unsigned, warning that someone in Whitehall was compiling a list of women who traveled north frequently and alone. My own name was nowhere in any of it. I had never once appeared in these letters, because I was the furniture. The car. The hands on the wheel. Eleven months of espionage conducted from inside the household and I had managed to learn everything except that I was in danger, because I had never believed I counted enough to be endangered.

That was the awakening, though it did not feel like waking at the time. It felt like drowning in a shallow room. Every envelope I opened was another door in a house I had lived in without seeing, and behind each door stood the same scene rearranged: men, a road, a courier who never arrived.

Because that was the phrase, wasn't it. I flipped forward through the notebook with fingers gone clumsy. July, the fourteenth. Edith's own hand to Miss Garrick:

> Have you heard anything further of Wrenn? He was due at the Anchor on Tuesday and has not come, nor sent word. His landlady says he took the night parcel train as usual. I dislike writing to you twice in one week about ghosts, but a man who carries what Tom carries does not simply fail to arrive.

*A man who carries what Tom carries.* I had copied that into the notebook too. I remembered pausing over it — I could even remember the afternoon, a hot one, flies against the pantry window, Edith humming downstairs while I held her private worry over a kettle spout — and I remembered deciding it was nothing. Organizers lost touch. Trains were late. Men were unreliable.

Tom Wrenn had failed to arrive on the fourteenth of July, and on some night close to it, somewhere on that same northern route, a courier had been killed in front of a child, and a minister's men had taken the proof off his body and thought they had taken all of it, and they had left a little girl alive only because killing children apparently still cost them something — or because they judged a child's eyes worthless. They were wrong about the eyes. But they had been right, God help them, about everything else. They had known the route because the route had been discussed in letters, and the letters had passed through a hallway in North London, through the hands of a woman who boiled a kettle and told herself she was nobody.

*Forget the face.* The note had been addressed to whoever found the child, but sitting there among my crimes I understood it differently. It was not only an instruction. It was a boast. We have done this thing, the note said, and the world will do the rest, because the world is excellent at forgetting faces it has been told to forget.

I had been forgetting faces for eleven months. Faces attached to names. Tom Wrenn, who laughed. Mrs. Ollerenshaw, who worried about him. The solicitor's anonymous client. The man at Crewe in the carriage that passed twice. I had read them all as scenery.

* * *

The lamp oil was low and I did not notice. I worked through the whole tin a second time, faster now, cross-referencing my notebook against the letters, and the pattern assembled itself with the terrible neatness of a timetable. Someone in the government — the gentleman from the ministry, the client who offered money — had wanted to know how the movement moved its documents. When flattery and payment produced only partial answers, someone had decided to answer the question directly: follow the couriers, intercept the parcels, and, when necessary, remove the couriers. And then — and here my blood went cold in a way the pantry chill hadn't managed — and then arrange for the removal to be blamed on the movement itself.

That last part I didn't know yet. I want to be honest about that. What I knew that night was narrower: a man was dead, a child had seen it, and the evidence of it had come to rest in Edith Marlowe's study one floor above my head. But the shape of the larger thing pressed against the edges of what I knew, the way a body presses against a sheet. Why leave the child alive within reach of witnesses? Why deliver her to a suffragist's door rather than anywhere else? Because a dead child raises questions and a living one can be discredited later; because a foundling in a famous radical's house is a gift to anyone building a case; because the men who killed Tom Wrenn were not finished, and the note — *forget the face* — was the first stroke of a much larger painting, and I was standing too close to see it.

Or perhaps I saw it and refused to name it. Both things are true. They were true all along, in fact; that is what I finally understood about myself in that pantry. I had not been blind. I had been practicing blindness, diligently, for years, and I had gotten good at it, and the practice had nearly gotten a child killed and had certainly gotten a laughing man from Manchester killed, because the route he died on had been described in letters that passed through my hands, and if I had ever once — once — taken my own reading seriously instead of treating it as entertainment, if I had said to Edith in May, *there is a man asking questions at Crewe, perhaps the couriers should stop*, then perhaps —

No. I checked the dates three times and made myself be fair, because guilt inflates itself if you let it. The letters gave warnings, but soft ones; no one, reading them honestly, could have predicted murder. That was the fair verdict. I rendered it and then I disregarded it, because fairness was not the point. The point was that I had spent a year telling myself history was a play performed elsewhere for an audience that included me only as staff, and the year had now presented its bill. Tom Wrenn had been real. The child upstairs was real. The oilcloth packet in the locked study was real, and somewhere south of it, in Whitehall, men with crests on their stationery were real, and none of them knew or cared that I existed — which was, I saw now, precisely the condition I had chosen for myself, and precisely the condition that made me useful to them. A conspiracy loves a person who believes herself invisible. She'll carry anything.

I put the letters back in their envelopes. My hands shook doing it, and I was ashamed of the shaking — ashamed that after everything, the part of me trembling was the part afraid of being caught, the servant's reflex, the fear of the mistress's displeasure, as if Edith's anger were the worst thing the world contained. I resealed each flap with the spoon and the lamp heat, and the wax-smell of the gum rose up familiar and foul, eleven months of evenings distilled into it.

Then I sat with the notebook open to a blank page and did the one honest thing available to me. I wrote down everything. Not for atmosphere this time. Wrenn, T., courier, Manchester, last heard from 14 July, failed to arrive at the Anchor. Route: Euston–Crewe–Stockport. Man asking questions at Crewe, March. Carriage passing twice, Stockport junction. Gentleman from the ministry, appointments missed. Offer of money for transport methods, May. List being compiled of women traveling north alone, June.

When I finished, the page looked like what it was: a map with one great hole in the middle. Everything around the edges of the killing was in my hand. Only the center — the men, the moment, the face the child could not be made to forget — was missing. And that center was walking around loose in a small head one floor above me, behind a door with a good lock, in a girl who had not spoken since the knock.

I climbed the stairs quietly, past Edith's study, where lamplight still burned in the gap beneath the door and I heard, faintly, the scratch of her pen — she was writing, at two in the morning, and I did not yet know what she had decided, only that her handwriting sounded different tonight, slower, pressing harder, as though she were carving rather than composing.

Outside Nell's door I stopped. I had no business there. I was the driver; the child was Edith's charge, Edith's problem, Edith's historical event. That was the old grammar and I felt it trying to reassert itself, the comfortable subjunctive of the bystander.

But I had read the letters. All of them. I knew now what the movement moved and why men died for it, and I knew that the missing piece of the map slept ten feet away from me, and I could not — I physically could not — walk past that door and go to my bed and resume being furniture.

I knocked, softly, once.

For a long moment there was nothing, and I almost retreated. Then, from inside, very small, a voice — rusty, frightened, but unmistakably a voice, the first words she had spoken in our house — said:

"Is he still following me?"

I stood in the dark corridor with my guilty notebook under my arm and understood that the question was not really directed at me. It was older than me. It had been waiting since the roadside, since the lantern-light, since her father's last words. But someone had to answer it, and Edith's pen was still scratching downstairs, and I was the one at the door.

"No," I said, and pushed the handle down. "No one is following you. Not tonight."

It was a promise. I would spend the rest of this story learning whether I could keep it.
