## 9. Nell Speaks

The child had been in the house for four days before she said a single word.

Bram kept count the way she kept count of everything — silently, obsessively, in a ledger no one would ever see. Four days of Nell sitting at the kitchen table with her hands folded like a small, obedient prisoner. Four days of untouched porridge until Edith coaxed it down spoonful by spoonful, pretending not to watch. Four days of that oilcloth packet carried everywhere — to the table, to the washstand, into bed — clutched against her chest even in sleep, so that Bram had taken to checking on the girl at midnight just to see the small shape of it rising and falling under the blanket.

On the fifth morning, it rained.

It was the sort of grey London rain that made the windows weep and the coal hiss, that turned Holloway Road into a river of umbrellas and omnibus wheels throwing up brown spray. Edith had gone out at seven — a committee meeting, then a printing appointment, then something she had not named and Bram had not asked about, because asking was a habit they had both agreed never to develop. The house belonged to Bram and Nell and the rain.

Bram was mending a tyre lever's worn grip by the stove when she heard it: a voice, thin as thread, coming from the parlour.

She went still. The grip rolled out of her hands onto the flagstones.

She moved to the doorway slowly, the way one approaches a bird that has landed on a windowsill — afraid that any noise at all would send it back into silence. Nell stood at the front window, on tiptoe, her chin barely clearing the sill, watching the water run down the glass. She was talking. Not to Bram, not to anyone. Talking to the rain.

"It come down like this," she was saying. "That night. It come down like this."

Bram's heart performed a slow, painful revolution.

"Nell," she said, very quietly.

The girl flinched as though struck. Her shoulders came up around her ears and she turned, and for a moment Bram saw the whole machinery of fear engage behind her eyes — the calculation every frightened creature makes, run or freeze, trust or bolt. Then something in the child's face gave way, like a knot slipping, and she crossed the room and pressed herself against Bram's legs with such sudden force that Bram staggered back a half-step and put both hands on the small shoulders to keep them both upright.

Nell did not cry. That was the thing Bram noticed first, and it frightened her more than tears would have. The girl simply stood there, gripping fistfuls of Bram's apron, breathing hard through her nose like someone who had learned that crying made things find you.

"Come and sit down," Bram said. Her own voice sounded strange to her — roughened, careful. "Sit by the fire. I'll make tea. Proper tea, with sugar in it, which is wasteful and which Miss Marlowe pretends not to notice."

Nell let herself be led. She sat in the chair nearest the stove, the packet in her lap, and watched Bram fill the kettle with the fixed attention of someone memorising a procedure. When the tea came, sweet and too strong, she drank it in small sips without being asked twice.

"You can talk," Bram said gently. "You know that, don't you? You don't have to. But you can."

"I know I can." The voice was small but not broken. It had edges. "I'm not simple."

"No," Bram agreed. "You're not simple."

They sat in the rain-noise and the fire-noise for a long time. Bram did not press. Pressing, she knew from watching Edith work a room full of hostile journalists, only taught people where the doors were. Instead she mended her grip, and peeled an apple in one unbroken spiral — a trick her father had taught her, a hundred years ago, in another life — and laid the peel on a saucer near Nell's elbow without comment.

Nell ate it. All of it.

Then, without looking up from the empty saucer, she said: "It were my dad what give me the packet. He wrapped it in the oilcloth hisself. He done it fast, like he were wrapping a parcel for Christmas but angry about it."

Bram set down the apple and the knife with great care, as though either might detonate.

"Where were you?" she asked. "When he wrapped it?"

"In the cart. Under the tarpaulin." Nell's eyes stayed on the saucer. "He said, You're the cargo now, Nellie. Cargo don't make a sound till it's delivered. He said it like a joke but it weren't a joke. His hands were shaking and he'd got blood on his cuff already, from somewhere, and he wiped it on his coat so I wouldn't see, only I seen."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere down the street a milk cart rattled past, its driver whistling something tuneless.

"Tell me about that night," Bram said. "All of it. Only if you want to. But if you tell me, I'll listen all the way to the end, and I won't interrupt you once, and afterwards nothing changes except that I'll know. Can you do that?"

Nell looked up at last. She had her father's eyes — Bram realised this with a lurch; the courier whose name Edith would not say in front of the child had given this girl those grey, level eyes — and they considered Bram with a gravity no seven-year-old should be issued.

"You won't send me away?"

"No."

"Even if I tell it wrong?"

"You couldn't tell it wrong. You were there. That makes you the only one telling it right."

Something shifted in the child's posture — a degree of straightening, a spine remembering itself. She put down the cup. She took a breath the size of her whole body.

* * *

"We live — lived — in Walthamstow," Nell said. "In a house what backs onto the railway cutting. Dad drove a delivery van for Mr. Prentice, stationery and such, but some nights he done other runs. I weren't supposed to know. I knowed anyway. Grown-ups think children are deaf."

Bram thought of herself, steaming letters over a kettle, reading words meant for other eyes. *Grown-ups think everyone is deaf,* she almost said, and did not.

"That night it rained like today. Dad come home late and he weren't right. He kept looking out the back window at the cutting, and he'd lit the lamp and then blowed it out, and lit it again, which he never done because lamps cost. Then there was a knock at the front, three knocks and a pause, and Dad went white. Not scared-white. Angry-white. Like when Mum used to—" She stopped. Started again. "Like when someone comes what you owe money to.

"He says to me, Nellie, go under the stairs and count to five hundred. And I goes, and I counts, but I counts fast like I always done, and I gets to five hundred in no time and I can still hear voices, so I starts again, quiet-like. And the voices get loud. Not shouting loud. That kind of loud what's worse than shouting, where people are talking very slow and very clear because they mean every word.

"I heard Dad say, 'I don't hold with that, and I won't carry it.' Just that, over and over, like a man saying prayers. 'I don't hold with that, and I won't carry it.'

"And then another voice says — I never heard him before but I'd know him anywhere, his voice was soft like butter going off — he says, 'Prentice thinks you will. Prentice paid for your van. Prentice holds your paper.'"

Nell's hands had found each other in her lap and were twisting, twisting. Bram wanted to cover them with her own and did not dare move, did not dare breathe, in case the thread of the telling snapped.

"Dad come to the stairs," Nell went on, quieter now, "and he opens the little door, and his face is wet, and I don't know if it's sweat or crying and I never asked and now I never can. And he gets the packet from under the floorboard — I seen where the floorboard is, I could show you the house blindfold — and he wraps it in the oilcloth and he puts it inside my coat, inside the lining what's torn, and he says, You're the cargo now. And he takes me out the back, down the cutting steps, in the rain.

"There was a lantern hanging on a nail by the goods shed. It swings when it rains, on account of the wind off the tracks, and the light goes round and round the walls like — like a lighthouse, only dirty. Orange. And Dad crouches down and holds my face in both his hands and his hands are freezing and he says—"

Her voice stopped. Not faltered — stopped, clean, like a cut wire. She stared at the fire and her lips moved once, twice, forming words that would not come out, and Bram sat with her fists clenched in her lap and waited, and hated herself for waiting, and knew that waiting was the only decent thing left to do.

"He says, 'Whatever happens, remember my face. Don't let them make you forget it. Forgetting's how they win.'" Nell swallowed. "And then he kisses my head and he says, Run along the cutting to Mrs. Okafor's, don't stop for nobody, and if anyone stops you, you're going to your auntie's and you've never seen me tonight. And I runned."

"But you looked back," Bram said. It was not a question. Children always looked back.

"I looked back." Nell's voice had gone flat and precise, a recitation, a stone wall built word by word between herself and the falling. "There was three men come out from behind the goods shed. One held the lantern up high so the light went in Dad's face. I could see him plain. Plain as I see you. And one of the others hit him with something — I didn't see what, it was too dark, only the noise of it, like a sack of spuds dropped off a cart — and Dad went down on the rails, and the man with the lantern held it low then, close, and looked at him a long time, and Dad weren't moving, and the man says something I couldn't catch, and then they all stood still while the rain come down, like they was waiting for Dad to get up and apologise.

"And then the soft-voice man — he were the one holding the lantern, I seen his hand, he'd got a ring — he says loud enough for me to hear, maybe he wanted me to hear, maybe he forgot I were there, and he says: 'Put it on the suffragists. Make it look like their lot done it. There's a meeting Thursday; leave what's needed where they'll find it.'

"And I runned then. I runned proper. Along the cutting in the dark with the packet banging my ribs, and I never stopped till Mrs. Okafor's door, and she took me in and she were crying too, and next day she put me on the train with a note pinned to me, and the note said take her to the lady what prints the truth, Miss Marlowe, and forget the face.

"But I never forgot it." Nell lifted her chin, and her father's grey eyes met Bram's across the little kitchen, and they were dry, terribly dry, two wells that had decided crying was a luxury the enemy could not be allowed to witness. "I remember it every time I shut my eyes. Him under the lantern. I remember his face better than our own parlour. So they never won that bit, have they? Whatever else they done. They never won that bit."

* * *

Bram discovered that she had been holding her breath for some measurable portion of the last ten minutes, and let it out slowly, and found that her hands were shaking, and folded them in her lap to hide it.

*Make it look like their lot done it.*

Edith had shown her the photographs two nights ago, by lamplight, after Nell was asleep: the ledger's neat columns, the courier's route, the payments initialed in a hand that traced back to a desk in Whitehall. Bram had understood it intellectually, the way she understood the mechanism of a differential gear — something turning, transmitting force, wheels within wheels. She had understood that men in government intended to murder women in the street and hang the crime on the movement, and she had understood it as one understands a diagram.

Now she understood it as rain. As a lantern swinging on a nail. As a man on a railway line saying *I don't hold with that* until the world stopped his mouth.

History, Bram thought, was not happening to other people. It had a face, and the face was seven years old and drinking sweet tea at her kitchen table, and it had been happening to Bram Vane all along, and she had been reading other people's mail while it gathered outside her window like weather.

"Nellie," she said — the nickname arrived without her permission, and Nell did not correct it, and something in the girl's shoulders came down half an inch — "the man with the ring. The soft voice. If you saw him again — a drawing, a photograph — could you know him?"

"Yes." No hesitation. None at all.

"And the others?"

"The tall one's got a scar through his eyebrow, like a worm. Another one coughs, wet-sounding, in his chest. I heard him coughing while they stood there in the rain, waiting for Dad to get up." Her jaw tightened. "They waited ages. Like it were a game and Dad had to say when."

Bram rose, walked to the window, and stood with her back to the child, looking out at the rain running down the glass, because if Nell saw her face just then the girl would learn something no seven-year-old should be issued either — the exact anatomy of rage in an adult who has understood, all at once, precisely what she is carrying and what it will cost.

*Forget the face,* the note had ordered. Whoever had abandoned Nell at Edith's door — Mrs. Okafor, or whoever had carried her the rest of the way — had been given an instruction, and had disobeyed it, and had passed the disobedience down the line like contraband. Remember the face. Bram thought of the packet in its oilcloth, of pages sewn soon into coat linings, of checkpoints and hollow books and a fixer named Crake whose name she had heard Edith say only once, quietly, the way one says the name of weather coming.

They were going to ask this child, eventually, to identify a minister of the Crown. Or they were going to spend everything — Edith's freedom, Bram's safety, the whole fragile machinery of the cause — getting the evidence to Manchester before Friday, so that the truth could reach print and make identification unnecessary. Either way, Nell was already in it. She had been in it since a lantern swung orange on a goods-shed wall.

The only question was whether the adults around her would be honest about that, or whether they would go on pretending, as Bram had pretended for months, that history happened to other people.

She turned around. She knelt down beside the chair, so that her eyes were level with Nell's, and she took the small twisted hands and stilled them, and she heard herself say words that she knew — even as they left her, even as some cold clerk deep in her brain stamped and filed them under *reckless*, under *unkeepable*, under *lie* — she heard herself say:

"Nothing is going to happen to you. Do you hear me? Nothing. Not while I've got two hands and a motor car and the stubbornness of ten Marlowes. They are not going to touch you. They are not going to find you. And one day — soon, I promise it — you are going to stand in a big bright room and point at the man with the ring and the whole country is going to look where you're pointing, and then it will be finished, and you will never have to be afraid in a doorway again."

It was a monstrous promise. Every rational faculty she possessed catalogued its impossibility: Crake closing in, three checkpoints, a fixer's net drawn tight around Holloway, a government that murdered couriers on wet railway lines. She promised it anyway, because the alternative was to let this child see the arithmetic in her face, and Bram Vane — reader of stolen letters, keeper of other people's secrets, observer of history from behind a steering wheel — discovered, kneeling on cold flagstones with a dead man's daughter's hands in hers, that there were some calculations she refused to perform.

Nell studied her for a long moment. Then she leaned forward and rested her forehead against Bram's, the way her father must have done a thousand times in a house backing onto a railway cutting, and said, with terrible, absolute faith:

"All right. I believe you."

Bram held her there, eyes closed, and felt the promise settle over them both like a debt entered in a ledger — and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like calm: *I will pay this if it ruins me.*

* * *

The front door opened at four o'clock, earlier than expected, and Edith came into the kitchen shaking rain from her hat, and stopped dead.

Nell was asleep in the chair by the stove, the empty teacup balanced precariously on her knee, the packet still hugged to her chest. Bram sat on the floor beside the chair — she had no memory of deciding to sit on the floor — with her back against the girl's legs, staring at nothing.

"She talked," Bram said, before Edith could ask.

Edith set down her hat. Very carefully. "All of it?"

"All of it. Lantern on a nail. Three men. A ring on the soft-voiced one's hand. And orders — spoken aloud, Edith, in the open air — to lay it on us. On the meeting Thursday." Bram's voice came out level, which surprised her; underneath it, something was burning with a heat she had never once felt in all her months of secret reading, and she suspected it would never entirely go out. "She remembers faces. Scars. A cough. She's a better witness than any photograph in that packet, and Halloran's men left her alive at the edge of a railway cutting believing otherwise."

Edith lowered herself into the opposite chair. For a moment the celebrated orator, the terror of Holloway Road, simply looked at the sleeping child, and Bram watched the same arithmetic move behind her eyes that had been moving behind her own all afternoon — and watched Edith arrive, visibly, at a different sum.

"Then she cannot stay here," Edith said softly. "Not one more week. Not one more night than we can manage. Crake is methodical, Bram. He will trace the Okafor woman, and the Okafor woman will lead him to a printed address, and then—" She did not finish it. She reached across and touched the oilcloth packet, once, with two fingers, as though it were hot.

"When?" Bram asked.

"Tomorrow. There are Friends in Stoke Newington — quiet people, safe people. I'll arrange it tonight." Edith's jaw set in the particular way it set before a platform speech. "The evidence travels separately. The child travels separately. Two parcels, two routes, and neither knows where the other is."

Bram looked at the sleeping girl, at the tear in the coat lining where a dead man's last act still hid, and felt the first cold edge of a resentment she would carry for weeks without naming it.

"And who carries which parcel?" she asked.

Edith met her eyes, and did not answer, and outside the rain went on falling on Holloway Road like it fell on Walthamstow, like it fell on everything.
