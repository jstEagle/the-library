## 10. The Courier's Widow

The address was a terrace house in Camden Town, three doors from a chapel whose windows had been boarded since the riots of the winter before. Edith Marlowe had Bram drive her as far as the corner and then walk the rest alone, because a woman of her name arriving in a motorcar drew eyes the way a struck match draws moths, and she could not afford to be drawn. She wore her plainest coat, grey wool with no braid, and carried nothing but a reticule with the oilcloth packet inside it, wrapped now in a shawl as though it were an infant she was smuggling past a customs officer.

Which, she thought as she climbed the steps, was not so far from the truth.

She knocked twice, paused, then twice again — the rhythm Mrs. Aldous had given her over the telephone that morning, in a voice so low Edith had to hold the receiver tight to her ear. The door opened on a chain, then closed, then opened fully onto a narrow hall that smelled of boiled cabbage and beeswax and, beneath both, of grief — that particular staleness that enters a house when a woman has stopped opening the back door because there is no one coming home through it.

"Mrs. Ferris," Edith said. "Thank you for seeing me."

"I didn't see you," said the woman in the doorway. "I let you in. There's a difference." She was perhaps thirty-five, though the face suggested a much longer acquaintance with weariness: pale, sharp-boned, dark hair pinned without ceremony, an apron dusted with flour as if she had been making bread she did not intend to eat. Her hands were red from water. She looked at Edith the way one looks at weather — assessing what it would do, and whether anything could be done about it.

"I know the difference," Edith said. "May I come further in?"

Mrs. Ferris stood aside. The front room was small and fiercely tidy. A photograph of a young man in his Sunday best hung above the mantel, slightly crooked, as though someone straightened it every morning and could not quite make herself stop touching it. A clock ticked with the patience of something that had outlived its purpose but knew no other trade.

"You'll take tea," Mrs. Ferris said. It was not a question. "You look like you haven't eaten since Tuesday."

"It's Thursday."

"Then I'm right." She went out, and Edith heard her moving in the kitchen, the clatter of cups, and understood suddenly that this woman had been waiting four days for someone to arrive whom she could feed. Grief needs a task or it eats its host. Edith sat very still and looked at the photograph until the man in it resolved into detail: a long jaw, a cowlick tamed with pomade, a smile that had been held too long for the camera and had begun to curdle into self-consciousness. Thomas Ferris. Thirty-one years old. A courier who had carried documents between Manchester and London for two years, according to Mrs. Aldous, and who had never once been late, and who had arrived at Euston on Monday last and been met by men he trusted, or men who looked like men worth trusting, and had not come home.

The tea came. Strong enough to stand a spoon in. Mrs. Ferris poured for both of them and then did not drink, holding her cup in both hands like a small warm animal.

"You're Mrs. Marlowe."

"I am."

"The newspaper people call you a harridan. My sister reads the *Mail*. She says they've got your picture next to pictures of fires."

"They have," Edith said. "It's good publicity for the fires."

Something moved at the corner of the woman's mouth — not quite a smile; more like a muscle remembering that smiling had once been among its duties. Then it was gone, and she set down her cup.

"You want to ask me about Tommy's work."

"I want to show you something," Edith said, "and ask you whether it is what it claims to be. Nothing more than that. If you tell me to burn it, I will burn it, and we will both forget this afternoon ever happened, and I will never trouble you again."

"That's a lie," Mrs. Ferris said calmly. "You'll trouble me again either way, because whatever's in that packet has got my Tommy's handwriting in it somewhere, and I'd know." She nodded at the reticule. "Go on, then. Open it here where I can see."

Edith unwrapped the oilcloth on the table between them. The photographs first — six of them, grainy, taken by someone with a steady hand and a hidden camera: men loading crates at a depot in Salford; a man she recognized now from the ledger's descriptions — Halloran's private secretary, a smooth-faced creature named Pell — counting banknotes across a pub table; and the last one, the worst one, a stretch of towpath at dusk with a shape lying in it and three figures standing over the shape, and one of the figures holding something that might have been a lantern and might have been a cosh.

Then the ledger. Forty-one pages in Thomas Ferris's neat clerk's hand: dates, sums, names reduced to initials, routes, and — beginning in March — entries that tracked payments from a discreet account to a series of men whose combined purpose became unmistakable page by page. The last entry, dated the day of his death, read only: *Delivering to M. Full account. They will not like it. If anything happens, it goes to the widow.*

"He wrote that the morning he died," Mrs. Ferris said. Her voice was level in the way of ice on a pond — solid-seeming, and thin. "He kissed me at the door. He never kisses me at the door, he says it makes him late. But he did that morning. And he said, 'If a man comes asking after me, say we quarrelled.' We hadn't quarrelled. Not in eleven years." She turned the cup in her hands. "I thought maybe there was another woman. Isn't that a joke. I lay awake all night being jealous of some poor girl, and all along it was this."

Edith let the silence sit. There was nothing to say to it that was not obscene.

"Is it genuine?" she asked at last. "The ledger. Could it have been fabricated? Planted? I have to know. Men are going to stake their liberty on these pages — possibly their lives. I won't carry a forgery into battle."

Mrs. Ferris looked at her for a long moment, and then reached across and opened the ledger to the inside cover, and pointed with one floured finger at a small annotation in the lower corner: *T.F. — acc. no. 4471 — begun Jan. 1911.*

"Tommy kept accounts the way monks keep prayers," she said. "Every book he ever owned has that written in it. Account number, date started. His father was a bank clerk and beat arithmetic into him before he beat anything else out. You couldn't forge that, not unless you knew him, and the only ones who knew him are sitting in this room and lying under a stone in Ilford." She closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a hymnal. "It's real. Every line of it is real. He knew what he was doing when he wrote it. He knew they might kill him for it, and he wrote it anyway, because he said—" and here her voice did break, just once, a hairline fracture quickly plastered over, "—he said somebody has to keep the books. Somebody always has to keep the books, Mrs. Marlowe, or the thieves get away with saying there was never any money missing."

* * *

They sat while the tea went cold. Through the window Edith could see the boarded chapel and, beyond it, the roofs of Camden sliding down toward the smoke-hazed centre of the city, where men in fine rooms were at that moment deciding how best to use a dead courier and a frightened child to hang a bombing around the necks of ten thousand women who wanted nothing but the vote.

"You mean to take it to Manchester," Mrs. Ferris said. It was not a question either. Word travelled; Edith had learned long ago that word travelled faster than trains, faster than telegrams, borne on no wires at all.

"On Friday," Edith said. "There's an editor there who owes me a debt and hates Halloran more than he fears him. Once the pages are photographed and wired to London papers, no minister alive can stuff them back in the bottle."

"And you'll carry them yourself?"

"I'm deciding who carries them."

Mrs. Ferris laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry and short, like a stick snapping. "Deciding. You sit there in your good grey coat deciding which of you gets to die on the road, and you think that's strategy." She stood up abruptly and went to the window, standing with her back to Edith, her arms folded hard across the apron. "Do you know what they told me? The police came Tuesday. Two of them, very polite. Very sorry. Tragic accident, they said. Robbery gone wrong. They asked had Tommy any enemies. They asked had he been behaving oddly lately, keeping late hours, receiving letters. They asked—" she turned, and her eyes were wet and furious, "—they asked if he'd been mixed up with the suffragettes. Because they'd heard, you see. Heard from whom? Nobody had heard anything. He wasn't mixed up with you. He didn't care about the vote one way or the other, he said women would get it when men got sense, and God knows when that'll be. But they wanted me to say it. They wanted me to hand them my husband's corpse dressed as your bomber, and when I said no, the younger one smiled at me. Smiled. Like we were all friends agreeing on the price of fish."

Edith rose slowly. "Mrs. Ferris—"

"They'll do it anyway," the widow said. "That's why you came, isn't it? That's why the child came to your door with her little packet. They're building the gallows out of paper and they don't even need the truth to hold it up. So yes, take it to Manchester. Yes, print it. Burn their names into the sky." She crossed the room and put both hands flat on the table, leaning toward Edith, close enough that Edith could smell the flour and the lye soap and beneath it, faintly, a man's tobacco that still lived in the upholstery. "But don't you run, and don't you hide, and don't you send some girl with hollowed-out heels to die in a ditch outside Stockport so that *you* can live to make speeches."

"I have never run from anything in my life."

"No?" Mrs. Ferris's voice dropped, and somehow that was worse than the fury. "You drive about in cars behind smoked glass. You speak from platforms with stewards round you. You write letters and sign them and let the newspapers argue about your character as if you were a bill before Parliament. That isn't courage, Mrs. Marlowe. Courage is my Tommy walking onto that platform at Euston knowing what he was carrying and who wanted it. Courage is that child keeping her mouth shut for four days because her father told her to and she's trying to be good. You've been brave the way rich men are generous — it costs you something, but it never yet cost you everything."

The words went in like a blade finding the seam of armour. Edith stood very straight and let them, because she had spent twenty years training herself to receive blows without swaying, and this woman deserved at least that much discipline from her.

"You may be right," Edith said quietly. "About all of it. But understand what you're asking. If I go underground, the evidence dies with my credibility. If I'm arrested, it dies in a police cell. The only way those pages save anyone — save the movement, save that child, save your Tommy's name — is if they're in print before Halloran's story is. And to get them there, someone must pass through three checkpoints built specifically to catch them. I am choosing that person carefully. That is not cowardice. It is the only arithmetic left."

"Arithmetic." Mrs. Ferris straightened. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, roughly, the way one brushes away flies, and when she spoke again her voice had gone cold and clear as January. "My Tommy was arithmetic too, to those men. A column in a ledger. An expense. And that child of yours — because she's yours now, whoever left her, she's yours — she's arithmetic. And you'll spend whichever of them the sum requires, and call it duty, and sleep at night because you genuinely believe you had no choice." She picked up the ledger and pressed it back into Edith's hands, folding Edith's fingers over the cover with both of her own work-roughened ones. "So I'm not asking you not to run. I'm telling you what running costs. It costs you becoming them. Men like Halloran move people about like parcels, Mrs. Marlowe. Don't you dare learn the habit."

* * *

At the door, Mrs. Ferris stopped her with one last thing.

"There's a coat of his in the cupboard. Tweed, brown, too big for me to alter." She said it looking at the middle distance, at nothing. "He had pockets sewn inside the lining. Double pockets. He used them for the important things — things he wouldn't trust to a bag." She finally looked at Edith, and her expression was the most terrible thing Edith had seen all week: not rage, not grief, but the flat practicality of a woman solving a problem with the only materials death had left her. "Take it. Whatever you're carrying Friday, sew it into Tommy's lining. He'd want that. He'd laugh, actually — he hated your movement, you know. Said you'd all get the vote eventually and then find out Parliament was just as useless to you as to us." A breath. "And then he'd do it anyway. He always did it anyway."

Edith took the coat. It hung heavy across her arm, smelling of pipe smoke and rain and a living man who was not living anymore.

At the gate she paused. Behind her the door had already shut; the widow wanted no lingering, no witnesses, no thanks. Down the street, parked at the corner where Bram waited with the car, everything looked ordinary — the motor, the driver's silhouette, the grey afternoon settling over Camden like ash.

Except that a man stood across the road from the car. Dark coat, bowler, unhurried, reading a newspaper he held too low to read. As Edith watched, he folded it, tucked it under his arm, and walked off toward the chapel — pausing, just briefly, to glance up at number fourteen. At the house of the courier's widow.

He had not seen her. She was almost sure he had not seen her.

But he had marked the car. And he had marked the house. And somewhere behind that mild, patient face, a report was already being composed in a neat, obedient hand: *Marlowe's vehicle observed, Camden, Thursday afternoon, outside residence of T. Ferris, deceased.*

Edith walked to the car with the dead man's coat over her arm and her spine straight as a lamppost, and when she climbed in beside Bram she said only: "Drive. Not home. Drive until I tell you to stop."
