## 11. Crake Calls at the House

He came on a Tuesday, which was the day the coal was delivered, and I have often thought since that he chose it for exactly that reason — a morning when strangers at the door would seem ordinary, when the sound of boots in the passage could be explained away by any nervous mind as sacks and shovels.

I heard him before I saw him. The knocker went twice, unhurried, the way a man knocks who knows the door will open regardless of how he treats it. Then Mrs. Quill's voice from the hall, pitched high with that particular courtesy servants use when their instinct is to bar the door instead: *one moment, sir.*

I was in the kitchen with Nell, teaching her to shell peas because her hands needed something to do and mine needed somewhere to be. She looked up when the knock came — she always looked up now, at every knock, every bell, every footstep heavier than a cat's — and I put my hand flat over hers on the table.

"Keep shelling," I said. "Peas don't wait."

It was a lie and she knew it was a lie and we both carried on anyway, which is its own kind of language between people who cannot say what they mean out loud.

I dried my hands and went into the hall.

* * *

He introduced himself as Inspector Crake of the Metropolitan Police, Special Branch, and he said it the way other men recite their decorations. He was not what I had expected, though I couldn't have said what I had expected. Not a brute. Worse than a brute: a careful man, medium height, forty or thereabouts, with a face like an unaddressed envelope — nothing written on it, everything possible inside it. He wore a good coat brushed clean and carried his hat in both hands, which struck me then as politeness and strikes me now as practice. A man learns to hold his hat so his hands have nowhere to go but where he wants them.

"Miss Vane," he said, before I'd given my name. "The driver. Mrs. Marlowe's driver." He smiled a small smile, the kind that lives entirely in the mouth and never reaches the eyes. "You're spoken of. Efficient."

"Mrs. Marlowe is at a committee meeting," I said. "In town. If you'd like to leave a message."

"I'm sure she is." He let that sit a moment too long, the way a man sets a weight on a table to see if it holds. "No message, thank you. Routine inquiries. A formality, really. May I come in? It's rather raw out."

There are questions that are not questions. I stepped aside because refusing would have been a confession, and because Edith had drilled one rule into me above all others in three years of driving her through hostile streets: *never give them a story. Make them bring their own; theirs will be full of holes.* So Crake brought his own story into the Marlowe house, and I hung his hat myself, and noted that his coat smelled faintly of tobacco and carbolic soap, like a police station trying to pass as a gentleman's club.

Mrs. Quill brought tea nobody wanted. Crake sat in the front room — the respectable room, the one with the photographs of Edith's father and the framed certificate from some forgotten temperance league — and he sat down without looking for the chair, which told me he had been briefed on the house, or had been in it before, or had studied plans of houses like this one until they were all the same house to him.

"Routine inquiries," he said again, producing a notebook. "Following up on the regrettable incident near Blackfriars last month. A courier. You may have read about it — though the papers gave it little space, which is itself interesting, wouldn't you say?"

"I don't read much besides road signs," I said. "I'm behind a wheel most of the day."

"Mm." He wrote something, or made a show of writing something. "And you drive Mrs. Marlowe everywhere?"

"Wherever she asks."

"Everywhere she asks." He repeated it softly, tasting it. "Loyal work. Dangerous, these days, being close to a woman like that. There are men who'd pay well to know her movements. And men who'd pay better to know her correspondence."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass. I kept my face still because my face is the one part of me I've always been able to govern. Behind my sternum my heart was doing arithmetic.

"I wouldn't know anything about her letters," I said.

"No," Crake agreed pleasantly. "Of course not. Drivers rarely do." And then he looked directly at me for the first time since the doorstep — a long, level, unhurried look, the look of a man reading a document — and I understood that everything he said next would be said to that document, testing its margins.

* * *

He asked about the night of the march on Holloway Road. He asked whether Mrs. Marlowe had addressed the crowds, and whether she had left early or late, and whether the Daimler had been seen near the river. Each question was small. Each question was a stitch. I answered them plainly and truthfully where truth cost nothing, which is the whole art of it — you give them true things until they stop checking, and the false things ride in among the true things like smugglers in a crowd of pilgrims.

Then he set down his notebook, folded his hands, and said, in a tone of a man changing the subject to something lighter:

"You have a child in the house."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled under Mrs. Quill's weight, listening as I was listening.

"A visitor's daughter," I said. "A friend of the movement needed somewhere safe for the girl while she settles a family matter. She'll be gone by Friday." I heard myself say Friday and could have bitten my tongue off; it was the date Edith had fixed for Manchester, and it fell out of my mouth because it was the only future I had rehearsed.

"Friday," Crake said, and wrote it down, and I understood I had just handed a man with a notebook a thread, and threads are all such men need. "Children are delicate things. Easily frightened. This one, I'm told, was found wandering — or perhaps delivered, accounts differ — after the Blackfriars business. A witness, possibly. A small witness." He turned his unaddressed-envelope face toward the window, toward the street, as if the girl might be passing by on the pavement. "Men do terrible things to witnesses, Miss Vane. Terrible things even to small ones. It's why we take such care to find them first."

"*Find* them," I said. "Not protect them."

His eyes came back to me, and for one unguarded half-second something moved behind them — not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but appetite. "One hopes," he said, "they are the same thing." Then the shutter closed and he was pleasant again, and pleasant was worse.

* * *

He rose to leave. I stood to see him out, already breathing toward the door, already composing the sentence I would say to Edith on the telephone — *he came, he asked, he knows about Nell* — and then he stopped.

He stopped in the hall, beside the row of hooks by the door where coats hang, and he lingered.

It was nothing. That is what I told myself as I watched him. It was nothing, it was a man pausing on his way out, it was the ordinary idleness of a hallway. But his gaze went along the hooks the way a customs officer's hand goes along a suitcase lining, patient, thorough, pretending not to search while searching. Edith's grey coat. Mrs. Quill's shawl. My driving gloves. And at the end of the row, hanging low because it was small, a child's coat — brown corduroy, one button missing, the hem darkened where Nell had knelt in wet grass the week she arrived and no one had yet known enough to keep her from kneeling.

Crake looked at that coat the way a man looks at a face he has been shown a photograph of.

He didn't touch it. That was the thing I kept returning to afterward, turning it over in the dark like a stone with something underneath. He did not touch it. A policeman with no interest in the answer would have touched it — flicked the collar, remarked on the size, made some light remark about children growing like weeds. Touching is innocent. Looking is not. He looked at the coat for four seconds, maybe five, long enough for me to count because counting was the only thing keeping me upright, and then he turned back to me with his envelope-face and said:

"Corduroy wears out quickly. Children are hard on clothes." And then, collecting his hat from my hands, gently, as if taking back something he'd only lent: "You've been very helpful, Miss Vane. Give Mrs. Marlowe my regards. Tell her—" he settled the hat onto his head at last, and beneath its brim his eyes were bright and narrow as a ferret's— "tell her I admire consistency. In public figures especially. Consistency is so rare. And so easily disproved."

The door closed behind him. His footsteps went down the area steps, unhurried, and paused — I heard it through the glass of the transom — paused at the gate. Counting windows, perhaps. Or writing down the address he already had.

* * *

I stood in the hall with the cold he'd let in still moving around my ankles, and I did the arithmetic I should have done on the doorstep.

He had known my name. He had known about Friday — no, he hadn't known Friday, I had *given* him Friday, God help me. He had known about Nell, or suspected enough to fish, and the hook had gone into the water and something had taken it. He had looked at a child's coat hanging on a hook and his face had said *yes, this one, this matches the description,* and whatever that description was and wherever it came from, it existed, it was circulating, it had reached the desk of a Special Branch inspector who now knew that the child he was hunting slept three floors above the hall where he stood.

The hunt had an address. Ours.

I went back to the kitchen. Nell had finished the peas. All of them, every pod emptied into the bowl, arranged with the fussy precision of a child who has learned that obedience is shelter. She looked up at me, and her eyes did the thing they did now whenever I came into a room — the quick inventory, *is she frightened, am I safe, what must I be* — and I sat down across from her and took her two cold hands in mine and lied to her the way I had decided, without ever deciding it, that I would lie to her until the truth could walk on its own legs.

"Just a policeman," I said. "About the march. Boring man. Boring questions."

She nodded slowly. Then she said, in her small scraped voice that was only just coming back to her, "He looked at my coat."

My blood went still.

"It's got grass on it," she said. "From the bad night. I keep meaning to brush it and I forget." Her chin trembled once and steadied, because she was learning steadiness from us, God forgive us, learning it like measles. "Are they going to make me remember the bad night out loud?"

"Not today," I said. "Not here. Never here."

But upstairs, ten minutes later, standing at the window of my room with the curtain pulled to a slit, I watched Inspector Crake cross the square below at a stroll, stop at the corner, take out his notebook, and write. He wrote for a long time. Then he raised his head, and though the distance was too great and the angle wrong for him to have seen me — though reason itself said *he cannot see you, Bram, there is glass and lace and shadow* — his gaze climbed the face of the house, window by window, the way a man climbs stairs, and rested, for just a moment, on mine.

Then he turned the corner and was gone, and I went downstairs to write the note that would reach Edith before he could reach anyone else, and my hand shook only twice: once writing the word *coat*, and once writing the word *Friday*.
