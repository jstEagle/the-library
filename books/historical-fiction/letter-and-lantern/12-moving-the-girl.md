## 12. Moving the Girl

Edith Marlowe had learned long ago that the hardest decisions were never the loud ones. Loud decisions announced themselves — a window smashed, a banner unfurled, her own name shouted through a magistrate's keyhole. Those she could meet with her chin up and her pulse steady, because they arrived wearing their consequences on the outside. It was the quiet decisions that cut. The ones made at a kitchen table over cold tea, with a child asleep upstairs and the rain ticking against the glass like a patient hand.

She sat now in the back parlour of the house in Camden, the ledger open before her, and forced herself to do arithmetic with lives instead of pounds.

The packet could not stay here. That much was settled. Crake — and she was certain it would be Crake; Halloran did not employ men who knocked twice — had already come to the door once under the flimsy pretext of routine inquiries about stolen postal property, and Edith had watched him from the landing above, watching his eyes travel the hallway the way an appraiser's eyes travel a room. He had lingered by the coat hooks. She had seen him do it. A man who catalogued coats was a man building an inventory of households.

And a household containing a child whose face appeared nowhere and whose father had died carrying that ledger was not a household — it was a confession waiting to be read aloud.

* * *

The Quakers had come to her, as it happened, or near enough. Ruth Pym and her husband ran a boarding house off Upper Street, kept a hush about them like a held breath, and had sheltered two of Edith's girls after Holloway Road when the papers printed their names beside the word *hysterical*. They asked no questions then. Edith doubted they would ask many now.

She wrote to Ruth on Tuesday morning, keeping the letter deliberately dull: *a friend's daughter, eight years old, recently bereaved, in need of quiet and country air until arrangements can be made. She does not speak. She is frightened of men in uniform.* Even so, her pen hesitated over the last line, because writing it made Nell's condition a fact among facts, an item on an itinerary, when the truth was that the girl's silence had weight, a gravity, and everything in the house bent faintly toward it.

Bram drove her to Islington on Thursday. Edith sat in the rear seat with the ledger sewn into the false bottom of her hatbox and watched Bram's shoulders in the driving mirror — set high, squared against something. The girl had been strange since the night Crake came. Not careless, which would have been dangerous, but *rigid*, the way a wire goes rigid before it sings.

"You have an objection," Edith said, when they turned onto Liverpool Road.

"I don't, ma'am."

"You've been disagreeing with me for six miles. You do it very quietly, but you do it constantly."

Bram's hands shifted on the wheel. In the mirror her jaw worked once. "It's nothing I haven't already lost the argument for."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you'll accept, ma'am." A pause. "You've decided."

Edith looked out at the shopfronts sliding past, the wet awnings, a coster arguing cheerfully with the weather. "I decided three days ago," she said, "and I've spent those three days hoping someone would give me a reason to change my mind. No one has. So we arrive where we arrive."

* * *

Ruth Pym answered the door herself — a broad, grey woman with flour on one wrist and eyes that took in Edith, the car, the small figure half-hidden behind Bram's coat skirt, and filed all of it without comment.

"This is Nell," Edith said.

The girl stood pressed against Bram's leg, thumb hooked into the seam of the coat pocket, staring at the doorway as though doorways had lately taught her not to trust them. She wore the coat Edith had bought her — a good coat, blue wool, too warm for October — and she clutched the rag doll she would not let out of her sight even to wash her face.

Ruth crouched. It was a thing Edith noticed and admired: most adults loomed at children, bending the world down toward them like a tent collapsing. Ruth simply folded herself smaller until they were level.

"Hello, Nell. There's bread still warm in the kitchen, if you're minded. And a cat who thinks he owns the place, though he doesn't, so don't let him tell you different."

Nothing moved in the child's face except her eyes, which flicked up — to Bram. Not to Edith. To Bram, quick as a struck match, asking a question in the only language she had left.

Edith saw Bram's throat move. Saw her start to kneel and stop herself, hands curling at her sides.

"She'll want to know how long," Ruth said quietly, straightening, addressing Edith now. "Children need a shape to things. Even a wrong shape is better than none."

"A fortnight," Edith said. "Perhaps less. I'll send word through the usual channels."

"And her people?"

"Her people are dead." Edith said it evenly, because it was true and because the child, whatever she understood of words, understood tones perfectly — better than any adult in that hallway, probably. "That's rather the whole of why she's here."

Ruth nodded slowly. Then, to Bram: "Will you walk her in? She seems to have appointed you."

"I'm not—" Bram began, and stopped, and Edith watched her driver lose a fight with her own face. "A moment," Bram said. "If ma'am permits."

Edith permitted. She stood in the narrow front garden with its smell of wet sage and listened — she was not proud of listening, but she had never claimed to be proud — while Bram crouched in the doorway and spoke to Nell in a low voice Edith couldn't catch, and the girl put her arms around Bram's neck and held on with the desperate economy of a drowning swimmer, and Bram held her back with her eyes open over the child's shoulder, looking directly at Edith across the little garden.

*Look what you're doing*, that look said. *Look what your strategy costs.*

*I know*, Edith's own face answered, as steadily as she could make it. *Count the alternative.*

* * *

They drove back through the early dusk, and Edith used the silence to finish the other half of the plan, the half she had not yet spoken aloud because speaking it would make Bram carry it too.

"The evidence travels separately," Edith said at last. "That's the principle. One parcel, one person, one route — and nothing else attached to it. No child. No household. Nothing Crake can follow backwards from the delivery to us."

"Separately how?" Bram asked. Her voice had gone flat and professional, which Edith had learned to read as a warning sign like frost on a windscreen.

"There are options. Rail is fastest but inspected. Road is slower but stoppable anywhere between here and Manchester. I'm having both mapped tonight."

"And the courier?"

"Someone with no history. No name in our files. No connection to this house that Crake can find."

The mirror showed Bram's eyes coming up. Held there a beat too long.

"No," Edith said.

"I hadn't said anything."

"You were constructing the argument. I could hear the scaffolding going up." Edith leaned forward slightly. "Bram. Listen to me as a strategist, since you decline to listen as anything else. If this goes wrong, whoever carries the ledger will be arrested, charged, and made into the story — *militant courier caught with seditious documents*, and Halloran's version writes itself around them like a glove. I will not hand him a driver. I will not hand him anyone whose loss the movement can't absorb."

"And you can be absorbed?"

"I'm expensive to arrest," Edith said. "That's precisely my value. A famous name in the dock makes headlines; headlines make questions; questions are the one weapon Halloran fears more than dynamite. If it must be someone—" she shrugged, and the shrug cost her more than the sentence had— "then it should be someone whose arrest serves the cause even in failing."

"That's not strategy, ma'am. That's martyrdom with a timetable."

"It's arithmetic," Edith said, "and I've done it longer than you."

Bram said nothing more. But her shoulders stayed high all the way home, and when she pulled up at the house she sat with her hands flat on the wheel a moment, staring through the windscreen at the dark, and Edith understood that the argument had not ended — it had merely gone underground, where Bram kept everything that mattered.

* * *

That evening Edith worked at the parlour table with the route maps spread out and weighted with teacups, and she heard, from the kitchen, the sound of Bram writing letters.

Not reading them, for once. Writing.

Edith knew the difference by ear now — the scratch-and-pause of composition versus the dry whisper of paper being unfolded. Bram had been writing a great deal lately, and burning nothing, and Edith found she did not want to speculate about what the drafts contained, because speculation led down roads she could not afford to walk just yet. There would be time after Friday. There had to be.

At nine o'clock Bram appeared in the doorway with two cups of tea, unasked, and set one beside the maps.

"The Quaker house has a telephone?" Bram asked.

"No."

"Then word travels by letter, or by visit."

"By visit. Mrs. Pym's nephew runs errands between there and a tobacconist in King's Cross. Dead drops, essentially, but slow ones."

"Slow." Bram stood looking down at the map, at the red thread Edith had run from London up through Crewe to Manchester, pinned at each checkpoint she'd identified. "So if anything happens to the girl—if Crake finds her—we wouldn't know for a day."

"We'd know fast enough to do nothing useful," Edith said. "Yes."

Bram's jaw tightened. "You're comfortable with that."

Edith set down her pen. "No," she said, and let the word sit there, naked, unsanded. "But comfort is a currency I stopped earning years ago, and I won't counterfeit it now by pretending otherwise. Every arrangement we make spends someone. My whole life's work is deciding who, and living with the receipt." She looked up. "You think I sent that child away lightly. I sent her away because every hour she sleeps under this roof, the probability that we all hang together rises, and I refuse to hang her for my convictions. That is the whole of it. That is the *only* whole of it."

For a long moment Bram didn't answer. Then she picked up her own cup and said, quietly, almost to the map:

"She asked me not to go."

Edith went still.

"She doesn't speak. But this afternoon, in the garden — she held my sleeve and she made a sound. Just one. Like a question with the words taken out." Bram's voice was level in the way of a man walking on ice he knows is thin. "And I told her I'd see her soon. I told her that. And now I'm standing here helping plan a route that may take me to Manchester and keep me there, or worse, and I gave an eight-year-old my word on a fortnight."

"Bram—"

"I know what you'll say. That promises to children are provisional. That the movement—" She stopped herself, breathed. "I know what you'll say. I just wanted you to know what it cost. Since you do your arithmetic so carefully. You should have the full figures."

She set down her cup and left the room, and Edith sat alone among her maps with the tea cooling and the red thread running north through all its pinned and patrolled towns, and admitted to the empty parlour what she would admit to no one else:

That the figures had never been complete. That there was always a line item she could not price — the sleeve-grip of a silent child, the look over a drowning shoulder, the sound a promise makes when it starts to break — and that every strategist in history had built her plans around that missing number and called the structure sound.

Outside, the rain began again. Somewhere north, in Islington, a girl was sleeping in a strange bed with a rag doll in her arms, and in the hall of Edith's house hung a coat that Inspector Crake had looked at too long, and on Friday, one way or another, the ledger would begin its journey north.

Edith blew out the lamp, and in the dark she allowed herself ten seconds of grief for the ease she had surrendered — then lit it again, and reached for a fresh sheet of paper, and began to write the letter that would set the courier's route.

She had just sealed it when she heard the front gate, and then footsteps on the path — unhurried, deliberate, entirely wrong for the hour —

and through the frosted pane of the front door, a tall shape raised its hat.
