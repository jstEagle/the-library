## 13. The First Checkpoint: Euston

I volunteered before I understood what I was volunteering for. That is the truth of it, and I have never been able to decide whether the truth excuses me or damns me.

It was the evening after Edith's visit to the courier's widow — a visit she described to me only in fragments, in that clipped voice she used when something had gone through her like a blade and she did not care to show the wound. She stood at the drawing-room window with her back to me, watching the street, which was empty, which was always empty at that hour, which was precisely why she watched it.

"The evidence travels Friday," she said.

"I know."

"You do not know, Bram. You know nothing, because I have told you nothing, because knowing things is dangerous." She turned. The lamplight caught the grey at her temples, more of it than there had been in the spring. "But I find I am tired of protecting people who refuse to be protected. So: the evidence travels Friday. Two of us were meant to go. Miss Hartley and myself. And Crake — you remember Inspector Crake, the man who lingered over Nell's coat as though it were a love letter — Crake has had us watched for eleven days. I counted his man twice on the corner by the tobacconist."

"Then don't go."

"That," said Edith Marlowe, "is not among the options."

I should explain what the book looked like, because the book mattered, because everything came down in the end to the width of a book's spine and the steadiness of my hands.

It was a sermons collection — *Discourses on Moral Duty*, 1889, purchased for fourpence from a barrow off Gray's Inn Road, chosen because no sergeant alive would open it twice. Edith and I worked on it through the small hours at the kitchen table while Nell slept upstairs in the maid's room, her door left open a crack because she could not bear it closed. Edith cut the block of pages with a razor and a ruler, sixty pages from the heart of the book, leaving walls of paper an eighth of an inch thick on every side. Into the cavity went the ledger, wrapped first in oilcloth and then in waxed paper, folded to the size of a prayer book. Photographs last, face to face so the emulsions would not scratch. Then the false pages went back over the top — sixty leaves of moral duty, cut thin at the spine and pasted down with flour paste — so that a casual thumb flipping through would meet nothing but paper all the way to the boards.

"It will not survive a careful search," Edith said, pressing the cover flat under the ironing board overnight. "Nothing survives a careful search. We are not betting on the book, Bram. We are betting on the sergeant."

"And if we lose the bet?"

She did not answer that. She wiped paste from her fingers and looked at the book lying there, grey and blameless, and said, "You'll take the morning train from Euston. The 8:40 to Manchester. You'll carry one suitcase, nothing more — a woman visiting a sick aunt carries one suitcase. You'll wear your driving coat, not your good one. You'll buy a third-class ticket, because inspectors are lazier about third class. And when they stop you — and they may not stop you at all — you will be bored. Do you understand? Bored is the safest thing a woman can be at a checkpoint. Bored women are invisible."

I said I understood. I was lying. What I understood, standing in that kitchen at two in the morning with paste drying under my fingernails, was that my hands wanted to shake and that I would not let them, and that this — the not letting — was going to be the whole of my life for the next thirty-six hours.

* * *

The morning of the 8:40 came up grey and mild, the kind of London morning that makes everything look washed out and harmless. I kissed Nell goodbye in the hallway, kneeling, and she put both arms around my neck and held on with the desperate strength children have, and I said, "Saturday. I'll be back Saturday," and felt the promise sit in my chest like a swallowed stone, because I had made that promise once before, recklessly, and I intended now to be the sort of person who kept promises even when keeping them required walking through a wall of police.

Edith did not come to the door. That was deliberate; we had agreed she would stay behind the curtains of the upstairs room, out of sight of the tobacconist's corner. But when I lifted my suitcase from the hall stand I found, tucked into the leather handle, a single white carnation, the kind she wore at rallies, and I understood it for what it was — not sentiment but armour, a thing to look at when the fear came, a reminder of who I was doing this for and why.

I took a cab to Euston. It seemed absurd, taking a cab, sitting inside with the ledger against my ribs like a second heartbeat, rattling through streets where any of Crake's men might have been watching — but Edith had insisted on it, because a woman who walks to a station with a suitcase is remembered, and a woman who arrives by cab is one of ten thousand.

Euston at half past eight in the morning was noise and steam and the great cold arch of the station roof holding all that human hurry in one echoing fist. Porters shouted. A barrel organ ground out something cheerful near the refreshment room. Pigeons moved along the girders overhead like thoughts too quick to catch. I bought my third-class ticket to Manchester, and my voice came out steady, and I was absurdly proud of that steadiness for nearly a full minute.

Then I saw them.

They had set up a table across the entrance to Platform Nine — not hiding it, not disguising it, just a plain trestle table with a police notice propped against the inkwell: **LUGGAGE INSPECTION IN ACCORDANCE WITH SPECIAL ORDER. ALL PASSENGERS FOR THE NORTH.** Behind the table stood two men. One was young and bored-looking, a constable with his helmet pushed back and his hands in his pockets. The other was older, heavyset, a sergeant with a moustache like a scrubbing brush and the eyes of a man who has spent twenty years looking for things and found most of them.

*Special order.* Not routine. Special orders do not drift down from heaven onto ordinary Thursdays. Somewhere between the kitchen table and this platform, someone had decided that Thursday's northbound luggage deserved a second pair of eyes, and I thought of Crake in his neat office matching routes and names, and my mouth went dry as chalk.

*Bored,* I told myself. *Be bored.*

There was a queue ahead of me — six passengers, seven — and I joined it and let the queue do its work. Queues are merciful. In a queue you are nobody, a parcel in a line of parcels, and I studied my fellow parcels with the desperate attention of a drowning woman studying flotsam: a commercial traveller with sample cases; an elderly clergyman reading *The Times*; a young mother with a basket and a baby who screamed with the wholehearted fury of the very young; two men in cloth caps talking football, their laughter easy and unguarded, and how I envied them, how I hated them a little.

The sergeant worked slowly. He opened the commercial traveller's cases and ran his hands along the linings without hurrying. He weighed the clergyman's bag in one hand and set it down again. At the mother he was almost gentle — he touched the baby's blanket with one finger and waved her through with something that might have been a smile. The football men passed with barely a glance. And all the while the queue shortened, one body at a time, until there were two ahead of me, and then one, and the stone in my chest had grown so heavy I could feel my own pulse in my ears, in my wrists, in the roots of my teeth.

I had the book in the suitcase, wrapped in a petticoat, laid flat beneath two blouses and a nightdress. On top of everything, deliberately placed where a first flip of the hand would find it, sat my sewing — a half-finished stocking, needles crossed in the wool, thoroughly domestic, thoroughly boring. Edith had taught me that trick too. *Let them find the knitting first. A man who has found knitting has already decided what kind of woman you are.*

"Next."

My name, if he asked it, was Mrs. Aldous. My aunt lived in Stockport and was poorly. I had rehearsed these facts until they sat in my mouth like coins, worn smooth.

I set the suitcase on the trestle table and stepped back and folded my hands, and waited to be bored.

"Purpose of travel, madam?"

"Visiting my sister in Manchester." Not the aunt — I had changed it in the night, deciding a sister sounded less funereal than an aunt, and now I panicked briefly, wildly, wondering whether the change had been wise, whether some clerk somewhere had a list of sisters, whether—

"Return ticket?"

"Single. My sister will see me home." Another coin, worn smooth. He grunted and turned to the case.

He unbuckled the straps. He lifted the lid. And then — God help me — he stopped, and looked at me over the suitcase, and said:

"Travelling light, madam."

It was not a question and it was not friendly. It was a hook dropped into still water, and every instinct I possessed screamed at me to fill the silence, to explain, to justify — *yes, I travel light, I've always travelled light, my husband says—* — and I bit down on all of it and gave him a small, tired shrug, the shrug of a woman who has heard this observation from porters and husbands all her life and finds it neither interesting nor new.

He watched me a moment longer. Then he went into the case.

He took out the sewing first, exactly as designed. He turned the stocking over in his big hands, noted the needles, set it aside. He lifted out the blouses one at a time and shook each with a snap that made the linen crack like a small whip, and every snap went through me like a blow, though my face — I checked it constantly, from the inside — my face remained the face of a woman waiting for a train. He shook out the petticoat. Under it lay the book.

*Discourses on Moral Duty.* Grey boards, cracked spine, fourpence worth of other people's virtue. He picked it up and I watched his thumb move along the pages' edge, that instinctive reader's gesture, fanning them — and the fan stopped a fraction short of the cavity, because the false leaves were pasted down and gave nothing away to a thumb, and the Sergeant's frown deepened by a degree that had nothing to do with suspicion and everything to do with a book that would not riffle properly, a book that felt, to a man who had handled ten thousand books, subtly wrong.

Open it, I begged him silently. Open it and see sixty pages of moral duty and be satisfied. Or open it and feel the hollow under your fingers and drag me out of this queue in front of God and everyone, and everything ends here on a trestle table beside a screaming baby, and Nell waits at a window for a woman who does not come back on Saturday.

He opened it at random. His eyes went down the page.

"'...it is the duty of every man to examine his conscience nightly,'" the sergeant read aloud, without conviction, "'for the unexamined conscience breeds complacency, and complacency breeds sin.'"

"Amen," said the elderly clergyman behind me, who had lowered his newspaper.

The sergeant glanced back at him. Something in the exchange — the boredom meeting the boredom, the whole grey tide of ordinary Thursday morning closing over the moment — broke whatever spell the book had cast on him. He closed the covers. He weighed it once in his hand, that weighing gesture I had already seen him give the clergyman's bag, the gesture of a man trusting his palm over his eyes.

And his palm, thank God, thank God, trusted wrong. The weight was right; Edith had seen to that, replacing the cut pages with the wrapped ledger until the balance matched within an ounce. The weight was right and the cover was dull and the morning was long and there were forty more suitcases behind mine, and Inspector Crake's special order had not specified books, because Crake did not yet know about books.

He dropped it back in the case. He replaced the petticoat, the blouses, the sewing, working faster now, the thoroughness draining out of him as the queue behind me lengthened. He buckled the straps. He stamped a chalk mark on the lid — a small white X, the most beautiful letter of the alphabet ever devised — and jerked his chin toward the barrier.

"Platform nine. Mind the gap at the carriage step, madam."

"Thank you, Sergeant."

I walked. I did not run; running is for the guilty and the late, and I was neither. I walked the way water flows downhill, naturally, inevitably, my hand light on the suitcase handle, and behind me the sergeant called *"Next!"* in the same tired voice, and the queue closed over the space where I had been as though I had never existed.

Only at the barrier, when the collector clipped my ticket and the platform's roar of steam and shouting swallowed me whole, did my knees inform me what my face had been refusing to tell them. I gripped a luggage trolley. I breathed. A porter asked if I was quite well, ma'am, and I said yes, thank you, the smoke, and he nodded, everyone blaming the smoke, the smoke excusing everything.

The train pulled out at 8:40 exactly. I had a seat facing the engine, third class, a fat man asleep opposite with his newspaper rising and falling on his chest. Through the window London began to slide away — backs of houses, yards, a church spire, the gasworks — and somewhere under the city behind me Edith Marlowe stood at an upstairs window counting Crake's men, and somewhere in a maid's room a child slept with her door open a crack, waiting for Saturday.

I took the carnation from my suitcase handle and held it in my lap the whole way to Rugby.

It was at Crewe, three hours later, that I learned the sergeant at Euston had been the easy part. The train slowed, and slowed again, and a voice in the corridor called out in a tone I had not heard before — not the bored bark of Euston but something crisper, younger, more awake — and boots began moving down the carriage, compartment by compartment, stopping at every door.

*"Luggage inspection. All passengers for the North remain seated."*

The fat man woke with a snort. Outside the window, Crewe's forest of signals stood black against a low sky, and the boots came closer, unhurried, methodical, and stopped outside our compartment door.
