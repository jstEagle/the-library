## 14. What Crake Knows

The map was the first thing Inspector Ambrose Crake saw every morning when he unlocked his office at Scotland Yard, because he had pinned it to the wall opposite his desk where the official portrait of the Commissioner ought to hang. He had moved the portrait himself, on a Tuesday in March, and no one had said a word about it — not the clerks who came and went with their ledgers, not the superintendent who signed his expenses, not even the Commissioner's own secretary, who had once remarked that the wall looked "very purposeful now." That was the thing about ambition done properly: it did not announce itself. It simply rearranged the furniture and waited for everyone to accept the new arrangement as natural.

The map showed London south of the river, drawn in Crake's own hand — he trusted no draughtsman's copy of anything that mattered — with ink lines radiating outward from a single point: the corner of Holloway Road where a courier named Thomas Pell had stopped walking on the night of the ninth of June.

Crake stood before it now with his hands clasped behind his back and let the lines speak to him. They spoke in the language he loved best, which was geometry. A man's life, reduced to intersecting strokes of iron-gall ink.

"Tell me again," he said without turning.

Sergeant Dilkes sat by the window with his notebook open on his knee, a heavy man who sweated through his collars by ten o'clock and had long since stopped apologising for it. He read from his notes in the flat recitative voice Crake required of him — no colour, no interpretation, only fact laid end to end like bricks.

"Pell left the Gaiety Restaurant at half past eight. Witness — the head waiter — puts him carrying a leather dispatch case, brown, brass corners. He walked north up Chancery Lane. At Holborn he took an omnibus as far as King's Cross. From there—" Dilkes turned a page, licked his thumb, turned it back. "From there we lose him for eleven minutes."

"And find him again where?"

"Holloway Road. Outside the shuttered premises of a chandler's shop. Two witnesses — a cab driver sheltering from rain under the awning of the Crown, and a woman at a first-floor window across the way. Both heard voices raised. The cab driver says three men set upon him. The woman says four, but she also says she'd taken laudanum for toothache, so we discount her arithmetic."

"We discount nothing," Crake said mildly. "We weigh it."

He crossed to his desk, which was bare except for what the desk of a careful man ought to bear: blotter, inkwell, a single folder bound in grey card. He opened the folder and regarded its contents with the satisfaction other men took from brandy or women. Photographs, six of them, taken by a man he paid out of an allowance that appeared nowhere in any budget — photographs of the pavement where Pell had died, of the chandler's shuttered window, of the alley mouth into which the body had been dragged before the river police found it two days later, bloated and pocketless, in the tidal mud below Limehouse.

Pocketless. That detail had been reported in the newspapers as ordinary robbery. The *Star* had given it four lines on page seven between a fire in Bethnal Green and a dog show at the Agricultural Hall. Robbery, the papers said, of a clerk.

But clerks, Crake knew, were not robbed of their boots as well. And clerks did not carry dispatch cases that were never recovered. And above all — this was the brick upon which he intended to build his whole edifice — clerks did not walk from King's Cross toward Holloway at nine o'clock at night unless someone was waiting for them there.

"Eleven minutes," he repeated. "King's Cross to Holloway is twenty minutes' walk in dry weather, longer in rain. So either our man loitered, or he met someone, or he was met." He tapped the map, at the point where his ink line bent eastward off the direct route. "And here is what interests me, Sergeant. He did not go straight. He cut down Caledonian Road and came at the chandler's shop from the side street, which adds six minutes to any journey and makes sense only if he was delivering something and did not wish to be seen approaching from the front."

Dilkes wrote this down, though Crake had said it twice before. Dilkes always wrote everything down; it was why Crake kept him. A man who forgot things made a poor instrument. A man who remembered them all made a fine one, provided he could be made to forget the things his superior preferred forgotten.

"Now," Crake said, and drew from the folder the paper that mattered most: a plain sheet listing names in his own cramped hand. "The chandler's shop. Leasehold, held since 1909 by one Josiah Meech, who also holds — note the coincidence, Sergeant, hold it up to the light and admire it — a small shareholding in the Amalgamated Cartage Company. Which company, since January of this year, has held the contract for cartage of departmental stores to the Office of Works. Whose Undersecretary is—"

"Halloran," Dilkes said.

Crake let the name hang in the room. He never said it loudly. Names like that were not to be spoken loudly, the way one did not strike a match near gunpowder; but neither did he flinch from it, and that, he believed, was the difference between himself and the run of policemen, who would have filed Pell's death under robbery and gone to lunch. The run of policemen saw a dead clerk. Crake saw a thread, and he pulled threads the way other men prayed — daily, patiently, in the faith that something would eventually unravel.

Halloran had summoned him in April. Not through channels — a note in a private hand, delivered by a private messenger, inviting Crake to a club in Pall Mall where inspectors were not members and were noticed for not being members, which was rather the point. They had talked for forty minutes beside a window that looked out on nothing but a blank party wall, and Halloran had said perhaps thirty words in total, and by the end of it Crake had understood his commission perfectly: find the suffragettes' connection to the bombing at the Treasury annexe before the papers connected anything else, and be thorough, and be discreet, and be rewarded.

Discreet. The word had been chosen carefully. Not *quick*. Not *just*. Discreet — meaning that whatever Crake found should arrive at its destination shaped exactly as the sender wished.

Crake had accepted, of course. One did not decline such invitations; declining them had consequences of the quiet sort. But he would be dishonest with himself — and self-honesty was the one luxury he permitted — if he claimed he accepted only from prudence. There was more in it than that. Crake was fifty-one years old, the son of a Birmingham screw-maker who had died with blackened lungs and a ledger of debts, and he had spent thirty years watching lesser men rise past him on the strength of family, or charm, or the simple accident of having been born on the right side of some deputy commissioner's dinner table. He had solved murders those men could not have found with a map and a lantern. His commendations filled a drawer. Commendations, he had learned, were the currency paid to men meant to remain where they were.

This affair was different. This affair came with a patron whose reach extended into the Home Office, and a scandal whose shape — once he fixed it, once he nailed it fast — would require a man of proven discretion to administer. There would be a promotion in it. Perhaps a knighthood, eventually; Crake permitted himself to think the word only at night, alone, the way a miser permits himself to count.

"Sergeant," he said, "the child."

Dilkes turned pages. "Still nothing from the hospitals or the workhouses. The orphanages are slower — the matrons don't like being questioned, they think we're after their funding irregularities." A grunt that might have been amusement. "But the railway people came through. King's Cross lost-property office. A porter remembers a girl, seven or eight years old, travelling alone on the evening of the tenth, put off at—"

"At Highbury," Crake said. "I know. I interviewed the porter myself on Saturday. You may move on to the coat."

"The coat. The pawnbroker on Essex Road took in a child's coat, grey, good cloth, on the eleventh. Pawned by a woman he describes as 'quality, slumming it,' who gave the name Mrs. Green and an address that turns out to be a coal merchant's yard." Dilkes looked up. "You want the coat traced forward?"

"I want it traced everywhere." Crake returned to the map and studied the district around Highbury Corner, where his pencil marks clustered thickest. Within a quarter mile of the point where a child had stepped off a train alone — a child, mark it, who had been put on that train by someone who wanted her far away from something — there lived perhaps forty thousand souls, and among them, somewhere, was a household that had received her.

He had been hunting the household for six days, and this morning, over breakfast, the hunt had closed its jaws.

He took from the folder a cutting — not from a newspaper, for newspapers printed what he allowed them to print these days, but from the programme of a public meeting held at the Alexandra Palace in May, a meeting addressed by speakers of the Women's Social and Political Union. The programme listed the platform party. Among the names: **Mrs. Edith Marlowe**, described in the breathless style of such productions as "the celebrated novelist and orator, whose letters from prison have moved the nation."

Marlowe. The name had been in his files since April, of course; she was on every list Halloran's people cared about, prominent enough to be useful as an example. But prominence was not evidence, and Crake dealt in evidence. It had been the *address* that woke him at four o'clock that morning, sitting bolt upright in bed with his wife muttering beside him, reaching for the pencil he kept tied to the bedpost like a weapon.

Mrs. Marlowe's residence, according to rate books he had consulted before dawn in the vestry hall while the clerk still smelled of gin: a house on Milner Square, Islington.

Milner Square lay within eleven minutes' walk of the chandler's shop where Thomas Pell had died.

Eleven minutes. The same eleven minutes unaccounted for in a courier's last journey. Crake did not believe in coincidence; belief in coincidence was the superstition of lazy detectives, their equivalent of the peasant's fear of ghosts. Eleven minutes was not a ghost. Eleven minutes was a door, and behind it stood a house, and in that house —

In that house lived a woman who received visitors at all hours, who employed servants, who drove about London in a motor car — the neighbours had mentioned the motor car, a Darracq, dark green, kept at a livery stable off Liverpool Road — and who had, on the twelfth of June, according to the same neighbours, taken delivery of nothing, sent out nothing unusual, and behaved in every respect like a woman with nothing on her mind.

Which was itself interesting. People with nothing on their minds were rarely observed so carefully remembering to seem unremarkable. But it was the servant girl from next door, interviewed over a shared bag of cherries by Dilkes, who had given Crake the sentence he had been waiting five days to hear.

*"There's a child there now. Come sudden-like, before my mistress was up. Nobody knows whose."*

A courier killed on the ninth. A child put alone on a train on the tenth. A house eleven minutes from the killing that received a child of unknown provenance on the eleventh or twelfth. And a dispatch case that had never been found.

Crake stood very still before his map, and felt the particular cold pleasure that came perhaps twice in a decade — the pleasure of watching scattered facts snap together like the blades of a lock. The child was not incidental. Children were never incidental; children were luggage, in a manner of speaking, and where there was abandoned luggage there had been a hasty departure, and haste implied fear, and fear implied knowledge. Someone had killed Thomas Pell in view of that child. Someone had then spent a day and a night deciding what to do about the fact, and had decided — this was the part Crake admired, professionally, almost against himself — to spend money and risk exposure rather than murder a little girl. Sentiment, or caution. With the quality it was usually caution: dead children brought detectives with warrants; missing children merely brought constables with forms.

It did not matter. Either way, the child had seen. And the child, if she had seen, could describe. And if she could describe, then whoever held her held the last living witness to a murder that certain powerful persons wished recorded as robbery — and possibly held something more. Dispatch cases did not vanish. Their contents travelled.

He thought of Halloran's face across the club table, that smooth ministerial blankness, and thought: you know what was in the case. You know it the way I know the shape of a lock by the scratches on its key. And you are afraid, and your fear has hired me, and my price is not money.

His price was the wall where the Commissioner's portrait used to hang.

"Sergeant," Crake said, turning from the map at last, "we will do this properly, because properly is how it must look. I shall apply to the magistrate this afternoon for a warrant to search the Marlowe residence — grounds being the investigation of a fatal assault, the tracing of stolen property, and the welfare of a minor child believed unlawfully held." He smiled without warmth. "The child is the key that opens every door. No magistrate on earth refuses a warrant sought in the interest of a lost little girl. We go in as guardians, Sergeant. We leave as whatever we choose to be."

"And if the woman's not home?"

"Then we wait. Or better—" he paused, savouring it— "we make ourselves agreeable to whoever *is* home. Servants talk. Drivers talk. And I am told Mrs. Marlowe keeps a young woman who drives her motor car." He had a description already, third-hand: tallish, quiet, watchful eyes, the sort who listened at doors because listening was cheaper than asking questions. He would want a word with her eventually. Listeners always knew where the bodies — or the dispatch cases — were buried.

He sat down at his desk and drew a fresh sheet toward him and began the warrant application in his small, exact, unforgiving hand. Each line placed like a brick. *Thomas Pell, clerk, deceased. Property unaccounted for. Child, female, approx. seven years, unregistered presence at premises of E. Marlowe, Milner Square. Proximity of said premises to locus of fatal assault: 0.4 miles.*

Halfway down the page he stopped, pen lifted, and allowed himself the day's one indulgence: he pictured the morning, some weeks hence, when a senior officer — not the Commissioner, something higher, something with a title — would shake his hand in a corridor and say *Crake, isn't it? The Marlowe business. Thorough work.* He pictured the new office, larger, with a window that faced the river. He pictured his wife's face. He pictured, though he would have denied it under oath, his father's face — the screw-maker coughing his lungs out in a back room in Birmingham, saying *you'll get on, lad, you've got the head for it* — and getting on being, at last, this.

Ambition, Crake reflected, was like the tide: it seemed gentle right up until the moment it had you entirely.

He finished the application, blotted it, sealed it, and handed it to Dilkes with instructions to take a cab to the magistrate's chambers and to say nothing to anyone on the way — nothing, the word repeated with weight, because word had reached him that morning, through a channel he paid for precisely so that it would reach him first, of something that had tightened his jaw and quickened his pulse.

A telegram intercept. Routine — Halloran's people had arranged access to certain wires months ago, and Crake skimmed the traffic the way a fisherman skims a river, patient for the silver flash. Last night's flash had come at eleven: a Manchester newspaperman, one Silas Renwick of the *Northern Courier*, known to hold advanced views and better sources, had booked himself onto the Friday express from Euston and had wired his office a single line:

*EXPECT ME WITH SOMETHING THAT WILL WAKE ENGLAND. FRIDAY WITHOUT FAIL.*

Something that would wake England. Carried south-to-north, or north-to-south, along the very line of rail that passed through Crewe, through Stockport, through the districts where Crake's borrowed men already stood watching platforms with their collars up.

Friday. Three days hence.

Crake rose, took his hat, and paused at the door to look back at the map one final time — at the web of ink converging, all of it, on a square in Islington where a green motor car stood ready and a child nobody owned slept behind a respectable door.

"Dilkes," he said softly. "After the magistrate, telephone the station masters at Crewe and Stockport. Tell them Inspector Crake's compliments, and that from tonight, every passenger on the Manchester expresses travels under our eyes." He settled the hat brim low over his eyes, and the smile beneath it had nothing of kindness in it at all. "Especially," he added, "any lady travelling alone."
