## 15. Edith's Sacrifice Planned

The house on Haverstock Hill had gone quiet in the way houses do when they are pretending to sleep. Edith Marlowe stood at the drawing-room window with the lamp unlit, watching the street below, where a man in a grey cap had now walked past four times without once looking at the houses. That was how she knew he was looking at them. Amateurs looked at doors. Professionals looked at nothing at all.

Behind her, Bram Vane came down the stairs with the particular softness of a woman who has spent years learning not to wake anyone — first her father, then landladies, then, Edith suspected, the whole indifferent world. She carried two cups of tea and did not ask whether Edith wanted one. That was one of the things Edith had come to value about her driver: she did not perform her attentions. She simply performed them.

"You've seen him," Bram said quietly.

"The grey cap. Yes." Edith took the cup. It was hot enough to hurt, which was useful. "He's been there since half nine. Crake's work, I should think. He wants to know our hours."

"Then we shouldn't be having this conversation here."

"We shouldn't have been having it anywhere," Edith said. "And yet here we are, and there is a child asleep upstairs who saw a man murdered, and there is a ledger sewn into the lining of your winter coat, and on Friday morning the *Manchester Guardian* will either hold the truth or Halloran will hold us. So we will have the conversation, Miss Vane, in my darkened sitting room, like conspirators in a bad play, because that is what we have become."

Bram said nothing to that. She sat down across from Edith with her cup balanced on her knee, and in the light from the street lamp through the curtains her face had that stillness Edith had learned to read over three years of shared roads and shared silences — the stillness of a woman assembling an argument she has not yet decided to make.

Edith let her assemble it. Patience was a weapon she had sharpened against Parliament for twenty years; she could spare some of its edge for a driver.

Finally Bram said: "You've been thinking about Friday."

"I think about everything. It's the curse of my profession."

"No. You've been thinking about what happens if it goes wrong." Bram set the cup down on the side table with a small precise click. "I've watched you all evening. You do that thing with your hands when you're rehearsing something you don't want to say out loud."

Edith glanced down. Her hands were folded, quite still now, but she supposed they had not been still before. "You know me too well. That will be inconvenient for both of us someday."

"It's already inconvenient."

"Yes." Edith rose and went to the mantel, where a framed photograph of Christabel Pankhurst stood beside a chipped bust of some Roman whose name she had never bothered to learn. She turned the photograph slightly, though it needed no turning. "Then let me say it plainly, since you've given me no graceful way around it. If someone must be caught carrying that ledger — if the checkpoints close and the net comes down and someone must stand in a dock and answer for it — then it must be me."

* * *

She heard Bram's breath change before she heard her voice. Three years of driving a woman through hostile streets teaches a person to listen with more than ears.

"No," Bram said.

"You haven't heard the reasoning."

"I don't need the reasoning. No."

"Bram—"

"It should be me." Bram was on her feet now, and her voice had that low, controlled heat that Edith had heard exactly twice before — once outside Holloway prison when a wardress had struck a girl of nineteen, and once in the kitchen at midnight after Nell had arrived, when Bram had held the oilcloth packet as though it might detonate. "I'm nobody. I'm a chauffeur. I don't give speeches. My name has never been in a newspaper except once, in a list, spelled wrong. If they stop me, they stop a woman with a forged railway warrant and a coat full of paper. They fine me. Perhaps they hold me a week. But if they stop *you*—"

"They stop Edith Marlowe," Edith finished. "Yes. Precisely. Say the rest of it."

"I won't say it because I don't believe it."

"Belief is not required. Only accuracy." Edith turned from the mantel. The street lamp put a pale bar across Bram's shoulders, and beyond her, upstairs, Edith could hear the faint creak of the floorboard outside Nell's room — Mrs. Quill making her rounds, or perhaps only unable to sleep, as none of them were sleeping. "Say it anyway. I find that arguments survive better in daylight than in the dark of other people's kindness."

Bram's jaw tightened. For a moment Edith thought she would refuse — Bram could be muleish in her silences, a quality Edith ordinarily found restful and tonight found maddening — but then she spoke, flatly, like a clerk reading charges.

"If they catch you, it's headlines. Your photograph in every paper from Land's End to John o' Groats. 'Militant leader arrested with seditious documents.' It confirms every story Halloran has planted. The bomb, the conspiracy, the violence — suddenly there's proof, isn't there, because *she* was carrying it. You'd hand them the narrative gift-wrapped. Whereas me—" Her voice caught, and she steadied it. "Whereas me, I'm a footnote. Nobody prints footnotes."

"That is exactly the argument," Edith said. "You've made it well. Now let me make the other half, the half you're leaving out because it doesn't suit you."

"There isn't another half."

"There is always another half. Politics is nothing but halves." Edith crossed to the window and parted the curtain a finger's width. The grey-capped man had stopped under the plane tree opposite, smoking, his face tipped up toward the window of number eleven, where old Mrs. Farrow kept canaries. He would stand there twenty minutes. They had time. "Listen to me, Bram, and listen properly, because this may be the most important thing I ever say to you, and I intend never to repeat it.

"A famous name is not only a target. It is also a shield made of public attention. If I am arrested, ten thousand women will ask why. Newspapers will ask why. Members of Parliament who have spent years avoiding me will be forced to say my name aloud in the Commons, and some of them will enjoy saying it, and saying it will commit them. Attention is oxygen, and I am very good at breathing it. A scandal that attaches to Edith Marlowe becomes a question the government cannot quietly bury. A scandal that attaches to an unknown chauffeur—" she turned, and met Bram's eyes, and did not soften, because softness now would be cruelty later — "becomes a file in a drawer. And files in drawers get lost, Bram, or get found again by worse men than Crake, and the woman attached to them disappears into Holloway for two years while the movement carries on as though she had never existed. I have seen it happen. I have written letters about girls it happened to. I will not write one about you."

"So your plan is to use yourself as a shield by standing in front of the bullet."

"My plan is to use myself as what I have always been. An instrument. I chose this instrument years ago, and it has a shape now, and the shape is useful." Edith returned to her chair and sat, arranging her hands deliberately in her lap where Bram could see them — still, composed, the hands of a woman who had addressed crowds of fifty thousand and taken eggs and stones from both. "You think I'm being noble. I'm not. I'm being tactical, and I want you to understand the difference, because nobility is a luxury and tactics are a discipline. If I go to prison, the cause gains a martyr and loses a voice — and I have trained voices behind me. Annie can speak. Ruth can speak. Half the women in this movement learned their sentences from watching me construct mine. The machine does not stop when I step off the wheel for eighteen months. It was built so that it wouldn't."

"And if they hang you?" Bram asked, and her voice cracked on it, just barely, just enough.

"They will not hang me. This isn't treason; it's possession of documents and whatever else Crake chooses to invent, and invented charges collapse under daylight, which is precisely why the ledger must reach Manchester regardless of what happens to its courier. But suppose the worst. Suppose they give me five years, seven. Cat-and-mouse, force-feeding, the whole apparatus." Edith lifted her chin. "I have been hungry for this country's attention for twenty years. I can be hungry in a plainer sense for a while longer."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be armor." Edith reached forward and took Bram's cold hands in her own, and felt the younger woman startle at the contact — Bram was not a woman who was touched often, and received touch the way some horses receive a strange saddle, with a stillness that was not entirely trust. "Duty is armor, Bram. It is the only armor that fits everyone. When the blows come — and they will come, on a platform at Euston or a road outside Stockport or a cell in Holloway — the woman wearing it thinks not *why me* but *this is the work*. And the work goes on. Do you see? I am not asking your permission. I am telling you the shape of Friday, so that when it arrives, neither of us wastes the moment hesitating."

Bram pulled her hands free — not roughly, but definitively — and stood, and walked to the window, and stood looking down at the grey-capped man as though she could burn a hole through him by concentration alone.

"You've already decided," she said at last.

"I decided the night Nell arrived. Before I opened the packet. Perhaps before I opened the door." Edith smiled faintly. "That is the privilege of age. One's decisions arrive ahead of one's knowledge of them, like bills."

"Then I'm deciding something too." Bram turned. In the half-dark her eyes were very bright, and Edith recognized the brightness and feared it, because she had seen it in young women on platforms and in prison yards and once, memorably, in a girl named Constance Lytton the week before Constance did the thing that ruined her health for good. It was the brightness of a decision arriving ahead of itself. "If it goes wrong — if they take you — I finish it. Me. I drive the ledger the rest of the way if I have to carry it in my teeth."

"That was always the arrangement."

"No. The arrangement was that I drive and you decide. I'm amending it. You decide, and I *finish*." Bram's voice dropped low and even. "Whatever you've planned for yourself, you've planned assuming I'll obey. And I will. I've obeyed you for three years and I'm not ashamed of it. But I'm not a soldier, Edith. Soldiers obey and forget. I'm a courier. Couriers deliver. Whatever happens to you happens *after* the delivery, and I intend to make certain of that order of events if it costs me everything you're so willing to spend."

For a long moment the room was silent except for the tick of the mantel clock and, far above, the small restless sounds of a child dreaming badly.

Edith found, to her surprise, that her throat was tight. Twenty years of oratory, and it took a chauffeur in a darkened sitting room to find the sentence she could not answer. She considered several replies — the witty deflection, the firm instruction, the warm dismissal — and discarded them all, because Bram would see through each one, and because lying to Bram Vane had somehow become, without Edith noticing the exact hour of it, a thing she no longer wished to do.

"Then we are agreed," she said finally. "On everything except the part that matters."

"On the part that matters, we're agreed too. We just disagree about who pays."

"One of us must."

"Or neither." Bram came back from the window. "You haven't planned for the possibility that it works. That I walk through all three checkpoints, the ledger reaches Manchester, the *Guardian* prints it Saturday morning, and Halloran resigns before Crake can serve a single warrant. You've planned your martyrdom so thoroughly you've forgotten to plan the victory."

Edith laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised, the first in days. "God help me, you sound like me at thirty."

"I sound like someone who intends to still be driving you in December." Bram retrieved her tea, cold now, and drank it anyway. "Which reminds me. The route. If I'm going Friday, I want to go over it again, every mile, every checkpoint, every timetable. And I want to change one thing."

"Oh?"

"The ledger travels loose pages in my luggage at Crewe — that's the weak point, you know it is, any inspector with a ruler finds a packet of photographs in ten seconds. I want it broken up. Distributed. Photographs in the hatbox, ledger pages sewn different places, dates memorized and nothing written. Let them find a piece and wonder if it means anything."

Edith studied her for a long moment — this quiet, watchful, self-effacing woman who had stood in her doorway three years ago with excellent references and an unreadable face, and who had somehow, somewhere along those three years of roads, become the person Edith trusted most in England.

"Do it as you think best," she said. "You're the courier."

Something moved through Bram's face at that — gratitude, and something older and sadder underneath it, like a stone surfacing in a pond — and Edith wondered, not for the first time, what history this woman thought she was merely observing.

* * *

Later, when Bram had gone up and the house had settled into its false sleep, Edith sat at her desk with the lamp lit low and wrote two letters.

The first was to Annie, brief and practical: instructions for the Friday rally, the wording of the resolution, a reminder about the new speakers' fund. Nothing that would interest an inspector. She sealed it and set it aside for the morning post.

The second took longer. It was addressed to the general secretary of the WSPU, and it was not to be posted unless word came that she had been taken. In it she set out, in her clearest hand, the location of duplicate papers, the names of three witnesses to Halloran's movements, and the argument — fully drafted, ready for the press — that would turn her arrest from a triumph for the government into a catastrophe. She wrote the way she built speeches: every clause load-bearing, no ornament that could not survive rough handling.

At the bottom, before signing, she paused. Then she added a final paragraph:

*If I am detained, the work proceeds without sentiment. Sentiment is for evenings such as this one. Tell Miss Vane she is not to blame herself, tell her she drove magnificently, and tell her that if she ever reads this letter it is because I wanted her to — which, given her habits with my correspondence, seems likely.*

She signed it, blotted it, and sat for a while with her hand resting on the page.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Not Mrs. Quill's tread — lighter, quicker, moving toward the stairs rather than away. Edith blew out the lamp and listened as Bram came quietly down, crossed the hall, and let herself into the study where the route maps lay spread beneath a locked drawer. A match scratched. Lamplight showed in a thin line under the study door, and stayed there, and did not waver, for a long time.

Edith smiled in the dark, and went to bed, and slept better than she had any right to.

She would remember that sleep afterward, in a cell with a straw mattress, as the last easy night for a long while — though of course she did not know it yet, and neither did the woman downstairs, tracing the line to Crewe with one fingertip, over and over, as though repetition could make a railway safe.
