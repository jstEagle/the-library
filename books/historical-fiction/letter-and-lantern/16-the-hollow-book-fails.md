## 16. The Hollow Book Fails

The train to Crewe smelled of coal smoke and wet wool, and Bram Vane sat very still in a third-class compartment with her hands folded over a book that weighed more than any book should.

Outside the window, Cheshire unspooled in the grey light of a November morning — hedgerows beaded with rain, pastures where cattle stood hip-deep in mist, a church spire rising and falling behind bare trees like something surfacing for breath. She had made this journey a dozen times in her life without thinking of it at all. Today every mile felt counted, tallied, spent from some ledger she could not see but knew was being kept.

The ledger was in her luggage. Not all of it — Edith had been ruthless about that, dividing the photographs from the pages, sending Mrs. Quill's copies north by a different hand entirely — but what remained was enough to hang three men and save a movement, and it was sewn into the spine of *The Poetical Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning*, a volume Bram had bought second-hand on Fetter Lane for sixpence and then gutted with a razor blade by lamplight while Nell slept upstairs.

Forty-one leaves. That was what remained after the division. Forty-one leaves of Halloran's own arithmetic: payments initialed, dates matched to a courier's last known route, a column headed simply *disposal* in a clerk's careful hand. Bram had read them twice more before sewing them in, and each time the word *disposal* had sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She had chosen Browning deliberately. A woman's poetry, thick enough to justify its bulk, dull enough that no inspector would want it. She had rehearsed her answers on the platform at Euston until they sounded like truth rather than rehearsal: visiting an aunt in Manchester; the book a gift; no, she traveled alone; yes, she worked, she was a driver, she drove for a lady, nothing political, just driving.

*Nothing political. Just driving.* The lie of her whole adult life compressed into four words, and she had delivered it to a sergeant at Euston with steady hands and a heartbeat she could feel in her teeth.

Euston had passed. Crewe would not.

* * *

She knew it before the train stopped. There was a difference in the platform — a stillness among the porters, a knot of men in uniform near the foot of the ramp who were not checking tickets, because ticket-checkers move and these men stood like furniture. One of them held a clipboard. Another turned his collar up against the draught and shifted his weight from boot to boot with the patience of a man being paid by the hour.

Bram did not look at them directly. She looked at the departures board, at a woman wrestling a wicker trunk, at the rain silvering the glass roof — and she took inventory of herself the way Edith had taught her to take inventory of a room. Hands: cold but steady. Face: she pressed her tongue to the roof of her mouth and arranged whatever lived there into blankness. Luggage: one carpet bag, one hatbox, one book.

The compartment door rattled open before she was ready.

"Madam." The inspector was perhaps forty, with side whiskers trimmed close and eyes the pale grey of a winter sky — not unkind eyes, which was worse. Unkind eyes you could hate. These were the eyes of a man doing a job he was good at. "London to Manchester?"

"To Manchester. My aunt's. I'm expected."

He glanced at her ticket, nodded, and set it face-down on the seat beside him, which was not how ticket inspections went. "Mind if I have a look at your things? Routine. We've had reports of certain persons moving between London and the North."

"Certain persons," Bram repeated mildly.

"Suffragists, madam. Militant ones. There's been talk of trouble at a rally." He said it the way a man reads a weather report. His gaze had already gone to the carpet bag.

"Search anything you like," she said, and heard, distantly, how easily it came out — and hated that too, because ease was becoming a skill and skills like that changed a person. She lifted the bag down and opened it under his eye: nightdress, spare collar, brush and comb wrapped in a towel, a tin of barley sugar, the hatbox with a single plain hat.

He went through it all with two fingers, quick and thorough, shaking each item as though it might confess. The towel. The sugar tin — he opened it, counted the sweets with a glance, closed it. Then his attention moved to the seat beside her.

To the book.

"That yours as well?"

"It is." She kept her voice level, almost bored. "A gift for my aunt. She's fond of poetry."

"Mm." He picked it up.

Bram watched him weigh it in his hand and understood, in a single lurching instant, everything she had failed to consider. The book was heavy. Books are heavy, yes — but this one was heavy *wrong*, dense through the middle like a brick, and a man who handled luggage all day, who shook out towels and weighed sugar tins, would know the weight of a book the way a butcher knows the weight of meat.

His thumb moved along the page edges. The cut pages of the hollow had been sanded and inked dark to mimic text-block, and at arm's length, in bad light, they passed. But this inspector did not hold it at arm's length. He brought it close to the lamp above the compartment door and tilted it, letting the light rake across the fore-edge, and Bram saw his eyes change — saw the mildness in them sharpen into interest, the way a terrier's whole body sharpens at a scent.

"Fond of poetry, your aunt?"

"Very."

"Hm. Thick volume." He fanned the pages gently with his thumb, and the sound they made was wrong — a soft, solid whisper instead of the dry riffle of paper — and Bram's mouth had gone dry as chalk. "You know, we've had instructions about books. Printed matter. Certain pamphlets get posted north in parcels, disguised as ordinary goods. Sedition travels well dressed as literature, apparently." He smiled to show it was nothing personal. "May I borrow a moment of your time at the table there? I'd like a proper look. It'll only take a minute."

Every plan she had made dissolved at once. In the lavatory, she had told herself, if it came to it — but there was no *it*, not yet, not here, not with him holding the book and her standing in a corridor of witnesses. She calculated distances the way she calculated traffic: the end of the carriage, the gap between trains, the water closet with its little lock and its little window too narrow for anything but regret.

"Of course," she said.

* * *

The corridor of a Midland Railway carriage is eighteen inches wide and smells of brass polish and other people's cigarettes. Bram walked ahead of the inspector with the carpet bag in one hand and her heart going like a piston rod, and she used those twelve feet of corridor to become someone else. Not Bram Vane, who had read stolen letters by lamplight and learned to love the view from inside history. Someone smaller. A niece. A woman with an aunt in Manchester and no opinions worth recording.

At the little fold-down table by the vestibule window, the inspector set the book down and drew on a pair of half-moon spectacles.

"Name?" he said, conversationally, opening the cover.

"Bram Vane."

"Address?"

She gave Edith's house — no. At the last syllable she swerved and gave her sister's address in Camden, invented three weeks ago for exactly this purpose, and prayed he had no reason to check. He wrote it down. He was writing everything down, and she realized with a chill that this was not a search so much as a record: names, addresses, destinations, all flowing north and south along wires to men whose paymasters she could name from memory, from forty-one leaves sewn in brown thread.

"Vane," he said. "Unusual."

"My father's fancy."

"Mm." He turned the first page. Sonnets from the Portuguese. His lips moved faintly — *How do I love thee? Let me count the ways* — and Bram thought, absurdly, wildly, *count them, count them all, keep counting and never reach the spine.*

Then the train slowed.

It was nothing — a signal check outside Crewe, the brakes sighing, carriages shuffling against their couplings — but the inspector glanced up at the window, and in that glance Bram saw her chance arrive wearing its worst possible face. Because when they stopped, another inspector would board. Two men at one table meant hands on the book from both sides, meant it laid flat under better light, meant the fore-edge examined page by patient page.

And she saw something else. The lavatory door at the end of the corridor stood open, waiting, empty, six steps away.

The train jolted to a halt. Steam hissed somewhere below like a kettle taken off the boil.

"Excuse me," Bram said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she covered it by pressing her hand briefly to her stomach. "The motion — I'm sorry — one moment—"

She was already moving. Behind her the inspector said something — *madam?* — half-rising, but a lady being ill was a problem every man on a railway had been trained since boyhood to flee from, and he hesitated precisely long enough. Six steps. Her hand caught the lavatory's brass latch. The door shut. The lock clicked, a small, cheap, miraculous sound.

The compartment was the size of a coffin standing up and smelled of carbolic and damp. Rain streaked the little frosted window. Below, the platform noise of Crewe rose up: whistles, shouts, the iron music of shunting wagons, boots on gravel.

She dragged the book from her bag with fingers gone clumsy, and the spine split along the stitching she had done herself, and the leaves slid out into her palm — forty-one pages of a minister's private arithmetic, soft as moth wings and heavy as sin.

There was no time. Through the frosted glass she could see the blurred shape of the inspector standing at the table, and another shape joining him, and the two shapes conferring, and one of them gesturing toward the lavatory.

Three pages. She could not burn forty-one — no time, no flame hot enough, and a scorched book in her possession was a confession. But three pages, torn small, fed to the little spirit stove—

She carried no stove. Of course she didn't. She was a courier, not a picnicker.

Her eye fell on the fixture below the window: the water tank, the copper pipe, and beneath it, bolted to the wall, the small gas jet that heated the wash water — a blue pilot flame no bigger than a thumbnail, burning day and night in ten thousand railway carriages, unnoticed by everyone except, at this moment, a woman kneeling on a lavatory floor with history in her hands.

*Disposal,* said the clerk's neat column in her memory. Very well. Disposal.

She tore the first leaf into strips the width of a ribbon. The pilot flame caught the paper reluctantly — paper wants to curl and blacken, not vanish — and the first strip went up in a bloom of orange with a whisper of ash, and she fed the next strip before the flame died, and the next, cupping her hand around the jet against the draught from the window. Smoke rose thin and acrid. The smell of burning paper in an enclosed space is not a subtle smell. It is a shout.

Page one: dates. Payments dated the week of the courier's death, initialed H.H., cross-referenced to a depot in Salford. Gone. Ash flaked down into the drain like grey moths.

Through the door, muffled: footsteps in the corridor. A knuckle rapping the wood. "Madam? Are you quite all right in there?"

"Quite — thank you — one moment—" Her voice came out high and strange. She coughed, elaborately, wetly, and heard the footsteps retreat a step, deterred by the universal terror of other people's illness.

Page two: names. Two of Halloran's men, named in full, named beside a date and beside the word *disposal*, and she had memorized them, she had made herself memorize them on the train from London, reciting them like a rosary — Aldous Finch. Sergeant-Major Reeve. She tore the leaf, fed it, watched the ink blister and go. The flame licked her fingertip and she bit down on the pain and kept feeding.

Smoke was pooling at the ceiling now, a grey ghost with nowhere to go. She wrenched the little window wider and the rain came in sideways, spotting the remaining pages, and she shielded them with her body as though they were a child.

Page three she hesitated over. It was the worst one and therefore the most necessary to lose: a memorandum in Halloran's own hand, initialled, referring to *the female agitators' forthcoming embarrassment* and authorizing expenditure *for services rendered in connection therewith*. Proof, in the minister's own letters, of intent. And proof that could not be allowed to sit in a police evidence locker in Crewe, catalogued and filed and quietly vanished, which is what would happen to it if this man opened the spine.

She thought of Edith saying, in the lamplit kitchen: *Whatever you must lose, lose it forward. Never let them take it — spend it yourself.*

She tore the memorandum into ribbons and gave it to the flame strip by strip, and the last of it curled away off her fingertips, and there was ash in the drain and smoke in the air and thirty-eight leaves left, hidden now beneath the false bottom of the hatbox, transferred in the four seconds it had taken the inspectors to confer — she had done it in the corridor, walking, one hand steadying the box, the pages flat against her ribs under her coat until the vestibule's blind angle gave her the moment.

No. That was not true and she knew it. They were still in the book. She had torn three pages free and burned them and the other thirty-eight lay in the gutted spine of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, six steps and one unlocked door away from two pairs of grey official hands.

The knock came again. Harder.

"Madam, I must ask you to open this door."

"One moment!" She flushed the lavatory — a great roaring theatrical rush of water — stuffed the book back into the carpet bag with the false bottom already gaping, slid the leaves home, pressed the flap down, and buried the book beneath the nightdress and the towel. Ash still clung to her fingers. She scrubbed them under the tap, scrubbed the smudge of soot from her cheekbone, dragged a wet handkerchief across her face, and let smoke pour past her out the door like a guilty secret given legs.

Then she unlocked it.

* * *

The corridor was full of steam-smell and burnt-paper smell and two inspectors' worth of suspicion.

The first one — hers, the one with the terrier eyes — stood with the clipboard man behind him, and both of them looked at the thin grey haze drifting along the ceiling with the expressions of men who had smelled many things on railways and liked none of them.

"Fire in the lavatory, madam?"

"The heater," Bram said. "I'm dreadfully sorry — I lit it — I was chilled through, and it spat, I think, or the paper of my handkerchief blew against it—" She produced the handkerchief, damp, and a charred corner of it that she had prepared in the four seconds between the flush and the unlock, tearing it against the copper edge of the tank. It was a good lie. It was even mostly adjacent to true. She held it out on her open palm like an offering, and her hand, God help her, did not shake, and she thought: *this is what I am now. This is what reading other people's letters has made me. A woman who can produce a burnt handkerchief on demand.*

The terrier-eyed inspector looked at the handkerchief. He looked at the scorch mark above the pilot flame — real, fresh, hers, a gift from the paper strips that had strayed. He looked, longest of all, at her face.

"You're not well," he said.

"No," Bram agreed. "I'm not."

He stepped aside to let her pass. It was that simple and that terrible — a nod, a half-step, a corridor suddenly open — and she gathered her bag and her hatbox and walked the length of it with her spine straight and her lungs full of smoke, and behind her she heard the younger inspector say, low: "Should've had a proper look at that book, sir. Heavy thing."

And the older one answer, tired, already turning away: "It's Browning, lad. Nobody smuggles Browning."

* * *

The Manchester train pulled out at 11:42. Bram sat in a compartment alone — she had chosen it, paid the difference, endured the conductor's glance — with the hatbox on her knees and the carpet bag at her feet, and for the first eleven miles she did not permit herself to move at all.

Then the landscape softened into the smoke-hazed outskirts of Stockport, and her hands began to shake, all at once, like a machine thrown into reverse, and she let them, because there was no one to see.

Thirty-eight leaves. She had crossed two checkpoints with the whole weight of the thing and come through on a joke about Browning and a burnt handkerchief, and the price had been three pages — the dates, the names, the memorandum. The heart of it, Edith would say. Or: the part most likely to be confiscated, spent in the only fire available, buying the rest passage. Loss that buys survival. Edith would say it without flinching, and mean it, and be right.

Bram found she could not yet say it at all. Her fingertips stung where the flame had kissed them, and every sting felt like a name — Finch, Reeve — going up in smoke that should have gone into print, into headlines, into the morning papers laid like indictments on a minister's breakfast table. She had burned evidence of murder to keep it from the murderers. There was probably a word for that kind of arithmetic. She suspected she would spend the rest of her life looking for it.

At Wilmslow the compartment door opened and an elderly clergyman settled opposite with a newspaper, and Bram composed her face into pleasant vacancy and stared out at the rain.

The paper was the *Manchester Guardian*. She read it upside down without meaning to, the way she had once read other people's letters — the old habit dying hard — and a small paragraph on the inner page reached up and took her by the throat:

**DISORDER EXPECTED AT FRIDAY RALLY.** *Police sources indicate heightened precautions... prominent militant speakers anticipated... Inspector Crake of the Special Branch said to be coordinating arrangements personally...*

Crake. In Manchester. Coordinating.

She read it three times. Outside, the rain came harder, and the telegraph wires beside the line dipped and sang, carrying — who knew what — toward the city where Edith sat in a journalist's office preparing arrest statements, where a child she loved slept under Quaker roofs, where a fixer with a photograph and a warrant request was closing like a hand.

Bram folded the newspaper down to its crease, very carefully, so the clergyman would think her merely tidy, and looked at her own reflection ghosted in the dark glass: a woman with smoke still faintly in her hair, thirty-eight leaves in a hatbox, and three hours left in which to deliver them.

Then, far back along the line, in a town she had already left behind, a knock was sounding on a quiet green door — though she could not have known it — and a small girl who trusted exactly one person in the world was looking at that door, and making up her mind.
