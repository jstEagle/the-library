## 17. Nell Runs

The Quaker house was called a Meeting House by the grown-ups, but Nell thought of it as the house with no pictures. There were no pictures on the walls and no pictures in the books and even the windows had plain curtains, white as milk, so that when the sun came through them it made shapes on the floor that moved all day like quiet animals she was not allowed to name.

Her name here was not Nell. Here she was *the child*, or sometimes, when the kind grey woman with the soft hands spoke to her, she was *dear one*. Dear one, will you take some bread? Dear one, are you warm enough? Dear one, you needn't tell us anything until you wish to.

Nell did not wish to. Wishing was for things like sweets and going home, and home was a word with a hole burned through the middle of it.

She had been at the Meeting House four days. She knew because she counted, the way Papa had taught her to count — one knot in the string for each day, tied small and tight in the corner of her cardigan where nobody looked. Four knots. At home there would have been five knots by now, because Fridays Mama let her have a penny for toffee and toffee-day came after church-day and this morning had been church-day, only without church, just sitting still on a hard bench while people said nothing at all for a whole hour.

Saying nothing for a whole hour was called Meeting, and it was supposed to be God listening. Nell decided God could listen better if He also gave her something to do, so she sat and counted the cracks in the ceiling instead. Eleven. Eleven cracks and four knots and no Mama and no Papa and one doll named Josephine who was the only person in the world who already knew everything.

*You must mind Josephine,* Miss Marlowe had told her, crouching down so their eyes were level, the way no grown-up ever bothered except her. Miss Marlowe had a voice like a bell rung indoors, big but kept careful. *Whatever happens, Josephine goes where you go. Can you promise me that?*

Nell had promised. Promising was easy. It was the keeping that made your chest hurt, like carrying water in your hands.

Josephine sat now on the narrow bed beside the window in the little back room they had given her, propped against the pillow with her painted face turned to the door. Her hair was real human hair sewn on strand by strand, brown like Papa's, and her left boot had come off somewhere between the house in London and the train and nobody had found it and Nell had cried about that exactly once, quietly, into the pillow, because crying twice felt like forgetting.

*Forget the face.* That was what the note had said. The woman from before — the tall one with the quick hands who drove the motorcar, the one whose coat smelled of petrol and lavender — had read it out loud and then gone very still, the way a cat goes still when it hears something under the floorboards.

Nell hadn't forgotten the face. That was the trouble. She tried every night. She shut her eyes and pushed at it the way you push at a splinter, and it wouldn't come out. The man's face, the one in the lane behind the coal yard, lit up yellow from below by his own lantern so his eyebrows made black caves and his mouth was just a hole with teeth in it when he said *pick it up, girl, go on, pick it up and don't look at him.*

But she had looked. She had looked at Papa lying in the wet with his hat rolled away from his head like a wheel that had come off, and his eyes open at the sky as if the sky owed him something, and she had looked, and looking was how the face got inside her where the note couldn't reach it.

*Dear one,* the grey woman had said the first evening, sitting very close, *you are safe here. Do you know what safe means?*

Nell had known. Safe meant the door was locked. Safe meant the lanterns were out. Safe meant nobody knocked.

* * *

On the fourth night, somebody knocked.

It was late. Nell knew late because the house made different noises late — the stairs ticked as they cooled, and somewhere below a clock cleared its throat before striking, and the wind got into the chimney and talked in its sleep. She was awake anyway. She was always awake at this part of the night, the part when the lantern light came back behind her eyelids, yellow and swinging.

The knock was soft. Two knuckles, twice. Not the knock of someone arriving. The knock of someone checking.

Nell sat up in the dark. Josephine tipped over gently against her arm. Below, floorboards shifted — the grey woman's room was at the front, and the sound came from the back, near the kitchen, near the door that opened onto the garden where the apple tree stood crooked as an old man listening.

The knock again. Softer. Almost polite.

Then a voice, low, through the wood. Nell couldn't hear the words. She didn't need the words. Voices were like weather; you knew rain coming before the first drop. This voice was the voice of men in lanes. This voice was the voice that had said *don't look at him* while she looked.

Her heart began doing the thing it did, fast and high, like a bird shut in a room beating at the window. Papa's bird had done that once, before Papa opened his hands and let it go. Nobody was going to open any hands tonight.

She heard the grey woman's door open far away at the front of the house. Heard slow feet on stairs, unhurried, the feet of a woman who believed locked doors were enough. And Nell understood something then with the whole cold length of her body, the way children understand things, all at once and forever: the knocking was not for the grey woman. The grey woman didn't matter. The knocking was a question, and the question was *is she here*, and the answer was yes, yes, she was here, she was the only thing in the whole plain house worth finding.

They had found the last place too. They had found Papa in a place he thought was secret.

Nell put her feet on the cold boards. Josephine went under her arm, head first, the way you carry a loaf. The window stuck — it always stuck, the grey woman had laughed about it, saying damp and honesty were much alike, both swelling in winter — but fear is a lever older than any hinge, and the sash gave with a groan Nell clapped her hand over as though the sound were a bird she could hold still.

Cold air. Wet grass smell. The garden ran down to a wall, and beyond the wall, if you climbed the coal heap the boys used, there was the lane, and beyond the lane the town, and beyond the town — she didn't know. Beyond the town was wherever the tall woman with the lavender-and-petrol coat was. Because that was the arithmetic Papa had left her, the only sum she trusted: *bad men find places, but that woman finds you.* She had come for Nell once, out of all the doors in London. She had held her hand across a street once, gripping hard at the traffic, and her grip had been the safest thing Nell had ever felt, safer than walls, safer than locked doors, because it was a grip that wanted to be holding on.

Below, the back door latch lifted.

Nell went over the sill.

* * *

The drop was farther than it looked. She landed badly, ankle turning on the flowerbed edge, and bit her cry in half and swallowed it, and lay in the wet cabbage leaves with Josephine crushed against her chest, listening.

The kitchen door was open. Lamplight spilled out onto the flags in a long yellow tongue, and in the light stood a man, and Nell saw him sideways between the beanpoles: a dark coat, a bowler held in one hand like a plate he'd been offered and refused, a face she knew.

Not the lantern face. Worse. A tidy face, patient, the face of a man who waited outside schools. He had been at the station once — she was nearly sure, nearly — watching the barrier with his hat low, and Miss Marlowe had steered her away by the shoulder without hurrying, which was how you knew it was serious.

The grey woman stood in her nightclothes with a candle, saying something calm. The tidy man said something back, soft. Neither of them sounded frightened yet, which meant it wasn't finished, which meant Nell should be farther away than a flowerbed.

She crawled. Cabbage leaf to cabbage leaf, beanpole to beanpole, Josephine's painted cheek pressed to her collarbone. The wall at the bottom of the garden was shoulder-high and topped with broken glass that glittered dully in the starlight like teeth that had given up. But the apple tree leaned against it, crooked old man that he was, and Papa had taught her trees. *Up you go, Nel, mind the dead branch, never trust a dead branch.* Her hands remembered before she did. Bark bit her palms. The dead branch held. She went over the glass by putting her foot where the tree had snapped the top course flat, and dropped onto the coal heap, and slid, and was in the lane.

Then she ran.

She ran the way rabbits run, in bursts and freezes, because Papa had shown her that too — *a rabbit that runs straight runs into teeth, Nel* — darting hedge to hedge, stopping with her heart slamming to listen, going on. Behind her the Meeting House stayed dark except the kitchen, and no one shouted her name, because nobody in the world knew her name anymore except Josephine and the tall woman, and neither of them was shouting.

The lane came out by the gasworks, where the great round tanks stood up against the sky like iron moons fallen over and left. She knew gasworks. Gasworks meant railways; railways meant yards; yards meant wagons, hundreds of them, coupled up in the dark like sleeping animals, breathing rust. Papa had worked two winters on the railway before the courier work, and he had walked her along a fence once at dusk and said, *see, Nel, they leave the gaps. Nothing in this world is watched all the way along. Find the gap and keep walking.*

Find the gap and keep walking. She said it to herself in time with her steps. Find the gap. Keep walking. Her ankle sang with every other step, a thin hot wire of pain, and she made a bargain with it the way Papa had taught her to bargain with cold: *you may hurt as much as you like so long as I can still move.* The wire agreed. The wire always agreed if you were firm.

Once, crossing an empty road, a constable's lantern swung far off at the corner of her eye and she flattened herself into a doorway and held Josephine so hard the doll's head clicked against her chin, and the light passed, and she breathed again, and hated herself a little for breathing, because Papa hadn't gotten to breathe.

Twice, dogs barked, and each time she stopped dead until they gave up, because barking that doesn't get answered is only a dog, but barking that gets answered is men.

The railway yard opened before her like a field of iron wheat. Wagons stood in long ranks under a sky gone grey-black, and somewhere a signal lamp swung green and green and green, and the rails sang a note so low she felt it in her teeth rather than heard it. She went in among the wheels. The smell was oil and coal-smoke and cold iron, and underneath it, faintly, impossibly, the memory of her father's coat, because he had smelled like this after work, and she pressed her face to Josephine's hair and let one tear come, only one, the rationed kind.

There was a gap between a cattle wagon and a coal truck, just wide enough, floored with gravel and shadowed on both sides. She backed into it. She sat down. She pulled her knees up and set Josephine facing outward, toward the yard, the way you'd post a sentry.

*You watch that way,* she whispered. *I'll watch the other.*

The ground was cold and it climbed up through her skirt and into her bones, and her ankle beat its wire, and far off a train came through without stopping, hauling its long noise across the night and away, and after it the silence was enormous, and Nell discovered that enormous silence is the loudest thing there is.

She thought about the grey woman waking to an empty bed. She thought about the candle guttering in the kitchen doorway. She thought about the tall woman — Bram, her name was Bram, a funny name, a boy's name almost, and Nell liked that about it — and about how Bram had promised nothing, exactly, but had looked at her once across the supper table with an expression Nell had no word for yet, an expression like a hand reaching.

*I'll come back for you* was a thing grown-ups said. What Bram did was different. Bram was a person who arrived. Nell had watched her arrive: out of rain, out of crowds, out of bad rooms. Some people were doors and some people were the ones who came through them, and Bram came through.

So she would go to the yard and stand in it, and Bram would come through the night and find her, because that was the whole of what Nell believed now, the last furniture left in the house of the world.

Sleep came for her the way sleep comes for children who have used themselves up, sudden and total, mid-thought, her cheek against Josephine's sewn hair, four knots in her cardigan cuff, one boot missing, the lantern face pushed down deep where even dreams had to shout to reach it.

In the wagon above her, unseen, a draught stirred papers some clerk had lost weeks ago. In the town behind her, lamps were being lit for the search that had not started yet. And miles south, on a dark road, a woman drove with pages of a dead man's ledger sewn against her ribs, driving north without knowing why her hands kept tightening on the wheel — the way hands tighten when somewhere, far ahead in the dark, something small has begun to run toward them.

* * *

She woke to boots.

Not many. One pair, maybe two, moving along the wagon ranks with the unhurried tread of men doing a job they expected to be dull. Gravel crunched. A torch beam swept the underside of the couplings, a long yellow finger poking into each gap in turn, coming closer, closer, and Nell pressed herself flat and put her hand over Josephine's eyes, because whatever happened, the doll should not see.

The finger of light slid along the gravel toward the gap.

And then — farther off, at the yard gate, a second light. Not a torch. A lantern, carried low and swinging, and with it a voice calling something to the men among the wagons, a woman's voice, sharp with a fear it was trying to disguise as anger, a voice Nell knew better than her own heartbeat, and she was up before she knew she was moving, out of the gap and into the open, limping, stumbling, running toward the lantern with Josephine hugged to her chest and her heart going like the express overhead —

— and the torch beam swung around and caught her full in the face.
