## 18. Finding Her in the Yards

The knock had come at half past nine, three blows of the brass knocker at the Quaker house on Booth Street, and by the time Mrs. Pilling reached the door there was nothing in the lane but a puddle catching the lamplight and a child-sized gap in the evening where Nell had been.

That was how the message reached me — not by wire, not by letter, but by Edith's hand gripping my wrist hard enough to leave marks, her voice stripped down to its frame. *"She's run. Bram. She's run."*

I did not remember crossing the hall. I did not remember fetching my coat or telling the maid where we were going or whether I told anyone anything at all. What I remembered was the cold coming through the car's canvas roof like a hand pressed flat against my scalp, and Edith beside me saying, over and over, in a voice that was trying very hard to be a strategist's voice and failing: "She'll go to the station. Children go to stations. Trains mean fathers, trains mean home. We'll find her at the station."

But the station at eleven o'clock was a cavern of steam and shouted departures, porters wheeling barrows through fog, and no small girl anywhere among the legs and suitcases. We walked it end to end twice. Edith questioned a woman selling chestnuts who shook her head; she questioned a policeman, which cost her something, and he shook his head too and advised her, with a kindness that made it worse, to try the mission houses come morning.

"Morning," Edith said when we were back in the car. Her hands were folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral. "Bram, it is November. It will be four degrees by morning."

I started the engine because starting the engine was the only thing I knew how to do.

* * *

We drove for an hour without speaking, circling outward from the station through streets that emptied as we went, past public houses exhaling yellow light and drunks, past shuttered mills whose windows reflected nothing. Every dark doorway I looked at twice. Every bundle of rags. Once I stopped the car and got out and turned over a pile of sacking behind a baker's shop, my heart going like a trip-hammer, and found only a sleeping cat that spat at me and fled into the dark.

Edith watched me do it and said nothing, but when I got back in she said, quietly, "You love her."

I put my hands back on the wheel. The steering wheel was cold enough to burn.

"I promised her," I said. "I told her nothing would happen to her. I told her that with her father's blood still on the pavement somewhere in London, I told her that looking into those eyes, and I had no right—"

"You had every right," Edith said. "A promise isn't a contract, Bram. It doesn't require collateral. It requires only that you meant it."

"I meant it."

"Then drive."

So I drove. And somewhere around one in the morning, turning off Canal Street onto a road I barely knew, it came to me the way these things come — not as thought but as memory, sudden and complete. Nell, three weeks ago, standing on a chair at the sink while I washed her hair, asking me about the sounds outside. The whistle she'd heard all day. *What makes the whistling?* And I'd said, trains, love, shunting, they move the wagons about all night, and she'd gone quiet in the way she went quiet when something pleased her, and later I'd found her at the window with her chin on her fists watching the far lights of the goods yards move and shift like constellations.

Her father had been a railway man. A courier on the London line, killed for what he carried. Of course. Of course she'd run to the trains. Not to board one — she'd never have managed a ticket, she'd have been stopped at the barrier in a minute — but to be near them. To be near the sound of him.

"The yards," I said. "The goods yards by the junction."

Edith was already reaching for the door handle.

* * *

The yards lay south of the canal, a hundred acres of iron and gravel stitched together with rails, fenced in chain-link and patrolled, in theory, by a watchman. In practice the watchman sat in his hut at the gate with a stove and a bottle, and the rest of the yard belonged to the fog and the freight. We left the car in a lane behind a coal merchant's and went in where the fence had been peeled back years ago by generations of boys, the wires curled aside like the lid of a tin.

Inside, the world changed scale. Wagons stood in ranks longer than streets, some loaded, some empty, their sides running away into the murk until you couldn't tell wagon from wagon from darkness. Overhead, gas lamps on tall posts burned in haloes, each one an island, the space between them black water. Somewhere a locomotive breathed. Somewhere couplings clanged, that enormous ringing note that travels through the ground before it reaches the air, and each time it sounded I flinched, and hated myself for flinching, because every clang might be her.

"Nell!" I called it low, the way you call into a church. "Nell! It's Bram!"

Edith moved along one row while I took another, our voices trading across the wagons, hers level and carrying, mine breaking on the second syllable every time. We had agreed — quickly, in the lane — that we would not shout her name full-voice, not here, not with Crake's men possibly already wired to every yardman and gateman in Manchester. But restraint dies fast in the dark. By the third row I was saying her name like a prayer with the words worn off, just the shape of it, just the breath.

The fog was the worst of it. It came off the canal in slow banks and pooled between the wagon wheels at knee height, so that walking the rows felt like wading through something drowned. My shoes soaked through inside ten minutes. My hands went numb on the cold iron of the wagon ladders I gripped and swung from, checking beneath brake vans, checking under tarpaulins that covered loads of pit props and machinery, each one a shape that for one whole heartbeat was a child curled up, and then wasn't, and then wasn't again, until the not-being began to feel like a verdict.

Once I found signs of life — a nest of newspaper under a wagon, flattened, recently slept in, smelling of fish and chips. A boy's camp, probably. A boy's camp, probably. I stood over it shaking.

Edith appeared at the end of the row, a grey silhouette with fog beaded on her hair, and for a moment neither of us spoke, and in that moment I understood that she was as frightened as I was, and that this frightened me more than the fog, more than Crake, more than anything — because Edith Marlowe did not frighten, Edith Marlowe *decided*, and if the deciding had failed then the world had come loose from its moorings.

"She was here," Edith said. "Or someone was."

"It could be anyone."

"It could." She looked down the endless row of wagons, and I saw her doing the arithmetic she could not help doing — hours until dawn, degrees until killing frost, the distance a seven-year-old can cover on legs that have already run a mile through strange streets. I saw her arrive at the answer and refuse to say it aloud. That refusal, that mercy of silence, I have never forgotten it.

"Keep going," she said instead.

* * *

I don't know what hour it was when I stopped feeling my feet. Time in the yards ran on rails of its own, marked by the passing of goods trains on the main line beyond the fence — a long thundering, a red lamp swinging past, and then the enormous quiet afterward in which my own voice sounded absurd and small. I called until my throat was raw. I climbed. I crawled. I knelt in cinder mud and put my face under wagons until grit filled my collar and my knees bled through my stockings, and I would have done it until morning, until doomsday, and the terrible thing, the thing I can hardly set down even now, is that some part of me was already rehearsing the finding of her too late. Rehearsing it so that it wouldn't ambush me. The mind is a coward that way; it builds the gallows in advance so the drop won't surprise it.

And then, in the fourth hour, between two flat wagons near the canal wall, I heard breathing.

Not a train. Not wind. Breathing — small, quick, the shallow catch-and-release of a sleeper who is not sleeping well. I froze. Fog moved between the wagons like a curtain being drawn by careful hands.

"Nell?" I whispered.

The breathing stopped.

I didn't dare move fast. I lowered myself to my knees — my knees sang, I ignored them — and leaned into the gap between the wagons, into the smell of axle grease and old wood and canal water, and there, wedged into the narrow shadow where the two wagons' wheels met, wrapped in the coat she'd been taken in, her cheek pressed against the cold iron of a wheel hub as though it were a pillow someone had given her, was Nell.

Her eyes opened. They were huge in the dark, and for one endless second they were full of pure animal terror — the look of a creature caught, cornered, ready to bolt into the black and be lost forever — and I did the only thing I could think of. I did exactly nothing. I stayed on my knees in the mud, six feet away, hands open and empty on my thighs, and I let her look at me.

"It's Bram," I said. Barely a breath. "It's only Bram."

She didn't move. The terror drained out of her eyes by fractions, like water out of a cracked jug, and what came up underneath it was worse: a vast, wobbling, exhausted grief, the face of a child holding herself together with both hands.

"You ran away," I said, and I hadn't meant to say it, it came out cracked and accusing and true.

Her chin trembled. "You was gone," she whispered.

I didn't understand. Then I did, and it went through me like a coupling slamming home. The knock at the Quaker house — she'd been told, presumably, that I was coming for her, that I'd fetch her myself. And when the knock came and it was strangers, voices she didn't know, Mrs. Pilling and her sister and a man's voice perhaps, a *man's* voice — to a child who had seen what she'd seen, who had been told by her dying father's last arrangement to *forget the face*, every unknown knock was the face coming for her.

"They knocked," I said slowly, "and you thought—" I couldn't finish it.

"He come at night," Nell whispered. Her voice was rusted from disuse. "At our house. He knocked polite." Her eyes fixed on mine, wide and ancient. "Polite ain't safe."

"No," I said. My own voice broke entirely. "No, love, you're right, polite isn't always safe. You were clever. You were so clever."

"Is it morning?"

"Not yet."

"Is he dead still?" she asked, and I understood she meant her father, and that in her arithmetic the world held only two states now — the time when Papa was alive, and the long night since, and that she checked, sometimes, whether the first state had come back, the way you check whether it has stopped raining.

"Yes, love," I said. Because I would not lie to her; that much, at least, I had learned. "He's still dead."

She nodded, unsurprised, unsurprisable, and then her face crumpled and the sob came up out of her like something surfacing from deep water, and I crawled forward through the mud and the grease and gathered her out from between the wheels, and she came into my arms stiff with cold and then all at once loose, boneless, clinging, her face buried in my neck, her whole small body shaking with the effort of crying silently, because somewhere she had learned that crying loudly gets you found.

"Shout," I told her, rocking her, my mouth against the filthy cold silk of her hair. "Go on. Shout as loud as you like. No one will find you here but us."

So she did. She cried the way children cry when they finally can — enormously, ruinously, with her whole body — and I held her between those two flat wagons under the haloed lamps and let it break over me, and I confess I cried too, silently, uselessly, tears freezing at my jaw, because I had spent the whole night rehearsing her death and instead here she was, alive, furious, alive.

* * *

Edith came at the sound — of course she did; she'd have come at a falling leaf — and appeared at the end of the gap with the fog swirling round her skirts, and stopped, and put her hand over her mouth.

Nell saw her and went rigid, and I felt the rigidity and understood it, and I said, "This is Miss Marlowe. This is Edith. She's been walking the yards all night calling your name, love. She's the one who sent me."

Nell studied her for a long moment from the fortress of my arms. Then, with the terrifying directness of children: "Did you knock at the house?"

"No," Edith said. She crouched, slowly, bringing herself down to eye level, heedless of the mud. "I was in bed hating everyone, waiting for morning. It was Mrs. Pilling's nephew come with a message about coal." A pause. "I am sorry I frightened you. It was the last thing in the world I wanted."

Nell considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she reached out one hand, still clutching — God help me — the rag doll she'd carried since the first night, its button eye hanging by a thread, and touched two fingers briefly to Edith's sleeve. A treaty, signed. Edith closed her hand around those fingers as though something precious had been placed in it.

"We're taking her home," I said. There was no discussion in it. "Not back to Booth Street. Home. Ours. Let Crake wire whatever he likes — she is not spending another night under a stranger's roof because we decided strategy beats heart. I'm done with that decision."

Edith looked at me across the child's head, and everything passed between us in that look — the argument we weren't having, the danger we weren't naming, the fact that I, the driver, the reader-of-letters, the observer, was giving orders to Edith Marlowe in a railway yard at four in the morning, and that she was taking them.

"All right," she said simply. "Yours, then. Ours." She rose, brushing mud from her skirt with hands that were not quite steady. "Wrap her well. We'll carry her out past the coal merchant's and—"

She stopped.

I followed her gaze down the long rank of wagons, toward the gate side of the yard, where the watchman's hut sat with its stove-pipe smoking. A light had come on there. And moving between the lamp posts, unhurried, methodical, a lantern swinging at knee height, were two figures — dark coats, bowler hats, working their way up the rows the way Edith and I had worked them, checking under wagons.

Checking under wagons.

Crake's men. Here, hours after she vanished, sweeping the one place a railway man's daughter would run. Which meant Crake had guessed it too — or worse, had known it all along, had wired the yards before we ever thought of them, and had simply been waiting, letting the cold and the dark do his work for him.

Nell had gone utterly silent against my shoulder. She had seen them too. Of course she had. She knew better than any of us what a lantern swinging at knee height meant in the dark.

I drew back between the wagons, pressing her down into the shadow, and Edith crouched beside us, and the three of us breathed fog and listened to footsteps on gravel coming closer, row by row, unhurried, certain.

"Bram," Edith whispered, so softly it was hardly sound at all, "how well do you know the canal wall?"

I looked past her, past the wagons, to the black water sliding by thirty feet away — and beyond it, the towpath, and beyond that, a lock gate, and beyond that, the lanes home.

"Well enough," I whispered back, and lifted Nell higher onto my hip.
