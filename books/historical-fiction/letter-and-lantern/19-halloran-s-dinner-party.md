## 19. Halloran's Dinner Party

The candles had been lit at seven, and by half past they were burning with the particular steadiness that only expensive wax and a well-paid manservant could achieve. Undersecretary Ambrose Halloran stood at the head of his dining room in Belgrave Square and watched the light settle on eleven places laid with his grandmother's porcelain, and he thought, not for the first time, that history was a great deal like a dinner party: it mattered less what was true than what was served, and to whom, and in what order.

He had dressed carefully. Not flamboyantly — Halloran never did anything flamboyantly — but with the precision of a man who understood that authority lived in small details. The studs were pearl. The watch chain was gold but not ostentatious. His cuffs showed exactly the correct quarter-inch beneath each sleeve. He was forty-six years old, grey at the temples in a way that flattered rather than aged him, and when he looked in the glass that evening he had seen what the newspapers saw: a reasonable man. A steady man. The sort of face that reassured.

That was the whole of his fortune, really — the face. Not the family money, which was respectable but thinning; not the connections, which were useful but conditional. It was the fact that when Ambrose Halloran entered a room, people believed nothing bad could happen there. He had spent twenty years cultivating that belief, and tonight he intended to spend it like capital.

The first guests arrived at eight.

* * *

Sir Reginald Fosse came early, as early men do — the ones who want something. He was Commissioner-adjacent, a man whose fingers rested lightly on several important throats within the Metropolitan Police without ever quite closing, and he brought with him the smell of cold air and cigar smoke and an eagerness he imagined he was hiding.

"Ambrose." They shook hands. Fosse's grip was warm and slightly damp. "Extraordinary evening for it. My wife sends her regrets — the Hartleys' thing, you know how it is."

"Give her my best." Halloran steered him toward the fire and the decanter. "You'll want the port later. This is the good claret's cousin — I keep it for warming up."

Fosse laughed, because men like Fosse always laughed at whatever Halloran said, and accepted a whisky instead, and for four or five minutes they discussed the weather and the King's health and the lamentable state of the roads between here and Brighton, all of it pleasant, all of it meaningless, all of it the necessary overture before the music began.

The others arrived in twos and threes. Colonel Merrick from the War Office, who ate as though rationing were already upon them. Mr. Pell, from the *Evening Standard*, who was not officially invited and therefore sat at the end of the table where the conversation would be loudest and most deniable. Lady Ashcombe, whose drawing room had launched three careers and buried two, wearing amethysts and an expression of magnificent boredom. And last, five minutes late in a way that was calculated to be noticed, Inspector Crake.

Halloran had debated inviting Crake at all. There were arguments against it — the man was police, and visible police at a private dinner raised eyebrows; he was ambitious in a way that made him useful but not safe; and he was, frankly, a little too much of what he was, all flat stare and folded hands, like a blade someone had tried to dress in evening clothes. But there was one argument for it that outweighed everything: Crake knew precisely what was about to be discussed, and watching him sit through it with his face arranged into blankness would tell Halloran more than any report.

"Inspector." Halloran took his coat himself, which was a signal, though only Crake would understand it. "You found us easily?"

"The fog thinned after six, sir."

"And your inquiries? Progressing?"

"Progressing." Nothing moved in Crake's eyes. "I expect to have everything settled inside the week."

"Good man. Sit near me."

They went in to dinner on the stroke of nine. Halloran seated Lady Ashcombe at his right, Pell at his left, and let the soup come and go while the talk ran along its rails — the Irish question, the budget, somebody's daughter's wedding — until the fish was cleared and the footmen withdrew, and then, with the timing of a man lifting a conductor's baton, Halloran set down his spoon and turned the evening.

"You'll have read the papers this morning," he said, to the table generally, "about the outrage in Camden."

There it was. A week ago, a bomb — a small one, amateurish, planted against the wall of a railway goods office, doing perhaps forty pounds of damage and no deaths, mercifully, though the papers had done their best to imply deaths anyway. Within six hours the usual voices had claimed it in the name of the Women's Social and Political Union, or been assumed to have; within twelve, Halloran's own people had quietly ensured that the assumption hardened into fact. Letters had surfaced. A pamphlet had surfaced. Witnesses had surfaced, blinking and certain. By now it was simply known, the way things become known, that the militants had crossed their Rubicon: from windows to walls, from rhetoric to powder.

"I read it," said Colonel Merrick, sawing at his fish. "Disgraceful business. Women throwing bombs. It's the end of something, mark me."

"It may be," Halloran agreed mildly, "the beginning of something instead."

Lady Ashcombe looked up. Pell's pen — he actually carried one to dinner, a fat silver fountain pen, and Halloran had counted on exactly this — hovered over his cuff.

"Go on," she said.

"Well." Halloran leaned back, and let his voice drop half a register, into the register of confidence shared rather than opinion offered. "For years we've managed these women the way one manages weather. An inconvenience. A nuisance to be endured. And every year the nuisance grows, because nuisance costs nothing to ignore. But a bomb — a bomb changes the arithmetic. A bomb means the public stops thinking of them as suffragists and starts thinking of them as what they are, if this continues: criminals. Enemies of order. And once the public makes that classification, gentlemen—" he nodded at Merrick, at Fosse, at the shadow of Pell taking notes with his fork hand— "once the public classifies, Parliament follows. And once Parliament follows, we can legislate. Properly. Finally. Restrictions these ladies' grandmothers would have fainted at, passed without a murmur, because everyone will agree they've earned it."

"You make it sound almost welcome," Lady Ashcombe said, with her thin smile. "A bomb on your own doorstep."

"On the country's doorstep, ma'am, and yes — welcome is too strong. Necessary, perhaps. One does not celebrate the surgeon's knife." He lifted his glass a fraction. "But I confess I sleep easier knowing the argument has finally been made in a language everyone understands."

Pell cleared his throat. "There are those saying the letters implicating the WSPU leadership seem remarkably — convenient."

"Are there?" Halloran smiled, warm and unhurried, and gave Pell the full weight of his reasonable face. "Convenient to whom, Mr. Pell? To the government, certainly — though I'd argue a government that wanted a pretext could have manufactured a far better one than some shrapnel-scarred brickwork in Camden. Convenient to the militants themselves? Hardly. Or — " and here he paused, delicately, the pause itself a kind of testimony — "perhaps Mr. Pell has heard something specific. Something I haven't. In which case I should very much like to know it, and so, I imagine, would the Home Office."

It was beautifully done, he thought, and allowed himself the interior satisfaction of it. The question wrapped in an invitation, the suspicion converted into a burden. Pell would print nothing. Pell would print *around* it — a line about ministerial calm in the face of insinuation, a portrait of Halloran as the unflappable servant of order — which was worth ten defenses.

"No, sir," Pell said. "Nothing specific."

"Then we needn't borrow trouble."

The entrée came and went. The talk loosened, spread, broke into tributaries — Merrick and Fosse arguing happily about cavalry, Lady Ashcombe conducting a low campaign against the wine — and Halloran let it run, because the center of the evening was not yet reached, and he never rushed a center. He ate. He drank moderately. Twice he caught Crake watching him across the candlelight with those grey, patient eyes, and twice Crake looked away first, and both times Halloran felt the small cold pleasure of hierarchy confirmed.

It was over the game course that Merrick, flushed and expansive, leaned across the corner of the table.

"This courier business," he said, too loudly, in the way of men who believe discretion is a volume setting. "The one who went missing on the northern line of yours. All squared away?"

The table did not quite go silent, but it contracted, the way rooms contract. Halloran kept his expression pleasant and his pulse level, and noted — filed away, stamped, dated — that Merrick had just told eleven people, including a journalist, including two policemen, that there *was* a courier business, and that it was Halloran's.

"There was an incident," Halloran said smoothly, "during the movement of some routine correspondence. Regrettable. Being handled."

"Man dead, wasn't he?" Merrick pressed on, wine-bright. "Or vanished. Vanished, I heard."

"Vanished," Halloran agreed, "which is worse for him and better for everyone else. More port, Colonel?"

Fosse shot Merrick a look of pure murder across the flowers. Lady Ashcombe's boredom had sharpened into interest. And Crake, at the far curve of the table, had gone very still — stillness itself, Halloran reflected, being the loudest thing in the room.

* * *

They left the table at eleven. Coffee was served in the long drawing room, under portraits of Hallorans who had done nothing memorable enough to be painted badly, and the guests distributed themselves according to the ancient physics of such rooms: the ambitious near the host, the bored near the fire, Pell near the door where he could slip out and file.

Halloran drew Fosse and Crake into the alcove by the window, where the fog outside pressed white against the glass and made the three of them, briefly, the only people in England.

"He's a fool," Fosse muttered, nodding back toward the dining room, toward Merrick. "Merrick. Talking about couriers at your own table."

"Merrick is a soldier. Soldiers think secrecy means speaking softly." Halloran sipped his coffee. "It doesn't matter. What matters is whether the story holds. Tell me about Camden."

"The story holds." Fosse turned his coffee cup, rotated it slowly on its saucer, a habit he had when assembling courage. "The letters are in place. Two witnesses have sworn statements. The pamphlet — the printing's good, incidentally, whoever did it — has been traced to a press in Bethnal Green that we can plausibly connect to a WSPU sympathizer. By Friday it'll be in three evening papers and one morning one, and after that it isn't a story anymore. It's a fact. Facts don't get retracted."

"And the loose ends?"

Fosse glanced at Crake. It was a glance Halloran catalogued and disliked: the Commissioner-adjacent man deferring to his own instrument, or seeming to defer, which in that world amounted to the same currency.

"There's a child," Crake said.

His voice was quiet and even, pitched for the alcove alone, and Halloran felt the evening tilt a degree, the way a ship tilts when something shifts below the waterline.

"A child," Halloran repeated.

"A witness. To the courier." Crake's hands were folded in front of him, and he spoke as though reciting inventory. "The courier had a daughter. Nine, ten years old. She was in the cart with him on the night road. When it happened, she ran. We lost her in the dark — or rather, someone lost her. Someone put her somewhere and told the neighborhood to forget her face."

"Who?"

"That's the question, sir." For the first time something moved behind Crake's eyes — not warmth, nothing so soft as warmth, but the gleam of a man approaching a sum he has waited all year to total. "Someone got to her before I did. Someone who knew what she'd seen. Which means someone else knows what's in the courier's pockets, or knows enough to fear it. And that person has kept the girl alive — kept her close, I'd say, because children who frighten people that much are usually killed, and this one hasn't been. So they think she's worth more breathing than buried. Evidence, maybe. Or insurance."

The fog pressed at the window. Somewhere behind them, Lady Ashcombe laughed at something the firelight said, and the sound seemed to belong to another century.

"And you're pursuing her," Halloran said.

"I'm pursuing everyone connected to the Marlowe household." Crake said the name plainly, without lowering his voice, and Halloran watched Fosse wince and decided not to. Secrecy, as he had just observed, was a matter of posture, not volume — and Crake's bluntness was itself a weapon, the assurance of a man who intended to win so completely that witnesses wouldn't dare exist. "I have warrants requested for the household staff records. I have a description of the girl circulating through every checkpoint north of London — Euston, Crewe, the Stockport road. Whatever moves between that house and Manchester will move past my men. And when it moves, I'll have the courier, the evidence, and the reason, all in one net."

"And Edith Marlowe herself?"

"If she's carrying it — she hangs herself. If she isn't, she hangs anyway, eventually, on the old charges. Either way, sir, by the end of the month the woman is finished and the frame is closed and nobody living can prove otherwise." Crake paused. "Except possibly a child."

"Then find the child first," Halloran said.

"With respect, sir — I intend to find everything first. That's rather the point of me."

Halloran studied him. There was a temptation, always, with instruments this sharp, to admire them, and admiration was the first step toward dependence, and dependence was the first step toward the gallows. He set the feeling down like a cup he didn't trust.

"One more thing, Inspector. The journalist angle. If a Manchester editor receives — say — documents. Photographs. Before Friday."

"Then Friday's papers print a different story, and everything we've built becomes evidence *against* the government instead of the militants." Crake delivered it without drama, a man reading a train timetable. "Which is why nothing reaches Manchester. Nothing crosses Stockport. I've wired ahead tonight, before I came to your dinner. Every sergeant on that road has a photograph and a name."

"You work quickly."

"I work," Crake said, "continuously."

From the doorway, Fosse cleared his throat and murmured that Lady Ashcombe was asking for the host, and Halloran set down his cup, straightened his cuffs, and became once more the reasonable man, the steady man, the face that reassured. He moved back into the light of his own drawing room with the smile already fitted, shaking hands, dispensing warmth, promising Merrick a day's shooting in November and Pell a background briefing he had no intention of giving.

Only once, crossing the room, did he allow himself the thought he had been keeping all evening in a locked drawer somewhere behind his ribs:

A child. Someone had taken in a child who had seen a man die, and kept her breathing, and meant to move her — or something — north, through Crake's net, inside the week.

It was sentimental. It was reckless. It was, Halloran thought, reaching for the decanter to refill Lady Ashcombe's glass, exactly the sort of mistake that decent people made, believing that mercy was invisible.

He raised his glass to the room. *To order,* he said, and eleven voices echoed him, and outside the window the fog lay over London like a held breath, and forty miles away on a dark road outside Stockport, sergeants were already pinning a photograph of a nine-year-old girl to their clipboards by lantern light.
