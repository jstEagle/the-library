## 20. The Second Checkpoint: Stockport Road

The rain began an hour out of Sheffield, a thin grey sifting that turned the road to grease and the moors to smoke. Bram drove with her shoulders low and her hands at ten and two, the way she'd been taught, and told herself that the ache between her shoulder blades was only fatigue.

It was not only fatigue. It was the ledger.

She had sewn it in herself on Wednesday night, in the kitchen at Holloway Road, by the light of one lamp while Nell slept upstairs with her mouth open and her doll — the new doll, the travelling doll — clutched against her chest. Edith had held the pages flat and read out the order of them, page by page, photograph by photograph, so that Bram would know what she carried even blindfolded, even in the dark, even if the coat were taken from her and she had to say, *no, officer, nothing there but an old woman's sewing*. Thirty-one leaves of paper and card. Four photographs pasted to linen backing. The whole conspiracy of Undersecretary Halloran reduced to something light enough to wear.

The needle had gone through leather, paper, and lining in a rhythm: *through, catch, pull; through, catch, pull.* She had thought of it as a heartbeat. She had thought, absurdly, of her mother teaching her buttons. She had thought of nothing at all for long stretches, which was its own kind of prayer.

Now the coat sat folded on the seat beside her, because no one wore a heavy driving coat in October rain without looking like a woman hiding something — or rather, they looked exactly like a woman hiding something, and she could not risk the look. The coat lay under a folded motor rug, and every time she glanced sideways — which was constantly — she saw only the rug's tartan edge, and beneath it, invisible, thirty-one leaves of paper that could hang half a government and get a child's father's name spoken aloud in court instead of whispered in a morgue corridor.

*Forget the face,* the note had said. She had not forgotten. She never would. That was rather the point of all this.

The A6 unspooled ahead through the wet. Cart traffic thickened as she came down toward the villages south of Stockport — coal lorries, a milk float, drays with tarpaulins snapping in the wind. Ordinary England, going about its ordinary Thursday. She found herself cataloguing the faces at the roadside the way Crake must catalogue hers: the boy leading a pony, the woman pegging washing in weather too wet for it, the two men outside the Wheatsheaf who stopped talking when the car passed and did not resume until it was well by. Word travelled faster than motors in these hills. Someone had telephoned ahead, or ridden, or simply stood where he could be seen standing. Roadblocks did not appear from nowhere. They appeared from conversations.

She had rehearsed the drive for two days. Edith had drilled her at the kitchen table with the map pinned flat and weighted at the corners with the salt cellar and the pepper pot and Nell's tin soldiers, borrowed without explanation and returned after, though the girl had watched from the doorway with the particular silence she used when she understood more than she was meant to.

"Two rail inspections you've already beaten," Edith had said, tapping Crewe, tapping Euston behind it. "This is the third. Roads are different from stations. At a station you're a passenger among passengers. On a road, you're a car. There are very few cars. You will be remembered whether they stop you or not."

"Then I should give them something worth remembering," Bram had said, "that isn't the truth."

Edith had looked at her for a long moment, and something in her face had been almost tender and almost grieved, as if Bram had just demonstrated both her fitness and her doom in the same sentence. "Yes," she'd said. "That's the whole trade, isn't it."

So Bram had built herself a reason to be on the Stockport road in the last week of October, 1913, alone, in Edith Marlowe's Wolseley, wearing her second-best skirt under a motoring duster, and the reason was this: she was Miss Vane, companion-secretary to Mrs. Marlowe, sent north to collect Mrs. Marlowe's sister from Buxton, who had taken a chill and whose own motor was laid up with a cracked block, and who was impatient, and whose brother-in-law — Mrs. Marlowe's man of business — had insisted the family car be sent rather than trust the railways in the strike weather. Every element of it was small and dull and checkable in the wrong direction. The sister existed; she was Edith's cousin, really, but cousins became sisters in families that didn't want questions, and no sergeant alive would wire Buxton to confirm a sick lady's exact relation. The cracked engine block was real; the Marlowe household's old Darracq had one. Details were bricks. She had laid them carefully.

What she had not planned for — what no rehearsal could fully plan for — was the clipboard.

* * *

She saw it first as a shimmer of oilskins at the bend above Hazel Grove, where the road narrowed between a stone wall and a drop into dripping fields. Then the lanterns. Then the barrier itself: two trestles and a plank, painted white, dragged across the carriageway, with a motorcycle propped beside it like a sentry's horse. Three men. One in a sergeant's uniform with the collar up and rain beading on his cap brim. Two in plain clothes with the unmistakable posture of men who were not plain at all — weight forward, hands free, eyes doing the talking.

Her heart performed a single enormous contraction, as if the whole organ had been picked up and wrung out once, and then settled back into work. This was the thing she had learned about fear since the knock at the door: it came like that now, in one hard wave, and then it went to work for her instead of against her. Fear made the world very sharp. Fear counted the men, noted the motorcycle's registration plate, marked the side lane behind the wall where a second lantern hung — a reserve post, or an escape net, depending.

She slowed early. Braking late at a barrier told them you were startled; braking early told them you were expected to brake. She brought the Wolseley down to walking pace twenty yards out, wound the window halfway — not fully, a woman alone doesn't sit boxed with strange men at dusk, any sergeant knows that, and a sergeant who knows it trusts it — and let the car roll to a stop with the handbrake set firmly enough to be heard.

"Evening," she said, before he spoke. First word is ownership of the conversation. "I'm told the road's stopped. Is it the flooding at the ford?"

The sergeant came around to her window. He was perhaps forty-five, red-faced, with the permanent damp misery of a man kept outdoors by other men's orders. His clipboard was under his oilskin cape, protected from the rain, which meant it mattered to him, which meant it mattered to someone above him.

"Flooding, madam? No. It's a police matter. Name and business, if you please."

"Bramwell Vane." Her own name, unhidden. Edith's counsel: *never invent a name you can't produce papers for; lie about circumstances, never about identity.* "Secretary-companion to Mrs. Edith Marlowe. I'm driving to Buxton for her sister, Mrs. Quill, who's taken a chill and can't travel down. Their motor's off the road — cracked block — so I've brought the Wolseley. Am I near Hazel Grove? I've been navigating by milestones and they're all weeping."

The joke cost her nothing and bought her a flicker at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. A flicker. Men who have stood in the rain all day forgive a great deal to a woman who treats them as human beings, and hate themselves for it after, which is its own protection, because a man who hates himself for liking you looks harder at the next traveller to prove his virtue.

"Mrs. Marlowe." He said the name flatly, and the flicker died, and Bram felt the temperature of the exchange drop five degrees. "That's the suffragist lady."

"She's my employer," Bram said. "And a very worried one tonight, with a sick relative and a useless garage. What's the police matter, Sergeant? Has there been an accident?" She let her voice do what voices do in the country: carry a little, invite the neighbourhood in. The two plain-clothes men had drifted closer, one to each side of the bonnet, and she refused to look at either of them directly, which drove them quietly mad, she knew. Men in plain clothes live on being noticed. Deprive them of it and they must manufacture their own importance.

"You could say an accident." The sergeant shifted the clipboard out from under his cape. "We're asking after a child, madam. Girl, seven or eight years of age. May have been seen travelling with a woman answering your description."

The rain ticked on the roof of the car. Somewhere behind the wall the reserve lantern swayed in the wind, throwing a moving stripe of yellow along the wet stone. Bram heard her own pulse in her ears, slow and stupid, like a clock in an empty house.

*Composure,* she thought, *is not the absence of terror. It is terror, properly dressed.* She had read that somewhere — one of Edith's letters, steamed open on a Tuesday, months ago, back when she had still believed she was reading about history instead of carrying it. The irony arrived with perfect timing and she declined to enjoy it.

"A child?" she said, and put bewilderment into it, genuine bewilderment, which was not difficult because underneath the performance some part of her was genuinely bewildered — bewildered that the world contained a clipboard with Nell's face on it, bewildered that she was sitting in the rain discussing a child she had promised, recklessly, hopelessly, that nothing would happen to. "Good heavens. Runaway?"

"We don't know that she's run anywhere." The sergeant turned the clipboard around.

There she was.

A photograph, grainy, taken — Bram's mind supplied instantly, horribly — from across a street, cropped close. Nell in her good coat, the blue one, the one she'd worn the night she arrived at the door in Holloway Road clutching the oilcloth packet, the one Edith had burned in the kitchen grate two days ago along with everything else the girl had touched on her first week. The coat was ash. The photograph remained. In it Nell's chin was up and her eyes were wide and fixed slightly left of the camera, at something — at someone — she had been told to forget.

Below the image, in a clerk's copperplate, the words: *WITNESS TO BE LOCATED. ANY INFORMATION TO INSPECTOR CRAKE, METROPOLITAN POLICE.*

Crake. The name went through her like a splinter finding its depth. She had met him once, on the step, with his hat dripping and his questions dressed as courtesies, and she had watched him linger over the coat hook for three seconds too long, and she had told herself three seconds meant nothing. The photograph proved otherwise. Three seconds had been a man memorising a hook.

"That's a poor little soul," Bram said. Her voice came out right — soft, troubled, a woman's voice over a lost child. Inside, some colder clerk observed that it had come out right, and filed the fact away, and she hated the clerk and was grateful to her in the same breath. "Is she hurt? Why on earth would anyone— is she lost, or—" She stopped, as a real person would, struck by the obvious. "You think she's been *taken*."

The sergeant's jaw worked. Plain-clothes man number one, on her left, leaned an elbow on the door frame, and Bram registered the smell of him through the gap in the window — wet wool and cigarette smoke — and registered also, with a lurch, the mud on his boots. London mud. They had come up on the night train. Crake was spending money on this.

"We think," said the plain-clothes man, "that she may be travelling with persons using her for purposes of concealment. Persons who might be driving north. Alone." He let *alone* sit in the rain between them. "You're a long way from London, miss."

"I'm a long way from everywhere," Bram said. "That's rather the nature of driving to Buxton." And then, because the moment demanded either flight or boldness and flight was a locked door, she leaned toward the clipboard, actually leaned, and studied the photograph of Nell with her head tilted, and said, slowly, "No. I don't believe— no. Though—" and hesitated.

Every man at the barrier stopped breathing. She felt it, the collective intake, three sets of lungs hanging on her hesitation, and understood with terrible clarity that she was holding four lives in the pause between her sentences: Nell's, Edith's, the widow's, her own. She let the pause run one beat longer than comfort allowed.

"The woman I relieved on the Manchester train yesterday," Bram said. "At Crewe. Dark coat, dark hat, a child asleep against her shoulder. I only thought— children on trains, one doesn't look, does one. But she got off at Crewe, I'm nearly sure. Or Macclesfield. I wasn't attending; I was reading." She shook her head, self-disgusted. "I'll swear I'd remember a face like that if I'd truly seen it. Poor lamb. She looks frightened half to death even in the photograph."

"Macclesfield," the sergeant repeated, and the pencil came out.

"Or Crewe. Truly, I couldn't say which — the train stopped so often I lost count. But neither's Buxton, thank God, so whoever she is, she isn't where I'm going, and I'm already late for a lady with a thermometer in her mouth and opinions about punctuality." She raised her wrist and consulted a watch she had set deliberately eleven minutes fast that morning. "Sergeant, may I? The ford road was flooded Tuesday, they told me at the last inn, so I've come round the long way and it's black as a boot out here."

The plain-clothes man hadn't moved from the door. Rain dripped off the brim of his soft hat onto the sill of the window. "Mind if we have a look at the car, miss? Procedure."

"Procedure," Bram agreed pleasantly, and her hands, resting on the wheel, did not tremble, because she had ordered them not to, and they were her hands, damn them, and they obeyed. "Though you'll find nothing but a rug, a flask, a toolbox, and a map of Cheshire that's done me more harm than good."

They looked. Of course they looked. The sergeant lifted the rug — the tartan rug, under which, twelve inches below the floorboards of her attention, the folded coat lay with its thirty-one sewn leaves — and shook it out with a flourish that scattered drops across her skirt, and found nothing, because the coat was beneath the seat cushion now, had been beneath the cushion since the last village, because she had moved it twice already today and would move it twice again, because a hiding place is only safe while it is moving. The plain-clothes man walked the length of the bonnet and opened the tool locker and stirred the spanners with one finger, and closed it. The other one — she had nearly forgotten him, which was dangerous, he was the dangerous one — crouched at the rear and examined the spare tyre and the luggage grid, empty, save for a leather valise containing a nightdress, a change of stockings, a hairbrush with silver-backed mirror, and Mrs. Quill's get-well parcel: a jar of honey, two lemons, a shawl. Props. All props. She had packed the life of a fiction into that valise stitch by stitch, and now she watched a stranger's fingers hover over it and thought, with a calm that frightened her more than any trembling could, *open the honey, please, open the honey, smell it, believe it.*

He did not open the honey. He straightened, wiped his hands on his handkerchief, and looked at her through the windscreen for a long, level moment — a look designed to make a guilty person speak, fill silence, apologise for existing. Bram met it with the mild, faintly bored patience of a woman who has been delayed by officialdom before and expects to be again, and inside her skull the clerk noted: *he is waiting for me to explain things nobody has asked about. Do not explain things nobody has asked about.*

The silence stretched. The rain filled it. Behind the wall the lantern swung.

"Well," Bram said at last, briskly, to the sergeant, ignoring the silent man entirely, "if there's nothing else? Only — should I ask after the child at the inns along the way? I'll be passing through several villages, and women notice children. We can't help it." She smiled, tired and warm. "If she's on this road, I'll keep an eye. You have Inspector Crake's direction there — shall I mention I've spoken to you? He seems to have the whole country looking. It must be serious."

Something crossed the sergeant's face at the name — a tightening, quickly mastered, and Bram's stomach dropped through the floor of the scene into a cold cellar beneath, because she knew that tightening. It was the look of a man who has been told, recently and emphatically, whom not to disappoint. Crake's reach had arrived here before she had. Crake's instructions had arrived here. Perhaps Crake himself was not far behind.

"No need to trouble yourself, madam." The sergeant stepped back and lifted the barrier himself, swinging the plank aside with the trestles grinding on wet stone. "On your way. Mind the ford road's shut — take the high route through Disley."

"Through Disley, thank you. Good luck with the little girl." And she released the brake, and let the clutch up smooth as cream, and drove through the gap they had made for her without once letting her eyes go to the mirror.

She did not breathe properly for two miles. She counted the miles instead, and at two she made herself exhale, and at three her hands began to shake, fine and violent, on the wheel, and she let them shake because there was no one to see, and because shaking was the body's honest receipt for what the mind had just signed.

*Persons using her for purposes of concealment.* So they had framed it — not *missing child*, but *child in danger*, child as victim, child as evidence against whoever loved her. Crake was building the story in advance, the way Halloran built stories, the way governments build stories: tell it often enough at roadblocks and railway stations and it becomes true by sheer repetition. If they found Nell, they would not find a witness. They would find proof that the conspirators had taken her.

And the photograph had been taken in London, on a street, weeks ago or days. Which meant Crake had known Nell's face since before the checkpoints went up. Which meant every checkpoint from here to Manchester was not a search. It was a filter with one mesh size, cut to the shape of a child in a blue coat.

Bram drove through Disley with the wipers slapping and the ledger pressing warm against the seat cushion beneath her, thirty-one leaves of paper that suddenly weighed as much as the car. Ahead, the lights of Stockport smeared upward through the rain like a city drowning in its own glow. Manchester was twenty miles further. The journalist's office was twenty-one. Friday was tomorrow.

She stopped at the first quiet lane beyond the town, engine idling, wipers going, and reached under the cushion, and drew out the coat, and held it against her chest for one full minute, feeling the stiff rectangular ghost of the pages through the wool, saying nothing, promising nothing, because promises made in cars at midnight had a way of becoming debts collected by other people.

Then she folded the coat into her lap, to stay there now, against her body, where it could not be separated from her without separating her from herself, and pulled back onto the road.

Behind her, far back at the bend above Hazel Grove, she saw the barrier swing shut across the carriageway, and the lanterns regather, and a motorcycle's headlamp blink awake — turning, she was almost sure, not north toward Buxton.

South. Toward the telephone. Toward Crake.

She pressed the accelerator down, and the Wolseley climbed out of the valley into the rain.
