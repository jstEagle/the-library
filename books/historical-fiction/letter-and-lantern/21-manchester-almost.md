## 21. Manchester, Almost

The offices of *The Northern Sentinel* smelled of ink, cold tea, and the particular staleness of rooms where men worked through the night and slept badly in their chairs. Edith Marlowe had been in a great many such rooms over the years — print shops raided by police, committee rooms turned into sanctuaries for fugitive pamphlets, once even a cellar beneath a Birmingham bakery where she had dictated an entire manifesto by candlelight while constables tramped the street above — and she had learned to read them the way a sailor reads weather. This one told her the paper was struggling. It told her the editor was brave but poor. It told her that if what she carried in her head was worth anything at all, it would have to be worth more than the building.

"You'll forgive the state of it." Joseph Ainsworth, the political editor, cleared a chair of proofs and a dead fern with the apologetic energy of a man who had not expected a famous woman in his office before nine o'clock. "We lost two advertisers last month. Both of them wrote to say it wasn't about the Vote."

"Everything is about the Vote," Edith said, "whether they admit it or not."

He laughed, then caught himself, glancing at the door as though the laugh might be reported. That was new, she thought. In 1908 he would have laughed loudly enough to shake the windows. Four years of Cat and Mouse and forcible feeding and ministers lying through their teeth in the Commons had taught even the press to lower its voice. She disliked it. She disliked everything that made brave men quiet.

"I've read your wire twice," Ainsworth said. "You understand what you're asking. If Halloran's people can prove we printed this — if they can prove we *knew* and didn't go to the police first—"

"They will not need to prove anything," Edith said. "They will simply assert it, and half the London papers will print the assertion before breakfast. I'm asking you to be faster than they are."

"And if I'm not?"

"Then I shall be arrested, the evidence will be seized, and in eighteen months' time some historian will write a footnote about a courier who died on the Stockport road for nothing." She said it lightly. Lightness was a discipline, like any other. "I've been a footnote before. They're not so bad. At least footnotes are accurate."

Ainsworth looked at her for a long moment. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the ink-stained fingers of a man who still set his own corrections rather than trust the compositors. "They say you don't sleep," he said. "That you work twenty hours a day and eat like a bird and have never once been seen to weep, even at Holloway gates."

"Whoever says that has never watched me eat," Edith said. "Sit down, Mr. Ainsworth. Let us do the work while we are both still free men and women."

* * *

The work took three hours, and it was good work, and she threw herself into it with the whole of her attention because attention was the only prayer she had ever believed in. The ledger pages Bram had memorized — no, not memorized; Bram did not yet know she had memorized them, standing in the hallway with her cap in her hands while Edith pretended to read — were transcribed onto fresh paper in Ainsworth's hand, in his office, so that no handwriting of hers would appear anywhere near the story. The photographs were copied by a man Ainsworth trusted, a stooped little fellow called Pell who said almost nothing and photographed everything, including, Edith suspected, her own face from three angles while pretending to adjust his lens.

She let him. If this went wrong, the country ought to know exactly whose face had tried.

"The sequence matters," she said, tapping the layout sheet. "Photographs first — the crest, the courier's effects, the burn mark on the ledger's corner. Then the ledger entries, dated, with the payments to the three men on the Stockport road. Then, and only then, my statement. The reader must meet the corpse before they meet me, or they'll decide I killed him myself for the publicity."

"That's cynical," Ainsworth said.

"That's Tuesday," Edith said. "Cynicism is only optimism that has learned arithmetic."

He wrote it down. She noticed that. Whatever else the next forty-eight hours held, Joseph Ainsworth was going to quote her somewhere, and she found she minded less than she should have. Vanity, her mother would have called it. Her mother had called most things vanity.

At noon a boy brought sandwiches and a copy of that morning's *Manchester Guardian*, and Edith read every word of it, including the shipping notices, because a woman waiting is either reading everything or thinking too much, and she had promised herself years ago never to think too much. Thinking too much was how one arrived at the question *what if Bram doesn't come*, and that question was a corridor with no doors off it, and she had walked corridors like that before, in Holloway, counting the days until a window showed her the season changing without her.

Bram would come. Bram drove like a woman being chased by her own conscience, which was, in Edith's private taxonomy, the fastest kind of driver there was.

She had not wanted Bram to take the ledger. She had argued against it — quietly, in the kitchen, with Nell asleep upstairs and the oilcloth packet open between them like a wound — and she had been overruled not by argument but by that look Bram got, the one that meant her mind had already left the room and was out on some dark road doing eighty miles an hour toward whatever she had decided to break herself against. You could not argue with Bram Vane when she wore that look. You could only make sure someone knew which direction she had gone.

So Edith had done the sensible thing. She had written down the direction.

It sat now in her breast pocket: a folded page giving the route, the checkpoints, the names of the Quaker family sheltering Nell, and a statement — her own, sworn before a solicitor Ainsworth produced from somewhere — setting out the whole affair from the knock at the door onward. If she was arrested, that page would find its way to Ainsworth within the hour. If *Bram* was arrested, God forbid, the same page would name her, describe her, and demand that the *Sentinel* raise such a storm about a missing driver that no minister's fixer would dare make her disappear quietly.

"You're very thorough," Ainsworth observed, watching her seal it.

"I am very frightened," Edith said, "and thoroughness is what frightened people do instead of screaming."

* * *

The afternoon went long. Manchester in October did not so much grow dark as surrender to it, the gaslights coming up along Deansgate one by one like a concession reluctantly granted. Rain started around four — fine, needle-fine, the sort that got inside coats and stayed there — and the office windows ran with it, turning the street below into a smear of umbrellas and omnibuses and one policeman who stopped outside the building, looked up for a long moment, and moved on.

Edith stood at that window for eleven minutes after the policeman left. She counted the minutes because counting was better than wondering whether he had recognized her silhouette.

"He does that," Ainsworth said behind her. "Every day since September. We think they're checking whether Pell is receiving post from London."

"Do they know about you?"

"They know about everybody," Ainsworth said. "That's rather the age we live in, Mrs. Marlowe. The question isn't whether they watch. It's whether what they see is worth acting on." He came to stand beside her, hands clasped behind his back, a small stooped man silhouetted against the wet glass. "Yours will be worth acting on."

"Yes," Edith agreed. "Which is why the timing must be perfect. Once the edition is on the streets, arresting me accomplishes nothing except confirming the story. Before it's on the streets—" She shrugged. "Before, I am merely a woman who was somewhere she shouldn't have been, carrying something she cannot produce, and Crake can build whatever he likes out of that."

"You know his name?"

"I know everyone's name. It's a professional habit." She had met Crake's kind before — not Crake himself, not yet, but his kind: the ambitious little men who did the dirty work and told themselves they were servants of order. They were always courteous. That was the horror of them. They held doors open for you on the way to the cells.

The wire machine in the corner chattered suddenly, and both of them turned to it as though it were a bird that had begun to sing. Ainsworth crossed the room and tore off the tape, scanned it, and his shoulders came down half an inch.

"Birmingham desk," he said. "Nothing. Just the evening markets."

"Of course," Edith said.

But she noted the moment, filed it away: the way her heart had jumped at the sound, the way disappointment had a taste, faintly metallic, like sucking a coin. She had not expected to be disappointed. She had told herself all afternoon that she was here to prepare, not to hope, and yet the wire's chatter had gone through her like a current, and its silence afterward was louder than its noise.

Hope, she thought, was the one argument she had never managed to win against herself.

* * *

At seven o'clock Ainsworth went down to the composing room and Edith was alone in the office, and she used the hour the way she used all solitary hours: working.

She drafted the statement first. Not the legal one — that was done, sealed, in her pocket — but the public one, the words that would run beneath the ledger transcription if the worst happened and she was taken before the presses rolled. She wrote it four times. The first draft was furious, and fury made prose that rang false, like a bell with a crack in it. The second was dignified, and dignity was worse; dignity sounded like a eulogy, and she refused to attend her own. The third she burned in the grate, page by page, watching the ink flare green before the black came.

The fourth began:

*I am writing this knowing that it may be published as the last thing I ever write freely, and I find I do not mind, provided it is not the last thing.*

She kept going from there. Two hundred words. Three hundred. She wrote about the courier — she had given him a name in her mind, Elias Crane, because the ledger gave his initials and a man deserved more than initials — and about the child who had seen him die and been ordered to forget it, as though forgetting were a thing that could be ordered, as though memory obeyed Undersecretaries. She wrote about the frame being prepared for the movement, the bomb that had not yet gone off but already had authors assigned to it. And at the end she wrote:

*They will say I am a hysterical woman. I am. I am hysterical with grief for a dead man I never met, with rage for a living child who must learn to close her eyes at night, and with a fury at this government that I intend to express at full volume for as long as they leave me breath to do it. Hysteria is only history arriving faster than polite society can absorb it. Let them call the ambulance. I will keep shouting from the stretcher.*

She read it back twice, made one correction, and folded it into the same envelope as the legal statement. Then she sat quite still for a moment in the gaslight, listening to the rain and the distant clatter of the linotype machines below, and allowed herself — briefly, deliberately, the way one allows oneself a single glass of wine — to imagine what capture would actually mean.

Holloway again. She knew the cell she would get; they had put her in D Wing twice and the walls remembered her. She knew the feeding tube if it came to hunger strike, and it would come to hunger strike, because she had sworn it in 1909 and a woman who broke one oath to herself soon broke them all. She knew the Governor's tired courtesy, the doctors' pens, the wardresses who were kind in ways the regulations forbade and cruel in ways they encouraged. None of that frightened her. What frightened her was smaller and sharper: the newspaper photographs of Edith Marlowe being carried to the van, and beneath them, in three-point type, the government's version of why — and the possibility, the real possibility, that some part of the country would believe it, because the country had believed such things before, eagerly, and asked for more.

A famous name makes better headlines, she had told Bram. She had not added the rest of the sentence: *and better headlines cut both ways.* If she fell with the ledger unpublished, her fall would bury it. No editor in England would touch evidence delivered by a woman the Home Office had just branded a felon and a bomber. Her arrest would be the story; the courier's death would be a paragraph; Nell would be a runaway child recovered by a diligent constabulary, and Halloran's men would be commended in dispatches.

That was the arithmetic she had been doing all day under the cover of editing copy. That was why the timing mattered, why the wire's silence sat in her chest like swallowed stone, why she had written four drafts of a statement instead of one.

If Bram was late, Edith Marlowe was not merely going to prison. She was going to prison *uselessly*. And uselessness, she thought, staring out at the rain-blurred lights of Deansgate, was the only death she truly feared.

* * *

At half past eight Ainsworth came up with two cups of tea and the news that the forme was set, locked, and waiting on her word.

"We go to press at eleven," he said. "Or we hold it, and you take the night train back, and we pretend today never happened." He said it gently. He was offering her a door, and they both knew it.

"If I take the night train," Edith said, "Elias Crane stays dead for nothing, and a seven-year-old girl spends the rest of her life believing she imagined her father's murder. I have been threatened with a great many things, Mr. Ainsworth. Being useless to that child is not among them."

He nodded slowly, and she saw him decide — saw the exact moment the decision completed itself behind his eyes, the way a tide turns though the water looks the same. "Eleven o'clock," he said. "God help us if you're wrong about your driver."

"She's never been late," Edith said. "She's early, always, and she complains about other people's driving the entire way, and she reads my letters."

Ainsworth raised his eyebrows.

"Steams them open," Edith said. "Badly. She thinks I don't know." She found, to her surprise, that she was smiling. "I have known for fourteen months. I let her continue because a woman who reads everything is a woman who notices everything, and one day — I told myself — one day she would notice something that mattered."

"And has she?"

The wire chattered.

Both of them froze. Ainsworth reached it first this time, tearing the tape free, and Edith watched his face while he read — watched the blood leave it in stages, the way color leaves a sky at dusk. He read it twice. Then he held it out to her without a word, and his hand was not entirely steady.

*STOCKPORT ROAD CHECKPOINT REPORTING STOP WOMAN DRIVER LONE PASSED THROUGH EIGHT FIFTEEN STOP DESCRIPTION MATCHES ADVISORY STOP HAVE WIRED DEANSAGE POST AWAIT INSTRUCTIONS*

Edith read it three times. Eight fifteen. It was now twenty past nine. Bram had come through Stockport nearly an hour and a half ago, alone, matching the advisory — Crake's advisory, Crake's net, closing link by link — and had not arrived, and every minute since eight fifteen was a minute in which a car could be stopped, a coat searched, a lining unpicked by careful government fingers under a government lamp.

"She came through," Ainsworth said quietly. "She's ahead of schedule."

"Then where is she?" Edith heard her own voice go flat and cold, the campaign voice, the platform voice, and hated how much it sounded like armor and how much she needed armor just now. "Deansgate is forty minutes from Stockport. It has been ninety-five."

"She may have been cautious. Doubled back. Watched the checkpoint before—"

"Mr. Ainsworth." Edith folded the wire into quarters, very precisely, and put it in her breast pocket beside the statement, beside the route, beside everything else she carried for other people. "Hold the presses. Keep the forme locked. And send someone to stand at the top of your stairs, because if that car has been stopped, the men who stopped it will be here within the hour, and I intend to be holding my statement and looking composed when they arrive."

"And if she hasn't been stopped?" Ainsworth asked. "If she's simply late?"

Edith looked out the window, down at Deansgate, at the rain and the lamps and the long dark road leading south out of the city, empty in both directions as far as the glass would show her.

"Then I have misjudged her," she said, "which I have never once done, and I do not propose to begin tonight."
