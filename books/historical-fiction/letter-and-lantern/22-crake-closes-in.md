## 22. Crake Closes In

The Quaker meeting house on Alton Street smelled of beeswax and damp plaster, and Inspector Ambrose Crake found the combination offensive. He had been raised in rooms that smelled of coal smoke and boiled mutton and his father's disappointment, and he had spent thirty years making certain no one would ever mistake him for a man who belonged in a place like this — plain benches, bare walls, a congregation of people who believed silence itself was a form of prayer. Silence, in Crake's experience, was where guilt went to hide.

He stood at the front of the empty hall while Sergeant Pell interviewed the caretaker by the door. Morning light came through windows so clean they seemed not to exist at all; it lay across the floorboards like spilled milk.

"Name's Josiah Fenwick," Pell said, returning with his notebook open. "Caretaker and general factotum, sir. Says he keeps the keys, sweeps up, minds the stove."

"Ask him who minded the children."

Fenwick was sixty-odd, with a beard trimmed in the old fashion and hands folded on his knees as though they had never once done anything hurried. He looked at Crake the way a man looks at weather he has already decided not to complain about.

"We have children through sometimes," Fenwick said. "Mothers in prison, mothers working. It's what the Meeting does."

"I'm not interested in the Meeting's charity," Crake said pleasantly. "I'm interested in one particular child. A girl, seven or eight years of age. Brown hair, cut short — badly, as if done in a hurry. She'd have arrived within the past week, possibly at night, possibly brought by someone other than her parents." He laid Nell's photograph on the bench between them. It was a poor thing, taken from across a street, the girl's face half turned away, but Crake had studied it until he could draw it from memory: the stubborn set of the small chin, the eyes too serious for their years.

Fenwick did not look at the photograph. That, in itself, told Crake a great deal. An innocent man looks at a photograph; a man deciding something looks at the wall above it.

"I couldn't say," Fenwick said.

"You could say a great many things if you wished. You could say whether a woman came here on Thursday night. Tall, dark-haired, well-dressed — the sort of woman whose coat alone costs more than your year's wages."

The old man's hands tightened very slightly on his knees. Crake watched the knuckles whiten and relax, whitening and relaxing, a tide going out.

"I mind the building," Fenwick said. "People come and go. I don't ask names. The Meeting teaches that every soul is equal before God, Inspector, and I've found it saves time not to learn which ones are which."

Crake smiled. He had a smile he kept for precisely this kind of conversation — patient, almost warm, the smile of a man who is not going anywhere and has nowhere else he needs to be. "That's a very tidy philosophy. Here's mine. A courier was murdered three weeks ago on the North Road. His throat was opened by men paid out of funds I can trace, though I won't trouble you with how. Before he died, he passed something into other hands, and those hands passed it to a child — a child who saw everything, Mr. Fenwick, everything — and then abandoned her at a stranger's door with a note instructing whoever found her to *forget the face*. Now. Does that sound like the work of souls equal before God? Or does it sound like the work of men who will burn this building down around your ears the moment you stop being useful to them?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked — children's shoes, or pigeons, impossible to say.

"The Meeting has no knowledge of any such matter," Fenwick said at last, and his voice was steady, and his voice was also a lie, and both things were perfectly clear.

"No," Crake agreed. "Of course it doesn't." He took back the photograph, tapped it once against the bench, and pocketed it. "But I'll tell you what I do know, since we're being generous with information. I know the child left this house. I know she didn't leave by the front door, because my man watched the front door all Friday night. Which means there's another way out — a yard, perhaps, a lane behind the kitchens. And I know that when she went, she went in company. Because a milkman on Alton Street remembers seeing a little girl run along the backs at dawn on Saturday, holding the hand of a tall woman in a dark coat. The milkman remembered because he thought it odd, a lady of that quality walking the muck lanes at five in the morning." He let that sit. "He gave me a very good description. Good enough that I recognized it before he finished giving it."

Fenwick said nothing. His face said nothing. But Crake had spent his career reading faces that insisted on saying nothing, and this one was shouting.

"A Miss Vane," Crake said, tasting the name. "Bram Vane. She drives for Edith Marlowe. Twenty-eight years of age, dark-haired, carries herself like a soldier who's forgotten which war. You may tell your Friends whatever comforts them, Mr. Fenwick. Tell them I'm a brute, tell them I'm Halloran's creature — you're quite right on both counts, and I'd be the last to dispute it. But understand what you're sheltering. That child is the only living witness to the murder of Thomas Ackroyd, and there are men far less patient than I am who want her silenced. Every hour she sits in one of your plain little rooms drinking your plain little tea, she is a candle burning in a high wind. I am, believe it or not, the gentlest of the parties looking for her."

It was a lie wrapped in a truth wrapped in a threat, and Crake delivered it with the smoothness of long practice. He watched it land — not in Fenwick's face, which remained shut as a ledger, but in the slight forward lean of the old man's body, the instinctive movement toward defense of something unseen overhead. Toward the stairs.

There were children upstairs.

* * *

In the car afterward, Pell waited with the engine running and a question on his face.

"Well?" Crake said, settling into the seat and pulling off his gloves finger by finger.

"They moved her, sir. Night before last, I'd wager. Fenwick's stonewalling, but the scullery door's bolted from inside now, and there's straw in the yard that wasn't trampled down yet. Fresh."

"Yes. They moved her." Crake looked out at Alton Street, at the respectable grey fronts and lace curtains and a world that imagined itself uninvolved. "Which means Marlowe's people know we've found the Quakers. Which means they'll move her again, and again, and each move puts her in new hands, and each pair of new hands is another thread, and I only need one thread, Pell, and patience to pull it." He put his gloves away and drew his notebook instead. "What I want now is the woman. Vane."

"She's the driver, sir?"

"The driver, the shadow, and — if I'm any judge — the conscience of that household. Everyone watches Marlowe; she wants to be watched, it's her whole theatre. But it was Vane who walked the child through the muck lanes at dawn. Marlowe arranges martyrdoms. Vane does the carrying." He flipped back through pages of observation notes, weeks of them, gathered from men hired to watch the Holloway house and paid too little to be subtle and too much to be honest. "Here. Fourteen October. 'Miss Vane attended the shop of Mrs. Loxley, stationer, Chalton Street, purchased two books.' Seventeen October. 'Miss Vane posted letters at the Western district office, four items, one registered.' Twenty-first. 'Vane drove to Euston alone with a valise, returned same evening.'" He looked up. "A driver doesn't take trains alone with valises, Pell. Drivers drive. When the driver takes the train, she's carrying something she daren't trust to the road."

"Or visiting her mum."

"Everyone has a mother, Sergeant. Not everyone visits Euston twice in a fortnight." He closed the notebook. "Get me the railway police liaison. I want passenger lists — third class especially, women travelling alone, northbound, from the ninth of October onward. Cross-reference against every name that appears anywhere near that household: servants' relations, suffrage subscribers, anyone in Marlowe's correspondence files." He paused. "And pull the file on the household staff again. There was a cousin somewhere, wasn't there? A woman who came to clean in September and stopped coming."

Pell found it. "Ada Merrick, sir. Left service without notice mid-September. Last known lodging in Camden."

"Find her. If she's gone quiet, find her landlady. Landladies see everything and forget nothing except what they're paid to." Crake gazed through the windscreen as the car pulled away from the meeting house, its plain facade shrinking in the glass. "Marlowe thinks in headlines. She believes the country is watching, so she plays to the gallery — the march, the speech, the arrest she can turn into a sermon. But underneath the theatre there's a machine, and the machine runs on ordinary kindness. Quakers and landladies and milkmen and cousins. Ordinary kindness leaves footprints everywhere, Pell, because kind people talk to other kind people, and none of them ever imagine that a man like me might listen."

"And do you, sir? Listen?"

"I do nothing else." He settled back. "Take me to the telegraph office. We're done being patient."

* * *

The Central Telegraph Office on St. Martin's Le Grand hummed like a hive in winter, clerks moving among the brass fittings with the brisk piety of men handling the nation's nervous system. Crake had standing authority to send official wires under Home Office letterhead — an authority granted casually by a permanent secretary who owed Halloran several favors and thought nothing of lending the machinery of state to private purposes. It was, Crake reflected as he composed, the most elegant part of the whole arrangement: that the conspiracy needed no conspirators beyond the ones already paid, because the government would happily conspire against itself for free.

He wrote slowly, in the clipped grammar wires demanded, and he wrote four copies.

TO POLICE SUPERINTENDENT EUSTON STATION: BE ON LOOKOUT WOMAN AGED TWENTY-EIGHT DARK HAIR TRAVELLING ALONE OR WITH CHILD FEMALE SEVEN YEARS STOP BELIEVED CONVEYING DOCUMENTS MATERIAL TO MURDER INQUIRY NORTH ROAD STOP DETAIN AND WIRE INSPECTOR CRAKE NEW SCOTLAND YARD IMMEDIATELY STOP EXERCISE DISCRETION NO ALARM TO SUBJECT —

He crossed out *no alarm to subject*. Discretion was wasted ink. What he wanted was exactly the opposite: he wanted the net to make noise. A frightened courier made mistakes. A calm one did not. Let every inspector from London to Manchester be peering into every railway carriage; let Bram Vane feel the pressure of eyes at every platform; fear traveled faster than trains and arrived before she did.

He rewrote the wire cleaner:

WOMAN TWENTY-EIGHT DARK HAIR MAY BE ACCOMPANIED BY CHILD GIRL SEVEN BROWN HAIR SHORT CUT STOP SUBJECTS OF URGENT INQUIRY RE MURDER AND THEFT OF OFFICIAL PROPERTY STOP IF SIGHTED DO NOT APPROACH OBSERVE AND WIRE CRAKE SCOTLAND YARD STOP THIS MATTER TOUCHES MINISTERIAL INTERESTS HIGHEST PRIORITY —

*Ministerial interests.* Three words that would straighten every spine from Euston to Stockport. He sent the same to Crewe, to Stockport, and to the Manchester city force with instructions to watch Deansgate and the newspaper offices — for he had reasoned it out by lamplight the night before, working backward from the murdered courier's route, from the geography of Marlowe's alliances, from the simple fact that evidence was worthless unless it reached print. Whoever carried that oilcloth packet was carrying it to a journalist. There were only so many journalists in the north willing to touch such material, and only so many ways to reach them, and every way ran through three chokepoints that Crake now owned.

He paid the extra for urgent priority and watched the clerk feed his words into the machinery of the empire. In minutes they would be crackling down copper lines buried beneath England, arriving in stations where bored sergeants would pin them to boards beside lost-property notices, and the whole apparatus of the law — slow, stupid, magnificent — would begin turning its attention to one tall woman and one small girl.

"Will that be all, sir?" the clerk asked.

"One more." Crake took a fresh form and wrote it in his own hand, not for the network but for himself, a memorandum to be filed where only he could find it: *Quaker connection established. Child removed from Alton Street premises night of 24th, in company of female matching description of B. Vane. All checkpoints instructed. Recommend surveillance intensification at Marlowe residence, Holloway. Subject Vane to be treated as principal courier. Subject Marlowe — hold.*

Hold. That was the word that mattered, and he turned it over as he walked out into the cold bright afternoon. Edith Marlowe he could arrest tomorrow; warrants were being drafted already on charges old enough to vote themselves, public order offenses from marches past, obstruction, incitement — thin stuff, but sufficient. Arresting her now, though, would be a gift. It would make her a martyr before the evidence was secured, flood the papers with sympathy, and scatter her people into the kind of frantic, heroic chaos that hid couriers beautifully. No. The order of operations was everything. First the child. Then the packet. Then, with the proof of the bombing plot safely in hand — proof that would hang the framing on the movement's enemies rather than its friends — then Marlowe, quietly, on the old charges, and Halloran's problem solved entire.

Unless, of course, the packet reached Manchester first.

Crake stood on the office steps and looked north, as if the distance itself might be persuaded to yield him a view. Trains were leaving Euston even now, pluming steam into the low grey sky, carrying hundreds of anonymous passengers toward the chokepoints where his wires were arriving ahead of them like outriders. Somewhere in that stream of faces was a woman who had read stolen letters and told herself she was nobody, and a child who had been ordered to forget a face and could not.

He touched his hat brim to the north, a small ironic courtesy.

"Run, Miss Vane," he murmured. "Run all the way to Manchester. I've wired every door, and every door has my name on it now."

Then he descended the steps, hailed his car, and gave the driver an address in Holloway — because while the nets were closing along the rails, there was one house on one street where the lights burned late, and a man who listened for a living knew that the best place to catch a courier was not the road, but the window she kept looking back at.

Behind him, in the telegraph office, the last of his wires clicked out its urgent alphabet, racing the trains north.
