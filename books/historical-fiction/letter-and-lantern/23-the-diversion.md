## 23. The Diversion

Edith Marlowe had spent twenty years learning the arithmetic of crowds.

She knew how many women it took to fill a street so that the newspapers could not call it a handful. She knew how many policemen a superintendent would send for two hundred marchers and how few he would send for forty. She knew that a crowd was not merely a number of bodies but a number of eyes — that every woman who came out of her kitchen or her counting-house to stand in the cold was an eye turned upon the state, and that the state, like any creature caught staring, grew uneasy and did foolish things.

Tonight she intended to make the state do something very foolish indeed.

She stood at the window of Mrs. Pankhurst's old rooms on Berners Street — borrowed rooms now, lent by a friend of a friend, the kind of lodging that appeared on no lease and paid rent in cash. Below, the lamps of Oxford Street burned in their long receding line, and the traffic moved through them like beads drawn along a wire: omnibuses, hansoms, a motor-van from Selfridge's with its great painted eye rolling past. Somewhere down that glittering throat of London, Bram was loading the reassembled ledger into the Darracq and checking the tyre pressures for the third time, because Bram checked things three times when she was frightened and pretended she was only being thorough.

Edith pressed her fingertips against the cold glass.

*You will not be there when she comes through,* she told herself. *That is the point. That is the whole architecture of the thing.*

The plan had been argued over for two days, and Bram had fought it with a stubbornness Edith might have admired in any other circumstance. Let the diversion be someone else, Bram had said. Let it be a hired speaker, a stranger, a name the papers wouldn't miss. And Edith had answered as plainly as she knew how: that was precisely why it could not be. A stranger drew nothing. Surveillance did not move for nobodies; surveillance moved for names, and there was no name in England that drew inspectors and photographers and column-inches like hers. If she stood on a platform in Islington at eight o'clock tonight, Crake's watchers would stand with her. Every man he could spare would be counting heads in a hall four miles from where a green Darracq ran north through the dark with a dead courier's ledger sewn into a driver's coat.

It was simple. It was even elegant. Edith distrusted elegance in plans the way she distrusted charm in men — it usually meant someone had fallen in love with the idea before testing it against the world.

And there was the other thing, the thing she did not say aloud, which sat beneath the argument like ballast under a keel. If anyone must be taken, let it be her. She had rehearsed arrest so often it had become a kind of second language. She knew what the cells were like at Holloway, the smell of carbolic and wet stone, the particular insult of a forcible examination. She had been fed through a tube once and had made them describe it in Hansard. A famous name in a police van was worth ten anonymous ones — it filled columns, it embarrassed ministers, it made the ordinary reader ask questions the movement could never afford to buy with ink alone. Her capture was not merely survivable. It was *useful*. She had built a life such that even her ruin served.

What she had not anticipated — what she refused, even now, to look at squarely — was how much heavier the arithmetic became when one of the variables was a girl with grease on her knuckles and a child's coat folded over her arm.

* * *

At half past six she dressed for a public meeting as though dressing for battle, which is what it was, and always had been.

The costume mattered. She chose the violet wool — the colour of dignity, they called it, though Edith privately thought violet was simply the colour of bruises grown fashionable — and the white blouse with the high collar that photographed cleanly under flash-lamps. She pinned the brooch, the small silver lantern her mother had left her, low on the lapel where a lens would find it. In the glass a composed woman looked back at her, fifty-one years old, grey coming in at the temples like frost advancing across a field, jaw set in the expression the sketchists loved and her enemies called arrogant and Bram, once, very late at night in the motor outside a meeting in Bristol, had called *magnificent*, and then gone scarlet and pretended to be examining the carburettor.

Edith smiled at the reflection. The reflection did not entirely believe it.

On the table lay the papers she had spent the afternoon preparing: a statement for the press in the event of her arrest, written in her flattest, most quotable prose; a letter to the *Manchester Guardian* that a trusted hand would post if word did not come by Friday noon; and a shorter document, sealed, addressed to no newspaper at all. She looked at that one longest. She did not open it. Some letters are written for the writer more than the reader, and this was one, and she had found in recent weeks — since a silent child arrived at her door carrying a packet wrapped in oilcloth, since Bram began looking at her with something raw and unguarded behind the usual driver's deference — that there were more of those letters in her than she had ever allowed.

A knock at the door: their hostess, a widow named Mrs. Aldous with quick frightened eyes and a spine of iron underneath. "Car's below, Mrs. Marlowe. Man watching the corner's lit his pipe twice in five minutes. They're on you already."

"Good," said Edith. "Let them count me into the motor. I want them certain where I am."

Mrs. Aldous hesitated in the doorway. She was not a political woman — she had lost a son at Spion Kop and considered all grand causes varieties of weather — but she said, quietly, "There's tea in the flask, if the evening runs long. And if it runs longer—" She stopped herself, and Edith understood: *if you don't come back.*

"Thank you," Edith said. "Mind the house. Mind nothing else."

* * *

The hall on Essex Road, Islington, held perhaps three hundred when she rose to speak, and it had been packed to that number partly by persuasion and partly by the oldest trick in the organizer's book: she had let slip, through two separate channels, that Marlowe would appear *and that something would be announced*. Nothing would be announced. The announcement was her body on a platform, visible, fixed, timed — a lighthouse switched on deliberately so that every ship hunting them would steer toward the light and away from the dark water where the real cargo ran.

From the moment her motor turned into Upper Street she felt them attach, the way remoras attach to a shark. A black Humber with two men in it fell in fifty yards behind. A man in a bowler walked past the hall doors twice while she was handing her coat to the steward, and the third time he did not walk past at all but settled against the railings opposite with the patient stillness of a man being paid by the hour. Through the high dusty windows Edith could see the gas lamp where he'd stationed himself, and beyond it, dimly, the glow of another pipe being lit.

*Count us,* she thought. *Count every head. Write it all in your neat little book.* The fury that rose in her was old and familiar and useful, and she banked it the way she'd learned to bank it, feeding it forward into the voice instead of letting it scatter.

She spoke for forty minutes.

She spoke about the Conciliation Bill and its slow strangulation in the House; about Cat and Mouse and the women who were released, sicken, and seized again like tide going in and out; and then, near the end, she lifted the register of her voice to the place where the room stopped being listeners and became a single animal, and she told them what was coming. Not the specifics — never the specifics — but the shape of it. "There are men in this government," she said, "who have decided that we are easier to destroy than to answer. They will try, before many weeks are out, to hang their own crime around our necks. When they do — and some of you will read of it in your morning papers — I want you to remember this room, and this hour, and know that whatever they print, we did not do it. We break windows. We break laws. We do not break faith." The hall went up like straw. Women were on their feet, hats trembling, the organist striking up though no one had asked him, and Edith stood in the noise with her hands flat on the lectern and thought, with a strange cold clarity beneath the triumph: *This is the last speech I may ever give free.*

Because she had counted the exits, and she had counted the watchers, and the numbers did not lie. Two men inside by the doors, pretending to be latecomers who never took their seats. The Humber outside. The pipe-smoker at the railings. And somewhere behind all of them, pulling the strings with his soft careful hands, Inspector Crake — who did not strike when the quarry stood in a crowd, because crowds made witnesses; he struck on the pavement afterward, or at the door of a house, in the quiet places where a body could be bundled without commentary. He would wait until she left. He would follow her home, or stop her on the road, or — if Halloran's nerve failed and the minister wanted the story buried before the ledger surfaced — he would not wait at all.

She descended from the platform into the wings and found the steward, a broad-faced Yorkshirewoman named Miss Tredwell who had been prison-struck twice and feared nothing on earth except rats.

"Back way," Miss Tredwell said. "Motor's there."

"I'm going out the front," Edith said.

Miss Tredwell stared. Then her face did the thing faces did when they understood — a slow settling, like earth after rain. "They're on you."

"They're on me, and I mean to keep them on me until midnight at least. Walk me to the door, and look as though we've discussed nothing but the price of coal."

* * *

The front steps of the hall at ten minutes past nine were a little theatre, and Edith played her part standing up.

She paused at the top of the steps to let the stragglers see her — the last women pressing out into the cold, a few of them waiting deliberately to shake her hand because they sensed, with the unerring instinct of the faithful, that this appearance meant something. She gave the crowd her profile. She let the bowler-hatted man at the railings get his long look. Down the street the Humber's engine ticked over, and its passenger-side door opened and shut, and a second man strolled toward the steps with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, unhurried, positioning himself for the walk-along that preceded every arrest Crake had ever managed.

*Follow me,* Edith willed them. *Follow me home. Sit on my doorstep till dawn. Fill your books with my comings and goings. Every minute I am the story, a green motor is not.*

Her own car — a hired Austin, driven by poor reliable Mr. Fenn, who believed he was conveying her to a supper engagement — waited at the kerb. She descended the steps slowly, letting the whole street watch, and as she reached for the handle a young woman pushed forward from the knot of stragglers, a factory girl by her hands, and gripped Edith's wrist with both of hers.

"God go with you, ma'am," the girl said, loud enough for the listening street.

"He generally does," Edith said, "though He takes His time about it," and the laugh that went round broke the tension like bread, and she got into the car, and the door shut, and the trap closed around her exactly as she had designed it to close.

Through the rear window she watched the Humber pull out behind them. The bowler-hat crossed the road and got into a cab. Two more shapes detached themselves from a doorway she had not even known held watchers — good, Crake was spending generously, which meant he was frightened too, and frightened men commit errors — and the little convoy assembled itself and moved south through Islington with Edith Marlowe at its centre like a nail through a sheet of paper.

Mr. Fenn drove well and asked nothing. At the Angel they turned onto the City Road as instructed, a route chosen to pass beneath the eyes of as many witnesses as possible, and at each crossing Edith marked the following lights in the glass. She was aware of the clock on the dashboard — Fenn kept a fine brass clock, wound daily — and of what the clock meant. By eleven, Bram should be past the Stockport road. By midnight, Manchester. By morning, a journalist named Quillan whose printing office smelled of ink and courage would hold photographs of a ledger that could unseat a minister of the Crown.

And she would be — where? Perhaps in this car, running its decoy errands until dawn. Perhaps in a cell. Perhaps, if Crake had finally decided that the risk of leaving her loose outweighed the messiness of taking her publicly, face-down on a pavement with her hat in the gutter and her wrist bent back, while the newspapers wrote tomorrow what she had spent her whole life teaching them to write: *Militant Leader Seized.*

She found that her hands, resting in her lap, had closed into fists, and she opened them deliberately, finger by finger, and looked down at her palms as though reading something there.

She was not afraid. That was the curious thing, the thing she examined now with the dispassion of a naturalist: fear was present, yes, a live wire running through her chest, but it carried a different current than it had at her first arrest, fifteen years ago, when she had shaken so violently in the van that a stranger had held her hand the whole way to the yard. This was older fear, colder, almost administrative — the fear of a woman signing a document she has read carefully and means to honour anyway. She had offered herself up years ago, in the general way one offers anything to a cause one loves. Tonight was only the specific version of the offer, with a date attached.

But there was a new clause in the contract, and the new clause had a name, and the name was spoken in a low voice over engine noise and smelled faintly of petrol and machine oil, and the new clause terrified her far more thoroughly than Holloway ever had.

*If they take me,* she had told Bram, two nights ago, in the lamplit garage with the ledger pages spread between them like a dealt hand, *you finish the delivery. You do not turn back. You do not come for me. Swear it.*

Bram had looked at her across the workbench with an expression Edith had catalogued for months without naming: the look of a woman discovering that history is happening to her personally, and that the person it is happening *through* is the one person she cannot bear to lose. And Bram had sworn it — had given her word with a soldier's flatness that fooled neither of them — and Edith had known, with the certainty she otherwise reserved for mathematics, that Bram Vane would break that oath the moment the oath conflicted with her heart, and that Edith would have to arrange the world so that breaking it was impossible.

Hence the diversion. Hence Islington. Hence the convoy of watchers trailing her through the City Road like ducklings after a hen. As long as every eye in Crake's employ was fixed on Edith Marlowe, no eye could fall on a green Darracq running north. She was not merely bait tonight. She was armour — worn, for once, by someone other than the cause.

"Mrs. Marlowe?" Fenn said, catching her eye in the mirror. "We've company again — third motor since Pentonville. Black Napier. Never seen it before."

Edith turned and looked back through the rain-beaded glass. The Napier rode close, closer than surveillance needed to ride, and its windows were curtained, which surveillance cars' windows never were. Her stomach performed a slow deliberate drop, the body's honest arithmetic outrunning the mind's.

Curtained windows meant Crake. Curtained windows meant tonight, here, on a dark stretch of road with witnesses scarce.

"Mr. Fenn," she said, and heard her own voice come out level, almost pleasant, pitched for the listening street as much as for the driver. "I find I've changed my mind about supper. Take us round by the canal — slowly, if you please."

Ahead, the black Napier's headlamps bloomed brighter, and swung wide to pass.
