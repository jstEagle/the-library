## 24. The Third Checkpoint: Deansgate

The rain had followed us the whole way from Stockport, thin and needling, as if the sky itself were trying to pick a lock. I drove with the wipers going and my heart going faster, and in the seat beside me Nell sat very straight, the way she'd learned to sit when she was frightened — spine like a poker, chin up, a small imitation of Edith that no one had taught her.

The doll sat on her lap.

I had sewn it shut at four that morning in a rented room above a chandler's shop, by the light of a lamp turned low, with Nell asleep on the bed behind me and a needle borrowed from the landlady and thread pulled from the hem of my own petticoat. The doll was a shabby thing, a rag body with a painted face gone soft at the cheeks, one of the few possessions Nell had carried out of the Quaker house when she ran. Its back had opened along an old seam with hardly any persuasion at all. Inside, folded small as communion wafers, lay the ledger — every page I hadn't burned at Crewe, plus the three survivors of that night, scorched brown at their corners but legible, every name intact. Halloran's name. Crake's name. The payments, the dates, the courier who never arrived because two men held him down beside a canal while a third did what they'd paid him to do.

I had stitched the seam closed with eleven stitches. I knew the number because I counted them to keep my hands steady, and then I counted them again on the drive north whenever the fear rose in my throat like water.

*Eleven stitches between Halloran and the front page.*

"Miss Vane." Nell's voice, very small under the drumming rain. "There's men in the road."

I had seen them already. Deansgate narrowed ahead where barriers crossed it — sawhorses, a police tarpaulin snapping wet in the wind, lanterns swinging on hooks, and beyond them the long grey face of Manchester in the murk, chimneys and cranes and the newspaper office six streets away, close enough now that I could almost smell the ink. A sergeant stood at the barrier with a clipboard sheltered under his cape. And behind him, hands clasped at his back, hat beaded with rain he didn't seem to notice —

Crake.

My foot wanted to lift off the accelerator. I made it stay down. There was nowhere else to go; there had been nowhere else to go since London. Every road out of the city was a funnel and we were in the neck of it, and the only question left in the world was whether Inspector Crake would look inside a child's doll.

"Bram." Nell used my first name only when the world had gone wrong. "Bram."

"I see him, love." I kept my voice level, kept it warm, the way you talk to a horse before a storm. "Now listen to me carefully, because this is the last part and it matters more than all the rest. Do you remember what I told you?"

"We're taking Dolly to be mended," she recited. "Her seams came open and we're taking her to the shop."

"That's right. And if anyone asks you questions, what do you do?"

"Cry," she said.

"And do you think you can do that? Truly?"

She looked down at the doll. When she raised her face again there was something in it older than eight years old ought to be, something I hated every man in that road for putting there. "I've been practicing," she said, "since Euston."

God help me. God forgive me. I put my hand briefly on hers where it gripped the doll, and I felt through the rag and the stuffing the crisp edges of thirty-one pages, and then the car rolled up to the barrier and I braked, and the world contracted to lantern-light and rain.

* * *

Crake came around to my side himself. He didn't hurry. That was always his manner — the unhurried man, the man for whom time was a servant rather than a master, though I knew better now, I knew how fast the sand was running through his particular glass, how many wires he'd sent ahead, how much of his future hung on stopping one woman and one child in the rain on Deansgate.

"Miss Vane." He touched his hat. Rain dripped off the brim. "A long way from Holloway Road."

"Business in Manchester," I said. "And you, Inspector? A long way from your desk."

"My work takes me about." His eyes went past me, unhurried, to the passenger seat. To Nell. To the bundle of her, the coat, the doll clutched to her chest with both arms the way children clutch things they have decided are theirs forever. "And who have we here?"

"This is Nell. She's unwell." I let just enough roughness into my voice, the irritation of any tired woman stopped on a wet evening. "She's had a fever. If you mean to keep us standing in the rain, Inspector, you'll answer to her doctor tomorrow, because I'll certainly tell him."

"A fever." Crake crouched slightly, bringing his face level with the window, and I watched Nell's lower lip begin its tremble — genuine, God help her, not one ounce of it performed. "Poor little mite. And where are you two bound?"

"The Royal Infirmary has a children's ward," I said. "Her aunt works near here. We're expected."

"Expected." He said the word as if tasting it, rolling it against his teeth. He straightened. "Sergeant, take the lady's particulars. Name, address, purpose of journey. Standard form."

"Yes, sir." The sergeant flipped a page on his clipboard, and I saw, in the wash of the lantern, what was clipped there beneath the forms. A photograph, soft-edged, taken from too far away: a small figure in a dark coat holding hands with a taller one outside a brick house with white trim. The Quaker house. The date stamped underneath was four days old.

They had been looking for her since the moment she ran. Perhaps longer. Perhaps from the beginning, and everything since — Euston, Crewe, Stockport Road — had been a net drawn slowly closed, and I had driven straight down its throat believing myself clever.

My hand on the wheel was perfectly still. I don't know how. Some animal part of me took over the business of appearing calm while the rest of me stood in a burning house watching the stairs go.

"You'll want to search the motor, I expect," I said, because refusing would be worse. I got out. The rain hit my face like a handful of needles. "Mind the mud on the running boards. And the spare is loose under the seat, so don't startle yourself reaching for it."

"Thorough," Crake observed mildly.

"I've nothing to hide, Inspector. Only a sick child and a long day."

Two constables went through the car with lanterns while Crake stood beside me, close enough that I could smell the wet wool of his coat and, beneath it, tobacco and ink. The sergeant wrote my particulars. I gave them truthfully — my real name, Edith's real address, because a lie found out was a confession and a truth was merely a woman driving her employer's goddaughter to a hospital. Truth was the last disguise left to me and I wore it like armor plate.

"They say you read letters," Crake said suddenly.

The rain filled the silence for me, mercifully, for a second or two.

"Pardon?"

"At the trial. They'll say a great many things." He was watching the constables pull up the rear seat, and he spoke as if remarking on the weather. "It's funny, Miss Vane, what people will do with paper. Steal it, burn it, sew it into linings." He turned his head. His eyes were pale and patient and bottomless. "You'd be amazed what turns up, in the end."

"Then I'm glad I'm only carrying a sick child."

"Mm." He glanced down at Nell through the window. She had begun, quietly, to cry — not the performance she'd promised, but something worse, something true: the exhausted sobbing of a child who had run through dark streets four nights ago and slept between railway wagons and been moved twice more since, and who could see, even at eight years old, that the pale-eyed man was asking about her. Her shoulders shook. Tears cut channels through the grime of travel on her cheeks. She pressed the doll against her mouth.

"There, now," Crake said, and his voice changed — softened, dropped into something almost kind, and that kindness frightened me more than anything else he might have done, because I had read enough of his correspondence to know exactly what his kindness cost. "No need for tears, little miss. No one's going to hurt you."

Nell cried harder.

"Inspector." I stepped between them, deliberately, my shoulder cutting the line of his sight. "She's ill and exhausted and she doesn't know you. Whatever business you have, have it with me."

For a moment something flickered behind his patience — annoyance, perhaps, or calculation recalculating. Then: "Of course. Forgive me." He gestured, and the sergeant tore off my receipt and handed it over. "You're free to proceed, Miss Vane. The Infirmary is straight on, then left at the church." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Though I wonder — the child's coat. May I?"

The word *coat* went through me like ice water. The coat. The coat whose lining had held the ledger until two days ago, whose stitching I'd picked out in a Manchester lodging house and resewn empty, whose ghost I'd carried ever since. On the hook at the chandler's shop it hung even now, innocent and hollow, and Crake had never seen it — and yet he asked for it, asked for the one garment associated with the child in his photograph, and I understood that he was fishing, casting, letting me decide which way to jump.

"The coat?" I frowned. "She's wearing it."

"So she is." He looked at it — the plain dark wool, buttoned to the throat, damp at the shoulders. "May I look at it? It's only that a child matching her description was reported missing some days ago, and coats carry marks. Initials. Laundry tags. You'd be amazed."

Inside the doll, thirty-one pages. Three feet away, the man who had written half the entries. Between them, a weeping girl.

I heard my own voice come out steady, bored, faintly insulted — and marveled at it, the way you marvel at a stranger.

"She's a child in a fever, Inspector, and you want to undress her at a police barrier in the rain. Go ahead. Write it in your report. I'll write it in mine."

The sergeant shifted. One of the constables by the car suddenly found his lantern fascinating. Crake held my gaze for a long, long moment, and I thought: *he knows. He knows and he cannot prove it and we both know that proof is the whole game.* A minister's fixer could not strip-search a weeping child on an open street without witnesses and paperwork and a reason, not when the newspapers were already sniffing, not when Halloran's beautiful narrative needed everything quiet and orderly. His power lived entirely inside procedure, and procedure had no page for a crying girl and her doll.

"No," Crake said at last. "That won't be necessary." He stepped back, touched his hat brim again. Rain ran from it. "I hope the little one feels better soon. Manchester weather is hard on children."

"It's hard on everyone," I said.

I got back into the car. My hands found the wheel. Somewhere far away, my heart resumed.

"Drive on," Crake told the sergeant, and the barrier swung aside, and I let out the clutch so smoothly that no one watching would ever know that moving my left foot required every ounce of will I possessed.

We passed the sawhorses. We passed the lanterns. We passed Crake standing in the rain with his hands clasped behind him, and I did not look at him, and I felt his eyes on the back of my neck the whole length of Deansgate like a hand laid there, light as a moth, heavy as a headstone.

* * *

Four streets. I counted them like stitches.

At the corner of the church Nell's sobs finally broke apart into hiccups, and then into the terrible quiet that follows. "Did I do it right?" she whispered.

"You did it perfectly."

"He said no one would hurt me." Her voice wobbled. "Was he lying?"

I wanted to say *yes*, instantly, cleanly, the way you comfort children. But she had earned better than clean lies, this child who had seen her father killed and carried the proof of it in her arms across half of England.

"He believes he wasn't," I said. "That's how men like him manage it. Now hold tight. Two more turnings."

The *Manchester Courier*'s offices rose out of the murk at the end of the street — a sooty stone face, windows mostly dark at this hour except the top floor, where a single light burned yellow behind rain-streaked glass. Waiting for us. Edith had wired ahead; the journalist, a lean, sharp-eyed man named Ambrose Fenn, had been sitting on this story for a week with his typesetter primed and his nerve fraying. All he lacked was the ledger. All that stood between those pages and the morning edition was a staircase, a door, and sixty yards of wet cobbles.

I pulled into the alley beside the building and killed the engine, and the sudden silence rang in my ears.

Nell didn't wait to be asked. She turned the doll over in her lap and began working at the seam with her small blunt fingers, finding the loose end of thread I'd left for her — I had planned even that, rehearsed it with her three times in the lodging house — and her tongue showed at the corner of her mouth in concentration. Eleven stitches. She got the fifth one loose as footsteps sounded at the alley mouth, and my blood froze until I saw it was only a dray horse hauling beer barrels, and then the seventh, and the ninth, and the doll gaped open like a small wound, and Nell drew out the folded pages, brown-cornered, rain-damp at their edges, and held them up to me with both hands the way you'd hold up bread in a famine.

"Take them," she said. "Quick, before anyone comes."

I took them. They weighed almost nothing. They weighed everything Halloran had built and everything Edith had given and everything Nell had witnessed beside a canal with lanterns swinging, and they were warm from riding against a child's chest for forty miles.

"Nell." I crouched by the car door so our eyes were level. Rain dripped off the alley eaves between us. "When I come back down, it will be finished. Do you understand? Tonight it gets printed, and printed means everyone, and everyone means it can never be used against you again. Your father's face—" My voice caught. I pushed through it. "Your father will get his name in the paper tomorrow, next to the truth. Everyone in England will read it."

She considered this with grave, wet eyes. Then she reached out and patted my cheek once, twice, the way you'd pat a horse before a storm.

"Hurry, then," she said.

I tucked the pages inside my blouse, flat against my ribs where no inspector's eye would ever fall, and I looked up at the yellow window at the top of the building, and I ran for the door.

Behind me, in the parked car, a child sat in the dark holding an empty doll — and I did not see, could not have seen, the tall figure detach itself from the shadowed doorway across the street and begin, unhurried, to cross.
