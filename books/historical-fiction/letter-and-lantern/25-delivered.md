## 25. Delivered

The rain found us on Deansgate and did not stop.

I had imagined this moment a hundred times between Euston and here — always in fragments, because my mind refused to hold the whole of it at once. The bookshop with its green blinds. The man who would open the door. The packet changing hands. In every version I was calm. In every version my hands did not shake. In none of them was there a child asleep against my shoulder with a doll sewn shut around three pages of a dead courier's ledger.

"Nearly there," I said, though Nell could not have heard me over the rain and her own breathing. Her cheek was hot against my collarbone. She had not let go of my hand since the checkpoint; when Crake's men waved us through — when Crake himself stepped aside, his eyes going from my face to hers and finding exactly what he expected to find, a driver and a tired child, nothing worth a warrant — she had gripped me so hard her fingers left marks that would still be there tomorrow, small crescents like the moon's own signature pressed into my skin.

The bookshop was where Edith said it would be: Hollis & Vane, secondhand books, the sort of establishment that existed on paper as a business and in fact as a listening post. The green blinds were half drawn. A light burned behind them, the color of weak tea.

I stood under the awning for one breath, two, letting the rain run off my hat brim, making myself look like what I appeared to be — a woman sheltering, waiting out the weather, no one at all. Then I climbed the four steps and knocked, twice, then once more after a pause, the way the note in Edith's hand had instructed. *Two and one. If anyone answers who is not Mr. Fenn, walk away and do not look back.*

Bolts turned. The door opened six inches on a chain, and an eye looked out at me — grey, bloodshot, unimpressed by the weather or anything else in the world.

"I'm looking for a first edition," I said. "*The Rights of Woman.*"

"Wollstonecraft." The eye narrowed. "Everyone asks for Wollstonecraft."

"The note said you'd know it wasn't about the book."

The chain rattled off. The door opened onto a narrow passage smelling of pipe smoke and old glue, and a small man in shirtsleeves stood back to admit us. He had the kind of face that newspapers never print — round, ordinary, forgettable — which I supposed was precisely why he was alive and useful. His eyes went past me to Nell, and something moved in them, quick and human, before the professional blankness came back down like a shop shutter.

"You're the Marlowe woman's driver," he said. It was not a question.

"Bram Vane."

"And this?"

"Nell." I heard my own voice change on the name, go soft in a way I hadn't permitted it to be in days. "She stays where I can see her."

Fenn considered this. Whatever he saw in my face decided him. "There's a chair by the stove in back. Missus Pym keeps the place — she'll bring tea, and she won't ask questions, because she can't hear them anyway." He raised his voice slightly and gestured, and from somewhere deeper in the house a stout woman appeared, took Nell's hand with the unhurried competence of someone who has raised children and buried some of them, and led her away without a word passing between them that needed hearing.

Nell looked back at me once. I made myself smile. I made myself nod: *go on*. She went.

Only then did I open my coat.

* * *

The lining had been Edith's idea, and Edith's needlework. Three nights ago in the house on Holloway Road, with the gas turned low and the curtains drawn, she had sat cross-legged on the floor of her study with the reassembled ledger pages spread around her like a card game gone wrong, sewing them into the false seam she'd cut along the coat's inner edge while I held the lamp.

"They'll search your luggage," she'd said. "They'll pat your coat pockets. They will not take apart a woman's coat seam unless they already know exactly what they're looking for, and if they already know that, we've lost anyway." She'd bitten the thread and looked up at me, and for a moment the armor slipped and I saw how tired she was, how afraid, though she would have denied both with her last breath. "You understand that if they do find it, it won't be a search anymore. It will be the end of everything we're trying to prevent. They'll bury it, and they'll bury whoever carried it, and they'll print the bomb story by Saturday."

"I understand."

"And Nell—"

"Nell goes separately. As you said."

"As I said," Edith repeated, and there was something in the way she said it — a question she was asking herself, not me. But she'd bent back over the coat and sewed until two in the morning, and I had sat with the lamp and watched her hands work, steady as machinery, and thought: *this is what conviction looks like close up. It looks like a woman ruining her eyes over a needle at 2 a.m.*

Now I peeled the seam open in Fenn's back room, over a table cleared of everything but a green baize cloth, and laid the pages out one by one. Photographs. The ledger itself, in the courier's cramped hand — dates, payments, initials that matched a private secretary's initials, and beside them, in another hand, the word *arranged* written again and again like a refrain. And the last page, the one that had made even Edith go quiet: a memorandum in Undersecretary Halloran's own handwriting, initialled, dated nine days before the bombing, discussing the *desirability of an incident attributable to the militant faction*.

Fenn read standing, moving down the table like a man walking a fence line. He did not touch the pages. He read each one twice.

When he reached the memorandum he stopped, and for a long moment the only sound in the room was the rain on the window above the baize cloth and the tick of a mantel clock that had been wrong since some decade neither of us had seen.

"Where was this photographed?" he asked finally.

"It hasn't been. Not properly."

"It has now." He was already pulling the cloth back, clearing space beneath the one window with good northern light. "Get the last page flat. No — flat *flat*, the corner's curling. There." He produced a camera from a cabinet as casually as another man might produce a teapot, an older plate camera, brass fittings worn bright. "We'll shoot every page twice, negatives developed within the hour, prints pulled tonight. Then the wire."

"The wire," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, thin, far away. "To whom?"

"Manchester Guardian gets the story. That's the point of Manchester — Halloran owns too much of the London press, but he doesn't own C.P. Scott, and everyone knows it. But the physical evidence—" He tapped the memorandum. "The physical evidence travels south. There's an editor in London who owes me a favor and hates Halloran more than he owes me the favor. If the Guardian publishes Thursday morning and the London proofs are already sitting in a safe by Wednesday night, there's nothing Halloran can do about either. He can't suppress what's already been seen."

He worked as he talked, fast and precise, sliding each page under the camera's glass. I watched the ledger become light, become chemistry, become something that could no longer be burned or buried or lost in a hollowed book at Crewe. Every click of the shutter felt like a nail driven into a coffin — I couldn't decide whose.

"Sit down," Fenn said, without looking up. "You look about to fall over."

"I'm fine."

"You've been awake—" He glanced at the clock, did the arithmetic, winced. "Thirty hours? More? Sit down, Miss Vane, or Mrs. Pym will have words with me, and she's the only person in Manchester I'm frightened of."

I sat. It was a mistake. The chair received me and all the tension I had been living on for three days drained out through the floorboards, and I discovered that my hands were trembling — not the fine shake of nerves but the deep, ugly tremor of a body presenting its bill. I put them in my lap and watched Fenn work and tried to think of nothing at all.

It didn't take. What came instead, unbidden, was Crewe: the lavatory's iron smell, the three pages curling in the basin, the match flaring, the inspector's footsteps in the corridor outside coming closer, closer — and my own face in the little mirror, saying *burn faster*, saying *you are allowed to lose three pages, you are not allowed to lose yourself*. I had watched forty years of other people's history burn in those letters I steamed open, and I had told myself it was reading. It had taken a fire of my own making to teach me the difference.

"Miss Vane." Fenn's voice, closer. I looked up. He was holding out a cup of tea, strong enough to stand a spoon in, and watching me with those grey eyes that missed nothing. "You got it here."

"I lost three pages getting it here."

"You got it *here*," he said again. "Do you know how many couriers I've dealt with in fifteen years? Most of them are brave and half of them are careful and nearly all of them fail at some point, because the people hunting them only have to be lucky once. You were lucky and careful and you failed *small*. Three pages out of thirty-seven." He set the cup in my hands. "When this is printed, those three pages will be the least of Halloran's problems, and he will spend the rest of his career wishing they were the most. Drink your tea."

I drank my tea. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the cobbles, and somewhere across the city Nell was drinking milk by a stove under the care of a deaf woman who asked no questions, and somewhere south of us Edith Marlowe was doing — what? I had left her in London with a plan I hadn't wanted to hear and a sentence I couldn't stop hearing anyway. *If anyone must be caught, Bram, it must be me. A famous name makes better headlines and worse consequences. That's not martyrdom. That's arithmetic.*

Arithmetic. As if a person were a sum.

"Negatives are drying," Fenn announced, emerging from his darkroom smelling of chemicals, wiping his hands. "Prints by ten. Wire goes now." He crossed to the telegraph form pad on his desk and began writing in a clerk's neat block capitals, reading half aloud as he went — code phrases, addresses, instructions wrapped in innocuous talk of shipping invoices and fabric samples. Watching him, I understood suddenly how large the machine was that we had fed: not one newspaper but a nervous system, wires strung across the country, editors and typesetters and linotype operators, hundreds of people who had never met Edith Marlowe and would nonetheless spend tomorrow setting her vindication in metal.

"How long?" I asked.

"Wire's instant. Confirmation from London—" He checked his watch. "If our friend's at his desk, an hour. If not, first thing. Prints go on the midnight train south in a sealed envelope addressed to his club, hand-delivered from the platform by a lad who doesn't know what he's carrying. By breakfast tomorrow an editor in London holds photographic proof that the Home Office manufactured a bombing and murdered a man to cover it." He said it flatly, the way one reads shipping news. Only his hands, smoothing the telegram form on the desk, betrayed that he knew what he had just said.

"And then?"

"And then," Fenn said, "it stops being our problem and becomes the country's. Which is rather the point." He looked at me then, really looked, the way doctors look. "Go and sleep, Miss Vane. There's a room upstairs. You've done your part."

"My part," I said. The tea had brought feeling back into my hands and with the feeling came everything else — Crake's face at the checkpoint, patient as a spider; the sergeant's clipboard with its photograph; the way Nell had cried without sound, tears simply arriving on her cheeks like weather, because she had learned already that sound was dangerous. "Mr. Fenn, do you know what happened to the man who carried this before me?"

He was quiet a moment. "I read the coroner's report. Fell under a train at Willesden. Very sad. Very convenient."

"He had a daughter."

"I know," Fenn said softly. "She's upstairs drinking milk."

"Yes," I said. "She is."

* * *

They woke me at half past five.

Or rather — Mrs. Pym woke Nell, and Nell woke me, appearing at the edge of my borrowed bed with her doll clutched to her chest and her hair sleep-flattened on one side, whispering, "The lady says there's toast, and the man says come quick, the talking box has said something back."

The talking box. The telegraph. I was downstairs with my boots half-laced before I remembered to be exhausted.

Fenn stood at his desk with a flimsy in his hand. He had the expression of a man trying very hard to look unsurprised and not quite managing it. Behind him the window showed Manchester turning grey-gold, gulls wheeling over the roofs, the city waking into a day that did not yet know what it was going to be told.

"Well?" My voice cracked on the single syllable.

He handed me the flimsy. I read it twice. The cipher resolved slowly under my eyes, my brain working at the speed of cold treacle, and then all at once it was plain:

**PROOFS RECEIVED AND VERIFIED STOP EDITOR HAS SEEN PHOTOGRAPHS AND MEMORANDUM STOP HOLDS THEM IN SAFE AND SAYS TELL YOUR PEOPLE LONDON WILL NOT BE QUIET STOP FIRST EDITIONS SET FOR THURSDAY MORNING ALL EDITIONS STOP GOD HELP US ALL AND HALLORAN MORESO STOP**

"Thursday morning," Fenn said. "Every edition. And that last bit isn't cipher flourish — that's the editor himself. He doesn't waste words. If he says London will not be quiet, then by God it won't be quiet." He took the flimsy back and looked at it almost fondly. "Miss Vane, do you know what happens tomorrow morning? Half-past six, the machines start. Seven, the vans roll. By eight o'clock there will be a hundred thousand copies of tomorrow's Guardian on trains to every town in England, and on the front page of every one of them will be Halloran's own initialled memorandum, reproduced full size, with the story underneath. There is no fixing that. There is no buying that back. By Thursday afternoon it will be read aloud in public meetings from here to Bristol."

By Thursday afternoon it will be everywhere, he meant. By Thursday afternoon it cannot be unheard.

Triumph should feel like something. I waited for it — the swelling, the relief, the great warm wave of *it is finished* — and what arrived instead was a weariness so complete it felt almost like grief, and underneath it, faint but persistent, the way you feel a tooth before it hurts: the knowledge of what tomorrow's noise would cost, and who had arranged herself to pay it.

Because Edith had done the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said a famous name draws the constables first. Somewhere in London tonight, in a house with its curtains drawn, she would be writing out her statement in that upright, unyielding hand, preparing to stand in front of whatever crowd assembled, preparing to be *visible* — visible as bait, visible as target — so that when the storm broke, it broke over her and not over a driver no one had ever photographed, not over a child no one was supposed to remember.

"We should wire her," I said. "She'll want to know it's confirmed. She'll be—"

"Waiting," Fenn said. "Yes. Wire her. Use the address in the note. Keep it short — confirmation only, nothing that names the contents." He was already writing the form. "And then, Miss Vane, you and the girl get on the afternoon train home, because if my experience of these affairs is worth anything, the next seventy-two hours are going to be the loudest this country has heard in years, and you'll want to be standing next to your people when it breaks. Not here. Not alone."

Home. The word landed strangely. Holloway Road was not home; it had been a post I occupied, a windscreen I sat behind, letters I read that were never addressed to me. And yet I could not think where else to go, and I suspected that was the whole of the truth about homes.

I sent the wire myself. Fenn dictated; my fingers were steadier than I expected. **CONFIRMED STOP PROOFS IN HANDS STOP THURSDAY ALL EDITIONS STOP COME WHAT COMES WE DID IT STOP** — and then, before I could stop myself, before the operator could take the slip: **B.**

Three letters. She would know what they meant, or she wouldn't, and either way they were true: it was me, I had carried it, I was the one who had read every letter and finally, at terrible cost, delivered one.

* * *

The afternoon train pulled out of Manchester London Road at twenty past four, and I had a corner seat with Nell asleep against my side before the platform lamps were lit. She had the doll. In the doll's cotton belly, folded small, lay the memorandum in Halloran's own hand — Fenn's insistence, over my objection. *Photographs can be called forgeries,* he'd said. *An original cannot. Split the risk. If the prints are stopped, the original survives, and vice versa.* So the child who had been told to forget a face now carried the proof of it across England in her arms, and slept, because children can sleep anywhere, having no idea what they carry.

I watched Lancashire slide past the window — mills, canals, the long red dusk of a working day ending — and I let myself count what we had won. Proof photographed. Proofs wired. An editor holding the originals in a safe that Halloran's money could not open. By breakfast, the frame-up dead in the water; by Friday, perhaps, Halloran's resignation demanded in the House; by spring, maybe, something worse than resignation.

And Edith. Edith standing on a platform tomorrow with her chin up, placing herself precisely where the falling building would land, because she had calculated that the movement needed its martyr more than its strategist, and had not asked anyone's permission, and would not.

The train rocked. Nell murmured in her sleep and turned her face into my sleeve, and I put my arm around her and looked out at the dark coming down over the fields, and I understood that triumph and dread were not two feelings at all but one feeling wearing two faces, and that from here to Thursday morning every hour was a coin spinning in the air, and no one — not Fenn, not the editor, not I — could say which side it would land on.

At Crewe the train stopped, and stopped again, longer than it should. A porter ran along the platform. Somewhere ahead, whistles — not the engine's kind.

I gathered Nell up and moved to the corridor, heart beginning its old hard work, and saw the stationmaster hurrying toward our carriage with an envelope in his hand and a strange look on his face, calling out as he came:

"Message for the lady travelling with the child — urgent — marked immediate—"
