## 27. The Trial Begins

They had built a wooden wall across the front of the Old Bailey to hold back the crowds, and the wall was already losing.

I saw it from the cab at half past seven in the morning, a week after the ledger went to print. People stood three deep along the pavement opposite, and when the doors opened for the lawyers' clerks they surged forward like water finding a breach. A woman with a banner — VOTES FOR WOMEN stencilled over the photograph of a courier's face — had to be pulled back by two constables who did not seem entirely sorry to be rough with her. Somebody had chalked HALLORAN LIED on the stone balustrade of the court itself, high up, where it would take a man on a ladder most of the morning to scrub away.

Edith Marlowe's trial was not a trial any longer. It was a weather system. It had its own pressure, its own fronts, its own capacity to flatten whatever stood beneath it, and I drove into it the way you drive into rain: headlights on, shoulders hunched, telling yourself you would come out the other side.

Nell sat beside me on the bench seat, in her good coat, her hands folded in her lap because I had asked her twice and she remembered. She was watching the crowd with the particular stillness she had learned in the railway yards — the stillness of a child who knows that being seen can be the most dangerous thing in the world.

"They've come for her," she said.

"Yes."

"Will they let us in?"

"Some of us." I had two press passes in my coat pocket, obtained through Mr. Ashworth's office in Manchester by means I had decided not to ask about. "You'll sit where I tell you and you won't move until I come back. Do you understand?"

She nodded. Then, because she was Nell: "Is Papa's book going to be there?"

The ledger. The photographs. Forty-one pages sewn into a doll's body and then photographed page by page on Ashworth's desk while the ink was still smudging from my sweating hands, and now reproduced in six newspapers across the country under headlines that varied only in their degree of fury. THE MINISTER'S LEDGER. MURDER AT THE MINISTRY'S DOOR. WHAT WAS THE COURIER CARRYING?

"It already is," I said. "That's why they're all here."

* * *

Inside, the courtroom had been rearranged for war.

Benches normally reserved for bored solicitors' clerks were packed with journalists — I counted shorthand writers from every London paper, two men from American agencies, a woman from a French wire service whose presence alone told me how far this had travelled. Sketch artists sat in the front row because cameras were barred, and one of them was already drawing the dock before anyone occupied it, getting the proportions of the oak panelling right so he could drop Edith into it later like a coin into a slot.

The gallery above held the public, admitted by ticket, and the tickets had become currency. I saw suffragists in their purple sashes sitting shoulder to shoulder with men in collar studs who had probably never shared air with such women before. Somewhere near the back, unmistakable even from below, sat a cluster of Quakers — plain hats, still faces — and I knew without looking that among them were the people who had sheltered Nell, and that they had come to watch what their small disobedience had helped set loose.

Mr. Ashworth found me in the aisle, pressed and rumpled and running on something stronger than tea.

"You look terrible," he said.

"You should see the other side of the story."

"I have. It looks worse." He steered me toward the press benches with a hand at my elbow, proprietary as a sheepdog. "Halloran's men have been working all night. You know that?"

"I assumed."

"Assumed nothing. They bought space in two evening papers — paid notices, dressed up as news — saying the ledger is a forgery manufactured by militant elements to obstruct justice. They've got a retired inspector of documents prepared to swear the paper stock is wrong for nineteen twelve." His mouth did something bitter. "It isn't wrong. I checked the supplier myself. But it doesn't matter today whether it's true. Today matters whether it *plays*."

"And Edith?"

"Marlowe," he said, correcting me out of habit even though we both knew I had earned the first name years ago and spent none of that credit, "arrived at Holloway at five this morning by police van. She requested no counsel of her own choosing until she'd read the brief twice. She's representing herself on the obstruction charge with a barrister advising from the bench — her choice, against everyone's advice." He paused. "She said — and I'm quoting her letter, which I am apparently allowed to show you since half of Fleet Street has seen it — she said: 'A famous prisoner is worth ten free ones. Let them put me on display.'"

The words landed in me the way they always did, somewhere below the ribs, where I kept everything I couldn't afford to carry in my hands.

"She's making herself the story," I said.

"She's making herself the *evidence*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference between her and every man in that building." Ashworth glanced at the door. "Here they come."

* * *

I have seen processions. I have seen marches broken by mounted police, seen ambulances carried shoulder-high through hostile streets, seen Edith Marlowe step onto a platform in Peckham while eggs burst against the woodwork behind her. None of it prepared me for the sound a courtroom makes when she enters it in custody.

It was not cheering. Cheering would have been simple. It was a low, involuntary noise, hundreds of people drawing breath at once, and then the silence that follows when everyone understands at the same moment that this is history and they are inside it rather than reading about it afterward.

Edith walked between two wardresses with her hands folded in front of her. They had not managed to make her look diminished. Her hair was pinned severely — prison regulation, I learned later; they had cut it shorter than she wore it and she had repinned what remained with a defiance visible from the gallery. She wore her own clothes, dark grey, plain, and she climbed the steps to the dock the way she climbed podiums: measuring each one, taking possession of it.

Her eyes crossed the room once, quickly, the way a lighthouse crosses water. I felt the beam pass over me and kept my face still. Nell was not in the room. I had left her with Ashworth's sister three streets away, and I would collect her at noon, and between those hours I would sit very still and do the only thing left to me, which was witness.

The charge was obstruction and conspiracy arising from the events of the spring — the old charges, dusted off, the ones Crake had assembled before the ledger made them radioactive. Everyone in the room understood the arithmetic. Halloran could not survive the courier's murder being laid at his door, so the machinery of prosecution would grind instead against the woman who had carried the proof, on charges that predated the proof, hoping the public's appetite for scandal would be fed either way.

The Crown's counsel rose. He was a heavy, deliberate man with a voice like a filing cabinet closing, and he began — I give him credit — exactly where Halloran's people wanted him to begin.

"My lord, before this court proceeds to evidence, it must confront the question of certain documents recently circulated in the press..."

He spoke for forty minutes. He called the ledger "a compilation of uncertain provenance, produced by interested parties, timed to coincide with these proceedings." He said the word *forgery* four times. He described the suffrage movement as an organization that had "demonstrated, repeatedly and publicly, its willingness to employ destruction and intimidation," and suggested that a movement capable of window-breaking was capable of manufacturing papers.

And through all of it, Edith sat in the dock with her chin lifted and her hands quiet, and occasionally she smiled — a small, private smile, the kind you might give a child reciting lines badly — and the sketch artists worked furiously, because that smile was worth more than anything the Crown said.

Then it was her turn, or nearly. The barrister advising her rose to object to the framing, was heard briefly, sat down. And then the judge — dry, precise, a man who clearly wished the newspapers were anywhere else — said, "Miss Marlowe, do you wish to address the question of these documents?"

She stood. The wardress beside her shifted; Edith didn't glance at her.

"My lord, I do."

Her voice filled the room without effort. I have watched her speak to ten thousand people in Hyde Park and never once strain; the courtroom was smaller work, but she treated it with the same respect, as though the truth required the same volume regardless of audience.

"The documents are genuine. I will say so here, on the record, under oath if the court requires it, and I will say it again wherever and however this court permits me to say it. The ledger records payments made by agents of Undersecretary Halloran to men who stopped a courier on the North Road in March of last year and killed him for what he carried." She paused, and the pause was a weapon; the whole room leaned into it. "I did not write that ledger. A dead man's widow gave it into my keeping, at risk to herself, because the law offered her no other protection. I carried it to Manchester because no newspaper would print what no minister would confirm. And I am standing in this dock today because the gentleman behind me" — she did not turn, she did not need to; every head turned for her — "cannot try the dead man's killers, so he has decided to try the woman who buried him."

Counsel for the Crown was on his feet. Objection. Characterization. The judge rapped his gavel, and the gavel sounded very small.

But the room had heard it. The shorthand writers had taken it down. By evening it would be in every paper in the country, and there was nothing Halloran's money could buy back, because you cannot purchase the past tense.

* * *

The morning broke at noon, as mornings in court do, and I went out into the corridor to breathe and found the world had changed shape while I sat inside it.

Newsboys were already on the steps — special editions, the machines barely cooled — and the headline on the one I bought read MARLOWE NAMES COURIER'S KILLERS FROM THE DOCK. Around the court, the crowd had doubled. Someone was selling buttons with the courier's face on them, poor man, his grave eyes reproduced a thousandfold for a penny, and I stood looking at that button for longer than I meant to, thinking: he was somebody's son, he was a husband, he carried a satchel, and now he is a cause. History does not ask your permission. It takes your face and puts it on a badge and marches on.

Ashworth came out wiping his glasses. "Halloran issued a statement at eleven. 'I welcome any proper inquiry.' Proper inquiry — meaning one he controls." He crumpled into a laugh with no humor in it. "Two backbenchers have already demanded questions in the House. One of them used the word *resignation*. You know what that means? That means someone counted votes last night and told him the numbers."

"And Crake?"

The name changed Ashworth's face. "Careful," he said quietly. "There's a man by the pillar who's been there since eight. Not press. Not police — not uniform, anyway." He didn't point. He was too good for pointing. "Crake's finished, everyone says so, everyone's writing his obituary. Men like that don't die quietly, Miss Vane. Cornered animals and cornered fixers, same rules."

"He's not the animal I'm worried about," I said. "He's the trap."

I went back in for the afternoon session, and the afternoon belonged to the Crown's witnesses, and it went worse for them than the morning had.

They produced a handwriting expert who examined the ledger's figures and conceded, under cross-examination that Edith conducted herself — rising in the dock, precise as a surgeon, the advising barrister leaning over to whisper and being waved gently away — that the hand was consistent with the courier's known correspondence, samples of which the Crown had obligingly provided themselves. They produced a witness who placed Edith in Manchester on the relevant days and could not place her anywhere near the North Road in March. Each witness came down from the stand slightly lighter than he had gone up, some fact left behind on the floor of the court, and the journalists' pencils moved like insects feeding.

At half past three, a commotion in the gallery. A woman stood up — middle-aged, plain-dressed, face I recognized from a photograph I had studied for an entire night in a house that smelled of lamp oil and fear — and her voice cracked across the courtroom before the ushers could reach her.

"That ledger is my husband's! I gave it freely! Ask me — why won't any of you ask me!"

They removed her. Gently, more or less. The judge threatened the gallery with clearing. And Edith stood in the dock and said, clear enough to reach the corridor beyond, "My lord, that woman is the widow of the murdered man, and this court has declined to hear her, and I would remind the court whose convenience that serves."

The gavel again. The pencils again. The headline wrote itself while I watched.

* * *

I collected Nell at four. She was sitting on Ashworth's sister's step with a piece of bread and butter she wasn't eating, and when she saw me she ran the length of the pavement and hit me at the waist hard enough to stagger.

"Well?" she demanded, into my coat. "Did they listen?"

"To every word."

"Did they believe her?"

I crouched down to her level. This was the question, wasn't it — the whole question, in a child's grammar. Whether the listening would turn into believing, whether believing would turn into justice, whether anything anyone did with paper and voice could outweigh what men with money and ministers' confidence could do with silence.

"Not yet," I said. "But they're writing it all down. And written things last."

She considered this with the gravity she brought to everything now, this child who had aged a decade in a season. "Papa said the pen beats the sword. He said it jokingly." Her chin trembled, just once, and firmed. "It isn't a joke, is it."

"No," I said. "It never was. The joke is that men keep bringing swords to a fight that pens win."

* * *

That evening I drove back through a city that felt different under its lamps.

Outside a newsagent in Camden, a knot of people stood reading the evening edition aloud to those who couldn't get close, passing the paper hand to hand like communion. An old man read Edith's words from the dock — *the woman who buried him* — in a quavering voice, and nobody laughed, and when he finished, a young woman in the crowd said, "So when does Halloran go?" and nobody answered, and everybody understood the answer: soon, maybe, if the pressure held, if the backbenchers held, if the inquiry wasn't strangled in committee, if the courts didn't convict her anyway on charges that had nothing to do with any of it.

Because that was the price tag, and I could see it hanging there as plainly as the headline. Hope had come to market and it had named its figure. For the ledger to save the movement, Edith had to stay in the dock. For the truth to be undeniable, its carrier had to be punishable. Every editorial demanding Halloran's resignation also, in the same columns, in the same measured sentences, deplored Miss Marlowe's methods and looked forward to due process taking its course. They wanted the fire and they wanted her burned as the fuel, and both at once, and they saw no contradiction.

I sat outside the house with the engine ticking and thought about the first time I steamed open one of her letters. I had told myself I was a spectator — a woman at the edge of the frame, holding a wheel, delivering other people's history to other people's destinations. Now the whole country was reading letters she never sent them, printed in a million copies, and she was in a cell for it, and I was the one still driving free.

History was not something that happened to other people. It had never been. It happened to whoever held the paper.

There was a light burning in the front parlour — Mrs. Quill, our neighbor, keeping the house as agreed, feeding the cat, airing the rooms against the day Edith came home to them. I went in and ate standing up and slept badly, and woke before dawn to the sound of the morning papers hitting doorsteps all down the street, soft as snowfall.

I fetched ours in barefoot. Page one. And page two. And then I stood in the cold hallway reading page three twice, because the story there had nothing to do with the trial at all.

*DISGRACED INSPECTOR MISSING*, said the sub-head. *Former Yard Officer Connected to Ministry Inquiry Fails to Appear; Rooms Searched; Papers Burned.*

Crake. Gone. His fires banked, his trail cold, and a man like that — a man who knew where every body of this business was buried, including perhaps one or two nobody else suspected — loose somewhere in England with nothing left to sell but the truth, and nothing left to lose.

I put down the paper and went upstairs to wake Nell, because whatever Crake did next, it would happen fast, and I had a sudden, cold certainty that it would come to our door.
