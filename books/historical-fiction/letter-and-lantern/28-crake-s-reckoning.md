## 28. Crake's Reckoning

The morning papers arrived at Scotland Yard before Inspector Crake did, and by the time he climbed the stone steps at half past eight they had already done their work. He saw it in the faces of the constables in the corridor — that particular quality of stillness men affect when a colleague has stepped into open water and the ice is singing. Nobody met his eye. A young constable named Pell, who only three weeks ago had asked Crake to sign his recommendation for a course of study in forensic photography, found something fascinating to examine on the floor tiles.

Crake walked past them all without breaking stride. He had learned long ago that a man's legs could carry him through anything if he gave them nothing else to do.

The newspaper lay on his own desk when he reached his office, folded to the relevant page as though some considerate hand had prepared it for him. Someone had. That was its own message.

*MINISTER'S MEN IMPLICATED IN COURIER DEATH,* ran the headline. Beneath it, reproduced in smudged halftone: a page of a ledger, columns of payments, initials, dates. And beneath that, in smaller type, the sentence that would end him:

*Documents delivered to this office yesterday by courier, having passed through three police checkpoints unexamined.*

Three checkpoints. His checkpoints. His men, his instructions, his wire sent to every station from Euston to Manchester demanding vigilance against a woman and child carrying seditious material — and the documents had sailed through all of it, photographed, printed, and set in type while he slept in his lodgings in Camden with the window open because the night had been warm.

He sat down. The chair received him with a creak that sounded, in the silence, almost like laughter.

* * *

He read the article twice. The first time his eyes slid over the words without gripping them, the way a man's hand slides off a wet rail; the second time he made himself go slowly, itemizing the damage as he would itemize evidence in someone else's case.

The ledger was real. Not a copy — or if a copy, an exact one, because the figures matched what Halloran had shown him in the minister's own study eleven months ago, the night Crake had understood, with a sensation like a trapdoor opening, exactly what sort of man he was now employed to be. The payments were there. The initials — *H.* for Halloran, *C.* for himself, appearing in the disbursement column with a modesty that was somehow worse than full disclosure, as though his corruption were so routine it required abbreviation. The date of the courier's death at Wilmslow. The sum paid to "the railwayman" who had been standing watch. Everything.

And there was more. A second piece, shorter, sharper: an editorial noting that Undersecretary Halloran had spent the past fortnight assuring the press that militant suffragists were preparing a campaign of bombing, that the police possessed intelligence confirming it, and asking — in the mild, surgical tone editorials reserve for killing — precisely where that intelligence had come from, and whether it might be located in the same ledger that recorded payments for a dead man's silence.

Crake set the paper down. His hands were steady. He noted this with detached professional interest, the way he might note the pulse of a suspect who was managing his fear better than expected. Then he poured water from the jug on the washstand and drank it, and discovered his throat worked only with difficulty.

Eleven months. It had taken eleven months to climb from inspector to the right hand of an undersecretary of state, and it had taken one morning to fall the whole distance and land somewhere below where he'd started, because a man who has served corruption is not merely dismissed — he is *used*, and then he is buried.

There came a knock. Pell again, with the day's correspondence, and Crake took the tray and said thank you — actually said *thank you*, heard his own voice emerge level and courteous — and watched the boy leave with relief naked on his face.

Among the letters, two mattered. The first was from the Home Office, requesting his attendance at eleven o'clock. No explanation. The second was a note in a hand he did not know, unsigned, containing a single line: *Burn whatever you have. They are already talking.*

They were already talking. Of course they were. Men like Halloran's circle survived by being the last rat aboard, and the first rats had certainly begun to swim by now. Somewhere in Westminster this morning, a secretary was emptying a safe. Somewhere a private secretary was drafting a statement in which his minister's conduct would be described as *unauthorized actions by overzealous subordinates*. Crake knew the grammar of these things intimately; he had helped construct enough of them for other people's downfalls.

He looked at his filing cabinet.

It stood in the corner, four drawers of steel, and behind its locked doors lay the true history of the past year: the memoranda in Halloran's hand, the telegrams, the list of witnesses interviewed and what each had cost, the photograph of a girl called Nell that had circulated through every checkpoint in the north, the transcript of his own interview with the landlady in Camden Town who remembered a woman driver with steady hands and a child's coat on her hook. Every document a prosecution would want. Every document that, in the right hands, proved not merely that Crake had known but that he had *built* the machinery — that the frame-up of the suffrage movement had not happened to him but had been, in the plainest sense, his work.

A man could destroy those papers and claim he had never seen them. Or a man could preserve them, and trade them.

Crake sat with the thought for a long time. Below the window, Whitehall went about its morning: omnibuses, errand boys, a detachment of guards marching toward the Horse Guards parade with that indifference to ordinary weather and ordinary morality that uniformed men acquire. Somewhere out there Edith Marlowe was sitting in a cell — arrested on old charges within hours of the story breaking, because Halloran's people had needed her silenced before she could stand in front of microphones and cameras and connect herself publicly to the ledger. Crake had signed no order for that arrest, but he knew whose instinct had shaped it. It was his own instinct, reported upward, acted upon downward. His fingerprints were on everything now. That was what ambition did, he thought. It didn't lift you out of the mud. It taught you to press your hands into it, deeper and deeper, until your prints were everywhere and the mud was you.

At ten minutes to eleven he opened the cabinet, took out the thinnest folder — the one containing his personal copies, the insurance he had assembled in the early confident weeks when he'd imagined blackmail rather than exposure — and carried it to the fireplace in the room next door, which was empty at that hour.

He burned it. He fed the pages in one at a time, watching each take flame at the corners and curl, and he told himself he was being prudent, that a dead man's insurance is worthless, that the ledger published in the newspapers already contained everything that mattered. All true. None of it honest. He burned the papers because destroying evidence was the reflex of guilty men and he had become, entirely, a guilty man, and the burning felt less like prudence than like amputation — like severing a limb he had grown so gradually he had never noticed it was not part of his body.

The photograph of the child he held longest over the flames. She stared up at him from the glossy stock, solemn, perhaps seven years old, the face of a witness he had hunted across half of England for the crime of having seen her father murdered. In the firelight her eyes seemed to follow the paper down.

He let it burn.

Then he put on his hat, checked his reflection in the darkened window — a lean, correct figure, grey at the temples now, respectable to the last millimetre — and went to keep his appointment with the Home Office.

* * *

The interview lasted nineteen minutes and consisted almost entirely of silences.

The Permanent Secretary did not accuse him of anything. Accusation required belief in one's own safety, and the man across the desk was plainly calculating, even as he spoke, how much of the coming wreckage he could position himself clear of. He asked questions instead. Whether Crake had received instructions from the Undersecretary's office regarding surveillance of suffragist organizers. Whether such instructions had been given in writing. Whether Crake had formed any view as to the provenance of certain financial records mentioned in this morning's press. Whether, in his professional judgement, the police had been *instrumentalized*.

Instrumentalized. Crake turned the word over like a coin he was being invited to bite.

"I followed instructions," he said. "From the proper channels."

"Did you ever question them?"

"No, sir." And then, because something in him — some last stubborn clerk of his conscience, the same clerk who had once joined the force believing the law was a lantern held up against the dark — added: "I should have."

The Permanent Secretary looked at him for a moment with something that was almost pity and almost nausea. "You will remain at liberty pending further inquiries," he said. "You are suspended from duty effective immediately. You will surrender your warrant card. Do not leave London. Do not communicate with any person connected to these matters." A pause. "I would advise you to retain counsel. Good counsel."

The warrant card went into a tray on the corner of the desk, among others, a small drift of cardboard identities. Crake looked at his own lying there — *Inspector T. Crake, C.I.D.* — laminated authority reduced to litter, and understood that he had just been weighed by the state and found to be disposable weight.

Walking out through the corridors, past the noticeboards and the smell of boot polish and ink, he felt eyes on his back the whole length of the building. By evening everyone would know. By tomorrow the newspapers would know his name; the Manchester journalist's next piece would see to that, because a story needs a face below the minister's, and Crake's face was the one in the files.

Outside, the light was hard and white, an autumn noon with winter waiting behind it. He stood on the steps and did not know which direction to walk, and the not-knowing frightened him more than anything the Permanent Secretary had said.

* * *

He went home. He packed.

He told himself he was not fleeing; he was *repositioning*. There was money — not a fortune, but eleven months of Halloran's gratitude banked in three accounts under names that would hold for a week, maybe two. There was a brother in Toronto who owed him nothing but might, for the family's sake, ask no questions. There was a boat train. There was always a boat train; the empire was built on them precisely so that men like Crake could vanish along its arteries.

He laid the things out on the bed with a housekeeper's tidiness: two suits, shirts, his father's gold watch, the passport in the name of Thomas Crane that he had procured eighteen months ago for reasons that had seemed, at the time, like prudence rather than prophecy. He looked at the case and felt no lighter for the packing. Flight, he understood suddenly, standing there with the strap in his hands, was simply guilt wearing luggage. Wherever he landed — Toronto, Buenos Aires, some whitewashed room above a harbour — he would carry the same cargo: the child's photograph curling in a grate, the widow in Wilmslow he had visited under false pretences, the sound of Edith Marlowe saying at her rally, in that clear carrying voice he had heard from the back of crowds a dozen times, *We do not ask to be believed. We ask to be heard, and we will make the hearing impossible to refuse.*

She was in Holloway now because of him. Not because of Halloran — Halloran had only wanted the story killed; it was Crake who had designed the machine that killed stories, Crake who had mapped the checkpoints and wired the descriptions and understood, better than anyone, that the way to bury truth was not to destroy it but to make its carriers criminals. And the machine had failed, and the truth had run through it like water through rotten boards, and the machine's builder was left holding the wreckage while the minister's people scattered like startled pigeons.

His telephone rang. He stood looking at it through two rings before lifting the receiver.

"Crake." Halloran's voice, low and rapid, stripped of its dinner-party warmth. "Have you seen the papers."

"I've seen them."

"Then you'll understand that the situation requires discretion from all parties. Complete discretion. I'm sure I don't need to spell out—"

"Your secretary has already spelled it out," Crake said. "*Unauthorized overzealous subordinates.* I read it between the lines of the Home Office's silence. Very tidy."

A pause on the line, in which Crake heard something new in the minister's voice, and recognized it with cold clarity because he had spent his career listening for it in other men: fear. Halloran was afraid of him now. Fear was a currency, and Crake was briefly rich.

"Inspector—" Halloran began, softening, reaching for the old intimacy.

"I'm not an inspector. I surrendered my card at noon." Crake kept his voice level, conversational, and found that he was smiling at the wall, a small tight smile with no pleasure anywhere in it. "You'll want to know what I intend to do. So will your friends. So will the journalists. Everyone wants to know what the instrument does when it's thrown away."

"Thomas." The use of his Christian name, the wheedling drop into familiarity. "Whatever you imagine you have—"

"I have nothing," Crake said. "That's the joke of it. I burned my copies this morning like a good servant. Everything I know is in my head, and heads can be quiet." He let that sit. "For a price. Or loudly. For nothing."

"You'd hang yourself alongside me."

"Yes," Crake said. "I've considered it. It's remarkable how little that consideration weighs, once a man has lost his warrant card, his name, and his country." He straightened the receiver cord with his free hand, an old habit, ordering small things because large ones had escaped all ordering. "Good afternoon, Mr. Halloran. I'll be in touch through counsel. If I decide to have counsel."

He rang off before the minister could answer, and stood in the quiet flat listening to the hum of the disconnected line, and understood that he had just made the only choice still available to him: not innocence, which was gone, and not escape, which was a slower version of drowning, but leverage. He would sell what remained of himself to whoever paid most — the journalists, the opposition, the prosecution — and he would live out his days wherever he landed knowing that every man who shook his hand would be shaking the hand of a thing that had once been a policeman.

He finished packing. He did not take the boat train. Not yet. First there was a solicitor in Chancery Lane who handled delicate matters, and a statement that could be drafted tonight and placed in safekeeping by morning — his confession-shaped insurance policy, the last page of the file of his own life.

As he snapped the locks shut, his eye caught the mantelpiece, where a framed photograph stood that he had forgotten to remove: himself at twenty-six, newly sworn in, standing beside his first governor outside this very building, thin-faced and bright-eyed and absolutely certain. He picked it up. The glass reflected the lamplight so that the young man's face shone white, like a face seen underwater.

He put the photograph face-down on the mantel and left it there, and carried his case down the stairs into the street, where the gas lamps were coming on one by one along the terrace, and a newsboy was running the far corner crying the evening edition — and Crake caught, above the rattle of carts, the boy's voice pitching his name, his own name, into the dark.
