## 29. Testimony

They gave me a witness room with a window that looked out on nothing but the blank brick face of the court annex, and I sat in it for two hours while the case against Edith Marlowe went on without me. I could hear nothing through the wall — only, once in a long while, the muffled rise of a voice reaching some pitch that carried, and then silence again, like a wave deciding not to reach the shore.

I had dressed carefully, which was itself a confession of sorts. A plain grey coat, no colour, no buttons that might catch light. Nell had brushed my hair for me at Mrs. Aldous's kitchen table, standing behind my chair on a stool, working the brush with the terrible concentration of a child who has learned that small rituals are what hold a morning together.

"You look like a lady in a picture," she said.

"I feel like a criminal in a dock."

"That's the same thing sometimes," she said, with the flat wisdom children acquire too early, and I had no argument to make against it.

At half past eleven a clerk opened the door and said my name wrong — *Miss Vane* rendered as something with a *w* in the middle of it — and I stood up, and my knees held, and I followed him down the corridor toward the courtroom where history, having spent all year pretending to happen to other people, had finally cleared its throat and asked for me by name.

* * *

The Old Bailey does its business under glass. Light comes down through the great dome in a pale column, catching dust, falling across the galleries where the public sits packed shoulder to shoulder. There were women up there — dozens of them, more than the ushers liked, their hats a dark tide above the rail. Someone had told me they came every day now. Someone had told me they were quiet, and that the quiet was worse than cheering.

Edith sat in the dock in a plain dark dress, her hair pinned severely, her hands folded on the rail before her. She looked thinner than she had seven months ago; Holloway had taken flesh from her the way frost takes moisture from a window, leaving everything else — the spine of her, the set of her jaw — harder and clearer than before. When I came in she did not smile. She looked at me the way she had once looked at me across the bonnet of the car on a wet night in Holloway Road: measuring whether I would flinch when it mattered.

Counsel for the Crown was a Mr. Fennimore, dry as old rope, with a voice designed to make outrage sound like bookkeeping. Counsel for the defence — ours — was a lean, weary man named Quill, whom Edith had chosen precisely because he looked like a man who had lost arguments before and intended to lose this one honestly.

"Your name," said Quill, once I had taken the stand and sworn, "is Bridget Anne Vane."

"Bram," I said. "Everyone calls me Bram."

"For how long have you been employed by the defendant?"

"Three years. As her driver."

"And in that capacity you handled her correspondence."

Here it was. The whole room seemed to tilt very slightly toward me, the way a carriage tilts going into a curve. I had rehearsed this sentence perhaps four hundred times, in kitchens and railway yards and the dark of my lodgings, and every rehearsal had ended differently, because the truth is a thing you cannot steer, only carry.

"Yes," I said. "I read her letters."

There it was. Said aloud, in a building built by an empire, under a dome through which God's own daylight fell. Somewhere in the gallery a woman made a small sound — not a gasp, exactly. More like a door closing gently.

Fennimore was on his feet before the echo settled. "My lord, if the witness is confessing to a criminal act—"

"The witness," said the judge, an old man whose name I have mercifully forgotten, "will be permitted to tell this court what she did, in her own words, and we will concern ourselves with the law of it afterward. Proceed."

Quill turned back to me, and I saw that he had known what I would say — that he had begged me, in chambers, three nights ago, not to say it, because a voluntary confession of intercepting mail would hand the Crown a stick with which to beat both me and the movement, and I had told him, with what I hope was courtesy and know was stubbornness, that the stick already existed, and that every day I declined to pick it up myself it lay there between us, ready for someone worse.

"Tell the court," Quill said heavily, "how it began."

* * *

How it began. That was easy, because it began so small.

"A letter arrived one evening in the winter of 1911," I said. "Miss Marlowe was at a meeting in Croydon. The envelope was from a man I knew to be ill — a correspondent she'd been worried about. I told myself I would steam it open only to see whether he was dying, and if he was, I would wire her at the hall." My voice sounded strange in the dome, flattened and far away, as though someone else were using it. "He wasn't dying. He was asking her for money. But once the flap was loose, I — read the rest. It took four minutes. Then I sealed it again and put it on her desk and never said a word."

"And after that?"

"After that it became a habit. A discipline, I told myself. I used the kettle in the scullery. I kept the timing to under five minutes per letter. I convinced myself I was — studying history." I heard the old phrase come out of my mouth and heard, finally, how thin it was. "I believed history was something that happened to other people, and that reading Miss Marlowe's letters was my way of standing near it without being touched by it."

"And was that true?"

"No." I looked at Edith in the dock. She was watching me with an expression I could not read, and I understood that whatever it cost her to hear this, she had decided it should cost her nothing visibly. "No. It wasn't true then, and I think some part of me always knew it wasn't true. History was happening to me the whole time. I simply hadn't agreed to notice."

Fennimore rose, unhurried, smoothing his papers as though the confession had been a gift he'd ordered in advance. "Madam witness. You admit to a sustained course of criminal conduct. Intercepting, opening, and reading correspondence not addressed to you — over what period?"

"Nineteen months."

"Nineteen months." He let it sit in the air. "And you did this to a woman you claim — what is the word your friends use — to serve?"

"I did it to a woman I loved," I said, and the word *loved* went up into the dome and hung there, and nobody in that courtroom, not Fennimore, not the judge, not the packed gallery, pretended for one second that I meant anything smaller by it. "Which is not a defence. It's only the truth about why."

Fennimore blinked once, recalibrating. Prosecutors hate a witness who gives them nothing to push against. He tried anyway. "You will appreciate, madam, that this court may struggle to accept the testimony of a self-confessed spy regarding matters of—"

"My lord," I said, turning to the bench without waiting for leave, because I had promised myself I would do this and doing it politely would still be doing it, "may I say why I am here? Not why counsel called me. Why I came."

The judge studied me over his spectacles for a long moment. "Briefly."

"In June of last year a courier named Thomas Ellery was murdered on the North Road. His killers worked for Undersecretary Halloran, and the purpose of his murder was to clear the way for a plot to blame a bombing on the suffrage movement. A ledger proving this crossed my path. I carried it from London to Manchester past three checkpoints, and it was published, and Mr. Halloran has resigned, and none of that would have happened if I had been the woman I claimed to be — a bystander with clean hands. It happened because I broke the law nineteen months running and got good at keeping secrets from people who trusted me." I turned back to Fennimore. "So yes, sir. Disbelieve my testimony. But disbelieve it knowing what kind of witness you're disbelieving: one with no virtue left to protect, who has come here to spend the last coin she has, which is the truth, badly kept."

The quiet in that courtroom was total. Up in the gallery, the dark tide of hats had gone utterly still.

Fennimore sat down.

* * *

They let me out at one o'clock for the luncheon adjournment, and I walked out onto the steps expecting — I don't know what. Consequence. Arrest, perhaps; Fennimore had muttered to the judge about the interception charge and received a noncommittal grunt, which meant the threat would hang over me like weather until someone decided whether to rain.

What I found instead was the press.

They were three deep along the railings, and when I appeared they surged like water finding a breach. Questions came at me from all sides — *Miss Vane! Miss Vane, is it true you read the letters? How many? Did Miss Marlowe know? Did she ask you to?*

"No," I said, stopping, because stopping was the only way any answer would survive the noise. "She never asked me. She never knew until I told her, and I told her too late to matter, and she forgave me faster than I've forgiven myself. That's the whole of it."

A young reporter with ink on his cuff pushed to the front. "Why confess it now? You could have testified about the ledger without ever mentioning the letters."

"Because the letters are how I learned to doubt everything except what I'd seen with my own eyes," I said. "And because somewhere out there—" I gestured vaguely northward, toward Holloway, toward every prison holding women whose real crime was refusing to be invisible, "—there are girls being told that the price of a conscience is silence. I paid that price for two years. I'm finished paying it."

Someone shouted something about heroines. I shook my head and pushed through, and a woman in the crowd — ordinary, middle-aged, wearing a shop-girl's coat — caught my sleeve as I passed and pressed something into my glove and was gone before I could speak. On the pavement later I opened my hand. It was a button. A cheap horn button, the kind off any coat in England, and I understood it completely, the way one understands things in wartime or grief: *we see you; we are dressed like everyone; we are everywhere.*

I stood on the steps of the Old Bailey holding a stranger's button, and for the first time since the night Nell knocked at our door, my hands stopped shaking.

* * *

The afternoon session belonged to the ledger. Quill walked the court through it page by page — the payments, the initials, the entry for the twelfth of June with its chilling bureaucratic tidiness: *N.R. — matter concluded — courier disposed.* Halloran's resignation had been framed by his allies as illness and by his enemies as cowardice, and the Crown's remaining strategy was transparently to sever him from Edith entirely: to argue that even if a conspiracy had existed, Miss Marlowe's connection to it was circumstantial, hysterical, invented by persons of dubious reliability.

Persons such, Fennimore implied with every second sentence, as myself.

He rose for his cross-examination just after three, and he had recovered his bookkeeping voice. "Madam Vane. This ledger. You carried it to Manchester yourself."

"I did."

"Alone. Past three police checkpoints. Evading — by your own account elsewhere — an inspector assigned to locate it." He smiled thinly. "You'll forgive me, but that is a remarkable performance for a chauffeuse."

"It is a remarkable performance," I agreed, "for anyone. Which is rather my point. If I could do it, in a borrowed coat, with a child's doll for a hiding place, imagine what men with resources could have done. Imagine, indeed, what men with resources *did* do, on the twelfth of June, on the North Road."

"Are you suggesting members of His Majesty's government commit murder?"

"I'm suggesting the evidence suggests it, and I'm suggesting you read it aloud to this court, sir, because you're the one being paid to handle it."

Laughter moved through the gallery — quickly suppressed, but unmistakable, and unmistakably female. The judge rapped for order. Fennimore's ears had gone pink, and he changed course the way a brawler changes hands.

"Very well. The ledger. Tell us precisely how it came into your possession."

And there it was — the question I had known was coming since the day Crake first called at the house, the question with Nell inside it like a stone inside a peach.

"A child brought it," I said. "To Miss Marlowe's door, on the ninth of July, at eleven minutes past midnight."

"A child." Fennimore pounced. "Name and age, please."

"She was nine years old. Her name is Nell Ellery. She is Thomas Ellery's daughter, and she watched those men kill her father, and the note pinned to her coat said *forget the face*, and she has not forgotten it, and neither shall I, and if you imagine for one moment that I will hand you her name and address so that Inspector Crake's colleagues can pay her a friendly call, you have misjudged this courtroom, this city, and me."

I had said his name — *Crake* — deliberately, and I watched it land. Fennimore's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. Everyone in that building knew whose inquiry had produced the arrest warrant for Edith; everyone knew the same inspector's signature sat beneath the affidavit swearing the child-witness testimony unreliable. To name Crake aloud was to tie the prosecution's leading instrument to the conspiracy's surviving architecture, and Fennimore knew it, and the gallery knew it, and I suspect the judge knew it.

"That will do, madam," said the judge quietly, but he wrote something down, and I saw him write it.

Fennimore tried twice more — once to establish that my reading of the letters proved a pattern of dishonesty, once to suggest that a woman capable of steaming envelopes might be capable of forging ledgers — and both times Quill rose lazily and observed that the Crown's position appeared to be simultaneously that the witness was too dishonest to believe and too ingenious to have failed, and the judge, who was old but not asleep, sustained us both times.

At ten minutes past five he adjourned for the day, announcing that closing addresses would begin tomorrow morning, and that verdict might follow by afternoon.

By afternoon. Twenty-four hours, give or take, between Edith and the rest of her life.

* * *

I went to her cell — they allowed it, one visitor, fifteen minutes, a matron within earshot — and found her sitting upright on the narrow bed as though she'd been expecting me since noon.

"Well?" she said. "Tell me everything. Start with Fennimore's face when you compared him unfavourably to a competent criminal."

"There was laughter," I admitted. "In the gallery."

"There was laughter in the gallery," she repeated, savouring it like wine. "Seven months I've sat in this building while respectable men explained to the newspapers that the movement consists of shrieking viragoes, and you stroll in for one afternoon and get the gallery laughing at the Crown." She shook her head slowly. "Bram Vane, the observer. Untouched by history."

"Don't."

"I'm teasing." But then the humour left her face, carefully, the way she removed her gloves. "Listen to me. Tomorrow they will convict me of something. Perhaps only the window at the Home Office, perhaps the conspiracy charge if Fennimore can dress it up. Either way I go back to Holloway, and either way the ledger stands published, and Halloran is finished, and the frame is broken. Whatever they read out tomorrow, that part is done. We did it. Do you understand? It's done."

"Not all of it." My voice came out lower than I meant. "There's the sentence."

"There's always the sentence." She reached through the bars — the matron pretended not to see — and took my wrist, her grip still strong despite everything. "Bram. Nineteen months of letters. You confessed it in front of the entire British press, and the world didn't end, and the cause didn't die, and you didn't die of shame, though I know you expected to. What did you learn on those steps today?"

I thought of the button in my pocket. Of the hats, still as a held breath.

"That I wasn't the only one reading other people's post," I said. "That half the country is standing outside locked doors trying to hear what's really being said inside. And that maybe the difference between a spy and a witness is only—" I searched for it. "—who you finally give the letter to."

Edith smiled then, fully, and for a moment she was the woman on the wet street with the lantern, three years younger, unbroken. "Give it to the future," she said. "It's the only correspondent who hasn't lied to us yet."

The matron coughed. Fifteen minutes. I stood, and Edith released my wrist, and as I turned to go she said, softly, so the words were mine alone:

"One more thing. Tomorrow, whatever they announce — watch Crake. Watch his hands, not his face. Men like Crake have begun to understand what happens to instruments when the hand that holds them is cut off." Her eyes glittered. "I want to know what he does when he realizes the hand is gone."

I walked out through the stone corridors with her words in my ears and the horn button in my pocket, and somewhere below me, in the administrative offices of the Old Bailey, Inspector Crake — come to review the transcript of the afternoon session, they said later, with unusual eagerness — was reading my testimony by lamplight, reading his own name in it, reading it twice.

And beginning, I think, to count the hours.
