## 30. The Verdict

They came for her at half past six in the morning, which Edith Marlowe considered a discourtesy even by the standards of His Majesty's prisons.

She had dressed before they knocked. That was the trick of it — to be already standing when the cell door opened, so that no one could say she had been caught unprepared, not even by the dawn itself. She had pressed her blouse beneath the mattress overnight, an old suffragist trick from Holloway's first incarnation, and she wore the dark grey skirt that made her look, she hoped, like what she was: a woman of property and principle being tried for something she had not done, in a court that did not much care about the difference.

The wardress who escorted her said nothing, but at the corridor's turn she paused — just a fraction, just long enough — and touched two fingers to her own collar. A signal. Edith had learned to read these small languages over the years, the grammar of sympathy smuggled through institutional stone. *Someone has brought you news. Someone is thinking of you.*

"Thank you," Edith said quietly, and meant it for more than the pause.

* * *

The Old Bailey had grown teeth since her last visit. There were barriers now along the pavement, iron railings bolted down against the crowds, and beyond them a press of bodies three deep at every entrance: women in the Movement's colours, men with newspapers folded under their arms, photographers perched on ladders like crows on a fence. When the prison van turned in through the gates, a sound went up — not the jeers she might have expected two years ago, but something lower and stranger, a collective intake of breath, and then voices, dozens of them, calling her name.

Edith could not see them from inside the van. But she heard them, and she found that her hands were trembling, and she was glad of it. Trembling meant the blood was still moving. Trembling meant she had not yet become one of those creatures the state manufactured so efficiently — calm, compliant, hollowed out and labelled.

Bram would be there. Bram had written — one letter, passed through the solicitor's office, four lines long and worth more than any speech Edith had ever delivered. *Nell is well. The proofs are safe. I will be in the gallery. Look up.*

Look up. As if Edith needed telling. She had spent forty years looking up.

* * *

The courtroom smelled of beeswax and wet wool and, beneath it all, the faint sourness of fear that clung to public buildings the way lichen clings to north-facing walls. The gallery was full. Edith kept her eyes forward as the wardresses walked her to the dock — she would not give them the satisfaction of watching her search the benches — but when she sat, she permitted herself the indulgence.

Third row. Grey coat, hat pinned severely, hands folded in her lap like a woman attending a funeral. Bram Vane, who had once told her, in the early days, that history was something that happened to other people, and who now sat in a courtroom gallery with the face of a woman keeping vigil.

Their eyes met. Neither smiled. It was not a morning for smiling. But Bram lifted her chin — a degree, perhaps two — and Edith felt it land somewhere beneath her ribs like a key turning.

Then the proceedings swallowed everything, as proceedings do.

* * *

They had charged her with conspiracy and sedition and, latterly, with "incitement to acts destructive of property." The bomb — the device Halloran's people had planted in the Clerkenwell storeroom and attributed, in sealed memoranda, to the Women's Social and Political Union — hung over the trial like weather. Sir Reginald Foss, the prosecution, was a heavy man with a heavy voice, built by God for pronouncements. He spoke the word *bombardment* when he meant *bombing*. He spoke of *these women* as though reading from a zoological catalogue. He produced witnesses who had seen Edith near the storeroom — witnesses whose travel warrants, as Bram's published ledger had shown to the entire reading public, had been drawn on a Home Office account.

That was the strange architecture of the trial: everyone in the room knew. The jury knew; the judge pretended not to know; Foss knew and ploughed on regardless, because his instructions came from above the law, and because a man like Foss had never in his life chosen the harder door. The ledger had run in the Manchester papers eleven days ago. By now it had been reprinted everywhere from Glasgow to Bristol. Halloran's name was in every mouth. And still the machine ground forward, because machines do not stop merely because their operators have been disgraced; they stop only when someone pulls the lever, and no one with authority wished to be holding the lever when history looked down at its hands.

Edith listened. She had gotten good at listening — it was most of what prison taught, besides patience and the mending of socks. She listened to Foss describe her as "a woman of considerable intelligence and correspondingly dangerous convictions," and thought: he means it as condemnation, and will never understand that half this gallery hears it as citation for honours.

When her own counsel rose — a tired, decent man named Aldous whom the Movement had scraped together fees to retain — he argued what could be argued. That the evidence linking her to the bombing was fabricated. That the courier's death and the forged memoranda formed a pattern of official criminality now documented and public. He did not say the words *Undersecretary Halloran* aloud; Judge Merrivale had warned him twice about impertinence, and a third warning would have cost him the case entirely. But he laid the documents on the table like a man laying out cards he knows are marked, and let the jury see them anyway.

And the jury — seven men and five women, for once the law had allowed women into the box, a concession won by precisely the sort of agitation Edith had spent her life conducting — the jury looked at the documents, and looked at Edith, and looked at the judge, and understood perfectly well what was being asked of them.

* * *

On the third afternoon, while counsel argued points of law that Edith suspected had been settled before either barrister was born, her attention drifted to the gallery again.

Bram was not alone. Beside her sat a small figure Edith had to lean forward to see properly: Nell, in a coat too big for her, her hands wrapped around the gallery rail, her eyes enormous and fixed on the dock. The Quakers had brought her. Or Bram had argued them into it — Edith could picture the argument, quiet and immovable, the way water argues with stone.

The child had been told, presumably, that the woman in the dock was on trial. What a six-year-old made of it, Edith could not guess. But Nell did not look away once, not in all those hours, and there was something in her stare — fierce, unblinking, the stare of a witness who remembered exactly what remembering had cost her — that steadied Edith more than any counsel's argument.

*You are why we are here,* Edith thought at her. *Not the cause. Not the movement. You, and your father, and the truth you carried in your head when no one would have blamed a child that age for letting it die.* She thought it with such force that Nell seemed to feel it; the girl's chin came up, mirroring without knowing it the gesture Bram had made, and Edith had to turn her face toward the window before the press saw anything usable.

* * *

The verdict came on a grey Friday, rain ticking against the high windows like a clerk counting out coins.

Judge Merrivale summed up for two hours. He was careful — more careful than Edith had ever heard a judge be in a political case — and his care was itself a document. He acknowledged, in the passive voice that English institutions use for their sins, that "certain irregularities in the provenance of evidence had been alleged." He instructed the jury that they must consider only the charges before them. He did not instruct them to disbelieve the newspapers. He could not. Everyone in the room had read them.

The jury was out for five hours. Edith spent them in the cells below, sitting very straight on a bench scarred by fifty years of nervous initials, listening to the building breathe above her.

She was not afraid of the verdict. This surprised her a little; she examined the absence of fear the way one probes a missing tooth. She had made her peace months ago, on the night she told Bram that if anyone must be caught, it should be her — a famous name makes better headlines and worse consequences, she had said, dressing duty up in strategy's clothes because Bram would have fought her otherwise. She had walked into that rally knowing Crake would come. She had whispered *finish it* and meant every word.

No — what she feared was smaller and sharper. She feared the years. Not the hardship of them; she had done Holloway before, and hunger strike, and force-feeding, and survived all three with her mind intact and her anger refined to a cutting edge. What she feared was time itself, sliding past the bars while the country moved on without her. Seven months of Bram and Nell's life, unlived in her presence. The lantern in the window burning for someone behind a wall.

The bell rang. The wardress came. Edith stood, and smoothed her skirt, and climbed the stairs into her future.

* * *

"Guilty of unlawful assembly."

The foreman — a carpenter, by his hands, a broad man with a face like a closed door — said it evenly, and then added, in the same breath, before the clerk could silence him: "Not guilty of the rest."

It was not how verdicts were returned. It was, Edith would think later, the most honest sentence anyone had pronounced in that courtroom in a generation. The jury had done arithmetic: they could not acquit a woman of Edith Marlowe's reputation outright, not with the government's face pressed so hard against the glass. But they had refused — flatly, publicly refused — to convict her of the conspiracy, of the sedition, of anything touching the bombing. They gave the state a lesser charge, the way one throws a smaller coin to a beggar one pities and fears, and they gave the truth everything else.

Merrivale's jaw tightened. Foss began gathering his papers with the briskness of a man leaving a room he finds distasteful. And the gallery — the gallery made a sound. Not a cheer; the ushers were moving too quickly for that. A sound like a wave deciding whether to break, a great indrawn roar held behind clenched teeth, and through it Edith heard, distinctly, one voice: Nell's, piping and clear and utterly unafraid —

"She didn't do it! She *didn't*!"

A usher's hand, gently applied. Bram's arm going around the small shoulders. And Edith, standing in the dock with sentence pending, laughing — actually laughing — until the judge threatened her with contempt and she mastered herself and stood instead with her chin high, meeting the eyes of every juror in turn. Thank you, she told them silently. You did what you could in the room you were given. That is all any of us do.

Six months, Merrivale pronounced, for the unlawful assembly — "a lenient term," he noted, "in view of circumstances which this court is not at liberty to discuss."

*Not at liberty to discuss.* Edith nearly laughed again. The whole empire was not at liberty to discuss it. The whole empire was discussing nothing else.

* * *

They read her the newspapers that evening — the wardress who had signalled in the corridor had arranged it, or perhaps Bram had bribed half the prison staff, which was likelier. Edith sat on her bunk with the pages trembling slightly in her hands, though her hands had been steady all day.

HALLORAN RESIGNS. The headline ran across four columns. UNDERSECRETARY QUITS AMID LEDGER SCANDAL — INQUIRY DEMANDED IN COMMONS. Beneath it, a photograph of Halloran descending the steps of a ministerial residence, hat low, mouth a thin line, surrounded by reporters whose questions the caption did not record but whose posture suggested wolves around a wounded stag. He resigned citing health. He cited health the way drowning men cite water.

There was more. The Home Office account that had paid the false witnesses' expenses — itemised, reproduced, circled in the *Manchester Guardian*'s dense type. The dead courier named at last, in print, with his widow's photograph: a plain-faced woman in black, holding a hat that was clearly not hers, staring into the camera with an expression Edith recognised from mirrors. The framing of the suffrage movement for the Clerkenwell bomb — collapsed, the leader-writer called it, like a table with its legs kicked away.

*Justice partial but real,* Bram had written once, in one of the letters Edith was never supposed to have known she'd intercepted. She had known for months, of course — had known since the second month of their acquaintance, when the steam marks on the envelopes grew too consistent to blame on the weather — and had said nothing, because she had wanted to see what Bram would become when the knowing finally surfaced. This, apparently: this woman who carried ledgers sewn into coat linings through armed checkpoints, who searched railway yards all night for a frightened child, who testified in open court about her own shame rather than let the archive lie.

Edith folded the newspaper and pressed it flat and found that she was crying, which annoyed her extremely, and then found that she did not care.

Halloran had resigned. The frame had broken. The Movement's name would be dragged through inquiries and committees for a year, and would come out the other side cleaner than it had gone in — not clean, never clean, politics left no one clean, but *cleaner*, and the country's ears had changed, she could hear it even through prison walls, in the tone of the newspapers, in the fact that a jury of respectable citizens had refused the state its conviction.

And she had six months to serve, on a charge that was true only in the sense that all her charges were true: she had assembled, unlawfully in the eyes of a law that had never counted her among the lawful, and she would assemble again the day she walked free.

* * *

The wardress brought the last item after lights-out, as promised. A single sheet, unsigned, in a hand Edith knew better than her own.

*Nell drew you a picture but they wouldn't allow crayon wax past the gate, so she described it instead: you, standing up, with the sun behind you. She says the sun is because you looked up. She says to tell you she remembered, and remembering was right.*

*I'll be here every visiting day. Finish your sentence; I'll finish everything else.*

*— B.*

Edith lay back on the narrow bed with the paper against her chest and listened to the prison settle into its nighttime machinery of bolts and boots. Six months. She had served longer for less. Somewhere across London a lantern burned in a window — Bram's signal, lit since the arrest, never extinguished — and somewhere a child slept who had been told to forget a face and had refused, and somewhere a minister packed his offices in disgrace while inquiry commissioners sharpened their pens.

Partial justice. Real justice. The kind you carry through checkpoints and serve sentences beside, the kind that costs what it costs and is worth it anyway.

Tomorrow was a visiting day. Edith closed her eyes and began, as she did every night now, composing what she would say — and for the first time since the dock, she found she did not know where the words would end, or how far past gratitude they might go, and she fell asleep smiling at her own cowardice, saving it all for the glass partition, saving it all for Bram.
