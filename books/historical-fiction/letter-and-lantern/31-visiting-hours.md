## 31. Visiting Hours

The train from Manchester to London took four hours, and I spent every one of them rehearsing what I would say. By the time the platform at Euston slid past the window — the same station where a sergeant had turned my luggage inside out and let me go by inches — I had composed and discarded eleven openings. *How is the food?* Too small. *The papers say* — no; she would know exactly what the papers said, better than I did, having seen them through bars. In the end I settled on nothing at all, which was, I was learning, the most honest thing I could bring her.

Holloway had changed its face since the night marches of two years before. Then it had been lit with lanterns and shouting and the wet shine of police batons in the gaslight; now it was only a long brick wall under a grey sky, and women queued quietly outside with parcels wrapped in brown paper and string. Fruit that would survive the search. Books with spines uncracked. Soap, because prison soap was a cruelty all its own.

I joined the queue with a parcel of my own: apples, a tin of barley sugar, and a copy of *Jane Eyre* whose hollow spine held nothing but a pressed violet from Nell's garden. I had learned about hollow books. I had also learned their limits. The violet was the whole message now — some things you carried plainly or not at all.

The wardress at the desk had eyes like ledger lines. She took my visiting order, stamped it without reading it, and said, "Twenty minutes. No touching. If either of you raises your voice the visit ends." Then she walked me down a corridor that smelled of carbolic and boiled cabbage, past a barred window where February light came in thin as watered milk, and into a room of trestle tables divided by a painted line down the middle.

Edith was already seated on the far side.

I had prepared myself for it — for the grey of the uniform, for the cropped hair they gave women who went on hunger strike, for the pallor of someone living on bread and milk and principle. Preparing oneself is a lie we tell in corridors. Seeing her was a blow anyway. Not because she looked broken. Because she didn't. She sat the way she sat in the car when the route ahead was blocked: spine straight, hands folded, eyes already working out the problem of me.

"Bram," she said, and something in my chest that had been clenched since the trial opened one careful inch.

"You've been eating."

"Under protest." Her mouth curved. "They force-feed the strikers with a tube and a funnel. I decided to spare them the theatre. It seemed wasteful to give them a martyr's story twice." She tilted her head. "You look terrible."

"Thank you. I've been practising."

"Practising what?"

"Saying things directly. You'll find it disorienting."

The wardress by the door shifted her weight. Edith lowered her voice accordingly, so that our words stayed between us and the paint.

"How is she?"

"Nell's well. Growing like a weed. Miss Hartley has her learning sums and she's furious about it, which I'm told is how you know a child is thriving." I kept my voice light because if I didn't it would crack along its whole length. "She draws. Constantly. Horses, mostly, now. Not crests. Miss Hartley thought you'd want to know that."

Something moved behind Edith's eyes — relief wearing its work clothes. "And the drawings of horses? Are they any good?"

"They're terrible. All legs."

"Good. Let her be terrible at safe things." She glanced down at her folded hands. When she looked up again, the lightness was gone, set aside like a coat. "Bram. Tell me properly. Not the version you'd give a newspaper. How is she really?"

I had come four hours to be asked precisely this, and still it nearly undid me.

"She wakes sometimes in the night," I said. "Not screaming. That would almost be easier. She just gets up and checks that the door is locked, and then comes and stands in my doorway until she can see I'm there, and then goes back. She doesn't remember doing it in the morning. Or she pretends not to, which is its own kind of courage." My throat tightened. "She asked me last week whether remembering her father's face was wrong. Whether that's why the men came. As though memory were the crime."

"What did you tell her?"

"That forgetting is the only unforgivable thing." I heard the words leave me and recognised them as true in a way that surprised me. "That he gave her his face to keep, and keeping it is the work now. Hers and ours."

Edith was quiet a moment. On the table between us lay my visiting permit, and beside it her own, and I noticed hers had been annotated in pencil in the corner — a tiny mark, a number. They numbered everything here. Every woman a file.

"And the case?" she asked. "The appeal?"

"The solicitor says the lesser charge won't hold on review. Six months becomes three. Three becomes time served, possibly by spring." I leaned forward. "Halloran resigned. There's talk of a select committee. Crake has vanished — someone saw him at Holyhead with two trunks, and someone else swears he never left London. Either way, he isn't writing the story anymore. We are."

"We," she repeated softly, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read until suddenly I could. "You said *we*. Do you know, Bram, when you first came to drive for me, you used to talk about the movement the way a man talks about weather happening in another county. Interesting. Regrettable. Someone else's sky." She shook her head slowly. "Now you sit in Holloway's visiting room and say *we are* as naturally as breathing."

"The sky turned out to be mine too."

"Yes." Something flickered across her face — the memory, perhaps, of lanterns on Holloway Road, of my hands white on the steering wheel while she stood in the crowd. "It did."

A clock somewhere struck the quarter hour. Our twenty minutes were bleeding away, and there was a thing I had carried up from Manchester in my chest like contraband, and I found, sitting opposite her across the painted line, that I could not smuggle it past my own checkpoints after all.

"I have to tell you something," I said, "and then you're going to use your remaining minutes telling me I was a fool, and I'd like you to know in advance that I accept the charge."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "Go on."

"In the house in Islington, before all of this — the letters you wrote, the ones I steamed open and read and told myself were history happening to other people—" My hands wanted to wring each other on the table and I made them be still. "There was one I never told you about. One I read forty times and never once understood until it was too late to matter. You wrote to a woman named Ruth Fenwick — I don't even know who she was — and you said, *the trouble with loving someone who watches you is that they learn to see you, and then you must live seen.*"

The wardress coughed. Edith didn't move.

"I thought it was about politics," I went on, quieter. "I thought you meant the country watching you, the newspapers, the movement. And it was only standing in the railway yard at four in the morning with Nell asleep against my shoulder that I understood you'd written it about yourself. About being known. About wanting it and fearing it in the same breath." I swallowed. "I am not going to ask you anything, Edith. I'm not going to make a claim on a woman serving a sentence, that would be theft of the worst kind. I only need you to know that I read everything, and I understood nothing, and now I understand that one sentence completely, and I have come four hours on a train to sit across a painted line and say it out loud."

The room was very quiet. Carbolic and cabbage and the tick of some unseen clock, and Edith Marlowe looking at me with an expression I had never once seen on her in two years of watching her face — unarmoured.

"You kept that letter?" she said at last.

"I kept all of them. They're in the archive now, at Miss Pym's library. Sealed. Your letters, my testimony, the ledger pages that survived Crewe. Whoever keeps the archive next will have all of it."

"Even the ones you shouldn't have read?"

"*Especially* those. A record with the sins left out isn't a record. It's a portrait." I managed something like a smile. "You taught me that. Or Nell did. Between you, I no longer know which lessons came from whom."

Edith looked down at her hands. When she raised her head, her eyes were bright and dry in the way that cost more than tears.

"When I wrote that letter," she said, "Ruth Fenwick had asked me why I never let anyone close. I told her it was strategy. Famous names draw fire, and you don't put the people you love in front of the guns." Her mouth twisted. "It was even true. But it wasn't the whole truth, and she knew it, and so did I. The whole truth is that I had already put someone in front of the guns, hadn't I. I handed you a child and a packet and a road full of checkpoints, and I called it duty because calling it trust would have required me to admit what trusting you meant."

"You didn't hand me anything," I said. "I volunteered. At Euston. Before you'd finished asking."

"Yes. That's what frightened me." She said it simply, without drama, and it landed harder than any speech she'd ever given from a platform. "I have stood on stages in front of ten thousand people and felt nothing I couldn't control. And I stood in my own hall watching you pack that hollow book, and my hands shook, Bram. Mine. Because I finally understood that history wasn't happening to other people for me either. It was happening to whoever I loved, and I had gone and loved someone with steady hands and a habit of reading other people's mail, and there was no checkpoint for that. No route around it."

The clock struck again. Five minutes. Perhaps less.

"So that's my confession," she said. "You brought yours across four counties. It seemed poor form not to match it."

I discovered that I was gripping the edge of the table. That the painted line ran between us like the equator, like the frontier of some country neither of us had ever expected to cross, and that it was only paint.

"When you get out," I said. My voice came out rougher than intended. "When they walk you out those gates — and they will, in spring, the solicitor's certain of it —"

"I know what I'll do," she interrupted gently. "I've had six months to plan it, and I plan everything, you know that. I will walk out, and there will be photographers, and I will give them my chin and not my heart. And then I will look past all of them, past the cameras and the crowds and Crake's ghosts, at one particular face. And if that face is there, I shall get into whatever motorcar it arrived in, and I shall not give a single speech the entire way home."

I laughed — an absurd, wet sound, badly timed, entirely necessary. Across the room the wardress straightened.

"Time," the woman called.

Edith stood. Up close, across the line, she looked older than her years and younger than her photograph, both at once. She extended her hand — all the touching permitted — and I took it, and her grip was warm and brief and said everything the regulations forbade.

"Finish it," she whispered — the old words from the rally, repurposed, softened. "Not the case. The case is finishing itself. Finish *this*." Her eyes went briefly toward the wardress, then back. "Write to me. Real letters, Bram. Postmarked and stamped and steamed open by half the Home Office, I don't care. I spent years sending letters into the world hoping the right person would read them over my shoulder. Don't make me wait for the archive."

"I'll write tonight."

"Good." She released my hand and stepped back into the posture they expected — chin up, shoulders square, the public Edith reassembled in a single breath. "And Bram — the apples. Give them to Nell. Tell her they're from a friend who is terrible at horses."

Then the wardress was walking me back down the corridor, past the barred window where the light had shifted one degree nearer to spring, and out through the great door into the cold.

I stood on the pavement a long moment among the departing visitors, holding my empty string bag, feeling lighter than I had any right to feel and heavier than I could explain. Somewhere behind that wall a woman was returning to a cell to wait out the winter, and somewhere north a child was drawing horses with far too many legs, and between those two points ran a road I had driven in the dark with contraband sewn into my coat — and I understood, finally and completely, that I would drive it again tomorrow, and the day after, gladly, for the rest of my life, if that was what the route required.

History was not elsewhere. It was whoever carried the letter through the checkpoint. And I had one to write tonight, first-class, heavily sealed, addressed to Holloway Cell 47.

I bought paper and ink at the station bookstall, and on the train north, as the lamps of the midlands came up out of the dusk one by one like a signal being answered, I began:

*Dear Edith —*

*I am given to understand that you always suspected who was opening your post. What you do not know is how long I have been writing answers.*

But the second page refused to come right, and by Crewe I had crossed it out twice, and it was only somewhere past Stockport — past the very bend where a sergeant had once held a clipboard bearing a child's face — that the true opening finally arrived, plain as a lantern in a window. I wrote it down quickly, before the train could shake it loose, and read it back, and knew it was the letter I had been composing my whole life without knowing.

It began: *You once told me history happens to other people. I have come to believe you were lying to yourself as thoroughly as I was lying to me — and that we were both lying for the same reason.*
