## 32. Nell's Question

Nell had learned to count by lamplight.

One for the coals settling in the grate. Two for the clock in the hall that ticked like a man walking who never arrived anywhere. Three for Miss Vane's boots on the stair, always two at a time going up, one careful step at a time coming down, because coming down you might be carrying something — a tea tray, an armful of linens, a child half-asleep who must not be woken.

Four for the lantern in the front window, which burned every night now even when there was no fog and no reason for it. Nell had asked once why they wasted the oil. Miss Vane had said, *It isn't waste if someone is looking for it,* which Nell had thought about for three days without deciding what it meant.

Tonight she lay in her bed in the room that had become hers — hers, with a hairbrush that was hers and a nightgown with her own initials stitched at the collar in thread the colour of forget-me-nots — and she listened to the house being a house. Floorboards talking. Rain trying the windows like a polite visitor knocking and knocking. Downstairs a door opened and shut softly, and then boots on the stair, two at a time going up past her floor to wherever Miss Vane went when the house was asleep, and then nothing.

Nell did not sleep. She had got very good at not sleeping since November. She could lie still as a coat on a hook and let the dark move around her, and while it moved she thought.

She thought about faces.

* * *

Her father's face lived behind her eyes the way a picture lives behind glass. She could take it out whenever she liked and look at it: the crease between his eyebrows when he read, deep enough to hold a pencil if he'd let her; the way his mouth went crooked before he laughed, pulling left, so you always knew the laugh was coming before it came; his whiskers against her cheek when he lifted her, scratchy as the doormat but warm, warm underneath the scratch.

She had been told once to forget a face. Not her father's — another one, a stranger's, a face lit from below by a lantern held by a shaking hand, a face that had looked at something terrible and then run. *Forget the face,* the note said. The note that came folded inside her coat with her father's blood drying stiff on the hem.

She hadn't forgotten either face. That was the trouble.

Because here was the thing Nell had worked out all by herself, lying awake in houses that were not hers, in beds that smelled of other people's soap: the men who had killed her father had never seen her face. She was sure of it. She had been small and shadowed and quick, and she had run, and the lantern light had slid off her like water off oilcloth. Nobody had ever come asking after a girl. They had asked after a packet, after a courier, after a woman seen at Euston with too much luggage. But no one had ever said *there is a child.*

Except Inspector Crake. Inspector Crake, who had stood in the hallway of Miss Marlowe's house with his hat in his hands like a churchgoer, and whose eyes had gone to her coat on the hook and stayed there a long moment, the way a cat's eyes stay on a curtain that has moved.

He had never seen her face either. But he had wanted to. And wanting, Nell understood, was nearly the same as seeing.

So she kept her face small. She kept it behind doors and under hats and inside the collar of her coat. And all the while her father's face stayed big behind her eyes, bigger than any face she'd been told to forget, and she carried both of them everywhere like two stones in one pocket, one she'd chosen and one she hadn't, and neither of them weighed anything at all and both of them weighed everything.

Tonight, with the rain knocking and the lantern burning its useless, necessary oil downstairs, the stones shifted in her pocket, and a question came loose that she had been keeping down for months the way you keep down a cough in church.

Was it wrong? Remembering?

*Forget the face,* the note said, and she had disobeyed a note. Disobeying notes was probably not a sin. But forgetting her father — that would have been worse than any sin she could name, and yet the note had ordered it, and grown-ups wrote the notes, and grown-ups made the rules, and somewhere in the space between those two facts Nell felt herself bending like a young tree in wind that wouldn't stop.

She needed it answered by someone whose answers bent the world back into shape.

She waited until the house had gone quiet enough to hear the rain properly. Then she pushed back the covers, found her slippers by feel — left one under the bed, right one hiding by the washstand where it always hid, the little coward — and went out onto the landing.

Light showed under Miss Vane's door.

* * *

Bram Vane sat at her desk with a letter half-written and could not finish it.

This had become a habit of late, these unfinished letters. Since Manchester. Since Deansgate. Since Edith's trial had filled the newspapers for weeks and then emptied them, leaving behind a verdict that satisfied the law and insulted everyone else, and a minister's resignation that tasted of victory only if you didn't chew it. Bram wrote letters now the way some people prayed — not expecting an answer, just needing the shape of words going outward, toward somebody.

This one began *Dear Miss Hartley* and concerned the archive, the boxes of letters and ledger pages she meant to carry to the librarian on Thursday, and it stalled at the second paragraph because through the wall she could hear a child not sleeping. She had learned the sound. Children who slept made one kind of silence, slow and even as breath. Children who were awake and lying very still made another kind entirely — a listening silence, a held-breath silence, the silence of a small person conducting surveillance.

Nell had been making that silence every night for a week.

Bram set down the pen. And then, because the universe has a sense of timing that no novelist would dare invent, came the smallest knock at her door — knuckle-light, apologetic, a knock that hoped to be mistaken for the house settling.

"Come in," Bram said.

The door opened six inches and Nell came through the gap sideways, like water finding a crack, dragging her blanket behind her because she was seven years old and seven-year-olds know that blankets are armour. Her hair was loose and wild from the pillow. Her feet were bare. One slipper had made the journey; the other had evidently been lost to enemy action on the landing.

"You're awake," Nell said, stating it like a charge sheet.

"So are you."

"I couldn't sleep." She stood by the door with her blanket clutched, doing the thing she did when something large was caught in her — going still, going watchful, a heron beside frozen water. "Can I ask you something?"

"You can always ask me something."

"It's a real one. Not a little one."

Bram put down her pen properly and turned her chair. This mattered. She had learned it mattered. Children could tell the difference between an adult who turned their whole body and one who merely tilted their head while keeping one eye on the interrupted work. "Then I'll give it a real answer. Best I can."

Nell considered this, the way she considered everything, thoroughly and slightly suspiciously, as though the world had once cheated her at cards and she intended never to be caught flat-footed again. Then she crossed the room — four steps, five, dragging her blanket like a bridal train — and climbed onto the end of the bed without invitation, because permission was a formality she reserved for strangers.

She arranged herself. She looked at her own knees. She looked at the fire. She looked at Bram.

And then, in the smallest voice Bram had heard her use since the railway yards — smaller even than the voice she'd used to say *he said mind the lantern, he said don't look back* — Nell asked:

"Was it wrong? Remembering?"

Bram kept her face steady. It cost something. Inside, everything lurched.

"Remembering what?" she asked gently, though she knew. God help her, she knew exactly.

"The note said forget the face." Nell's fingers worked at the edge of the blanket, pleating it, unpleating it. "But I didn't. I remembered him." A pause. "Both of them. Him and the bad one with the lantern. I remember them both every day and sometimes at night too, and—" The pleating stopped. Her chin came up, and beneath the fear in her eyes was something harder and older, something that had no business living in a seven-year-old's face and had moved in anyway, paying no rent. "Am I wicked? Because I didn't do what the paper said?"

There it was. The question underneath the question, the one Bram had been circling for months without naming it, the one that had started with steamed envelopes over a kettle and ended with a hollow book at Crewe and pages curling in a lavatory basin, ash swirling away down the water. *Was it wrong to know things I was told not to know? Was it wrong to keep what I was told to give up?*

"No," Bram said.

Just that, first. Just the word, plain and level, laid down like a plank across a gap, because the child needed footing before she needed reasons.

"No," she said again, and reached across and stilled the small hands on the blanket. "Listen to me, Nell. Listen carefully, because I'm going to say this once and I mean every word of it, and I don't say things I don't mean. Not anymore."

Nell nodded, solemn as a jury foreman.

"That note was written by a man who helped kill your father. You know that now, don't you? You've known it a long time."

Another nod. Smaller.

"He wasn't giving you a rule. He wasn't like your teacher telling you not to run in the corridor. He was trying to hide a crime, and you were the only witness, and he thought a child could be folded up small like a piece of paper and posted away into nowhere." Bram heard her own voice go low and hard, and softened it deliberately. "Forgetting wasn't his to order. It never was. Your memory belongs to you. It's yours the way your hands are yours and your eyes are yours. No one gets to post a note through your head telling you what to keep and what to throw out."

"But remembering makes me sad," Nell said. It came out like an accusation — against whom, she perhaps didn't know. Against the world entire, possibly. "Miss Quill at school says sad things should be put behind you. She says it in assembly. *Put it behind you and look forward.*" She recited it with the flat contempt children reserve for maxims handed down by people who have clearly never needed one. "If I put Daddy behind me, how will I know what his face looks like? If I put the bad man behind me, then nobody knows what the bad man looks like except me, and then he wins, doesn't he? He gets to be invisible."

"Yes," Bram said quietly. "That is exactly how he wins."

"And I don't want him to win."

"No. Nor do I."

Nell worried at this, her brow furrowing in a way that was so precisely her father's — Bram had seen the photograph, had studied the crease between the brows — that for a moment Bram had to look at the fire to keep her composure. The girl was a palimpsest. Underneath the fear and the flinch and the sleeplessness, the dead man's face kept surfacing in his daughter's expressions, ink showing through new paper. She would carry him her whole life whether she chose to or not. The only choice was whether she carried him in the open or in hiding.

"If it's wrong to be sad," Nell said at last, working it out aloud, "and it's wrong to forget, then—" She stopped. Frowned deeper. "Then being sad is just what happens when you remember right. Isn't it. It's not the punishment. It's not the naughtiness. It's just... the weight."

Bram stared at her.

Seven years old. Seven years old, and the child had walked around the entire theological problem and come out the far side carrying the answer like a basket of eggs, unbroken. Somewhere in London there were judges and barristers and newspaper editors who had spent a season failing to arrive at what Nell had just assembled between one sentence and the next.

"That's it," Bram said. "That is exactly it. Grief isn't a punishment for remembering. It's the price of loving someone, paid after. And it's worth paying. Every time, it's worth paying."

"Even when it's heavy?"

"Especially when it's heavy. The heavy ones are the people who mattered most."

Nell absorbed this, drawing her blanket up around her shoulders like a shawl, an old woman in miniature, a magistrate convening court in a child's bed. Outside, the rain eased and then thickened again. Downstairs, faintly, the lantern flame shifted in its glass with a sound like a sigh.

Then she asked the second question, the one Bram had been waiting for since the first, the one Nell approached the way she approached hot baths and unfamiliar dogs — sidelong, watching for teeth.

"Did you forget anything?" she asked. "On purpose? To make yourself less sad?"

And Bram, who had confessed to a courtroom full of strangers that she had steamed open letters over a kettle, who had said *guilty* to interception before a judge while Edith watched from the dock with her chin up, found this small question harder to answer than all of it.

"I tried," she admitted. "A long time ago. Before I knew you. There were things I'd read that frightened me, and I told myself I was only a driver, only a pair of hands on a wheel, that history happened to other people and I just drove them to where it happened." She shook her head slowly. "I tried to forget that I'd seen. It didn't work. Do you know why?"

Nell shook her head.

"Because the people we forget don't stop existing. They just lose us. And I decided—" Bram stopped, started again. "I decided I'd rather be haunted than be the reason someone was alone. Your father isn't less alive in you because it hurts. He's more alive. Every time you remember his face, he exists somewhere in the world again, in you. You're where he lives now."

"Like the lantern," Nell said suddenly.

Bram blinked. "What?"

"In the window. You said it isn't waste if someone's looking for it." Nell's eyes were very wide and very bright in the firelight, the way they got when a thought landed hard. "Daddy's in me like the lantern's in the window. Burning. Even when it's silly. Even when nobody's looking. Especially then."

Bram had to turn fully to the fireplace. She busied her hands with a poker that needed no poker-ing. When she trusted her voice again she said, "Yes. Precisely like that. You are frighteningly clever, Nell Courier, and I hope you know it."

"My name's not Courier."

"It is tonight. It's yours to wear whenever you want it. It's a good name. It means someone who carries things through."

Nell considered this, testing it like a new tooth with her tongue. Then she yawned — hugely, crackingly, the great involuntary surrender of the seven-year-old body — and scowled at her own treacherous mouth, and Bram bit down on a smile.

"I'm not tired," Nell announced.

"Of course not."

"I'm just resting my eyes."

"Naturally."

"Will you stay till I'm asleep? Just till. Then you can go back to your letters."

So Bram banked the fire, and took off her shoes — because a bed was for sitting on properly, and Nell was watching even with her eyes resting — and sat against the headboard while the child curled into the warm dent her body had left, blanket to her chin, one hand fisted in Bram's sleeve as though afraid the whole conversation might be repossessed if she let go.

Bram sang, very quietly. Not well. She had never claimed otherwise. It was an old song about green valleys and false lovers that her grandmother had sung, and Nell had never heard it before November and now demanded it nightly under the fiction of indifference. By the second verse the fist in her sleeve had loosened. By the third her breathing had gone long and even, the sleeping kind of silence, the good kind.

Bram sat a while longer in the dark, listening to rain and breathing, thinking: *this is what the ledger was for.* Photographs and columns of figures, names and dates and payments — all of it, every smuggled page and burned page and sewn seam, all of it for this: a child asleep in a warm bed who knows her father's face and keeps it lit, and who will grow up believing that remembering is not wickedness but work. Custody. The word Bram had been reaching for since the librarian's visit took shape at last. Memory needs custodians. Someone must keep the lantern trimmed and the window clean.

She eased the small fingers off her sleeve, tucked the blanket higher, and stood.

At the door she paused. Nell's voice came sleep-heavy from the pillow, barely a murmur:

"Bram?"

"Yes, love?"

"When Mizz Marlowe comes home — when she walks out — can we both be there? At the gate? Both of us holding the lantern sign?" A drowsy pause. "So the first thing she sees is two faces she knows. So she doesn't have to search the crowd."

Bram stood in the doorway with her hand on the cold knob, and something in her chest went tight and full at once, the way it did at the best and worst moments, which were often the same moments.

"She won't have to search at all," Bram said. "We'll be the easiest thing she's ever seen."

Satisfied, Nell burrowed deeper, and within a minute her breath had gone slow and even as tide. Bram closed the door most of the way, left it a crack — the crack Nell preferred, the proof that doors in this house opened rather than sealed — and returned to her desk.

The letter to Miss Hartley waited, unfinished at the second paragraph. Beside it lay a fresh envelope, blank, and Bram looked at it a long time. An idea had been forming for days, half-grown, shy: a letter addressed not to any living soul but to whoever came after — to the keeper of the archive, to some stranger in a year she could not imagine, the future reader of these boxed and catalogued lives. A letter written by the woman who had finally stopped pretending to be only a driver.

She drew the envelope closer. Took up her pen. Dipped it, and hesitated—

—and from the street below, muffled by rain and glass, came the sound of footsteps stopping at the gate. Deliberate. Unhurried. Familiar in a way that made Bram's heart slam once, hard, against her ribs.

She crossed to the window and pulled back the curtain, and the lantern in the panes threw her shadow tall across the ceiling behind her.

Down at the gate, thin and upright in the lamplight, a figure stood looking up at the house — thinner than the photographs, greyer, unbowed — and raised one hand toward the burning window in salute.
