## 35. The Lantern in the Window

The lantern had been Bram's idea seven years ago, though she had never once admitted it aloud.

It was the winter of 1906, and they had been coming back from a meeting in Hackney through fog so thick that the horse — the old mare, Bess, long since gone to pasture and then to earth — had needed Bram's voice more than her reins. Edith sat beside her with her collar up and her hands folded, silent in the way she was silent when a meeting had gone badly, when some alderman had smiled at her the way men smile at things they intend to outlast. The cab lamps made two small yellow rooms in all that grey. And at the corner of Marlowe Street, outside the house that wasn't yet theirs but would be within the year, there was a light burning in the front window. Just a candle behind glass, set by Mrs. Petch who kept the ground floor and worried about everyone.

"There," Edith had said, leaning forward as though the light were a person come to meet them. "You see? Someone is home."

Bram had looked at the little flame and felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was knotted. She'd said, "We ought to have one of those. A signal. So you always know."

Edith had turned to look at her — Bram remembered it precisely, the fog on the window, the lamplight catching the grey starting at Edith's temples even then — and said, "A lantern in the window means I'm home safe. What does it mean if you hang it?"

And Bram, twenty-four years old and certain that her whole life would be spent watching other people's history from the driver's bench, had said: "That I'm waiting."

They had laughed about it. They had formalized it over supper like girls inventing a code for a schoolyard — one lantern meant *waiting*, unlit meant *gone ahead*, two meant *trouble, don't stop*. It was a joke that became a habit, and the habit became a language, and like everything between them it was never spoken of again directly, because speaking it directly would have required saying what it stood underneath.

Seven years. Two arrests, three house moves, one hunger strike, one broken window, four hundred letters Bram had steamed open at midnight and sealed again with a kettle and steady hands. The lantern had hung in every window of every house. It had burned during the night of the Holloway Road march while Bram sat in the dark car with her hands on the wheel and blood on the pavement fifty yards away. It had burned every single day that Edith was in prison, because Bram could not bear an unlit window, and because somewhere under everything else she believed — superstitiously, shamefully, the way soldiers believe in boots — that if the light went out, Edith would know, across whatever distance separated them, and would lose heart.

Tonight she took it down from the shelf above the kitchen range and cleaned the glass with her sleeve.

* * *

Seven months. She had counted them the way misers count coins, which is to say constantly and without pleasure. One hundred and nine days before Edith's transfer to Aylesbury, then the months after conviction — the lesser charge, the judge's careful sentence, three years reduced by remission to something just under a year — and through all of it the machine of the trial grinding on in the newspapers while Halloran resigned in disgrace and the ledger photographs ran full-page under headlines that used the word *conspiracy* without irony. Bram had read every word. She had cut some of them out. She kept them in the hollow book, the book that had failed her at Crewe and redeemed itself ever after as an archive: the pages that survived, the charred stubs of the three she'd burned, Nell's drawing of the crest done in pencil on butcher's paper.

Nell was setting the table. Seven years old now, or eight — no one had ever established which, and Nell refused to be pinned down on the point with the stubbornness of someone who has learned that adults' records are not always to be trusted. She laid the forks with great ceremony, tines up, exactly aligned, because precision was her way of loving things.

"Is it today?" she asked. Not for the first time. She had asked at breakfast, and at dinner the night before, and eleven times yesterday.

"The governor said this morning," Bram said. "The governor doesn't lie about release dates. Too much paperwork."

"What time?"

"Half past nine. The train gets in at ten past ten."

Nell considered this, aligning another fork. "I'm going to give her my drawing. The new one. Not the crest."

"Good," Bram said, and meant it. There had been a season, eighteen months ago, when Nell drew nothing but the crest — the eagle and the crossed keys, Halloran's office seal — over and over, dozens of versions, until Dr. Lowndes at the Quaker house suggested gently that perhaps she could draw other things too, and Nell had looked at her with the flat, ancient stare only frightened children can manage, and said, "If I stop drawing it, I'll forget why I remember." No one argued with that. Eventually the drawings thinned on their own, replaced by horses and houses and, lately, portraits: Edith at the table, Edith with her chin up, Edith behind bars drawn with such accuracy that Bram had to leave the room the first time she saw it.

"And you'll hang the lantern," Nell said. It was not quite a question. It was a line item on an agenda.

"I'll hang the lantern."

"Where she can see it from the cab."

"From the gate," Bram corrected, gently. "She won't be in the cab tonight. I'm not driving her home. She'll walk."

"She'll want the cab."

"She'll want to walk," Bram said, because she knew. Seven months of prison corridors, of walls and wardresses and the particular humiliation of being watched at one's most private moments — Edith Marlowe would want open air the whole way, would want to see the sky doing its ordinary business, would want her own feet on her own streets. "But she'll see the lantern from the corner. That's the point of it."

Nell nodded, satisfied, and went to fetch the good cloth.

* * *

The lantern itself was nothing. A ship's lantern, brass gone green at the seams, bought secondhand from a chandler off Cable Street in 1908 for sixpence. Its glass had been cracked once — the night of the police raid in 1911, a constable's elbow, an apology never offered — and Bram had replaced the pane rather than let the crack stand, though she kept the old piece wrapped in a drawer because throwing it away felt like discarding testimony. It took paraffin, a wick trimmed to a thumb's width, and a match held steady while the flame found its feet.

At half past eight Bram carried it out to the front room and opened the window sash despite the October cold. The hook was screwed into the frame at exactly the height Edith could see from the corner of Marlowe Street — they had tested this, absurdly, one evening in 1909, Edith standing at the corner while Bram adjusted the hook by inches, calling out *higher, lower, there* like a woman hanging a picture. From that corner, on a clear night, the flame showed as a small gold star in the black rectangle of the window. Nothing more. Everything.

Brim filled, wick lit, glass clean. She hung it and stepped back onto the pavement and looked up at it from where a cab would sit, and it burned, small and absolute, against the dark.

Mrs. Petch's old window was a bakery now, electric-lit, garish, selling pies to men coming off shift. The street had changed around the signal the way streets do — motor lorries where carts had been, new faces at new windows, a poster for a picture palace where the recruiting office used to be — but the corner of Marlowe Street was still dark enough, still quiet enough, that one small flame remained legible. That was the thing about signals, Bram thought. They didn't need the world to hold still. They only needed one person who knew the code.

She stood in the cold longer than she needed to. Her breath made ghosts. Down the street, the gas lamp at the corner hissed its eternal note, and beyond it the city went on with its evening — trams clanging toward their sheds, a dog barking at nothing, the vast indifferent hum of London, which had absorbed the trial, absorbed Halloran's fall, absorbed Edith's imprisonment as one item among thousands, and would absorb this homecoming too unless someone insisted otherwise. Bram had spent years believing history was something that happened to other people, and then years learning it happened to her, and now she stood in a doorway beneath a lit lantern and understood the third thing, the harder thing: that history mostly consisted of people insisting, against the hum, that certain small facts remain visible. A courier's face. A child's memory. A light in a window meaning *I am here, I am waiting, come home.*

"You'll catch your death," Nell said, appearing at her elbow with a coat over her arm. She held it up. It was Edith's coat — the grey one, brushed and mended at the cuff where the cell door had caught it, mended by Nell herself in stitches that marched like soldiers.

"I've been colder," Bram said, taking it anyway.

"When she comes round the corner," Nell said, with the gravity of a general briefing a subordinate, "we're not going to run to her. We're going to stand here. Because running is for people who aren't sure."

Bram looked down at her. Eight years old, maybe nine, and she had learned this somewhere — from whom? From watching, presumably. From seven months of visiting-room glass and letters read aloud by a solicitor and a household holding its breath. *Running is for people who aren't sure.* Bram wanted to laugh and wanted to weep and did neither, because Nell was watching her face for confirmation, and confirmation was what she deserved.

"We're going to stand here," Bram agreed. "And the lantern will say the rest."

"What does the lantern say?"

Bram hesitated. In seven years she had never translated it. It had been easier to leave it in cipher, the way she had left everything — the letters she'd read, the love she hadn't named, the promise to a child in a railway yard renewed nightly in silence.

"It says," Bram said slowly, "that whoever hung it isn't going anywhere."

Nell studied her, then nodded once, satisfied, and went inside to check the table again.

* * *

Ten minutes past ten. The street had emptied of everyone but a milk cart making its last rounds and a cat conducting its own patrol along the opposite wall. Bram stood at the gate. The lantern burned above her. Inside, Nell pressed her nose to the glass, forbidden to come out until the moment, vibrating with the effort of stillness.

Bram heard the train before she saw anything — the far-off rhythm of it, arriving at the station half a mile east, a sound she had listened for every night for seven months and heard only tonight. Then footsteps. Not hurrying. Deliberate. A stride she knew better than her own heartbeat, thinner than it had been, harder, but unmistakable, the stride of a woman who had spent a year refusing to hurry for anyone.

Edith came around the corner of Marlowe Street.

She was thinner. The year had taken flesh and given back something else — a kind of density, a compression, as though prison had pressed her down into a harder substance. Her hair was shorter, cropped close in the way of women the authorities had decided not to negotiate with, and it suited her brutally well. Her coat was her own, the grey one, and her chin was up, and her eyes went past Bram entirely — past the gate, past the child's pale face at the window — and fixed on the lantern.

Bram watched her stop.

It lasted perhaps four seconds. Edith Marlowe, who had faced magistrates and mobs and the Interior Minister's fixer without blinking, stood on a dark street corner and stared at a sixpenny ship's lantern in a window, and did not move, and did not speak, and her eyes were bright in a way that had nothing to do with the flame.

Then she walked the rest of the way, and neither of them ran, because running was for people who weren't sure.

She stopped at the gate. Bram stood inside it. Between them: twelve inches of iron railing, seven months, four hundred letters, one confession made publicly at trial, one whispered *finish it*, one promise made to a sleeping child between wagons. All of it present. None of it spoken.

"You lit it," Edith said. Her voice was roughened, lower than before, a voice re-cut by disuse and defiance.

"Every night."

"I know. They get the papers in Holloway. There was a photograph — the trial, outside the court — and the window was in it. Behind you." Edith's hand rose and closed around the railing bar, knuckles whitening. "I looked at that photograph until I'd worn it soft. One hundred and ninety-three nights, Vane. You never missed one."

"One hundred and ninety-three," Bram said. "You counted too."

"I counted everything." Edith looked at her then, fully, and the brightness spilled — not tears, not quite, but the pressure of them, held at the dam the way Edith held everything. "The letters. The archive. Testifying about the interception in open court — you knew what it would cost you and you did it anyway. I read the transcript four times."

"You sent me a letter saying finish it. This is finished."

"This part is finished." Something moved behind Edith's eyes — not weariness, or not only weariness. Bram had seen that look before, across a supper table, after a bad meeting: the look of a woman already composing the next speech. "There were women inside who have no one. Their names aren't in any ledger. Their sentences don't end because a minister fell." She stopped. Her grip tightened on the rail. "But that's tomorrow's argument."

"It can wait until after supper," Bram said. "Nell has arranged the forks."

Something broke open in Edith's face then — the smile, the real one, the rare one, the one Bram had spent seven years earning in increments. "Forks."

"Tines up. Aligned. She's been rehearsing since Tuesday."

Edith pushed the gate open. It swung with its familiar complaining creak, the hinge Bram had oiled twice a week for seven months so that it wouldn't creak tonight, and it creaked anyway, and Edith laughed — a short, cracked, unpracticed sound, the laugh of someone using a muscle that atrophied — and then Nell was through the front door and down the path, protocol abandoned, running, because she was eight years old and some rules are made to be broken by the person who made them, and Edith dropped to her knees on her own doorstep and caught her.

Bram stood back. Above them the lantern burned. Below them the city hummed its indifferent hum. And Edith, kneeling, her face buried in the child's shoulder, lifted one hand without looking — found Bram's wrist, gripped it hard, once — and let go, and that was the whole conversation, and it was sufficient.

Inside, the table was set. The fire was lit. On the shelf above the range, next to the hollow book, lay the morning's post: three letters, ordinary-looking, forwarded from the solicitor. Bram had not opened them. She would not steam these; that woman was seven years gone. But one of them bore the crest of the National Union, and one bore a Manchester postmark, and the third was addressed in a hand she recognized from a hundred intercepted envelopes — the journalist's hand, the man whose office had received a doll containing a government's ruin.

Edith hung her coat on the hook. She looked at the letters, then at Bram, and her eyebrows rose in a question that was also an answer.

"After supper," Bram said.

"After supper," Edith agreed — and reached up, and touched the lantern's warm glass once, lightly, like a salute, before following Nell into the light.
