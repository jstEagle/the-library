## 37. The Movement Moves On

The hall on Whitworth Street had been a chapel once, then a warehouse, and now it was ours for two hours a week at a rent that Miss Pankhurst's treasurer called extortionate and I called cheap. It smelled of lamp oil and damp plaster and, faintly, of the crowd itself — four hundred bodies in a room built for three hundred, their breath fogging in the October cold because the caretaker claimed the boiler could not be trusted and I suspected he simply would not light it for women who wanted the vote.

I stood at the back with my coat still on and listened to a girl from the spinning mills — nineteen, perhaps twenty, a Lancashire voice like flint dragged over slate — explain to the room why she had walked eleven miles to be there.

"They printed it in the *Guardian*," she said, holding up the paper as though it might fly from her hand. "Printed it. Names and dates and a government ledger, right where my dad could read it over his supper. He used to say we were hysterics. He read it twice and then he went quiet, and this morning he says to me: *Tell them I'll pay your fine if they arrest you.*"

Laughter, but not easy laughter — the kind that comes when something long clenched has begun, very slightly, to unclench.

I had heard a hundred versions of that story since August. In Manchester and Salford, in Stockport and Oldham, men and women who had dismissed us as shrieking spinsters or worse were reading the Halloran ledger the way they read scripture: aloud, in kitchens, arguing over it. A minister of the Crown had planned to blow up his own narrative with a bomb meant for us. He had killed a courier named Aldous Wren to keep it quiet. And he had very nearly succeeded, because for years the country had been willing to believe anything of a woman who shouted.

That was what had changed. Not the law — the law was the same stone it had always been. Not Halloran's party, which had closed around him like a fist. But the ears. The ears of England had opened half an inch, and through that half inch a great deal could be shouted.

I knew precisely how much could be got through half an inch. I had spent seven months learning the dimensions of a cell door.

* * *

After the meeting I did not go straight home. There was no home in the old sense; there was Bram's flat above the garage on Nile Street, and before that there had been Holloway, and before that a house in Camden I had given up rather than let the bailiffs make a theatre of it. My life now fit in three suitcases and a tin box of papers, and I had never felt less burdened.

Bram was waiting by the car with her collar turned up against the drizzle, and Nell was asleep in the back seat under a travelling rug, her head tipped against the window, one hand curled around the rag doll that had carried a government's crimes through a police cordon while grown men searched everything else.

"Well?" Bram asked.

"Four hundred," I said. "And thirty subscriptions signed on the spot."

"And the mill girl?"

"The mill girl is going to be trouble." I said it with pleasure. "In two years she'll have her own branch. In five she'll be impossible. You'll see."

Bram opened the door for me, and as I settled into the seat I caught the smell of the car — oil and rain and the ghost of cigarettes Bram pretended she didn't smoke — and thought, not for the first time, how many miles of fear this machine had carried and how ordinary it seemed now, parked outside a wet chapel, waiting like a good dog.

We drove south out of the city with the lamps lit and the streets running silver. Nell slept on. Bram drove the way she always drove, both hands loose on the wheel, eyes moving constantly — mirror, road, mirror — a habit I finally understood had less to do with traffic than with checkpoints.

"Bram," I said, after a while. "The Wigan branch wrote today. They want me to speak at their rally on the fourteenth. Open air, market square. They expect five thousand."

"That's a large number."

"It is."

"And Crake?"

"Gone to ground. Resigned quietly — no scandal attached, nothing proved, just an inspector who discovered his health required him to retire to the coast. Halloran survives too. Shorn, but alive, and a shorn animal is more dangerous than a dead one." I watched the lamplight slide across her cheek. "Wigan will have its plainclothesmen. There will be someone at the edge of that crowd with a notebook, writing down every name he can catch."

"And you'll go anyway."

"I'll go anyway."

She was silent for half a mile. Then: "I'll drive you."

"You always do."

"Not always," she said, so quietly I nearly missed it under the engine. "There was a night on Holloway Road when I sat in the car with my hands on the wheel and watched the police wade into the march, and I told myself I was only the driver. Only ever the driver." Her jaw worked. "I've been meaning to say that I'm sorry for the year I wasted telling myself that story."

"Bram—"

"No. Let me finish, because I've rehearsed it at red lights for weeks like a coward." She took a corner more sharply than the corner required. "History wasn't happening to other people. It was happening in the passenger seat, and in the letters I steamed open, and in the packet a child brought to your door, and I was in all of it whether I consented to be or not. So — no, not always the driver. Sometimes the courier. And the courier goes where the letter goes. That's the whole of it."

I reached across and put my hand briefly over hers on the wheel. Neither of us said anything else, because some things survive being said only if you don't elaborate, and I had learned in seven months of prison conversation — forty minutes a week, a wardress standing at my shoulder listening to every word — exactly how much can be carried in silence between two people who have agreed on the important things.

Nell stirred under the rug and murmured something about lanterns, and Bram's whole body softened at the sound, the way it always did, the way mine was learning to.

* * *

The tin box came out at the kitchen table that night, after Nell was abed in the little room off the kitchen that she called, with the absolute territorial certainty of children, *my house*.

I spread the correspondence across the oilcloth and began again the work that had filled every evening since my release: the rebuilding. Seven months in prison does not merely cost you time. It costs you threads. The woman who ran our Birmingham finances had drifted off during the trial, frightened or weary or both. Our Liverpool printer had been arrested twice since July on charges that would not survive daylight but would certainly survive long enough to spoil a print run. And somewhere in Whitehall, in offices with new names over the doors, men who had dined with Halloran were deciding how much of the old machinery could be salvaged and pointed at us again.

Miss Trentham's letter was the worst of the evening's crop. She had organized our Kent branches for six years, and her letter was typed, formal, and devastating in the way of all resignations written by tired people: *the cause has cost me my sister's regard and my sister will not return it while my name appears in the newspapers beside yours. I am fifty-one years old, Edith. I find I want my sister back.*

I read it twice. Then I set it down and did not burn it, because I had made myself a promise in my cell that I would stop destroying things that hurt to look at. Memory needs custodians. That was Bram's phrase, borrowed from the librarian, and I had adopted it as discipline.

Instead I wrote a reply. Three drafts — I kept all three, even the intemperate first — before I found the true one:

*Dear Margaret — Your sister is entitled to you; the cause is not. Come back when you can, or don't come back at all, and know that neither choice makes you less than you were. We will hold your branch open the way one holds a door. Yours, E.M.*

It was not strategy. It was simply true, and I had learned — slowly, expensively, in a cell where I had nothing but time to audit my own accounts — that the movement I wanted belonged to could not be built entirely on sacrifice. Sacrifice was the coin we had been forced to spend. It should never be mistaken for the treasury.

* * *

Friday I went to Liverpool, and it was there, in a print shop behind a shuttered tobacconist, that I understood what the frame-up's collapse had actually purchased.

The printer was a Welshman named Cadwallader, missing two fingers of his left hand, and he showed me the forme locked up for Saturday's broadsheet: the Wigan speech announcement on one side, and on the other, reprinted from the London weeklies, the extract from the Halloran ledger with Aldous Wren's name set in bold.

"They read it in the pubs," Cadwallader said, wiping ink from his stumps with a rag gone grey with use. "Read it aloud, like. Men who wouldn't say a civil word to a suffragist a year ago are quoting your dead courier now. *Forget the face,*" — he tapped the note reproduced below the photograph, the note that had come tied to a child — "and then they look at each other and nobody laughs. You've done something to them, Mrs. Marlowe. I don't fully credit it myself, and I'm printing the thing."

"What we've done," I said, "is fail beautifully enough to be believed."

He laughed, then saw I hadn't been entirely joking, and grew serious. "Aye. There's that in it. Dead martyrs convince the living; live ones only inconvenience them."

On the train back I sat opposite a commercial traveller who stared at me for three stations, plainly working out whether I was who he suspected, and finally leaned across and asked, not unkindly, whether it was true that the government had meant to blame us for the bombing.

"It is true," I said.

"My wife said so." He nodded, satisfied, and returned to his paper, and I sat looking out at the dark fields rushing past and felt the strangest sensation — the sensation of being believed in advance. For twelve years I had addressed rooms full of people who had already decided I was lying about my own life. Now strangers on trains took my word as a starting point. Half an inch of open ear. Through it, everything.

But — and here is the thing I want set down plainly, because the newspapers will not set it down and the pamphlets will round the corners off it — half an inch is not victory. Halloran resigned nothing; he was merely shelved, and shelved ministers have a way of returning with the next change of weather. The Cat and Mouse Act still stood. The force-feeding tubes were still manufactured, ordered, and used, in a prison system that had learned nothing from the scandal except better discretion. And somewhere a younger, cleverer man than Halloran was reading the same post-mortem reports we were, drawing the opposite conclusion: not *we were caught* but *next time, kill no couriers.*

The fight was far from won. Some nights the arithmetic of it sat on my chest like a cat: twelve more years, at least, of this pace. Twelve more years of halls and rallies and arrests and Bram driving me home afterwards with her hands loose on the wheel and her eyes on the mirrors. I would be sixty. I might spend my sixtieth year in a cell. I had made peace with that arithmetic in Holloway, and I made peace with it again on the Liverpool train, and I expect I shall have to make peace with it periodically for the rest of my life, the way one re-makes a bed that keeps coming undone.

Weary hope. That was the whole doctrine, if I had one. Not the bright hope of 1906, when we believed a Liberal government might simply be reasoned with. Hope with calluses. Hope that had learned to cook on one ring and sleep sitting up.

* * *

Sunday evening, Nell sat on the kitchen floor doing sums with a stub of pencil, tongue between her teeth, and Bram darned socks, and I read the Wigan committee's latest letter aloud because they had enclosed a question they wanted answered publicly and I liked to test my answers on the strictest audience available.

"They ask," I read, "*whether the vote will mend everything.*"

Nell looked up from her sums. "Will it?"

"No," I said.

She considered this with the gravity she gave all questions, which was more gravity than most adults gave any. "Then why do it?"

Bram's needle paused. I set the letter down. This was the question, wasn't it? The one underneath every subscription slip and every arrest warrant. The child asked it because children ask the load-bearing questions while the rest of us decorate the walls.

"Because the vote won't mend everything," I said, "but without it, mending is somebody else's decision. Right now, if the government decides to say a bomb was ours — yours, mine, your father's — there is nothing we can do inside the law to say otherwise. No newspaper of our own big enough. No court that must hear us. Nothing but running, and hiding, and hoping a journalist believes us." I held up the doll, propped against the coal scuttle. "You remember what rode in that. What if there had been no journalist? What if Mr. Fenn had said no?"

"He didn't say no," Nell said.

"Because the ledger was undeniable. But most wrongs are deniable, Nell. Most of them stay deniable forever, because the people they're done to have no way to make anybody listen. The vote is not the mending. It's the hearing. Everything else waits on the hearing."

She frowned at her sums, working it through. "Like when I couldn't talk," she said at last, "and nobody knew what happened to Papa. Even though I knewed it the whole time."

My throat closed. Beside me, Bram had gone very still.

"Yes," I managed. "Exactly like that."

Nell nodded, satisfied, and bent back to her column of figures, having dismantled in one sentence the argument that had taken Parliament forty years to avoid. Bram reached over and squeezed my wrist once, hard, and resumed her darning, and the kettle sang, and the lamp burned, and outside the rain came down on Nile Street with the patient indifference of English weather toward human arrangements.

Later, when Nell was asleep, Bram said, "Wigan. Fourteenth. Five thousand people, open air, plainclothesmen with notebooks." She said it while packing the tin box, not looking up. "If you're going, I want to know tonight. I'll need to arrange the route and the fallback car and tell Fenn's office where to wire."

"I'm going."

"I assumed." She closed the box. "There's a letter on your desk. Came special delivery from London this afternoon. I didn't steam it open," she added, and there was the old joke in it, worn smooth now, almost tender. "Even though it's addressed to you and I'm technically allowed."

"Technically allowed," I echoed, and picked my way upstairs to the desk.

The envelope was thick, official cream, stamped with a crest I recognized and had hoped never to handle again: the Home Office. Inside, a single page, typeset, signed by a deputy secretary whose name meant nothing and whose signature meant everything.

*Dear Mrs. Marlowe — In view of recent public discussion, the Department has resolved to appoint an independent inquiry into the circumstances surrounding the events of last spring. The Department would welcome your cooperation as a witness at its convenience. An early indication of your willingness would assist the Secretariat in framing its schedule.*

I stood holding it under the gaslight for a long moment, feeling the trap in it the way one feels weather in an old wound — because an inquiry was what we had demanded for months, and an inquiry was also what Halloran's friends wanted, an inquiry being the instrument by which governments bury things at public expense. Terms of reference. Chosen chairman. Private sessions. Evidence heard and filed and never printed.

Or — if the terms were forced wide, if the press stayed hungry, if witnesses like a mill girl from Whitworth Street and a widow in Camden refused to be managed — it was the biggest room we had ever been given, with the whole country listening through that half-inch of open ear.

They were inviting me to walk into a room full of the men who had built the frame-up and answer their questions on their ground. Or to refuse, and let them write the history of the refusal.

I carried the letter downstairs. Bram looked up from the stove.

"Well?"

"It's an invitation," I said, setting it on the table between us, face up, crest showing. "To testify. To the government's own inquiry into the government's own conspiracy." I smiled, and felt the weariness and the stubbornness arrive together, old companions, taking their seats. "They've built us an arena, Bram. The question is whether we bring our own lion."

Bram read the letter twice. Then she looked up, and I saw in her face the same expression she'd worn at the checkpoint outside Deansgate — terror and resolve welded into a single seam — and she said:

"When do we leave for London?"
