## 40. Lantern and Dawn

I had meant to blow out the lantern at first light.

That was the arrangement, after all — the signal Edith and I had agreed on years ago, back when a lantern in the window of the Holloway Road house meant *I am here, I am watching, all is well*. A signal is not meant to burn forever. A signal is meant to be seen and then extinguished, its work done, its flame folded away until the next dark hour requires it. I stood in the kitchen doorway with my hand still warm from the match and watched the light through the glass, and I could not make myself cross the room to end it.

Outside, the street was turning from grey to gold. October again — eleven months since Nell had appeared on the step with her oilcloth packet clutched like a heart she'd been told to carry, seven months and three days since they took Edith away in front of the cameras, six weeks since the verdict came down and Halloran's resignation letter went out under the morning editions like a bird released too late to matter much but early enough to matter some. Six weeks since I had hung this lantern and begun to keep it lit.

Nell was at the table.

She had let herself down the stairs the way she always did now — one careful step at a time, hand on the rail, bare feet making no sound at all, a habit from the yards that I hoped would leave her eventually and secretly hoped would never leave her entirely, because it was hers, it was proof of what she had survived and how. She was setting the table for four. She did this every morning now without being asked. Three plates, three cups, and then — after a moment's consideration, every single morning, the same small deliberation as if the arithmetic might change overnight — a fourth.

"Miss Marlowe takes tea strong," she informed me, in the grave voice of someone reporting intelligence gathered under difficult conditions. "But not burnt. Mrs. Pym makes it burnt."

"Mrs. Pym makes it strong," I said. "There's a difference."

"She scolds the leaves." Nell laid the fourth cup down with great precision, handle turned to the left, because Edith was left-handed and the child had noticed this before any of us had thought to mention it. "You don't scold anything."

"I scold plenty."

"Not the tea." She looked up at me then, and the morning light caught her face, and I did the thing I do now every day, the thing I once did only when I was afraid: I looked at her face on purpose. The face she'd been ordered to forget. The face a man in a good coat had tried to erase from the world with a note and an abandoned child, and which had instead become the most remembered face in England for one furious fortnight last spring, printed in half the papers in the country beside the words *WITNESS, AGED SEVEN*.

It was a good face. It was going to be a stubborn face. God help whoever earned that stubbornness.

"Sit down, Bram," she said. "You're doing the standing thing."

"The standing thing."

"Where you stand in doorways and look at things like you're deciding whether to run." She pulled out a chair for herself with both hands. "Miss Marlowe says you've always done it. Since before."

"Since before," I agreed, and sat.

Because Edith had come home.

* * *

She had come home the way rivers return to valleys — inevitably, and having cut new channels on the way. Seven months inside had thinned her; the grey at her temples had spread like frost across a windowpane; there was a new hesitancy in her right hand, the one the warders had gripped too hard during the forcible feeding attempts before the lesser charge made such theatrics unnecessary. But when she walked out through those gates into the crowd and the camera smoke, thinner and unbowed, she had found my face in it within four seconds. I counted. Four seconds in a crowd of two hundred shouting people, and her eyes went past the journalists and the banner-carriers and the women weeping openly into their muffs, and found mine, and stopped.

She hadn't said anything. Neither had I. We had walked to the car together through the noise, and Nell had broken from Mrs. Pym's grip and run the last ten yards and hit Edith at the waist like a small battering ram of grief and joy, and Edith had gone down on her knees right there on the pavement in front of every lens in Manchester, and held her, and that photograph ran larger than the one of Halloran's resignation, which was the only victory I have ever cared about in the whole sordid business of pictures.

Now she was on the step. I could see her through the frosted pane of the front door — her outline, hands clasped behind her back in the old parade-rest of the podium, head tilted up toward the sky the way she did when she was taking the measure of a morning. She had been out there twenty minutes. She liked the cold. She claimed it kept her honest.

I poured the tea. Four cups. Strong, not burnt, unscolded.

When I opened the door, the smell of the street came in — coal smoke and wet stone and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, the green smell of the river, carried up on a wind that had traveled all night to reach this exact doorstep. Edith turned.

"You've let the lantern burn past dawn," she said.

"I know."

"It's been burning since six weeks ago Tuesday."

"I know that too."

She looked at me over the rim of the morning, and her eyes were the same eyes that had found mine in the crowd, and there was something in them I had spent years pretending not to see and months learning not to flinch from: the whole of her attention, given freely, without agenda or audience, to me.

"A signal's meant to go out," she said gently. "Not stay lit."

"This one stays lit," I said. "New arrangement. I'm amending the terms."

"Are you." Not quite a smile. The almost-smile she used when she wanted me to argue properly.

"The old terms were for watching for trouble," I said. "The new ones are for remembering it can come back. There's a difference. Mrs. Pym taught me that about tea."

That got the real smile — brief, crooked, tired around its edges, entirely hers. She came inside. She took the cup Nell had set for her, handle to the left, and she looked down at the table, at the fourth place laid with such ceremony, and something crossed her face that I recognized because I had worn it myself for eleven months: the expression of a person who has learned exactly how much a small daily ritual can hold.

"Nell," she said, "you've counted us again."

"Someone has to," said Nell. "In case."

"In case," Edith agreed gravely, and sat down, and the table was full.

* * *

We ate the way people eat who have learned that ordinary breakfasts are not guaranteed. Mrs. Pym brought toast and opinion in equal quantities — she had returned to us in the spring, declaring that she refused to let the government have the satisfaction of an unsupervised household, and she had not stopped supervising since. The wireless was off. The papers lay untouched on the sideboard where they'd been delivered; I had read them already at five, alone in the kitchen, an old courier's habit I couldn't shake — *Marlowe Freed; Minister's Fall Complete; Inquiry Widens* — and I had decided the others could meet the news at their own pace, over toast, rather than mine.

Edith read the headlines anyway, because she was Edith. She skimmed them the way she skimmed police charge sheets, with a kind of professional contempt, and set the paper aside.

"They'll want me in Manchester by Thursday," she said. "The union hall. There's a meeting — the Lancashire women have been carrying this alone all summer and they deserve to hear it from someone who's been in a cell for it."

"Thursday," I said.

"Thursday. It's nothing. A platform, a speech, the usual." She buttered her toast with the deliberate calm of a woman announcing a route through checkpoints. "Though Crake's name came up in yesterday's testimony again. He's still loose. Disgraced men are more dangerous than employed ones; they've nothing left to lose and a great deal to prove."

"He won't come near you," Nell said suddenly. She said it with her chin up and her spoon gripped like a weapon, and both Edith and I went quiet, because we knew — we had both learned it from her — that children's certainties are not naivety. They are decisions. She had decided Crake was a coward, on evidence no adult had thought to collect: he had sent other men to do his looking, always, even at the end, even at Deansgate, where he had waited by the barrier himself and still made a sergeant reach for the doll before he would touch it. "He sends people. That's what he does. He doesn't come."

"That's very possibly true," Edith said. "And if it is true, it will remain true partly because we refuse to be afraid of him in our own kitchen. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Nell, and went back to her egg with the satisfaction of a treaty signed.

I looked at the two of them — the famous woman and the witness, the martyr who wouldn't die and the orphan who wouldn't forget — and then at Mrs. Pym, arms folded in the doorway, radiating vigilance over the toast rack, and I felt the strange weightless grief-and-gratitude of a person whose family assembled itself out of catastrophe. None of us had chosen each other. All of us had chosen each other. Both things were true, and the truth lived at this table, handle turned to the left.

"There's one more thing," Edith said, quieter now. She reached into the pocket of her cardigan — prison-issued wool, she refused to replace it, another small war she intended to win — and drew out an envelope. It was creased, softened by handling, addressed in a hand I knew better than my own. Addressed to me.

"You wrote this in March," she said. "Before the trial. Before everything. They let you have paper in the visitors' room and you wrote it and gave it to the matron and the matron gave it to me and I—" she stopped, and started again, which she almost never did. "I read it forty times. Perhaps sixty. And I never answered it, because answering it inside those walls would have made it contraband, and some things should never be contraband. So I'm answering it now, out loud, at your own table, over eggs."

My hands had gone still around my cup. Eleven months ago I had been a woman who steamed open other people's letters and called it observation. Then I had been a woman who confessed it from a witness box while the gallery gasped. Somewhere between those two women there had been one long sleepless night in March when I had written, finally, the letter I had been drafting in my head for years — the one about the lantern, and the looking, and how history had never once happened to other people, and how I loved her, plainly, in ink, where it could be seized as evidence.

"You don't have to say it back," I managed. "You answered it every day since—"

"Bram." Gently. With the voice she used on crowds of ten thousand, scaled down to a kitchen. "Let me finish. I've had seven months to compose and I'll not be interrupted by modesty."

She put her hand flat on the table. The hesitant one. She let me see the hesitation and then she steadied it.

"I love you," she said. "I loved you before Holloway Road, when you sat in that car with your hands on the wheel and would not come down, and I understood that you were guarding something in yourself and I resolved not to dig for it. I loved you through the packet, and the checkpoints, and Crewe, and the yards at three in the morning when I found you asleep sitting up with a child in your coat. And I love you now, out here, where saying it costs nothing but is worth everything. That is my answer. It has been my answer since the first time you lied to me about the letters and were so transparently terrible at it."

Nell looked up. "You read her letters?"

"I confess it," Edith said solemnly, "and I forgave it, because she read them the way saints pray — compulsively, guiltily, and to no good purpose whatsoever."

"I had excellent purposes," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and nobody minded.

* * *

Afterward, when the plates were cleared and Mrs. Pym had gone down to argue with the milk boy, Edith and I stood together at the front window. Nell was in the yard teaching the neighbor's cat to sit, with limited success and unlimited patience. The street was fully awake now: carts, bicycles, a newsboy working the corner with the resigned rhythm of a boy selling the aftermath of miracles.

"Thursday, then," Edith said. "Manchester. Will you drive me?"

"Every mile."

"And the lantern?"

I turned and looked at it — still burning on its hook by the window, absurd against the full daylight, a small yellow defiance that anyone passing could see and no one passing could interpret.

"It stays," I said. "That's the amendment. Trouble gets one look at this house and knows we saw it coming the first time and we'll see it coming again. Vigilance isn't a signal you send and take down, Edith. Not anymore. Not for any of us." I nodded toward the yard, toward the small figure kneeling in the grass with her hand out to a cat that would never, ever sit. "She taught me that. Forgetting is the only unforgivable thing. So we remember. Out loud. In the window. Every day."

Edith was quiet a moment. Then she reached over and turned the lantern's key a fraction brighter, though the flame scarcely needed it, though the sun was fully up, though every practical instinct either of us owned said *put it out, the night is over.*

"Amended and ratified," she said.

And that was the morning — eggs and testimony, a treaty with a cat, a signal burning in broad daylight because we decided it should. Peace, I thought, watching Nell laugh as the cat stalked off in dignity, peace is not the absence of the watch. It is the watch, kept willingly, in company.

Then the knock came at the door. Three raps, unhurried, unfamiliar — and through the frosted pane, a silhouette I did not know, holding something flat and wrapped in brown paper, waiting to be let in.
