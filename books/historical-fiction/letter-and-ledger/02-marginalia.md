## 2. Marginalia

Three days after I inked the last of the coastline, I went back to the atlas the way one goes back to a grave — knowing nothing will have changed, needing to see it anyway.

The workshop was cold enough that my breath showed when I spoke to myself, which I had begun doing more often than I cared to admit. Master Vane's chair still held the shape of him, or I imagined it did: the slight lean to the left where his bad hip favored the window, the cushion worn pale at the crown where his head rested while he thought. I had not moved it since the ferryman carried him up from the water wrapped in sacking. Some part of me believed that if I disturbed the chair, the rest would follow — the smell of walnut oil and cut vellum, the little tower of blotters on the second shelf, the whole architecture of a life I had been permitted to stand inside for eleven years — and so I left everything as it was and worked around the absence like water working around a stone.

That morning I worked at the margins.

An atlas is not finished when its coastlines are drawn. That is the lie apprentices believe and masters let them believe until they are ready for better. The truth is that an atlas is finished when its margins are full — the soundings along every estuary, the tide-tables penciled in the corners, the compass roses with their sixteen points and their careful flourishes, the small dense annotations that turn a picture into a document. A coastline tells you where the land ends. The margin tells you what the water does there, and why anyone should care.

Master Vane's margins were famous, at least among the handful of people who knew his work. He wrote them like a man talking to himself across years — observations begun in one season and completed in another, corrections of his own earlier corrections, notes in two hands because his writing had changed after the fever of '39. I had grown up reading those margins the way other children read psalms. They were the closest thing I had to his voice now, and so on that cold morning, with the brazier down to embers, I began at the first sheet and read every word he had ever written sideways onto the world.

I told myself it was work. It was grief wearing work's coat, but grief is allowed its disguises.

* * *

The first sheets were innocent. Of course they were. The northern coast, drawn when he was younger than I am now, all confidence and clean line, the margins crowded with soundings in fathoms and half-fathoms, each number followed by the character for *sand*, *mud*, or *rock*. I checked a dozen against the guild survey of 1731 and found them agreeing within inches. This was the master I knew: a man who would walk four miles at low tide to settle whether a shoal lay at seven feet or seven and a quarter, who once made me remeasure the width of the mill race three times because my figures were honest but my penmanship was not, and he could not abide a number that might be misread even if it could not be wrong.

*A map is a promise,* he used to say, tapping the table with two fingers, tap-tap, like a heartbeat heard through wood. *Anyone may draw a line. The promise is that the line means what it says. Break that, and you are not a mapmaker anymore. You are only a decorator of lies.*

By midday I had reached sheet nine. The river country. Marrowfleet's reach.

I want to be exact here, because exactness was his religion and I inherited it whether I wanted to or not. Sheet nine covers the lower river from the Vellamouth bend to the marshes beyond Marrowfleet, and its coastline was the last he drew before the drowning — no. Before his death. I will not soften it further than the river already has; the drowning implies accident, and accident implies water minding its own business, and I no longer know that the water was minding its own business, though I did not know it yet that morning either. On that morning I believed the river had taken an old man who walked too near its edge in the dark, as rivers do, indifferently, forever.

Sheet nine's coastline was finished. Its margins were not.

Here is what I found, and I set it down in order, because order was the only comfort available.

In the lower right quadrant, beside the channel markers, the soundings stopped matching. Not by much — never by much, which is precisely why they unsettled me. Where the guild survey recorded nine feet over the Marrowfleet bar, Vane's margin read *7½ — new*. Where the survey gave a channel depth of eleven feet below the weir pool, his note said *8 — and less after rain*. Small discrepancies. A foot, a foot and a half. But a mapmaker does not write *new* beside a sounding unless something has changed the bottom, and a change of that kind — a river growing shallower over its own bed, silting up against the natural run of things — wants a cause. Silt comes from upstream works. From dams. From sluices closed and channels turned.

I sat back and looked at the note for a long time.

There were others. Along the stretch above Marrowfleet, where the survey showed the old flood-channels open and marked as relief cuts — the cuts dug, at great public expense, to carry off spring water so that the low villages would not drown — Vane had written, in his smaller later hand: *silted? or shut? must walk this in March.* And beneath it, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper: *walked it October. Gates shut. Who shuts relief gates in autumn?*

My skin prickled. I remember that distinctly — the prickling along my forearms, the way the workshop seemed to grow quieter, the fire ticking down to nothing behind me. But I also remember what came next, which was nothing. No revelation. Just a question in a dead man's hand, and the wind moving the ivy against the window glass, and my own breath hanging in front of me like a small ghost.

Questions in margins are not evidence. Questions in margins are old men worrying, and Aldous Vane worried the way some men drink — constantly, privately, about everything.

I turned the page.

* * *

It was near dusk when I found the ciphered note. I know the hour because the light had gone the color of pewter and I had lit two candles without noticing I'd done it, which is how absorbed work announces itself.

The note was on sheet twelve, in the margin beside the marsh edge below Marrowfleet, in a place where a sane cartographer would put nothing at all — blank marsh, unmarked ground, no soundings, no features worth annotation. And the note was not words. It was figures.

A string of them, maybe forty characters long, arranged in pairs: numerals mixed with letters, some repeated, laid out with the even spacing of a man who had done this many times before. Beneath it, a single word in plain hand, underlined twice:

*Remember.*

I recognized the cipher. Every apprentice of Vane's learned it in the first month — his own variation on the book cipher, keyed to the guild almanac, taught to us as a game. *Maps carry secrets, Silas,* he said, the winter I was thirteen, my fingers blue with cold in the unheated shop. *Not ours. Other men's. A surveyor sees everything and must forget most of it. So we keep a private alphabet, and if ever a thing must be written that must not be read, we write it so.* He had laughed then and rapped my knuckles with the ruler, gently, for looking too solemn. *You'll likely never use it. Old men's habits. Like keeping your boots by the door in case of flood.*

I fetched the guild almanac for 1746 from the shelf — his copy, its spine broken at the tide tables — and I worked the cipher by candlelight, and my hands were steady, and then they were not.

The message was short. Seven words.

*The water was bought. Ask who paid.*

I read it perhaps thirty times. I copied it out on scrap paper and burned the scrap, then wished I hadn't, then copied it again. I checked my keying twice, letter by letter, because I wanted — God help me, I wanted desperately — to have made an error. To find that the seventh character was misaligned, that the true text said something banal, *the water was high*, *the water was cold*, anything that belonged to the world of rivers instead of the world of men.

But the text said what it said. *Bought.* *Paid.* Words with owners in them. Words that require a buyer and a seller and a price, and none of those things belong in a margin about marshland.

I did what any sensible person would do. I talked myself out of it.

* * *

Consider, I told the empty shop, standing at the window with my arms crossed against the cold, how old he was. Sixty-eight. Consider what the fever took from him in '39 — not just the steadiness of his left hand, but whole weeks of memory, seasons gone missing, and afterward the way he sometimes called me by his dead daughter's name and sometimes called me nothing at all. Consider that he had spent the last year increasingly in the grip of a suspicion he never named: checking the lock twice, asking me whether strangers had come to the shop, watching the road from the window with an expression I had taken for absent-mindedness and now, in the failing light, could not take for anything at all.

Old men's paranoia. That was the phrase I used, standing there. I dressed the ciphered note in it like a coat, buttoned it up, and told myself the fit was good.

Because look at what the alternative required. It required that the magistrates of Vellamouth — respectable men, churchwardens, the very bench whose names appeared in gilt letters on the guild hall wall — had sold the flood-relief works meant to protect a village of their own county. It required that the sluice gates had been deliberately shut. It required that Marrowfleet — and here my mind flinched away from the arithmetic, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth — Marrowfleet had not drowned by act of God in the February floods, but by act of man, forty-one souls spent like coin. And it required that Aldous Vane had known, had written it down in secret, and had then walked into the river six weeks later, alone, at night, three days after being seen — though I did not know this yet — arguing with one of the very men the note accused.

Denial is not stupidity. Denial is a load-bearing structure. I had built my entire year on it: that the death was accident, that the atlas was only an atlas, that grief was the worst thing the winter would ask of me. Pull out one timber and the roof comes down on your head. So I did not pull. I stood in the cold shop and I rebuilt the timber with both hands, faster than doubt could remove it.

He was old. He was frightened. Frightened men see purchasers in every shadow. Perhaps the note referred to something smaller — a bribe over a fishing rights dispute, a payment to close a private cut, the ordinary venality of river politics, which I knew existed the way I knew weather existed, as background, as nothing that concerned me. Perhaps *bought* was his bitterness speaking, the old man's conviction that everything decent was for sale, a sermon in cipher, aimed at no one.

And the measurements? The shallow soundings, the *gates shut*?

Silt, I told myself. Rivers silt. Works decay. The relief cuts were twenty years old and the county had always been slow with maintenance money — that much was common knowledge and cost nothing to believe. An old surveyor walks a decaying sluice, finds the gates jammed shut with rust and neglect, and writes *shut* because that is what his eyes report. Neglect, not conspiracy. Rot, not murder. The difference between those two words is the difference between a sad world and a monstrous one, and every instinct I had that evening chose the sad world, because the sad world lets you go on living in it.

I banked the fire. I cleaned my pens. I put sheet twelve back in its place in the stack, face down, the way one turns a photograph toward the wall.

Then I stood in the middle of the workshop and admitted, to the dark, to the chair that still held his shape, that I had not turned the page past sheet twelve. That I had stopped. That some part of me — the part he trained, the part that could not abide a figure that might be misread — already knew there was more, and did not want to find it.

*A map is a promise.*

Whose promise, Master? Yours? Mine? What did you promise, and to whom, and what did it cost you to keep it — or to break it? You left me an unfinished coastline and a sentence in cipher and no one to ask, and I hated you for it, briefly, thoroughly, the way one hates the dead for dying badly: not for the sin but for the silence.

I slept in the loft that night rather than walk home through the frost. In the morning the light came up gray and clean and reasonable, and by daylight the note looked smaller, the way night fears always do. Paranoia. Old age. Fever damage. I said the words aloud like a charm, three times, and almost believed them.

Almost. Because there remained one fact I could not charm away, and it was this: Aldous Vane did not make errors. Not of measurement, and not of meaning. If his margin said the gates were shut, the gates were shut. If his cipher said the water was bought, then somewhere in this town, someone had sold it.

And there was a second fact, smaller, which I noticed only as I was wrapping my scarf and preparing to face the street: the ciphered note was not the last thing he had written on sheet twelve. Beneath it, in pencil, faint as breath on glass, were two more characters of the same cipher — a fragment, abandoned, unfinished. As if he had begun another message and stopped.

Or as if someone had interrupted him.

I was still standing there, my thumb resting on that faint penciled ghost, when I heard footsteps on the frost-hard lane outside — unhurried, deliberate, the steps of a man arriving rather than passing — and then a knock at the workshop door, three times, courteous as a summons.
