## 4. A Visitor with Clean Boots

The frost had come in the night without announcement, the way important things do. Corven Hale stood at his window above the market square and watched Vellamouth wake beneath a skin of white — the fishwives' stalls dusted like sugar loaves, the river steaming where the current kept its temper, the bridge arching over all of it, black stone on grey water, the town's one boast and the town's one throat. Everything came through that bridge or across that river. Everything could be stopped.

He dressed slowly, by lamplight, choosing the coat he wore for occasions that must not look like occasions. Dark green wool, good but not grand. A magistrate might wear it to inspect a granary. A neighbor might wear it to call on a widow. It was the coat of a man with nothing on his mind, which was precisely why it took so long to choose.

His clerk, Petch, waited at the foot of the stairs with his hat held wrong — brim forward, like a boy.

"The guild hall ledgers," Hale said. "Have them moved to my cellar by Friday. Tell Master Quillon it is a matter of safekeeping. Damp gets into that archive room; you've seen the mold on the older folios."

"Yes, sir." Petch hesitated, hat turning. "Master Quillon asked whether copies might be—"

"Quillon may make whatever copies he likes," Hale said pleasantly, "once the originals are secure. There is no hurry about copies. Copies are not what anyone steals."

Petch looked as though he wanted to ask what anyone would steal, and was wise enough not to. That was why Petch still had his post. The last clerk who'd asked questions — well. Questions were like wells. You didn't know how deep they went until someone fell down one.

Outside, the cold had teeth. Hale walked the long way, past the shambles and the salt store, because the long way took him along the lane where the mapmaker's workshop stood, and he wished to see it first from the outside, as a stranger might. The shutters were open. Lamplight burned behind the diamond panes even now, near nine o'clock, and a shadow moved across the light with the particular economy of a hand that knew its work. Not grief-labor, then. Not a woman drifting through a dead man's rooms. Industry.

That was either very good or very bad, and Hale intended to learn which before the bell rang noon.

* * *

The workshop smelled of tallow and size and the cold-mineral bite of iron gall ink, a smell Hale associated unpleasantly with documents he had signed and preferred not to remember. He knocked once on the open door, out of courtesy, and entered without waiting, also out of courtesy, since the two gestures cancelled each other and left only the impression of ease.

She was at the bench with her back half-turned, a goose quill in her hand, and she did not startle. He noted that first. Most people startled. She finished her stroke — a long, unbroken line along the edge of something he couldn't see — blotted it with sand, and then turned, setting the pen down parallel to the bench's edge with the precision of ritual.

"Magistrate Hale." Her voice was level. Young, but level. "You honor the shop."

"Master Crane." He used the name deliberately. Silas Crane — an apprentice's name, a workman's name, and he watched to see if she'd correct him to *Mistress*. She did not. Interesting. "I hope I find the shop keeping well. Grief is poor company in winter."

"Grief keeps its own hours, sir. We've come to an arrangement."

He smiled at that, and meant the smile to be seen as genuine, and saw it land as nothing at all. Aldous Vane had trained this one well. Or perhaps she'd arrived trained, the way some ore arrives already refined.

The workshop was exactly as he remembered it from two visits in the old man's lifetime: the long pine bench scarred and wax-dark, the racks of pens sorted by nib, the great press in the corner with its screw blackened by decades of hands, the wall of maps hung three deep — counties, coastlines, a vast chart of the estuary that dominated the north wall like a window onto another country. And on the bench, open, weighted at four corners with lead seals: a large folio.

An atlas. Half-finished. *Vane's atlas*, the guild had gossiped about it for years — the master's great labor, the coast surveyed inch by inch, the work of thirty winters that he had never consented to publish. Hale had assumed it would die with him, unfinished, unsaleable, a curiosity for the estate auction. He had not assumed it would be lying open under an apprentice's living hand.

"You're continuing it," he said, letting mild surprise into his voice like water into wine. "The atlas."

"Someone must finish the coastline." She said it simply, as though stating that bread required baking. "A map left incomplete is worse than no map. It promises a country and delivers a rumor."

"A map is a promise." Hale turned the phrase over. "Yes. He used to say that. In the guild hall, whenever anyone proposed cutting a survey short for want of funds. 'A map is a promise, gentlemen, and broken promises have drowned better towns than ours.'" He let the words sit. *Drowned.* Let her hear it or not hear it; either response told him something.

"He talked a great deal," Silas Crane said. "Mostly while working."

"And worked while talking. Yes, I remember." Hale moved along the wall of maps, unhurried, his gloved fingertips clasped behind his back — touching nothing, cataloguing everything. The estuary chart. A plan of the toll roads. An older thing, foxed with age, showing Marrowfleet and the levels around it as they had been before — he paused before it a half-second longer than a man merely admiring cartography would, and made himself move on. "I've come, in fact, with a commission."

"Then you'll want the guild rate book." She reached for it.

"I'll want your hand," Hale said, "not the guild's. A private commission. The county map — the new one, the one the assizes use — contains errors. Small ones. Boundaries drift. Parishes have been mislaid. I should like a corrected copy drawn, for my own study. Discreetly."

"Discreetly," she repeated.

"Corrections are sensitive work. If word got out that the official map was wrong, every litigant with a hedge dispute would be at the county office by Michaelmas. Better it be done quietly, by one reliable pair of hands." He smiled again. "Yours, I'm told, are the most reliable in Vellamouth. Your master said so himself, more than once, when he was — in his cups. Forgive me. When he was frank."

Something moved behind her eyes — not anger, he thought, or not only. A recalculation. He filed it.

"The fee would be generous," he continued. "Ten guineas now, ten on delivery. And there is the matter of your standing. The guild has been slow to confirm apprenticeships where the master has died intestate. With my recommendation, the confirmation could be expedited. You'd hold the shop in your own name by spring."

It was a good offer. He had built it carefully, layering money upon legitimacy upon belonging, each one a strap across the lid of a chest. Ten guineas was more than the shop cleared in a season. The confirmation was the thing she would need within the year, the thing she could not get without friends among the bench. And the commission itself — a corrected county map, in her own hand, commissioned by a magistrate — would bind her publicly to him, would make her complicit in whatever corrections he chose, would give him, should it ever prove necessary, a document in *her* writing to hang beside anything inconvenient she might produce.

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, a cart passed, its iron-shod wheels cracking the ice-glaze in the lane, and the sound filled the silence between them like a held breath finally released.

"The county map is guild property," she said. "Any correction would need the guild's warrant and the guild's seal. I can't take private commissions against the charter — you'll know that better than I, sir, having helped draft it."

"Charter provisions bend," Hale said mildly, "for those who serve the bench. But of course — if you feel the constraint, we needn't speak of it again today. Think it over. Winter is long."

"I don't need to think it over. The answer is no."

Not *no thank you*, not *perhaps in spring*, not any of the soft refusals by which civilized people decline one another. No. Clean as a ruled line. Hale felt something shift in his assessment of her, the way a surveyor feels ground change underfoot from gravel to clay. Vane had been slippery, evasive, a man whose defiance was always dressed as pedantry. This one refused plainly. Plain refusal was rarer and more dangerous. It could not be reasoned with, only managed.

"Well," he said warmly, "honesty is a virtue in a tradeswoman. I shan't press." He turned back toward the door, and then, as though it had just occurred to him — and he permitted himself the small vanity of knowing how well he performed the just-occurred-to — he glanced down at her hands.

They rested on the bench on either side of the atlas, and the fingers of the right hand were stained to the second knuckle with ink, the iron-black stain that no soap fully lifts, that works down into the whorls of the skin and stays for weeks. A working hand. An active hand.

"You've been busy," he observed. "Since the funeral, I mean. Most mourners let the ink dry for a season."

"The coastline won't wait for mourning, sir. Tides don't observe grief."

"No," Hale agreed. "Tides are remarkably indifferent to us all." He stepped closer to the bench — one step, no more, the step of a man admiring craftsmanship — and looked down at the open folio.

The page showed the coast east of the estuary, drawn in Vane's fine, tireless line, and beyond the paper's edge, faintly, the ghost of the next leaf showed through: more coastline, waiting. The margins were crowded with the old man's annotations — soundings, dates, tide tables in his cramped ciphered shorthand. Nothing legible. Nothing alarming. But the atlas lay open at the stretch of shore nearest Marrowfleet, and whether that was chance or habit or something else, Hale could not tell from looking, and not being able to tell displeased him more than anything he had found.

"She was a good woman, the sea," he said, conversational, studying the drawing rather than the girl. "Generous right up until she isn't. Took Marrowfleet in a single night, and forty-one souls with it — you'd know the number better than I, perhaps. Vane surveyed that village twice. Once before, once after. I often wondered what he made of the difference."

Silence. He let it run three heartbeats, four.

"He made a map of it, of course," Silas Crane said at last. "Maps are what he made. Of everything."

"Of everything," Hale echoed softly. "Yes. That was the trouble with Aldous." He straightened, and put on the face he saved for leaving, the regretful, reasonable, harmless face. "Consider the commission, Master Crane. The offer stands through the winter. Good paper is patient, and so am I — up to a point that would surprise you."

He crossed to the door, then paused on the threshold with one hand on the frame, and delivered the last of it lightly, almost tenderly, the way a man mentions weather:

"Workshops burn, you know. Especially in winter, with candles and spirits of wine everywhere, and a tired apprentice working late. The guild lost three shops to fire in my grandfather's time. Terrible losses. Whole archives gone in an hour." He shook his head at the tragedy of it. "Lock up careful, when you go out."

And he was gone, down the lane, his boots clean of the workshop's sand and size, his coat dark green and blameless, a man with nothing on his mind walking toward the market square where everyone could see him.

* * *

Behind him — he did not turn to check, but he knew it as he knew his own signature — the light in the workshop window did not go out.

She was still working. Of course she was. The coastline wouldn't wait for mourning, and neither, apparently, would she. Hale turned this over as he walked, testing it from every side like a coin he suspected of clipping.

A dead man's atlas, open on a living bench. Margins full of cipher. A refusal given too fast and too cleanly, with no counter-offer invited — which meant the refusal wasn't about money or charter at all. Which meant she had already decided something, or already found something, or already feared something enough to want no obligations recorded between them.

Or — and he held this possibility up to the light too, honestly, because honest accounting had kept him alive and comfortable for thirty years — perhaps she was merely loyal. Perhaps it was nothing but an orphan's stubborn fidelity to a dead master's unfinished promise, and the whole of it would end with the atlas published, the coastline completed, and Marrowfleet rendered in fine permanent ink for every schoolchild in the county to trace with a finger.

Hale stopped at the corner of the market square, where the fishwives were chipping ice off their stalls, and considered that last possibility properly, because it deserved consideration.

A published atlas was not evidence. A published atlas was only paper. Except that Vane's margins had never contained only geography — the old man had kept accounts of everything, tide and trade and grievance alike, in that damnable shorthand of his, and Hale had spent one uneasy evening, years ago, watching Vane write in those margins across the guild hall table and understanding, in the wordless way one understands a knife on a table, that the mapmaker was recording *him*.

The frost thickened on the fishwives' scales. Somewhere downriver a bell buoy clanked, patient and idiotic.

There was, Hale reflected, a simple solution to atlases. They burned like anything else. But burning drew eyes, and a burned atlas proved that something in it had mattered, and proof of mattering was itself a kind of evidence. No — the answer was subtler. The answer was to know precisely what she had, precisely what she'd understood, and to be holding the leash of her livelihood — the apprenticeship, the shop, the guild standing — when the moment came to take it from her.

He had offered her ten guineas. She had declined in four syllables.

Very well.

"Petch," he said, when he reached his chambers, shrugging off the green coat, "two matters. First — find out who holds the debt on the lease of the mapmaker's shop. Second—" he poured himself a measure of the French brandy he kept for thinking, "—write to the garrison. Ask Captain Erris, with my compliments, how many men he can spare to watch a doorway without appearing to watch it. A quiet doorway. On the mapmaker's lane."

Petch wrote it down. His pen scratched like a nervous animal.

"And sir?" Petch ventured, at the door. "The guild hall records. Shall I still tell Quillon Friday?"

"Friday," Hale said, and drank, and looked out the window at the river, where the ice was beginning, quietly, patiently, to build.

Across the water, invisible from here, lay the mudflats where Marrowfleet had been. Forty-one souls. He had never once counted them himself. He had others to do his counting — and now, it seemed, the dead man's apprentice had begun counting too.
