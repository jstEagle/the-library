## 6. First Frost, Last Crossing

The river told Tam Ossler things before it told anyone else. It had done so for forty years, and he trusted it the way other men trusted their wives or their God — imperfectly, but with his whole life riding on the trust.

On the morning the bridge was to close for the season, the river spoke in a language of small sounds: the tick and groan of ice forming along the reeds, the hollow knock of frozen shallows beneath the pilings, the long sigh of water moving under the crust like breath beneath a blanket. Tam stood on the Vellamouth landing with his hands wrapped around a clay mug of something hot and bitter, and he listened, and he understood that winter had arrived not as an event but as an accumulation — a thousand small surrenders by the water, each one barely worth noticing, until the whole river lay still.

He spat over the side of the landing into the black gap between the boards. The gap steamed faintly. *Not yet,* the river said. *But soon.*

Above him, spanning the narrows where the Vella ran quick and deep before it spread into its marshes, the old timber bridge stood grey against a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Frost furred every rope and railing. The toll house at the far end wore its snow like a wig worn slightly askew. Somewhere on the far bank, Nell Tolliver was already up; he could see the smoke from the toll house chimney standing straight as a plumb line in the windless cold.

Tam had ferried people across this water since he was fourteen years old, back when his father owned the boat and the boat was younger than Tam was now. He knew every mood the Vella had. He knew the spring moods, when she came down brown and muscular from the high country carrying fences and dead sheep and once — though he never told this to anyone who hadn't earned it — a piano. He knew the summer evenings when she lay so flat and golden you could read your own face in her. And he knew this: the hard, patient silence of first freeze, when the river stopped speaking plainly and started keeping secrets under her skin.

It was the season of secrets. It always had been. But this year there were more of them than usual, and one of them had a name.

* * *

He saw Silas Crane come down the ferry path a little after noon, walking fast with her collar up and her arms crossed against the cold, and he thought — not for the first time — that she walked like her master had walked, like a person going somewhere with a purpose too large for the road to hold.

"Tam." She stopped at the edge of the landing, boots planted on the frost-stiffened planks. Her breath came out in white ribbons. "Is it true? They're closing the bridge tonight?"

"At dusk," Tam said. "Last cart goes across at sundown. After that, nothing crosses until the thaw, and no telling when the thaw comes. Could be February. Could be late March, if the winter's mean."

She looked at the bridge the way a prisoner looks at a wall. Then at the water. Then at him.

"And the ferry?"

"The ferry's done too, girl. Ice'll take the hull apart if I try. Come January the channel freezes bank to bank, and then folk cross on foot if they're fools, or wait for the garrison post to open its inspection if they're not." He shrugged. "Winter is winter. It closes everything. That's the arrangement."

"The arrangement," she repeated, softly, as if the words tasted strange.

He studied her. She was twenty-three, maybe twenty-four, thin as a drafting pen, with ink permanently worked into the creases of her knuckles so that even scrubbed clean she looked like she'd been handling letters all her life. Which, he supposed, she had. Three months since they'd pulled Aldous Vane out of the water below the weir, three months since Tam had carried the old man's body up the bank himself, his arms under the sodden shoulders, and felt how light a drowned man becomes, as if the river takes more than just the breath out of you.

"You look tired, Miss Crane," he said.

"I'm always tired."

"That's not what I mean."

She gave him a look — quick, guarded, gone again — and he understood that whatever was behind the look wasn't for him. Not yet. Maybe not ever. People carried their burdens onto his landing every day, and most of them set them down somewhere else. He'd learned not to reach for them.

"They say the magistrates ordered the closure early this year," Silas said. "Two weeks ahead of the usual date."

Tam turned his mug slowly in his hands. "They say a lot of things."

"Do you believe it?"

"I believe the toll keeper does what the toll keeper's told. I believe the garrison does what the garrison's told. And I believe the river doesn't care who tells whom what." He looked at her steadily. "Why do you ask, girl? What is it you need to be crossing?"

For a moment he thought she would tell him. Her mouth opened; the white breath came out; and then some door inside her shut, quiet as a latch falling home.

"Nothing," she said. "Nothing yet."

*Yet.* He heard it plain as a church bell. He let it go.

* * *

By mid-afternoon the word had gone around town, as words do, faster than any horse: last crossing at dusk. Carts began to gather on the near approach by four o'clock, when the light was already failing — a farmer with winter apples, a tinker with his pots chiming on the cart bed, a widow with two goats tied bleating in the back, a peddler whose face Tam knew from a hundred crossings and could not have named for money. The horses stamped and blew steam. The drivers hunched in their collars. Everyone wanted over the water before the water decided for them.

Tam stood at the landing and watched them queue, and felt the old seasonal grief move through him, familiar as arthritis. Every year the bridge closed and the town split in half like a loaf torn by hand — the north bank and the south bank, and between them a mile of freezing mud, and then a mile of freezing water, and then nothing at all. Families on opposite sides would not see each other till spring unless they walked out on the ice, which half of them did, and which killed somebody nearly every year, usually a boy showing off.

This year, though, the grief had a different taste. This year the closing felt less like weather and more like a hand coming down over a table.

He didn't know why he thought that. Or rather — he did know why, and the knowing sat in him like a swallowed stone.

Because of what he'd seen the night Vane died.

* * *

He had told Silas about the argument. He hadn't been able to help it — she'd come to his hut below the weir with questions soft as feathers, and Tam Ossler had never in his life been good at refusing a grieving woman. But he had not told her everything, because some of it he did not understand himself, and a man who ferries for a living learns to be careful with what he says on the water.

Here was what he had seen. A month before the drowning, on a night in October, he'd been running a late passenger across — a lawyer's clerk from the assize circuit, drunk and talkative — and as he'd brought the boat in toward the north stairs, he'd seen two men standing at the end of the weir walk. One was Aldous Vane; he'd have known the old man's stoop anywhere, that mapmaker's stoop, head bent as if perpetually reading the ground. The other had been Magistrate Corven Hale, unmistakable even in bad light, because Hale stood the way a fencepost stands, straight and planted and owning the view.

They had been arguing. Not shouting — shouting carries over water, and there'd been none — but arguing the way ice argues with a hull: slow, grinding, irresistible pressure against pressure. Vane's arm had come up twice, jabbing at the dark, and Tam, poling closer, had caught fragments. Something about *numbers*. Something about *the sluices*. And then Hale's voice, low and even and terrible in its mildness: *"Master Vane. You are a servant of the county. Remember whose county it is."*

Tam had let his passenger off at the south stairs and poled back out, and by the time he passed the weir again the two men were gone, and the river had gone on doing what rivers do, which is erase.

A month later, Vane was in the water.

Accident, said the coroner. An old man, fond of his evening walks, a wet October night, slick stones above the weir pool. Tam had stood at the inquest with forty other townsfolk and watched the coroner — a comfortable man appointed by the same bench that paid the coroner's fees — pronounce it without once looking up from his papers. Tam had said nothing. What was there to say? *I saw them argue once*? Half the town had seen them argue twice. Arguing wasn't drowning. Rivers drowned old men every autumn; it was practically the river's trade.

And yet.

And yet the stone stayed swallowed, and every year at first freeze the stone got heavier, because Tam Ossler had lived on this water long enough to know one thing above all others: the river keeps what it's given, but it also gives back what it's taken, given time. Bodies float up in spring. Truths too, sometimes. You just have to be alive and watching when they surface.

* * *

Dusk came down like a lid.

The last cart went across at half past four, the tinker's, its lamps swinging, the horse's hooves ringing hollow on the frost-hard boards. Tam watched it recede toward the far toll house, smaller and smaller, until Nell Tolliver came out with her lantern to meet it, and the lantern's glow made a small warm island in the gathering blue. He heard the toll bar creak down behind it — a sound like a bone settling — and then the toll house lamp went dim, shuttered for the season, and the bridge stood empty in the last light, its long spine of timber fading from grey to charcoal to nothing.

The crowd on the near bank dispersed. The farmer, the widow, the goats, the peddler — all gone into the lanes, into the taverns, into the warm rooms where winter would be endured. The cold thickened. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Tam was turning to go back to his hut when he saw that Silas Crane was still there.

She had not crossed with the carts. She had not tried to. She stood at the very end of the landing, past the last bollard, where the planks ended and the water began, her hands flat on the frost-slick rail, looking out at the bridge she could no longer use. The ice had crept further out while they talked; sheets of it rode the slack tide near the banks, clicking together like crockery. In the middle channel the water still ran black and alive, but it was running slower now, sullen, already thinking about stopping.

He came and stood beside her. Neither of them spoke for a while. On the water, that was proper. You didn't chatter at a river that was making up its mind.

"My master used to bring me down here," Silas said at last. Her voice was rougher than usual, scraped thin by the cold or by something else. "When I was new to the workshop. He'd stand right here and tell me to draw the bridge from memory. Then draw it again. Then again, until I stopped drawing what I assumed and started drawing what was true. He said—" she stopped. Started again. "He said most lies aren't told, Tam. Most lies are just drawn badly. Assumed. People map the world the way they wish it were, and then they navigate by the lie, and then they drown in the place the lie said was shallow."

"He was a wise old fool," Tam said.

"Yes," she said. "He was."

There was something in the way she said it — a weight, a crack, a door held shut with both hands — that made Tam turn and look at her properly. In the blue dusk her face was pale as paper, and her eyes had that fixed quality he'd seen in sailors staring at a squall line: fear being held very still and examined.

"What is it you're carrying, Miss Crane?" he asked quietly. "That's the real question, isn't it? Not what you need to cross. What you need to carry."

She didn't answer. But her hand moved — a small involuntary motion, touching her coat where it hung heavy at her breast, the way a woman touches a locket, or a letter, or a wound.

Tam nodded slowly, as if she had answered.

"Then hear an old ferryman," he said. "The bridge is closed, yes. The ferry's hauled out, yes. But listen to the water." He tipped his chin at the channel, where the last open water breathed its faint steam into the dark. "First freeze isn't the death of the river. It's the river holding its breath. There's a week — maybe two — when the middle still runs, when a man with a strong boat and a strong back can get across below the garrison post if the fog sits right and the sentries are warming their feet at their stove instead of watching the water. After that, the channel locks, and nothing moves but the ice. And then in spring—" he smiled, without much humor, "—in spring the river opens again, whether anyone likes it or not. The thaw is the one thing in this world no magistrate can buy."

Silas looked at the black water. Tam watched her watch it, and saw her doing sums — not numbers, but distances, days, dangers. Mapping. She couldn't help it. It was in her blood the way tides were in his.

"Spring thaw," she murmured. "That's the deadline. For everyone."

"For everyone," Tam agreed. "Whoever's waiting for what you've got — they've got until thaw too. Same clock. Same trap." He paused. "You understand that, don't you? Closing the bridge doesn't only keep you in. It keeps them honest, or it lets them work. Winter cuts both ways, girl. Whatever happens on that water happens slow, in the dark, with nobody to witness."

The wind came off the river then, the first real wind of the season, carrying the smell of iron and coming snow. Silas shivered — or perhaps didn't shiver, perhaps only stood straighter, the way a mast stands straighter when the load comes on.

"Thank you, Tam," she said.

"For what?"

"For telling me the river holds its breath. Instead of telling me it's dead."

She turned and went up the ferry path, and he watched her go, a slight figure pulling her coat tight, her shadow long on the frozen mud, until the dark took her at the bend by the osier beds. Then Tam Ossler stood alone on his landing with his empty mug and his swallowed stone, listening to the ice click and grind in the shallows, and he thought about the argument at the weir, and the coroner's dry voice, and the way Corven Hale had come down to the landing two weeks ago with two of his men, saying he wished to *inspect* the ferry arrangements for the winter, and had stood exactly where Tam stood now, looking exactly where Tam was looking, at the water, at the bridge, at the town beyond it — the way a man looks at a board before he moves a piece.

*Same trap,* he had told the girl. He hadn't told her the rest of what he thought, because he hadn't found the words yet, and Tam Ossler was careful with words on the water. But the thought was this:

A trap that closes gently is still a trap. Sometimes gentleness is the cruelest part of the design — because a gentle trap lets you feel safe right up until the spring, when the ice goes out of the river, and everything that has been held under it all winter comes floating up at once.

Out in the channel, the last open water breathed its thin ghost of steam, slower now, slower.

Holding its breath.

So, Tam understood, was everyone else in Vellamouth — and somewhere up the hill, behind lit windows, in a workshop that smelled of ink and linseed oil, a young woman with ink in her knuckles had just begun to count the days until the thaw.
