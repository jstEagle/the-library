## 9. The Widow of Marrowfleet

The poorhouse stood at the low end of Tanner's Lane, where the street gave up pretending to be respectable and became mud. Silas had passed it a hundred times without seeing it — a long grey building with a roof that sagged in the middle like a tired back, its windows fogged with the breath of too many bodies, its door painted a green that had once been meant to look welcoming and now looked like pond water. She had never had reason to go inside. Vellamouth's poor were as invisible to her as Marrowfleet had been on Master Vane's atlas: present, drawn even, but unnamed.

She carried a basket. In it lay two loaves she'd bought from the baker on Fetter Street — good bread, not the grey loaf the poorhouse issued — and a small pot of honey, and beneath these, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom where no curious matron would rummage, the ledger.

She had debated that all night. To carry it out of the workshop was to risk everything; to leave it behind while she went asking questions felt like leaving her heart in a drawer. In the end she'd sewn it into the false bottom of the basket, working by candlelight with the small neat stitches Vane had taught her for mending map folds, and told herself that a woman carrying bread to the poor was the most unremarkable creature in Vellamouth. Only her hands, ink-stained to the second knuckle, marked her as anything but what she seemed.

*You could still stop,* said some sensible voice in her head, the one that sounded like Tam Ossler. *Take the bread, take the honey, ask your questions gently, and walk away.*

But she had seen the sluice gates drawn in Vane's hand, annotated in his tight cipher, and she had read the last entry in the ledger dated four days before the water came down the valley, and there was no walking away from that. There was only forward, along the mud of Tanner's Lane, toward the green door.

A woman answered her knock — not the matron but a resident, stooped and quick-eyed, who looked at the basket before she looked at Silas's face. "Bread," Silas said. "For Edith Marr, if she's here."

"Everyone's here," the woman said, with a bitterness that had gone smooth with age. "Who's asking?"

"A friend. Of — of Marrowfleet."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes, a shutter half-drawn. "Wait there." The door closed. Silas stood in the cold and listened to the poorhouse breathe: a coughing somewhere deep in the building, the scrape of a chair, a child crying in a thin exhausted thread, the smell of boiled cabbage and wet wool seeping out through the frame.

When the door opened again, the woman jerked her head. "End room, past the wash-house. She'll see you. Don't be long — Matron counts heads at midday and she counts them mean."

* * *

Edith Marr sat by the one window in the end room, and the first thing Silas understood about her was that she was mending. A shirt across her lap, needle rising and falling with the unhurried rhythm of someone who had done ten thousand hours of exactly this. Her hands were red at the knuckles and steady. Her face was long and lined, weathered like a fence post, and her eyes — this was the second thing Silas understood — were the eyes of a person who had already survived the worst thing that would ever happen to her and knew it.

"You're young," Edith said, by way of greeting. Her voice was flat riverland vowels, the accent of the drowned valley. "I expected someone older. Or someone in fine cloth come to tell me I'm being moved again."

"I'm not from the magistrates."

"No one is from the magistrates until they are." She set the shirt down, folded the needle into the cloth with a practiced motion, and looked up. "Sit, then. Chair's broken but it holds. You've bread in that basket and something else besides. I can smell the honey, and under it paper and lamp-oil. You've been burning the midnight over something written."

Silas sat. For a moment she didn't know how to begin. She had rehearsed a dozen openings on the walk down — *Madam Marr, I am an apprentice mapmaker, I have found evidence concerning the flood* — and every one of them sounded, out loud in her head, like either madness or treason or both.

"You were in Marrowfleet," she said finally. "The night it flooded."

Edith Marr did not flinch. That was the thing Silas would remember afterward, the thing that made her certain: other people, when Silas named the village, looked away, or made the small pained noise people make at funerals, or began immediately to speak of the weather and God's will. Edith looked directly at her, and waited.

"I was," Edith said.

"My master was Aldous Vane. The surveyor. He drew the valley maps — the ones the relief commission used."

"I know who Aldous Vane was." Something crossed Edith's face, too fast to name. "He came to Marrowfleet twice. Autumn of '44 and again in the spring. He measured our fields and drank tea with my husband and told him the levees were sound. Told him so to his face, with a map in his hand."

Silas's throat tightened. "Yes."

"And now he's drowned himself, they say. Drunk, they say. And you're here with bread and honey and something hidden in your basket, three years late." Edith picked up her mending again, though Silas noticed she didn't sew; she just held it, the way a soldier holds a weapon he has decided not to use yet. "So say what you've come to say, girl. I'm old and the fire's small and I don't have time left for circling."

Silas took a breath. "The sluice gates upstream. At the weir above the ferry bend. On the night of the ninth of November — three years past. Were they open or closed?"

The room went very quiet. Outside, somewhere, the child had stopped crying. Even the wind in the eaves seemed to lean closer.

Edith Marr set the shirt down entirely. She turned on her stool to face Silas fully, and when she spoke her voice had changed — lower, slower, each word placed like a stone in a wall.

"Who wants to know?"

"I do. And in time — I hope — a judge will."

"A judge." Edith laughed, one dry note, no humor in it at all. "Three years ago I stood in the guild hall with my hair still full of river mud and told the bailiff what I saw, and he wrote it down, and then he wrote down something else instead, and then they gave me bread and six shillings and a place in this house and told me to be grateful." She leaned forward. "So you understand why I ask. Who wants to know? Not your name. Names change nothing. Who stands behind you?"

"The truth stands behind me," Silas said, and heard how young she sounded, and hated it, and said it anyway. "I have proof. Written proof. Payments. Signed."

For the first time, Edith Marr's steadiness cracked — not into tears but into something more terrible, a stillness so complete it was like watching ice form. "Signed," she repeated. "By whom?"

Silas thought of the ledger sewn into her basket, three feet of cold air away. "I can't say. Not yet. Not until it's safe to say."

"And you think it will ever be safe?" Edith shook her head slowly. "Child, I watched forty-one people die in one night. My husband. My daughter. My sister and her boys. I watched the water come because the gates above us were shut — shut, girl, barred, with new iron on the locks — and the millpond filled and filled and then let go all at once, the way a bucket lets go. Forty-one people. And do you know what the record says?" Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it; it stayed level as a sounding line. "It says an unprecedented spate. It says acts of God. It says the village of Marrowfleet, population thirty-nine, suffered grievous loss."

"Thirty-nine," Silas whispered. "There were forty-one."

"There were always forty-one," Edith said. "They couldn't count us right alive either. We were the wrong sort of people in the wrong sort of valley, and the water was the excuse for everything after." She looked at her hands. "You asked about the gates. I'll tell you what I saw, since you're fool enough to ask and brave enough to bring bread. My man was the lock-keeper's cousin. On the evening of the ninth we walked up past the weir — courting, if you can credit it, married nine years and still walking. The path runs above the sluice channel. We saw the gates closed and the winch locked, and we saw a lantern below the winch house, and men's voices. Two men. One of them wore a magistrate's sash — you could see the ribbon even in the dark, it caught the lantern light. And my man called down, friendly-like, asked if there was trouble with the weir. And the voice that answered said, *All in order, go home.*" She paused. "Those were the last words my husband ever heard spoken to him. *All in order, go home.*"

Silas found she was gripping the arms of the broken chair. "Did you tell anyone? Afterward?"

"I told everyone. I shouted it in the guild hall until they escorted me out. I wrote to the county clerk. Do you know what happened to my letters?" She smiled, and it was the bleakest thing Silas had ever seen. "Neither do I. They went somewhere. Things go somewhere, in this town. They go into cellars and ledgers and locked drawers, and the river goes on being called an act of God."

*The water was bought.* Vane's ciphered hand rose up in Silas's memory, and for the first time the words did not sound like an old man's paranoia. They sounded like a confession he hadn't dared write plain.

"Master Vane," Silas said. "Did he know? About the gates?"

Edith studied her for a long moment. Whatever she saw in Silas's face seemed to decide something. "He came to me," she said. "Eight months ago. Winter before last. He came to this room, same as you, smelling of ink and shame, and he sat in that same broken chair, and he asked me the same question you just asked." Her eyes narrowed. "And I told him what I told you. And he went white as paper and put his head in his hands and said, *Then I drew the map that buried them.*"

The words landed in Silas like stones dropped into deep water. *Then I drew the map that buried them.*

"He said the survey was falsified before it ever reached his bench — that the levels he was given showed the levees sound, the spillways sufficient, the village safe at flood-stage. He said he'd trusted the figures because they came under the commission's seal." Edith's mouth twisted. "And then he said — I've kept this word like a coin under my tongue for two years — he said, *I will make it right, Mrs. Marr, if it costs me everything.* And I said, *It will.* And he said, *I know.* And he left. And eight months later he was in the river."

Silas could not speak. Grief and anger and a fierce, terrible pride were all tangled together in her chest — pride because Vane had gone to Marrowfleet's widow with his hat in his hands; grief because he had been dead three months and was still teaching her things; anger because *everything*, it turned out, had cost exactly what Edith Marr had priced it at, and the payment was still coming due.

"He talked of an apprentice," Edith said quietly. Watching her. "A girl. He said she had the truest hand he'd ever taught. He said—" Edith stopped, and for the first time her voice roughened. "He said she would finish what he started, if anything happened to him. Was that vanity, do you think? An old man comforting himself? Or did he know?"

"He left me the atlas," Silas said. "Everything's in it. Everything he found."

"Then you're past the point where bread and honey settle the account." Edith reached down beside her stool and lifted a small wooden box, worn smooth, with a sliding lid. From it she took a scrap of cloth — no larger than a hand's breadth, coarse linen, dyed in a pattern Silas had never seen and knew instantly she would never forget: bands of madder-red and indigo crossed with threads of ochre, woven in a diamond lattice that seemed to shift when she looked at it directly, like water over stones.

"What is it?"

"Marrowfleet dye-work. My mother's pattern. Every household in the valley had its own variation — ours was the diamond lattice. You could stand at any door in Marrowfleet and tell whose washing hung where." Edith pressed it into Silas's palm and closed Silas's fingers over it with her own hard red hands. "There isn't a yard of it left in the world now except scraps. They burned what the water spared — bedding, clothing, everything — *for fear of fever,* they said." Her lip curled. "They wanted no cloth walking around town that people might recognize. No reminders."

"Why give it to me?"

"Two reasons." Edith held her gaze. "First: when you stand wherever it is you mean to stand and say what you mean to say, show this, and say Edith Marr of Marrowfleet sent it. Let them see the pattern. Let them remember we wove things. We were people who made beautiful things, girl, whatever their records say. Thirty-nine souls, indeed."

"And the second reason?"

Edith released her hand and sat back. When she spoke again her voice was quiet and absolutely clear, the voice of a woman laying a stone in a wall that would have to hold weight.

"The second reason is that I am going to sign whatever you put in front of me. My statement, sworn, about the gates and the sash and the lantern and the last words my husband ever heard. I've kept it in my head for three years because there was nowhere safe to put it down." She nodded at the basket, at the shape sleeping inside it. "Whatever's in there — it needs witnesses, or it's just paper. Paper burns. Paper gets lost in cellars. But paper with living names attached — that's harder. So find me a magistrate's clerk who isn't bought, or a justice of assize, or a priest, or the devil himself with a quill, and I'll swear it before any of them." Her jaw set. "I've been silent longer than I was ever married. I'm done with it."

Silas looked down at the scrap of cloth in her palm. The diamond lattice caught the weak window-light and seemed almost to move. She thought of the atlas on her bench with Marrowfleet's name scraped away, and of the ledger sewn beneath the loaves, and of Vane standing in this exact broken chair with his head in his hands, saying *I will make it right.*

"Yes," she said. "I'll find someone. Before the thaw."

"Before the thaw," Edith repeated, and something in her face suggested she understood precisely what the thaw meant — the bridge opening, the river running, the world's traffic resuming and burying everything under its ordinary noise. "Then hurry. And girl—" Silas paused at the door. "Watch Corven Hale. He wears his courtesy like my people wore their dye-work: beautifully, and so you'll know exactly whose he is."

Outside, the cold had sharpened. Silas walked back up Tanner's Lane with the basket heavy against her hip, the ledger riding against the bread like a second heartbeat, and the scrap of cloth in her pocket where her thumb kept returning to it, tracing diamonds.

She was halfway up the lane, thinking of clerks and oaths and which parish records office kept a neutral seal, when she saw the figure ahead of her, standing at the corner of Fetter Street in a fine wool coat with his boots immaculate above the mud.

Magistrate Hale was not looking at her. He was looking at the poorhouse.

He stood there a moment longer — unhurried, thoughtful, a gentleman admiring the view — and then he turned, and began to walk, unhurried, toward the green door.
