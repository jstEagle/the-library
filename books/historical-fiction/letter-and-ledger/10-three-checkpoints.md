## 10. Three Checkpoints

The candle had burned to a thumb's width when Silas Crane finally admitted what she was doing.

She had told herself it was practice. A cartographer's exercise, nothing more — a study in scale and distance, the kind of drill Master Vane had set her a hundred times. *Draw what you know,* he would say, tapping the table with one ink-stained finger. *Then draw what you don't know. Then find out why the two disagree.* She had drawn the town square from memory at fourteen, the harbor at fifteen, the churchyard with its tilted stones at sixteen, each time finding some error in her recollection that sent her back into the streets with her sketchbook under her arm like a hymnal.

But this was not practice. This was a route.

Three sheets lay on the bench before her now, pinned at the corners with the brass weights she'd used since childhood. On each sheet, rendered in the fine grey wash she reserved for preliminary work — nothing permanent, nothing that could be called evidence if the sheets were seized — stood one of the three gates between Vellamouth and anywhere else.

She looked at them the way she imagined a general might look at a country he intended to cross, or a thief at a house he intended to enter, and the comparison made her stomach turn, because she was neither of those things. She was a mapmaker's apprentice with ink under her fingernails and a dead man's secrets sewn into the lining of a coat that hung on the hook by the door.

*The toll gate first.*

The southern road left Vellamouth through a gap in the old town wall where the masons had never bothered to rebuild after the fire of '31. The toll gate sat there like a cork in a bottle: a timber arch, a plank across the cobbled way, and a low stone hut where the toll keeper warmed his hands over a brazier from Michaelmas to Lady Day. She had passed it a thousand times without seeing it. Now she drew it from memory and found, to her unease, that she saw everything.

The arch was older than the gate itself, its keystone carved with a fish that the weather had worn to a suggestion. The plank barrier swung on iron hinges, and it could be lifted clear if a cart were wide enough to warrant it — grain carts came through that way at harvest, paying double. The hut had one window facing the road and none facing the river side, which meant a man inside it could see who approached along the causeway but not who walked the towpath behind the hedge. The brazier smoke went up and east in the prevailing wind, which meant that on most winter mornings the keeper's eyes watered and his attention narrowed to the fire and his own chapped knuckles.

And there was Nell.

Silas paused, quill hovering, and then wrote the girl's name in the margin — small, in cipher, the same simple substitution Vane had taught her for labeling drafts: *the daughter keeps the list.* Old Tolliver's eyes were failing; everyone knew it; his daughter kept the ledger of who had paid and who was owed forbearance, and she took the coins with a smile that Silas had always read as ordinary cheerfulness and now understood might be something more like inventory. A girl who counted other people's money all day learns exactly how much a man is worth.

What did Nell cost? What was she loyal *to*? Silas did not know yet, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a stone she kept swallowing. But she knew this: the toll gate was not really a gate. It was a question asked by a man who wanted to go back to his fire, answered by whoever paid. Gates like that opened for confidence. They closed for hesitation.

She shaded the blind side of the hut, marked the hinge-side of the barrier, and noted in her small ciphered hand: *approach with paper. Guild stock travels free.* It was true — guild materials moved through the toll untaxed, a privilege so old nobody remembered granting it. Nobody questioned paper going out of Vellamouth. Paper was the town's least dangerous export. That assumption, she thought, looking down at her own hands, was about to be worth more than any coin in the ledger.

* * *

The garrison post was a different animal entirely.

It occupied the old granary on the north road, two storeys of honey-coloured stone with slitted windows that had once been built for defending grain against rioters and now defended nothing but the magistrate's peace of mind. Six men were billeted there in winter, though Silas had rarely seen more than four together. They wore the county colours — dull green facings, gone shabby at the cuffs — and they drilled on the yard on mornings when the frost was light enough, their sergeant's voice carrying as far as the market cross.

She drew the building, and then she drew what the drawing revealed, which was worse than the building.

The post stood where the north road narrowed between the granary wall and the drainage ditch — a funnel, deliberately or not, that put every traveller within six feet of the sentry box for the length of a slow cart's passing. There was no blind approach. There was no hedge, no towpath, no second road. The surveyors who had laid out that stretch — and she felt a cold twist in her gut as she thought it — had been guild men. Had one of them been Vane? Had her master drawn the very corridor through which she would someday need to flee?

There was no answer in the atlas. There never was, anymore; every page she turned now seemed to hold a second map underneath the first, the way a palimpsest holds an older writing beneath the scraping.

She forced herself to be exact. Four men on watch in rotation, changing at dawn, noon, dusk, and midnight. The noon change was slack — she had watched it from the wool merchant's doorway often enough, waiting out rain, and the men came off duty yawning, handing over with a wordless jerk of the chin. The sentry at noon was usually the young one, the fair boy with the chilblains who read a pamphlet when he thought no one saw. A boy who reads is a boy who imagines himself elsewhere. Boys imagining themselves elsewhere do not look closely at carts.

But the sergeant —

Silas set down the quill and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw orange stars.

The sergeant's name was Brack. Everyone in Vellamouth knew Brack the way you know weather: not warmly, but thoroughly. He was a broad, grey-tempered man of perhaps fifty who had served the county longer than anyone cared to remember, who spoke perhaps forty words a day and wasted none of them, who had once stood in the market square in the pouring rain for three hours because his orders said the square was to be watched until dark and dark had not yet come. He did not drink. He did not gamble. He did not laugh at jokes unless the person telling them outranked him, and even then only briefly.

He was also, according to Tam Ossler — who ferried garrison supplies across the channel and heard everything said on both banks — one of the names on Corven Hale's private payroll. Not the public stipend the magistrates drew for maintaining order. The other list. The quiet one. The one that bought a man's eyes and turned them politely away.

*A man who cannot be bought twice,* Silas wrote in cipher beneath the drawing of the sentry box, and then stared at the words until they frightened her properly. Because a man on one payroll could be moved by another. But Brack — Brack was paid to be incorruptible. That was his salary's entire purpose. You did not bribe Brack; you *were seen* not bribing him, and his presence was the bribe, reassurance made flesh, standing in his dull green coat at the narrow place in the road.

Which meant gate two would not open for coin or charm or confidence.

It would have to open for something else, and she did not yet know what that something was, and the not-knowing made her want to burn all three sheets and go back to inking coastlines for the rest of her short, safe life.

She did not burn them. She blew out the guttering candle instead, sat in the dark, and listened to the workshop tick and settle around her — the cooling stove, the stretched paper contracting on its frames, the river speaking under the floorboards in its constant low voice. Somewhere out there in the dark, Marrowfleet's drowned lanes lay under their skin of ice, and Edith Marr sat in the poorhouse with her sewing, and Corven Hale slept the sleep of a man whose town was well-managed.

*You are studying terrain,* she told herself, the way she'd once told herself *you are only practicing*. *Terrain does not hate you. Terrain simply is. Learn it, and it will carry you.*

* * *

The third checkpoint was the river itself, and the river did not keep a roster.

The ferry inspection stood at the winter landing below the bridge stairs — a plank hut, a chain stanchion, a brazier of its own, and two inspectors appointed by the Corporation to examine outgoing cargo for contraband and outgoing persons for debt. In summer, when the ferry ran hourly and the wherries crowded the stairs like gulls, the inspection was a formality performed at speed, a glance into baskets, a wave-through. In winter, when the crossing ran twice a day at most and the ferryman was half-frozen and foul-tempered, it was slower and meaner, conducted in the hut's fug of tobacco while the travellers stamped and steamed like cattle.

Both inspectors were Hale's appointments. One of them, Fenner, owed his position outright to the magistrate's patronage and made no secret of it; he wore a silver-chained seal on his neck like a wife's locket. The other was younger, a silent man named Quill — Silas had never learned his first name — who did the actual searching while Fenner did the talking, and who searched with a thoroughness that seemed less like diligence than like appetite.

Tam had described him once, spitting into the channel water: *"Searches like he's looking for something personal. Like he wants to find it. Other men search hoping to be done. That'un searches hoping."*

She drew the landing. The hut, the chain, the stairs cut into the clay bank, the shingle below where the boats grounded. The sight lines: from the hut door, an inspector could see the whole stair and the first thirty feet of the path down from the town. From the shingle, once a traveller reached the boat, the hut could see nothing — the bank's shoulder blocked it — but by then the inspection was over anyway. Everything happened in the twenty feet between the top of the stairs and the gunwale.

Twenty feet. She measured it off on her drawing with dividers and wrote the number small beside it, and the smallness of it appalled her. Her whole future, the whole truth of Marrowfleet and the sluice gates and the payments signed in three magistrate hands — all of it would pass or fail inside twenty feet of frozen mud, judged by a man who *hoped*.

Unless she crossed where there was no inspection at all.

Her pencil moved before her caution could stop it, out past the edge of the official landing, downstream along the bank to where the alders grew and the clay gave way to gravel, to the stretch Tam Ossler called the Narrows — where the channel pinched between the town bank and the far meadow and the current ran fast enough to keep thin ice from forming even in the cruelest January. A boat could cross there. Tam's boat. Tam, who pulled her master's body from the water and had not let her pay him for the coffin nails, saying the river owed Vane that much and he was merely collecting.

But Tam's loyalty was a rumour, and rumours sank. And the Narrows in February was not a crossing; it was a wager with black water, with floes coming down from the upper weirs grinding and groaning like millstones, with a channel that shifted its deeps every flood. She had seen the ice break up once, when she was nine, and she still dreamed of the sound — that long tearing roar, like the world's spine cracking.

She looked at what she'd drawn: three sheets, three gates, and between them a woman with ink-stained fingers carrying folded papers against her ribs.

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin. Silas rolled the three sheets together, tied them with plain twine, and slid them into the hollow space behind the bench's lower drawer where Vane had hidden his best brushes from the damp. Then she stood at the workshop window with her cold tea and watched the town wake, and made herself do the last thing, the hardest thing, the thing that separated craft from terror.

She made the list.

Not of the gates. Of herself.

*What I have:* the ledger pages, copied twice over in her own hand, folded flat and distributed — some in the coat lining, some sewn into the false spine of a prayer book, one, the most important, disguised as an ordinary survey memorandum with its damning figures dressed in innocent notation that only she could read. *What I am:* a guild apprentice, known about town, unremarkable, a woman alone whom no one feared and everyone overlooked — and she had spent three days learning to stop resenting that overlook and start spending it like currency. *What I lack:* a reason to travel that no inspector would think to doubt. Papers that would survive Fenner's eye and Quill's fingers. A crossing that would survive the Narrows. And time — God, time, the ice already creaking at night on the far shallows, the thaw coming like a creditor.

Every plan needs one thing to go right, she thought. Her plan needed five things to go right, arranged in a line, each depending on the last, like stepping stones across exactly the kind of water she'd just spent the night fearing.

Vane would have laughed at it. Not cruelly — he'd have laughed the way he laughed at her first coastline, aged twelve, wobbly as a wet rope — and then he'd have sat down beside her and said, *Good. Now you know where the errors live. Errors are honest things; they tell you precisely where to work.*

"All right," she said aloud, to the empty workshop, to the wall maps, to the ghost who hung among them. "Now I know where the errors live."

She washed her face in the basin, bound her hair plainly, put on the grey coat with its sewn lining, and picked up her satchel. There was a commission she had been putting off answering — Hale's letter still sat on the corner of the bench, heavy cream paper, his seal unbroken, offering work and implying consequences in equal measure. She would have to answer it today, or fail to, and either answer was a move on a board she had only begun to map.

But first there was a toll gate to walk past, slowly, openly, a sketchbook under her arm — because the best way to study a checkpoint, Vane had always said, was to be seen studying nothing at all.

She locked the door behind her and left the key where the key always lived, and walked down into the waking town toward the fish-smoke and the frost and the first of her three gates.
