## 11. A Commission Refused

Corven Hale had a theory about doors. In thirty years of public service he had learned that every door in Vellamouth told you something before it opened — the weight of its hinges, the polish of its latch, the depth of the scuff at its foot. The guild hall's oak doors told him that men with clean boots came and went without thinking. The workhouse door told him that no one who entered expected to leave quickly. And the door of Aldous Vane's mapmaker's workshop, which he now stood before on a grey morning in late autumn, told him something else entirely: it was locked from within.

He knocked twice, evenly, the way a man knocks when his time is valuable but not urgent.

From inside came a small commotion — a stool leg scraping stone, the whisper of paper being gathered, a pause too long to be innocent and too short to be guilty. Then footsteps, light and quick, and the bolt drawn back.

Silas Crane stood in the doorway with lamplight behind her and the river fog at her back, or rather he stood in the fog and she in the light; either way the arrangement suited her better than him. She was smaller than he remembered, thinner, with ink worked so deeply into the creases of her fingers that her hands looked like they belonged to an older woman — a woman who had been holding pens for fifty years instead of perhaps five. Her hair was pinned up carelessly, strands escaping at her temples, and there was a smudge of Prussian blue along her jaw like a bruise she'd earned honestly.

"Magistrate Hale." Her voice was level. Not warm, not cold. Level, the way a spirit level is level: a tool for measuring whether things were true.

"Mistress Crane." He removed his hat — courtesy costs nothing and buys much — and stepped past her into the workshop before she could decide whether to invite him. It was an old habit. He had found that most people's hospitality was not conviction but surprise, and surprise could be walked through if one moved briskly enough.

The workshop smelled as it always had, though he had only been inside twice while Vane lived: size and lamp oil and the mineral bite of fresh ink, and under all of it the river smell that clung to everything in Vellamouth like a second skin. The long bench ran beneath the south windows where the light was steadiest. Papers were stacked at its far end with suspicious neatness. A half-finished sheet lay in the center, weighted at the corners with river stones worn smooth — Vane's old habit, Hale remembered, because lead weights stained the vellum and brass marked it.

The atlas was not visible. That interested him more than if it had been.

"I won't waste your morning," Hale said, settling himself onto the visitor's stool — Vane's stool, actually; apprentices stood, masters sat, guests took what they were given. "Nor mine. I've come with a commission."

"Vane's commissions ended with Vane," Silas said. She remained standing by the door, one hand still resting on the bolt, as though she might yet show him out by the simple mechanism of refusing him entrance retroactively.

"And yet the workshop is open, the bench is lit, and your fingers are blue to the knuckle. You're working, Mistress Crane. Work requires pay. Pay requires patrons. I am offering to be yours."

He let that sit. He had learned long ago that silence was a form of leverage most men never learned to use, because most men could not bear their own company for ten seconds at a stretch. Hale could bear his own company indefinitely. He did it every evening over supper, and found it agreeable.

Silas crossed to the bench — not quickly, not slowly — and began tidying papers that were already tidy. "What commission?"

"The county." He drew a folded document from inside his coat and laid it on the bench between them, squaring its edges to the bench edge with two fingers. "The quarter-sheets in circulation are forty years old. Roads have moved. Bridges have fallen. Marrowfleet—" he said the name the way one says any name, flatly, a coin dropped on a table "—is gone from them entirely, which is accurate but untidy. The bench has voted funds for a new county map. Corrected. Current. Authoritative. Drawn here, in this workshop, by the late master's own apprentice, working from his materials."

"His materials," Silas repeated.

"His surveys. His notes. His plates." Hale smiled the smile he used at dinners when someone made a joke about taxes. "You would be paid handsomely. Twenty pounds on acceptance, twenty on delivery. Your name engraved beneath the title cartouche — *drawn by S. Crane* — which is more immortality than most people in this town will get, and cheaper than most."

She looked at the document without touching it. He watched her eyes move across it and saw the exact moment she reached the clause he had written third, after two pages of generous-sounding preamble: *all source materials to remain the property of the Commission upon completion, together with all drafts, notes, and working papers.*

"You want the atlas," she said.

"I want a county map," Hale said pleasantly. "If Vane left materials toward one, the Commission sees no reason they should molder in a rented room above a chandler's shop. Public works belong to the public."

"He wasn't making a county map."

"No?"

"He was making an atlas of the river. Coasts, channels, soundings. It's nothing like a road survey."

"Then you'll find little of use in it," Hale said, "and can surrender it without loss. Unless—" and here he allowed the faintest tilt of his head, the smallest opening of a door onto a darker room, "—there is some reason the materials should not come into the Commission's keeping."

The fog pressed against the windows. Somewhere down the lane a cart passed, iron-shod wheels loud on cobbles, then fading. Silas set down the papers she had been straightening and looked at him directly for the first time since he'd entered. Her eyes were grey-brown, river-colored, and they held something he recognized from interrogations conducted in better-lit rooms: a person deciding, in real time, how much of the truth they could afford to carry on their face.

"Master Vane's papers are his legacy," she said. "I'm finishing what he started. When the atlas is done, it will be published properly, through the guild, with the guild's approval. If the Commission wants a copy of anything relevant to the county roads, I'll gladly provide extracts. But the originals stay here."

Too smooth. That was his first thought, and his second, and it troubled him precisely because it should have satisfied him. A guilty woman refused clumsily. A frightened woman refused with excuses. An innocent woman — and here Hale paused, because he had met perhaps three innocent women in his life and none of them had run workshops — an innocent woman asked questions, fumbled, flushed, said *let me ask the guild*, said *I don't know*, said *yes*. Silas Crane did none of these. She declined the way a ferryman declines a passenger in flood season: courteously, firmly, and with the clear understanding that the matter was already decided and further discussion was weather.

Which meant she knew. Or she suspected. Or — the third possibility, the one that kept him awake on ordinary nights and would keep him awake on this one — she was simply her master's student to the bone, and Vane had taught her that a refusal given cleanly revealed less than a refusal given stumbling.

"Extracts," Hale repeated. He rose from the stool, unhurried, and walked the length of the bench, looking at nothing and everything: the grinding stone, the jar of goose quills sorted by cut, the burnisher wrapped in flannel, the row of ink pots labeled in Vane's precise upright hand — *lampblack, oak gall, indigo, sepia* — and, at the end, one pot unlabeled, its contents a green-black shade he did not recognize. New ink. Mixed recently. For what purpose did an apprentice mix a new ink?

"A fine shop," he said. "He kept it well. Kept *you* well, I think. He spoke of you, near the end. At the guild dinners, when the wine had gone round. Said he'd found a mind like a graver — that you cut true lines the first time." He turned back to her. "It would grieve him, wherever he is, to think his last work might be... mishandled. By grief. By inexperience. By ambition. There are so many ways good work goes wrong, Mistress Crane, and almost all of them are forgivable. Almost."

"I'll take my chances with forgiveness."

"Yes," Hale said softly. "People do."

He put on his hat. At the door he stopped, and — because a man in his position must always leave something behind, some token that could be read afterward as generosity or as warning depending on how events unfolded — he drew a coin from his purse and set it on the corner of the bench. A half-crown. Good silver, bright from the mint, the king's profile unblemished.

"For your time," he said. "Consultations are billable. Even refused ones."

"That isn't necessary."

"Nothing necessary has ever happened in this town," Hale said. "Everything that happens here is chosen." He opened the door onto the fog and the smell of the river, and paused there, half in the grey light, letting the cold air move past him into the warm shop. "I'll tell you something for free, since you won't take the silver's advice. Winter is coming. The bridge closes. The river freezes, and frozen rivers are honest — everything on them moves slowly, and everyone can see everyone else coming. Come spring, the ice breaks all at once, and things that have waited all winter come downstream in a rush, and some of them are worth catching and some are only wreckage. The wise man stands on the bank in February and asks himself which he'll be." He touched his hat brim. "Good day, Mistress Crane. Consider the commission. Offers of this kind do not improve with keeping."

The bolt slid home behind him before his boot had fully cleared the threshold. He noted that too. A woman who barred her door at eleven in the morning in a town where no one had been robbed in nine years was a woman guarding something, and whatever she guarded, she had begun guarding recently — since his last visit, perhaps, or since the night Tam Ossler pulled a drowned mapmaker out of the shallows below the weir and everyone agreed, in that comfortable collective way towns have, that old men and strong currents and dark water needed no further explanation.

Hale walked back up the lane with his collar up, and the fog closed around him like a page turning.

* * *

In his office above the guild hall, with the shutters open to the grey afternoon and a fire going against the damp, Hale took out a clean sheet and wrote three lines in his private shorthand — a cipher of his own devising, taught to no clerk, known to no living soul:

*Crane refuses. Too readily. Atlas present, hidden. Watch the shop. Watch the girl. Watch the ferryman.*

He read the lines twice, then fed the paper to the fire and watched the words curl and blacken and rise.

The trouble with Silas Crane — and he had anticipated this trouble, had named it in his own mind months ago, standing at Vane's funeral in a respectable black coat while the guild murmured about the sad waste of a great talent — the trouble was that she had been made by Aldous Vane, and Aldous Vane had been the finest technical mind the county survey ever produced, and fine technical minds did not panic, did not bluster, did not make the small human errors that made other people manageable. They planned. They measured. They drew their lines true the first time.

And a line drawn true, once seen, could not be unseen.

Hale poured a measure of brandy and did not drink it. Through the window he could see the river, low and pewter-colored between its banks, already skinning with ice at the margins where the current slackened. In six weeks, seven, the bridge would close and the town would fold inward like a hand closing over a coin, and nothing would enter or leave except by the ice itself, which bore carts and bore men and bore, occasionally, secrets across its white miles — until thaw, when everything the winter had carried came apart at once.

He thought about the girl's hands. Ink to the knuckle. Working, even now, even this morning, with the wolf at the door wearing a patron's coat. She was copying something. Preparing something. A girl alone with her dead master's papers and a steady hand was either building a monument or building a case, and Hale had survived thirty years in public office by assuming, whenever both interpretations were available, the worse one — and by moving first.

There was a legal instrument for shops like hers. Stolen guild property, unlicensed practice, irregular custody of a deceased member's effects. Paper existed for exactly this purpose; that was why paper existed. It would need a warrant, and warrants needed signatures, and signatures were the currency in which he was richest.

But not yet. A search that found nothing was worse than no search at all; it taught the quarry the shape of the trap. First the warrant would be drafted and shown around, mentioned at dinner, allowed to reach her ears the way news reaches everyone in Vellamouth — sideways, and slightly early. Let her run. A guilty woman with a satchel full of stolen papers, running for the bridge or the ferry in broad daylight, needing only to be stopped once, searched once, seen once —

Or let her hide it. Hiding was its own confession, and hidden things could be found.

Hale drank the brandy at last, one swallow, and set the glass down with a click.

On the bench in her workshop, the half-crown lay where he had left it, bright as a small cold moon. Payment or warning. By spring, everyone in Vellamouth would know which it had been.

Downriver, the ice was beginning to speak — thin clicks and sighs in the dark, the sound a frozen river makes when it is dreaming about breaking.
