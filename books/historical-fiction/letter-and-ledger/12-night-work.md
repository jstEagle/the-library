## 12. Night Work

The candle was a bad one — tallow, bought from the chandler on Cordwainer Lane because it was a halfpenny cheaper than wax, and it burned with the smell of a butcher's shop and a guttering flame that leaned toward every draft. Silas had told herself she would buy wax candles when the work mattered. The work mattered now. She had simply run out of the kind of money that buys light worth working by.

She trimmed the wick with her thumbnail and pulled the ledger out of its hiding place beneath the false bottom of Vane's plan chest — the drawer where he had always kept his finest pens, though she had learned in three months of grieving him that he had never kept his finest pens there at all.

The workshop at night was a different country from the workshop by day. By day it smelled of size and hide glue and the iron-gall bite of fresh ink; by night those smells went down into the wood of the benches and came up again older, darker, like the memory of themselves. The great south window threw a grey rectangle of frost-lit sky onto the floorboards. The river made its small persistent noise against the pilings below, patient as a clerk waiting to be paid.

Silas sat down at Vane's own bench, not hers. She had avoided it for weeks without admitting why. It felt like sitting in a pulpit. But tonight she needed the good lamp-angle of the north window, and the wide drawing surface scarred with forty years of compass points, and above all she needed to be close to what remained of his hands.

She opened the ledger.

It was smaller than she remembered. That was the trick of terrible things, she thought — they shrank when you looked at them directly, became almost ordinary, a sewn gathering of rag paper no bigger than a prayer book, its boards scuffed at the corners as if it had been carried in a coat pocket for years. Which, she supposed, it had been. She had found it inside the atlas binding only days ago, and since then she had taken it out and put it back perhaps fifty times, like a tongue returning to a broken tooth.

*The water was bought.* His ciphered note in the margin. She had dismissed it once as an old man's paranoia, the sourness of a craftsman passed over for commissions. She could not dismiss it now. Three names signed for payments across eleven pages. Corven Hale. Aldous Weir. Mistress Josiah Fenwick, who signed with a neat merchant's X and the word *per procurationem* after it, as though legality were a thing you could stitch onto theft like braid onto a uniform.

And the last entry, dated the fourth day of the week before Marrowfleet drowned: *To the works at the sluice, for labour rendered* — forty pounds, the largest single sum in the book, entered in a hand so steady it might have been written by a man counting out coins at his own table while the rain began.

Silas dipped her pen. She had cut a quill that afternoon from one of the last goose feathers in the jar, and it was a good quill, responsive, and she hated how much pleasure she took in it, how the craft of the thing still sang in her even as the purpose of the thing curdled.

Her task was simple and enormous: copy the ledger entire, every entry, every date, every signature, in her own hand but faithful to the original line by line, so that if the original were seized or burned there would remain a witness. Vane had taught her copying in her first year — *a copy is not a lesser map, Crane, it is a map that can survive fire* — and she had thought then that he meant floods and candle accidents and the ordinary mortality of paper. She had not known he was preparing her for this.

She worked. The candle guttered and she fed it. Outside, a dog barked twice near the toll road and stopped, and the silence afterward was louder than the bark. Her breath made a small ghost over the page. The entries came under her pen in their grim procession: sums for timber never delivered to the levee; sums marked *contingency* that corresponded to nothing in any guild record; sums paid to men whose names she recognized from the garrison roster Nell had recited to her like a litany — Brack excepted, thank God, Brack clean — and each entry she copied twice, once in fair hand and once abbreviated into a cipher of her own devising, so that the copy itself would be useless to anyone who seized it without the key, and the key lived nowhere but in her head.

It was going well. That was the horror of it, looking back. It was going *well*, the way a crossing goes well until the ice speaks.

She was two-thirds through, deep into the third gathering, when the handwriting changed.

She noticed it the way a musician notices a wrong note — not with the mind first but with the body, a flinch along the forearm, a wrongness in the rhythm of the letters before she understood what the letters said. The previous entry had been written in the round, confident secretarial script that ran through most of the book, the hand of a man employed to keep accounts. This entry leaned. It slanted hard to the right, the ascenders tall and nervous, the descenders of the *g*s and *y*s dragged into long tails like things trying to escape downward off the page.

She knew that hand.

She had spent six years under it. Six years of correction marks in the margins of her practice charts, six years of *Crane, your coastlines wander — commit, girl, commit* written in exactly this slant, this impatient tall ascension, these tails. She had watched this hand lay the final ink on the atlas coastline three months ago, or rather she had laid it herself, tracing his intentions, learning his letterforms so thoroughly that she could forge them — and had forged them, only days ago, on a guild travel pass, weeping over the signature.

*Aldous Vane,* the entry read. *Received, thirty pounds.*

Dated the eleventh of October. Nine days before the sluice gates closed. Nine days before Marrowfleet drowned.

Silas set the pen down very carefully, as if the bench were moving.

The candle flame stood perfectly still, and in that stillness she saw the whole shape of the thing at once, the way you see a coastline complete from a height you cannot climb: the payments flowing into the ledger month after month, Hale's name recurring like a tide mark; and then, nine days before the flood, Vane's own name entered among the rest — thirty pounds, received — and then the entries after it thinning, the amounts shrinking, the annotations beginning, cramped into the margins in that same leaning hand: *no works undertaken at the levee — queried*; *Weir declines to answer*; *the gates are to be shut, I am told, for the mill race — whose mill?*

He had taken the money first.

She read it again. And again. As if repetition might alter it, the way a map redrawn ten times might finally match the territory. It did not alter. Thirty pounds, received, Aldous Vane, in his own unforgeable hand, dated nine days before forty-one people went into the water at Marrowfleet and did not come out.

*No,* she said aloud, to the empty workshop, and the word fell flat on the floorboards like a dropped tool.

Because she knew the rest — she had lived the rest from the other side. She knew the scraped-away name on the atlas page, MARROWFLEET effaced so thoroughly the vellum was thin as skin there. She knew the ciphered marginal note, *the water was bought*, which she had called paranoia. She knew the ledger hidden in the binding, which a man hides when he intends to use it. He had taken the money, and then he had begun, in his careful cartographer's fashion, to build the case against himself. He had copied nothing cleanly, committed nothing openly, signed no confession — he had done what he always did, what he had taught *her* to do: he had recorded. Observed. Measured. Left the truth in the margins where a trained eye would find it and an untrained one would pass over.

Perhaps he had meant to go to the assizes in spring. Perhaps he had meant to confront Hale. Perhaps he had simply frozen, as she was freezing now, between the man he'd been and the act required of the man he'd discovered himself to be. And then the river had settled the question for him, on a night in November, after an argument Tam Ossler had witnessed with Magistrate Hale.

Or Hale had settled it.

She could not know. That was the punishment, she realized: not the guilt itself but the permanent suspension of knowing. If Vane had died innocent, she could have mourned him purely. If he had died a coward, she could have condemned him cleanly. Instead he had died in the middle, mid-stroke, an unfinished coastline, and she was left holding both the crime and the repentance in the same pair of hands, and there was nowhere to put either down.

* * *

The candle burned down past the point where a sensible person would replace it. Silas did not replace it. She sat in the shrinking circle of light and let the cold come up through the bench into her bones, and she took the ledger apart in her mind the way she had once taken apart a damaged astrolabe, piece by piece, to understand the mechanism.

Thirty pounds. In 1747, thirty pounds was a year's wages for a skilled man. It was new boots and coal through winter and meat twice a week. She thought about what it had bought Vane, materially. Nothing visible. The workshop was the same shabby, magnificent clutter it had always been — the same cracked leather globe with the Indies worn away by thumbs, the same jars of quills sorted by stiffness, the same stove that ate coal and gave back half the heat. He had not drunk — she had never seen him drink anything stronger than small beer. He had not gambled. Where had the money gone?

And then, with a lurch she felt physically, in the stomach, in the throat: *her.*

Six years ago she had been nothing — a parish orphan with quick eyes and dirty cuffs, set to scrubbing the guild hall floors, who had lingered too long watching the mapmakers and been caught by the sharpest-eyed of them tracing coastlines in spilled salt. He had taken her as apprentice against the objection of half the guild. Apprentices cost. Food, clothing, the schooling fee, the premium paid to the guild for her articles — she had never asked who paid it, because orphans learn early not to ask who pays for them. She had assumed charity, some faceless benevolence of the town.

Thirty pounds was very nearly the price of an apprenticeship.

She put her hand flat on the table to stop it shaking. No. No, that was not proof, that was grief making patterns the way frost makes ferns on glass — beautiful, meaningless. Men took bribes and did not spend them on orphan girls. And yet the thought would not leave; it lay down beside her like a second body in a narrow bed. Because she remembered — she had never forgotten, she had only never examined it — the night she finished her probationary chart, the survey of the Vellamouth reach, and Vane had held it to the lamp for a long time and said, quietly, not in his teaching voice but in another voice she had no name for yet:

"You have to be more accurate than the honest people, Crane. The honest ones can afford errors. We can't."

She had thought he meant: we who are poor. She had thought it was a lesson about precision being the poor man's honor.

Now she heard the other meaning underneath, the way you hear the second voice in a round: *we* — we who have something to hide. Accuracy as absolution. Every exact measurement a small payment against a debt. A map is a promise — yes, and some promises are made to buy back a betrayal.

Had she been accuracy? Was she the payment?

She laughed, once, a short ugly sound that startled her, and pressed her knuckles against her mouth. The candle was a stub now, drowning in its own grease, and the shadows had come up around the benches like water filling a hold.

* * *

She should stop. That was the clear thought, the sensible thought, arriving with the coldest hour: stop copying, close the ledger, put it back in the binding, hang the wall map straight tomorrow and be a mapmaker, only a mapmaker, and let Marrowfleet stay scraped off the world. Forty-one people were dead either way. Her copying would not raise one of them. And the danger — she had felt Hale's courtesy like a blade wrapped in wool, she had seen the coin he left on her bench and understood it was a price quoted — the danger was not abstract. Men who would close sluice gates on a sleeping village would not hesitate over a woman with ink-stained fingers.

But underneath the sensible thought was another, older, installed in her like the iron rod is installed in a building's frame, bearing load she had never noticed until now: *you do not stop a chart because the weather turns.* You do not abandon a survey halfway because the country proves uglier than the commission suggested. You finish. You finish or the work dies unfinished, and an unfinished map has killed sailors, she had heard him say, has killed soldiers, has killed whole settlements that built their houses on a guess.

And — she made herself look at it squarely, in the last of the light — if she stopped now, then Vane's whole design failed. Not his atlas. His repentance. Whatever mixture of cowardice and courage had produced that leaning hand and those cramped marginal queries, the ledger was the only honest thing he had left behind, the only place where the truth of Marrowfleet still existed in ink. To bury it was to make his death mean nothing — to confirm that the bribe had succeeded after all, merely delayed by thirty years and one dead man's conscience.

She relit the candle from the stub. New wick, same cheap tallow, same butcher-shop smell. She sharpened the quill.

"Then I'll carry both," she said to the dark, to him, to whatever of him was in the room — and she found she could not decide whether she meant both names on the atlas, both truths in the ledger, or both halves of him, the man who took the money and the man who wrote *whose mill?* in a shaking margin. All of it. She would carry all of it. That was apparently what inheritance was: not the shop, not the skill, but the whole weight, undivided.

She turned back to the changed entry and copied it last of all, deliberately, though chronology argued otherwise. She drew the words *Aldous Vane — received, thirty pounds* in her own hand, letter by letter, and it was the hardest writing she had ever done, harder than the forged pass, because the forgery had betrayed a system and this betrayed a love. Her pen slowed on *received*. She made herself render it accurately — the date, the sum, the name — because accuracy was the whole point, was the only religion she had ever been given, and a falsified record, however merciful, was just another closed sluice gate.

Then, beneath it, in her own hand, in her own voice, she added a note that no court would require and no archive would expect:

*Copied faithfully, including this entry. The hand that received the payment is the hand that preserved the proof. Let the judge weigh both.*

She blotted it, and sat looking at the two names — his in the old entry, hers in the new — side by side on one line of one page, closer together than they had ever stood on any title page.

The candle was burning low again. Through the frost-furred window the sky had gone from black to the grey-black that precedes neither dawn nor hope, only the mechanical turning of the world. And it was then, folding the ledger closed, that she saw it — a thing she must have touched a hundred times and never read: pressed faintly into the inside of the back board, as if a pen had been rested there too long, the ghost impression of a few lines of writing. Not ink. Indentation only, legible to fingers and raking light, the way a coastline survives under later surveys.

She tilted the board toward the candle. The grooves caught the light and shadowed, and letter by letter, in Vane's leaning, escaping hand, a sentence surfaced out of the paper like a drowned thing rising:

*If you are reading this, Crane, I am sorry — and I was always going to be sorry, which is worse.*

Below it, half-effaced by the sewing thread of the binding, the beginnings of something more — a direction, a place, the letters spelling out the first syllables of a name she almost knew.

Silas held the board to the flame and read faster, her heart going like a ferryman's oars, because outside, far down the toll road, a horse was coming through the frozen dark at a pace no honest rider keeps before dawn.
