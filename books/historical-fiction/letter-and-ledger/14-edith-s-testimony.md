## 14. Edith's Testimony

The poorhouse smelled of boiled cabbage and wet wool, the way it always did in winter when they kept the windows sealed against the cold and let the damp have its way with everything else. Edith Marr sat in her corner of the women's room with her sewing basket on her knees, mending a shirt that belonged to no one in particular — one of the master's cast-offs passed down through four men until it reached her, frayed at the elbows, worth more as rag than garment. She sewed because sewing kept her hands from shaking. Her hands had wanted to shake for nine years.

Nine years since Marrowfleet. Nine years since the water came up the lane like something that had been waiting politely outside a door, and then stopped waiting.

She had learned, in the poorhouse, to be small. It was not so different from what she'd been before — a fisherman's wife learns to be small, learns to fold herself around weather, around seasons, around the moods of a man who has come home empty-handed three weeks running. But there was a difference between folding yourself around weather and folding yourself around other people's eyes. In Marrowfleet she had been poor but visible. Here she was fed and clothed and invisible, which the matron seemed to consider a fair trade, and which Edith had accepted for nine years without complaint.

Complaint was expensive here. She had watched others learn it. Old Hobbes had complained about the gruel and found his blanket ration halved. Widow Tice had complained about the master's nephew pinching the girls in the laundry and had been moved to the infirmary within the month, and the infirmary was where people went to be quietly forgotten. The poorhouse answered to the parish, and the parish answered to the magistrates, and everyone knew it the way everyone knows where the floorboards are rotten — by feel, by the give underfoot, without ever needing to look down.

So when the girl came — the mapmaker's girl, Crane, with ink still ghosting the creases of her knuckles — and asked, in a voice pitched low enough that only Edith could hear it over the clatter of the room, *tell me about the sluice gates*, Edith had felt the whole architecture of her smallness tremble like a wall in wind.

* * *

That had been two days ago. The girl had not asked her to sign anything then. She had only listened, and listening was itself dangerous; Edith knew that much. A woman who listens is a woman who might repeat. And yet the girl's hands had been steady on the edge of the bench between them, ink-stained and young, and her face had done what faces in this place were trained never to do: it had shown grief without shame.

Edith had told her about the gates. She had not meant to. The words had come up out of her like water finding a crack — nine years held behind a door and then the door simply gone.

They had closed them, she said. The sluice gates above the weir. On the night the river rose, someone shut them, and the water that should have spread thin across the flats came instead down the single channel straight at the village, and it came fast, faster than any flood she'd known, and there was no time. There was never any time after that night. There was only before and after.

She had said all this in a whisper, looking down at her mending, and the girl had written nothing, had carried nothing away but the words themselves, and Edith had gone back to her shirt and her cabbage smell and her smallness, and had lain awake half the night listening to the wind worry the shutters and thinking: *they will know. Someone will tell the matron. Someone always tells.*

No one told. Two days passed, and no one told, and Edith began to understand that the silence itself was a kind of answer — that the world outside her smallness had not yet noticed her at all, and would not, unless she made it notice.

And now the girl was coming back. Edith could feel it the way you feel weather change in an old wound.

* * *

She came at the visiting hour, wrapped in a grey coat too big for her, carrying a covered basket as though she'd brought bread for the inmates, which some did, sometimes, out of guilt or piety or both. The matron signed her in with the bored efficiency of a woman whose charity was a ledger entry, and the girl walked the length of the room to Edith's corner as though she'd rehearsed it — no hesitation, no glancing about, just a direct and unremarkable progress that Edith recognized with a jolt as the walk of someone who had studied how not to be seen moving toward a thing.

"You've brought your work," the girl said, nodding at the basket. "Good. We can talk while you sew."

"I can talk while I do anything," Edith said. "It's the one skill poverty perfects."

The girl almost smiled. Then she set the basket down between them, opened it, and took out paper. Three sheets, folded once, and a small pot of ink, and a pen with a worn nib. She had written something already — Edith could see the close, careful hand through the fold, the hand of a person trained to make letters sit straight.

"What is it?"

"A statement." The girl kept her voice level, conversational, the voice of two women discussing hems. "Of what you told me. What happened the night of the flood. Your name at the bottom, and the date. Nothing more."

"And who reads it?"

"The assize court. Eventually."

"Eventually," Edith repeated. The word tasted like the gruel — technically nourishment, practically an insult. "And before eventually? Who reads it while it waits?"

The girl didn't lie to her. Edith watched her decide not to, saw the little flinch of honesty cross her face like cloud-shadow over water.

"Anyone who finds it," the girl said. "I'll keep it hidden. But hidden isn't safe. Hidden is only hidden."

"That's more than most things manage." Edith took the sheets. She read them slowly, though her reading was slow anyway, self-taught late in life, letter by letter the way her husband had taught her from a psalter on Sunday evenings when he'd had drink in him and patience to match. The girl had put it plainly. No flourishes. *On the night of the 14th of November, in the year 1738, the sluice gates above the weir at Marrowfleet were closed. I saw the torchlight of men at the gatehouse. I heard the timbers drop.* Plain, and dated, and signed nowhere yet.

"You wrote that I saw torchlight," Edith said. "I told you I thought I saw torchlight."

"Did you see it?"

Edith looked at the page. Somewhere behind her, a woman coughed the wet racking cough that half the room shared by midwinter. The fire in the great hearth had burned down to its orange bones. Through the frost-furred window she could see the yard, the wall, the road beyond the wall, and beyond the road the dark line of the river valley, and beyond that, invisible, the place where Marrowfleet had been, where forty-one people had been, where her husband had stood in the doorway holding their door shut against the water with his shoulder and his arms and finally his whole body, and lost.

"Yes," she said. "I saw it. Two lanterns at the gatehouse, swinging. I thought at first it was the keeper checking the gears. But the keeper was drowned that night with the rest of us, wasn't he. He'd no reason to be up at the gates closing them and every reason to be home moving his own furniture upstairs." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, a voice she hadn't used in years, and it frightened her a little, how easily it came back. Grief, it turned out, did not wear out with use. It only waited.

"Then that's what the statement says."

"They'll say I'm mad." Edith folded the pages along their crease, precisely, the way she folded everything. "A poorhouse woman, nine years past, half-blind — they'll say the grief turned my head. They'll find neighbors who remember me weeping in the lanes. They'll find plenty. I wept everywhere for two years. I'm not ashamed of it, but they'll use it."

"They may."

"And you'll take the statement anyway."

"Yes," the girl said.

Edith studied her. Young, but not foolish-young — or foolish in the particular way that the best people were foolish, the way her husband had been foolish, going back into the water for the Prentice boy when any sensible man could see the current had changed its mind. There was a word for that kind of foolishness in Marrowfleet and the word was *decent*, and decency killed her husband, and she had spent nine years trying to cure herself of missing it.

"If I sign," Edith said, "you understand what happens to me if it goes wrong."

"I understand."

"No. You understand what you're afraid of happening. That's different. Let me tell you what actually happens." She kept her voice low and even, the seamstress's voice, the voice that wouldn't carry. "The matron gets a note from the magistrate's office. Very polite. Concerned for my health. And I'm moved to the infirmary, and the infirmary is warm and quiet and nobody visits, and one morning there's a fever, and one evening there's a burial in the parish plot with my name spelled wrong on the board. I've seen it done twice. Once for a man who witnessed a theft at the corn mill, once for a woman who knew something about the master's nephew. Nobody asks questions about the dead in the parish plot. The dead are the most respectable people here."

The girl had gone very still. Her hands, Edith noticed, had curled slightly on her knees, the ink-stained fingers pressing into the wool of her skirt as though gripping a gunwale.

"So I won't keep it here," Edith went on. "Whatever you imagine — under the mattress, sewn in my cloak — no. Anything on my body or in my bed is found the first time they turn out our corners, and they turn out corners whenever they please. If I take your papers, I choose where they live. Agreed?"

"Agreed," the girl said, and Edith believed her, and believed also that the girl understood the agreement cost her something — that she was handing her witness over to a witness's judgment, and witnesses judged harshly; they had survived by knowing exactly what could go wrong.

Edith picked up the pen. The nib was dry-ish; she touched it to the inkpot the girl had brought and wiped the excess against the rim, a motion so old and practiced it required no thought at all, and it occurred to her, in the strange sideways way thoughts arrive at such moments, that the last thing she had signed was the poorhouse admission roll, nine years ago, her name wobbling onto a list among paupers and vagrants and widows, the name of a woman who had owned a house and a boat-share and a husband and had, by the end of that single line of ink, none of them.

She signed now beneath the girl's plain sentences. *Edith Marr, widow, of Marrowfleet.* She wrote *of Marrowfleet* even though the girl hadn't, even though the form had space only for her present dwelling. She added it because it mattered. Because for nine years the records had called her *of Vellamouth Poorhouse*, as though Marrowfleet had never existed anywhere except in the mouths of survivors, and she would not let her own hand add its weight to the erasure.

The letters came out steadier than she expected. Her hands had wanted to shake for nine years, and now, with the shaking finally given somewhere to go, they held still.

"There," she said, and heard how thin her voice had gone. She cleared her throat. "Now. Where does it live?"

* * *

She had thought about it for two days, in the wordless way she thought about everything — not with arguments but with images, the way a woman who has mended the same shirts for decades knows fabric by touch before she knows it by name.

Her sewing basket went everywhere with her and was searched with everything else; ruled out. Her cloak lining: ruled out, cloaks were taken for delousing each spring. Under the floorboard by her cot: there was no loose floorboard; she'd checked years ago, out of an instinct she'd been ashamed of then and was grateful for now.

But there was the basket itself.

It was an old basket, willow, older than her marriage — her mother's before her. Over the years its weave had begun to fail, and she had repaired it as she repaired everything, working new willow splits into the old pattern, and at the base, near the handle-socket where the stress was worst, she had built up a double layer, a patch woven over a patch. Between those two layers there was a space. Not a hiding place — nothing so deliberate. Just the ordinary hollow that exists inside all mended things, the gap between the wound and the repair.

She worked the folded statement into that gap while the girl watched, her fingers doing the weaving blind, by touch, drawing three fresh splits of willow across the opening with quick economical motions, tucking the ends, pressing the patch flat. It took less than a minute. When she turned the basket over, the bottom showed nothing but an old woman's careful repairs, the kind of mending that makes an object uglier and keeps it alive.

"The matron's clerk went through it in the autumn," Edith said. "Lifted the lid, poked the wool, shook it upside down over the table. He never looked at the base. Nobody looks at the base. People look where things are kept, and things are kept in the middle of baskets, not underneath them."

"It's good work," the girl said quietly. "The weaving. My master would have—" She stopped.

"He'd have approved?" Edith finished for her, and something crossed the girl's face that was not quite pain and not quite laughter. "You loved him, your master. It's all over you. Like ink."

The girl looked down at her stained fingers as if seeing them for the first time. "He drowned," she said. "In October."

"I know. Tam Ossler brought him in. Tam brings everyone in." Edith smoothed the last split of willow flat. Outside the frost-furred window the light was failing, blue hour settling over the yard, and somewhere in the building a bell would ring soon for supper, and the day would end the way days ended here, indistinguishable, one grey bead on a grey string. "You think they drowned him, don't you. The magistrates. You think your master was murdered over whatever he knew."

The girl was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was careful as her lettering. "I don't know what I think. I know what's in his atlas, and I know what's in the margins, and I know he argued with Corven Hale the night he died, and I know that's four facts that fit together too well to be comfortable and not well enough to prove."

"That's how murder usually fits," Edith said. "Like a patch that almost matches the original weave. Close enough that only someone who made the first basket can see the difference." She lifted the basket to her knee, felt the small stiff weight of the folded papers against the wood, already becoming part of the object, part of the ordinary. "I'll carry it. I'll mend over it and mend over it, and if anyone comes for me — if they move me to the infirmary, if they spell my name wrong on a board — then you come and you cut the base out of this basket, and you take what's mine to the court yourself. Promise me that. Not that you'll protect me. I'm past protecting. Promise me the papers get there whether I do or not."

"I promise," the girl said, and Edith believed her, because the girl said it the way people say true things — too plainly, with none of the ornament a lie requires.

The supper bell rang, cracked and tuneless, and the room began its slow migration toward the benches. The girl rose, gathered her empty basket and her inkpot, and paused.

"Why?" she asked. It burst out of her, unpolished, younger than anything else she'd said. "Nine years of keeping quiet. Why sign now?"

Edith looked at the window, at the last grey light on the wall, at the direction — any direction — of the drowned village.

"Because quiet is a debt too," she said. "I've paid it nine years running, and it's never once been marked down. Time to stop paying the wrong account."

The girl nodded, once, and went — that same rehearsed, unremarkable walk, unhurried, invisible — out through the door and into the blue cold.

Edith sat alone in the corner with the basket on her knees and the room draining around her, and she pressed her thumb lightly against the base of it, against the hidden crispness of the folded paper, the way you press a bruise to confirm it's real. Forty-one names. One of them hers by marriage, one of them hers by heartbreak, all of them hers by survival, which was not the blessing people took it for.

Then the matron's key turned in the far door, and the evening began, and Edith Marr picked up her needle and her mending and became again the small, invisible woman the poorhouse required — except that now, under the willow and the wool and the nine years, she carried the one thing in Vellamouth that could not be mended over, and waited, stitching steadily in the lamplight, for whatever came next through the door.
