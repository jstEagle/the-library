## 17. Hiding the Atlas

The warrant came at dusk, folded twice and delivered by a boy who would not meet her eyes.

Silas stood in the doorway of the workshop long after his footsteps faded down Copper Lane, the paper held loosely between two fingers as though it were something dead — a bird brought in by a cat, offered up without apology. The light behind her was candlelight; the light before her was the last grey of the day going blue over the rooftops toward the river. Between those two lights she read the words again, though she had already memorized them on the first reading.

*Whereas it hath been represented to the Worshipful Magistrates of Vellamouth that certain instruments, surveys, and records appertaining to the Guild of Mapwrights have been unlawfully removed from the Guild Hall; it is therefore ordered that the premises of the late Aldous Vane, mapmaker, be searched for the recovery of said property...*

*Two days.*

He had given her two days. Corven Hale, who could have come with soldiers and a locksmith inside an hour if he had truly wanted the workshop turned out, had instead sent her a courtesy wrapped in legal language. And Silas understood the gift for what it was: not mercy but a trap with generous proportions. If she ran now, tonight, with satchel and lantern, she would be seen leaving, and a woman seen fleeing before a search has only one meaning. Guilt was not proven by flight, the law said — but men did not need proof to believe what they wished, and Hale wished it thoroughly.

So she would stay. And everything he wanted would be here when he came for it — which meant her work, these next two days, was to make certain that *here* was no longer where anything was.

She closed the door. She slid the bolt, then the wooden bar Vane had installed years ago against flood season, when the river sometimes came up Copper Lane uninvited. The bar settled into its brackets with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Then she turned and looked at her master's workshop as if seeing it for the first time as a thing that could be taken apart.

* * *

It was strange, she thought later, how a room changes once you imagine it stripped.

For three months this place had been grief made architectural: his stool with its worn crescent in the seat, his spectacles folded on the ledge by the north window where he'd left them the morning he walked down to the ferry and did not come back. She had moved nothing. Dust had been permitted to gather like a second apprentice, one who worked slowly and asked no questions and kept everything exactly as it was. She had told herself this was mourning. Now, holding the warrant, she understood it had also been cowardice dressed as devotion — because a room untouched is a room unexamined, and she had not wanted to examine it.

She lit three more candles. She set them at intervals around the bench so that no single shadow could own the space, a habit drilled into her from her first week — *never judge a line by lamplight alone, girl; every light lies from one side.* Then she took the atlas down from the drawer beneath the plan chest, unwrapped the oilcloth, and laid the ledger beside it.

The ledger was small enough to disappear into a coat pocket and damning enough to hang a town. Forty pages, perhaps fewer. Payments entered in three hands, signed by three names she now knew better than her own pulse: Corven Hale. Josiah Fenn. Alderman Mott. Sums that matched, entry by uncanny entry, the relief funds voted by this very bench of magistrates to save Marrowfleet from the river — funds that had never reached the sluice gates, never reached the embankment crews, never reached anything except the pockets of the men whose signatures closed each line. And the final entry, dated eight days before the water came through the gates they had quietly let rust shut.

Forty-one people. She carried the number now the way she carried the ledger — always, everywhere, against the ribs.

She could not keep both the original and her copies in one place. That truth sat in her like swallowed stone. Whatever happened in two days — whether Brack's searchers were honest men doing honest work or Hale's creatures doing dishonest theater — a single hiding place was a single failure away from ending everything. Vane had taught her that too, in gentler terms, about coastlines: *never trust a harbor with one approach. If the wind turns, you're wrecked on your own convenience.*

Two hiding places, then. But where?

Her eyes went first, inevitably, to the floor. There was a cellar hatch behind the plan chest, stone steps down into cold and dark, and she made herself consider it honestly before rejecting it: cellars were the first place any searcher looked, because cellars were where everyone hid things. A loose hearthstone — same failing. Under the floorboards — the boards would show their disturbance to anyone who knew wood, and Brack struck her as a man who knew wood. She walked the room slowly, candle in hand, and let the craft answer where her fear could not.

Fear looks for darkness, she thought. Craft looks for plainness.

And then she stopped, mid-stride, and looked up.

* * *

The wall map dominated the eastern wall the way a mountain dominates a valley. It was Vane's masterwork of the county — six feet of hand-colored paper on linen, mounted on rollers of seasoned oak, showing the whole watershed from the fells above Thistlecombe down to the estuary below Vellamouth. Every farm, every ford, every meander of the river rendered in his patient, loving ink. It had hung there since before Silas came to him as a half-starved orphan of eleven; she had learned to draw, in those early years, by copying pieces of it until her arm ached and her eyes swam. It was the first map she had ever loved and the last thing a visitor saw when they left, because it hung directly opposite the door.

Which was, she realized, standing in the middle of the floor with her heart beginning to move faster, precisely why it had never once been looked at.

People do not study what is permanently in front of them. The map had become furniture, wallpaper, part of the room's bones. In eleven years she could count on one hand the times a visitor had remarked on it, and Hale himself — Hale, who had stood in this room three weeks ago assessing what Vane left behind, whose eyes missed nothing — Hale had glanced at it the way a man glances at a window. Plain sight was not merely concealment. Plain sight was the best-made blind of all, because the quarry walks right past it and calls it scenery.

But a map was paper, and paper could be searched, lifted, held to the light. No. Not the map itself. The map's *frame*. She crossed the room and put her hands to the oak casing, feeling along the bottom rail where the linen rolled under, and found what she was looking for: the hollow. Rollers needed housing, and housing meant depth, and depth meant a channel of air running the width of the bottom bar behind the decorative facing — three fingers deep, four feet long, invisible from outside, accessible only if you knew to lift the lower retaining strip, which was pegged in place with pins so fine and flush that she herself had never noticed them until this moment, when she went looking with a purpose.

Vane had built it, she was almost certain, for storing spare charts. A craftsman's economy, nothing more. But craftsmen leave doors behind them whether they mean to or not, and tonight one of those doors would hold the most dangerous forty pages in the county.

She worked quickly and she worked cleanly. First she waxed the pins — a whisper of beeswax on each head so they would draw without protest and seat again without a sound. Then she measured the ledger against the channel with a pair of dividers, frowning. Too thick by half. Even flattened, even pressed, forty sewn pages would bulge the facing like a swallow under a snake's skin. Anyone with a fingertip would feel it.

She sat back on her heels and thought about thickness the way she thought about coastline: as a problem of reduction that must not become a problem of distortion.

The answer, when it came, was almost insulting in its simplicity. She didn't need the whole ledger hidden whole. She needed the ledger *preserved*. So she would take it apart — gently, reversibly, with a bookbinder's care rather than a butcher's haste — and divide the sections. Half into the frame channel, interleaved with oiled cloth against damp. Half into the atlas itself, sewn back into the binding where they had lived undiscovered for months already, in the very object Hale believed he was hunting, hidden among maps of harbors bought with drowned money.

Split evidence, split risk. If the frame was found, the binding survived. If the binding was found, the frame survived. Neither hiding place knew about the other, and neither could betray it.

Before she cut the first thread she laid the ledger open on the bench and copied, one final time, the last page entire — dates, sums, signatures — onto a slip she folded small. This slip would go into her coat. Because a hiding place can burn, and a coat can be torn off her back, but something committed to memory in her own hand, rehearsed nightly until the numbers lived behind her eyes, was the one copy that no search could reach. Vane had made her memorize scales and bearings for just such reasons. He had never imagined the reasons would turn out to be these.

* * *

Disbinding a book you have sworn to protect is a peculiar agony. Her hands shook at the first cut of the knife under the sewing thread, and she stopped, and pressed her palms flat against the bench, and breathed until they steadied.

*You are not destroying it,* she told herself. *You are dispersing it. Seed grain goes into many furrows so that one bad season doesn't end the harvest.*

The sections came free one by one. She wrapped the first half in oilcloth, then in waxed linen, making a parcel thin as a prayer book. Working on the step-stool, she raised the retaining strip — the waxed pins giving way with barely a sigh — and slid the parcel into the channel behind the oak facing, laying it flat along the full length so that no single spot showed elevation. Then she lowered the strip, seated the pins, and stepped down.

From six feet away, the wall map hung exactly as it had hung for eleven years. From two feet, the same. She ran her palm along the bottom rail and felt nothing but wood and paint and time.

The remaining sections she re-sewed into the atlas's spine, matching the original stitch pattern so faithfully that she caught herself smiling at the irony of it — all those hours learning to reproduce Vane's hand, his knots, his margins, and the skill finally earning its keep in forgery and concealment. When she finished, the atlas lay closed on the bench looking exactly as it had looked that morning: an unfinished masterpiece, valuable, mourned-over, innocent.

She stood back and surveyed the room. Two caches, one memory. It should have felt like safety. It felt instead like having hidden a lit lamp inside a paper house and then being asked to sleep there.

Because here was the flaw she could not engineer away: *she* was still the common factor. Every hiding place pointed back to her. If they arrested her — not searched her, *arrested* her — the frame and the binding would give up their secrets within a day. The evidence could survive a search. It could not yet survive *her*.

Which meant the copies — her true insurance, the duplicates she had bled over for six nights by candlelight — could not stay in this building at all. They had to live on her body, moving when she moved, and eventually they had to leave Vellamouth entirely, bound for the assize court before the thaw opened the ferries and Hale's writ ran fat and easy across the whole waterlogged world.

* * *

She took her oldest coat from its hook — brown wool, mended at both elbows, a garment so unremarkable that it had achieved invisibility the way the wall map had — and spread it across the bench. Then she fetched her sewing box, and a spool of strong linen thread, and a needle, and by candlelight began the slow, finicky surgery of turning a lining into a vault.

She chose the left panel first, over the ribs, where the coat's interlining was thickest. With a razor she parted the seam along the bottom hem-line, working the stitches loose rather than cutting, so the repair afterward would look like repair rather than renovation. Into the gap she fitted the first copy-portfolio: her duplicate ledger pages, trimmed close, wrapped in waxed cloth, sewn flat. Then the second portfolio over the right hip, splitting the weight. Last, flat against the small of her back, the folded statement Edith Marr had pressed on her in the poorhouse parlor, Edith's careful schoolhand spelling out sluice gates and closed paddles and the night the water came — *they shut them, miss, I heard the chains myself, I heard them* — a testimony that weighed less than a letter and more than an anchor.

She stitched everything closed with small, regular, deliberately uneven stitches — the sewing of a busy woman patching wear, not a conspirator building armor. When it was done she put the coat on and stood before the tall looking-glass Vane used for checking the perspective of foreground figures.

A thin woman in a shabby brown coat. That was all. She turned; the glass showed her nothing but a tired apprentice at the end of a long day. She pressed her hands against her ribs and felt, beneath wool and linen, the faint stiffness of paper — felt it only because she knew to feel for it. Would Brack? Brack, who was thorough and humorless and had once made her unwrap every roll of vellum in the plan chest while he watched with eyes like wet slate?

Perhaps. Perhaps. She could reduce that chance but not erase it, and the way to reduce it was practice: she would wear the coat everywhere, sleep in it, learn its altered drape until her body forgot the difference and her movements betrayed nothing. A mapmaker trusted field-tested instruments. Very well. She was now carrying the instrument, and the field would test it soon enough.

She took the coat off, folded it over the chair back, and allowed herself ten seconds — she counted them, deliberately, the way Vane had taught her to time an exposure of sensitized paper — of simply standing in the middle of her ruined, rearranged, re-sewn, re-hidden world.

The candles had burned low. Outside, frost was forming on the panes in fern-patterns, and somewhere down toward the water a dog was barking at the ice. Three days ago this room had been a museum of grief. Tomorrow it would be a stage set, arranged for an audience she despised, every surface performing innocence.

* * *

There remained one thing she could not decide, and it was the thing that kept her at the bench long after she should have slept.

The atlas itself — the true atlas, Vane's life's work with its scraped-away village and its ciphered margins — should she leave it lying openly on the bench, or conceal it too? Every instinct said hide it. But instinct had argued for the cellar an hour ago, and instinct had been wrong. Consider it as Hale would: a search that finds nothing finds a suspicious nothing. A search that finds an incomplete, half-finished, obviously beloved atlas — that finds a grieving apprentice's ordinary disorder — finds a story that makes sense. Let them lift it, flip its pages, see Marrowfleet drawn nameless and think it an old man's sloppiness or sentiment. Let them find exactly the treasure the warrant described and no more.

The best hiding places, she wrote in her head, are the things they expect to steal.

So she left it out. Squared its corners to the edge of the bench, weighted its corners with the flat stones Vane had used, angled it as a working document gets angled when its worker means to return in the morning. She even left his spectacles beside it. Then she cleaned her brushes, stoppered her inks, raked the stove to embers, and stood a long moment at the north window with the candle pinched low, looking out at the dark shape of the river and thinking about the ferryman Tam Ossler, and the toll gate, and the garrison road, and the three walls between her and the assize court — problems for tomorrow, or the day after, problems she would solve the way she had solved tonight's, one bearing at a time.

On the sill, her breath frosted the glass. Below, far off, a lantern moved along the quay — one light, pausing, moving on. Watching, or merely walking home; she couldn't tell, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a splinter.

Two days. Somewhere across the sleeping town, Corven Hale was also awake, she'd have wagered her good pen on it, also counting. The search party would arrive with the morning light the day after next, boots on her floorboards, hands on her master's work.

Let them come, she thought, pinching the last candle. Everything worth saving was either behind oak, inside wool, or locked behind her eyes — and the rest of the room was ready to lie beautifully.

She climbed the stairs to the loft, wearing the coat, and lay down on the narrow bed without undressing. Sleep came slowly and then all at once, heavy as river water.

In the grey hour before dawn she woke once, certain she had heard the front door — but the bar was seated, the frost unbroken on the inside pane, and only the river spoke, shifting its ice, counting down the winter in a language of cracks.
