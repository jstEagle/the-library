## 20. Winter Fair

The river froze hard enough by the second week of the dark months that Vellamouth forgot it was a town with a bridge and remembered instead that it was a town with a market. The ice came down the valley in stages — first a rind of white at the banks like lace on a sleeve, then a gray skin that creaked at night, and at last a deep black glass that rang under the hammers of the fair-builders as they drove their stakes into it. By the morning of Saint Aldhelm's Fair, the whole breadth of the river between the bridge and the mill race had become a street: a street of sawdust and straw laid over ice, lined with booths of canvas and wattle, lit from dusk onward by lanterns whose light went strange and watery through the frozen air.

Silas Crane had not wanted to come. She had told herself so for three days, standing at her bench with the copied pages folded small against her breastbone, telling herself that a fair was no place for what she carried, that eyes were everywhere at a fair, that Hale's men drank mulled wine at every stall and laughed too loudly at nothing. But Tam Ossler had come to the workshop the evening before, stamping snow off his boots in the doorway, and said the thing she could not argue with: *A traveling clerk comes to every winter fair in the county. Assize man, out of Marleford. They say he takes depositions on the spot, seals them, carries them himself to the spring sessions. If you mean to put paper in front of a judge, girl, there's your judge's own hand, come to you.*

Come to you. As if justice were a peddler with a tray.

She dressed plainly — wool, patched at both elbows, her hair bound back in the way that made people call her *boy* and look away, which she counted a kindness. The copies of Edith Marr's statement she had divided into four packets, each no larger than a prayer book, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn into the seams of her coat: two at the shoulders, one at the left hip, one inside the collar where it pressed against her neck all day like a hand reminding her why she had come. The original stayed home behind the wall map, hanging in plain sight in the workshop where Brack had already looked at it once and moved on. She checked the lock twice anyway. She stood a long moment in the cold street afterward, looking up at her own dark windows, feeling the way a courier must feel leaving a house full of gold with nothing but the letter itself for armor.

Then she walked down to the ice.

* * *

The fair smelled of woodsmoke and scorched sugar and the iron-and-fur reek of the beast pens. A bear was being led on a chain somewhere near the far bank, and its low grumbling carried across the frozen water like weather. Skaters went past in bright ribbons — apprentices from the wool hall, girls with ribbons of dyed yarn braided into their hair, a fat merchant on the arm of two sweating clerks who would feel it tomorrow. Somewhere a fiddler was playing a reel that kept breaking down and starting over, as if even the music couldn't quite trust the footing.

Silas walked the length of the ice-street slowly, doing what Vane had taught her to do with any unfamiliar ground: surveying first, committing second. *A map is a promise,* he used to say, tapping the bench with his knuckle until the ink pots rattled. *You don't promise what you haven't measured.* So she measured. The garrison post stood at the landward end of the bridge, its shuttered window glowing orange; two soldiers outside, one of them the young one who chewed cloves against the cold, the other — she felt her stomach drop a half-inch — the other was Brack, arms crossed, breath steaming, watching the crowd with the patience of a man watching a tide he had been told to watch forever. At the toll gate, Nell's father sat wrapped in three coats inside his little hut, and above him Nell herself perched on a barrel, selling hot cider to skaters, her laugh carrying farther than the fiddle. When Silas passed within ten feet of the toll hut Nell caught her eye and did not smile, which was its own kind of message: *I see you. I know you're working. Go carefully.*

And there — near the middle of the ice-street, at a trestle table borrowed from the wool guild and hung with the assize court's faded green banner — sat the clerk.

He was younger than she had imagined. That was her first thought, and her second thought was that she had imagined him old because she needed him to be wise, and neither thought helped her at all. He was perhaps thirty-five, thin, with ink stains on his right cuff that she recognized the way you recognize a family resemblance, and he was writing — actually writing, bent over the table while a queue of townsfolk shuffled up with their small grievances: a boundary dispute, an unpaid debt, a woman swearing out a complaint about a neighbor's dog that had frightened her hens into molting. He took each one seriously. Silas watched him take the hen-woman seriously for a quarter of an hour, nodding, asking questions, writing in a round careful hand, and something in her chest loosened and tightened at once. Here was a man who wrote things down for a living and believed in the writing. Here was the whole machinery of law reduced to one tired man at a table on a river of ice.

She bought a cup of cider she didn't want, mostly to have something to do with her hands, and waited, and watched, and waited some more. The light went from gray to blue to the amber of the lanterns. The bear was led away. The fiddler gave up entirely and was replaced by a man with a hurdy-gurdy that ground out the same eight bars over and over like a millstone.

It was nearly full dark when the queue thinned. She approached the table the way she had rehearsed approaching it forty times on the walk down: as a petitioner. Small papers first.

"Master Clerk," she said. "I've a matter for the assize court. It can't wait for spring."

He looked up. His eyes were pale gray and very tired, and behind the tiredness there was the particular alertness she had seen in Vane when a client brought him a map that someone else had faked: the instant professional hunger for a true thing.

"Name?" he said.

"Crane. Silas Crane." She let him write it. Watching another hand shape her name gave her a queer vertigo, as if she were watching her own signature being forged. "Vellamouth. I'm — I was apprentice to Aldous Vane, the mapmaker."

Something flickered in his face. Not recognition exactly. Recollection — the way a man recalls a name attached to a caution.

"Vane," he repeated. "The drowned mapmaker."

"The drowned mapmaker," she agreed, and heard how the words sounded coming back at her, and hated the fair, the lanterns, the hurdy-gurdy, all of it.

"And your matter?"

"I'd rather give it to you privately," she said. "There are men here whose wages depend on my matter staying unwritten." She nodded, very slightly, toward the garrison post at the end of the bridge. Toward Brack.

The clerk followed her glance. Then he did the thing that she would turn over and examine for weeks afterward, looking for the moment it went wrong: he hesitated. Not long. Two heartbeats, maybe three. But he glanced at the garrison post before he answered, and he glanced at it the way a man glances at a thing he has been warned about or paid regarding, and though he covered it by rubbing his eyes and saying, "Aye, we can step behind the booth, there's room enough," Silas felt the first cold thread go through her and knew — the way she knew a coastline was wrong before she knew *why* it was wrong — that something in this transaction had already tilted.

Still. There was no other road. Edith was gone into the workhouse infirmary. The thaw was coming early; Tam had read the ice and the sky and given her days, not weeks. This tired gray-eyed man with honest ink on his cuffs was the door, and doors do not stay open.

They stepped behind the booth. The canvas cut the wind and most of the lantern-light, and the noise of the fair became a muffled sea-sound. She took the packet from the seam at her hip — the least important of the four, she told herself, the decoy, though her hands shook so badly unwrapping the oilcloth that the lie barely held — and laid it on the trestle beside his ledger book.

"A deposition," she said, keeping her voice low and level the way Vane had taught her to keep it when accuracy mattered more than comfort. "Signed and witnessed. Concerning the closing of the Marrowfleet sluice gates on the night of the flood, three winters past. Given by Edith Marr, widow, survivor of that village, presently lodged in the Vellamouth poorhouse. And beneath it—" she turned the page— "an account of payments. To certain magistrates of this town. From funds granted by the county for flood relief."

The clerk did not touch the papers. He looked at them the way a man looks at a snake that might be a stick.

"You understand what you're handing me," he said quietly.

"I understand precisely."

"These are sitting magistrates. If this is true, it doesn't stop at Vellamouth. It goes up the bench. It goes—" He stopped himself. He rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Who else knows you have this?"

"No one living whom I fear," she said, which was almost true, and she watched him hear the *almost*.

For a moment — one long moment, in which the hurdy-gurdy wheezed and the ice boomed somewhere beneath them like a whale turning over in sleep — she believed it would hold. He picked up the packet. He weighed it in his hand. He looked at her face, and whatever he found there — grief, stubbornness, the ink-stained desperation of an apprentice trying to finish her master's sentence — made him nod slowly, and reach for his pen, and say, "Depositions taken at fair courts carry the same weight as any, if sealed proper. Give me a quarter hour and—"

That was when the lanterns along the ice-street began going out.

Not all at once. In a ripple, from the bridge end toward the mill race, one after another, as if the wind had found a seam in the night and was blowing along it. And through the sudden deepening dark came the sound of boots — many boots, in step, unhurried, the cadence of men who had done this before and expected no trouble.

Silas turned. Coming up the ice-street through the dying lanterns marched six soldiers of the garrison, and at their head, wrapped in a good coat with a fur collar, walked a man she knew only by sight and reputation: Under-Sheriff Quill, who served the magistrates' writs and was said to serve them the way a knife serves a hand.

"There," said a voice behind her, and it took her a full second to understand that the voice belonged to the clerk's own assistant — a boy of sixteen she had not even noticed until now, standing at the corner of the booth, pointing at her with the terrible simple honesty of the very young. "That's her. She was seen talking to him. Master Petch, that's the one from the mapmaker's shop, that's the one they said—"

*Petch.* So the clerk had a name. She learned it in the same breath she learned he was lost, and both learnings arrived together like a blow.

"Master Petch," said Under-Sheriff Quill pleasantly, arriving at the booth with his men fanning out around it, "you're a long way from Marleford."

"I attend the winter fairs by warrant of the circuit," Petch said. His voice was steady. His hands, flat on the table, were not.

"You do. And sometimes other men's papers find their way into your warrant, don't they." Quill held out a gloved hand. "By authority of the bench of Vellamouth, I'm instructed to detain any person circulating seditious material against the sitting magistrates, and to seize such material for examination. You'll come along quiet, Master Clerk, and be released when it's sorted — I've no quarrel with Marleford and I don't want one. The lady, though." His eyes came around to Silas, and they were pale and patient and utterly without malice, which was the worst thing about them. "The lady I think we'll walk home ourselves."

Every instinct she had screamed *run* — and she measured the run in the half-second her training allowed her: thirty feet of crowded ice to the bank, Brack at the garrison post between her and the bridge, the crowd too thin now, lanterns dead, skaters drifting homeward. Nowhere. He had chosen the hour well. Of course he had. Men like Quill always chose the hour well; that was what they were paid for.

Petch stood up. To his credit — and she would remember this too, would carry it with the rest of the debt — he stepped sideways, putting his body half between her and the soldiers, and swept the packet off the table into his coat.

"You'll not search me without a Marleford writ," he said. "My papers are privileged until filed. Touch them and the circuit will hear of it, Quill, and the circuit is not fond of you."

"The circuit," Quill said, "is three days' ride away and fond of whoever feeds it." He sighed, genuinely, wearily, a man annoyed at a stain on a day's work. "Take him."

They took him. It happened quickly and quietly, which was the horror of it: no shouting, no scene, just six soldiers and a clerk and a boy assistant suddenly surrounded by other bodies, and the green banner of the assize court taken down off the trestle by a soldier who folded it without ceremony, like laundry. Petch did not fight. He caught Silas's eye once over a soldier's shoulder, and what passed between them in that look she could not fully read — warning, apology, and something else, something urgent, his gaze flicking downward toward his own coat, toward the packet inside it, then hard into her eyes as if to say *count it, remember what you gave me, remember how many pages.*

Fourteen pages. She counted them in her mind, there on the ice, while they marched him away: fourteen pages, Edith's statement and the payment record, in her copy-hand, and if they were seized they were not merely lost, they were *theirs* — proof in enemy hands, and worse than proof, a list of everyone who might hold more.

The boy assistant was allowed to go, after a soldier emptied his satchel onto the ice and kicked through the pens and blank folios. He gathered his things with shaking hands and fled toward the bank without looking back.

Quill turned last to Silas. Up close he smelled of wet wool and peppermint, absurdly, horribly domestic.

"Go home, mistress," he said. "Finish your atlas. Some work isn't meant for the likes of us." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, and that was the most frightening thing the whole night had shown her. "Happy Saint Aldhelm's."

* * *

She did not go home. Or rather, her feet carried her home but the rest of her stayed on the ice, replaying it: the clerk's glance at the garrison post, the hesitation, the boy's voice — *that's the one they said* — the lanterns going out in a ripple, timed, arranged. Someone had marked Petch before he ever sat down at that table. Someone had known to expect a petition about magistrates, or had simply been told to watch the assize man and see who came to him. Either way, the fair had not been a chance. The fair had been a net strung across the one open road, and she had walked into it carrying the only copy she could not afford to lose.

In the workshop she lit one candle and stood in the middle of the floor, shaking, listening to the wind worry the shutters. Three packets still rode hidden in her coat. Behind her, on the wall, the largest of Vane's maps hung serene and silent with the original ledger flat in its frame, patient as a seed. She thought of Edith in the infirmary saying *come back with the law*, and of the law being marched off the ice between six soldiers with its own banner folded under someone's arm.

They had her handwriting now. That was the thought that finally dropped her into the bench chair, cold all through. Fourteen pages of her copy-hand, the same hand that filled the atlas, the same hand that had forged nothing yet but soon must. If the magistrates spread those pages before the spring assizes, every document she produced would be suspect — *forged, obviously forged, look how neatly it matches the forger's known script.* They had not just taken evidence. They had taken her name off the map of credible witnesses.

The candle guttered. Outside, far off, the river boomed again under its skin of ice — the sound Tam had taught her to count, the sound of the thaw beginning its argument with winter. Ten days, he had said. Maybe fewer.

She sat in the dark with her master's unfinished coastline above her and understood, with the clarity that arrives only after a loss, what the next move had to be. Not another clerk. Not another stranger's honesty. The remaining packets would go forward the only way left: carried by her own body, past the gates, through the thawing country, to the assize town itself — and if the copies failed, the original in the wall map frame would have to come out and travel too, and the workshop stand empty, and everything risked on one crossing.

First, though, there was a boy to find. The assistant had fled toward the bank, and boys who watch their masters arrested remember faces, and directions, and words whispered over a soldier's shoulder. Whatever Petch had tried to tell her with his eyes, the boy might hold the rest.

Silas pinched out the candle, took up her coat, and went back out into the cold to hunt through the fair's wreckage for the one witness the magistrates hadn't bothered to take.
