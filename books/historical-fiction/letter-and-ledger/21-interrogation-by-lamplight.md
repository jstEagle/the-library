## 21. Interrogation by Lamplight

The garrison post at Vellamouth had one room fit for conversation, and it was not a room anyone would have chosen for comfort. Corven Hale had chosen it anyway. It sat at the back of the building, away from the road, with a single high window that admitted the grey of the February afternoon and nothing else — no view of the river, no view of the town, nothing to distract a man from the business of being questioned. A table. Two chairs. A lamp with a brass reflector that threw its light in a hard cone across the tabletop and left the corners of the room in a brown murk.

Hale had spent forty years learning what lamplight could do. In his younger days in the customs service he had watched men sit in that cone of light for hours and begin, without knowing it, to lean toward it — toward the warmth, the clarity, the sense that somewhere outside the circle there was darkness and inside it there was safety, and that the man who controlled the lamp controlled both. He had never once raised his voice in this room. He did not intend to raise it now.

The assize clerk's name was Petcher. He was perhaps fifty, dry as a pressed flower, with the ink-stained cuffs and the apologetic stoop common to men who had spent their lives writing down other people's words. He sat on the far side of the table with his hands folded, waiting with the patience of his trade. Sergeant Dunn stood by the door, arms crossed, doing his best impression of a man who understood what was at stake. Dunn's best impression was not good. Hale had long since stopped expecting better.

"Master Petcher," Hale said pleasantly, settling into the chair opposite. "You've had a long day on the ice."

"A long enough one, sir." The clerk's voice was thin but level. "The crossing was rougher than I'd like."

"I imagine. The ferry inspection is tedious even in summer." Hale let the pause stretch. Somewhere above them, snow ticked against the high window. "You were detained this afternoon coming off the river fair. I want you to understand at the outset that this is a formality. A matter of routine. Papers, permissions, the usual friction between jurisdictions. Nobody thinks you've done anything wrong."

"No, sir."

"And yet." Hale smiled. He had been told, in another life, by a woman whose opinion he had valued more than most, that his smile made people want to check their pockets. He deployed it now. "And yet here we are, in a cold room, at lamplighting, when I'm sure you'd rather be at the inn with your feet near the fire. So let us be efficient, and get you to that fire all the sooner."

Petcher inclined his head a fraction. Waiting.

"You came through Vellamouth on assize circuit business," Hale said. "Collecting statements, is that right? Depositions, affidavits — the paper trail the spring sessions will want?"

"That's the ordinary course, sir. I collect what's tendered to me. I don't solicit."

"No," Hale agreed softly. "You don't solicit. You collect what's *tendered*." He let the word sit on the table between them like a coin of uncertain metal. "And what has been tendered to you in Vellamouth, Master Petcher?"

"Nothing out of the ordinary, sir. A boundary dispute from upriver. A debt case. A widow's petition about a fence line." The clerk recited it in the flat cadence of a man reading an inventory he had read many times before. "Nothing out of the ordinary."

Hale nodded slowly, as though each item were a small gift he was unwrapping. The boundary dispute. The debt case. The widow's fence. All plausible. All the sort of thing a clerk would genuinely carry. And all of it, he suspected, exactly as true as it needed to be — because a clever courier did not travel empty-handed and did not travel obviously armed. A clever courier carried three harmless papers over one dangerous one, so that any search found exactly what a search should find.

He knew this because he had been the courier, thirty years ago, carrying dispatches through worse towns than Vellamouth for masters less patient than himself. He knew every trick in the trade not because he'd invented them but because he'd used them.

"The winter fair draws a crowd," he said. "Merchants, carvers, the ice-cutters. And travelers who prefer to move in company rather than alone on frozen roads. You were seen speaking with a young woman. Dark hair. Ink on her fingers. She keeps the late Master Vane's workshop by the churchyard."

Something moved behind Petcher's eyes — not guilt, Hale noted, but calculation, brief and quickly shuttered. The calculation of a man deciding how much he was permitted to remember.

"I spoke with a great many people at the fair," Petcher said. "It's my habit to make myself available. People are shy of the law until they meet it sitting down."

"They are indeed." Hale leaned back, letting the lamplight fall full on his own face now, deliberately — showing the clerk a patient, reasonable, faintly tired official. "Describe her to me. The young woman. If she comes to mind."

"Ink on her fingers, you said." Petcher's mouth twitched. "I couldn't say, sir. At a fair, everyone looks alike after an hour — hats and scarves and red noses. If she has business with the court, she'll find her way to me at the sessions like everyone else."

"Of course she will," Hale said warmly. "Unless she's already found her way to you."

The clock in the guardroom beyond struck five. Somewhere in the walls a pipe knocked as the frost worked at it. Petcher sat perfectly still, hands folded, face arranged into the mild blankness of a man who had spent decades being shouted at by lawyers and had learned that stillness was armor.

Hale studied him the way he studied maps. Not the bold lines — the contours. The places where the surface changed without announcing itself.

There was one such place. When Hale had said *unless she's already found her way to you*, the clerk's thumbs had pressed together — a small motion, half a heartbeat, then released. Not fear. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a password he hoped he wouldn't be asked to repeat aloud.

So. Something had passed at the fair, or something had been meant to pass, or something had been arranged to pass and been interrupted. Dunn's men had seized the clerk's satchel within minutes of recognizing him; Hale had gone through it himself while Petcher was being brought in, standing in the guardroom with the door shut. Three documents: the boundary dispute, the debt case, the widow's fence. Each properly drafted, properly witnessed, properly dull. No ledger pages. No statement bearing a Marrowfleet name. Nothing signed by anyone named Edith Marr.

Either the girl had not yet made her delivery — and the clerk's detention had caught the evidence mid-journey, wherever it truly was — or the delivery had already been made elsewhere, hidden, and the three innocent papers were chaff scattered deliberately across the trail.

Both possibilities were bad. But only one of them was urgent tonight.

* * *

Hale poured two cups of mulled wine from the jug Dunn had brought and set one before the clerk, an act of hospitality calculated to the inch. Petcher took it, murmured his thanks, and drank like a man who knew better than to refuse anything in that room.

"Tell me about the spring assizes," Hale said, changing the ground abruptly, the way he liked to — a sudden shift of terrain dislodged more than a direct question ever had. "What's on the docket? What's the mood in the county?"

"The mood, sir?" Petcher blinked.

"You travel the circuits. You hear what people say in inns and courtyards. Is there discontent? Talk of flood relief, of Marrowfleet, of — grievances?"

"Marrowfleet." The clerk said the name carefully, tasting it. "People speak of it, sir. As people speak of any drowned village. With pity, and then they change the subject. There's no docket business in it that I know of. No suits pending. A dead village can't file a claim."

"No," Hale said. "A dead village can't file a claim. But the living can file claims *on its behalf*, given cause and given paper." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "You'll understand, Master Petcher, that Vellamouth is a sensitive jurisdiction just now. We have had losses — a respected master of the guild, drowned last autumn. Property disputes. A bridge closed for the season. When a town suffers, rumors grow in the cracks like mold in wet plaster. My colleagues and I have a duty to keep the record clean. Clean records, clean courts. That is the whole of my interest tonight."

"I admire a clean record, sir," Petcher said. "It's why I do the work I do."

"Yes. I imagine it is." Hale smiled again, and this time let it fade naturally, as if patience itself were wearing out. "Here is what happens next. Sergeant Dunn will escort you to the White Hart — the good room, upstairs, with the lock on the *outside* of the door, purely so the household staff don't trouble you. You'll have supper, a fire, whatever you ask for. In the morning, if the roads allow, you'll continue your circuit. I am detaining nothing of yours permanently." He paused. "But you'll forgive me if the sergeant walks you as far as the next town. For your protection. The roads are bad, and a man carrying court documents is a tempting mark for footpads."

"That won't be necessary—"

"It's done," Hale said mildly, and the mildness closed the discussion like a lid. "One more thing, and then I'll let you get to your wine in earnest." He drew from his coat a folded sheet and laid it on the table, turning it so the clerk could read it upright. Not a warrant. A list — the assize schedule for the spring, copied in Hale's own hand, accurate to the day. "Your sessions convene in nine weeks. I'll be attending, in my capacity as magistrate. I always attend. I like to see the machinery work." His finger rested beside the date. "If, between now and then, any document concerning Vellamouth finds its way into the county record — any petition, any deposition, any *tendered* paper touching on flood relief, on survey records, on the guild, on the late Master Vane — I would take it as a courtesy if word reached me before the session opened. Not instead of the law, Master Petcher. Ahead of it. There is nothing improper in warning a magistrate what's coming. Magistrates hate surprises. Surprises make courts inefficient, and inefficiency harms the very people the courts exist to serve."

Petcher looked at the list for a long moment. Then he looked up, and for the first time something human showed through the clerk's professional blankness — not defiance, exactly, but a kind of weary, professional candor, the honesty of a man whose whole life was records and who disliked being asked to bend them.

"If such a document exists," Petcher said, "it will reach the session by the proper channels, sir. I can't warn you ahead of it. It isn't my office to warn. I receive, I carry, I deliver. If I began choosing which deliveries to announce, I'd be a different kind of man entirely, and you wouldn't trust me either."

The silence held for four or five seconds. Hale found, to his own mild surprise, that he almost respected it. Almost.

"No," he said quietly. "I suppose you wouldn't be trustworthy, at that." He rose, took the schedule back, and tucked it away. "Sergeant. See Master Petcher to the Hart. Good room. Supper. Whatever he likes."

Dunn opened the door. Cold air knifed in from the corridor, making the lamp gutter. Petcher stood, bowed the smallest possible bow, and followed the sergeant out. At the threshold he paused — half-turned, the lamplight catching the grey at his temples.

"She had ink on her fingers, you said," he remarked, to no one in particular. "In my experience, sir, it's worth remembering that ink stains deeper than blood, and washes out harder. Whoever she is." And he went, his footsteps fading down the stone corridor, unhurried as a man keeping an appointment with his own supper.

Hale stood alone in the cone of light and listened to the frost working at the window.

*Deeper than blood.* Was that a message? A sympathy? Or merely the reflexive wit of a paper-man? He could not tell, and the not-knowing sat in him like a swallowed stone.

* * *

He walked home through streets blue with early dark, his boots crunching on refrozen slush, and did his thinking the way he always did it — walking, hands clasped behind his back, breath smoking. The town slid past him: shuttered shops, a drunk singing to himself outside the Anchor, candlelight in a poorhouse window where someone was moving behind a curtain too slowly for health. He catalogued it all automatically, the way Vane had once catalogued coastlines. Every town was a map. Most men never learned to read their own.

Let us suppose, he thought, that the girl Crane had tried to pass something at the fair. Suppose the clerk was her instrument. Then the detention had worked: the channel was cut, the copies — if copies existed — were still in Vellamouth or destroyed, and the girl now knew her courier had been taken. Which meant she knew the net existed. Which meant she would either freeze — hide everything, go silent, wait out the winter — or she would run.

Which would she do?

He reviewed her as he walked. The few times he had spoken with her, in the workshop that smelled of size and lamp-black, she had answered him smoothly, competently, with a composure that was itself a kind of information. Grief did not produce that composure; discipline did. Vane had drilled her well. And Vane — here Hale's jaw tightened — Vane had been drilling her for years on material Hale had assumed was safely buried. The atlas. God only knew what else the old man had left lying in plain sight, sewn into bindings, scraped under names, hidden in margins. A mapmaker's shop was a fortress of concealment. Every map was a lie agreed upon; every margin a place to write the truth.

She would run. He felt it with the certainty he felt changes in weather. She had too much spine to freeze, and too much to lose by waiting. And when she ran, she would run toward the spring assizes, because there was nowhere else to run *to* — the law was the only door big enough for what she thought she carried.

Very well. Then the task was simple, and simplicity was a luxury he intended to enjoy.

He reached his house on Chandler's Row, dismissed his housekeeper's questions with a word, and sat at his desk with the lamp trimmed low. He wrote three letters.

The first went to Captain Verrell at the garrison post below the ferry landing: double the watch, effective tomorrow. Not the ferry inspection alone — the whole road south, the toll gate, the ford at Hobb's Mill, the drover's path over the ridge that optimistic smugglers believed nobody knew about. Every man on the payroll, and Hale knew precisely which men those were, to be reminded that winter pay doubled for vigilance and stopped altogether for negligence. He wrote it plainly, without threat, because Verrell understood arithmetic.

The second letter went to the toll keeper at the north gate — Noll Fenwick, a careful, frightened, adequately greedy man — with instructions to report every cart leaving town carrying paper, books, parcels, or luggage heavier than its owner's circumstances justified. Hale allowed himself a thin smile at that phrase. *Heavier than its owner's circumstances justified.* It was amazing how much contraband could be described that way.

The third letter took longest. It went ahead, by fast rider, to the assize town — to no single name, because names were dangerous, but framed as a circular to the commission of the peace regarding irregularities anticipated at the spring session: a disgruntled apprentice, possibly deranged by grief, likely to present forged or stolen guild property as evidence against respectable magistrates. He kept it measured. He kept it sorrowful. He described Silas Crane the way one describes weather approaching — regrettable, but a fact to prepare for. By the time she reached the assize town, if she reached it, half the bench would already know her face, her name, and her supposed infirmity, and the ground would be salted before her first seed touched it.

He sealed all three, set them for the morning riders, and sat back.

The lamp burned steady. Outside, the wind had come around to the northeast, the wind that scoured the river ice and thinned it from beneath, and he thought — not for the first time that winter — about thaw. Everyone assumed winter was the season of entrapment: closed bridges, closed roads, a town locked in amber. But Hale had lived beside this river for sixty years, and he knew that winter was not the trap. Winter was the delay. The trap was the thaw — the moment the ice broke and the ferries ran and the inspections resumed and the whole country opened at once, every road suddenly passable, every fugitive suddenly mobile. Spring was when all debts came due.

He would be ready for spring. He had been ready for spring for twenty years.

Only one thing troubled him, and it troubled him the way a splinter troubles a hand — small, deep, impossible to leave alone. The clerk's parting words. *Ink stains deeper than blood.* And beneath that, the thing he had not said to Dunn, had not written in any letter, had barely admitted to himself: the search of the clerk's satchel had been *too clean*. Three documents, each perfect, each dull. In thirty years of customs work Hale had never once opened a courier's bag and found it honestly boring. Boring bags were built. And built bags meant a builder, and a builder meant the girl had planned for seizure before she ever stepped onto the ice at the fair.

Which meant the copies might not have been on the clerk at all.

Which meant they might still be in Vellamouth — on her body, in her shop, in some hiding place he had searched once and searched wrong.

Hale snuffed the lamp and stood in the dark of his study, listening to the wind worry the river ice, and permitted himself, briefly, the unfamiliar sensation of being ahead on every front and certain of none.

Tomorrow, he decided. Tomorrow he would walk past the mapmaker's shop again, slow, in daylight, and look at what hung on its walls.

For in the end it was always the walls men forgot.
