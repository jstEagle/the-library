## 22. Edith Taken

The news came the way winter news comes in Vellamouth: carried on breath, passed hand to hand, half-frozen by the time it reached you.

Silas heard it first from a boy selling charcoal outside the guild hall, who had it from a laundress, who had it from the poorhouse cook's sister, who had seen the cart with her own eyes. By the third telling the story had already grown its details like frost growing feathers on a window — but the bones of it stayed firm, and the bones were enough.

They had taken Edith Marr from the poorhouse in a closed cart at first light. Two men from the workhouse infirmary and one Silas did not recognize, a man in a good coat who stood back from the lifting and only watched. They said she was ill. They said it was for her health.

*For her health.* Silas turned the phrase over as she walked home through the grey morning, testing its weight the way she would test a coin between her teeth. It was a smooth phrase. It was made to be swallowed whole. And like all smooth things that arrive without cost, she could feel the edge hidden inside it.

Edith Marr was not well — that much was true. The poorhouse winters took their toll on everyone, and Edith coughed in the mornings now, a dry ratcheting sound that seemed to come up from somewhere deeper than her lungs. But Edith had been not-well for two years and the poorhouse had never once moved her anywhere. The infirmary was where they sent people to be tended, and where they sent people to be *kept*, and the difference between those two things was decided by whoever signed the order.

And Silas knew whose signature would be at the bottom of it.

She climbed the workshop stairs with her hands so cold she could barely work the latch, and inside, the room received her the way it always did — smelling of linseed oil and old paper, Vane's chair by the window still angled toward the river as though he might come back and sit down mid-thought. She had not moved that chair since October. Some part of her believed, in the stubborn animal way that has nothing to do with evidence, that moving it would make him dead in some new and permanent way.

She sat down at the bench instead of at the chair, which was its own small surrender, and laid out what she knew.

The clerk at the garrison post had been questioned eleven days ago. Nothing had been found on him — Silas's copies were gone, seized, folded into some drawer in Hale's keeping, but the clerk himself had known nothing worth charging. Yet Hale had doubled the watch. He had written ahead to the spring assizes; Tam had heard that from the ferry gossip, secondhand and certain. And now, before the ice had even begun to groan, before there was any legal reason to move a sick old woman anywhere at all — Edith Marr was in a cart.

Witnesses were being collected.

Not protected. Not silenced outright, either — Hale was too careful for silencing, silencing left marks, and marks invited questions. Collected. Gathered into places where he controlled the door, the medicine, the visitors, the record. A witness in the workhouse infirmary was a witness who could be produced when useful, kept quiet when inconvenient, and discredited when necessary — *her mind wanders, you understand, the poor woman, we moved her only out of charity*. Every possibility arranged in advance, like a surveyor plotting a road through country that hasn't been asked whether it wants a road.

Silas put her palms flat on the bench and breathed until the shaking stopped.

It was guilt, and she knew it for guilt, and knowing its name did nothing to lighten it. Because Edith had given her something — the truth about the sluice gates, told in a voice like dry reeds, with her hands working at a scrap of Marrowfleet-dyed cloth as if the cloth itself needed comforting. Edith had given her that knowing what the poorhouse was. Knowing that the magistrates' reach went into every ward and every ledger in this town. She had given it anyway, because Silas asked, and because forty-one people deserved to have been more than water.

And now the asking had a price, and someone else was paying it.

*You should have taken her out of there weeks ago*, said a voice in Silas's head that sounded unpleasantly like her own conscience wearing Vane's patience. *You should have found her a room. You should have—*

But with what money? With what papers? An orphaned apprentice with no name of her own could not rent a chamber or vouch for a pauper; Vellamouth was a town built entirely of signatures, and Silas Crane had almost none of them. That was the architecture of the place, she understood now — not the bridges and the toll gates but the signatures, the whole invisible lattice of who may speak and who must stay spoken-for. Edith Marr had spent her life on the far side of that lattice. So had Silas. The atlas was her way through it, and she had been slow, and slowness had a body count that did not die but simply vanished behind doors.

She got up and lit the lamp, though the day was barely past noon, because she could not bear the grey.

* * *

She went to the poorhouse that afternoon, because going was the only thing her hands knew how to do, and because there was a chance — a thin chance, the kind of chance she had learned to map precisely — that the removal had been exactly what it claimed. That Edith was warm and fed and dosed with willow bark and complaining about the porridge.

The poorhouse matron was a broad, tired woman named Mrs. Kettleby whom Silas had known slightly, once, through the church alms list. She received Silas in the entrance hall, where the smell of boiled cabbage and wet wool hung like weather, and she wrung her hands the whole time she spoke, which told Silas more than her words did.

"They came at dawn," Mrs. Kettleby said, low, glancing at the stairs. "Before the others were even awake. There was an order signed — proper, sealed, all correct — saying the woman Marr required care above what we provide." Her hands twisted. "Which is true enough, God knows. We've two blankets where we need four, and the doctor comes Thursdays if the roads allow. But it wasn't the *manner* of it. They wouldn't let me go up with them. They had her dressed and out before the kettle boiled."

"Did she say anything?"

"She asked for her basket." Mrs. Kettleby's voice caught on it. "Her sewing basket. She keeps — kept — everything in it, thread and scraps and her mother's thimble, and the man in the good coat said effects would follow, and I know how effects follow, miss, I've seen how effects follow." She looked at Silas then, directly, and her eyes were red-rimmed and frightened and asking something she could not afford to ask aloud. "She was a good lodger. Quiet. Mended for the other women. Whatever they want her for — it isn't illness, is it."

It was not a question, and Silas did not insult it with an answer. "Where is the infirmary exactly? Which wing?"

"The north wing, off the Marsh Road. But—" Mrs. Kettleby stepped closer, dropping her voice to nearly nothing. "There's a man on the door now. There's never been a man on the door. He asked *my* name, miss, and I've kept this house eleven years."

Silas thanked her and left a coin in the poorbox, which was a useless gesture and felt necessary anyway, and walked out into the afternoon with the cold coming off the river in long flat sheets.

A man on the door. Effects that follow. A witness gathered like grain into a barn whose owner decides the threshing.

She thought of Edith's hands on that scrap of dyed cloth — the Marrowfleet pattern, interlaced lines like waterweed, like a net, like writing in a language only drowned people read — and she understood with a clarity that felt physical, almost painful, that she was watching the last chapter of a process that had begun the night the sluice gates closed. Everything since — the flood, the scraped name on the map, Vane's death, the ledger sewn into the binding, the seized copies, the warrant, all of it — was one continuous motion, one long deliberate gesture, and she had spent three months believing she was reading it when in fact she had been standing inside it.

Hale was not reacting to her. He was finishing something.

* * *

That evening Tam Ossler came up from the ferry with ice in his beard and bad news stacked under his tongue like cordwood.

He sat in Vane's chair — the only person besides Silas permitted near it — and held his cracked hands toward the brazier while Silas poured him the hot wine she kept for exactly these visits. Outside, the light was failing in that peculiar winter way, not darkening so much as thickening, the sky closing over the town like a lid.

"Ice'll hold another fortnight, maybe less," Tam said, by way of opening. "Then the channel runs and nobody crosses without the garrison's say-so. You knew that."

"I knew that. What else."

"Two things." He drank, wincing as it burned the raw places. "First: the ferry inspection post on the south bank — they're building it out. New timber arrived today, sledges of it. Come thaw there'll be a proper checkpoint, not just a shed and a bored corporal. Somebody means to look at everything that leaves this town come spring. Everything."

Silas closed her eyes briefly. *Somebody means to look at everything.* Hale was not merely sealing the town against her; he was building the seal in advance, patient as masonry, so that whenever she moved — and he clearly believed she would move, come thaw, come desperation — the net would be waiting, new-timbered and complete.

"And the second thing."

Tam looked at his wine. When Tam Ossler avoided your eye, it meant the news was worse than the silence around it. "Second thing. Man I know works the workhouse kitchens. Says the infirmary's taken four patients this month. Four, from different parishes. All elderly, all alone, all—" he waved a hand vaguely, "—all *nobody's*. And here's the part that sits wrong with me. Three of the four came from villages on the flood line. Marrowfleet. Ashcombe. That little place upriver, Tarn Mill."

The brazier ticked. Somewhere across the river a dog began to bark and thought better of it.

"He's gathering them," Silas said. Her voice sounded very level in her own ears, which frightened her more than shaking would have. "Everyone who saw the water do what it shouldn't have done. Everyone old enough to remember the night. He's putting them all in one building, under one roof, behind one door with one man on it."

"That's what it looks like from the kitchen," Tam agreed grimly. "Could be coincidence. Could be charity. Rich men are charitable in strange directions sometimes."

"You don't believe that."

"No," Tam said. "I don't believe that."

He finished the wine and sat a while longer, and neither of them spoke, because there is a kind of understanding that talk ruins. Through the window the river showed itself as a faint paler darkness among the darknesses, holding its frozen breath, waiting. Ten days, Tam had said earlier in the winter. Now: a fortnight, maybe less. The clock was audible again, ticking under everything, and it ticked in water-time, in the slow colossal patience of a thing that had already swallowed one village and would happily swallow the proof of it.

After he left, Silas stood at the bench with the lamp turned low and let herself feel the whole weight of it, because she had learned that feeling it fully once was cheaper than carrying it unexamined.

She had done this. Not alone — never alone; Hale had dug this pit years before Silas Crane ever learned to cut a quill — but her questions had marked Edith. The poorhouse answered to the magistrates; Edith herself had said so, calmly, folding her cloth, a woman stating the terms of her own captivity the way one states the price of bread. And Silas had gone anyway, had asked anyway, had accepted the gift of testimony without being able to shield the giver. That was the arithmetic of it, and no amount of noble intention balanced the column. Vane would have said — Vane had said, a hundred times, bent over some disputed measurement — *good intentions are not accuracy, girl. Check your work.*

Her work had gotten a sick old woman moved behind a guarded door in a closed cart before the kettle boiled.

So. Urgency, then. Not despair — despair was a luxury priced beyond her — but urgency, clean and cold as the river under its ice. If Hale was collecting witnesses, then the witnesses he had collected were the ones who mattered most, and Edith's testimony — the sluice gates closed on the night of the flood, closed by hand, closed against the water's own logic — was not decoration on Silas's case. It was load-bearing wall. A ledger could be called forged. A ciphered note could be called paranoia. But a living woman who had watched the gates shut, who had lost her village and her husband to the backed-up water, whose memory the magistrates had gone to the trouble of warehousing — that was testimony no bench could wave away without waving away its own reflection.

Which meant, Silas thought, staring into the lamp flame until it printed its ghost on her vision, that she had to reach Edith before the thaw. Before the checkpoints hardened into timber and iron. Before whatever Hale intended for his collection of nobodies came due.

And she would need help, and luck, and probably a lie good enough to survive a man on a door.

* * *

She worked late, because work was the only prayer she trusted. The atlas lay open before her, Marrowfleet's coastline inked in full now — she had done it weeks ago, in defiance, giving the drowned village back its outline if nothing else — and beside it the copied ledger pages, her own careful hand imitating Vane's imitating corruption. She spread them out and studied them the way she'd study terrain before a crossing: what she had, what she lacked, what the ground allowed.

What she had: the original ledger, hidden in the frame of the great wall map hanging above the hearth in plain sight. Her copies of the ciphered marginalia. The memory of Edith's account, precise as a survey, delivered in a voice that had not trembled once.

What she lacked: Edith's signature. A statement in her own hand, witnessed, dated — the thing that turns memory into evidence. And Edith had made one; Silas knew she had, had watched her hide it in the sewing basket weeks ago. A sewing basket that had not followed her. *Effects will follow.*

Silas's blood went cold by degrees, the way the river went cold, gradually and then completely.

The basket hadn't followed because it wasn't meant to follow. The basket contained a signed statement naming sluice gates and a flood and, by implication, the men who had bought the water. Hale's men had lifted Edith at dawn and taken her away from it, and somewhere in that house — in the poorhouse storeroom, or already in Hale's keeping, or burned in some grate before breakfast — was the single document that could hang the men who had drowned Marrowfleet.

Unless Edith had written another.

Silas sat very still, and beneath the fear a small fierce hope kindled, the kind that survives winters by burning its own wood. Edith Marr had survived a flood by being the sort of person who noticed water levels. She had survived the poorhouse by being the sort of person who mended for the other women and missed nothing. Such a woman did not keep her only statement in one basket.

Tomorrow, Silas thought. Tomorrow she would carry medicine to the north wing infirmary — she had a genuine pretext, apothecary deliveries ran weekly, and Nell's father could put her name on the list — and she would stand in front of the door with the man on it, and she would find out what Edith had held back, and whether love of the dead was strong enough to smuggle truth past a guard in a good coat.

Outside, far off, the river made a sound it had not made all winter — a deep shifting groan, ice settling or beginning to doubt itself. Silas listened to it with her pen unmoving above the page.

Ten days. Maybe fewer. And somewhere behind a guarded door, an old woman was holding the rest of the truth in hands that had already given everything once.

She trimmed the lamp, and got back to work.
