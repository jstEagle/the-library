## 23. The Workhouse Visit

The medicine came from Widow Petch's back room, and it was mostly chalk.

Silas watched the old woman wrap the packet in brown paper, tying it with a length of twine that had clearly served a dozen other parcels before this one. "Willow bark and chalk," Petch said, not looking up. "For the bowels of the poor. It won't hurt them. It won't help them either." She knotted the twine with a jerk. "You'll want to be quick about it. Sister Halloway counts her visitors like a moneylender counts coin."

"I'll be quick."

"You'll be careful," Petch said, and now she did look up, her eyes red-rimmed and shrewd above the paper packet. "There's a difference, girl. Quick is what gets you seen. Careful is what gets you home." She pushed the packet across the counter. "I told Edith Marr you'd come. I told her nothing else. A woman my age learns to keep her mouth the way she keeps her purse — closed, and only opened for good reason."

The workhouse stood at the low end of Tanner's Lane, where the street gave up pretending to be respectable and became mud and standing water. It was a long, gray building with small windows set high in the walls, so that the people inside lived at the level of the sky rather than the street. In summer the smell of the dye works downwind covered its own smells; in winter there was no such mercy. Smoke hung over it in a flat ceiling, and beneath the smoke Silas could smell lye, boiled cabbage, wet wool, and beneath all of it something older and sweeter that she chose not to name.

She had dressed for the part. Her good cloak — Vane's money had bought it two years ago, when he decided his apprentice should look respectable at guild dinners — had been traded to Petch's neighbor for a coat of undyed wool gone gray with washing. She carried the medicine packet in a basket with a loaf of black bread on top, because a woman delivering physic might plausibly carry bread, but a woman carrying only physic looked like a woman going somewhere specific. Specificity was dangerous. Vane had taught her that too, though he'd meant it about coastlines: *the detail that stands alone is the detail that betrays you.*

Sister Halloway received her in a cold parlor that smelled of vinegar. She was not what Silas had expected. She was not large or loud; she was a dry, precise woman with hands folded on the table like a closed book, and she listened to Silas's explanation — the widow Petch sent me, the fever in the infirmary ward, the willow bark — with the attention of someone checking sums.

"Edith Marr is not febrile," Sister Halloway said.

"No, sister. The fever is in the men's ward. But Widow Petch thought — Mrs. Marr being elderly, and winter coming on—"

"Widow Petch is generous with other people's stores." Sister Halloway unfolded one hand, laid it flat. "You may leave the medicine. You may have ten minutes with the old woman, since you've come all this way through the mud, and I am not without Christian feeling. But I will say this once, and then you will go." Her voice didn't rise. It simply became very exact, the way a scale becomes exact. "Mrs. Marr has been moved to our infirmary for her health. Whatever stories she tells herself about Marrowfleet, they are old stories, and old women's memories are like old cloth — they tear easily, and what tears cannot be mended into anything useful. Do you understand me?"

Silas understood that the magistrates' reach extended even here, even into this vocabulary of health and kindness, and that Sister Halloway either believed her own words or had been given them to repeat, and could not tell which was worse. "I understand, sister."

"Ten minutes." She rang a small hand bell, and a girl of perhaps twelve appeared, barefoot despite the cold, and was dispatched to fetch Mrs. Marr to the infirmary stair.

* * *

They let Silas sit with her on the infirmary landing, where a window gave onto the yard and the light came down gray and even, the light of a day that has already given up on itself. Edith Marr was smaller than Silas remembered. The poorhouse had taken weight off her the way water takes paint off wood, slowly and everywhere at once. Her hands lay in her lap, knuckles swollen, fingers crooked around an invisible spindle. But her eyes were the same — quick, dark, and level, eyes that had watched a village drown and had refused, ever after, to look away from anything.

"They brought me down special," Edith said. "Must have cost them something, moving an old woman down a stair." She smiled without much humor in it. "Sit you down, girl, before your knees give out. You're holding yourself like a fence post in a gale."

Silas sat on the bench beside her. The bread she set between them, and Edith broke it with the economy of someone who has never wasted a crumb, and ate a little, and wrapped the rest in her apron for later, and only then did she look at Silas properly.

"Well," she said. "You've the look of someone who's been reading maps in the dark."

"Is it so plain?"

"To me it is. I spent forty years reading faces across a market stall. Faces are just maps, if you know the country." She chewed, swallowed. "Tell me."

So Silas told her — quietly, in the space between the wind at the window and the distant clatter of the laundry — about the fair, about the clerk with ink-stained cuffs who had seemed honest, about the garrison post and the copies seized from his satchel while he stood white-faced among the guards. About Hale doubling the watch. About the search warrant still valid, the workshop still marked, every road out of town now watched twice as hard.

"They took everything," Silas finished. "Everything I'd copied. Three months of night work, gone into a magistrate's strongbox. For all I know it's ash by now."

Edith was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "And how do you feel?"

The question caught Silas off guard; grief and fear had been shouting so loudly she hadn't checked on anything quieter underneath. "Angry," she said finally. "Ashamed. I walked him into it. I picked him because he looked safe."

"Aye. And?"

"And—" Silas stopped. And underneath the anger, if she was honest, there was something else, something that had been growing since the night of the fair, since she'd stood in the crowd watching the clerk led away and felt not despair but a cold, bright clarity: *they took the copies because I let them see the copies.* "And I learned something," she said slowly. "I learned that they watch what moves. They watch roads and gates and satchels and clerks. They took three months of copying because copying travels."

Edith's mouth curved. "Go on."

"But the original ledger never left Vellamouth. It hangs on a wall in my workshop behind a map of the estuary, and Corven Hale stood in front of it not six weeks ago and saw nothing, because it wasn't moving. Because it was *home*." Silas heard her own voice drop, steadier now. "I don't need to get evidence out of this town, Edith. I need to get *me* out, with proof enough to make the assize judges listen, and the proof that matters most can't travel until I do. So the question isn't whether the copies are lost. The question is what I carry that can't be copied."

Edith turned her face toward the window. Below, in the yard, the barefoot girl was beating a rug against a post, each strike flat and final as a door closing.

"When the sluice gates closed," Edith said, "there was a sound. I've tried to describe it to folk and they nod and look sorry, but they don't hear it. It was like a great door shutting under the water. Iron on iron, and then the river backing up against it, and the whole valley went quiet in a wrong way — birds stopping, cattle walking in circles. We stood on the church rise and watched the water come up the lane like it was being *poured*, slow and deliberate, like something with intent. Forty-one of us on that rise. Forty-one." She held up her crooked hands. "These are the hands of a woman who sold fish for forty years and buried a husband and a daughter in one morning. There is nothing left in them. But—" and here she reached into her bodice, into a hidden seam Silas realized must have been picked open and restitched many times, "—these are also the hands of a woman who learned to write her name at the parish school, sixty years ago, and has kept it sharp."

She drew out a folded paper, soft with handling, and pressed it into Silas's palm. Silas opened it. It was a statement, written in a cramped, determined hand, dated and signed — *Edith Marr, born Edith Tallow of Marrowfleet, witness of the closing of the north sluice, the ninth night of October, in the year 1741* — and below the signature, in the same ink, a second line Silas read twice before she believed it:

*Witnessed also: my daughter, Ruth Marr, aged nine years, who stood beside me on the church rise and counted the gates.*

"This is the second copy," Edith said. "The first went to the assize clerk, same as yours. Which means it's in a magistrate's strongbox now, or an ash bucket. But I wrote three." She smiled, and this time there was iron in it. "Old women are like maps, Silas Crane. Everyone assumes we show the same country we showed last year. Nobody thinks to check the margins."

Silas's throat had gone tight. "Why three?"

"Because I buried a daughter in that flood and the magistrates wrote her into their ledger as *losses, miscellaneous*. Miscellaneous." The word came out of her like a stone dropped down a well. "If I'm to testify, I'll testify properly. Once for the record, once for the strongbox, and once for whoever finally comes with the law instead of the ledger. That's arithmetic, girl. Even the poorhouse teaches arithmetic."

Then she took Silas's hand — those ruined, spindle-crooked hands closing over hers with surprising strength — and her voice changed, became quiet and absolute, the voice of someone stating terms.

"Now you'll listen to me, because I've had time to think in this place, more time than I ever wanted. They brought me here to be collected, like the papers. They think an old woman in an infirmary is a file in a drawer. Let them think it. But hear this—" and her grip tightened, "—you are not to come back here. Not for me. Not for any reason. If you come back, you confirm I matter, and the moment I matter to *them*, I become leverage against *you*. An old woman is cheap to keep and cheaper to threaten, and they will find that out, and I will not have my death — if it comes to that — bought at the price of your failure. Do you understand?"

"Edith—"

"*Do you understand?*" 

Silas looked at her — at the swollen knuckles, the apron-wrapped bread, the eyes that had counted gates while a valley drowned — and understood that she was being given something she had not asked for and could not refuse: the gift of being made unimportant, which is sometimes the largest thing one person can do for another.

"Yes," Silas said. "I understand."

"Good." Edith relaxed, and tucked the last of the bread away, and for a moment was simply an old woman on a bench in a gray light. "Then here's what you'll do instead. You'll go home, and you'll finish whatever it is you're making, and when the ice breaks you'll be ready to move, because Tam Ossler will tell you the river before anyone official does — that man reads ice the way I read faces. And when you stand in front of those judges—" she tapped Silas's knee with one crooked finger, once, like a schoolmistress, "—you tell them about the sound the gate made. Judges have heard ledgers all their lives. Make them hear the *door*."

From below came footsteps on the stair, measured and unhurried. Sister Halloway, keeping her own count.

Edith leaned close, and her whisper was dry as autumn leaves. "Don't come back for me, child. Come back with the law. Anything less, and you're just another visitor counting my minutes." She pressed Silas's fingers shut around the folded paper. "Now put that where you put the things that matter, and walk out of here like a woman who delivered chalk and cabbage and nothing else. Can you do that?"

"I can."

"Then go. And mind—" the ghost of the market-stall smile, one last time, "—the margins. Always check the margins."

The footsteps reached the landing. Silas rose, basket on her arm, face arranged into mild, tired gratitude, the face of a woman whose errand was done. Sister Halloway stood in the doorway with the keys at her waist, and her gaze passed over Silas the way a customs officer passes over cargo that declares nothing worth opening.

"The medicine?" she said.

"Delivered. Widow Petch sends her respects, sister."

"Respects are cheap." But she stepped aside, and Silas went down the stairs past wards of quiet, watchful faces, out into the mud and the smoke-smell of Tanner's Lane, and did not run, because running would have been specific, and specificity was betrayal.

It was only at the corner, where Tanner's Lane bent toward the river, that she allowed herself to stop and press her hand flat against her coat, against the lining where the paper lay folded small and soft with handling. Three statements written, one destroyed, one seized, one surviving. Forty-one names, and one of them a child named Ruth who had counted gates.

Below her, at the bottom of the hill, the river lay under its ice, gray-white and silent, and Silas found herself listening to it the way Edith had described listening — waiting, without knowing why, for the sound of iron on iron.

Ten days, maybe fewer. Tam had said the ice would break early.

She pulled her collar up against the wind and started home along the bank, and behind her the workhouse windows watched her go, small and high, full of people living at the level of the sky.
