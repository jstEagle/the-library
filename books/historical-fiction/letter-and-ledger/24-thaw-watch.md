## 24. Thaw Watch

Tam Ossler had spent forty-one winters reading the river, and he had never once been wrong about the break.

He knew it the way other men knew weather in their knees, or knew a woman's temper by the set of her shoulders. The Vellamouth ice spoke to him — not in words, of course, though his father had claimed as much after too much rum, but in sounds. In the long groan that rolled up from beneath the ferry landing at dusk like something enormous turning over in its sleep. In the high thin singing at dawn when frost tightened its grip and the whole grey plain of it seemed to hum. A frozen river was an instrument, Tam thought, and every year it played the same slow music toward its own destruction, note by note, and a man who listened could tell you exactly which movement it had reached.

This morning's note was wrong.

He stood on the landing boards at first light, breath steaming, coffee can cooling forgotten in his fist, and listened to a sound he'd heard only three times in four decades: the early groan. Not the deep patient complaint of January ice settling into itself, but a sharper thing, a cracking-whine that started far out past the channel markers and ran toward shore like a whisper passed down a row of gossips. The sound of ice losing faith with itself.

"Damn," he said quietly, to nobody. The river was empty of boats, locked under its lid, and the town behind him was still mostly shuttered against the cold, so he said it again, louder, just to hear a human voice confirm it. "Damn."

Ten days. Maybe fewer. He'd have staked the ferry on it, and the ferry was all he had.

* * *

He made himself walk the channel anyway, because knowing a thing in your bones and proving it to your eyes were different trades. The ice near the pilings was still thick enough to hold a cart horse — he stamped it, heard the solid dead knock of good ice, nodded. But forty yards out, where the current ran faster beneath, the surface had gone a color he didn't like. Good winter ice was white or blue-white, opaque as bone. This stretch had turned a dark glassy grey, the kind of ice that looked strong from a distance and lied up close. And along the eastern bank, where the sun found it first, meltwater stood in shallow lenses on top of the floes, catching the morning light like coins scattered across a coffin lid.

Coins. That was a thought he hadn't invited. He pushed it away and it came back, the way thoughts do.

There were things sleeping under this ice that would wake when it broke. Not fish — fish he could bear — but other cargo. Every winter the river took something, and every spring it gave some of it back, bloated and turned wrong, delivered to the reeds downstream for the living to identify and bury. Two winters ago it had given back a smuggler's skiff, stove-in, full of wet brandy casks. Last winter it had taken old Josiah Pell, who everyone said had gone to visit his sister in the interior, and returned him in March looking like a man who had changed his mind about the trip.

And this winter it had taken Master Aldous Vane.

Tam pulled the body out himself. He didn't talk about it much, because there wasn't much worth telling — a mapmaker, drowned, no marks on him, an accident the coroner wrote down in two lines and the town forgot inside a month. Rivers drowned people. It was what rivers did; nobody blamed the water. But Tam had pulled enough bodies to know the difference between a man the river caught and a man the river was given, and Aldous Vane, floating face-down in the shallows below Marrowfleet with his boots still tied, had sat somewhere Tam couldn't name. He'd told Silas Crane once, back in the autumn, about the argument — Vane and Magistrate Hale, voices raised on the towpath the night before, seen by the miller's boy who saw everything and repeated half of it. He'd watched her take that piece of information the way a wall takes a battering ram: standing very straight, showing nothing, changing forever.

She'd come back since. More than once. Each time with more questions dressed as small favors — help me understand the currents here, where does the floodwater go when the sluices close, how fast does the river run in thaw. He answered because she paid in honest coin and honest company, and because a man who spends his life on water learns to recognize another person being carried somewhere they cannot stop going.

Now he stood on his own landing, listening to the ice sing its early song, and understood that the clock he'd been hearing wasn't only for him.

Once the river ran, everything changed. The ferry inspections would resume at full strength — garrison men on both banks, papers checked, satchels opened, cargo prodded with bayonets wrapped in cloth so they wouldn't cut anything valuable. All winter the crossing had been a sleepy affair: one boat, one bored customs man who took Tam's word for his manifest and warmed his hands at the brazier more than he read. But open water meant commerce meant revenue meant soldiers, and Magistrate Corven Hale's reach grew longer every year, like ivy over a dead tree. Whatever Miss Crane intended to carry across this river before spring — and Tam had known since the poorhouse business, since Edith Marr was moved to the infirmary like a parcel being collected rather than a woman being cared for — she would carry it through checkpoints that woke with the thaw, hungry and attentive.

Ten days. Maybe fewer.

He spat into the meltwater pooled on the boards and watched it spread, thin as spilled ink.

* * *

He found her at the workshop, naturally. Where else. The lamp burned behind the frosted window even though it was barely mid-morning, and smoke rose from the chimney in a thin determined line, and Tam thought, not for the first time, that the girl kept hours like a monk keeping vigil. He knocked the particular knock he used — three raps, pause, two — because a man who ferried people learned quickly that some doors needed code.

Silas Crane opened it with ink on her fingers and shadows under her eyes and the look of someone deciding whether you were a problem or a tool.

"Morning, Miss Crane."

"Mister Ossler." She stepped back to let him in, which she did not do for everyone; he counted that as payment received. The workshop smelled the way it always smelled — linseed oil, lampblack, tallow, and under it all the river-damp smell of paper that has been stored near water too long. The big wall map hung above the bench, Vane's masterwork, the coastline of half the county rendered in ink fine as hair. Tam had never much cared for maps. They showed you where you were, which was usually somewhere you couldn't afford to leave.

"I need to tell you something," he said, "and I'll be quick about it, because I don't think either of us likes me saying it."

Her chin came up slightly. Waiting.

"The ice is going early." He held up a hand before she could speak. "Before you ask how I know — forty years on this river. The groan came last night, the early kind, the kind that means the current's got underneath and is working. There's meltwater lying on the floes by the east bank already, and it hasn't topped freezing in three days. Ten days, Miss Crane. Maybe fewer if we get rain off the hills, and my shoulder says we will."

The lamplight moved in her eyes while she took that in. She didn't argue. That frightened him more than arguing would have. People who argued were still hoping you were wrong.

"When does the inspection resume?" she asked. Her voice was level. Apprentice-trained, he supposed. Mapmakers' hands and magistrates' witnesses both learned to keep their voices level.

"Full strength the day the channel opens. They post a man at the landing before the first ferry crosses, always have. Garrison rotation comes up from the fort — six men, sometimes eight, and a customs officer who actually reads manifests instead of using them to wipe his nose. And Hale—" he paused, choosing, then decided she deserved the whole weight of it, "—Hale will want his men watching the crossing like hawks on a hare. A woman traveling alone, in a season when nobody travels without reason, carrying anything worth hiding. If you're not across before the break, you'll be crossing in front of an audience."

"And after the break?"

"After the break," Tam said, "the river doesn't care whose business is on it. Floes the size of chapel roofs, moving faster than a galloping horse, grinding everything. I won't row a soul through the run-off, not for money, not for God, not for love. Nobody crosses during the break. Then inspections settle in for the season and stay thick until autumn." He let that sit. "So whatever your plan is, it lives or dies inside ten days. The river's giving you a window, and windows are like ice — they look solid right up until they aren't."

She turned away from him then, toward the bench, and he saw her look at the atlas lying there — the great unfinished thing, Vane's life's work with her corrections crowding its margins — and he saw her hand move toward it and stop, hovering, like a hand over a candle flame testing how close it could go.

"Toll gate," she said, half to herself, counting on her ink-stained fingers. "Garrison road. Ferry landing." Three fingers. Three gates. He'd heard her use the phrase before, to Nell Tolliver, over cards at the Anchor — *the plan of three gates* — and had pretended deafness, because pretending deafness was a ferryman's second trade. "Three days for the road, if nothing goes wrong. Two for preparation. That leaves five."

"That leaves five," Tam agreed. "Minus whatever goes wrong."

"Something always goes wrong."

"Aye." He scratched under his cap. "In my experience it goes wrong at the water. Land's forgiving. Water isn't. Which brings me to the other thing I came to say."

She looked at him.

"I'll row you across," he said. "At the ice, in the dark, through the leads in the channel before the garrison posts. It's the worst crossing I'll ever make and I've made bad ones. But I'll do it once, and I'll do it on my signal, not yours, and when I say go, you go, and when I say hold, you hold, because the difference between a floe and a skull is about a foot of oak and half a second of obedience." He met her eyes. "You understand? I'm not offering you a ferry. I'm offering you a chance, and chances drown people who hesitate."

For a long moment she said nothing. The fire ticked in the stove. Somewhere out on the river, faint but unmistakable, the ice groaned again — that same wrong early note, rolling up through the floorboards, through their boots, through the soles of them both, as if the river itself had leaned in to listen to what was being promised over its body.

"You've thought about the route," she said finally. "Through the broken channel."

"I dream about it," Tam admitted. "Have done since November, though I didn't know why till now. There's a lead opens along the north shore when the main sheet lets go — deep water, fastest flow, but it runs clean between the floes for maybe two hundred yards if the wind's west. West wind pushes the pack east, leaves the near channel workable. We get a west wind and a dark night and I can put you on the far bank before the garrison's finished breakfast."

"And if the wind's east?"

"Then we wait for a west wind, and hope the garrison waits too." He smiled, and knew it came out crooked. "River gives nothing free, Miss Crane. You know that better than most, these days."

She almost laughed — a small, hard exhalation, no humor in it, the laugh of a person doing arithmetic with terrible numbers. Then she went to the shelf, took down a bottle of Vane's brandy that Tam suspected had survived three funerals and one bankruptcy, and poured two fingers into two cups.

"To the ice," she said, raising hers. "May it break slowly."

"Now that," said Tam, touching his cup to hers, "is the first sensible prayer I've heard in Vellamouth all winter."

They drank. Outside, the light was beginning to fail already — February shortening the days like a miser counting coins — and as he set his cup down, Tam heard it again, clearer now, closer: not a groan this time but a crack, sharp and singular, like a rifle shot fired somewhere out in the middle of the grey plain. Both of them went still. Neither moved toward the window. Neither needed to.

"Ten days," Silas said.

"Maybe fewer," Tam said, and reached for his cap. "I'll send word the moment the wind turns. You keep your bags packed, Miss Crane, and keep them light — everything you carry across that water, you carry on your back and in your arms both, remember. The river takes what isn't held."

He was nearly to the door when she spoke again, quiet, not quite asking.

"Tam. When it breaks — what does it sound like?"

He stopped, hand on the latch, and considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because the answer was the truest warning he owned.

"Like the world ending politely," he said. "Like a thousand doors opening all at once, a mile wide. Like God clearing His throat after a long silence." He turned the latch. "You'll know it. Everyone in Vellamouth will know it. And the first thing everyone will think, the very first thought in every head on both banks, will be *the crossing is closed* — except the ones waiting for it to open. So mind who looks happy when the ice goes out."

Behind him, the lamp flame bent in the draft from the opening door, and out on the river, patient as arithmetic, the ice sang its early song — ten days, maybe fewer — counting down the winter for anyone willing to listen.
