## 25. The Plan of Three Gates

Tam Ossler's boathouse smelled of tar and frozen rope, of the particular cold that lives in wood that has spent its whole life touching water. The river beyond the door was a gray-green plate of ice, groaning now and then like an old man turning in bed — a sound I had learned, over the past week, to hear as a clock.

I had chosen this place because no one came to it. The ferry was hauled up for winter; Tam's trade died with the crossing; and the magistrates, who thought in ledgers and ledgers only, had no column for a boatman with nothing to sell until thaw. That was their blindness, and it was mine to spend.

Nell arrived first, cheeks scalded red from the walk down from the toll house, breath smoking, her father's ledger wrapped in sacking under her arm as though it were a loaf she'd stolen from her own kitchen. Which, in a way, she had. She stamped the snow off her boots on the threshold and looked around at the dim interior — the upturned hull like the ribcage of some great beached animal, the nets hanging from beams, the single lamp Tam had lit and turned low — and I watched her decide, visibly, that she belonged here. She was sixteen. She decided things quickly; it was one of the things I loved about teaching her, and one of the things that frightened me most about involving her.

"Your father knows you took this?" I asked, nodding at the bundle.

"He knows his list goes missing on wash days." She set it on the upturned hull between us and unwrapped it with the care of someone handling a relic. "He doesn't know why. He doesn't want to know why. He told me once that the toll book is the only honest thing in Vellamouth because it can't lie about what crossed and when." Her mouth went wry. "I didn't have the heart to tell him about the second book he keeps under the counter."

"The one where the names get adjusted," I said.

"For a fee." Nell shrugged. "I've known since I was twelve. It's how we eat in bad winters. Don't look at me like that, Silas. You asked me to help you, not to be holy about my family."

"I'm not looking at you any particular way," I said, which was a lie, because I was looking at her the way Vane used to look at a coastline he was seeing for the first time — with hunger and dread in equal measure.

* * *

We laid everything out on the hull the way Vane taught me to lay out a survey: all instruments visible, all assumptions written down, nothing trusted that hadn't been measured twice. The lamp threw our three shadows long against the tarred planks.

"Say it plain," Tam said. He sat on an upturned crate with his great hands loose between his knees, and he had the stillness of men who work alone on water — a stillness that is really patience, and patience that is really readiness. "Start at the gate nearest and walk me through it like weather. What's coming and how hard."

So I did. I had drawn it, two nights before, on the back of a ruined chart — the road east out of Vellamouth rendered in my smallest, cleanest hand: three marks along its length, each labeled in pencil I could erase.

"Gate one is the toll bar itself," I said. "Half a mile past the last houses. Brack mans it on even days, Hodge on odd ones. Brack cannot be bought — Nell's told me, and I believe her, and I'd rather plan around an honest man than gamble on a dishonest one. Hodge takes coin but counts it slowly and remembers faces badly. Either way, the gate wants paper. A cart needs a reason to leave town in February carrying weight."

"And your reason?" Tam asked.

"Nell's father's toll list." I touched the ledger. "Every cart that crosses gets entered by class — timber, grain, fish, wool, and guild freight. Guild freight pays half rate and isn't opened, because opening guild freight requires a guild officer and there isn't one within twenty miles who'll stand in the snow to do it. If my cart is entered as guild freight — paper stock going to the binderies at Ashford Cross — the toll keeper writes it, waves it through, and the guard at the bar sees only the entry."

"My father writes those entries," Nell said. "And if it's my hand writing them while he's at his dinner—" she made a small, deft gesture, a pen-stroke in the air, "—then it's written. The list is the truth afterward. That's the thing about books. Whatever's in them becomes what happened."

I looked at her. "You understand what you'd be doing. If this fails, the first question anyone asks is whose ink is on that line."

"If this fails," Nell said, "the first question anyone asks is a long way behind the question of what they do to the girl who carried the papers. I've done my arithmetic too." She said it lightly, the way young people say terrible things, because they haven't yet learned the trick of saying them heavily. "Besides. My mother crossed at Marrowfleet the week before it drowned. She was visiting her sister. She came home on the last coach and never spoke of it except once, when she was dying, and she said the water had sounded wrong for days and nobody in authority would say why." She smoothed the ledger's cover with her palm. "I'm not helping you for you, Silas Crane. Don't flatter yourself."

I didn't tell her that her mother had already told me this, or nearly — that the story had come out of her sideways, in drawing lessons, the way truths come out of children. Some inheritances arrive as maps. Hers had arrived as silence.

* * *

"Gate two," I said, and my mouth had gone dry, so I drank from the flask Tam pushed across without being asked. Gin, harsh enough to make my eyes sting. Good. I wanted to feel every part of this plan in my body, the way I wanted to feel every stroke of what I was about to attempt. "The garrison road. Two miles past the toll, where the road narrows between the escarpment and the marsh. There's a checkpoint hut and a corporal with a roster, and every cart is stopped, walked around, and its pass read aloud back to it."

"A pass," Tam said.

"A guild travel warrant. Signed by a guildmaster, countersigned at the hall, good for one cart, one driver, one route, one season." I reached into my coat and brought out the model — a genuine warrant I had borrowed from the workshop's files, one of Vane's own, issued three years ago for a survey trip and expired since. It lay on the hull between us, soft with handling, its seals brown and brittle as autumn leaves. "This is what they look like."

Tam leaned over it without touching. Nell touched it immediately, tracing the signature with one fingertip, and looked up at me with an expression I recognized because I had worn it myself, months ago, over a scraped-away village name.

"You're going to write it," she said. Not asking.

"I'm going to write it."

"In his hand."

"In his hand. His seal impression I can take from the wax on the old warrants — melt it, press it fresh, tint it with the same ochre the hall uses. The paper I'll age with tea and smoke. The countersignature is just a clerk's flourish; clerks all flourish the same." I heard my own voice going flat and technical, the voice I used when the work frightened me, and I let it, because the flatness was load-bearing. "It has to survive being read aloud by a bored corporal by lamplight. That's the whole test. Not beauty. Believability at ten inches, in bad light, to a man who wants his supper."

"And if Brack is on the roster that day?" Tam asked quietly.

"Then I wait. Or turn around. Brack can't be bought, and I won't insult him by trying."

Tam grunted — a sound with a great deal folded inside it. "Brack searched your shop," he said. "Brack stood in front of your wall map long enough to count his own heartbeats. Men say he marked you."

"Men say a lot."

"They do. And sometimes they're right." He let it sit there between us, unargued. That was Tam's gift: he put a thing down and declined to pick it up again, so that you had to carry it yourself and see how heavy it was.

* * *

"Gate three," I said, and now I set down the gin, because this part needed no dulling. "The ferry inspection. Except the ferry's hauled out and the inspection post is a hut with a stove in it and usually one bored man, and in winter, mostly, nobody leaves Vellamouth by water because the water is trying to kill them."

"But," Tam said.

"But the ice is going out early. Everyone on the river says it. Ten days, maybe fewer — you told me yourself, Tam. When it starts to break, the garrison will empty that hut. Every man who can stand will be stood along the banks watching the floes come down, because when the ice runs, it takes the piers, and when it takes the piers, the magistrates' wharf property goes with them, and Hale cares more about wharf property than he has ever cared about anything that breathes."

"So the third gate opens itself," Nell said, wonder in it.

"The third gate opens itself and shows you its teeth while doing it," I said. "Because crossing running ice in an open boat is how boatmen die, and I'll be doing it with the only copy of the evidence sewn into my coat."

"Not the only copy," Tam said.

"The only copy that matters. Edith's statement, the pages of the ledger, my notes." I pressed my hand flat against my chest, against the lining where they rode. I had grown so used to their weight that their absence, imagined, felt like a wound. "The originals stay in the wall map. If I drown, they're found eventually — or they're not, and Marrowfleet stays a blank on the coast, which is what it's been for four years anyway. But if I cross, and reach the assize court at Kingsmere before the spring session closes—"

"Then forty-one people get to have happened," Nell said softly.

"Then forty-one people get to have happened," I agreed. "On paper first. Paper is where everything real begins. Vane taught me that. He meant it as a craft lesson." I looked at the lamp flame. "It wasn't."

* * *

Tam got up then, restless, and went to the door and opened it a crack, and the cold poured through like water finding a breach. Beyond him the river lay under a sky the color of pewter, and far out in the middle distance something cracked — a deep report, like a distant cannon, rolling upstream to us across the frozen miles.

"Hear that?" he said. "That's the ice talking in its sleep. Come thaw, it wakes up mean." He shut the door and turned back to us, lamplight catching the seams of his face. "Now hear my part, since we're laying it all on the hull. You cross below the garrison, at the old eel weir, where the channel stays open longest because the current funnels there. I know that water like my own hands. But understand what you're asking. Broken ice isn't a surface, girl. It's a crowd — a crowd of plates the size of tables and the size of churches, all shoving, all grinding, and the spaces between them close faster than a man can row. Halfway across, the river decides. Not me. Not you. The river."

"Then we go when you say the river's in a mood to allow it," I said. "That's why it's your boat and not mine."

"That's why it's my boat," Tam agreed. "But hear the other thing. Floes grind a hull. My boat will leak by the far bank if she leaks at all, and you'll be bailing with your hat while I row, and anything not sewn to your body had best be sewn to your body." His eyes went to my chest, to the lining, and something moved in his face — the ferryman's professional grief for cargo lost to water, which for him was a whole private liturgy. "You'll want to choose, halfway out, what matters most. Hands do that choosing before the head does. Practice the choice now, in the warm, so the hands already know."

I thought of the satchel I kept under the workshop bench — Vane's portrait in charcoal, my first attempts at lettering, six years of apprenticeship drawings, the whole archaeology of what I had been before I became this. I said nothing. Tam saw me say nothing, and nodded slowly, and let it go, the way he let everything go: downriver, toward the sea, out of anyone's keeping.

* * *

We assembled it then, all three of us leaning over the hull, and I drew it in the frost on the upturned keel with my fingernail because it seemed right to draw it where the next breath would erase it. Gate one: the toll, Nell's pen, guild freight, paper stock, half rate, waved through. Gate two: the garrison road, the forged warrant, Vane's resurrected hand, my own steady heartbeat doing impersonation of a courier's boredom. Gate three: the eel weir, Tam's boat, the breaking ice, the river's mood.

"Every plan has one thing that must go right," Tam said, when I finished. "One hinge. Usually the planner picks the wrong hinge and hangs herself on it." He tapped the frost-drawing at the toll gate. "This one? Your friend's pen? That's a small hinge, well-oiled. Fine."

He tapped the garrison road. "This one. Ink and nerve. Also fine — ink is your trade and nerve is yours too, God help us both."

His finger moved to the third mark, the eel weir, and stopped. "This one isn't a hinge. This one's the whole door. And the door is held shut by a river that hates everybody equally." He straightened. "So here's my condition, Crane, and it's not a condition, it's the price, and you can pay it or walk. We go when I say. Not when the ice looks ready. Not when your fear says now or never. When I say. On the water, my word is the magistrate, the judge, and the weather all in one coat. Agree to that or find another boatman."

"I agree," I said. "Gladly." Gladly, because all my life I had wanted exactly one thing from authority: someone competent to obey.

"And mine," Nell said. "Two conditions. First — if it goes wrong at my father's gate, you never knew my name. You never met me, you never gave me drawing lessons, and I sold you the paper stock out of greed, nothing else. Swear it."

"I swear it."

"Second." She hesitated, and for a moment she was sixteen again, all the armor off. "When it's done — when the judge reads it, whatever happens after — come tell me. Not a letter. Come to the toll house and tell me to my face what forty-one people having happened sounds like. I want to hear it out loud. In words. I've been carrying the shape of it since my mother died and I need the sound."

The lamp fluttered. Outside, the river spoke again, longer this time, a groan that climbed and broke.

"I'll come tell you," I said. "Whatever it sounds like."

* * *

They left separately, Nell first with the ledger rewrapped in its sacking, then Tam to check his boat's seams by feel in the dark, and I stayed a moment alone in the boathouse with the lamp and the hull and the frost-map already sweating and blurring on the keel. Three gates. One hinge of ink, one of nerve, one of black grinding water. Hope assembled from parts, like Tam's boats — no plank new, every plank holding, the whole seaworthy only so long as nothing asked any plank to do more than its share.

I breathed on my fingers and drew one last mark in the frost beside the third gate: a small square with a diagonal, the cartographer's sign for a bridge — for a crossing that exists, that holds, that can be trusted. Then I wiped it away with my sleeve, because hope drawn in frost is not yet a bridge, and I had ten days and one signature left to make it true.

Ten days. And tomorrow night, by candlelight, I would sit down before a blank warrant and bring a dead man's hand back to life — and whatever answered me in that ink, I would have to carry across the river in my chest along with everything else.
