## 26. Forging the Master's Hand

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Silas finally sat at Vane's own desk.

She had avoided it for three months. Even after his drowning, even after she had begun sleeping in the workshop to keep watch over the atlas, she had left his desk untouched — its leather top worn pale in two elbow-shaped hollows, its inkwells arranged in his particular order (iron gall nearest the hand, walnut for washes, the little pot of gum arabic chipped at the lip and never replaced because he said it held his luck). To sit there was to admit he was not coming back. She had preferred the bench, where apprentices belonged, where she could pretend the chair behind her was merely empty rather than finished.

Tonight she pulled the chair out and sat down in his hollows, and they fit her almost exactly.

* * *

She had spent two days preparing, and both days had taught her the same humiliating lesson: that she knew Master Aldous Vane's hand better than she knew her own face, and that this knowledge was now the most dangerous thing she owned.

A guild travel pass was a simple document, deceptively so. A single sheet of rag paper, folded twice, stamped with the guild seal in red wax, bearing the name of the bearer, the purpose of travel, the destination, and the signature of a master of the guild. The garrison post at the river bend checked passes against the guild's register of masters — but only against the names, never against the hands. No one at a checkpoint in February read handwriting like a mapmaker did. They read *whether* it was written, not *who* had written it.

That was the flaw in the system, and the flaw was made of the same assumption that ran through everything: that paper told the truth because paper was expensive and seals were hard to counterfeit. Hale believed that too. It was, Silas thought, the one belief she could use against him.

But first she needed paper that would lie convincingly.

* * *

Vane's stock of guild paper lived in the third drawer of the flat file, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. She had counted it once, weeks ago, the way she counted everything now — forty-two sheets, each watermarked along the left margin with the guild's device: a pair of dividers crossed over a river. She took one sheet now and held it to the candlelight, watching the watermark swim up like a fish under ice.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake; instead they moved with the terrible calm of a body doing something it had already decided to do, the way Tam Ossler's hands went about rowing whether the river was kind or murderous.

She cut the sheet to size with the bone folder, scoring and folding until the creases were soft and old-looking. New folds betrayed new documents. She pressed the fold under Vane's paperweight — a river stone, smooth as an egg, which he had carried from Marrowfleet thirty years ago and which she had never thought about until Edith Marr put that scrap of dyed cloth in her palm. The stone had come from a village that no longer appeared on any map. He had kept it on his desk his whole life, in plain sight, weighting down the papers of a town that had paid him to forget it.

She wondered if he had ever noticed what he was doing. She wondered if he had noticed and done nothing. Both possibilities hurt in different places, like a crack running through a single pane of glass.

* * *

Ink first. This was the part she could do without thinking, and she let herself do it without thinking, because thinking would undo her.

Vane mixed his iron gall himself, and his mixture ran darker than the shop-bought sort, with a faint blue-black heart to it when it dried — a color she could find blindfolded among fifty samples. She ground the gall nuts fine, added the rainwater he always insisted on (*never the well water, Crane, the well water has iron enough of its own*), and a whisper of cloves, because he liked the smell and claimed — wrongly, she was sure — that it kept the ink from freezing. The workshop filled with the scent of it, and the scent did what scents do: it reached backward through time and set the whole room ringing. For a moment she was nine years old again, standing on a stool because the bench was built for men, watching him write while snow came down outside the window like static, like the world erasing itself.

*Accuracy is a moral act,* he had told her, the first winter, guiding her wrist along a coastline. *Anyone can draw a line. The promise is that the line means something. A map is a promise, Silas. Break it once and every map you ever make is broken.*

She dipped the pen. She began to write.

Not the pass itself — not yet. First, practice. On a scrap of the cheapest paper she wrote the phrase *By order of the Guild of Surveyors and Mapmakers of Vellamouth* fourteen times, varying the pressure, varying the slant, letting her own habits fall away one by one the way she'd strip a coastline of its false detail before drawing the true one. Vane wrote quickly but never carelessly; his letters leaned forward like men walking into wind. His capital G was a strange thing, almost a spiral, drawn in a single unbroken motion — a habit from his youth in the Low Countries that he'd never corrected because he said a man's letters were his fingerprints and he saw no reason to sand his off.

His lowercase r ended in a small downward hook, like a bird settling. His t was crossed low, impatiently. He dotted his i's slightly to the right of center, always, without exception, and Silas had once asked him why and he had said, *Because I am always slightly ahead of myself, Crane. It is my best quality and my worst.*

On the sixteenth attempt she laid the scrap beside a genuine letter of his — one of the dozen she had saved from the burn pile of ordinary correspondence, invoices and supply orders, the mundane sediment of a dead man's days — and she could not tell which line was which. She held them under the magnifying lens until her eye watered. Two hands, one hand. The apprentice had become the master's echo so thoroughly that the echo could now speak for him.

That should have felt like tribute. It felt like theft.

* * *

She let the candle gutter lower. Outside, the frost had furred the window glass in feathers, and beyond it Vellamouth lay quiet under a moonless sky, every chimney smoking, every door barred, the town holding its breath the way it had held it all winter — a town that knew something was rotten in the magistrate's cellar and had decided, collectively and without discussion, to know nothing at all. She had grown up in that silence. Orphaned at seven, passed between aunts who passed her on like an unwanted parcel, she had learned early that survival meant seeing everything and saying nothing, until Vane had seen her sketching boats on the quay and said the only sentence that had ever made her future feel possible: *Who taught you to draw water like that?*

No one, she had said. No one had taught her anything.

*Then I will,* he said. And he had. And everything he taught her she was about to use to betray the thing he served — though she could not make herself say *betray him*, because the guild was not the man, and the man, whatever he had done with the ledger's money, had died trying to unmake what he'd helped build. Hadn't he? She still didn't know. She might never know. The letter she hoped for, the explanation she dreamed of finding tucked in some drawer, did not exist as far as she knew. All she had was his handwriting, and tonight she was going to wear it like a mask.

* * *

The text of the pass she composed carefully, in the guild's plain formulaic language, which Vane had always found tiresome and copied out with visible irony:

*Let it be known that the bearer, Silas Crane, apprentice of this guild, travels upon guild business, being charged with the conveyance of paper stock to the county survey office at Ashford Mills. Passage granted through all tolls and posts within the guild's jurisdiction. Valid until the feast of Saint Matthias.*

Ashford Mills was real, small, and far enough that no one would send word ahead quickly. Paper stock was plausible — the guild shipped reams constantly, and Nell's father's toll lists showed cartloads of it passing weekly. An apprentice carrying paper was the least remarkable thing on any road in England. The dates were right; she had checked the almanac twice. Saint Matthias gave her eleven days. Tam said the ice would give in ten, maybe fewer. The arithmetic was tight as a drumhead, and she had built it that way deliberately, because slack invited inspection.

Then the seal.

Vane's seal matrix hung on a nail inside the cabinet where he kept his valuable tools — brass, worn smooth, the guild's dividers-and-river rendered in mirror image. She heated the sealing wax over the candle, watching it go from stick to bead to a slow red pool, and her stomach turned over, because the smell of sealing wax was the smell of every finished map, every completed commission, every moment in seven years when a piece of work was finally, truly done. Sealing wax meant *it is finished and it is true.* She had been raised on that smell the way other children were raised on bread.

She poured it onto the fold. She pressed the matrix down. Held it. Counted to five, as he had taught her — *count, don't guess; guessing is how wax blisters* — and lifted it.

The guild's mark sat in the red wax, crisp and perfect and false.

* * *

And then there was only the name left, and she discovered she could not do it.

Not the forgery itself — her hand was ready, warmed, fluent in him. But the signature. *Aldous Vane.* Three words she had watched him write hundreds of times on hundreds of maps, the flourish on the final e trailing off like a wake behind a boat. Writing it would be the true crossing. Everything before was preparation; this was the act. Somewhere between the pen touching paper and the pen lifting, she would stop being an apprentice finishing her master's work and become a counterfeiter wearing a dead man's face.

She set the pen down. She got up. She walked the length of the workshop and back, past the wall map with its hidden heartbeat behind the frame, past the dummy atlas lying on the bench like a decoy duck, past the window with its feathered frost. Her breath made ghosts on the cold glass.

*I am doing this for you,* she said to the dark room, to the empty desk, to the man who had fished her out of a life with no prospects the way Tam had fished him out of the river — too late, but gently. *Do you understand? They will call it forgery. Let them. You taught me that a line must mean something. Well. These lines mean Marrowfleet. These lines mean forty-one people who were bought and drowned and scraped off the page. If my lines are crimes, then your lessons were crimes, and I learned them perfectly.*

The room said nothing. Rooms never did. But the river stone sat on the desk where he had kept it, weight on paper, Marrowfleet holding down the documents, and she chose to take that as an answer.

She sat back down. She dipped the pen.

*Aldous Vane.*

The G-spiral of the opening letter — no, wait, his name began with A, and his A was a narrow tent with a high crossbar, struck through with a single confident stroke. Then the l's, tall and level. The d with its looped ascender. The o closed tight. The u with its trailing exit stroke. The s that curled like a wave deciding to break.

Her hand shook on the final word. Not from fear. From grief arriving all at once, the way weather arrives, the way cold comes off a frozen river — no negotiation, no warning, just the whole weight of it landing. Because writing his name was the closest she would ever come to hearing his voice again, and she was using it to lie. Because the skill that made the lie possible was his gift to her, given freely across a workbench, over seven winters, in patience and clove-scented ink and the sentence *a map is a promise* — and promises, it turned out, could be forged by anyone who had been trusted with the original.

The tears came without drama, hot and fast, dropping toward the page. She jerked the document clear and let them fall on the scrap paper instead, on the sixteen practice signatures, blotting the ink of the truest ones. She cried the way she did everything now — quietly, efficiently, hating the efficiency, hating that even her grief had learned economy from a man who wasted neither ink nor time.

When it was done she sat looking at the signature until the candle flame steadied and her eyes stopped stinging. It was perfect. It was flawless. It was indistinguishable from the hand of Aldous Vane, Master of the Guild of Surveyors and Mapmakers of Vellamouth, drowned man, bribe-taker, witness-from-beyond, teacher.

He had signed his maps to certify that they were true. She had just signed hers to certify a lie that would carry the truth to safety. She could not decide if that made her a traitor to his memory or its last faithful servant, and she suspected the answer was that it made her both, permanently, and that this was simply what inheritance was: taking everything your dead leave you, the gifts and the debts together, and spending it as wisely as you can.

* * *

She worked until the candle was a puddle and the window glass had begun, very faintly, to grey.

There were other documents to prepare — a second copy of the pass sewn into her coat lining beside the ledger pages, a list of the checkpoint rotations Nell had copied from her father's desk, the route memorized and re-memorized until she could draw it blind: gate one at the toll, gate two on the garrison road, gate three at the ferry below the post. Brack's name she had written and crossed out three times. Brack could not be bribed. Brack was the fixed rock in the channel, and rocks were not bribed, they were *read*, and she had not yet learned to read him.

She sanded the pass, folded it, and slid it into the oilskin wallet that would live against her ribs. Then she cleaned the pen — properly, as he had drilled her, nib wiped and dried or the gall would eat it overnight — and stood his tools back in their places, and looked at the desk that fit her almost exactly.

One more thing. She took a fresh sheet of cheap paper and wrote, in her own hand, plainly, without artifice: *If I do not come back, the atlas on the bench goes to the assize court at Ashford Mills, and the wall map is not to be taken down by anyone but me. — S.C.* She weighted it under the river stone, where Vane's luck lived.

Outside, the east had gone the color of pewter. In four hours Nell's cart would be at the door, loaded with honest bales of paper stock and one dishonest passenger. In five days, if the ice held, she would cross the black water at the third gate. In ten days, the thaw.

Silas banked the fire, took up her coat, and stood a moment in the middle of the workshop with her hand flat on the wall map — feeling, or imagining she felt, the hard rectangle of the original ledger through the canvas and the frame, the true heart of the house beating behind the plaster.

"Keep it safe," she told it. "I'll come back for you."

Then she heard, faint and unmistakable, the sound of cart wheels turning onto the lane in the dark — an hour early — and a knock at the workshop door that was not Nell's knock at all.
