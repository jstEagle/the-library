## 27. Departure

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width of tallow, and Silas let it. She wanted the dark to come slowly, the way winter did — not all at once, but in stages, each one easier to bear than a sudden ending would have been.

She had spent the evening saying goodbye to things without touching them.

It was a strange arithmetic, she thought, sitting at the bench where she had learned her letters and her lines and her silences: that a room could hold so much of a person and still be left behind intact. The workshop was exactly as Master Vane had kept it, or nearly so — she had restored it over three months, plank by plank, habit by habit, until any visitor might believe he had only stepped out to the chandlery and would return smelling of pitch and pipe smoke. The graver lay in its groove. The burnisher slept in its leather sleeve. His apron hung on its nail by the door, stiff with old size and older sweat, and she had not washed it because washing felt like erasure and she had sworn, somewhere in the marrow of her, never to erase again.

The atlas dummy sat on the bench beneath her hands. She had built it over four nights of careful forgery — no, not forgery, she corrected herself, *completion*, though the word rang false even in the privacy of her own skull. It looked finished. That was the point. It looked like an atlas that had been waiting only for its master's final signature, a work interrupted but innocent, containing coastlines and soundings and nothing else. She had aged the paper with tea and smoke. She had blotted a corner with rainwater to suggest neglect. She had even, in a flourish that would have made Vane raise his eyebrows, drawn a small compass rose on the title page with a single flaw in its northern point — the kind of imperfection a proud craftsman would leave and curse himself for later.

If they searched, let them find that. Let them open it page by page and find forty miles of honest coastline and no ledger sewn anywhere in its binding. Let Brack run his humorless fingers along the spine and feel nothing but glue and thread.

Her real copies rode against her ribs, sewn into the lining of her coat in flat packets wrapped in oilcloth, thin as communion wafers. When she stood, they shifted against her heartbeat like a second pulse. She was learning their weight the way a sailor learns ballast — not as burden but as the thing that kept her upright.

* * *

There were hours yet before dawn. She used them the way her master had taught her to use the last light of a working day: not for new labor, but for checking.

*A map is a promise,* Vane used to say, tapping the bench with two fingers. *Anyone can draw a line, girl. The promise is that the line is true — that a man who trusts your river mark won't drown trusting you.*

So she checked.

The wall map hung above the hearth where it had hung for eleven years — Vane's largest commission, the great survey of the Vellamouth reach, six feet of vellum in a frame of blackened oak. Inside its frame, pressed flat between backing boards, lay the original ledger: the true pages, the magistrate signatures, the payments signed and dated, the last entry written the week before Marrowfleet drowned. In plain sight. Hanging in the most conspicuous place in the room, because the best hiding place is the one a searcher has already dismissed.

She had oiled the frame's joints so they wouldn't creak if anyone moved it. She had dusted the glass. She had even straightened the map itself, nudging it a hair's width to the left so that it hung precisely level — because Vane had been precise, and a crooked map in an empty workshop would say *someone was here in haste*, and haste was the one confession she could not afford to make.

The hearth held banked coals that would die within the hour. Good. A cold workshop was an abandoned workshop; warmth suggested recent life.

She walked the room as she had walked it a thousand times, and for the first time she let herself see it as a stranger would — as Hale would, if he came. The smell of linseed and cut paper and the faint iron tang of gall ink. The racks of pens sorted by nib width. The window with its north-facing light, the best light, the honest light, which showed every flaw in every line and had shown her, once, the scraped-away name of a drowned village.

On the shelf above the drying rack stood the jar of Marrowfleet dye-cloth Edith had given her — no. She had taken that with her weeks ago; it was folded now inside her coat beside the oilcloth packets, a scrap of patterned wool no bigger than her palm, dyed in colors that existed nowhere else in the county since the sluice gates closed. She touched it through the fabric. Forty-one people, unrecorded. She was carrying them out of town the only way they could be carried: as ink, as testimony, as lines on paper that someone in authority might finally be made to read.

* * *

Near the end — she could tell by the quality of the black beyond the windows, that particular velvet that comes an hour before first light — she allowed herself the thing she had forbidden herself all evening.

She sat down at Vane's chair.

Not her stool at the drafting table. His chair, the wide-backed one behind the master's desk, which she had never once sat in while he lived, because apprentices did not, because the world had orders and she had been raised inside them. She sat in it now and it swallowed her, too big by half, smelling of him still — tobacco and tallow and something underneath that was simply *old man*, the scent of a body that had worked sixty years at the same bench and died in cold water with its boots full of river.

"I'm taking your atlas," she said aloud, to the dark room. Her voice sounded wrong in it, too young. "Or finishing it. I haven't decided which word is truer."

The room said nothing. Rooms are loyal that way.

"You took their money." The words came out flatter than she intended. "You took it, and then you wrote it down, and then you tried to give it back by giving them the truth instead. I don't know yet whether that makes you a coward who repented or a brave man who broke. I think maybe I'll know when this is done. I think maybe you didn't know either."

Somewhere across town, in the direction of the garrison, a dog began to bark and then thought better of it. The coals ticked as they cooled. Frost was forming on the inside of the window glass, feathering up from the sill in patterns no cartographer could improve upon — nature's own marginalia, drawn and redrawn nightly, signed by nobody.

She got up from his chair. She would not sit in it again until — she refused to finish the thought. *Until*, she told herself firmly, and left it hanging there like a bridge with no far bank.

* * *

The last preparations were small ones, and she performed them with the tenderness of ritual.

She filled the inkwells — all of them, even the ones she wouldn't need, because an apprentice leaves the workshop ready to be worked in. That was the discipline. You did not leave a bench dirty; you did not leave a pen dry. Whoever came after her, whatever happened, the room should say: *we were interrupted, not ended.*

She laid out the day's imagined work on her own drafting table: a fair copy of the estuary chart, weighted at the corners with the four brass seights, exactly as she'd have left it any ordinary night. The dummy atlas on the bench, angled as if just set down. His apron on its nail. His chair pushed in.

Then she stopped, hand on the shutter, and went back for one thing more.

From the drawer of the master's desk she took the stub of red sealing wax and the guild seal — no, not the seal; that stayed, that was guild property and its absence would be noticed before she reached the toll gate. She took instead a clean sheet of his good rag paper and wrote, quickly, in her plainest hand:

*Gone to Marsh Bend to survey the winter channel for the spring charts. Back within the fortnight. — S.C.*

A lie with a surveyor's grammar. If anyone found it, it bought her days. If Hale found it, it told him she was frightened and running blind, and perhaps — this was the gambit, and she knew how thin it was — perhaps it would make him look at rivers instead of roads.

She left it weighted under the seight on the estuary chart, half-covered, the way a note is left when a person expects to return and does not trouble to make it prominent. Every detail argued *I will be back.* She had become, she realized with a strange cold pride, very good at drawing rooms that lied.

* * *

The key.

That was the last of it, and the hardest.

Vellamouth workshops did not carry keys home. Keys hung under stones, under the third flagstone by the door where generations of masters had left them for clerks arriving early and apprentices returning late — a whole civic faith built on the assumption that a town's men were mostly trustworthy and its locks were mostly ceremonial. Vane had mocked the custom once, gently: *Any thief worth fearing can pick a lock, Silas. The stone is for the honest people.* Then he had smiled at whatever he saw in her face and added, *Which is most of them. Remember that, when you're tempted to forget it.*

She knelt in the frozen yard. The flagstone came up with a crackle of ice in its seam, and the iron key lay in its bed of grit exactly where it had lain since the night he drowned — since Tam Ossler had brought her the news and she had walked here through the rain and lifted the stone with hands that didn't shake until afterward, until she was inside with the door bolted and three months of his unfinished work staring at her from every wall.

Three months she had kept the key on a cord around her neck. Three months the workshop had been hers alone, sealed, sovereign, the last country where Vane was still alive.

Now she set the key down in the grit, and it made a small iron sound against the stone, and that sound undid her.

She knelt there in the freezing yard with her bare hands on the frost and wept — not the grief of the funeral, which had been shock wearing grief's clothing, nor the grief of the ledger's discovery, which had been anger wearing grief's clothing, but the plain article itself: she was leaving her home, and she did not know if she would see it again, and the door she was locking was the door of everything she had been.

The sky in the east had gone from velvet to slate. Somewhere a cockerel made an experimental noise and reconsidered.

She put the flagstone back. Pressed it flush. Scattered a pinch of grit across the seam with a surveyor's eye for what disturbed ground looks like — almost nothing, almost exactly nothing.

* * *

The coat was heavy with its hidden pages. The satchel on her shoulder held her drawings — Vane's portrait, done the second year of her apprenticeship, his eyes caught mid-look with that expression between amusement and assessment that had been his entire manner of love; her own earliest sketches, wobbly coastlines and a cat drawn eleven times badly; the scrap of Marrowfelt cloth wrapped around them all like a reliquary. She had debated leaving the satchel. She could not. Some cargo is not evidence. Some cargo is the reason there is anyone left to carry evidence.

At the gate she stopped once and looked back.

The workshop stood black against the graying sky, its chimney cold, its windows reflecting the first pewter hint of dawn. The wall map hung inside, invisible from here, holding its secret the way the river held its dead — patiently, in the dark, waiting for a reading.

"Keep it safe," she whispered, and heard how foolish it was, and said it anyway, because a promise spoken is a promise half-kept.

Then she turned south, toward the toll gate and the long white road, toward Nell waiting with a cart of guild paper stock and a smile loaded like a weapon, toward three gates and a frozen river and a court that might or might not listen — and she walked, and did not run, because running draws the eye, and she had learned from the best teacher in England how a person moves through a landscape without disturbing it.

Behind her the frost thickened on the workshop windows, drawing its nightly coastlines on the glass, naming villages that no longer existed, and beginning, with the first gray touch of morning, silently, to melt.
