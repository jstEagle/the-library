## 28. Gate One: The Toll

The cart smelled of size and rags.

Silas had spent the night before making it so — boiling hide scraps and old parchment ends in the workshop's copper until the whole room stank of glue, then drenching burlap sacks with the stuff and stacking them under a tarpaulin that had genuinely once covered guild paper deliveries. Beneath the sacks, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn flat against her ribs beneath her coat, lay the ledger copies: thirty-one leaves of Vane's numbers and her own testimony of them, folded small enough to sit against her heart like a second, harder heartbeat. Above them, on the seat beside her, she'd arranged the props with a forger's love: a guild manifest in her best imitation of the clerk Hobbes's cramped hand, a delivery chit, a stub of red sealing wax still warm-looking as if pressed an hour ago. Everything true except the truth.

She drove out before the sun cleared Marrowfleet Hill, when the frost stood in the wheel ruts like sugar and her breath made a small ghost that walked ahead of the mare. The mare was Tam's loan, a placid gray named Bess who had hauled eel traps for eleven years and could not be persuaded to hurry by anything short of fire. That was fine. Hurry was suspicion. Slow was paper stock going to the fair at Colderbridge, slow was boring, slow was invisible. She repeated it to herself the way Vane used to repeat meridian corrections: *slow is invisible, slow is invisible.*

The road to the toll gate ran along the top of the flood bank, and from there Silas could see the river lying white and solid to the far willows, the ice thick enough now that the winter fair tents had gone up midstream like pale mushrooms. Under that whiteness, somewhere off the ferry stair, lay the place where Tam had pulled Aldous Vane from the water three months ago, his hands still ink-stained, his coat pockets full of nothing because someone had emptied them. She did not look. Looking was another kind of hurry.

The toll gate came into view the way it always did: a timber arch across the causeway, the toll house squatting beside it with its chimney smoking, and the barrier arm raised against a sky the color of unprimed linen. Two figures waited. Nell, in her father's too-big wool coat, arms crossed against the cold, breath steaming around her grin even from fifty yards. And behind her, stamping his boots on the frozen mud, a garrison man in a gray cloak — not Brack, thank every saint who'd listen, but one of the younger ones whose name Silas didn't know and therefore couldn't predict.

Her mouth went dry. She had planned for Brack or for no one. She had not planned for *unknown*.

*Slow is invisible.* She shook the reins gently. Bess clopped forward at the speed of porridge.

"Morning!" Nell called, loud enough to wake the dead and any magistrates pretending to sleep. "Morning, mistress! What have we got, then?" She was already coming around to the back of the cart, hauling herself up onto the tailboard with the easy grace of a girl who'd climbed this cart since she could walk, since it had been her father's cart and her father's gate and the whole world had been eight feet of timber arch and the smell of horse.

"Guild paper stock," Silas said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; she'd rehearsed steadiness all night along with the route. "For the Colderbridge fair clerks. Manifest's in my pocket."

"Paper." Nell wrinkled her nose theatrically and thumped one of the glue-stiff sacks. "Smells like a tannery married a library."

"That's the size," Silas said. "Boiled hide. It's how they make it stick."

Behind Nell, the soldier had drifted closer. Up close he was young — nineteen perhaps, with the raw face and chapped knuckles of a boy who'd been somebody's farmhand two winters ago and hadn't learned yet how to stand like a wall instead of like a boy. He looked at the sacks. He looked at Silas. His hand went, uncertainly, toward the tarpaulin.

"Guild freight goes through free," Nell said quickly, hopping down between him and the cart with a smile that rearranged her whole face into something warm and slightly wicked. "Papa's rule, and the magistrate countersigned it — you can see the seal on the board there if you can read, which I'm sure you can, being garrison and all."

"I'm supposed to check loads," the boy said. His voice cracked on *loads*, and Silas saw him hear it crack, and saw the flush climb his neck.

"You're supposed to check *carts*," Nell said, "and this is the only cart today, and I've already checked it, and I checked it yesterday, and the day before, and in all that time do you know what I found? One live hen, two kegs of sprats, and a fiddle nobody could play." She leaned in, conspiratorial, close enough that Silas — watching from the driver's bench with her hands folded very still in her lap — could see the exact architecture of the flirtation: the tilt of the head, the lowering of the voice, the eyes that stayed on his a half-second longer than courtesy required. "If you want to look in the sacks, I won't stop you. But you'll be smelling of boiled hide till Candlemas, and the other boys will laugh at you, and I'll tell them it was me who dared you."

The boy's flush deepened. He looked at Nell the way men looked at things they wanted and were afraid to reach for. "It's procedure," he said, but the word had gone soft.

"Procedure," Nell agreed solemnly. "Very important. My papa says without procedure we'd be nothing but a road with a fence on it." She plucked at the boy's cloak collar with two fingers, straightening it, a gesture so casually intimate that Silas felt heat rise in her own cheeks on his behalf. "There. Now you look procedural."

And the boy laughed — a helpless, cracking, nineteen-year-old laugh — and stepped back, and the war for the toll gate was over before anyone had admitted it was fought.

Nell turned and beckoned Silas forward with two fingers, all business again, though her eyes were dancing. "Manifest," she said, holding out her hand.

Silas passed it down. Nell read it upside down first, then right side up, mouthing the words with enormous diligence, then stamped the chit with her father's toll stamp — a worn wooden thing that left more smudge than impression — and waved the barrier arm up with a flourish. "Guild freight. Free passage. Mind the pothole past the mile oak, it'll have your axle."

"Thank you," Silas said, and put her weight on the reins, and Bess rolled forward under the arch.

She was through. The wheels crunched past the shadow line of the gate, past the toll house, past the boy in the gray cloak who was watching Nell and not the cart, and the road opened ahead of her — bare fields, hedgerows furred with frost, the long gray thread of it running south toward Colderbridge and the assize court and everything that mattered.

She did not breathe yet. That was the discipline Vane had taught her without ever naming it: *finish the stroke before you lift the pen.* A coastline isn't done when the last headland is drawn; it's done when the ink is dry and the map is closed and your hand has stopped shaking. She kept Bess walking. She kept her shoulders loose. She counted fence posts the way she'd count seconds — one, two, five, ten — and at twenty she permitted herself one glance in the side of the polished brass lantern mounted on the cart's front board.

In the curved reflection, small and warped, the toll gate receded. Nell was leaning on the barrier arm talking to the soldier. The soldier was laughing. Nobody was following.

At forty fence posts, where the road bent around the mile oak — the same potholed stretch Nell had warned of, bless her — Silas finally let the air out of her lungs, a long controlled exhale that fogged the frost on her sleeve. Her hands, when she looked at them, were steady on the reins. Her heart was not; it went on hammering against the ledger pages sewn at her ribs, as if the paper itself had a pulse, as if the forty-one drowned names stitched invisibly into those copied lines were trying to beat their way out into the morning.

"One gate," she whispered. "Two remain."

* * *

The first mile after the bend she allowed herself nothing — no thought beyond the road, the mare, the weather. This too was craft. Vane had taught her that a surveyor who thinks about the cliff while measuring the field will walk into the sea. So she measured the field: the ruts, the light, the mare's ears swiveling at crows, the smell of glue rising faintly from the sacks behind her like the ghost of the workshop she might never see again. She had left the workshop intact. The wall map hung in plain sight with the original ledger sleeping in its frame, and the dummy atlas sat squared on the bench, and the key was under the stone where Vane always left it, and if Hale's men came while she was gone they would find exactly what a guilty woman running would leave behind — or exactly what an innocent woman finishing her master's work would leave behind, depending on the eye that looked. She had tried to make it read both ways. She would know by spring whether forgery worked on rooms as well as paper.

At the second milestone she let herself think of Nell, and smiled despite everything.

The girl had taken to the plan the way riverbank takes seed — instantly, greedily, without asking what it would cost. *You wave the cart through,* Silas had told her two nights ago in the toll house, over Nell's father's bad tea, *and you say nothing else, ever, to anyone.* And Nell had said, *I know what my mother's village was called,* and that had been the end of the discussion, because Nell's mother had come from Marrowfleet and married the toll keeper and moved eight miles upriver and lived — the only one of her family who had. Forty-one people unrecorded, Silas had told her at the fair, and Nell had gone quiet in that particular way, and Silas had understood that she wasn't recruiting an ally anymore; she was arming a grief.

The soldier had been luck, though. Pure luck, or whatever passes for luck when a girl spends years learning which guards linger and why. *That one's new,* Nell had said just yesterday, nodding at the gray cloak across the yard. *Came down from the fort at Hallowmas. Eager. But eager boys are easy, Silas — they haven't learned to be ashamed of wanting anything yet.*

She had been right. Silas owed her. The debt ledger in Silas's head — the one that had started with Vane and grown to include Edith, Tam, Brack, Nell — gained another line, written in the same ink as all the others: *paid forward, never paid off.*

By the third milestone the sun had climbed enough to burn the frost off the southern hedge sides, and the road began to fill with ordinary traffic: a drover walking geese to market, their noise like a quarrel in heaven; a carter with lime; two women on foot with baskets, gossiping at the volume of women who have walked together for thirty years. Silas nodded to each of them and received nods back, and each nod was a small miracle of ordinariness. To these people she was a tradeswoman hauling paper. The most dangerous thing about her, as far as they knew, was the smell of the glue.

She stopped at noon at the standing stone where the roads forked — south to Colderbridge, east down to the river landing where Tam would wait tomorrow night with his boat below the garrison post. She watered Bess from the trough, ate bread and hard cheese standing up, and let herself, for ten minutes only, unfold the thought she'd been keeping folded since dawn.

Gate one had opened. But the plan of three gates needed every part to hold, and parts failed. Brack was off-roster sometimes; Tam had said the ice would break early, maybe within days; and somewhere behind her, in Vellamouth, Hale would soon learn that a cartload of guild paper stock had passed the toll the same week a warrant expired unserved. He was not a stupid man. Stupid men burned records in cellars and got caught. Hale moved like water finding the low ground — patient, level, always toward the crack in things.

And she had refused him. She heard again the click of the coin he'd left on her bench, payment or warning, and her own voice declining too smoothly, and the way he'd smiled at the smoothness, cataloguing it. Men like Hale didn't chase carts. They changed what the carts carried, or they changed what the road meant. By tonight there might be a rider on this very road asking after a woman with a gray mare. By tomorrow, perhaps, the word *paper* would have a different weight at every checkpoint between here and Colderbridge.

She folded the thought away. Ten minutes was the allotment. She climbed back onto the bench, clicked to Bess, and took the south road.

* * *

The afternoon went gold and then pewter. Clouds came up from the southwest in long gray shelves, promising sleet by dark, and Silas found herself glad of it — bad weather was a friend to travelers who wished to be unmemorable. She passed the lime carter again, camped at the roadside; passed the geese, now penned in a farmyard, still quarreling. At the fourth milestone a rider passed her going north, and her whole body clenched around the ledger until the rider resolved into a priest on a mule, and then unclenched so slowly it hurt.

Relief, she was learning, was a liar. It arrived early, dressed as safety, and it wanted only one thing: for her to stop looking. Vane's margins had taught her that too — *the measurements that don't match are where the story lives.* Somewhere behind her, Nell was still smiling at a boy in a gray cloak, and the smile would keep him at the gate for an hour, maybe two, but not forever. Somewhere behind her, the road bent back toward Vellamouth, and along that road, sooner than she wanted, would come whatever Hale sent.

So she drove. She drove through the first spits of sleet and the first true dark, and when the Colderbridge lights finally pricked the horizon like pins in black cloth, she counted them twice, because counting was what mapmakers did when they needed the world to be real.

One gate down. The mare's hooves rang on the frozen ruts, carrying her toward the garrison road, toward Brack, toward whatever waited at the second turning of her life — and behind her, faint under the sleet-wind, Silas thought she heard the river beginning, very quietly, to sing against the underside of the ice.
