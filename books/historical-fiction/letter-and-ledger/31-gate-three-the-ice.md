## 31. Gate Three: The Ice

The garrison post at Ferry Reach was meant to be the hardest gate, and instead it was a door standing open on an empty room.

Silas stopped at the edge of the road and looked at it for a long moment before she trusted it. The building was low and stone-shouldered, built to watch the river where the channel ran narrowest, its single window facing the water like a half-closed eye. In summer she had seen soldiers there in ones and twos, boots up on the table, dice clicking, a kettle going. Now the window was dark. The door stood ajar by a hand's width, swinging gently in the wind off the ice, and from somewhere beyond it came the sound of men — not the bored murmur of a garrison but a crowd's noise, voices raised, a shout of alarm or delight that she could not tell apart at this distance.

They were all at the bank. She understood it before she had finished the thought: the ice was going.

She had heard it herself for the last hour as she walked down from the garrison road — a groaning deep under the world, long and slow, like some enormous animal turning over in its sleep. Tam had warned her. *Ten days,* he'd said at the thaw-watch, squinting downriver with his ruined eye. *Maybe fewer. When she starts talking, don't be standing on her.* But hearing it and hearing it were different things. This was not a groan anymore. It was a conversation, crack answering crack along miles of black-and-white water, and every man whose orders put him within earshot had abandoned his post to go and watch the river break its winter fast.

The empty room breathed cold air at her when she pushed the door wide. A stool tipped over. Cards scattered on the floor, mid-game, faces up: the knave of clubs, the nine of hearts, a king nobody had come back to claim. A tin cup held tea gone grey with cold, a skin of ice across its surface thin enough to see through. On the wall hung the inspection ledger, chained at its corner the way guild ledgers were always chained, and beside it a list of rules in a clerk's careful hand, the ink faded where thumbs had rubbed it passing by.

*All persons crossing by ferry shall present papers for inspection.*

Nobody had come back to claim the king. Nobody was going to inspect her either. Silas felt the laughter rise in her and pressed it flat, because laughter this close to terror was a dangerous thing; it loosened the hands. She touched the coat lining instead — the copies, sewn flat against her ribs, warm from her own body — and stepped back out into the wind.

The path to the water was churned to mud and frozen again in ridges. At the top of the bank, the whole world opened out.

The Vellamouth River had been a road all winter. She had crossed it a hundred times in thought if not in body, the white span of it holding carts and skaters and the winter fair's tents, solid as any street in town. Now it was coming apart. Downstream the main flow had already opened, a seam of black water widening hour by hour, steaming faintly in the cold air like something alive and breathing. Upstream the ice still held, but it was groaning, shifting, its edges lifting and dropping against the banks with a sound like slow thunder. And between those two conditions — between the water that had won and the ice still fighting — lay the broken channel, the crossing place, a mile of floes grinding and tilting in the current, plates of ice the size of barn doors shouldering one another, riding up over each other's backs with a shriek of splitting wood, then sliding under and vanishing.

Tam's boat waited at the foot of the bank, pulled up among the reeds, and Tam himself stood knee-deep in the dead rushes, watching the channel with his whole body, the way a man watches a fight he has money on.

"You're late," he said, without turning. "I said dawn."

"The garrison road took longer than I planned." She did not say *Brack*. Some things she was keeping even from herself until she was safely on the other side. "Your ice is early."

"My ice is right on time," Tam said. "It's the year that's early." He turned then and looked at her, at the coat, at the way her hand rested against her ribs without seeming to rest anywhere at all. His face was a map of old weather, creased and salt-stained, and his good eye read her as easily as she read a coastline. "You've got it on you."

"Yes."

"Not in a satchel."

"No."

He nodded slowly. "Good," he said, and she knew what he meant, because he had heard about the fair, everyone had heard about the fair, how a traveling clerk had been taken at the garrison post with copies on him and everything in his bags confiscated inside an hour. Things carried loose got taken. Things carried against the skin stayed with you, or you both went under together. "Then listen. It'll take a quarter hour to cross if the channel holds where it is. It won't hold where it is. So it'll take longer, or it'll take us, and we won't know which till we're in the middle." He spat into the reeds. "When I say lie flat, you lie flat. When I say bail, you bail. Don't pray. Praying takes your hands off the work."

"I don't pray," Silas said.

"Good girl," said Tam, and shoved the boat into the water.

* * *

The first ten yards were easy, and that frightened her more than anything that came after.

The near channel was slack water behind a shelf of shore ice, black and glassy, and the boat slid over it without a sound, oars dipping and rising, dripping light. Above them the ice shelf curved away like the lip of a cup. Silas sat in the stern with her hands flat on her knees and made herself look at the far bank — a gray line of alders, a thread of smoke, the road south that led, after three days' hard travel, to the assize town and the visiting judge and the reading of the ledger aloud in chambers. She had rehearsed the words so many times they had worn smooth as river stones. *I have evidence. I am willing to testify.* Fourteen words. She carried them alongside forty-one names she had never met, and Edith Marr's scrap of cloth in her pocket dyed in the Marrowfleet pattern, blue and rust, small enough to close her fist around.

Then the current took them, and the world began to move.

It happened between one stroke and the next. The slack water ended and the river's true voice started up beneath them, deep and muscular, and suddenly they were not gliding but being carried, swung sideways, the bow slewing toward a floe the size of a hayrick that came at them out of the steam and the spray. Tam swore with great fluency and no volume, leaned into the oars, and the boat answered — barely — scraping past the floe's edge with a sound like a knife drawn along a whetstone. Silas saw the underside of it as they passed: green-black ice, thick as a wall, streaming with water, older than the winter, older perhaps than the town.

"Sit forward," Tam snapped. "Weight amidships. You're not cargo, sit *properly*."

She moved. The channel narrowed ahead where two great pans had jammed together, humping up in the middle into a ridge of shattered ice like the spine of some drowned animal breaking the surface. The current funneled through the gap and the water there was moving faster than a horse walks, corrugated with standing waves, and the noise was tremendous now, the grinding of the floes a continuous roar punctuated by cracks like musket fire. Spray came off the ridge and froze on the gunwales. Her breath froze on the wool over her mouth. Somewhere under that roar was the river bottom, and above it only six inches of oak planking and one old man's arms.

"Hold," Tam said, and then, "—no. No, we go. Left side, hard—"

A floe the size of a threshing floor detached itself from the upstream jam and swung down at them like a door closing. There was no time to be afraid. There was time only to watch Tam do the arithmetic — angle, current, the length of his oars — and to feel the boat leap as he gave everything he had, and to hear the floe pass them close enough that its shadow went over her like a bird's, close enough that she could have laid her hand on it, trailing beads of frozen spray, humming with the pressure of the whole river behind it.

And then they were through the narrows, and into the middle of the channel, and the middle of the channel was where the river kept its teeth.

* * *

Later, trying to remember it, Silas would find her memory had done what memory does with terror: it broke the quarter hour into fragments and filed them without order. The hull shuddering as a submerged pan scraped along beneath them, a long shudder like a cart going over cobbles. Tam's voice, level as a surveyor's: *Bail.* And her snatching the wooden scoop from the bilge and throwing water over the side, arm burning, the water in the bottom only an inch but an inch that wanted to be five. The smell of the river opening, mud and rot and mineral cold, the winter's sealed breath let out all at once. A seal — impossible, absurd, this far upstream — no, not a seal, a drowned ewe, turning slowly under the clear black water, its wool waving like weed, and she looked away from it and made herself keep bailing.

Through all of it she was aware of one thing only, beneath everything, steady as a pulse: the weight against her ribs.

She had sewn the pages in herself, eleven nights ago, by candlelight, with Nell's help holding the seams. Twelve sheets of copied ledger, each entry traced letter by letter in her best hand, including the entry in her master's writing, including his name, including the sum. They lay against her body in a flat packet wrapped in oilcloth, and every jolt of the boat she felt them there, and every jolt she thought: *this is what I am carrying, this is Marrowfleet, this is Edith, this is the sluice gates closed on a sleeping village, this is the proof that the water was bought* — and the thought was not abstract. It had weight. It pressed on her ribs with every breath. She had never in her life carried anything so heavy that weighed so little.

Halfway across, the river changed its mind.

She felt it before she saw it — a change in Tam's rowing, a hitch, his head coming up, his one eye fixed on something downstream. Then she saw it too. Below them, where the open water widened toward the main channel, a floe had grounded and others were piling against it, and the jam was backing up the current like a dam, and the water around their boat was slowing, pooling, rising — no, not rising, *tilting*, the surface going glassy and wrong —

"Backwater," Tam said. "We've got maybe a minute before it breaks. When it breaks, everything moves at once. Get low. Hold onto the thwart and whatever else happens, hold on."

Silas got low. She gripped the thwart with both hands and put her cheek nearly to the wet planking and watched the water stand still around them, utterly silent in that strange suspended pool, floes locked on every side, the boat sitting motionless as if painted onto the scene. Steam rose off the black water between the pans. Far off, the ice boomed. Her heart was so loud she was sure Tam could hear it.

The jam broke with a sound like the sky tearing.

Everything moved at once, exactly as he had promised. The pooled water released in a single muscular shove and the boat went with it, slewed end-for-end, spun between two grinding pans that came together where they had been a boat-length apart, and Tam was rowing in air, literally rowing in air, the blades striking the surfaces of the closing floes and pushing off them like a man poling through a doorway that was shutting. Wood shrieked. Spray hit her face like flung gravel. The port gunwale dipped toward the black water and Silas threw her weight starboard without deciding to, the way her hands had once thrown themselves between a falling ink bottle and a finished chart, and the gunwale rose, grazed a pan with a bone-deep crunch, and cleared it.

And the packet moved.

She felt it happen — felt it as clearly as she had ever felt anything. The boat's lurch had dragged at her coat, and the stitching that held the oilcloth packet against her ribs — the seam Nell had held, the seam she had rushed, the seam on her left side that she had told herself was strong enough — had given. Not torn through; slipped, unraveling, the thread letting go stitch by stitch as the coat swung. The packet slid. She felt it travel the length of her torso like a slow cold finger, from ribs toward hip, from hip toward the open hem, and the hem was hanging open over the water, and below the hem the river ran black and fast between the grinding pans, and anything that fell into that water would be under the ice pack within seconds and gone to the sea within hours, and everything would be gone with it — the entries, the sums, the names, Edith's testimony, Vane's confession, Marrowfleet's one chance to exist on paper—

"Tam!" Her voice cracked apart on his name.

He didn't ask what. He saw it — her hand clamped at her side, her body twisted double over the gunwale, the white edge of oilcloth showing at her hip like a sliver of moon. He shipped one oar, caught the other in the water in a braking stroke that nearly tore it from his hands, and brought the boat's head up into the current so that it stalled, wallowing, the floes sliding past them on either side close enough to touch.

"Take your coat off," he said. "Quickly, now."

"If I let go of the hem it goes—"

"Take. The coat. Off." Each word struck like an oar blade. "Arms behind you, slide it down like a sleeve. I'll hold the boat. Move, girl — the jam below won't hold forever, and neither will I."

She did what he said, because there was nothing else in the world to do. She let go of the hem — the packet lurched downward and a sound came out of her that was not a word — and she forced both arms backward, shrugging the coat down off her shoulders, down her arms, the sleeves stiff with frozen spray, her hands finding the cuffs and dragging them over her knuckles while the coat hung from her wrists above the running water with the packet sliding down inside it, down, down toward the open cuff —

She caught it.

Her right hand closed on the oilcloth through the wool with the last joint of her fingers, an inch of cloth, less. For one full second she hung there, bent double over black water, arms pinned behind her, the river pulling at the coat's hem like a live thing tugging a rope, the floes grinding past, and Tam leaning on both oars with his face gray and set, holding the boat against the current by nothing but his arms and seventy years of knowing this river. Nobody spoke. The river spoke for all of them.

Then she walked her fingers up, one joint, another, closed her whole hand around the packet, and drew it up out of the cuff like drawing a sword.

"Coat's lost," Tam said. "Let it go."

She let it go. It fell onto the water, spread its arms, and slid under a passing pan of ice without a splash, gone as if it had never existed — and Silas knelt in the bottom of the boat in her shirt and waistcoat in the freezing wind, clutching twelve sheets of paper to her bare ribs, shaking so hard her teeth chattered, and discovered that she was laughing. Small, cracked, horrible laughter, tears freezing at the corners of her eyes, and Tam looked at her with his one good eye and did not laugh and did not tell her to stop.

"There's my coat in your satchel," she managed. "In the bag. Under the drawings—"

"Won't fit over that shirt, but we'll make it fit. Sit still." He rowed on. "You caught it."

"I caught it."

"Aye." He glanced back at the water where the coat had gone, then at the far bank, closer now, the alders resolving out of the gray. "River keeps what it's owed. Always has. Question is what it thinks it was owed." He grunted, hauling on the oars. "Reckon it can have the coat."

* * *

They grounded in the reeds below the alders, in slack water behind the last shelf of southern ice, and Tam held the boat steady while she climbed out on legs that did not entirely belong to her. The bank was mud and last year's leaves. The smoke she had seen was a charcoal burner's camp, distant and indifferent. The road went south.

Tam climbed out after her and stood a moment looking back at the channel they had crossed, at the floes riding down toward the sea in the gray light, carrying their winter with them. His hands were bleeding at the palms where the oars had worked, and he wiped them absently on his thighs.

"Quarter hour," he said. "Told you."

"It was nearer twenty."

"Don't tell anyone. I've a reputation." He looked at her then, at the ruined coat bundled under her arm with the packet rescued out of it, at her face. "You'll want to sew that back in proper before you walk a step further. And Crane." He waited until she looked at him. "That thing you caught today. It isn't yours. Never was. You're just the one carrying it. Remember the difference, when they come asking what gives you the right."

She didn't answer. There wasn't an answer yet.

She found a stump at the treeline, out of the wind, and sat and took out Nell's little sewing kit — needle, thread, thimble, the tools of a toll keeper's daughter who mended everything that passed her father's gate — and began, with fingers that would not stop trembling, to stitch the packet back into the lining of her one remaining coat. Stitch by stitch. The river roared behind her, breaking its winter all the way to the horizon. Ahead, the road ran south through the bare trees toward the assize town, and somewhere on that road, riding hard through the same thaw, she had no doubt at all, was Corven Hale.

Three days. Maybe fewer. She bit the thread, stood up, and began to walk.
