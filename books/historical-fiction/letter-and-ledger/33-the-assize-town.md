## 33. The Assize Town

The road forgot itself three miles from Harrowgate. What had been packed earth became cobbles, and what had been cobbles became a kind of grey stone worn round at the edges, so that Silas's cart wheels seemed to roll over the backs of sleeping animals. She had not slept properly in four days. The last inn had refused her — full, they said, though she'd seen the empty window with a candle in it — and the one before that had asked for papers she could show only by opening her coat, which she would not do in front of strangers.

So she came into the assize town at dusk on the fourth day, cold to the marrow, her hands stiff around the reins, and the town rose up before her like something out of her own work: a skyline of roofs and chimney smoke and the square tower of the shire hall, all of it rendered in the fading blue-grey light that mapmakers call *the hour of no shadows*, when every object looks equally true and equally false.

Harrowgate was larger than Vellamouth by perhaps five times, but it had the same architecture of authority. There was the market square with its iron ring where the pillory stood. There was the guild hall with its carved crest over the door — a river and a set of scales, which struck Silas as either very honest or very cynical. And there, at the top of the hill above the town, the courthouse: a long low building of pale stone with windows already lit against the dark, gold light lying across the cobbles like spilled oil.

She stopped the cart at the bottom of the hill and looked at it for a long time.

Inside her coat, against her ribs, the ledger copies lay sewn between two layers of linen, flat and patient. Forty-one names lived in that paper. Edith's testimony lived there. Three magistrate signatures lived there. Vane's own hand lived there, in an entry she still could not read without her throat closing. She had carried them through a toll gate, past Brack's steady eyes, and across a river that had tried to eat them halfway over. She had traded her satchel — Vane's portrait, six years of apprenticeship sketches, the drawing of her own hands learning to hold a pen — to keep that paper dry. Her hands had chosen without consulting her, and she had hated them ever since, and she had not yet decided whether the hatred was grief or shame or simply exhaustion wearing another face.

The courthouse windows glowed. Somewhere inside that building was a clerk who took filings, a judge who read them, and a system that — if you believed what Vane had taught her — bent toward accuracy the way water bends toward the sea.

*A map is a promise,* he had said. She wondered, sitting in the cold with the reins biting her fingers, whether a courtroom was also a promise, or only another kind of map — drawn by men who owned the land it described.

She turned the cart up the hill.

* * *

The courthouse did not close at dusk. That much she learned from a boy sweeping the steps, who told her, without being asked twice, that the assize sessions began in nine days and the building stayed lit day and night until then because "his Honour don't like arriving to a cold house." The judge was already in residence, then, or soon would be. The machinery was turning. Whether it would turn toward her or over her remained to be seen.

Inside, the entrance hall smelled of tallow, wet wool, and old paper — the smell of law, she thought, which is the smell of arguments that have outlived everyone who made them. A corridor ran back into shadow, doors on either side, most shut. At the far end, under a lamp, sat a man at a high desk, writing. He was sixty or more, thin as a plumb line, with spectacles pushed up onto his forehead and ink to his second knuckle. He wrote the way Vane used to write: completely, as though nothing else in the world were occurring.

Silas stood before the desk for a full minute before he noticed her.

Then he looked up, took her in — the mud to her knees, the frost-nipped cheeks, the coat too heavy for spring and too worn for winter, the ink stains on her right hand that no amount of scrubbing would lift — and said, in a voice like a drawer closing:

"Petitions are received at eight in the morning. Filings at ten. Complaints against persons are heard Thursdays." He returned to his page. "Today is Friday."

"I've come eighty miles," Silas said. "And I'll be gone before Thursday."

That brought his eyes up again. They were pale, watery, unimpressive eyes, and behind them something moved that was not unimpressive at all — the quick assessment of a man who has spent forty years deciding which petitions matter before their owners finish speaking.

"Eighty miles," he repeated. "From where?"

"Vellamouth."

Something changed in his face. Not alarm. Recognition — the recognition of a man hearing a word he has been expecting from a direction he hadn't predicted.

"Vellamouth," he said again. He set down his pen with great care, aligning it parallel to the desk's edge. "You are the fourth person this season to come to me from Vellamouth."

Silas's heart performed a slow, deliberate drop. "Who were the others?"

"That, madam, is not information I give away for the price of standing in my hall after hours." He removed his spectacles entirely now, folded them, and regarded her with the naked eyes of a man who trusted lenses less than his own judgement. "But I will tell you what I tell everyone, because it is the truth and the truth costs me nothing: this bench hears petitions every session. Most die in a drawer. Some die in a fire — we had a small one last year, in the records room, very unfortunate, very quickly extinguished." His voice did not change at all. "If your petition concerns matters of local governance, local magistrates, local money, I advise you to think carefully about how far you wish to carry it. The river runs both ways here too."

"I'm not carrying gossip," Silas said. "I'm carrying evidence."

"Everyone says that."

"It's written evidence. Survey records. Payment entries. Signatures."

Now he went very still. It was the stillness of a man who has handled dangerous documents before and knows their weight by description alone, the way Tam Ossler knew a storm by the smell off the water.

"Signatures," he said softly.

"Three of them."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he stood — slowly, joints cracking like green wood — and lifted the lamp from its bracket. "Come away from the open hall," he said. "Walls here have ears the way ships have rats. You learn to feed neither."

* * *

He led her down a side corridor into a narrow room lined floor to ceiling with pigeonholes, each stuffed with rolled parchment tied in ribbon — red for filed, black for pending, faded pink, Silas noted, for whatever third category existed that no one spoke of. A single candle burned in a sconce. The clerk — he gave his name as Underclerk Pellow, "under, always under; the over-clerk is a fool and the chief clerk is dead, which is the natural order" — closed the door, wedged a chair beneath the handle out of habit rather than necessity, and turned to face her.

"Now," he said. "You will speak plainly and you will speak once, because I am old and my memory is better than my patience. What is your name, your trade, and your grievance?"

"Silas Crane. Mapmaker's apprentice — journeyman now — of Vellamouth. My master was Aldous Vane, drowned last autumn." She watched the name land. It landed somewhere; Pellow's mouth tightened. "My grievance is that Marrowfleet was flooded two years ago when the flood-relief funds meant to save it were instead paid out — signed for — by three sitting magistrates of my town. One of them is Corven Hale. I have the payment ledger. I have a surviving witness who saw the sluice gates closed the night of the flood. And I have come to file it before the spring assizes."

The candle flame leaned in some draught she couldn't feel. Pellow did not move for so long that Silas counted his breaths: four, five, six.

"Marrowfleet," he said at last. "Forty-one souls, if the parish returns are honest, and they usually aren't. Entered in the county rolls as 'lost to inundation, cause: act of God.'" His lip curled slightly. "God gets blamed for a great deal in this county. He has never once been available for comment."

"You know about Marrowfleet."

"I know about everything that crosses this desk, Miss Crane, which is both my profession and my punishment." He lowered himself onto a stool. "Sit. No — stand, if you prefer, you have the look of someone who doesn't trust chairs. Very well. Now hear me, because what I say next is the only kindness anyone in this building will offer you free of charge."

He folded his hands.

"Corven Hale is not merely a magistrate of Vellamouth. Hale sits in concert with Justices Morden, Ashby, and the elder Quill — half the commission of the peace for this whole district, bound together twenty years deep in land deals, ferry rights, and each other's silence. When you accuse Corven Hale, you are not accusing a man. You are accusing a weather system. Do you understand? A man can be arrested. A weather system must simply be waited out — and it rains on whoever stands in the field."

"The judge—"

"The judge is the Honourable Mr. Justice Wrenn, and he is, God preserve me, an honest man, which makes him rarer and more dangerous than any crook in the building. Honest men attract honest cases, and honest cases get people hurt. Wrenn cannot be bought — I have watched men try, and I have watched him make one of them cry, which I enjoyed enormously — but Wrenn can be *buried*. Drowned in procedure, delayed, transferred, promoted away. There are men in London whose entire art is making honest judges into retired judges. If you bring this forward, you must bring it so clean, so documented, and so fast that burying it takes longer than judging it."

"Then I need to file today," Silas said. "Tonight. Before Hale knows I've arrived."

Pellow smiled, and it was not a pleasant sight. "Child, Hale has known since you crossed the ice. Word travels faster than horses — it travels by dinner tables and debt books and wives who write to each other. The question is not whether he knows. The question is what shape his counter-move takes, and whether you're still holding your paper when it arrives." He tapped the desk. "Show me nothing yet. Understand me — *nothing*. Not a page, not a scrap. If I take possession of your evidence tonight and anything happens to it between my hands and the record, you have lost everything and I have gained a funeral. Evidence lives longest in the body that bled for it."

"Then what do I do?"

"Tomorrow morning, eight o'clock, you stand in the petition queue like any widow with a boundary dispute. You state your business aloud — 'filing of documentary evidence in a matter touching public funds' — those words exactly, so the hall hears it, so witnesses hear it, so that from the first moment this exists publicly and cannot vanish privately. You ask for the oath. Sworn statements bind the court's attention in ways paper alone does not. Then—" he raised one finger "—you ask specifically for filing *before Justice Wrenn personally*, citing risk of local interference. That phrase is a key. It fits a lock Wrenn installed himself two years ago, after our little fire in the records room. He will see you himself. Probably within the day."

"And if he refuses?"

Pellow gathered his pen, his ink, his spectacles, moving with the economy of a man who wasted nothing, including hope.

"If he refuses," he said, "then you will have learned the truest lesson this building teaches, which is that the door is always technically open, Miss Crane, and it is always nearly shut, and the whole of the law lives in that gap." He paused at the door, lamplight catching the rims of his spectacles. "Eight o'clock. Sleep somewhere defensible. And miss—" he glanced back, and for one moment the dry clerk fell away and something older and wearier looked out of his face, "—the others from Vellamouth. Two gave up and went home. The third never reached the queue at all. Ask yourself, before morning, which one you intend to be."

He left her alone among the pigeonholes, with the candle and the ribbons and the faint smell of the fire that had almost happened.

* * *

She found a bed at last in a chandler's loft above the tallow sheds, offered by a woman with a scarred forearm who asked no questions and took coin without counting it. Silas lay in the dark under a rough blanket, listening to the town settle — dogs, a drunk singing, the creak of the shire hall flagpole in the wind — and did the thing Vane had taught her to do before every difficult survey: run the ground in her mind until the ground was hers.

Tomorrow, eight o'clock, the words: *filing of documentary evidence in a matter touching public funds.* Then the oath. Then the key-phrase: *risk of local interference.* Then Wrenn.

She rehearsed it until the sentences wore smooth as river stones.

Against her ribs, the ledger pages pressed warm from her own body heat, and she thought of Edith in the workhouse infirmary saying *come back with the law*, and of Tam watching the ice go rotten on the river, and of Nell at the toll gate, and of Brack saying *I know what hangs on your wall* — all of them now downstream of whatever she did at eight o'clock tomorrow morning.

Sleep came finally, thin and shallow, and broke just before dawn with the sound of hooves in the street below — many hooves, hard-ridden, stopping beneath her window. She crept to the sill and looked down into the grey light.

Six riders in good coats, travel-muddied, blowing their horses. And at their head, dismounting with the unhurried grace of a man arriving precisely when he intended to, Magistrate Corven Hale looked up — not at her window, not at anything in particular — at the shire hall itself, pale stone going gold with sunrise.

Then, briefly, deliberately, his eyes travelled up the chandler's loft and rested, for half a second, on the window where Silas stood.

He tipped his hat.

And walked inside.
