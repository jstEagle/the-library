## 37. Return Across the Water

The river had come back to itself.

Silas felt it before she saw it — a change in the ground under her boots, in the air off the water, in the way the road to Vellamouth had turned soft and dark with meltwater, sucking at each step as though the earth were reluctant to give her up. For two days she had walked the long way round, through the assize town's outlying farms where the ditches ran loud and brown and the willows stood ankle-deep in their own reflections. Now the valley opened ahead of her, and there was Vellamouth, sitting on its bend of the river like a thing that had been holding its breath all winter and had only just remembered how to exhale.

The ice was going out in pieces. She could hear it from half a mile away: a deep, irregular groaning, then a crack that rolled across the water like distant cannon fire, then the grinding hiss of floes shouldering against one another in the current. Great white plates tilted and sank and rose again, gleaming in a thin spring sun, and between them the water showed black and quick and alive. Gulls had come up from the coast to quarrel over the shallows. Somewhere a church bell rang the hour, and the sound carried farther than it did in summer, when leaves swallowed everything.

She stopped at the crown of the last hill and looked down at it all — the town, the river, the bridge with its ice-battered piers, the smoke rising straight from forty chimneys into still air — and found she did not know what she felt. Relief was in it somewhere, and grief, and a tiredness so complete it had gone past the body and settled into something older, as if she had been walking since the night Master Vane drowned and only now noticed the distance she'd covered.

*You're alive,* she told herself, testing the words. *You carried it. It's done.*

It didn't feel done. It felt like a ledger with one column filled in and the other waiting.

She came down into the town by the ferry road rather than the bridge road, because the bridge was still closed — she could see the barriers stacked at either end, the timbers being inspected by men who moved slowly, with the unhurried confidence of people paid by the day. The ferry was running again, or trying to; Tam Ossler's boat sat low in the broken channel, and even from the bank she could see him arguing with the current, poling floes aside with an oar wrapped in rag to keep it from splintering. He looked thinner. His beard had gone fully grey over the winter, or perhaps he'd simply stopped bothering with it.

He saw her before she reached the landing stage. He went still for a moment — the whole boat going still with him, riding the current sideways — and then he laughed, a short bark of a laugh that carried across the water, and raised the oar in salute.

"By God," he called. "I said you would. Didn't I say it?"

"You said the ice would break early," Silas called back. "That's not the same thing."

"It is the same thing." He brought the boat in against the pilings with three expert knocks of wood on wood. "Come down here where I can see you. Let me look at what walked out of winter."

She let him look. She knew what he saw: a woman with a month of road on her, coat worn shiny at the elbows, hands still ink-stained in the creases no amount of washing reached, eyes that had learned to measure rooms for exits. Tam studied her the way he'd once studied the river before a crossing, reading what lay beneath the surface.

"They'll be saying things about you in the taprooms," he said quietly. "Half of it bad. Hale's people got their story out first — thief, forger, madwoman. But the assize record came upriver on the mail packet Tuesday, and the public crier read it on the guild hall steps." He paused. "Your name's in it. Both names. Yours and his."

"I know. I asked for it that way."

"I know you did." He looked out at the grinding ice. "Forty-one," he said. "They read out the names of the dead. All forty-one. First time anyone's said them aloud since the flood." His voice caught on something and he cleared it roughly. "My brother-in-law's sister was one. Never knew her name till Tuesday. Never knew she had a name, if you follow me. She was just 'one of the Marrowfleet drowned.'"

"She has a name now in the record forever," Silas said. "And a village. They entered the boundaries too. Marrowfleet exists on paper now. Nobody can draw it away again."

Tam nodded slowly, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the river spoke instead — that endless shifting conversation of ice and water, of a season dying loudly.

"The workhouse," Silas said. "Edith Marr. Have you heard—"

"Freed Thursday last." He grinned suddenly, and the grin made him twenty years younger. "Judge's order came down with the assize record. Two magistrates' men came to fetch her out themselves — had to, mind, because the infirmary master swore up and down she was too frail to travel. Frail! That woman told the infirmary master exactly where he could put his frailty, in front of witnesses, and walked out under her own steam carrying her sewing basket like a bishop carries his crook." He shook his head admiringly. "She's lodging with the Widow Petchey on Tanner's Row. Says she's not setting foot in another institution so long as she draws breath. She asked after you. Twice."

"Twice?"

"Once politely. Once like a woman who meant to have an answer." Tam held out his hand to help her toward the gangplank, then thought better of it — the plank was iced at its edge — and simply steadied her elbow as she stepped aboard. Up close she could see the new lines around his eyes, and something else: a kind of settledness, as though a question he'd carried for years had finally been answered. "There's one more thing," he said, lowering his voice as the current took the boat out from the landing. "Brack resigned."

Silas turned. "Resigned?"

"Gave up his post Monday morning. Walked into the garrison, laid his sword and his warrant card on the duty officer's desk, and walked out. No explanation given. None asked, mind you — the garrison's in disarray since the arrests, half of them wondering whose pockets they were keeping warm and whether their own names are next." Tam poled them clear of a drifting slab the size of a hayrick. "But there's talk. Talk that when the judge's clerks came through asking questions, Brack answered every one of them true. Talk that he named names the arrested men would rather he hadn't." He glanced at her sidelong. "Talk that he let someone through a checkpoint he oughtn't to have."

"He searched my workshop," Silas said. "He stood in front of the wall map for the length of ten breaths. He knew. He must have known, and he let me go anyway, and then he let me pass on the garrison road, and he told me he knew what hung on my wall — and I've spent a month not knowing whether that was mercy or a leash he meant to pull later."

"And now?"

"Now I owe him a debt I don't know how to pay, to a man I never liked, for reasons I'm only beginning to understand."

Tam grunted. "That's most debts, girl. The good ones especially." He swung the boat toward the town stairs. "Speaking of debts — your toll's paid. Has been since Tuesday."

"What do you mean?"

"Nell. Nell Ashdown. She's been standing at her father's gate every market day telling travelers the toll's suspended for one passenger due any day now, coming up from the assize town, and if they argue they can take it up with her personally." He laughed again, softer this time. "Her father gave up arguing by Wednesday. Girl's got her mother's way of winning arguments without raising her voice."

The boat kissed the stairs. Silas climbed out onto wet stone, and the town took her in.

* * *

Vellamouth smelled different after the thaw — mud and cut wood and the green ghost of things beginning under the brown, and underneath it all the river's cold mineral breath. People looked at her in the street. She had expected that, and braced for it, but what she had not expected was the manner of the looking. Not the flat, careful blankness of folk afraid to be seen knowing something. It was more like the way people look at weather that has finally changed — with relief, with curiosity, with the small superstition of those who believe a hard winter ends when somebody walks back into town carrying spring in their pockets.

A fishwife touched her sleeve outside the salt shed. "God keep you, mistress Crane," the woman said, and was embarrassed by her own boldness, and hurried on. A boy selling hot chestnuts refused her coin. At the corner of Cordwainer Street, an old man lifted his hat to her, and she realized with a jolt that she did not know his face, and understood that it did not matter. Word travels strangely in river towns. It travels faster than boats and slower than fear, and it arrives changed, and somehow truer.

The workshop door, when she reached it, was just as she had left it. Locked. The key was still under the stone where Aldous Vane had always left it — she checked first, kneeling in the wet alley with her heart going hard, before she allowed herself to fit it to the lock. Inside, the air was cold and dry and smelled of size and linseed and lamp oil and him, faintly, stubbornly, the way a coat keeps the shape of shoulders long after the shoulders are gone.

Everything waited where she had staged it: the finished-atlas dummy squared on the bench, its dummy pages crisp and innocent; her tools laid out in their proper ranks; the dust of two months lying over it all like a second, paler set of plans. And above the bench, filling the wall from beam to beam, the great wall map of the Vellamouth reach — Vane's masterpiece, the map he'd worked on for eleven years and never quite called finished.

The original ledger lay behind it still. She could not see it, of course. She could see nothing but the map itself: the river drawn in Vane's hand with such loving exactitude that the water seemed to move when the light shifted, the marshes stippled fine as skin, the towns named in his beautiful upright capitals. Her eye went where it always went — to the stretch of coastline below the confluence where a village should have been and wasn't. Where a scraped patch of blank vellum sat like a hole burned in a photograph.

Soon, she promised it. Not yet. There was one visit to make first, and the light was already slanting long through the west window.

* * *

Tanner's Row smelled of oak bark and tallow, and the Widow Petchey's house was the narrowest on it, wedged between a cooperage and a derelict dye-works, its single upper window hung with geraniums too optimistic for the season. Silas knocked. Footsteps, slow but firm, and the door opened on Edith Marr.

She was smaller than Silas remembered — the poorhouse had taken flesh off her that the workhouse infirmary had not returned — but she stood upright, and her eyes, which Silas had first seen swimming with old drowning, were clear and level and dry.

"So," Edith said. "You came back."

"I said I would."

"You said you'd come back with the law." Edith stepped aside to let her in. "Did you bring it, then? All of it? Every ounce?"

"All of it. And some of it cost more than I had."

"In my experience," Edith said, closing the door, "that's the only kind worth having."

The room inside was small and immaculate. A fire burned in a grate barely bigger than a Bible. On the table, spread open, lay the sewing basket, and beside it — Silas's breath caught — a length of cloth dyed in the Marrowfleet pattern, the interlocking wave-and-comb design that had once marked every door-lintel and cradle-board in the drowned village. Edith followed her gaze.

"Widow Petchey had a loom. I haven't sat idle." She touched the cloth almost shyly. "First time in nine years I've woven it. My mother taught me the pattern when I was six. I thought it had died with everything else. Turns out a thing doesn't die just because everyone agrees not to look at it." She looked up sharply. "Sit down, girl. You've the look of a woman who hasn't eaten since the thaw began, and I won't have testimony fainting in my parlor."

There was bread and dripping and tea brewed strong enough to stand a spoon in, and Silas ate like the traveler she was while Edith watched with a hostess's stern satisfaction. Then, when the plate was empty, Silas told her. All of it. The toll gate and Nell's hand waving her cart through; the forged pass wept over by candlelight; Brack standing in the empty garrison road saying *I know what hangs on your wall*; the black channel opening in the ice; the moment halfway across when the satchel of drawings slipped and her hands chose — instantly, treacherously, irrevocably — the ledger over Vane's portrait, over her own apprenticeship sketches, over every soft thing she owned. How she had hated herself on the far bank, and how she hated herself still, a little, every time she thought of what floated away downstream in a canvas bag.

Edith listened without interrupting. When Silas finished, the old woman was quiet for a long time, looking into the fire.

"My husband chose the same way once," she said at last. "Flood night. The water was coming and there was time to carry one thing out of the house. He took the account book of his shop. Forty years of accounts. Left the portraits, left my mother's clock, left—" her voice did not break; it simply narrowed, like a channel narrowing—"left our daughter's cradle. Carried the book to high ground. Sat with it on his knees all night." She turned to Silas. "He was never sorry. He said, *a man's accounts are his word made paper.* I used to think that was hardness. Now I think it was the only prayer he knew how to say." She reached across the table and put her rough hand over Silas's ink-stained one. "You saved the word, girl. The pictures are gone, but the word is safe. That's not nothing. That's everything, near enough."

"I lost his face," Silas whispered. "I can't always hold it anymore. Some mornings I wake and I have to build him piece by piece — the way you build a coastline from soundings. And I'm frightened of the mornings when I stop needing to build it."

Edith squeezed her hand once, hard, and let go.

"You'll want to go home now," she said, in the tone of a woman dismissing a guest kindly but finally. "You've a map to finish. Don't sit here mourning in my parlor when there's work on your bench. Mourning keeps. Work doesn't." At the door she pressed something into Silas's hand: a scrap of the new-woven cloth, small as a psalter, the wave-and-comb pattern bright as the day the dye was mixed. "For the atlas," Edith said. "However you mean to use it. Marrowfleet should be in it more ways than one."

* * *

The toll gate stood where it had always stood, at the neck of the bridge road, its barrier freshly painted, its little hut with its stove-pipe chimney looking absurdly domestic against the wreck of ice still piled along the banks. Nell Ashdown was not in the hut. She was leaning against the barrier post with her arms crossed and her chin up, watching the road from the assize town with the fixed patience of a heron watching water — and when she saw Silas coming up the rise she pushed off the post and walked to meet her, and did not run, though Silas could see the running in her, held in check by seventeen-year-old dignity.

"You're late," Nell said.

"I'm early. The thaw isn't even finished."

"The thaw finished Tuesday. You're four days late by the thaw and a month late by my count." But her voice wobbled on the last word, and she stopped, and looked at Silas properly, head to boots, the inventory of a friend. "You look terrible," she announced. "You look like a woman who slept in ditches and argued with rivers."

"One ditch. One river. The river argued harder."

Nell nodded as if this confirmed everything. Then she stepped forward and hugged her — quickly, fiercely, before either of them could grow embarrassed by it — and stepped back, and was brisk again. "Toll's paid," she said. "Paid in full, forever, by order of the management." She jerked her thumb at the hut, behind which, visible through the little window, her father sat ostentatiously reading his list book and pretending very hard not to watch them. "He says — and these are his words, I'm quoting — *anyone who walks to the assizes in winter and comes back alive gets free passage in this life and the next.* So. No toll. Ever. Argue with me and I'll make a scene."

"There's no toll to argue about."

"Good. Then we understand each other." Nell fell into step beside her as they passed the barrier, and lowered her voice. "The garrison's a shambles, by the way. Half the men transferred, half the rest drinking. Brack's cottage by the mill is empty — he went east, they say, to his sister's people. But he left something at the toll house for you. Wouldn't say what it was. Said you'd know." She fished in her apron pocket and produced a small parcel wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord. "Been keeping it under the counter. Father kept asking if it was dangerous. I told him it was guild property and he stopped asking."

Inside the oilcloth was a sketchbook. Silas knew it at once — cheap bound board, water-marked corners, the kind sold three for a penny at the winter fair. But this was her sketchbook, the one taken with the satchel on the ice, or part of its contents: and opening it, she found the pages pressed flat and dried with care, each one blotted and interleaved, salvaged from whatever the river gave back. The river had kept the satchel two days, Tam had told her once, before surrendering it to a net below the weir. Someone had gone to the weir. Someone had dried the pages page by page, and sorted the ruined from the saved, and carried the saved fifty miles east and posted them back by way of a toll keeper's daughter.

On the flyleaf, in a blunt soldier's hand, block capitals:

*NOT ALL OF IT CAME UP. BUT MOST DID. THE RIVER OWES YOU NOTHING NOW.*

Beneath it, smaller, as if added later and with difficulty:

*I SEARCHED YOUR SHOP BECAUSE IT WAS MY ORDERS. I LET YOU PASS BECAUSE IT WAS MY SISTER'S TOWN. WE ARE EVEN. DO NOT THANK ME. GO DRAW YOUR MAPS.*

Silas stood in the middle of the bridge road with the sketchbook open in her hands, and the thaw-water roared under the bridge, and Nell watched her with the tactful silence of someone who has learned when questions are welcome.

Vane's portrait was there. Water-stained along one edge, one eye washed to a pale suggestion — but there, the strong jaw, the ink-dark hands, the mouth that always seemed about to finish a sentence it considered obvious. And beneath it, her own earliest sketches: the workshop window, the compass roses she'd practiced, the first coastline she'd ever inked, crooked and proud. All of it. Almost all of it.

"He kept his sister's town," Silas said softly. "All those years, following orders he despised. He kept it."

"That's what my mother did too," Nell said. "Kept things. You find out afterward what people kept, and it explains them better than anything they said." She bumped her shoulder against Silas's, companionable. "Come on. Walk me home the long way. And tell me everything the crier left out — and don't you dare tell me it's all in the assize record, because I've heard the assize record twice and it reads like a laundry list. I want the parts with blood in them."

They walked up through the town as the light went gold, past the guild hall where the magistrate's cellar stood empty now and its survey records had been carried back upstairs into daylight, past the poorhouse whose infirmary master had, Tam reported, developed a sudden and convenient illness of his own. The ice was nearly gone from the channel. By tomorrow the bridge would open. Boats would run, carts would cross, the world would resume its ordinary traffic, and beneath all of it, in the public record, in forty-one read-aloud names, in a village restored to paper, something irreversible would continue to hold.

At the corner where their ways parted, Nell hesitated.

"You'll finish it, then?" she asked. "The atlas. His atlas. With Marrowfleet put back in."

"With both names on it," Silas said. "Hers and his. His and hers."

"Good." Nell nodded once, satisfied, and turned away, then turned back. "One thing. When you were gone — I went past your workshop sometimes. Just to look at the windows. Make sure nobody was in there." She smiled, a little sheepishly, a little fiercely. "The wall map's hanging crooked. Since before the thaw, maybe. You might want to look at it."

Then she was gone down the lane, and Silas stood alone in the gold light, and the crooked map hung in her mind like a bell about to ring.

Crooked. Since before the thaw. Since before she left.

She walked home fast, then faster, the sketchbook clutched to her chest, taking the alley at nearly a run, fitting the key with shaking hands. Inside, the workshop was cold and blue with evening. She lit the lamp with fingers that would not quite obey her, dragged the bench beneath the great wall map, and climbed up, and reached behind the frame — and found the ledger where she had left it, and found, tucked flat against the backing board behind the ledger, a folded sheet of paper that had not been there when she hid it.

The fold was old. The paper was foxed with damp. And the hand, when she opened it by lamplight, was the hand she knew best in all the world — Aldous Vane's, written fast and hard, dated the night of his death.

*If you are reading this, Silas, then you have chosen the harder thing, and I am prouder of you than I ever managed to say...*

Outside, the last of the ice went by in the dark, grinding southward, making way.
