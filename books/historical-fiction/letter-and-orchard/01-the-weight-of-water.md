## 1. The Weight of Water

The sea at Iloran is kindest an hour before the sun, when it lies flat and gray-green as a sheet of hammered pewter, and a man can pretend—for the length of one held breath—that it has never drowned anyone.

Vesper Okonkwo counted his way down into it. He always counted. Four strokes to clear the harbor pilings, eight to pass the drop-off where the light went from honey to iron, twelve more before his hands found the first shell of the mother-of-pearl beds. By then his lungs had begun their slow complaint, a tightening he had learned to hear the way other men heard church bells: not as warning, but as timekeeping.

Forty-one. Forty-two. The knife slipped under the shell's foot and pried. Forty-three.

He was twenty-six years old and had been counting since he was sixteen, which was the age at which the Meridian Trading Company had purchased the remainder of his father's debt along with the remainder of Vesper himself. Ten years of indenture. Ten years of breaths measured out in fathoms. He knew to the second how long his body would forgive him for leaving the air behind, and he knew—though he kept this knowledge folded away, like a letter too painful to reread—to the day how long his own contract would run.

Two years, four months, eleven days. Not that anyone was counting.

The shells came up in his net by feel more than sight. Down here the world was all shadow and suggestion, the pearl beds a dark orchard on the harbor floor, and Vesper worked them the way Mama Sefu worked her fig trees behind the compound wall: patiently, without greed, taking what ripened and leaving the rest. A diver who took everything took nothing next season. A diver who rushed surfaced with blood in his ears or didn't surface at all.

His father had rushed. Or rather—the thought arrived, as it did every dawn, punctual and unwelcome—his father had been *made* to rush, quota raised the week the rains failed, boatman ordered to keep him down past reason because the Meridian ledger demanded numbers and numbers do not care about lungs.

They said lost at sea. They said it kindly enough, the neighbors, heads bowed over the fish stew at the wake. Lost at sea, poor man, the water gives and the water takes. And ten-year-old Vesper had stood in his mother's doorway and known, with the terrible clarity children are granted precisely once, that the sea had not taken anything. The sea had simply been there, doing what the sea does, when men who owned paper decided his father could be spent.

He'd told no one. That was the first silence, and it fit him so well he wore it for a decade after.

* * *

Surfacing, he breathed the way he'd been taught—not the gasp of a drowning man but a long, controlled exhale, air paid back to the sky in installments. Old Tomas, holding the skiff steady against the current, spat over the side in the traditional manner of Iloran boatmen, which was to say, with commentary.

"You were down long," Tomas said.

"I was down the usual length."

"The usual length got longer last month."

"The quota got heavier last month," Vesper said, hauling the net over the gunwale. "The length followed it. Water's honest that way."

Tomas grunted, which among Iloran boatmen constituted a philosophical agreement, and began to row them back toward the company pier. The sun had cleared the headland now, gilding the masts, the warehouse roofs, the counting house with its shuttered windows that always made Vesper think of a face politely closing its eyes. Gulls wheeled and screamed their opinions. Somewhere on the shore road a cart driver cursed his mule with real artistry.

It should have been a beautiful morning. Most mornings were, if you didn't look too hard at any single part of them, and Vesper had become a master of the partial look. Look at the light, not the pier guards. Look at the gulls, not the tally board. Look at the water, never at the water.

At the sorting shed, the day's take was poured onto wet tables and the sorters moved down the rows, separating shell by size and luster while a clerk recorded weights in a book that would, by evening, be copied into a larger book, which would by week's end be carried to the counting house and entered into the master ledger itself. Vesper sometimes imagined the whole town's life flowing downhill through those books, stream into river into reservoir, until every fig stolen and every breath held ended up as a column of figures in a locked room.

He was coiling rope when he heard the new clerk arrive.

* * *

The new clerk's name was Mr. Fenwick, though nobody used it, and he had come out from the company's northern offices three weeks earlier with soft hands, a soft hat, and the expression of a man perpetually surprised to find himself somewhere with weather. He was young. He was earnest. He was learning the trade the way an apprentice learns anything: badly, loudly, and with a great deal of ink on his fingers.

"Okonkwo," he called across the shed now, waving a slate as if hailing a distant ship. "Your boat. The Kestrel? You're listed as crew lead?"

"I'm listed as the only crew," Vesper said. "Tomas rows. Tomas isn't listed. Tomas is a fact the ledger hasn't discovered."

Fenwick laughed—a bright, unguarded sound that several older divers turned to stare at, the way one turns toward a crack in a wall—and then caught himself and arranged his face into something more administrative. "Right. Yes. Well. There's been a—" he consulted the slate "—an incident. Boat Seven. Diver named Adisa. He went down at the outer beds yesterday afternoon and—"

Vesper's hands went still around the rope coil. He knew Adisa. Everyone knew Adisa—broad-backed, quick-grinning, three seasons from paying off his own contract, with a daughter who waited on the seawall each evening and counted boats the way her father counted breaths.

"He didn't come up," Fenwick said. "The boatman says the current was bad off the point."

"And?" Vesper said. It came out flatter than he intended. Flatness was the skill; flatness was the whole trade. But underneath the flatness something old and cold turned over, the way silt turns over when a keel drags the bottom.

"And I have to record the outcome." Fenwick looked down at his ledger page, and Vesper saw—because he had spent ten years learning to read the company's paper the way priests read scripture—that the form offered certain prescribed words. *Lost.* That was the word for it. *Lost at sea*, the phrase that at least admitted the water had won, that at least left a family something true to carve on a marker.

Fenwick's pen hovered. Behind him, at the shed door, stood Mr. Calloway, the senior factor, arms folded, watching with the patient attention of a man supervising a lesson. Calloway was not soft anywhere. Calloway believed, sincerely and profitably, that the Meridian Trading Company had done great good in Iloran, lifting the natives from idleness through disciplined labor, and that the records ought to reflect the dignity of that enterprise.

"Diver lost," Fenwick began writing, "at—"

"Fenwick." Calloway's voice was mild. Everything about Calloway was mild; that was what made him frightening. "Think about the phrasing. *Lost* implies negligence on the part of company operations. Implies we sent a man into unsafe conditions. Auditors read these entries. Northern auditors, with no understanding of local conditions. What actually happened, from an operational standpoint?"

Fenwick blinked. "He... drowned?"

"What happened," Calloway repeated, gently, the way one repeats a question to a child who has given a wrong answer, "is that a contracted laborer failed to return to his station. We don't know why. Men abscond. Especially men near the end of their terms, who grow—restless. The entry should reflect the uncertainty."

Vesper watched the pen move. He felt the moment happen the way you feel a change in current underwater—a pressure shift, invisible and total.

*Absconded.*

Fenwick wrote it in a careful clerk's hand, and the word sat on the page looking official, looking *true*, which was the most dangerous thing a lie could do. Absconded meant fled. Absconded meant a man who had abandoned a debt, a daughter, a boat, a grave-less death chosen instead of responsibility. Absconded meant the company owed nothing—not compensation, not inquiry, not so much as a search. Somewhere in the north, an auditor reading the Meridian accounts would see a tidy column: divers lost, divers absconded, the latter marked for debt recovery against estate.

Against estate. Vesper's stomach went cold and quiet. He remembered that phrase from another page, another year. He remembered a house on the shore road with a blue door that had stopped being theirs the same week his father stopped breathing.

"That's not what happened," he said.

The shed went very quiet. Sorters froze mid-reach. Even the gulls outside seemed to pause in their arguments.

Calloway turned to him slowly, pleasantly, the way weather turns. "Excuse me?"

"The current off the point runs hard at slack tide," Vesper said, and made his voice a thing with no corners. "Any diver knows it. No one absconds into that water. Sir." The *sir* arrived late and cost him something, but it arrived.

A silence stretched, during which Vesper performed the arithmetic he had performed ten thousand times before: the value of a truth weighed against the value of staying whole enough to work, to eat, to walk home unbroken through the gate at dusk. Truth was worth much and bought little here. Silence was cheap and bought everything.

"Mm," said Calloway. "Diver Okonkwo. Your opinion on currents wasn't requested." He smiled—it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "But since you're interested in accuracy, I'll note that your own contract has two years remaining, and that accurate men finish contracts faster than talkative ones. Do you follow?"

"Yes, sir."

"Good man." Calloway clapped Fenwick on the shoulder. "You're learning, boy. The ledger tells us what happened. That's its purpose. Otherwise it's just gossip with columns."

He walked out. The shed exhaled. The sorters resumed, and the gulls resumed, and the world resumed pretending.

Fenwick stood over his page a moment, cheeks flushed, and then—Vesper would remember this later, would turn it over in the dark like a shell with something living inside—he hesitated, pen lifted, and in the margin beside *absconded*, in letters so small they were almost apology, he wrote a date. Just the date of Adisa's death, entered nowhere else on the form, required by no field. Then he blotted it quickly and closed the book.

Their eyes met across the wet tables. Fenwick looked away first.

* * *

That evening Vesper walked home along the seawall, where Adisa's daughter still stood counting boats, waiting for one that had already been reclassified as having never needed to come back.

"Papa's boat is late," she said, not to him exactly, but to the space beside him, where adults became confessors.

"The currents were hard today," Vesper said. "Sometimes boats go round to the far anchorage."

She nodded, satisfied, and ran off toward the lamps of home, and Vesper stood a long moment watching her go, feeling the two silences inside him settle together like sediment—the old one, ten years deep, and this fresh layer settling over it, fine and gray and permanent-seeming.

Behind the compound wall, Mama Sefu's fig trees rustled in the evening wind, and somewhere beyond the harbor the sea lay down flat again, keeping, as always, everything it was ever given.

And in a locked room in the counting house, in a book with a brass lock and a leather spine, a clerk's neat hand wrote *absconded* one more time, and the ledger grew one line heavier, and Vesper Okonkwo walked home beneath a sky going gold and thought, for the first time in years, not about how to survive the paper—but about what it would mean, someday, to answer it.
