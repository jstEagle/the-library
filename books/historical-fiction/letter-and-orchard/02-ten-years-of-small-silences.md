## 2. Ten Years of Small Silences

The first fig Mama Sefu ever gave me was stolen.

I know this because she told me so herself, many years later, in the tone of a woman confessing to a crime she intended to commit again. *I stole it from my own tree,* she said. *Because a thing given freely teaches you nothing, and a thing stolen teaches you who to trust.* She was not a woman who made sense on the first pass. You had to let her words sit like fruit in the sun until they softened into something you could eat.

But I am ahead of myself. In Iloran we say the sea keeps time better than any clock, and stories are no different—they rise when they rise.

* * *

Ten years old. Rain season just ending, the compound walls of the Meridian Trading Company streaked green with damp, and my mother three days dead of the fever that came up the coast on a cargo ship nobody would name. I remember standing in the company office with wet sandals and dry eyes while a man with a gray vest read numbers aloud to another man with a grayer vest. My father's debt—my father being nine years drowned by then, though the company preferred the word *lost*, as though he had misplaced himself somewhere among the sponges and could be found again if only someone looked hard enough—that debt had passed to her, and from her to me, because debt in Iloran was the one inheritance that never spoiled.

"Indenture," said the gray-vested man, tapping the paper. "Standard term. Ten years. He'll be fed."

"He's ten," I said.

They both looked at me with mild surprise, as if a chair had spoken. The younger one smiled without warmth, the smile of a man who has heard every objection the world can offer and found them all profitable. "He'll be fed," he repeated, and stamped the paper, and that was that. The stamp was red. I remember thinking it looked like something bleeding politely.

They took me through the gate behind the counting house, past the drying racks where sponge and shell hung like strange laundry, past the long shed where the divers' families lived in rows—the compound, we called it, the way you'd call a cage a garden—and installed me in a bunk between a boy named Tomas whose elbows were all knuckles and an old man named Efua-Ben who had dived so long his ears had given up entirely and now communicated exclusively through squinting.

That first night I did not cry. This was not bravery. It was arithmetic. Crying cost breath, and breath, I already understood, was the only currency a diver's son owned outright.

What I did instead was count. The slats in the bunk: eleven. The snores in the shed: nineteen, one of which belonged to a goat that had wandered in and refused all negotiation. The years remaining on my contract: ten, minus the hours already served, which I decided counted, because nobody stopped me.

I have been counting ever since. It is a habit I recommend to no one, and yet I cannot imagine who I would be without it.

* * *

Mama Sefu's orchard lay on the other side of the east wall—a wall of coral stone older than the company, older probably than the town, furred with moss and defended by a bougainvillea that had opinions about trespassers. Nobody in the compound knew exactly how old she was. Efua-Ben claimed she had buried two husbands and one husband's ghost, which had been the more difficult funeral. The children said she was a witch. The adults said she was a widow with land, which in Iloran amounted to nearly the same accusation.

Her figs leaned over the wall into our compound like a bribe nobody had officially offered. Green-gold, fat, hanging at exactly the height of a hungry ten-year-old's reach.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is supposedly the theme of my life now: I did not climb that wall for courage or for poetry. I climbed it because I had eaten half a bowl of porridge in two days and my stomach had begun making demands in a language older than contracts.

Tomas spotted for me. This meant he stood below whispering *hurry up hurry UP* in a manner that helped nothing whatsoever. I got one hand on the low branch, then a knee, then—I will admit it—a moment of pure weightless joy, because the top of a wall is the closest a child comes to flying, and even a compound has weather above it.

"You'll break your neck," said a voice below me, "and then I'll have to bury you, and I'm out of decent shovels."

Mama Sefu stood in her garden with her hands on her hips, small and brown and folded like a walnut, wearing a headwrap the color of a storm deciding what to do. She did not look angry. She looked interested, which was considerably more frightening.

"I'm sorry," I said, hanging there like a bat caught mid-thought.

"Sorry." She tasted the word. "Are you sorry, or are you hungry? Because sorry climbs down empty-handed either way, but hungry climbs down with a plan."

I had no plan. My face said so.

"Come down properly," she said. "Not like a lizard. Like a person. If you're going to steal from me, do it with dignity."

I climbed down properly. She picked two figs off the branch above my head—one for me, one, after a pause, for Tomas, who had attempted a strategic retreat and failed—and she watched us eat them with the expression of a general reviewing troops she had not asked for but intended to keep.

"That tree was planted by my first husband," she said. "A fool. A loving fool, which is worse. He planted it the week we married, saying we'd eat from it every year of our lives." She snorted. "He died before the first crop. So you understand the arrangement. The figs belong to the dead, and the dead are generous, and anyone who says otherwise can take it up with them."

Then she turned her back on us, which was dismissal, and which was also, I understood even then, permission to come back.

We came back the next day. And the next.

* * *

Here is what I know now that I did not know then: Mama Sefu had been watching the compound for years. She knew which families were new, which children had gone quiet, which bunks held boys who flinched at the counting-house bell. Her grudge against the Meridian Company was older than mine and had sharper teeth—her second husband had dived for them too, and come up once too often on a short rope, and the ledger had recorded him as *lost*, and the compensation had recorded itself as *negligible*.

But she never said any of this to me at ten. At ten, she gave me jobs.

"You'll weed," she announced on my third visit. "Stealing is a profession; weeding is a punishment. You've earned both."

So I weeded. Tomas hauled water and complained about it in three languages, one of which he invented himself. A girl called Adaeze, whose mother shelled shrimp at the drying racks, turned up ostensibly to return a borrowed sieve and stayed for eleven years. By the end of that first rain season there were six of us in Mama Sefu's orchard on any given afternoon, and the company guards at the gate had concluded we were doing agricultural labor, and Mama Sefu had concluded they were fools, and both parties were satisfied.

It was not freedom. Let no one sentimentalize it. When the bell rang for the dive boats I still went down to the water with the other indentured kids and counted my breaths and learned the water's moods the way you learn a landlord's moods—carefully, usefully, without love. But there was a difference now, and the difference was this: I had somewhere to put the things I couldn't say. Under the fig trees, in the smell of crushed grass and overripe fruit, a boy is allowed to be silent out loud, if that makes sense. Silence with witnesses. Silence that someone else was keeping warm for you.

Mama Sefu told stories while she worked, and her stories were ninety percent insult and ten percent scripture, and we loved every syllable. About her husbands. About the year the sea froze—she claimed this; we disputed it; she won by sheer volume. About names.

"The company takes your name first," she said once, snapping the heads off weeds with terrifying precision. "Did you notice? They don't call you Okonkwo. They call you 'the boy.' Debt doesn't have a name; debt has a number. That's how they get away with it. It's harder to rob a person than a number."

"So what do we do?" asked Adaeze, who was seven and had not yet learned that some questions are rhetorical.

"What do we do?" Mama Sefu laughed, a sound like a shutter slamming in wind. "We remember. Remembering is the only rebellion they haven't figured out how to tax. Say your name, boy."

"Vesper Okonkwo."

"Louder. The figs need to hear it."

"Vesper Okonkwo."

"Good. Now say your father's."

I hesitated. I had not said my father's name aloud since the office with the gray vests, where the man reading numbers had pronounced it wrong anyway—softened it, flattened it, filed it.

"Jonah Okonkwo," I said finally, and the orchard went very still, and even the bougainvillea seemed to lean in, and Mama Sefu nodded once, satisfied, as though a debt had been paid in the correct coin at last.

* * *

The first lie I ever told was also for Mama Sefu, which felt appropriate, given that she had taught me most of what I knew about truth.

I was eleven. The rains had come early and mean, and the company had cut the compound rations—not from cruelty, the clerks insisted, but from *necessity*, a word they used the way other people used salt, on everything. The children in the shed had gone thin and loud. And Mama Sefu's own stores were running low, because she had been quietly feeding half the compound for months out of a pantry she pretended was full.

I found this out by accident, hauling water past her kitchen window, and seeing her measure out the last of the rice with hands that shook slightly, and set aside the smaller portion for herself with the ease of long practice.

She caught me looking. Of course she did. The woman had eyes in the back of her headwrap.

"Well?" she said. "Say whatever you're going to say and then help me with these beans."

I should tell you what I wanted to say, which was something enormous and grateful and impossible for an eleven-year-old to build with words. What I actually did was walk into the kitchen, pick up the bean basket, and lie.

"Tomas stole a sack of rice from the warehouse," I said. "He's going to leave some on your step tonight. He made me promise not to tell."

She looked at me for a long time. Long enough for me to hear the rain start on the roof tiles, long enough to count four heartbeats and regret the fifth.

"A thief and a liar," she said at last. "You're turning into quite the young professional." And then she smiled—small, crooked, real—and went back to her beans, and that night a sack of rice appeared on her step that had absolutely nothing to do with Tomas, and neither of us ever mentioned it again for thirty years.

I have thought about that lie a great deal since. People assume lying is what silence does when it gets ambitious. But that lie wasn't ambitious. It was load-bearing. It let an old woman keep her pride and keep her giving, and it let me give something back without naming the debt—because naming a debt, I had learned from the gray vests, is often just a way of collecting one.

Some truths you carry openly, like firewood. Others you bury deep and plant things over, and let the roots do the remembering.

* * *

Which brings me back to the present—to now, to the occupied town, to the soldiers at the gates and the counting house sealed like a struck bell and Sergeant Diallo's patrols walking the harbor road where I used to run with wet feet and a mouth full of stolen fig.

Because here is the thing about ten years of small silences: they add up. Eleven slats, nineteen snores, one goat, one father, one name spoken to fig trees until the fig trees knew it better than the company did. Every silence I kept was a brick, and I built a wall out of them without noticing, and I lived inside it safe and slowly erased.

And now the victors propose to burn the one building in Iloran where all those silences were written down—every debt, every *lost*, every *absconded* that was really a drowning, including, I am increasingly certain, one entry bearing the name Jonah Okonkwo.

Mama Sefu sat under her trees yesterday, older now, hands still quick, watching the smoke of army cookfires drift over her wall. "They're going to burn it," I told her. "The ledger. Marchand's people. They're going to burn it and write a kinder story."

She snapped a weed off at the root without looking at it.

"Then somebody had better learn to write faster," she said.

I thought she was speaking generally. I was wrong. She reached into her apron and produced a stub of pencil and a sheet of packing paper, and laid them on the wall between us like a knife on a table.

"Start with your father," she said. "You remember everything else. Start with him."

And that is how I came to be walking, this morning, toward the counting house I have hated for twenty years—with a rope order in my hand, a pencil in my shirt, and the sudden vertigo of a man about to look directly at the thing he has spent his whole life counting around.
