## 3. The Ledger of Names

The rope order came down at midmorning the way all rope orders came down: through a chain of mouths that never touched the work themselves. The harbor foreman told the yard boss, the yard boss told the tally-man, the tally-man shouted across the drying racks at Vesper, who was elbow-deep in a net that smelled like ten years of fish and regret.

"Counting house wants forty fathoms of three-inch. You. Now. And don't dawdle — Osei says if you're back after the noon bell he'll dock your ration."

Vesper did not point out that the noon bell had been rung late twice this week by a bell-boy who liked to sleep in, and that therefore time itself in Iloran was negotiable while his dinner ration apparently was not. He simply wiped his hands, said nothing — silence was a coin he kept in excellent condition — and went to collect the rope.

It should have been a simple errand. Forty fathoms of three-inch hemp, signed for, carried home on his shoulder like a great pale snake. He had done it a hundred times. But the counting house was the one building in Iloran Vesper had never been inside, and something in him — some small, traitorous part that had survived ten years of being trained out of existence — wanted to look.

He told himself, walking up the shell road with the coiled rope bumping against his hip, that he would not look. Looking was how people got into trouble. Looking was what his mother had done, once, at a company man who had shortchanged her at the weigh-station, and everyone knew how that story ended. In Iloran you kept your eyes on your own hands and let the world go about its dishonest business without your supervision.

But the counting house doors stood open because of the heat, and the heat, Vesper reflected later, was an accomplice.

* * *

Inside, the air changed entirely. It went from salt and tar to paper, ink, and lamp oil — the smell of a different country, one where nobody got wet for a living. The front room held a counter, a clerk's stool, a wall of pigeonholes stuffed with receipts, and a ceiling fan that turned with the weary dignity of a thing that had given up long ago but saw no reason to alarm anyone.

Behind the counter sat a man Vesper recognized by reputation before face: Ilya Petrov, the second clerk. The divers called him the Pale Pencil behind his back, partly for his coloring and partly because everything he wrote seemed to come out of someone else's account. He was a thin northerner with a face like unsalted porridge and, at present, a bottle of arrack tucked beside his inkwell with the optimism of a man who believed he was hiding it well.

He was not hiding it well. His collar was open, his ledger-hand trembled, and when Vesper set the rope coil on the counter with the soft thump of good manners, Ilya Petrov looked up at him with eyes that had clearly been having an argument with themselves all morning.

"Forty fathom," Vesper said. "Three-inch. For the harbor lines."

"Yes. Yes." Ilya patted the counter as though the signature book might be hiding under it. "Sign. There." He pushed the book over, then squinted at Vesper's mark — a careful cross, since reading and writing were skills the company extended to property only in one direction — and something in his face went strange and soft.

"You can't write," he said. Not cruelly. Almost wonderingly, like a man noticing rain indoors.

"No, sir."

"Hm." Ilya took a pull from the arrack bottle with the frankness of a man past pretending, and looked at Vesper the way one looks at a locked door one has walked past every day for years. "Ten years you've been on their books. Okonkwo. That's you. V-okonkwo, entry four-forty-one."

Vesper's stomach performed a small, cold maneuver. "You know my number, sir?"

"I know everyone's number." Ilya said it flatly, without pride. Then, with a jerk of his thumb toward the inner door: "Everyone's."

Now. This was the moment any sensible person would pick up the rope and leave. Vesper knew this the way he knew the count of his breaths underwater — deeply, structurally, in the body. A drunk clerk inviting you to linger in the counting house was not hospitality. It was weather turning. Sensible people got under cover.

"I should get back," Vesper said. "Osei will dock my—"

"Ration. Yes. They dock rations the way other companies pay wages." Ilya snorted, which dissolved into something that was half laugh and half cough, and then he did the last thing Vesper expected. He stood up, swayed, caught the counter, and pushed open the inner door himself.

"Come and see," he said. "Since you're already here and already damned."

* * *

The inner room was smaller and darker, lined floor to ceiling with ledgers — hundreds of them, spines outward, each one a year or a vessel or a class of human activity the company preferred to keep arithmetic rather than conversation. Dust hung in the lamplight like something the room was thinking about.

And on the great oak table in the center, open, lay the master ledger.

Vesper had imagined, in the vague way one imagines the inside of a church, that it would feel holy. It did not. It felt heavy. That was the only word his mind supplied, standing three steps away: *heavy*, the way water is heavy, the way a drowned thing is heavy. The ledger was the size of a ship's hatch cover, bound in green leather gone black at the corners, and its pages were dense with columns ruled in red ink — names, sums, dates, and in the outer margin, small annotations in a clerk's hand that Ilya's own hand, he now noticed, matched exactly.

"Go on," Ilya said. He had lowered himself into a chair by the table and was watching Vesper with the arrack bottle loose between his fingers. "Look. Nobody looks. That's the whole trick of it, you understand? Ten years you've worked for these men and you've never seen the book they keep you in."

"They keep me in it?" Vesper asked, though he already knew.

"Where else would you be?" Ilya gestured at the page with the bottle, sloppily. "Here. Entry four-forty-one. Your indenture price, your interest accruals, your deductions — boots, salt, the fine for the broken net in your third season, do you remember the broken net? They remembered."

Vesper did remember. He had spent two weeks of rations paying for a net that had been old when it broke.

His eyes moved down the column, and that was when the vertigo began.

Because there, below his name, was his father's.

Okonkwo, E. Diver, second class. And next to it, in the death column, not the word the priest had said over the empty grave — not *lost*, not *taken by the sea* — but a date, a figure, and a notation so brief it seemed to have been written by someone in a hurry to be elsewhere:

*Debt settled by estate seizure.*

Vesper read it four times. The words didn't grow clearer; they grew heavier. Estate seizure. Their house. The one-room house with the crooked door and the fig tree Mama Sefu's wall leaned toward — the company had taken it the same week the sea took his father, and he had known that, had always known that, the way you know a bone healed wrong. But knowing it in your body and seeing it in red-ruled ink, entered in a fair hand, balanced to the penny — those were different kinds of knowing. One hurt. The other made the whole world tilt, because it meant the hurt had been *recorded*. Filed. It had happened so officially that paper remembered it better than he did.

"The deaths are all in there?" he heard himself ask. His voice came out steady. Ten years of practice will do that; grief has learned to use a level voice in Iloran.

"Every one." Ilya's voice had gone quiet too, and something in it had stopped being drunk. "Every diver lost, every debt transferred, every widow paid off or—" he turned the page with one finger, gently, the way you turn the bandage on a wound you caused, "—or *quieted*. Look at the margin column. See the little marks?"

Vesper saw them. Small circles, penciled beside certain entries. Beside each circled name, further down, a sum under a heading written in the trade-tongue: *disposal.*

"What does disposal mean?" he asked, though his mouth had gone dry enough that the question came out as a whisper.

"It means," Ilya said, and drank, and his hand shook worse going down than coming up, "that the company paid someone to stop asking. Or paid the family not to speak. Or — in the case of the ones with two circles — paid the harbormaster to record 'absconded' instead of 'lost,' because a man who absconds forfeits his estate to the company, and a man lost at sea leaves it to his widow. Do you understand? We didn't just write down what happened. Some pages, we *decided*. The sea takes what it takes. But the margins—" he tapped the penciled circles with a fingernail gone yellow, "—the margins are ours."

Vesper stood very still. Somewhere outside, the noon bell rang late, and neither of them moved.

He thought of his father's name in that margin. He scanned back, found the entry again, and felt his heart knock once, hard, against his ribs. No circle. Just the flat notation, the settled debt, the seized house. His father hadn't even merited a payment. The company hadn't considered his family worth silencing. They'd simply taken the house and assumed the grief would mind its manners, and it had — it had, for ten years, it had.

Paper knows more truth than any mouth in Iloran dares speak, Vesper thought, and the thought arrived with the specific dizziness of standing on a harbor edge at low tide, looking down at pilings that go much further down than the water suggests. Every lie the town lived inside — *the sea is hungry, the company is fair, the dead are at peace* — was contradicted somewhere on these shelves, in a fair hand, in triplicate.

"Why are you showing me this?" he asked.

Ilya looked at him for a long moment. The lamplight caught the sweat on his forehead and the shame underneath it, which was older and less shiny.

"Because you signed with a cross," he said finally, "and I've watched crosses sign that book for ten years, and I drink because—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "Because a man came to me in March. A diver named Tomo. He wanted to know what he still owed, exact figure, because he'd counted his breaths his whole career and wanted to count his debt just as exactly before the season ended. I told him. Fourteen pounds, seven shillings. He died in April. And in May his widow came and asked what she owed *now*, because the company man told her the debt had grown — funeral costs, you understand, administrative fees — and when she argued, the entry got a circle." He turned another page, almost tenderly, and there was Tomo's name, circled twice. "I made that circle, boy. My hand. My pencil."

The room was very quiet. Above them the weary fan creaked through another revolution of its long surrender.

"Then burn it," Vesper said. It came out before he could weigh it — the first reckless sentence he had spoken aloud in Iloran in a decade, and it tasted strange on his tongue, like breathing surface air too deep.

Ilya laughed, a small terrible sound. "Burn it? There are copies. There are abstracts in the district office, summaries in the charter reports, memoranda to the board. Burning this—" he laid his palm flat on the green leather, "—would be like burning one wave and calling the sea gone." He took his hand away as if the book had bitten it. "No. Paper like this doesn't burn. It just waits for someone who can read it."

"And no one can," Vesper said. "That's the trick. You said it yourself. Nobody looks."

"Nobody *may*," Ilya corrected softly. "There's a difference, and the difference is men with rifles." He straightened, and something like the old clerk returned to his spine, the porridge face firming into procedure. "You've seen nothing. You carried rope, you signed with your cross, you left. Say it back."

"I carried rope," Vesper said. "I signed with my cross. I left."

"Good." Ilya tore off the receipt slip and held it out — then, at the last moment, did not release it. His fingers stayed on the paper, trembling, and his eyes were suddenly, terribly clear.

"One more thing, boy. There are ships expected. Company ships — and not the usual ones. Big hulls, gray funnels, flying colors nobody in this harbor has saluted before. The tally-men are whispering. War comes up trade routes the way silt comes up a channel, slow and then all at once." He released the slip. "When it comes, the first thing men fight over isn't gold or grain. It's records. Whoever holds the paper holds the past. Remember that."

Vesper took the slip, hoisted the forty fathoms of rope onto his shoulder, and walked out into the white glare of noon feeling the weight of two things at once: hemp on his back, and his father's name behind his eyes, written in red-ruled ink, balanced to the penny, waiting on a shelf for someone who could read it.

At the top of the shell road he paused, out of habit, and looked at the harbor.

There were new shapes on the horizon. Three of them. Gray, patient, moving with the unhurried certainty of things that intended to arrive whether Iloran approved or not — and at their masts, colors no one in the port town had saluted before.
