## 4. Gunboats at First Light

Vesper Okonkwo learned about the army the way he learned most things: from gulls.

They had gone quiet. A decade of dawn dives had taught him that gulls were honest creatures—they screamed when there was fish, they screamed when there was danger, and when soldiers came up the coast road in the dark, they simply left, abandoning the harbor to a silence so complete he heard it before he opened his eyes.

He lay in his bunk in the divers' longhouse and listened. Not drums. Not the company bell. Boots—hundreds of boots, moving in step, which no one in Iloran ever did because Iloran could not agree on anything except the price of rope.

By the time he reached the compound gate, the sky had gone the color of pewter and the harbor was full of gunboats. Three of them, gray as old knives, sitting in water where yesterday there had been fishing smacks and one optimistic paddle-steamer called *Fatima's Pride* that had never once lived up to her name. Soldiers were already on the quay, forming lines, unrolling wire.

An officer stood at the harbor mouth reading from a paper. His voice carried over the water with the flatness of a man who had read this same paper in nine other towns.

"By authority of the Provisional Charter of the Victorious Powers, the port of Iloran and all assets therein—including but not limited to the Meridian Trading Company, its vessels, warehouses, ledgers, contracts, and personnel—are hereby placed under requisition for the duration of the administrative transition."

Vesper stood very still among the other divers gathering at the fence line, and did what he had trained himself ten years to do: nothing.

Beside him, old Tambo whispered, "Transition to what?"

"To us being quiet," Vesper said, "in a new direction."

Tambo considered this and nodded, because it was as good an explanation as any.

The town held its breath. That was the remarkable thing—not panic, not cheering, just the whole of Iloran going still the way a diver goes still when something large passes overhead. The bakers kept baking, because hunger does not wait for politics, but they baked without singing. The fishwives gutted their catch without arguing about prices, which Vesper had personally never witnessed before and found deeply unsettling.

And through it all, the Meridian Company men stood on the counting house steps in their good coats, and Vesper watched them do the arithmetic of survival behind their eyes. He knew that arithmetic intimately. It was the same sum every diver performed each morning: how much breath, how much debt, how much silence would get me to evening.

Only now the company was doing it too.

He almost smiled. Almost. Because somewhere under the dread, a small cold voice in him observed: *they are learning what we have always known.* And another voice, quieter and less kind, asked: *and who will write down what happens to them?*

Nobody. Nobody wrote down what happened to people like them. That was the first rule of Iloran, older than the company, older than the guns.

Or so Vesper believed until the day the ledger changed hands.

* * *

Sergeant Amara Diallo had been a soldier for four years and had hated nearly every hour of it with a thoroughness she reserved for things she could not escape, like conscription and boiled mutton.

She stood on the quay at Iloran in the gray morning light, checking her third list of the hour, and reflected that the Victorious Powers had a genius for making victory feel like inventory. Requisition the harbor. Requisition the warehouses. Requisition, per Order Forty-Seven, "all commercial records and archives pending historical review"—which meant, in the language she'd learned growing up between two empires, *nobody upstairs knows what to do with the paperwork either.*

"Sergeant." Corporal Vesely jogged up, young and earnest and permanently out of breath. "The company clerk says the master ledger can't be moved without the factor's signature."

"The factor is under arrest."

"Yes, Sergeant, but he says his signature is still legally valid."

Diallo looked at him. Vesely had the grace to look embarrassed. "He said that? While under arrest?"

"He was quite insistent. Also he offered me his watch."

"What did you say?"

"I said I couldn't take bribes before breakfast."

"Good instincts," Diallo said. "Take the watch after lunch."

It was not, strictly speaking, the sort of discipline the manual recommended. But Diallo had learned something in four years of marching behind other men's flags: the empire ran on rules, and rules ran on the goodwill of tired people deciding not to enforce half of them. She enforced the ones that mattered and let the rest breathe. So far it had kept her alive and mostly decent, which was more than she could say for Captain Osei, currently on the counting house steps explaining the Provisional Charter to a row of pale company factors like a schoolmaster reading poetry to men who only spoke arithmetic.

She walked the quay. That was her method—walk everything, see everything, let the lists come second to the looking. Lists lied. Places didn't.

Iloran smelled of salt and tar and, faintly, of ripe figs from somewhere inland, which struck her as absurdly peaceful for a town that had just been conquered before breakfast. The fishing boats sat at anchor under guard. The warehouses yawned open, soldiers inside stacking crates with the particular incompetence of men who had joined the army to avoid lifting things.

And everywhere, the locals watched her. Not hostilely—that was the strange part. They watched the way she imagined mice watched a new cat: professionally, taking notes, drawing conclusions she would not be told.

A boy crossed her path carrying a coil of rope, head down, steps measured. Conscription age if he'd been born on the other side of the water, Diallo thought. Here he was probably already owned; the charter papers mentioned the company held "labor obligations" it wished to transfer. She'd read that phrase twice. *Labor obligations.* Four years in the army had taught her that when documents got polite, people got hurt.

"You," she said.

The boy stopped. Didn't flinch—she noted that, noted it hard, because flinching was honest and this wasn't. He turned with a face like a closed door and waited.

"Your name?"

"Vesper Okonkwo, Sergeant."

"Diver?"

"Yes, Sergeant."

"For the company?"

"For whoever pays, Sergeant." A beat. "Currently the company."

Despite herself, Diallo felt the corner of her mouth move. There it was—the whole war in miniature, delivered by a boy with rope. *For whoever pays.*

"Where's your pass?"

"I don't have one, Sergeant. I've never needed one. The sea doesn't read."

Somewhere behind her, Vesely made a noise like a swallowed laugh. Diallo stared at the boy, who stared back with perfectly respectful blankness, and she understood that she had been outmaneuvered by someone half her size and a tenth of her armament.

"Get a pass," she said finally. "New administration. Everything needs a pass now."

"Yes, Sergeant." He shouldered the rope and went, and she watched him go and thought: *that one will be trouble or dead, and I hope it's neither.*

Then she went back to her lists, because that was the job. The lists said: requisition the harbor, requisition the warehouses, requisition the archives pending historical review. She checked off each item with a stub of pencil and tried not to think about what *pending historical review* meant, or why a company's debts and deaths needed reviewing at all, or who, exactly, would do the reviewing.

The pencil scratched. The town held its breath. Somewhere inland, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

* * *

By afternoon, the occupation had developed routines, as occupations do, and Vesper watched them form with the attention of a man studying weather.

Soldiers at the warehouse doors. Soldiers at the harbor wire. A soldier at the counting house gate who had already, in six hours, perfected the thousand-yard stare of a man guarding paper. The company factors hovered around the victors' officers like gulls around a gutting table, and the officers tolerated them with the weary courtesy of men who had not yet decided whether these were allies or luggage.

Mama Sefu appeared at the compound wall at dusk with a basket of figs, ostensibly for the divers, actually for intelligence.

"They took the counting house keys at noon," she reported, low, arranging figs with great ceremony. "All of them. Even the cellar key Ilya Petrov keeps hidden in his boot—he gave it up himself, which surprised everyone, including Ilya."

"Why would he give it up?"

"Because a sergeant asked nicely, and Ilya has never once in his life been asked nicely." She selected a fig for herself, bit it, chewed with judicial slowness. "There's an historian coming. Word from the market. Some great pen from the capital, come to write down how it all happened here."

Vesper went still. "Write down how what happened?"

"How the company ran Iloran, I expect. How benevolent it all was." Her voice was dry as seed husks. "The victors always bring a writer, child. Guns take the town; ink takes the story. Your father taught you that?"

"My father taught me to count my breaths."

"Well," Mama Sefu said, "count those too. You'll need both."

She gathered her basket and paused at the gap in the wall, and for a moment the humor fell away from her face like a cloth pulled off a table. "Listen to me. Whatever they write, whatever they burn, whatever they decide we were—you remember what you were. Memory is the one storeroom they haven't found the key to yet."

She left. Vesper sat against the wall with a fig going soft in his hand, and thought about keys.

That night he lay awake doing the new arithmetic, the occupation arithmetic, and it frightened him more than any dive ever had. Before the gunboats, the rule had been simple: the company owned the words, so the divers stayed silent, and silence was safe because everyone agreed on the lie. Now there were two sets of owners, two sets of words, and the space between them was exactly wide enough for a man like Vesper to fall into—or, possibly, to stand in.

Who must be silent about what? That was the question now. The company needed silence about the ledger. The army needed silence about its orders. And somewhere in the middle, small and expendable, the people of Iloran needed silence about everything, as always.

But Vesper lay in the dark and felt something shift in him, tectonic and slow. For ten years he had been silent because silence was survival.

Tonight, for the first time, silence felt like a debt.

And if there was one thing Vesper Okonkwo understood better than breath, better than fear, better than the weight of water—it was debt. Who owed it. To whom. And what happened when the account came due.

Three days later, he saw the ledger carried out of the counting house under seal, two soldiers on either side of it like pallbearers, and he understood that the account had already begun to move.
