## 5. Requisition

The soldiers came for the ledgers on the third morning after the gunboats, and they came with clipboards.

Vesper noticed the clipboards before he noticed anything else—the neat little slates of authority, the pencils on strings like rosaries—and he thought, absurdly, that his father would have found it funny. All those ships, all those rifles, all that gray machinery of conquest rowed ashore at first light, and the men carrying it marched up the counting house steps clutching paper like schoolboys late for lessons.

He was stacking rope coils in the warehouse yard when the order came down. A corporal with a voice like a dropped crate announced that all company property was hereby requisitioned under the victors' charter—every crate, every manifest, every weight and measure—and that all persons employed by the Meridian Trading Company would continue their labors *in service of the provisional administration*, which was a long way of saying: same work, new masters, no questions.

"Same work," muttered Dossa beside him, an old diver whose hands had gone soft as kelp from too many years ashore. "Same rope, same debt. Only now the interest belongs to somebody else."

"Hold your tongue," Vesper said quietly.

"I've been holding it forty years. It's getting heavy."

Vesper almost smiled. That was the trouble with Dossa—he made you want to laugh in places where laughing got people counted. And everything, these days, was being counted.

* * *

They counted the harbor first. Then the salt sheds, the drying racks, the cooperage, the eleven whaleboats and four longboats and one leaking punt that some soldier solemnly recorded as *vessel, small, condition poor*, as though the army might weep over its loss. Vesper watched from the yard, rope over his shoulder, learning them. That was the old habit, the ten-year habit: watch, count, keep silent. Count the sentries at the gates—six, changing at odd hours, bored. Count the officers—one major, two captains, a clerk-caste of lieutenants who measured things. Count what they looked at and what they didn't. They looked at guns and boats and barrels of salt fish. They did not look at divers, except as line items. *Labor, indentured, native: one hundred and forty souls.* Vesper heard a lieutenant say *souls* without any irony at all, and filed it away in the dark drawer where he kept such things.

By noon the counting house doors stood open for the first time in Vesper's memory—not open for business, but open like a wound. Soldiers went in and out carrying armloads of paper. Receipt books. Charter documents. The great brass scales, hauled out on a handcart, swinging gently, absurdly, like something being taken to be married rather than confiscated.

And then, just past midday, they brought out the ledger.

Vesper saw it from thirty paces and knew it instantly, the way you know a parent's coat hanging on a stranger. It was not beautiful. It was an ugly, honest thing—half a forearm thick, bound in cracked leather the color of dried blood, its corners worn round by forty years of hands. Two soldiers carried it between them on a plank, the way men carry anything heavy and awkward, and a third walked behind holding the clipboard against his chest like a breastplate.

Vesper set down the rope coil and stood very still.

He had seen it only once before, through a doorway and across a room, but a man does not forget the book that owns him. Every diver in Iloran was in there—not as flesh, but as figures. Debts entered in a fair clerk's hand. Payments deducted. Deaths recorded in a column so narrow the entries barely fit, as if even the paper had been instructed to give dying as little room as possible.

The soldiers carried it down the steps. One of them stumbled slightly on the last stair—a big man, unaccustomed to the weight—and caught himself, and the ledger lurched in his grip, and for half a heartbeat it hung tipping over the cobbles like a drunk over a rail, and Vesper's whole body clenched around the thought *don't drop it, don't you dare drop it,* and he could not have said, even to himself, whether he meant *drop it into the harbor, let the sea have it back* or *drop nothing, ever, that book is the only place my father is still written down.*

The soldier steadied it. They loaded it onto a wagon with the scales and the charter box and a strongbox of company seals. A lieutenant licked his pencil and wrote something on his clipboard, and Vesper read the words upside down from twenty feet away, because ten years of watching clerks had taught him to read upside down the way other boys learned to whistle:

**LEDGER, MASTER — SEALED. EVIDENCE PENDING HISTORICAL REVIEW.**

*Historical review.* Vesper turned the phrase over like a coin of uncertain metal. He knew what review meant when the company did it: it meant a man sat with your account and decided how much of your life remained yours. He was beginning to suspect that history, done by victors, worked much the same way. Somebody would sit with that ledger—all those narrow death-entries, all those struck names, including, somewhere in the middle years, a line reading *Okonkwo, T., lost at sea, debt transferred*—and somebody would decide what it meant. Whether it meant anything. Whether drowned men were a cost of doing business or a scandal or simply, conveniently, nobody.

The wagon rolled toward the customs shed, where a sentry snapped to attention over nothing. Vesper picked up his rope coil again because a man who stands still too long gets noticed, and noticing, these days, was dangerous in both directions.

But he watched the wagon until it turned the corner. Cold clarity settled over him, not like fear but like deep water—the pressure of it, the way it presses equally from every side and lets you know exactly how far down you are. His father's true fate was in that book. Not the story Mama Sefu told softly on winter nights, not the story the company told its creditors, but the actual ink-and-paper fact of what had been taken and what had been paid and to whom. For ten years that truth had slept in the counting house, unread, harmless, *theirs*. Now it belonged to men with clipboards who would decide what it meant, and Vesper understood with perfect certainty that whatever they decided, it would not be the truth. Victors write benevolent histories; everyone in Iloran knew that the way they knew the tide tables. The only question was whether anyone would still be alive who remembered otherwise.

He was. He remembered. But memory, Vesper thought, hauling rope, is a poor witness. Memory forgets dates. Memory forgives. Memory dies.

Paper doesn't.

The thought arrived quietly, the way dangerous thoughts do, dressed in ordinary clothes, and Vesper tucked it into the dark drawer and shut it and went back to work.

* * *

In the afternoon they requisitioned Mama Sefu's orchard—or part of it.

Vesper was carrying a basket of fallen figs along the compound wall when the soldiers arrived at her gate: two of them, plus the clipboard lieutenant, who consulted his papers and informed the widow Ishikawa-Sefu that the south rows of her orchard lay within the requisitioned harbor perimeter and would be required for the storage of military materiel.

Mama Sefu stood in her gate with her hands folded on her apron, a small woman built like a question mark and about as easy to straighten out. She listened to the whole speech without blinking. Then she said:

"The fig trees?"

"The trees will be removed as necessary."

"Removed." She said it the way a priest says a word in a dead language. "Those trees are older than your army, Lieutenant. Older than the company. I planted the eldest the year my husband died, and he has fed every child in this quarter who was hungry enough to climb him, including several of your predecessors' fathers, though I doubt they mention it."

"Materiel storage," the lieutenant repeated, with the patience of a man reading weather to a stone.

"You'll want the south rows," Mama Sefu said. "Of course you will. Everyone wants the south rows. They get the sun." She sighed, and something shifted in her face—not surrender, Vesper saw, but repositioning, the way she repositioned chairs. "You may store your materiel. You will not cut one tree. If a single branch comes off a single fig, I shall come to your major's office every single morning for the rest of your posting and tell him about it, at length, beginning with the year of my marriage, and I promise you, young man, I remember *everything*."

The lieutenant looked at her. The two soldiers looked at her. Vesper, frozen behind his basket by the wall, looked at her and felt the cold clarity of the morning crack, just slightly, into warmth.

"We'll need access," the lieutenant managed.

"Through the north gate. You'll wipe your boots." She stepped aside with the air of a queen granting a border concession. "And someone should tell your quartermaster that the ground floods in the south rows after rain. His materiel will float. But what do I know? I'm only the woman who has kept those rows alive through two storms and one company."

They took the south rows. They did not touch a branch. As they tramped off, one of the soldiers glanced back at the old woman in the gate, and Vesper would have sworn on his father's grave-marker that the man looked less like a conqueror than like a boy who'd just been scolded by somebody's grandmother and couldn't decide whether to be angry or relieved.

That evening Vesper delivered the fig basket to her kitchen, as he always did, and stayed, as he always did, and she put honey on bread and pushed it across the table without comment, because in that kitchen affection was served the way water was served—without ceremony, but always.

"They're taking everything," he said finally. "Boats, sheds, the scales. The ledger most of all. Sealed. *Evidence pending historical review.*"

Mama Sefu chewed slowly. "Historical," she said. "That's a new word for Iloran."

"It means they'll tell it however suits them."

"It means that." She wiped her hands on her apron. "My mother lived through the last change of flags. Do you know what she remembered of it? Not the battles. She remembered that suddenly all the tavern songs were different, and everyone pretended the old ones had never existed." She looked at him over the table, eyes sharp as a gull's. "Songs can be forgotten, boy. Ledgers can be burned. What can't be forgotten is whoever learned the old ones by heart."

Vesper said nothing. But his hand, resting on the table, had gone still, and Mama Sefu looked at the stillness of that hand for a long moment, and then, being who she was, said nothing either—only rose and cut more bread, as though nothing important had passed between them, which was her particular genius.

* * *

Two evenings later, Vesper was sent to the customs shed with a requisition chit for tar and canvas. The sentry waved him through without looking up. Inside, the shed smelled of hemp and salt and lamp oil, and the requisitioned wealth of the Meridian Trading Company lay stacked under tarpaulins in the yellow lamplight: the brass scales, the charter box, crates of manifests—and there, on a table apart from the rest, alone, with a sentry dozing in a chair beside it, the master ledger. A strip of official paper had been pasted across its cover. Vesper could read it upside down from the door, because of course he could:

**SEALED — EVIDENCE PENDING HISTORICAL REVIEW — UNLAWFUL TO OPEN OR REMOVE**

It lay there under the lamp like a body laid out. That was the image that came to him and would not leave: not a book at all but a corpse awaiting burial, and the wrong kind of burial—the kind where nobody speaks the name, where the prayers are said by strangers in a language the dead don't know. Somewhere inside that leather skin was his father. *Okonkwo, T.* Four letters and an initial, and beneath them, in a narrow column, whatever actually happened the week the company took their home. Vesper had spent ten years not knowing and telling himself he didn't want to know, and now the knowing sat twenty feet away under a drowsing guard, sealed by men who had never heard his father's voice.

The sentry snored. The lamp flickered. Vesper stood in the doorway with his chit for tar and canvas and felt the cold clarity come back, deeper this time, colder and clearer both.

They would burn it. Not soon—there would be a review first, a historian, some penman from the capital with clean hands—but eventually, inevitably, once the official account was drafted and safe, the evidence would become inconvenient, and inconvenient evidence in Iloran had always ended the same way. Ash in the harbor wind. He had seen the company burn old manifests before; there had been a cheerful little fire behind the cooperage one spring, and the clerks had roasted chestnuts on the coals of other men's debts. The victors would be tidier about it, but the ending would rhyme.

Unless someone wrote it down first. Unless someone copied every page, every name, every narrow entry in the column of deaths, before the men with clipboards decided what it all meant.

It was a mad thought. He examined it honestly, turning it over like the coin it was, looking for the flaw. He could not read well enough—not the cramped clerk-hand, not fast, not pages at a time. He had no access. He had no allies. He had, he realized, standing in the tar-smelling dark with his heart going slow and hard as a hammer on a hull, almost nothing except a widow who remembered everything, a wall full of stolen figs' worth of children who had grown up on stories of the drowned, and ten years of practice at watching without being seen.

Almost nothing. In Iloran, that was practically an arsenal.

The sentry stirred, and Vesper moved on, collected his tar and canvas, signed—made his mark—on the requisition form, and walked out past the ledger without looking at it again. Looking was a habit he could not yet afford.

But that night, lying in the divers' dormitory listening to the tide work the harbor pilings, he made himself a promise with the shape of a plan inside it, the way a seed holds a tree: *before they decide what it meant, someone will know what it said.*

The next morning, a notice went up on the counting house door, fresh ink, official seal. All persons having knowledge of company operations would present themselves for interview by order of the Provisional Administration. A historian was arriving from the capital on the morning boat.

His name, the notice said, was Edouard Marchand.
