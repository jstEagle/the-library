## 6. The Historian's Pen

The historian arrived on a Tuesday, in a carriage too fine for Iloran's roads, and the first thing he did was lose his hat to the wind.

Vesper watched it happen from the counting house steps, where he stood waiting to be told to carry something. The hat—broad-brimmed, pale straw, the kind of hat that had never known salt air—lifted off the man's head as he stepped down, sailed twice around the courtyard like a gull considering its options, and settled in the harbor shallows. The historian stared at it for a long moment. Then he took out a small notebook and wrote something down.

"He's writing about your hat," whispered Tomas, the youngest of the counting house boys.

"No," Vesper said quietly. "He's writing about us. The hat is just how we look to him."

The man's name was Edouard Marchand, and everyone learned it within a day, because the company men said it so often and so loudly. *Monsieur Marchand of the Capital. Monsieur Marchand, official historian of the Liberation of Iloran.* He had a commission under the new charter, sealed in wax the color of dried blood, authorizing him to record "the true and grateful history" of the town's deliverance from the Meridian Trading Company—which was to say, the company that still ran the warehouses, paid the wages, and held the indenture papers, only now under a different flag.

Vesper turned this over in his mind the way he turned over shells at low tide, looking for what lived inside them. The company had not fallen. It had changed costumes. And now it had hired a man with a fine notebook to describe the costume as skin.

They summoned the townsfolk first—the fishmongers, the chandlers, old Benoit who claimed to remember everything and remembered mostly wrong. Vesper watched them come out of the requisitioned customs hall one by one, blinking in the afternoon light, each of them wearing the same expression: the look of people who had been told precisely which memories to have and were trying them on for fit. Mama Sefu went in on the third day and came out with her chin high and her eyes flat as coins.

"Well?" Vesper asked her that evening, in the orchard, under the fig tree whose lowest branches they'd stripped bare together ten years ago.

"They wanted to know if I felt liberated," she said, sorting fruit with more force than figs required. "I told them I felt my knees. At my age, that is the only liberation I trust." She glanced at him. "The man taking notes looked like he wanted to laugh. I think there may be hope for him. Or he is simply better at hiding than the others."

"And what did you tell him?"

"I told him the company was always generous to me." She set down a bruised fig with great care. "Which is true, if you don't count anything that matters."

* * *

Edouard Marchand had written three books before this one, and all of them had been about dead men. This was why he had wanted the commission. Dead men could not contradict you; their wars were safely finished; you could arrange them on the page like flowers in a pressed-glass case. Living towns were another matter entirely.

Living towns lied to him.

Not clumsily—he had expected clumsy lies, the stammering evasions of the guilty. What unsettled him was the polish. Every witness in Iloran gave him the same account, word for word in places, as though they had all been reading from an invisible pamphlet: *the Company treated its divers well, the divers were proud of their work, the town prospered, the army arrived and was welcomed.* A fishmonger said "prospered" with the stress on the second syllable. An old woman said "welcomed" while looking out the window at the soldiers requisitioning her neighbor's boat.

Marchand began keeping a second notebook.

In it he wrote things like: *Witness 14 used the phrase 'no diver ever drowned who followed protocol.' Asked how many drowned. Witness 14 became very interested in a moth.*

And: *Witness 9 (chandler) says the Company 'never kept records of misfortune.' But every trading concern keeps records of everything. One does not run a ledger business without ledgers. Where are the ledgers?*

It was on the fourth day, reviewing his notes by lamplight in the requisitioned customs house, that Marchand noticed the strangest thing of all. The coached witnesses gave identical answers—and identical silences. There were gaps in every testimony, shaped exactly alike, like holes cut by the same paper pattern. Around those holes, the witnesses talked freely. Inside them, nothing.

A historian learns early that the shape of a silence can tell you more than the words around it. These silences had the shape of names. Of numbers. Of a ledger.

He wrote that down too.

* * *

On the fifth day, they came for Vesper.

Two company clerks and a soldier, arriving at dawn as if he might be harder to find after breakfast. "You're wanted as a native witness," said the elder clerk, whose name was Pell and whose breath smelled of the company's own rum. "Don't be clever. Answer the gentleman's questions and you'll be back at work by noon."

Vesper had spent ten years learning what happened to clever indentured men, so he nodded and walked between them through streets still wet with morning, past the harbor where the seized boats sat at anchor flying a flag he hadn't grown up under.

The customs hall had been transformed. A desk now stood where the tariff tables used to be; a rug lay over the stain where old Captain Reyes had once been sick during an audit; and behind the desk sat Marchand, minus his hat, looking less like a conqueror's chronicler than like a schoolmaster dreading the examinations he himself had set.

"Sit, please," Marchand said, in trade-tongue that was accented but careful. "Thank you for coming. This will not take long. I am writing the history of the liberation, and I wish to include the voices of the town. Your voice."

"Yes, sir."

"You are a diver for the Company?"

"I am, sir."

"For how long?"

"Ten years, sir."

"Ten years." Marchand wrote it. "And how would you describe the Company's treatment of its divers?"

Here it was. Vesper had prepared for this question the way he prepared for a dive: checking every knot before he left the surface. He let a small smile rise, the smile of a simple man recalling simple kindnesses. "Good, sir. They fed us. We had a roof. Mama—" he stopped, corrected, "—a widow near the compound gives us fruit sometimes. Off her own trees. Very generous town."

"Generous," Marchand repeated, and wrote it. "Were there dangers? Diving is dangerous work."

"Some men drown," Vesper agreed mildly. "The sea is the sea."

"The sea is the sea," Marchand echoed, and for a moment his pen paused above the page. Something flickered across his face—not suspicion, quite. Recognition. Vesper had seen that look before, on the face of a new overseer realizing, mid-inspection, that the divers below him understood far more of his language than they admitted. The look of a man discovering the water was deeper than his charts showed.

"How many?" Marchand asked. "In your time. How many men drowned?"

Vesper counted silently, letting the pause stretch just long enough to seem like honest difficulty. "I couldn't say, sir. I mind my own line. That's how you stay alive down there—you mind your own line and you don't look into other men's water."

Something moved behind the historian's eyes. He wrote: *minds his own line,* and underlined nothing, and asked the next question.

"And the Company? Did it keep account of such losses?"

"Company keeps accounts of everything, sir." Vesper let that sit, then added, with the guileless warmth of a man offering a compliment, "That's what makes them good traders, isn't it? They never forget a thing."

Marchand's pen stopped entirely.

For three seconds the room was silent except for the harbor sounds coming through the open window—a soldier shouting, a gull arguing with the soldier—and in those three seconds Vesper watched the historian arrive somewhere and try to decide whether to stay. Then Marchand closed his notebook, thanked him for his candor, and said he might have follow-up questions later.

"Anytime, sir," Vesper said, standing, bowing slightly. "Happy to help with the history."

At the door he paused, struck by a sudden generous impulse. "Sir? If you're writing about the town—you should write about the orchards. Everyone remembers the orchards. Sweetest figs on the coast. That's the part worth keeping."

He walked back to the compound in the bright indifferent morning, feeling the peculiar cleanliness of a lie built entirely from truths. He had said nothing false. He had said nothing at all. And yet somewhere behind him, a man with two notebooks was beginning to understand that Iloran's silences had architecture—that someone had built them, deliberately, and that buildings imply builders.

Pell walked beside him, satisfied. "You did well. Simple answers. He likes simple."

"Everyone does," Vesper said.

* * *

That night Marchand sat in the customs hall with both notebooks open and poured himself the last of a bottle he had been making last since the capital.

The official account was assembling itself beautifully. He could write it in a week: the grateful port, the benevolent company misunderstood by distant critics, the orderly transition under the victors' charter, the happy natives. His career would flourish. There might even be a medal.

Instead he found himself drawing, in the margin of his private notebook, a diagram. Two columns, headed DEBT and DEATH, with a third column beside them whose header he could not bring himself to guess at. Below it he wrote:

*Every witness coached. Coached poorly, or coached perfectly? Perfect coaching looks like this—uniformity plus uniform holes. Who drilled them? The Company's men, obviously. But here is the question my commission forbids me to ask: if the story needs this much drilling, what is the drill protecting?*

*One diver today told me the Company "never forgets a thing." He smiled when he said it. I have interviewed generals, ministers, and one embezzler of national reputation, and that diver is the only man in Iloran who spoke a true sentence to me. I am nearly sure of it. I am not sure which sentence it was.*

He blew out the lamp and sat in the dark listening to the harbor, thinking about a straw hat floating in shallow water, and how the whole town had watched him chase it, and how not one person had laughed until he laughed first—permission granted, carefully, collectively, the way a town grants everything.

There was a ledger somewhere. He would find it. Whether he would print what it said was a question he was not yet ready to ask, and so he did what careful men do with unbearable questions: he wrote it down, closed the book, and hid it under the floorboard where he had already begun hiding the other things.

Three days later, Sergeant Amara Diallo would request an appointment with him—an interview, she said, about transport logistics. She would close the door behind her. And the first thing she would say, in a voice like a woman stepping off a ledge, would be: "Historian. Before you write anything else, you should know the Company's casualty figures are wrong. Deliberately. And I can prove how much by."
