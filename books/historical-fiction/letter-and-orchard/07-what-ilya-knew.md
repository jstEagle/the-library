## 7. What Ilya Knew

The fish market at dawn smelled the way honesty ought to smell, Ilya Petrov had always thought — brine and blood and nothing hidden. He had thought a great many clever things in his life, mostly while drunk, and this was one of the few that still held up.

He crouched behind Kofi Mensah's third stall, where the eels were packed in wet sacking and nobody with any sense lingered past first light. He had learned, over eleven days of hiding, that people avoided the eel stall for reasons of smell rather than sentiment, which made it the safest room in Iloran for a man who did not wish to be found.

His ribs pressed against his shirt like the staves of a barrel left too long in the sun. He had eaten half a dried herring that morning and the other half the night before, and he had begun composing his own obituary in the margins of his mind, in the neat clerk's hand that was now the only thing he owned outright: *Ilya Petrov, forty-one years of age, keeper of accounts, keeper of silences. Survived by no one, owing everyone.*

It was not a bad line. He might have used it in a letter if there had been anyone to write to.

Then a shadow fell across the eels, and Ilya looked up into the face of Vesper Okonkwo, and understood that his obituary would have to wait.

* * *

Vesper did not look surprised to find him. That was the worst part. He looked like a man who had done arithmetic and arrived at an answer he didn't like but trusted anyway.

"You're thinner," Vesper said.

"I'm economizing." Ilya's voice came out rusty, like a drawer not opened since spring. "The company discontinued my salary along with my position. Apparently honesty is a redundancy."

Vesper crouched down beside him among the eels, folding himself with the ease of someone who had spent ten years learning to take up as little space as possible. Up close Ilya could see the diver's marks on him — the pale crescents under his eyes where the pressure left its signature, the hands scarred faintly at the knuckles from rope and coral. Twenty years old, perhaps. The company's books said nineteen. The company's books said many things.

"How did you find me?" Ilya asked.

"The fishmongers talk. They say a thin man has been sleeping near the ice house and paying for bread with buttons." Vesper paused. "You paid with buttons?"

"They were good buttons." It sounded defensive even to him. "Brass. Military issue. I took them off a coat I no longer require."

Something moved at the corner of Vesper's mouth. Not quite a smile. The beginning of one, maybe, held in reserve, the way sailors hold a rope ready to run.

"Come somewhere warmer," Vesper said.

"No."

"Yes."

"Vesper." Ilya made his voice flat and official, the voice he had used across counting-house desks for fifteen years. "Whatever you think I can do for you, I can't. I'm finished here. When the army finishes cataloguing what the company kept, they'll want someone to blame, and I am the most blameable object in Iloran. Every page in that ledger passed through my hands. If you're seen near me, some of that ink will rub off on you. Ink does that. It's practically its whole profession."

Vesper considered this with the patience of a man who had learned to hold his breath for four minutes and could therefore outlast almost anything, including clerks.

"Mama Sefu says," he said finally, "that a man who hides behind eels is either very wise or very frightened, and that wise men come in out of the cold eventually because wisdom includes knowing when you're being stupid."

"She sounds like a formidable woman."

"She keeps figs."

"Ah," said Ilya, who had lived in Iloran long enough to understand that this explained everything. "Figs."

* * *

They walked through the waking market, Ilya hunched inside his stolen coat with its missing buttons, feeling every eye like a hand on his collar though in truth nobody looked twice at him. That was another thing the ledger had taught him: how thoroughly a man can be erased while still walking around. He had entered divers into that book the way one enters crates — weight, condition, destination — and he had never once imagined the process running in reverse. Now he knew how it felt from the inside. Like being a figure in a column, waiting for someone to strike you through.

Vesper led him not to the compound but to the seawall below the drying racks, where the wind carried off the smell of fish and replaced it with the smell of salt, which was at least an honest smell, and sat down on the stones. Ilya sat too, because his knees had opinions about standing.

For a while neither spoke. Below them the harbor lay grey and crowded with requisitioned hulls, gun-grey, flying the victors' colors. Somewhere on the far quay soldiers were counting barrels. Counting was happening all over Iloran this morning, Ilya thought. It was the only prayer anybody still knew how to say.

"You looked at the second column," Vesper said.

Not a question. Ilya closed his eyes.

"You saw it," Vesper pressed, gently, terribly. "That day. Fetching rope. You let me look too long, and I saw it. There were two columns of figures after each name. Debts. And something else."

"There were payments," Ilya said, to the harbor, to the water, to nobody. "After a diver was lost. The company paid the family. A small sum. Enough for a funeral and a season's rice and not enough for anything else. And in exchange —"

"In exchange they signed."

"A mark. Most of them couldn't write, so a mark. A mark against the name, and the entry changed. 'Lost at sea' became 'debt settled by estate seizure' became, in the later years, 'absconded,' because absconded divers' families weren't owed anything at all, and the payment column went quiet." He laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as old paper. "You'd be amazed what a word can do, Vesper. One word, chosen carefully, and a drowned man becomes a runaway. A widow becomes a creditor. Grief becomes a settled account. I chose those words. Fifteen years. I was very good at choosing them."

The tide worried at the pilings below. Vesper said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy; Ilya had spent eleven days alone with his voice and discovered it needed an audience or it curdled.

"There's more," he said. "Since we're confessing. I kept carbons."

Vesper turned his head. In the grey morning light his eyes were the color of deep water, and Ilya had the sensation of being looked at by the sea itself, which takes everything and gives back only what it chooses.

"When I first took the position — young, idealistic in the way that only means ignorant — I couldn't bear it. So I made carbon copies. Quiet ones. Names, dates, payments, causes. A little parallel book, the true twin of the false one. I told myself I'd use it someday. Testimony. Justice. All the words young men use for things they'll never do." His hands, wrapped around his knees, had gone white at the knuckles. "And then one night old Meridian's son — the son, not the father; the father at least had the decency to die before I could hate him properly — called me in and showed me a drawer. Full of ash. He said, 'We hear you've been practicing your penmanship at home.' He didn't search me. He didn't have to. I went home and I burned every page in my own stove, and I fed the fire myself, page by page, and I remember thinking how quick it was. Eleven years of names. Twenty minutes of flame."

"What names?" Vesper asked. Very quietly. Very steadily.

Ilya looked at him then and understood, with the clarity that comes to drowning men, that this question was the reason the boy had found him. Not kindness. Not curiosity. Arithmetic.

"Your father's was among them," Ilya said.

The wind came off the water and pushed between them, and for a moment Vesper Okonkwo was perfectly still, the way he must have been still underwater, still in the compound, still through ten years of small silences — and Ilya thought: *there it is. That's what I made. That stillness. I helped make that.*

"He went down off the Meridian shoal in the autumn of his fourth year," Ilya went on, because stopping now would have been the last cruelty. "Rope fouled. Two other divers saw it happen. The company recorded 'lost at sea,' and then the payment column shows three silver pieces to his household, marked received. But your mother never received them, did she."

"No," Vesper said. The word came out level. Underneath it, Ilya heard the ocean. "She died that winter. And the house — the company took the house. Said his debt wasn't settled. Said the payment covered the funeral only."

"It covered whatever the signature said it covered. And the signature —" Ilya stopped. Started again. "There was no signature, Vesper. For your father's entry there is a mark in the received column in *my* handwriting. I wrote 'received' myself, on instruction, and the money stayed in the company's till, and your family's silence cost them nothing because they never knew they were supposed to be selling it."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the tide to turn. Ilya counted it the way he counted everything, out of habit, out of cowardice, out of the clerk's disease of measuring time so as not to live in it.

When Vesper finally spoke, his voice had not risen. That was the frightening thing. Ten years in the compound had taught him to keep his voice level through anything, and what came out of him now was level as a frozen lake.

"You burned them."

"Yes."

"All of them."

"Yes."

"And now the original sits in a soldier's warehouse, sealed as evidence, waiting for historians to decide what my father meant." Vesper picked up a stone and held it, weighing it, not throwing it. "And you are hiding behind eels."

"I am hiding behind eels," Ilya agreed. It seemed important to be accurate.

"They will burn it. The counting house archives. Everyone whispers it — sanitation, they call it. A fire to tidy up the story before the new history gets written. And when it burns, that's the end. No copies. No carbons. Nothing but what the victors choose to print." He turned the stone over in his fingers. "Unless somebody writes it down again."

Ilya laughed — really laughed this time, a cracked, astonished sound. "Write it down? From memory? Fifteen years of entries, hundreds of names, exact sums, exact dates? Boy, I can't remember what I ate yesterday. Yesterday I ate half a herring. Or possibly that was the day before."

"You remember the figures."

"I remember —" He began to deny it and found he couldn't. Because it was true, and both of them knew it was true, and there are some truths a man cannot unlearn even to save his skin. "I remember everything," he said miserably. "That's the curse of the trade. Numbers go into me and they don't leave. I could tell you the quarterly tonnage of shell for nine consecutive years. I could tell you the interest rate applied to widows versus orphans — there *was* a difference, Vesper, we charged orphans less, someone in the office had a soft heart and it showed up as a rate adjustment, cruelty with a rounding error." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I could reconstruct most of the second column from habit. Payments, dates, dispositions. Not all. Perhaps not even half. But more than anyone else alive could give you."

"Then half is more than zero."

"That isn't the point."

"What is the point?"

"The point," Ilya said, "is that the last man who tried to keep a true copy of that ledger ended up feeding it to his own stove, and the man who taught me the trade died of drink with the company's seal on his pension papers, and the boy who just asked me to do it again is twenty years old and owns nothing but his lungs. The point is that paper burns, Vesper. Paper *always* burns. That's practically paper's whole profession."

Vesper set the stone down. Carefully, deliberately, the way a man lays down something he could otherwise throw.

"My father's name is going to burn," he said. "And my mother's. And Kofi's brother, and the Adeyemi girls' father, and every drowned man whose family took silver that never came, or signed marks they couldn't read, or got the word 'absconded' carved onto their grief by a drunk clerk with good penmanship." He stood, unhurried, and looked down at Ilya with the terrible patience of the young. "Paper burns. But I spent ten years learning what doesn't. Mama Sefu remembers every debt ever forgiven her, and she's been forgiving debts for fifty years. The children remember what their fathers earned per shell, because their mothers recited it like prayers. The town remembers. It's been remembering in silence this whole time, waiting for somebody to ask it out loud."

He extended his hand. Ilya stared at it.

"I know a cellar," Vesper said. "Under an orchard. With lamplight and fruit crates and a woman who frightens soldiers. Come write it down, Ilya. Before they burn it. Write it down ugly and incomplete and in your disgraceful handwriting — I don't care. Half a truth on paper beats a whole truth in ash. Somebody has to put it on record, and God help us, you're the only man in Iloran who knows the numbers."

"You understand," Ilya said, "that if they catch us, they will hang me. And probably you. And certainly the fig woman."

"Mama Sefu would like to hear you say that to her face."

Despite everything — despite the ribs and the herring and the eleven days of eels — Ilya felt the corner of his mouth move. "She sounds formidable."

"She keeps figs."

And that, Ilya Petrov reflected as he took the boy's hand and let himself be pulled upright on legs that had forgotten how to stand for anything, was how the second-worst decision of his life got made. The worst had been feeding the carbons to the stove. This one, at least, had lamplight in it.

"One condition," he said, brushing the fish-smell from his coat, straightening as much as a starved spine allowed.

"Name it."

"I get the window seat near the lamp. Old eyes. And if we're doing this — if we're truly doing this —" he drew a breath that hurt all the way down, "— we start tonight. Because I've been counting the wagons on the quay road, Vesper. Soldiers count everything, but they count fastest when they mean to burn it soon. And the wagons are multiplying."

* * *

They walked inland together through the waking streets, the drowned clerk and the diver's son, past stalls opening and bread ovens breathing their first heat, and if anyone noticed the tall thin foreigner moving with sudden purpose through Iloran, nobody remarked on it. The town had gotten very good, these eleven days, at not remarking on things.

But Ilya, marching toward a fig orchard and a crime, felt something loosen in his chest that he had assumed was scar tissue. It occurred to him, somewhere around the second street, that for the first time in fifteen years he was walking toward work that mattered, and that the numbers in his head — all those faithful, cursed, unforgotten figures — had simply been waiting, all this time, for someone to ask them to testify.

Behind them the harbor bells rang the hour. Ahead, above the compound wall, the fig trees stood dark and patient, like witnesses who had finally been summoned.
