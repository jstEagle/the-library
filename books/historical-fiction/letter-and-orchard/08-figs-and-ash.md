## 8. Figs and Ash

Mama Sefu knew something was wrong the moment Vesper came through the orchard gate without knocking. In ten years the boy had never once failed to knock—three soft raps on the gatepost, always three, because two meant trouble and four meant he'd brought a friend to help with the picking. Three was *I'm here, and everything is ordinary*. Today there were no raps at all, and he was not alone.

Behind him came a man she recognized from the counting house, or rather from the doorway of the counting house years ago: pale as unripe pear flesh, collarless now, his clerk's coat gone gray at the elbows. He looked like a man who had been wrung out and hung up to dry and had forgotten to come back inside.

"Boy," Mama Sefu said, setting down her basket of windfall figs, "you've brought me a ghost."

"Mama," Vesper said, which was not an answer, but then it rarely was.

The ghost cleared his throat. "Petrov," he said. "Ilya Petrov. Formerly second clerk of accounts, Meridian Trading Company." He said *formerly* the way other men said *widowed*.

"I know who you are," she said. "I've watched you cross my wall's shadow for ten years going home drunk enough to mistake the gutter for your bed. I know everyone in this town. That is what being old in Iloran means—you become furniture that remembers."

Vesper almost smiled. Almost. She saw him stop himself, and her heart, old and stubborn thing that it was, went cold. Vesper Okonkwo did not stop smiles halfway unless the smile would be a lie.

They sat under the big fig tree where the shade pooled deepest, and the boy told her. He told it plainly, in the flat voice he used for numbers, which she had learned long ago was how he said things that frightened him: the army, the requisition, the ledger sealed like a corpse awaiting burial. And then the rest—the copying nights not yet begun, Ilya's confession of the second column, the payments made to grieving wives to keep their mouths shut, and the plan forming between them like a bruise.

Mama Sefu listened without interruption, which cost her. She was a woman who had raised nine children of her own and buried five, and who had spent thirty years learning that the fastest way to spoil a young person's confession was to interrupt it with your own opinions. So she sat. She shelled beans. And when he finished, she looked at Ilya Petrov for a long moment.

"You burned them," she said. "Your copies."

"Yes." His voice cracked on the word like dry bread.

"And now you want my cellar."

Ilya flinched. Vesper opened his mouth, and she put up one hand, the way she'd done to a thousand children about to defend a thousand bad ideas.

"No. Let the man answer honestly. It's the first honest thing anyone from that counting house has ever said to me." She turned back to Ilya. "You kept copies once. You were afraid, and you burned them, and now you're afraid again and you want an old woman's floorboards between you and the fire. Say yes or no."

"Yes," Ilya said. Then, quieter, with something in it that surprised her—not self-pity, but wonder—"Yes. That's exactly what it is. I have no right to ask it."

"Hm." Mama Sefu set down her beans. She looked at the fig tree above them, heavy with the late crop, the fruit splitting sweet along one side where the birds had been at it. Her husband had planted that tree the year before their first child came. Everyone in Iloran thought of her orchard as a little kingdom of peace, and they were fools. An orchard was not peace. An orchard was a war you fought every year against rot and frost and greed, and won slowly, and never finally.

"My husband sold fruit to that company for twenty-two years," she said. "They paid him late and weighed short and called it generosity. My eldest son signed on as a diver because they promised the debt would clear in three seasons. Do you know what they wrote in their book when he drowned?"

Ilya went very still. Vesper closed his eyes.

"They wrote nothing at all," Mama Sefu continued. "Nothing. No entry, no name, no line. A man goes into the water and does not come out and the paper says he was never there. I asked. I walked into that counting house with my good shawl on and my grief folded up small so it wouldn't take up the whole room, and a clerk—younger than you, boy, younger than *he* is—told me the records concerned company property only." Her voice stayed level. It had taken her decades to learn level. "My son was not property. Therefore no record. Therefore no widow's account. Therefore silence."

The bees worked the fig blossoms overhead. Somewhere beyond the wall, a soldier's boot heel rang against the harbor stones.

"So yes," Mama Sefu said. "You'll have my cellar. And my crates. And whatever else this foolishness requires." She pointed a bean-shell at Ilya before relief could soften his face. "But understand me, clerk. I am not doing this because I forgive you. I am doing it because the dead cannot write their own names, and someone must, and God has evidently decided it will be a coward, a silent boy, and an old woman. If that is the army He has chosen, I will not argue with Him twice in one lifetime."

* * *

They met in the kitchen that evening, because the cellar was too obvious and the orchard too open, and because Mama Sefu maintained that no conspiracy worth its salt had ever survived without bread in reach. She baked while they talked, partly to feed them and partly because her hands thought better when they worked.

It was Ilya, of all people, who brought the news that changed the shape of everything.

"The archives," he said. He was sitting where the lamplight didn't quite reach, a habit she supposed ten years behind a desk teaches you. "I should have led with it. I'm sorry. I keep burying the worst part." He turned his cup in his hands. "There's a sanitation order. Cholera precautions, officially—the victors are nervous about disease in the port quarter. Unofficially, Marchand's staff wants the original documents destroyed once the official history is drafted. The counting house archive. Everything. The ledger, the correspondence, the dive rosters, the burial payments—all of it. Burned."

"When?" Vesper asked.

"A month. Perhaps less. The order hasn't been posted, but I heard the quartermaster's clerks arguing about wagons."

The ladle stopped moving in Mama Sefu's hand. She hadn't realized she was holding it until it went still.

"A month," she repeated.

"It could be sooner," Ilya said miserably. "That's the thing about orders like these. They arrive early. They never arrive late."

She looked at the boy then—at Vesper, twenty years old and carrying his father's drowning the way other men carried a stone in the shoe, quietly, everywhere—and saw him do arithmetic behind his eyes. She had watched him count breaths underwater since he was twelve; she knew the look. He was counting pages. Names. Nights.

"There are hundreds of names," he said. "Maybe more. We can't copy it all by memory in a month."

"No," Mama Sefu agreed. "We can't."

"But we don't need all of it," Ilya said slowly, and here the clerk woke up inside the broken man, the way she'd seen a cook wake inside a woman twenty years widowed the first time someone asked her to cater a wedding. "A ledger proves itself by its pattern. One name is an accident. Ten names is a scandal. Fifty names with dates and sums and matching signatures—that's a system. We need enough to show the shape of the thing. The shape is what convicts."

"The shape," Mama Sefu said, "and the names. Don't you dare let them talk you into trading names for shape, clerk. The historians can find the shape themselves once someone shows them where to dig. What they will never find on their own is that Amara Ndiaye died on the fourth of March and her children got eleven silver pieces for it. The names are the point. The names are the whole point."

Ilya bowed his head. "The names," he said. "Yes."

Vesper was looking at her strangely—not with surprise, exactly, but with that recalibration he did whenever she revealed a depth he hadn't charted. She'd seen it since he was small. People assumed a widow with an orchard was a simple country of one field.

"You knew about the payments," he said. Not accusing. Asking.

"My daughter-in-law took their silver after my son drowned," Mama Sefu said. "Eleven pieces. She bought a goat with it and hated the goat until the day she left Iloran." She resumed stirring, though the dough needed nothing more. "So yes, boy. I knew. I've known for fifteen years, and I said nothing, and I have regretted my silence the way you regret a tooth you didn't have pulled. You live around it. You learn to chew on the other side. But it never stops aching."

The kitchen was quiet except for the fire and the night insects and, faintly, the sea.

"Well then," she said briskly, because briskness was a tool same as any knife, "we have work. Clerk—can you reconstruct the figures? Truly? Not approximately, not 'more or less,' but well enough that if some historian sets your columns beside a surviving scrap, they'll match?"

Ilya sat up straighter than she had yet seen him. "Numbers don't leave me," he said. "People leave. Numbers stay. I can give you the payment schedules for six years from memory, and the rest I can rebuild from the patterns—company bookkeeping is lazy, repetitive, arrogant. Arrogance is legible."

"And you, boy. Your circle. The divers' children."

"Some of them already carry pieces," Vesper said. "Talia can recite the death column for three years. The Osei brothers know the debt entries because their mother read them aloud every night until she died—she wanted them angry, she said, anger keeps better than grief."

"Smart woman." Mama Sefu wiped her hands on her apron. "Then here is what I bring to this table, since we're apparently making an inventory of ourselves like the army does. I bring the cellar—dry, deep, entered through the preserves room, which smells so strongly of vinegar that no soldier alive will linger in it past his first breath. I bring crates. Forty at least, false bottoms if the carpenter on Rope Street still owes me for the quinces of '09, and I assure you he does; the man has owed me for eight years and will die owing me, which suits us both. I bring fruit, which moves through this town in every direction and is inspected by no one, because nobody in the history of warfare has ever searched a crate of overripe figs—they simply want it gone." She allowed herself one thin smile. "And I bring fury. Thirty years of it, banked and patient, hotter than anything your army burns with. Fury is the one thing they didn't requisition."

Vesper laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed, but real. She counted it a victory greater than anything the harbor garrison had managed.

* * *

Later, when the men had gone—Ilya limping off toward the fish market where he'd taken a cot above a smokehouse, Vesper walking the wall-shadow way home—Mama Sefu stood alone among her trees in the dark and let herself feel the size of what she had done.

She was seventy-one years old. She had buried a husband, five children, and one empire's worth of illusions. By every reasonable measure she had earned the right to spend her remaining years on figs, gossip, and grandchildren not her own, in roughly that order.

Instead she had just volunteered her home, her harvest, and her neck.

"You old fool," she told the trees affectionately. The oldest fig rustled in reply, or perhaps it was only the wind; with that tree it had always been hard to say.

But beneath the fear—which was real, she was honest enough with herself to know that, and growing heavier each day like a jar filling with rain—there was something else, something she hadn't felt since her son's funeral: purpose with weight to it. Direction. For fifteen years her anger had sat in her like a stone in a field, useful only for tripping over. Now it was being built into a wall.

She went inside, banked the fire, and began, methodically, to count her empty crates.

Two days later Vesper knocked on the gatepost—three raps, then, after a pause, a fourth—and when she opened it he was standing with six children arranged behind him like a jury, ranging from a girl of nine with her chin set exactly the way her drowned father's had, to a lanky boy already taller than Vesper whose hands shook and whose voice, Mama Sefu would soon learn, could recite forty debt entries without drawing breath.

"Mama," Vesper said. "We need the cellar tonight."

She looked at the children, and at the fear and the fury braided together in their faces, and stepped aside to let them in.

"I'll put water on," she said. "Whatever we're going to do, we'll do it properly fed."
