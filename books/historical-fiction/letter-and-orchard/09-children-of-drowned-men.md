## 9. Children of Drowned Men

The trick, Vesper decided, was figs.

He had learned this from Mama Sefu years ago, though she would deny teaching it to anyone. Grief kept children quiet the way debt kept divers under, and neither kind of silence broke on command. But a fig, split open in the palm, sweet enough to make the mouth forget it had been holding itself shut—that could do what no amount of coaxing could.

So on the first evening after they had agreed—Vesper, Ilya, and Mama Sefu, three conspirators arranged around a cellar full of empty fruit crates—he went looking for the children of drowned men, and he brought a basket of figs with him.

There were eleven of them in Iloran that he knew of. Children whose fathers had gone into the water for the Meridian Trading Company and not come out of it. Some were indentured themselves now, working the shallows their fathers had worked the deeps of. Some had been taken into other households. All of them had one thing in common, which was that nobody had ever asked them to remember anything out loud before.

* * *

The first was Beto.

Beto Ferreira was fourteen and built like a mooring post, with his father's wide shoulders already coming in and his mother's habit of staring at people as though daring them to be interesting. His father, João Ferreira, had drowned six years ago off the north reef, and Vesper had been there when it happened. He remembered the boat going quiet, the way it always did, and he remembered Beto's mother not crying at the counting house window because crying cost money you didn't have.

He found Beto mending nets behind the salt sheds and sat down next to him without saying anything, which was the correct way to approach Beto about anything.

They mended in silence for a while. Then Vesper held up the basket.

"Fig," he said.

Beto took one. Ate it whole. Held out his hand for another.

"Mama Sefu's trees," Vesper said.

"I know her trees." Beto spat a seed into the water. "I used to steal them. Before."

"Before your father?"

"Before everything." Beto said it flatly, the way boys say things they've decided not to feel anymore. "Why are you here, Okonkwo? You don't talk to anybody. Everybody says so. Ten years and you've said maybe forty words, and half of them were 'rope.'"

"That's true," Vesper admitted. "Mostly rope."

"So?"

So. Vesper turned a fig over in his fingers. There was no good way to say it. There was no gentle way to ask a boy to carry his dead father around inside his head forever and then hand him over, name and date and debt, to a stranger's keeping.

"The soldiers sealed the ledger," Vesper said finally. "The master ledger. Every diver. Every debt. Every—" He stopped. "Every death. They're going to burn it. And then they're going to print a book that says none of it happened the way it happened."

Beto went still. Even his hands, which never stopped moving, went still.

"They're going to say my father ran," he said quietly.

"They marked nine men 'absconded' last year alone. Your father wasn't one of them. His entry is different. But it doesn't matter which entry it is if the paper burns."

"And you want me to—" Beto looked at the sea, then at Vesper, and something in his face shifted from suspicion to a terrible understanding. "You want to know what I remember."

"Do you remember?"

"My mother made me learn the numbers." Beto's voice had gone strange, recitation-voice, the voice children use for prayers and punishments. "After he died. She said if the company ever cheated us—and they cheated everybody—if they ever came to cheat us specifically, I'd need to know what we were owed. Forty-one piastres against. Interest at two percent monthly, compounded. Entry dated the ninth of March, six years past. Signed Petrov, I., clerk second class." He blinked, startled at himself. "I didn't know I still had all that. It's been sitting in my head like a stone and I thought it was just a stone."

"It's not a stone," Vesper said. "It's a page."

Beto looked at him for a long moment. Then he reached into the basket, took a third fig, and put it in his shirt pocket, which Vesper understood to mean yes.

"Who else?" Beto asked.

"That's what I'm doing tomorrow."

"Take me. I know where most of them live. You'd walk past them otherwise—they're small, and they hide."

* * *

Interlude — the record of Adaeze Nwosu, aged eleven, taken by dictation in the orchard, first evening:

My papa's name was Emeka Nwosu. He went down on the fourteenth of August, five years past, at the deep beds. The ledger says lost at sea but that is wrong because I heard the clerk tell the harbor master he hit his head on the coral shelf and they knew it before the boat even landed, and they docked his final wages anyway for the air he used. Two hours of air, thirty-seven coppers. My mama argued and the clerk wrote something else down while she was arguing. I watched him write it. I remember the shape of the letters even though I couldn't read them then. I can read now. I taught myself off shipping crates. If you give me paper I will copy every word I know and I will make the letters exactly like his letters, because his letters are the ones that lied, and I want mine to sit next to his and argue.

* * *

Adaeze Nwosu came to the orchard the second evening with her younger brother Odion, who was seven and mostly silent, and who carried in his head, it turned out, an entire season of tide-tables and delivery dates that he recited the way other children hummed. The third evening brought Sela and Miriam Okoye, twin girls whose father's debt had been transferred to their own indentures upon his death—a detail both twins recited simultaneously, in unison, having apparently agreed beforehand, which gave it the terrible cadence of a liturgy. The fourth evening brought Tomas, who stammered, and whose fragments came slow and hard-won, and who wept once, briefly, and was passed a fig by four children at the same time until he laughed.

By the sixth evening there were nine of them sitting on upturned fruit crates in Mama Sefu's cellar, lamplight swinging gently with the draft from the stair, and Mama Sefu standing at the bottom step with her arms folded, surveying them like a general reviewing troops she hadn't asked for and intended to keep.

"Nine children," she said to Vesper, quietly, in the doorway. "In my cellar. Reciting debts."

"You offered the cellar."

"I offered it to grown fools with a plan. This looks like a school for ghosts." But her mouth was doing the thing it did when she was pleased and fighting it, and she set down a tray of bread and salt fish among them with the tenderness of a woman laying out offerings.

Ilya Petrov sat at the far end with a salvaged scrap of paper and a stub of pencil, and he was the strangest sight of all—the disgraced clerk, drunk no longer, hair combed for the first time since his dismissal, listening to children recite figures and writing them down with the feverish joy of a man returned to the only country he'd ever belonged to, which was arithmetic.

"Again," he said to Adaeze. "The thirty-seventh coppers. Was it deducted from the estate or added to it?"

"Added. They fined him for dying, essentially. Air he breathed was company air."

Ilya's pencil stopped. "Yes," he said softly. "That was policy. I wrote the policy memorandum. 1887, spring, circular eleven." He looked up at Vesper with an expression caught between shame and something worse, which was usefulness. "I remember all of it. The circulars. Every rule that ground them down, I helped draft or transcribe, and it's all still in here." He tapped his temple. "A decade of filing, and the filing won't leave. Do you know what that's like? To wish your own memory would rot and it won't?"

"Yes," said nine voices, more or less together, and Ilya laughed—a cracked, astonished laugh—and bent back over his paper.

* * *

They developed a method, as all conspiracies must or die.

Beto was the anchor. He had the loudest memory and the least fear, and when a child stalled—when the numbers went foggy, when grief closed over the entry like water over a sinking man—Beto would lean forward and say, steadily, "Start with the name. Just the name. We'll find the rest together," and somehow the rest always came.

Adaeze was the pen. She copied everything twice, once in the cellar and once later, hidden, in a second hand she'd invented herself, so that no single handwriting could be traced to any single child. She was eleven. Vesper tried to imagine explaining this to the army and failed completely.

Sela and Miriam cross-checked each other, twin against twin, and where they disagreed—and they disagreed often, furiously, over copper counts and interest months—Ilya would settle it from the clerk's side, reconstructing the calculation from habit until the twins grudgingly concurred.

And Vesper did what Vesper had always done best, which was nothing, visibly. He listened. He kept watch at the top of the stairs. He counted breaths, as he had counted them underwater for ten years, except now he was counting them for other people—for nine children breathing in a cellar, holding ten years of drowned men inside their chests, letting it out one number at a time.

On the seventh night, Tomas raised his hand. Actually raised it, as though in school, which made Miriam snort, which made everyone laugh, which made it easier for him to speak.

"My father's entry," Tomas said. "I've never said it out loud. Not ever. My mother pretends the ledger doesn't exist. She says remembering keeps you poor." He swallowed. "Is it true? That saying it helps?"

The cellar went quiet. Even the lamp seemed to hold its flame still.

Mama Sefu, from the stairs, answered before anyone else could.

"Tomas Almeida," she said. "Your father Rui, entered the water the twenty-second of June, four years past, and did not come out, and the company recorded it as *lost*, and lost means they owed nothing, and owing nothing meant your mother signed herself deeper into the very debt that killed him." Her voice was not cruel. It was exact. "That's what the paper says. I read it over a clerk's shoulder once, God forgive me, and I have carried it four years waiting for someone to ask me to set it down. So yes, boy. Saying it helps. Carrying it alone is what kills."

Tomas stared at her. Then he recited his father's entry, complete, unbroken, no stammer anywhere in it, and when he finished, nine children said his father's name back to him—Rui Almeida—in the manner of an amen, because Adaeze had proposed it earlier that evening and the others had adopted it without discussion, and so it went now, name after name, the circle giving each man back to his child for the length of a spoken moment.

Rui Almeida.
Emeka Nwosu.
João Ferreira.

Vesper stood at the top of the stairs and did not go down, because his own turn was coming, three nights hence, and he needed to be able to breathe.

Interlude — Sergeant Amara Diallo's field journal, same week, undated entry, written quickly:

Third day of inventory. The harbor manifests balance; the warehouse seals hold; the requisition proceeds. What does not proceed is sleep. Today a boy watched our wagons pass with his chin up and his fists closed, and I thought: that is not the face of a conquered town. That is the face of a town doing sums. I have begun making notes of my own. I am not certain yet what they are for. Filed nothing. Burned nothing either. Note that.

* * *

The near-disaster came on the eighth night, and afterward Vesper would think: of course it came. Nine children cannot keep a secret the way one silent man can, because children are not built for silence; they are built for exactly the opposite of silence, magnificently and constantly.

Odion talked in his sleep. Tide tables, mostly—dates and depths murmured into the dark of Mama Sefu's spare room where the youngest ones napped before the evening sessions. But on the eighth afternoon his aunt, visiting the market, heard him muttering over the fish stall—"*entry dated the ninth of March, six years past, forty-one piastres against*"—and laughed and told the fishmonger, and the fishmonger told a garrison runner buying sardines, and by evening a corporal had been dispatched to ask casual questions about whether the Nwosu household kept accounts.

Mama Sefu met the corporal at her gate with a basket of overripe figs and the performance of her life.

"Accounts?" she said, delighted, deaf, and slightly mad, in that order. "Child, the only accounts I keep are which tree owes me rain and which neighbor owes me a ladder. These children help me sort fruit for preserving. Sugar weights! That's all the numbers they're mumbling—sugar weights and jar counts, the way children do when an old woman sets them to work. Would you like figs? Take the figs. Take two baskets. Tell your sergeant the widow Ishikawa sends her compliments and her plums are coming in soon if the army has any use for plums, which I doubt, since plums are useless for war."

The corporal, twenty years old and bewildered, took two baskets of figs and left.

Inside the cellar, nine children had sat in absolute darkness for eleven minutes. When Mama Sefu came down the stairs she found them silent, composed, and terrified, and she lit the lamp and said, "Well. Now you know how it feels. Now we work faster."

* * *

But it was the ninth night that mattered, and Vesper knew it from the moment he came down the stairs, because the crate in the center had been cleared, and a lamp had been set beside it, and nine faces turned toward him with the patience of people who had agreed among themselves—again without discussion—to wait for him.

"We got to yours last," Beto said. Not unkindly. "We saved room."

Vesper sat on the crate. The lamplight swung. Somewhere above them the orchard moved in the night wind, the leaves doing their dry whispering, and below, in the dark of the earth, the roots went down and held.

He had rehearsed it, of course. In the water, over the years, he had recited his father's entry a thousand times to himself, the only prayer he owned. But rehearsal is not the same as speaking, and speaking is not the same as being heard.

"Okonkwo, Chidi," he began. His voice cracked on the second word and he let it. "Diver, first class. Entered the water on the fourth of November, ten years past. Did not surface. The ledger records the cause as *lost at sea*. The ledger is lying. The line was cut. Everyone on the boat knew the line was cut, and the boat was captained by a man named Duval, and Duval received a bonus that season for reducing equipment losses." He drew a breath, the long disciplined breath of a man who has done his deepest work underwater. "His debt, at time of death, stood at sixty-two piastres. The estate was seized the following week. Our house. The house I was born in. Sold for nineteen piastres to pay a debt of sixty-two, and the difference—" his voice did crack again here, and he let it again, "—the difference was not returned. It was absorbed. Circular nine, 1885. Absorption of surplus estate value. Ilya wrote the circular. Ilya is sitting right there, and he is sorry, and sorry is worth something now, because we are writing it all down."

Silence. Then, nine voices, soft and level, the amen of the found family, the living archive giving him back his father for the length of a spoken moment:

"Chidi Okonkwo."

Adaeze dipped her pen and wrote the name at the top of a fresh page, and beneath it the entry, and beneath the entry the truth, and the lamp flame steadied, and outside, far across the sleeping town, a bell in the garrison tower rang the midnight hour—one, two, three strokes—and Vesper felt the cold weight of the calendar pressing down on all of them, because the burning order could come any day now, and they were nine children with a pencil, racing a fire.

Beto leaned close. "How many names do we have?"

"Thirty-one recovered. Full entries. Fragments of perhaps twenty more."

"How many are in the ledger?"

Vesper looked at the lamplight, at the small determined faces, at Mama Sefu's shadow motionless on the stair.

"Two hundred and six," he said. "We have until they strike the match to find the rest."
