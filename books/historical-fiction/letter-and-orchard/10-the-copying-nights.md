## 10. The Copying Nights

The cellar smelled of earth and old sugar, and by the third night Vesper had learned to love it the way a man loves a hiding place that has not yet betrayed him.

Mama Sefu's house sat close against her orchard wall, and beneath its kitchen floor lay a room dug into the cool clay—a root cellar, she called it, though in truth it was a museum of every harvest she had ever refused to waste. Jars of preserved lemons glowed amber on the shelves like small lanterns. Sacks of onions breathed their quiet onion breath into the dark. And in the center, on a trestle table that had once held funeral bread and now held paper, lamplight pooled around the beginnings of a book.

"You'll ruin your eyes," Mama Sefu said, setting down a second lamp without being asked. She said this every night. It was, Vesper had come to understand, less an objection than a blessing—the closest thing to approval her pride allowed her to speak aloud.

"My eyes are company property," Vesper said. "They've been diving for them ten years. They can squint a while longer."

That earned him one of her laughs, the short dry ones that sounded like a branch snapping in winter. Then she pulled her shawl tighter and went up the stairs to keep watch at the kitchen window, because that was the arrangement: the old woman above with her knitting and her sharp ears, the children below with their ghosts.

* * *

There were six of them now, counting Ilya.

Vesper had chosen them carefully, or rather they had chosen themselves, arriving at his elbow in the market and at the well with the particular gravity of people carrying something heavy they could not set down. There was Nour, seventeen, whose father's entry read *drowned, debt transferred to widow*, and who could recite the transfer dates the way other sailors recited tides. There were the twins, Basi and Feran, fifteen and identical and only distinguishable because Feran stammered when frightened and Basi never did anything of the sort. There was little Adaeze, twelve, who had been seven when her brother went under off the north shoals and who remembered, with terrible precision, the exact sum written beside his name, because she had seen the clerk write it through a window and had spent five years making sure her memory kept it safe when no one else would.

And there was Ilya Petrov, forty-some years old, disgraced, half-starved, and possessed of a mind so thoroughly shaped by columns and sums that he dreamed in ledger lines. He sat at the table now with his sleeves rolled up, a stub of pencil moving across scrap paper, reconstructing figures from pure habit the way a man without hands might still feel an itch in his missing fingers.

"Third quarter, eleven years back," he murmured. "Diving season closed early—storms. The company advanced winter rations against next season's catch, which means every diver's debt increased by—" He stopped, counted on his fingers, frowned. "By nine crowns four bits. Except the Okonkwo accounts. Those were adjusted differently."

Vesper kept writing. "Say it."

"Adjusted *down*. Rations credited, not debited." Ilya looked up, and in the lamplight his face was pale and miserable. "Your family's account was credited, Vesper. After your father drowned. Someone in the front office wanted the numbers tidy before inspection. It's the only entry in eleven years where mercy appears in the column, and it isn't mercy at all—it's arithmetic. A dead man's debt made smaller so the year's totals would balance clean."

Vesper wrote it down. His hand did not shake, because he would not permit it to shake, and this was the discipline he had learned in ten years of silence: you could feel everything, so long as your hands believed otherwise.

"It balances," he said quietly. "Everything with them balances. That's what we're here to unbalance."

Nour looked up from her page. "Is that why we're doing it? To unbalance them?"

Vesper considered how to answer, because the children deserved better than slogans. "We're doing it," he said finally, "because my father's name is written in that ledger as a line item. Not a man. A line. And when they burn the original, the line is all that's left of him unless someone copies it out first." He tapped the table. "Every name we write tonight is a name they can't erase tomorrow."

Adaeze, who had been silent for an hour, spoke up. "My brother's line says 'lost.'" Her voice was very steady. "He wasn't lost. Lost is when you misplace a thing. He was *under the water*, and the boat didn't wait, and then they wrote 'lost' because it was shorter than the truth."

No one said anything for a moment. In the cellar, silence had a different quality than it did anywhere else in Iloran—down here it was not survival, it was agreement.

"We'll write it longer," Vesper said. "That's the whole work. Writing it longer."

* * *

The copying had a rhythm, and after a week the rhythm had become liturgy.

Ilya reconstructed figures. The children recited what memory held—names, dates, debts, the strange cold vocabulary of disposals—and Vesper transcribed, because his hand was steadiest and because, as he told them, ten years of keeping his mouth shut had taught him exactly how much a sentence could hold if you packed it tight.

Basi had the deaths of the south fleet. Feran had the debts of the same families, and the two lists interlocked like fingers clasped. When Feran faltered—which was often, when a name came too near his own household—Basi would simply continue the recitation in his twin's place, seamless as water filling a footprint, and no one remarked on it because remarking would have broken something delicate.

Tonight Feran faltered over a date and Basi carried it, and Vesper wrote, and Ilya checked the sum against a figure he'd rebuilt from habit, and for a moment the whole table worked like one instrument.

"There," Ilya said softly. "That's the fourth quarter of the south fleet balanced against the death rolls. Do you know what this is?" He touched the pages almost reverently. "This is a ledger that tells the truth. In twenty-two years at the Company I never saw one. I built the other kind. Column by column. Forgive me, but—I built the other kind."

"You're unbuilt now," Mama Sefu said from the stairs. She had come down unnoticed, a plate of preserved figs in her hands, and she set it among the papers with the fearless disregard for documents that only a woman who had buried three husbands and outlived two governments could manage. "Eat. Truth-keeping is hungry work. My mother always said a lie sits easier on a full stomach, and I imagine the reverse holds."

"Mama Sefu," Vesper began, "the figs are near the ink—"

"The figs are near nothing. Ink dries; figs spoil." She pushed the plate toward Adaeze. "Child, eat. You're all skin and remembering."

It became a ritual, the figs. Every night the plate appeared, and every night the children ate, and somehow the sweetness made the names easier to say. Vesper noticed that they had begun to arrange the evenings around small mercies: Nour brought a stub of candle wax for sealing finished pages into oilcloth. Basi carved a better pen from a goose quill he'd found behind the fish market, presenting it with the solemnity of a sword. Even Ilya began to stand straighter, as if the work were slowly returning vertebrae to a bent spine.

Once, near midnight, Adaeze fell asleep with her cheek on her own transcription, and Vesper lifted her gently and carried her to the cot in the corner that Mama Sefu had rigged from grain sacks. She murmured in her sleep—not words, but numbers. Reciting still. Even dreaming, she was keeping the archive alive.

Vesper stood over her a long moment. *This is what prayer looks like now,* he thought. Not kneeling. Copying.

* * *

The knock came on the ninth night.

Three blows, measured and official, at the kitchen door above—and every soul in the cellar went still as poured wax. Vesper blew out both lamps between one heartbeat and the next, his hands moving on instinct, and the dark came down over the table, the pages, the six of them, complete.

Above, floorboards creaked. Mama Sefu's voice, unhurried as Sunday: "Coming, coming. An old woman moves at an old woman's speed, soldier."

Boots on the step. Vesper pressed the finished pages flat against his chest inside his shirt, feeling his own heart hammer against the paper as though trying to read it. Beside him in the dark, he heard Feran's breathing go quick and shallow, heard Basi's hand find his brother's shoulder and press it still. Ilya had gone utterly rigid, and Vesper reached out and gripped the clerk's forearm—*you are a guest eating preserves, nothing more*—and felt the tremor there gradually still.

Through the floorboards, muffled but audible:

"Patrol. Curfew check. Why is your lamp burning past hour?"

"Lamp? Child, that's not a lamp, that's my preserving stove. Sugar boils late or it crystallizes. You want crystallized figs?" A pause, withering in its length. "Of course you don't. No one wants crystallized figs. That's the entire tragedy of crystallized figs."

A second voice, younger, uncertain: "Ma'am, regulations say—"

"Regulations. Yes. I raised four sons on regulations, may they rest, and every one of them learned that a regulation is a thing men write when they've never had to feed anyone." The creak of a chair; she had, apparently, seated herself. "Now. Are you going to search my kitchen, or are you going to be embarrassed about the state of it? Because I warn you, boy, I've been canning since before your mother was courted, and there is syrup on that floor older than the Meridian Company, and it does not apologize."

A long silence, in which Vesper could picture the soldiers' faces with painful clarity—the sergeant weighing his dignity against the prospect of wading into an old woman's sticky kingdom, and dignity losing, as it usually did.

"...Curfew's at the bell, ma'am. Douse the... stove."

"I'll douse it when the sugar sets. Unless you'd like to stir it. Hands?"

"No, ma'am."

"No, I thought not. Goodnight, soldiers. Mind the step; it bites."

The door closed. Boots retreated down the lane. And upstairs, after an interval sufficient to establish plausibility, Mama Sefu's voice floated down through the floor, dry as autumn leaves:

"The sugar is set. You can light your lamps, you ridiculous conspirators."

* * *

When they came up into the kitchen, she was stirring a pot that contained, as far as Vesper could tell, nothing but conviction. The whole house smelled of boiling fruit and cinnamon—an alibi built of aromatics.

"How did you know what to say?" Adaeze asked, wide-eyed.

"I didn't. I said whatever came, loudly, until they wished to be elsewhere. Loudness is a door, child. Most soldiers will take the wall around it." She ladled out actual figs—she'd thrown real ones in during the pause, Vesper realized, committing fully to the fiction—and handed bowls around. "Now sit. Eat. And then back to your prayers."

"Prayers?" Ilya said faintly.

"What would you call it? Six people in a cellar, whispering the dead back into the world by lamplight." She sniffed. "In my village we called that prayer. We just used different paper."

They ate. The twins argued softly about whether a patrol sergeant could be bribed with preserves (Basi: yes; Feran: only good ones). Nour copied out a correction to a date. And Vesper sat with his bowl cooling in his hands, looking at the faces gathered around his landlady's table—the clerk, the orphans, the widow—and felt something in his chest that had no room to exist during the years of silence.

Fear, yes. Fear had moved in and would not be evicted. But underneath the fear, warm and unreasonable: *these people.* This improbable congregation of the overlooked, armed with quills and figs and stubbornness, writing a book the army intended never to exist.

"Mama Sefu," he said. "Thank you. For the lie."

She looked at him over her spectacles for a long moment. "First lie you ever told for someone else, you were ten years old," she said. "Stolen figs. Told me it was crows." She returned to her pot. "You've improved. Crows don't boil sugar."

* * *

Past midnight, when the children had gone up to their pallets and Ilya had gone home through the orchard gate with pages sewn into his coat lining, Vesper stayed below to bank the lamps and square the finished sheets.

He counted them the way he once counted breaths underwater. Forty-one pages. Names, dates, debts, deaths, and now Ilya's reconstruction of the payment column—the quiet money that had bought families' silence, entered in a fair hand that Vesper was careful to make nothing like his own. Forty-one pages of the longer truth.

At the bottom of the stack, newest, lay tonight's final entry, the one that had cost Feran the most:

*Divers lost, south fleet, spring — including Feran & Basi's father, entry annotated 'debt settled by estate seizure.'*

Estate seizure. The house taken the same week the man drowned. Vesper had watched Basi copy that annotation without expression and understood that some silences were not emptiness but pressure, like deep water, and that one day soon the twins' turn would come to break—that the circle was descending, name by name, toward its deepest entries, toward griefs not yet spoken aloud.

Including, Vesper knew with a certainty that sat in him like swallowed stone, his own father's line. They were working toward it alphabetically, seasonally, inevitably. Two nights away, perhaps three.

He banked the lamp. Climbed the stairs. Stood a moment in the dark kitchen, listening to the orchard breathe outside the window.

Tomorrow, he thought. Or the night after. His father's true entry, recited in a child's clear voice while Vesper wrote it down in a fair hand, and the whole cellar watching him to see whether the man who taught them silence could survive hearing the truth spoken.

He found he did not know the answer.

He turned up the lamp again and, unable to help himself, took out tomorrow's blank page and looked at it a long time—the empty rectangle waiting to receive whatever the record actually said about Amadi Okonkwo—and then he blew the flame out and stood in the dark, holding the shape of his father's name in his mouth like a stone he was not yet ready to set down or swallow.
