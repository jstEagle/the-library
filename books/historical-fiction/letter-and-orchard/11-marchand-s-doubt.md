## 11. Marchand's Doubt

Edouard Marchand had been in Iloran eleven days, and he had learned three things about the town: that the fish were excellent, the wine was terrible, and everyone lied to him with the same pleasant, practiced smile.

He sat now in the requisitioned counting house, in what had once been the company factor's office and was now his, at a desk that had once belonged to the factor and still smelled faintly of his pipe tobacco—something dark and sweet, like burnt honey. The official history of the Meridian Trading Company's annexation lay before him in the form of forty pages of careful draft, and Marchand hated every one of them.

Not because they were false. That was the trouble. They were *smooth*. Every source agreed with every other source so neatly that reading them back felt like listening to a choir where all the singers had been given the same sheet of music—and told, on pain of dismissal, to stay exactly on pitch.

The company men had been generous with their time. The harbor master had walked him along the quay for two hours, pointing out improvements. The warehouse superintendent had produced charts showing rising tonnage, rising profits, rising—there was no other word for it—gratitude among the diving crews. Even the indenture contracts themselves, which Marchand had requested and received in tidy bundles, read less like bondage than like an educational opportunity with unfortunate paperwork.

"Ten years' service," Marchand murmured to the empty office, flipping through one. "In exchange for passage, lodging, and a debt that somehow never diminishes."

The arithmetic bothered him. It bothered him the way a stone bothers a boot—not enough to stop walking, but enough to change how you stood.

He had raised it once, delicately, with Mr. Calloway, the company's senior man in Iloran, a broad pink fellow with the unshakeable cheer of a man who has never once doubted himself in public.

"Debt schedules," Calloway had said, beaming over tea. "Naturally there are deductions. Board, equipment, training. A diver's kit is expensive, Mr. Marchand. The pearl shells alone—"

"And the families?"

"Provided for, within the compound. We are not monsters."

*We*, Marchand had noted. Not *I*. An interesting pronoun for a man who seemed to make every decision between breakfast and dinner.

Marchand dipped his pen and wrote at the top of his draft, in small letters he would later erase: *Ask who pays for the funerals.*

There came a knock—two sharp raps, military, unhurried.

"Enter."

Sergeant Amara Diallo filled the doorway the way weather fills a horizon. She was tall, dark-skinned, with the kind of stillness that made other people fidget, and she had been assigned to him as liaison and protection detail—a phrase that Marchand suspected meant *handler*, though neither of them had said so out loud. In eleven days she had spoken perhaps sixty words to him, most of them directional: *the harbor is this way; the factor will see you at four; do not go past the second warehouse after dark.*

"Sergeant," he said. "Sit, please. If you have the time."

"I have the time." She took the chair opposite the desk without ceremony, her hands resting on her knees. Her eyes went briefly to the papers, then away, with what might have been indifference or might have been something else entirely.

"I wanted your impressions," Marchand said. "Of the occupation. You've been here since the first day, I understand. Before me, certainly."

"I have."

"The official reports describe the transfer of authority as orderly. Welcomed, even."

Diallo looked at him for a long moment. Marchand had learned to wait. Waiting was most of his profession, though nobody ever credited it. Everyone thought history was made of battles and signatures; really it was made of silences, if you had the patience to sit inside them until they broke.

"They gave us bread on the first day," Diallo said finally.

"Bread."

"At the harbor. Some of the women from the market. Not a celebration—the colonel thought it was, and wrote it up as one—but it wasn't. It was the way you feed something that might bite. You give it bread so it doesn't eat you instead."

Marchand set down his pen. "That's not in any report I've read."

"No, sir."

"Would you say the town welcomed us?"

Another silence. This one lasted long enough that Marchand counted the sounds of the building around them: gulls outside, the creak of the warehouse beams, somewhere below a clerk's pen scratching at inventory lists that would take weeks to finish.

"I would say," Diallo said carefully, "that the town is waiting to learn what we intend to remember."

Marchand blinked. "That's a historian's answer, Sergeant."

"My mother taught school. Before." Something crossed her face and was gone. "She believed everything was a lesson, whether you admitted it or not."

"And what is this one teaching?"

"That depends on the textbook." Diallo rose, and Marchand thought the interview was over—she moved toward the door with that economy of hers, no wasted motion—but at the threshold she stopped. "You asked for casualty figures. From the annexation. I brought them."

"You could have left them with the adjutant."

"Yes, sir." She did not move.

Marchand waited again. He was getting better at it.

"There are two sets of numbers," she said. "The ones in the file, and the ones that happened. The file says four dead during the taking of the town. Four. One soldier, three resisters."

"Is that inaccurate?"

"The file is accurate to the file." She turned then, and her eyes held his with a flatness that was not hostility—it was exhaustion, the deep kind, the kind that comes from carrying something heavy a long way and being forbidden to set it down. "I was on the quay when the gunboats fired. I helped carry bodies off the breakwater afterward. I stopped counting at nine, and I had only counted ours."

"Ours."

"Soldiers, civilians. Ilorani. Men who threw stones at armed infantry, which is not resistance, Sergeant Marchand, it is arithmetic. Nine plus the ones I didn't see. The company says their divers lost no one that day because officially the divers weren't involved. But two of the dead were divers, pulled off their boats by men who wanted to know what the company paid for pearls. They answered honestly, which was 'nothing.' So they died knowing the truth of their own employment, at least."

The room had gone very quiet. Somewhere below, the clerk's pen kept scratching, indifferent as rain.

"Why are you telling me this?" Marchand asked quietly.

Diallo considered the question as though weighing it on a scale. "Because you asked for impressions, and I'm giving you mine. Because my orders are to assist you, and I've decided that assisting you means assisting you." A pause. "And because my mother would have asked."

Then she left, boots measured on the stair, and Marchand sat alone with two numbers that refused to reconcile: four, and nine-and-more.

* * *

That evening he dined badly at the officers' mess—mutton again, boiled into surrender—and listened to the colonel hold forth on the pacific character of the local population. "Remarkable people. No fight in them at all. The company's had ten years of perfect labor harmony."

"Perfect," Marchand repeated, into his wine, which was also terrible. Everything in Iloran was excellent except the things that mattered.

"Your history is coming along?" the colonel asked. "Calloway says the drafts are very fine."

"Calloway has seen the drafts."

"Well, naturally. Company cooperation, and all that. Sensible arrangement. They know their own story best, eh? Like asking a man to describe his own house."

"Or his own ledger," Marchand said, and the colonel laughed and moved on to discuss horses.

Marchand walked back to the counting house alone through streets that emptied politely as he passed. He found himself thinking of his father, a minor customs official in a minor port, who had spent thirty years recording cargo and had died believing that paper was honest because paper had no reason to lie. *It's the mouths you have to watch, Edouard,* he'd say. *Paper only knows what someone taught it.* And then, inevitably: *which is why you must always ask who did the teaching.*

At his desk, by lamplight, Marchand opened his draft and read the sentence he had written that morning:

*The annexation of Iloran was accomplished with minimal loss of life, the population having accepted the new charter with notable calm.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he took out a fresh sheet, and beneath his official heading, in his smallest, least legible hand—the handwriting of a man writing something he does not intend anyone else to read—he began a second document.

*Private memorandum. For no file.*

*I am beginning to suspect that everything I have been sent here to write is a eulogy for a murder, delivered as a wedding toast.*

He wrote until the candle guttered: the two casualty counts, side by side. The contracts whose debts never diminished. The word *disposals* he had glimpsed in a company index and been assured meant "equipment written off"—though why equipment required a column for next-of-kin, no one could tell him. The bread on the quay, given the way one feeds a thing that might bite.

When he finished, he did something he had never done in fifteen years of professional service. He folded the memorandum small and sewed it into the lining of his coat, behind the pocket, where a man keeps things he means to keep.

Then he blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the counting house settle around him—beams ticking, sea air through shutters, and below, faintly, the sound of the inventory clerks working late, counting everything in Iloran except the things that counted.

He thought: *I can write the smooth version. I can write it well; I have always written well; that is the whole of my talent and half my curse. Calloway will praise it. The Ministry will print it. In twenty years it will be the truth, because printed things become true if no one contradicts them.*

He thought: *Or.*

He did not yet know what *or* contained. Only that Sergeant Diallo had stood in his doorway like weather, and that somewhere out in the dark town, under the same stars, there were people who knew the difference between four and nine, and were keeping count.

Three days later, a note appeared under his door in a hand he did not recognize, unsigned, smelling faintly of figs:

*If you want the real numbers, ask the orchard woman behind the compound wall. Come alone. Come honest.*

Marchand read it twice, folded it into the lining beside his memorandum, and stood for a long while at the window, watching the harbor glitter with its innocent morning light.

Then he reached for his coat.
