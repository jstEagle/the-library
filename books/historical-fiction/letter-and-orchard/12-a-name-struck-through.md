## 12. A Name Struck Through

The cellar smelled of lamp oil, brine, and Mama Sefu's quinces ripening in their crates above our heads—a sweetness that drifted down through the floorboards like an apology the world hadn't asked her to make.

Nine of us sat around the trestle table that night. The children had claimed it as theirs: Tano with his quick fingers and quicker mouth, little Besa who could recite a column of figures the way other children recited prayers, the Odell twins who finished each other's dates and debts without looking at one another. Ilya sat apart, as always, his reconstruction sheets spread before him, his pen moving with the mechanical grace of a man doing arithmetic in his sleep. Mama Sefu kept to her stool by the stairs, mending a net she had no use for except that her hands needed something gentle to do while her ears did the hard work.

We were deep into the autumn entries of my ninth year in the company's service. That was how we'd learned to navigate the ledger—not by year of the world but by year of me, because grief indexes itself to whatever it was standing beside when it happened.

"Read it again," Besa said. "The one from the third week of the storm season."

Tano bent over the copied page. His voice had settled into a strange solemnity over these weeks of copying, the flippancy burned out of him like impurities out of metal. "'Diver entry, Okonkwo, Emeka,'" he read. "'Contracted debt, forty-one marks, twelve pence. Diving seasons completed—'"

I already knew what came next. I had known since the moment we began this page, some animal part of me counting down the lines the way I once counted breaths on the bottom. But knowing a thing is coming does nothing to blunt it. The sea taught me that. You can see the swell building for a full minute before it breaks over you, and still it takes you off your feet.

"'—seasons completed, four. Status.'" Tano stopped.

The lamplight made a small warm circle on the paper, and outside it the cellar went on being a cellar—crates, cobwebs, the drip of the cistern—but inside that circle the word waited.

"Status," Ilya said quietly, from his corner. "It will say 'settled.' They always wrote 'settled.'"

Tano looked up at me. He was fourteen and he had the decency to look like he wished the floor would open and take him somewhere else. "Do you want—"

"Read it."

He swallowed. "'Status: debt settled by estate seizure. Dwelling and effects appraised at thirty-eight marks, four pence. Balance written against company account. Widow's tenancy terminated, seventh day of storm season.'" He paused. "There's a note in the margin. Small hand. It says—" He squinted. "It says 'no further inquiry permitted.'"

*No further inquiry permitted.*

My father had not been *lost at sea*. That was the story Iloran told itself, the story the company let stand because drowned men make better saints than evicted ones. *Lost at sea*, with all its salt and sorrow, its suggestion of the sea's great indifferent appetite, no one's fault, everyone's grief. We had even been given a marker for the memorial wall with those three words cut into it. My mother used to touch it every morning on her way to market, two fingers, brief, the way you'd touch a doorpost.

But the ledger said otherwise. The ledger said that in the same week the sea took my father—or the week the company said the sea took him, and there was a difference now, wasn't there, a difference I would carry for the rest of my life—the company had walked into our house with an appraisal book and priced our roof, our table, my mother's cooking pots, and called the whole sum a settlement. Thirty-eight marks and four pence. Four pence. Someone had stood in our kitchen and counted the value of the spoons.

They had taken the house while my mother was still sitting shiva by the water, still leaving bread out for gulls because grief makes people do foolish tender things. And when she died three years later—of the coughing, of the cold, of the slow arithmetic of having nowhere—it was because the company had already spent her roof.

And they had written it down. That was the part that undid me, though I didn't understand it until much later that night. Not the theft itself. Theft I understood; I had served ten years among thieves who wore good coats. It was that a clerk—Ilya's predecessor or perhaps Ilya himself, God forgive them both—had dipped his pen and recorded the crime in a fair hand, and then another hand had added *no further inquiry permitted*, and both men had slept afterward, eaten afterward, gone home to their own roofs.

Paper remembers. That was what I'd told the children when I first gathered them, back when the plan was still more hope than plan. Paper remembers so mouths don't have to. I had meant it as comfort. Tonight the memory turned on me like a hook. Because if paper remembered this—if it remembered the appraisal of my mother's spoons—then paper also remembered every other family in this room's history, and every family not in this room, and there was no version of the truth left standing where the company came out clean.

"Vesper." Mama Sefu's voice, low, from her stool. "Breathe, boy."

"I'm breathing." I was. I was always breathing. It was the only skill the sea ever truly gave me.

"You're diving," she corrected. "There's a difference. Come up."

I tried to answer and found I had no voice. Ten years of silence, and now that I finally wanted to spend words, the hoard wouldn't open.

It was Besa who broke it, in the way children do, without ceremony and therefore with perfect aim. She climbed off the bench, came around the table, and put her small hand flat on the copied page, covering the entry entirely.

"He was mine too," she said.

I looked at her. Her father had drowned the winter after mine. I had never made the connection—had never let myself—and here was this child, nine years old, making it for me.

"The men who go down," she said, with the terrible simplicity of children quoting adults, "they're all ours. So your father is half mine, and my father is half yours, and nobody gets to be lost alone." She smoothed the page under her palm as if she could iron the entry flat. "That's what my mama said. Before."

Something in my chest gave way. Not gently. There was nothing gentle about it. It was a wall going down, and walls don't crumble prettily; they come down in slabs, and everything behind them comes pouring out at once, unsorted, undignified, ten years of it.

I heard myself make a sound I had never made in front of another living person. Then I was bent double over the table with my face in my arms, and the sound was coming out of me in waves, and I could not have stopped it any more than I could have stopped the tide. I wept for my father, who had gone into the water with forty-one marks of debt on his back because the company paid divers in advances and charged them for rope, for lamps, for the very air they breathed compressed into tanks. I wept for my mother touching the false marker every morning, believing the sea, thanking the sea for its honesty when the real thief had been dry-shod the whole time. I wept for the house—with its fig tree in the yard, my father's fig tree, which I had not thought of in years and which arrived in my memory now whole and green and unbearable—and for the strangers who must have moved into it before the figs ripened that summer.

The room held still around me. Nobody told me it would be all right, which was the only mercy available, because it might not be, and we all knew it.

Then Mama Sefu's arms came around me.

She was old and small and she smelled of quinces and woodsmoke and the particular soap she'd made herself since before I was born, and she held me the way she had held me at ten years old, the first night after my mother's funeral, when I had stood in her doorway refusing to cry because crying felt like spending money I couldn't replace. She had fed me stolen figs then. She had no figs tonight, so she held on instead, one hand cradling the back of my head like I was still that boy, and she rocked slightly, and she spoke into my hair in the old island dialect, the words soft and half-nonsense and entirely comprehensible: *there now, there now, the storm passes through, the tree stands.*

"I hate them," I got out, when I could get words at all. It came out ragged and shocked me with its own sound. I don't think I had ever said *hate* aloud in my life. Silence had been survival; silence had rules; and hatred was the loudest thing silence contained. "I hate them, Mama Sefu. They took the house. They took it while she was still—while the body was barely—"

"They took mine too." Her voice was steady, but I felt her arms tighten. "Forty years ago, different company, same arithmetic. My husband's boat. 'Settled by estate seizure.' I never saw the paper. Nobody showed us the paper. They just came with the appraiser and we stood in the road." She drew back and looked at me, and her old eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear. "You know what I did? Nothing. I kept my head down and kept my orchard and told myself surviving was enough." She touched my cheek with a hand that had picked fruit for sixty years, callused and impossibly gentle. "Surviving isn't enough anymore, is it, Vesper?"

"No," I said. And the word came out steady, and hearing it steady frightened me more than the weeping had.

Ilya spoke from his corner, hesitant, the guilt in his voice worn smooth as river stone from weeks of confession. "For what it's worth—the margin note. 'No further inquiry permitted.' That phrase appears eleven times in the death entries I can reconstruct. Always on the seizures. It was a standard flag. When a diver died owing money and the estate covered it, the regional office flagged the file against inspection." He turned his pen over in his fingers. "I flagged files myself. I told myself it was clerical."

"It was clerical," I said, and my voice surprised me again, this time with its gentleness. "That's the whole horror of it, Ilya. It was clerical. A man drowned and a house changed hands and a widow walked into the street, and somewhere a clerk filed it between lunch and the end of the day." I looked around the table—at the children who had inherited this arithmetic, at the old woman whose orchard had fed the families the arithmetic emptied out, at the disgraced clerk who was spending his shame like currency to buy back a piece of the truth. "This is why the book has to survive. Not for vengeance. For the record. So no one ever gets to say it was the sea."

Besa took her hand off the page. The entry sat there in the lamplight, plain as bread: *debt settled by estate seizure.* I made myself look at it until it stopped being a wound and started being evidence.

"Copy it twice," I told Tano. My voice had come back fully now, level, and something in the room shifted to match it. "Once for the master copy, once for the name-list. My father goes on the list under his own name, with the true cause beside it. Emeka Okonkwo. Died in company service. Home taken the same week." I paused. "And strike nothing. No names struck through. Whatever else happens, whoever reads what we save—nobody gets erased in our book."

Tano nodded and bent to work, and the pens took up their whisper again, that sound I had come to love more than any sound in Iloran, the sound of the truth being written down by people who had every reason to stay quiet.

Mama Sefu stayed beside me a while longer, then rose, groaning theatrically in the way that was her one indulgence, and went up the stairs to fetch bread and the last of the honeyed tea. At the doorway she stopped.

"For what it's worth," she said, "your father's fig cuttings. I took cuttings from that tree the spring before they seized the house. They're growing on my east wall right now. Have been for fifteen years." She smiled, small and fierce. "They didn't get everything, boy. Write that down too."

She went up, and I sat with the others in the lamplight, and I thought: this is what rage is for, properly used. Not fire. Rootstock. You take the cutting before they seize the tree, and you grow it somewhere they aren't looking, and one day the fruit feeds the grandchildren of everyone they robbed.

We worked past midnight. It was Besa, near the end, drowsy-headed, scanning ahead in her column out of habit, who went suddenly still and said, "Vesper. There's an entry dated eight days after your father's. Same flag. Same phrase."

I leaned over. Another diver's name. Another seizure. And below it, a third, and a fourth—five estate seizures in a single storm season, all flagged *no further inquiry permitted*, all clustered in the weeks when the company knew the army's inspectors would someday want its books and had begun, quietly, to tidy them.

Five families in one season. And if the pattern held—

"Tano," I said slowly, "how many more flagged entries are in the season pages we haven't reached yet?"

He flipped forward through the copied leaves, counting under his breath, and the color left his face before he answered.

"Vesper," he said, "it doesn't stop at five."

* * *

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a patrol lantern swung past the mouth of the alley above the cellar stairs, and we all froze as one—pens lifted, breath held, the oldest habit of our lives—until the light slid away and left us in the dark with the number none of us had yet dared say aloud.
