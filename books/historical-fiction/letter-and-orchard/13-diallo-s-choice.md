## 13. Diallo's Choice

Sergeant Amara Diallo had learned, in eleven years of soldiering, that the most dangerous orders were never the loud ones. Nobody ever got hurt because a colonel shouted. It was the quiet paperwork that killed people—the requisition slips, the transport manifests, the little columns of figures that decided where men and wagons and crates went, and when, and whether anyone was waiting for them at the other end.

So when Captain Verhoeven handed her the folded order that Tuesday morning—*Escort archive wagons to the burning ground, departure Friday, dawn*—she read it twice, and what frightened her was not the word *burning*. What frightened her was how casually it sat there between *departure* and *dawn*, as if it were a delivery of boots.

"Burning ground," she said.

Verhoeven didn't look up from his coffee. He was a small, neat man with the complexion of someone who had spent his whole career indoors signing things. "Sanitation order. The old counting house archives are contaminated—mold, rats, who knows what the company was keeping in there. Engineers say the structure's unsound anyway. We burn it, we level it, we put up a customs post." He said it the way a man describes sweeping a floor.

"And the ledger? The one under seal? *Evidence pending historical review?*"

"Reviewed," Verhoeven said. "That's what the burning is. Review complete."

She stood there a moment longer than was comfortable for both of them. Then she saluted, which in the conscript army of the Charter was less a gesture of respect than a way of ending conversations you couldn't afford to have.

Outside, Iloran smelled of salt and frying fish and, faintly, of smoke from the harbor furnaces. She walked past the counting house on her rounds—a gray stone building with a clock that hadn't worked since the occupation began—and saw two soldiers already stacking barrels beside it. Fuel oil, probably. They'd been told to prepare. Three days' preparation for a fire, she thought. That's thorough. That's *organized*. That is a thing done by people who want to be able to say afterward that they followed procedure.

Her father had been a schoolteacher in Kessou before the war. He used to say that evil rarely needed liars; it only needed clerks. She'd thought it was one of those things fathers say to sound wise. She was beginning to suspect her father had simply read too many requisition slips himself.

* * *

The problem, as she laid it out that evening on the back of an inventory sheet, was this:

The order said Friday. The burning ground—the old lime kilns past the eastern ridge—was four hours' march with loaded wagons. If the wagons left Friday at dawn, the papers would be ash by Friday noon, and whatever was inside them would become whatever the historians said it was. And she had met the historians. Or rather, she had met *the* historian, Marchand, a thin, apologetic man who interviewed soldiers like a doctor asking where it hurt and wrote down something gentler than what you said.

She could refuse. Refusal meant arrest, meant her name in someone else's ledger, meant her family in Kessou learning about it through a form letter. She had seen what happened to Sergeant Okafor, who had refused to clear a market stall: six months, dishonorable discharge, and a wife who now took in washing.

She could obey. Obey meant the smoke over the ridge on Friday, and her own hand on the manifest that put it there.

Or—

She looked at the blank transport manifest on her desk. Form 11-C. *Cargo description, quantity, escort strength, destination, authorized delay notations.* She knew this form better than she knew scripture. Every soldier did; forms were the real religion of the army, and 11-C was its most tedious psalm.

And here was the thing about Form 11-C that no one thought about, because thinking about forms was beneath everyone: it had a line for delays. *Authorized delay notations.* Weather. Road repair. Wheel failure. A sergeant could write almost anything there if her handwriting was confident enough, and Amara Diallo's handwriting was the handwriting of a woman who had once copied out her father's entire book collection by candlelight because he believed beautiful script built character.

She dipped her pen.

What she wrote, over the next hour, was a masterpiece of bureaucratic slowness. The road east, she noted, required inspection after the rains—this was even true; she had seen the ruts herself. Wagons carrying sealed archival cargo required double axle checks per standing regulation, and the regimental farrier was currently occupied with the artillery horses—also true; the man had three horses down with hoof rot and a temper to match. Escort strength had to be drawn from the harbor watch, which was itself stretched thin by the customs inspections—true again, blessedly true; the occupation ran on truths arranged badly.

By midnight she had assembled a document so dense with legitimate obstacles that approving it would take Verhoeven a day, countersigning would take the quartermaster another, and actually resolving all of it—the inspections, the farrier, the escort rotation—would take, by her careful arithmetic, four days minimum.

Four days. She stared at the number and felt something in her chest that was not quite pride and not quite terror but lived in the same house.

She had just bought time for a fire not to happen in. She had no idea what anyone would do with it. She wasn't even sure, honestly, what *she* wanted done with it. She only knew that somewhere in that building were pages with names in them, and names were the one thing a soldier learned never to treat as cargo.

* * *

Verhoeven approved the manifest in forty minutes, initialing without reading, because Verhoeven believed that anything boring must be correct. The quartermaster took until Thursday afternoon, complaining the whole time about axle regulations invented by idiots. The farrier swore at her, inspected two wagons, declared one sound and one "a coffin on wheels," and demanded a replacement wheel from the depot—which the depot did not have, and would need to requisition from the naval yard, which would take—

"A week?" she asked sweetly.

"Eight days, if the naval yard keeps drinking its budget."

"Then we wait eight days."

"We'll *all* wait eight days," the farrier said darkly, "because God help the army that moves cargo on my axles."

She walked back through the town feeling lighter than she had any right to feel, and stopped at the fish market mostly because she wanted to stand among civilians for ten minutes and remember what unafraid breathing sounded like. The stalls were loud, argumentative, alive. An old woman was haggling over mackerel with the ferocity of a border negotiation. A boy—thin, quick-eyed, one of the diver children who haunted the docks—was helping stack crates, and he watched her with the flat, careful look that all of them gave soldiers, the look of people counting exits.

"You're the one who made the wagon man angry," the boy said suddenly.

She blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"The farrier. He's telling everyone. Says some officer buried the convoy in paper." The boy grinned, quick and crooked. "He says nobody's ever beaten him with a form before."

"I didn't beat anyone. There are regulations."

"There always are," the boy said, with a knowingness that sat strangely on his young face, and went back to his crates.

Diallo stood a moment in the fish-smelling light, feeling oddly seen, and wondered—not for the first time since the occupation began—who exactly she was working for these days.

* * *

Marchand found her on Saturday, or perhaps she found him; afterward neither could say. He was standing at the edge of the harbor square watching gulls fight over offal, notebook closed for once, looking like a man who had mislaid his opinions.

"Sergeant Diallo." He nodded. "You escorted me to the widow's compound last month. The fruit woman."

"Mama Sefu."

"She told me figs keep better than memories." He smiled faintly. "I wrote it down. I'm not sure it will survive the editorial process."

"Nothing survives the editorial process," Diallo said. "I've seen the manifests."

Something passed between them then—not trust, nothing so fast as trust, but recognition, the way two swimmers in rough water recognize each other's stroke. Marchand glanced around, lowered his voice.

"The archive convoy. I hear it's delayed."

"Roads," she said. "Axles. Farriers."

"Of course. Bureaucracy is very thorough here." He turned his hat in his hands. "Sergeant. If—I say *if*—a historian wanted to consult certain sealed documents before they were, hypothetically, destroyed for sanitation purposes... how much time might such a hypothetical historian have?"

She looked at him for a long moment. This was the moment, she understood, where complicity changed direction—where a soldier stops being the wall and becomes the door, quietly, without ceremony, the way most important things happen.

"Eight days," she said. "Possibly nine, if the naval yard keeps drinking."

Marchand exhaled, and it seemed to take ten years off his face. "Thank you," he said. "You should know I intend to use every hour."

"That's why I bought them."

* * *

Sunday night she sat alone in the guardroom and did the accounting soldiers do at night, the honest kind, the kind with no forms.

She had falsified nothing, technically. Every obstacle on that manifest was real; she had merely arranged them, the way a cook arranges ingredients, into a meal of delay. Her hands were clean in the way that mattered to tribunals and dirty in the way that mattered at three in the morning. She was a soldier of the Charter, sworn to its order, and she had just spent that order like small change to buy time for—what? For a historian she half-trusted. For townspeople whose names she didn't know. For documents she'd never seen, containing deaths she couldn't verify, recorded by a trading company she'd helped conquer.

It made no sense. It made perfect sense. Both things were true, and she was learning to hold them together, the way you carry two buckets of water without spilling either: slowly, evenly, and without looking down.

There was a knock. One of her corporals, yawning. "Sergeant, dispatch from division. Priority."

She broke the seal, expecting routine orders, and read it twice.

*Revised schedule. Archive destruction advanced. Burning ground operation commences within seventy-two hours. All transport delays cancelled. Convoy departs Wednesday, dawn.*

Someone above Verhoeven had grown impatient. Someone had looked at her beautiful, tedious manifest and cut through it like a knife through wet paper. Four days of careful work—gone. Whatever Marchand meant to do with his eight days, he now had three. Whoever else was counting on that time—and she thought of the boy in the market, of the farrier's gossip traveling where gossip travels—had three days too, and didn't know yet that the clock had collapsed.

The corporal was still standing there. "Anything wrong, Sergeant?"

She looked at the dispatch. Orders were orders. Orders were the whole architecture of her life, load-bearing walls she had never once questioned, and here was another one, ordinary as boots, telling her exactly when the fire would start.

"No," said Amara Diallo, folding the dispatch into her pocket instead of the order book, a small theft no tribunal would ever weigh. "Nothing's wrong. Wake the harbor watch at five. And corporal—"

"Yes?"

"When you make your rounds tomorrow, walk past the fish market. Slowly. You don't need to stop anywhere. Just walk slowly, and let people see the route is clear."

The corporal frowned, shrugged, and left. Outside, the tide was coming in, patient and enormous, the way truth sometimes is.

Three days, she thought. God help us all, we move on Wednesday.

Unless somebody moved first.
