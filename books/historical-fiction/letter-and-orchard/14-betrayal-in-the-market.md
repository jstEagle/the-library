## 14. Betrayal in the Market

The trouble with teaching children to remember numbers was that children were proud of remembering numbers.

Vesper understood this the way he understood the pull of the tide—by living with it long enough to stop fighting it. For three nights now, Kofi had been reciting the harbor-warehouse entries in his sleep. Mama Sefu had heard him through the thin wall of the cellar and reported it at breakfast with the dry tone of a woman cataloguing a new leak in the roof.

"The boy dreams in columns," she said, cracking an egg into the pan. "Debt figures, dates, a man called Odum who owed four pounds eleven. He apologizes to this Odum in his sleep for getting the shillings wrong."

"Does he get them wrong?"

"Once. I corrected him."

Vesper put down his bread. "You corrected him."

"I have an ear for arithmetic," Mama Sefu said, unrepentant, "and no patience for errors, even sleeping ones. The point, child, is that if he's saying it aloud in his dreams, he'll say it aloud awake. Boys that age are like kettles. You can keep them off the fire, but they'll find one eventually."

She was right, of course. She usually was, which Vesper found both comforting and deeply irritating, often in the same moment.

He should have pulled Kofi from the market rounds that very morning. But the fishmonger Bala's stall sat directly across from the company warehouse gate—the best listening post any of them had—and Kofi could count the soldiers coming and going faster than any of them, faster even than Vesper, whose counting had always been inward, downward, toward the bottom of the sea rather than the surface of the street. So Vesper told himself: one more day. One more day and then we rotate him out.

One more day. Every disaster he had ever witnessed began with those words, spoken by someone who believed the water would stay calm just a little longer.

* * *

It happened at half past ten, in the way disasters do happen—not with thunder but with a small, ordinary sound.

Vesper was two stalls down, buying oranges he did not need because Mama Sefu had sent him to buy oranges and because standing still in a market drew eyes. He heard Kofi's voice first, bright and carrying, doing what it did best: reciting.

"—and the second barge took on forty-one crates on the ninth, but the manifest said thirty-eight, which means three went somewhere, and Sergeant Okoro signed for them, and Sergeant Okoro also signed for the missing rope coil in March—"

The boy was performing. That was all. There was a little crowd around Bala's stall the way there always was when something interesting happened, and Kofi—ten years old, gap-toothed, vain about his memory the way other boys were vain about running or fighting—was giving them the numbers like a street magician gives them cards.

Vesper moved before he finished thinking, which was how divers survived, and reached the edge of the circle just as the second thing happened: a gray-uniformed corporal pushed through the crowd, and behind him, at the mouth of the lane, stood a figure Vesper knew too well.

Ilya Petrov. Frozen. A basket of herring dangling from his hand like an apology.

Vesper's stomach dropped the way it did above a bad reef—no bottom visible, nothing to catch you.

Because Ilya wasn't supposed to be here. Ilya came to the market on Thursdays only, early, when the stalls were still being set up and the patrols were changing over, because Thursdays were safe. Today was not Thursday. And Vesper had watched enough men move through Iloran's streets in ten years of silence to know what a man looked like when he was walking where someone else wanted him to walk.

* * *

They took Kofi behind the customs shed.

Vesper did the hardest thing he had ever done in a decade of hard things: he stayed among the oranges. Because a boy caught reciting figures was a curiosity; a boy with a grown man hovering near him was a conspiracy. Because the circle lived or died by whether each member looked alone. Because—because, because. All the reasons, stacked like sandbags, and behind them the simple animal scream of *he is only ten years old and you are leaving him.*

Mama Sefu arrived within minutes, drawn by the same current that had drawn every idler in the market, and planted herself at the front of the gathering crowd with a widow's perfect entitlement to be anywhere. Vesper drifted to her side. Neither of them spoke. Her knuckles were white around her walking stick; her face was arranged into mild, elderly disapproval, as though the whole affair were a nuisance involving chickens.

Through the shed's open door, Vesper could see a slice of what happened inside. The corporal—a young one, sunburned, uncertain—had Kofi on a crate. An older sergeant stood by the desk, turning the boy's cap over in his hands.

"Where'd you learn your figures, boy?"

"School." Kofi's voice was steady. Vesper felt a fierce, terrible pride and hated himself for feeling pride instead of terror. "Before the charter. Master Ekwueme's school, by the chapel."

"There's no school by the chapel anymore."

"No, sir. It closed."

"So where've you been learning since?"

"Nowhere, sir. I just—remember things. Mama says it's a waste, all I remember and nothing useful to do with it."

A ripple of laughter went through the crowd outside—nervous, complicit, human. Even the sunburned corporal's mouth twitched. And Vesper saw the moment the shape of the danger changed: the sergeant turned the cap over again, looked at the gap-toothed boy swinging his legs on the crate, and visibly recalculated. This was not a spy. This was a child showing off. Children showed off. The empire was not built on arresting every child who counted crates.

But the sergeant was thorough, in the way of men who had learned that thoroughness kept them employed.

"Who taught you about manifests? About signing?"

"I watch," Kofi said simply. "You can see the warehouse gate from Bala's stall. Everybody watches. Watching's free."

That was true, and it was the most dangerous kind of answer, because it was true. The sergeant grunted. He asked names—who else watched, what did the fishmonger say—and Kofi gave him Bala's opinions on the price of mackerel and the weather and the moral failings of the harbor master, delivered at length, until even the corporal looked bored. Kofi had learned from the same school Vesper had: the school of the empty answer, the answer that filled the air so completely there was no room left for the real question.

Still, they held him two hours. Still, they walked him home "to see his mama," which meant seeing whether his mama existed and what kind of house kept him. Mama Sefu detached herself from the crowd and followed at a widow's amble, arriving at the compound door at the same time as the soldiers, arms folded, demanding to know if the Emperor's army made a habit of frightening old women's boarders. The sergeant apologized. Actually apologized. Kofi went inside, and the door shut, and Vesper, watching from across the lane with a bag of oranges he would never explain to anyone, let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since dawn.

Then he saw the sergeant pause at the corner. Pull out a small book. Write something in it.

Not nothing, then. Just—not yet.

* * *

They gathered that evening in the orchard instead of the cellar, sitting among the fig trees where conversation could be disguised as pruning, though none of them pruned anything. The light went gold and then gray. Nobody started talking until Mama Sefu, at the wall, pronounced the street empty.

"He gave up nothing," Vesper said. "You all saw it."

"He gave up plenty," Ilya said.

Vesper turned. Ilya sat on an upturned crate, herring basket beside him, hands wrapped around a cup of tea he hadn't touched. His face in the fading light had the gray, translucent look of paper held against a flame.

"They wrote it down," Ilya said. "Names, habits, the market post, the Thursdays—I don't know what exactly, but sergeants don't write things in little books for their health. They're not suspicious yet. Suspicious comes later. First comes the file." He laughed, short and bitter. "I built files for twelve years, Mr. Okonkwo. I know the life cycle of one better than I know my own face. Today we became a file."

"We don't know that," Vesper said.

"We absolutely know that."

"And I'll tell you what else I know." Vesper kept his voice level, which cost him. "I know you weren't supposed to be in that market today. I know today isn't Thursday. I know I've watched you walk that lane twice this week like a man following footsteps laid down ahead of him."

The orchard went very quiet. Somewhere beyond the wall, the town sounds continued obliviously—a cart, a dog, the evening bell—but under the fig trees the silence had weight, the silence of water pressing on a diver's chest.

Ilya set down his untouched tea. "Say what you mean, Vesper."

"You were followed. Or you were seen, and someone walked you back to us to see where you'd go. Either way, you led them to Bala's stall, and Bala's stall is twenty feet from where Kofi sits, and Kofi is ten years old and spent two hours today learning what happens to boys who are near us."

"Do you think I don't know that?" Ilya's voice cracked upward, and for once the drunkard's smoothness fell away entirely, and underneath was just a tired, frightened, ashamed man. "Do you think I walked into that market for pleasure? I went because—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "There was a man at the end of Fish Street. Gray coat. He's been there three days. I thought—if I changed my days, went when I shouldn't, acted normal—he'd read it as normal. Instead I walked straight into the one scene in Iloran I should have given my right arm to avoid." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I'm sorry. God help me, I'm sorry. I've been sorry since 1909 and it hasn't improved anything."

Vesper wanted to hold on to the anger. Anger was simpler than fear, and fear was what he actually carried—that the work, the copying, the names, the small lamplit prayers of the cellar, had already become a thing that could reach out and take a child. But anger at Ilya required believing Ilya had chosen betrayal, and Vesper had spent too many hours watching the man reconstruct figures from pure habit, tongue between his teeth, careful as a monk, to believe he chose anything except badly.

"He didn't give up the circle," Vesper said finally, to the group, because the group needed it said. "Kofi gave them a schoolmaster who's dead, a fishmonger's opinions, and a philosophy of free entertainment. If they come back, he'll do it again. But—" and here he made himself say the rest, because the circle deserved the truth even when the truth was a knife, "they'll come back differently next time. Quieter. And we can't be in the cellar when they do."

"Scatter?" Mama Sefu said.

"Split the work. No more full circle in one place. Two houses for the writing, never the same two nights running. Kofi stays off the market entirely—"

"He'll hate that," said Adaeze, fifteen, who remembered dates the way other people remembered songs.

"He'll hate it alive," Vesper said, and nobody argued.

Mama Sefu tapped her stick once against the wall stone—the gavel of their small illegal parliament. "My cellar keeps its books tonight, and tomorrow night, and every night after, until the fruit goes out or the army comes in. I'm too old to scatter and too stubborn to start." Her eyes found Ilya, and softened by perhaps a millimeter, which from Mama Sefu was a pardon with honors. "And you, clerk, will walk me home. Slowly, by the long way, and we will discuss which streets you may henceforth be seen on, because apparently no one else in this conspiracy remembers that old women are invisible and therefore excellent escorts."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone, Vesper found Kofi on the cellar steps in the dark, not crying, which was worse.

"They wrote my name wrong," the boy said. "In the little book. 'Kofy.' With a y." His chin trembled once and steadied. "I didn't tell them anything, Uncle Vesper. Not about the copies, not about the names, not about anything."

"I know."

"Bala got arrested instead of me, almost. Did you see? They asked him why he lets loiterers at his stall and he talked for forty minutes about mackerel and they just—left. He says mackerel saved my life." A wet, hiccupping almost-laugh. "He's telling everyone he's a hero now. He's charging people to hear the story."

Despite everything, Vesper laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, and Kofi grinned up through the dark, gap-toothed, alive, and for a moment the fear loosened its grip.

Then footsteps on the path above. Quick ones. Vesper was on his feet with his heart in his throat before the figure resolved against the last gray light: Mama Sefu, breathless, stick forgotten in her grip, which she had not done once in ten years.

"Vesper." Her voice was low and urgent. "The clerk's gone."

"Gone?"

"Walked me to my gate, thanked me kindly, and then—Adaeze saw him. Heading for the harbor. Toward the counting house, Vesper, after dark, after today, with his confession in his coat pocket." She caught his sleeve. "He thinks they'll arrest him for the market. He's going to turn himself in before they can take him quietly."

Vesper was already running.
