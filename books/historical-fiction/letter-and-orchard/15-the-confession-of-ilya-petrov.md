## 15. The Confession of Ilya Petrov

The fish market at closing time smelled of brine and honest labor, which was more than could be said for the man crouched behind the gutting tables with a stolen pencil stub in his pocket. Ilya Petrov had spent eleven years among ledgers so clean they squeaked, and now he lived among fish scales that clung to his sleeves like small accusations. He had decided, in the way of men who have finally run out of places to hide from themselves, that the scales were more becoming.

He was writing on the backs of wrapping paper—those wide, soft sheets the fishmongers used for herring—and he had learned to write small. Very small. A clerk's trick: fit more truth per square inch, waste less paper, leave fewer pages for someone to find. He had once fit an entire quarter's bribe schedule onto a single sheet folded no larger than a communion wafer. That particular skill had earned him a commendation from the company. It would now, he hoped, earn him something better than a commendation, though he did not expect it to earn him anything comfortable.

His hands shook. Not from fear—or not only from fear—but from the peculiar trembling that comes over a man who has carried a weight for so long that setting it down feels like falling.

"You're still here," said a voice, and Ilya's heart performed a maneuver no heart should attempt before supper. "I told you not to be still here."

Vesper Okonkwo stood at the end of the alley between tables, a basket of mussels on his hip as though he were merely a young man buying his supper and not the most dangerous thing Ilya had seen in Iloran since the gunboats. The boy—no, not a boy anymore; Vesper was twenty-one and had the stillness of a man twice his age—glanced up the alley, down it, then sat on an overturned crate as if he owned it and the whole indifferent sea besides.

"I'm always here," Ilya said. "It's where I live. In the grammatical sense."

"In every sense."

"Every sense," Ilya agreed. "I've become a man of one address and no future. My old colleagues would call that efficiency."

Vesper did not smile. Vesper rarely smiled, and when he did it was like sun through fog—brief, unexpected, gone before you'd finished being grateful. Instead he set down the basket and looked at the wrapping papers spread under Ilya's elbow, at the tiny crawling figures covering them, and his face did something complicated.

"How much is there?" he asked.

"Fourteen pages so far." Ilya smoothed the topmost sheet with a hand that wanted very much to stop shaking and refused all persuasion. "Names, dates, sums. Every payment Meridian made to keep a widow quiet. Every payment made to keep a mother from asking where her son's body went. Every payment made—" here his voice caught on something, and he waited it out like a man waiting out rain, "—every payment *I* recorded, and *I* delivered, and *I* counted twice because I was thorough, God forgive me, thoroughness was my one virtue and I applied it to evil."

* * *

The trouble with being a coward, Ilya had often reflected, was that cowardice demanded such stamina. Courage was a single wild moment; cowardice was a career. For eleven years he had risen before dawn, shaved, dressed in his good grey coat, and walked to the counting house past the orchard wall where an old woman grew figs she was not supposed to sell him, and he had done the day's arithmetic of other people's grief, and he had walked home again, and he had slept—this was the miracle, this was the shameful marvel—he had *slept*, most nights, reasonably well.

Not all nights. There had been the night after Anselm Okonkwo drowned, when the entry came across his desk and Master Corvane stood at his shoulder saying, "Debt settled by estate seizure, Petrov, standard form, don't dawdle," while out in the harbor the dead man's crewmates were still dragging the water with hooks. Ilya had written the words in his beautiful clerical hand—the hand his father had paid three years' wages to train—and he had slept that night too, badly but soundly enough, and in the morning he had sharpened his pen and begun again.

That was the thing about paper. Paper forgave everything. Paper took any lie you gave it and held it flat and neat forever, patient as a saint, willing to wait decades for someone to come along and read it aloud.

Well. Someone had come along.

"I need to ask you something," Vesper said now, in the fish-market dusk, "and I want you to think before you answer, because I only get to ask once."

Ilya braced himself. He had been bracing himself for two weeks, ever since the boy in the memory-circle—the sharp little one, Tomas, who could recite forty entries without breathing—had been caught whispering figures to a fishmonger and questioned by soldiers, and given up nothing, blessed silent child, and the circle had scattered into the town's cracks like spilled coins. Since then Ilya had noticed a soldier who lingered too long by the market gate. Since then he had written faster.

"Ask," he said.

"If they take you—if they arrest you or worse—will you talk? Will you give them the circle?"

And there it was, the question Ilya had been dreading, asked with the terrible gentleness Vesper used for questions he already knew the shape of.

"No," Ilya said.

Vesper waited. Vesper was excellent at waiting; ten years of indenture will teach a person that silence has more rooms than speech.

"You're waiting for me to explain why 'no' isn't enough," Ilya said. "You're right to wait. Here is why." He gathered the fourteen sheets, tapped them square against the crate the way he had once tapped ledgers square against his desk, and held them out. "Because I am going to write everything down. All of it. Signed. Dated. With my full name, my position, and my own hand attesting that I personally recorded these payments and delivered these bribes. And I'm going to give it to you tonight, and after tonight I won't know anything worth telling anyone, because everything worth knowing will be in your keeping, not mine. They can arrest me and interrogate me until doomsday. I'll be able to offer them nothing but my opinions on the weather and my sincere regret."

"They might not care whether you know anything," Vesper said quietly. "They might just want you gone."

"Yes," Ilya agreed. "That is also possible. I have considered it." He tried for lightness and heard it land crooked. "I've had a great deal of time to consider it. Fish markets are very contemplative at closing."

* * *

He wrote the confession over three nights in the loft above the cooperage, where the barrel-maker's widow let him sleep in exchange for sweeping and for making her laugh, which was easier work than it sounded because Ilya had discovered that a man with nothing left to lose becomes unexpectedly funny. Fear burned away pretense like flame burning varnish off wood, and underneath, it turned out, there had been a person all along—a person who remembered jokes his grandmother told in a village he would never see again, and who found, telling them to a barrel-maker's widow in a foreign port, that they still worked.

On the first night he wrote the preamble. He labored over it. He wanted it precise, unimpassioned, damning in its calmness—the way a well-kept ledger is damning. *I, Ilya Antonovich Petrov, formerly senior recording clerk of the Meridian Trading Company, Iloran station, do hereby attest...*

On the second night he wrote the payments. This went quickly, horribly quickly, because his memory was exact. Eleven years of figures sat in him like ballast. The sum paid to the widow Marchetti to stop her petitioning about her sons—one hundred and forty crowns, less than the company spent annually on lamp oil. The sum paid to the harbor priest for funerals conducted without questions. The standing quarterly amount budgeted, in the master ledger under the innocuous heading "Community Relations," for the silencing of families of lost divers. He wrote the dates. He wrote the names of the paymasters. He wrote his own initials at the bottom of each page, the way he had initialed every page of every ledger, because a record was not a record without accountability, and accountability, he understood now at last, at thirty-four years of age, cut both ways.

On the third night he meant to write a conclusion and instead wrote the truth about Anselm Okonkwo.

He had not planned to. But Vesper's name was in the ledger—he had checked, that first vertiginous afternoon in the counting house, and found the boy listed as collateral against his father's debt, a line-item, a figure—and it seemed to Ilya that a confession which named the payments but not the people was just another kind of tidy bookkeeping. So he wrote: *In the matter of Anselm Okonkwo, diver, drowned 14th of the Harvest Moon, the estate seizure was executed six days after the drowning while the family observed mourning, per instruction of Station Master Corvane, who stated the delay was "already generous." The widow Okonkwo had died the previous spring. The child, Vesper Okonkwo, aged eleven, was entered into indenture against the residual debt of four crowns, eleven shillings.* Four crowns, eleven shillings. Eleven years of a life, purchased at the price of a decent saddle.

Ilya put down the pen and was sick behind the loft's water barrel, and then he came back up and finished the page, because a confession interrupted by vomiting was still a confession, and the night was short, and he was, whatever else, a thorough man.

* * *

They met at the orchard wall on the fourth evening, in the hour when the fig trees threw long shadows and Mama Sefu pretended, loudly, to be pruning things that did not need pruning, thereby granting the two men at the far corner of the garden both privacy and the illusion of innocence.

She was a remarkable woman, Mama Sefu. She had taken Ilya in during the worst weeks after his dismissal, fed him stewed figs and contempt in equal measure, and informed him within the first hour that she had known what Meridian was for thirty years and had simply lacked the paperwork to prove it. "You clerk-men," she'd said, pressing a bowl into his hands, "you think ink is nothing. Ink is everything. Ink is how the strong remember what they did so the weak can't forget it happened. Now we'll turn it around, God willing and figs permitting."

Figs permitted, apparently. The cellar beneath her house now held three crates of preserving fruit and, beneath the false bottom of the middle crate, sixty-two pages of copied ledger in five different hands, including the fierce careful script of a girl named Adaeze who had never met her father but could recite his death-date, his debt-total, and the exact sum paid to make his mother stop asking about either.

"She wants to see your handwriting," Vesper had reported, awed. "She says she wants to know what evil looks like when it takes pride in itself."

"Tell her it looks like this," Ilya said, and showed her, next visit, his beautiful descending numerals, and the girl had studied them and said, "My numbers look angry," and Ilya had said, "Good. Yours should."

Now he stood at the orchard wall with the confession wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, heavier than any document had a right to be, and Mama Sefu's shears went *snip, snip* somewhere behind him like a patient clock counting down his courage.

"It's done?" Vesper asked.

"It's done. Forty-three pages. Signed and dated." Ilya drew it out and held it between them without letting go yet, the way one holds a bird one is about to release. "There's one condition."

Vesper's jaw tightened. Ilya raised a hand.

"Not a demand. A request. When this is over—when Marchand prints it, if he prints it, when the world reads it if the world reads it—I don't want my name defended. I don't want speeches about how I helped in the end. I was the machine, Vesper. I was the hand that wrote 'debt settled by estate seizure' while your father's friends dragged the harbor. Let the record say so plainly. If my testimony costs me everything, let it at least cost me honestly. Promise me the copy keeps my crimes intact. No softening. I've had eleven years of softening. It's how men like me survive."

For a long moment Vesper said nothing. Then he reached out and took the oilcloth bundle, and his grip was steady, and his eyes when they met Ilya's held something Ilya had not seen directed at him in many years—not forgiveness exactly, forgiveness was too large a word and not yet earned, but perhaps the ground forgiveness might someday grow in.

"The copy keeps everything," Vesper said. "That's the whole point of it. We're not writing a kinder history. We're writing a true one."

"Then take it." Ilya let go. His hands, freed of their burden, kept shaking anyway; some tremors are structural. "Take it and hide it deep, and don't tell me where. That's the last piece of it—if I don't know, they can't make me know, and I can't lose it, and neither of us has to wonder what I'd do under pressure. I'd like to find out, actually. What I'd do under pressure. I've always wondered. I suspect it's nothing impressive."

Behind them the shears stopped. Mama Sefu's voice floated across the garden, dry as August: "If the two of you are finished conducting treason by my pear tree, there's stew, and it won't improve with sentiment."

* * *

Over stew—eaten fast, because the patrols had doubled since the boy in the market and everyone moved now like water finding cracks—Mama Sefu laid out the news, and the news was bad in the specific way bad news had gotten lately, which was to say it arrived pre-shortened, like a rope being paid out too quickly.

"Diallo sent word," she said. "The fire date's moved up. Not weeks. Days. Three days, maybe less." She looked at Ilya over her spoon with those old unfooled eyes. "And there's a sergeant asking after a discharged clerk. Description matches you. Asking at the fish market, at the cooperage. Somebody talked, or somebody guessed, or somebody's just thorough. Either way, Petrov, your contemplative period is concluding."

Three days. Ilya set down his spoon and did the arithmetic he could not help doing—it was reflex, it was religion—pages copied versus pages remaining, hours in the cellar versus hours of darkness, and the numbers came out wrong. The numbers came out short. Sixty-two pages existed and the master ledger ran past two hundred, and three days was not enough, and somewhere in his chest a cold voice that sounded distressingly like Station Master Corvane observed that this was why one planned ahead, Petrov, really, this sloppiness—

No. He silenced the voice. He was done taking instruction from that accent.

"Then it has to be tomorrow night," Vesper said. "All of it. Everyone copies until dawn if they have to. Adaeze can go all night; she says sleep makes her forgetful and forgetting is the one thing she refuses to do."

"And you," Mama Sefu said to Ilya, not unkindly. "What happens to you tomorrow night?"

Ilya considered the question with the attention it deserved. Tomorrow night the copying would finish or fail. The morning after, the counting house would burn, and with it the original of every crime he had ever transcribed—and with it, very possibly, any pretense that the authorities didn't know exactly who had been helping the divers' children remember. A dismissed clerk, half-starved, seen loitering near an orchard connected to a funeral procession connected to a historian's suspicious interest. He was, when he added the column honestly, the most obvious loose end in Iloran.

"I thought," he said, "that I would write one more letter."

Mama Sefu's eyebrows rose. Vesper went still, in that particular total stillness of his, the stillness of a man listening with his whole body.

"To Sergeant Diallo," Ilya said. "Unsigned except for what matters. Telling her where the copies will be if we fail. Insurance, of a sort—an archive's archive. If they take us all and burn the cellar too, let there be one thread left for a decent woman to pull." He managed a smile, and found, to his mild surprise, that it was mostly real. "Besides. I'm told she falsified a transport manifest for us. It seems only polite to warn her that she's harboring cowards."

"You're not a coward," Vesper said. It came out flat and immediate, the way true things do.

"My dear boy, I am precisely a coward—a coward who has finally found something scarier than his fear, which is the only definition of courage I've ever trusted. Don't gild me. Gilding is for statues and I intend to survive this as a man, if I survive it, with my flaws catalogued and initialed." He pushed back his stool and stood, and his knees announced their objections as usual, and he ignored them as usual. "Tomorrow I'll help copy until my hand gives out. Tonight I write to Diallo. And then—"

He paused at the door, in the last of the orchard light, and looked back at the two of them at their table: the old woman with her shears and her fury, the young man with his silence and his ledger of ghosts, the strange small family that captivity and catastrophe had assembled out of spare parts. Eleven years ago he had written a child into bondage in beautiful clerical script. Now that same child was going to write him into history, or bury him beside the truth, or both, and either outcome struck Ilya as more grace than he had any right to expect.

"Then I sleep well," he said. "For the first time in eleven years. You'll see. Guilt shared is guilt halved, and guilt written down—" he tapped his breast pocket, where a single blank page waited, "—guilt written down is practically a vacation."

Outside, the first patrol lanterns were moving along the harbor road, and the sea kept up its long indifferent arithmetic against the stones. Ilya turned toward the cooperage and began, with a coward's steadiness, to count the steps home.

Three days, the sergeant's note had said. Three days until the flames. Which meant, though none of them yet knew it, that they had rather less even than that—and that the coffin was already being built.
