## 16. The Burning Order

The order came down on a Tuesday, which Vesper would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days the company had always paid out rations late.

He heard it first as a sound: the drumbeat of boots in the harbor square, sharper than usual, doubled. Then Sergeant Diallo's voice carrying over it, flat and official, reading from a paper to the assembled garrison. Vesper could not hear the words from the drying racks where he coiled rope, but he could read the shape of them. Orders read twice are orders meant to be obeyed quickly. Orders read twice while a clerk scurries between officers with a calendar under his arm are orders about *time*.

By noon the town knew. The counting house archives—every ledger, every manifest, every scrap of the Meridian Trading Company's thirty years of paper—would be burned on Saturday morning. Not for the historical review Marchand had promised in his careful interviews. For sanitation, they said now. Fever season coming, they said. Rats in the paper, they said.

"Rats," Mama Sefu repeated that evening, shelling beans at her kitchen table with the particular violence of an old woman who has been lied to by professionals for six decades. "Rats in the paper. In my day we blamed rats for everything too. Missing grain. Missing children. Missing husbands." She crushed a bean pod with her thumb. "The difference is, when I blamed the rat, everyone knew I was lying."

"They moved it up," Vesper said. He stood in the doorway because sitting felt impossible. "It was supposed to be weeks. We had weeks."

"We had weeks," Mama Sefu agreed, "the way a fish has water." She looked up at him, and her eyes, which could be soft as overripe figs when she wanted, were hard as pits now. "How much is left?"

"The memory-circle has recited through the D divers. Debt columns, death dates. The second column—the payments—Ilya's reconstructed most of it from habit, he says, though his handwriting shakes worse every night." Vesper swallowed. "But we haven't copied the actual pages since the E's. And the name-lists—the sworn ones, the ones the mothers signed—we only have half."

"Then we work faster."

"It isn't enough time to work faster. It's three days."

Mama Sefu set down the beans. She wiped her hands on her apron with great ceremony, the way she did before saying something she intended to be remembered. "Vesper Okonkwo. When your mother died, the company gave me forty minutes to decide whether to take you or let the parish take you. Forty minutes. Do you know what I decided in those forty minutes? Nothing. I decided nothing. I opened my door and you walked in and sat at this table like you'd been invited your whole life, and *then* I decided." She pointed a flour-dusted finger at him. "Time is not what decides things. Time is what cowards blame."

He almost smiled. Almost. "You're calling me a coward?"

"I'm calling you slow to sit down. Sit down. We're going to need to count crates."

* * *

Sergeant Amara Diallo had learned, in eleven years of soldiering, that the worst orders never arrived shouting. They arrived quietly, in clean ink, with a signature at the bottom belonging to a man who would never smell the smoke.

*Requisitioned materials designated for destruction per sanitation directive 9-C. Archive wagons to proceed to the burning ground Saturday, dawn. Escort detail: two corporals, eight privates.*

She signed the acknowledgment because refusing to sign was a career ended and a family in the capital left without remittances, and because signing a thing and meaning a thing had long ago become, for her, separate acts. But her hand hesitated over the date. Three days. The historian Marchand had been promised a month to consult the records. She had watched him in the warehouse evenings, sleeves rolled, actually *reading*, actually asking questions no one coached him to ask. Whatever else Marchand was—and she suspected he was a man who had made his peace with comfortable lies—he was a careful one. Careful men, given time, sometimes became dangerous men.

Given three days, he would become nothing at all.

She found him at dusk in the requisitioned customs office, hunched over company shipping manifests with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't trust. A lamp guttered between them. Outside, a patrol passed, singing something tuneless.

"You've heard," she said.

Marchand looked up. "I've heard. Sanitation." He said the word the way one handles a dead bird. "Fever season. Rats."

"That's what the directive says."

"And what do *you* say, Sergeant?"

Diallo glanced at the door. Closed. She crossed the room anyway and stood close, so the words wouldn't have far to travel if the walls were listening. "I say I've escorted supplies to that burning ground before. Grain, mostly. Once a shipment of Bibles with the wrong translation. I have never once seen fever live in a filing cabinet." She set a folded slip of paper on his desk. "And I say that a sergeant who falsified a transport manifest last week to buy you four extra days would find it very strange if the army then took three of them back."

Marchand stared at the slip. "You did what?"

"Bought days. Don't thank me. Thanking takes longer than we have." She tapped the paper once. "This is a market note. There's a woman—Mama Sefu—who sells preserved fruit behind the fish market. If a historian happened to want figs, excellent figs, the kind worth writing home about, she'd understand any message tucked inside the wrapping. Understand?"

"I'm not certain I want to understand."

"No one ever does. That's how they get away with it." She straightened. "Saturday, dawn, historian. The wagons leave when I sign them out. I sign them out slow. Paper jams happen. Oxen go lame. I am very good at lame oxen." At the door she paused, not turning around. "For the record, Monsieur Marchand, I don't know what you'll do with whatever that woman gives you. I don't want to know. But my grandmother used to say that God forgives the hand that feeds the fire, if the hand also carries water. I intend to carry a lot of water."

She left. Marchand sat alone with the lamp and the market note and the strange, vertiginous feeling that his book—which he had told himself for months was merely his career rendered in prose—had just become something with weight, and consequences, and a deadline.

He went to find a basket.

* * *

The note reached the orchard wrapped around a purchase of figs so extravagant that Mama Sefu suspected espionage immediately.

"Nobody buys two dozen preserved figs," she announced, unwrapping them at the kitchen table while Vesper and Ilya watched. "Not for a funeral. Not for a governor. Two dozen figs is either a bribe or a code, and no one bribes me, I'm terrifying." She peeled back the last layer of cloth and there, in a spidery scholar's hand, was the message:

*MADAM—YOUR FRUIT IS SUPERB. REGRETTABLY, THE MARKET CLOSES SATURDAY AT DAWN. ALL REMAINING STOCK MUST BE MOVED BEFORE THEN. —A FRIEND OF THE ORCHARD*

Silence held the kitchen for a moment. Ilya read it upside down, lips moving, and went gray. "Saturday," he whispered. "They moved the fire. They—" His hands began their tremble, the old clerk's tremble, and he pressed them flat against the table as if pinning them down. "Three days. Vesper. The archive, the original ledgers, everything we couldn't reach—"

"Everything we couldn't reach burns Saturday," Vesper finished. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which was its own kind of lie, the oldest one he owned.

Mama Sefu had gone very still. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, barking thing with no humor in it whatsoever.

"A friend of the orchard," she repeated. "That's a soldier's phrasing. Or a spy's. Either way, someone in that army just spent their own money on two dozen figs to warn an old widow." She shook her head. "The world gets stranger every year, and I keep outliving the strangeness. Well." She stood, and something in the standing was decisive, final, the sound of a door being locked from the inside. "We stop working like conspirators and start working like lunatics. Ilya. How many hours can you write before your hand gives out?"

"In my prime? Sixteen." He flexed the trembling fingers ruefully. "In my current condition, I make no promises past ten."

"Ten will do. Vesper—"

"Already thinking it." He pulled the crate list from his pocket, the false-bottomed fruit crates Mama Sefu had built for preserves, the ones with a finger's width of hidden depth beneath slatted bottoms. "We have nine crates finished. A coffin holds six comfortably if we pack the fruit tight and put the papers flat beneath. We were going to need two trips. Now we need it done in one cortege."

"One funeral," Mama Sefu corrected. "And funerals, thank God, cannot be postponed. Grief keeps its own calendar. Which means someone must die."

They all looked at her.

"Not literally," she snapped. "Honestly, the faces on you. Old Tomas Ferreira, rest him, died in the spring and was buried upcoast near his sister's people. Nobody in this town will swear they attended. A widow burying an old friend upcoast, taking fruit for the mourners—it's sad, it's ordinary, it's *boring*. Soldiers wave boring things through. I've been boring for seventy years; it's the most effective armor I own."

Ilya was staring at her with an expression Vesper recognized—he'd worn it himself, ten years ago, walking through her gate for the first time. The look of a man discovering that a fortress could be built entirely out of an old woman.

"You've done this before," Ilya said. "Hidden things."

Mama Sefu began gathering the figs back into their cloth with brisk, unoffended hands. "I kept a husband off three conscription lists and a still off four tax rolls, and I did it all before you were weaned, clerk. The only new thing here is what we're hiding." She paused, and her voice gentled by a degree, which from Mama Sefu was practically a lullaby. "We hide the truth. Well. About time somebody hid *that* from somebody who deserves it."

* * *

That night the cellar burned with lamplight instead of waiting for it.

Vesper had thought he understood urgency. He had counted breaths underwater for a decade; he knew about clocks that ran in heartbeats. But this was different. This was a whole town's drowned history racing a match, and every page they transcribed was a breath held against the dark.

The memory-circle worked in shifts. Little Adaeze, whose father had drowned off the northern shoals, recited the F-column entries in a clear schoolroom voice while Bintou wrote them out, tongue between her teeth, spelling each name like a prayer she refused to misspell. Kofi, twelve and gap-toothed, had memorized forty-one debt figures and defended them like a dragon defends gold. Between entries they ate Mama Sefu's bread and argued softly about whose turn it was at the good pen.

Vesper copied until his eyes swam, checked Ilya's figures, copied more. Somewhere past midnight, Adaeze fell asleep mid-recitation, her head tipping onto Bintou's shoulder, and nobody woke her. Her last entry hung unfinished in the air, and Vesper completed it from memory, the letters blurring.

*Feranmi, O. Lost [struck through] settled, 14th year.*

His father. Even now, even here, the struck-through word caught in his chest like a hook.

"You should sleep," Ilya murmured beside him, not looking up from his columns. "Even the sea lets divers come up."

"After you."

"I'm a coward, Vesper. Cowards are excellent at enduring; it's our one talent." The old man's mouth quirked. "Go up. Tell Mama Sefu I said the figs were superb."

Vesper climbed the cellar stairs into the grey beginning of Wednesday and found Mama Sefu already awake, already packing fruit into the first coffin crate, pressing each piece down with the tenderness she gave to living things.

"Three days," he said.

"Two and a half, if we're honest, and we're always honest in this kitchen." She didn't look up. "Sleep, boy. Tomorrow we die beautifully—old Tomas Ferreira, may he forgive us—and the dead carry the truth out of Iloran on a cart of figs." She glanced up then, and for one unguarded moment the iron in her face gave way to something older and wearier and fierce with love. "And if the soldiers stop the cortege—if it all goes wrong—remember what I taught you about doors. You don't knock on fate's door and wait. You walk in and sit down like you were invited your whole life."

Above the orchard wall, the eastern sky was going pale, and somewhere across the sleeping town the counting house stood in the dark, full of paper, full of names, holding its breath for Saturday's fire.

Two and a half days. Vesper went inside to sleep, and dreamed of figs, and of fire, and of his father's name written whole.
