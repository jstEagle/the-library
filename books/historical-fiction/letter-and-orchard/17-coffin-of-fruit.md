## 17. Coffin of Fruit

The night before the funeral, Mama Sefu taught us to lie with carpentry.

"Watch my hands," she said, and we watched. She had turned her cellar into a workshop of grief, sawdust on the flagstones, a plane and two chisels laid out like surgical instruments. The false bottom she built was a thing of beauty—thin as a communion wafer, fitted so precisely into the fruit crate that when she pressed it down, the seam vanished entirely. "My husband made coffins," she said, running her thumb along the joint. "Forty years. A good coffin hides nothing, Vesper. It holds everything. There's a difference."

Above us, the town was already burning in small ways. From the cellar's high window I could see the glow of bonfires the soldiers kept lit for warmth, and hear the occasional bark of laughter from men who had been told they would be heroes by Tuesday. Three days. That was what Marchand's market note said—the fire moved up, the counting house to burn before the week was out, all archives declared unsanitary, all history reduced to ash and then to a paragraph.

Three days, and here we were, building boxes inside boxes.

Ilya sat in the corner with his hands wrapped around a cup of tea he never drank, watching Mama Sefu work with the expression of a man watching someone else perform his own surgery. He had spent twenty years handling company paper; now the company would handle none of it, and everything he'd ever touched was about to become contraband.

"The weight is wrong," he said quietly. "A crate of figs that size should weigh more."

Mama Sefu didn't look up. "Then we'll add more figs."

"I'm serious. If a sergeant lifts it—"

"If a sergeant lifts it," she said, setting down her chisel, "he'll find figs. Top layer, middle layer, packed tight the way I've packed them for forty years at market. Nobody in the history of soldiering has ever dug beneath the third layer of figs looking for anything but worms." She picked up the chisel again. "Soldiers are not curious people, Ilya Petrov. Curious people get shot. They are thorough people, and thorough means they check the lid, shake the box, poke the top. Thorough does not mean they take apart a widow's packing."

She was right, mostly. But mostly was a word that could hang you.

* * *

We worked until the lamp burned low. The copied ledger went into two crates—Ilya insisted on splitting it, so that if one was lost the other might survive, though he said it the way a man says *if one twin dies*—and the confession, signed and dated in his careful clerk's hand, went into a third with the sworn name-lists the children had copied out over eleven nights. Eleven nights of lamplight, of small hands reciting columns like psalms, of Mama Sefu standing watch at the top of the stairs with her knitting, which contained no knitting at all, only needles and the patience of an ambush.

The false bottoms fit. We tested them by candlelight, tilting each crate, listening for the whisper of paper. Nothing. Then Ilya, unable to help himself, lifted one crate and set it down hard, twice, the way a dockhand tests a load.

A page shifted. A sound like a moth against glass.

We all froze.

"Straw," Mama Sefu said immediately. "Packing straw between the layers. Muffles everything, fills the space, and gives me an excuse to make the crates heavier." She looked at Ilya with something almost like affection. "You were right about the weight after all. Don't let it go to your head."

So we rebuilt them: ledger pages wrapped in oilcloth, bedded in straw, figs above, straw again, figs again, until each crate weighed exactly what a grieving woman's tribute should weigh and made exactly no sound at all. When we finished, I put my ear to the wood the way I used to put my ear to the sea-wall, listening for what the water knew. The crates said nothing. They smelled, overwhelmingly, of ripe figs, sweet and heavy as a secret.

"They're beautiful," whispered Adaeze, the youngest of the memory-circle, who had come down with her brother to help pack and had been forbidden to touch anything and had touched everything.

"They're fruit," Mama Sefu corrected. "Beautiful gets people suspicious. Fruit just gets eaten." Then she paused, and her voice went softer than I'd ever heard it. "Except these. These don't get eaten. These get buried with Tomi Adeyemi, God rest him, who sold me my first orchard ladder and never once asked why a widow wanted to climb trees. Tomorrow he carries our truth out of this town, because tomorrow everyone in Iloran will be too polite to search a coffin."

Tomi Adeyemi had died on Sunday, eighty-one years old, in his sleep, having lived long enough to see gunboats in his harbor and short enough to miss whatever came after. Mama Sefu had known him sixty years. She had volunteered for the mourning, the wailing, the procession—and then stood in her kitchen and said, in a completely steady voice, "Every coffin leaves town under escort of custom. No soldier alive will stop a burial to count figs. And if one tries—" her eyes flashed "—I will make him wish he'd drowned."

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, the kind of morning where smoke hangs low and every breath tastes of the fires the army kept burning. The cortege assembled outside Mama Sefu's wall: six bearers, four of them old men of the orchard quarter and two borrowed cousins, all of them told nothing except that the widow Sefu's tribute crates rode in the cart behind the coffin and were not to be jostled, on pain of her wrath, which was a threat with real historical precedent.

I drove the cart. This had been argued about. Mama Sefu wanted Ilya hidden in her cellar until the coast cleared, and Ilya wanted to walk in the procession itself, penitent, visible, a mourner among mourners. In the end Mama Sefu won by the simple method of refusing to continue the conversation, and Ilya sat beside me on the cart bench wrapped in a shawl, disguised as an ordinary man going to bury an ordinary friend, his hands folded over his stomach like any other tired old clerk.

The coffin came out last. Plain pine, well-made—Tomi had built his own, years ago, which Mama Sefu called the most honest thing a carpenter ever did. Someone had laid sprigs of fig leaf along the lid.

And then the soldiers came.

There were four of them at the checkpoint by the harbor road, and a sergeant, and the sergeant had the particular boredom of a man three days from burning a building and being told it was hygiene. He raised one gloved hand and the whole procession stopped, the coffin-bearers shifting their burden, the women behind beginning the low keening that Iloran women do, a sound designed by centuries of practice to make any interrupting official feel like a monster.

"Sergeant," Mama Sefu said, before he could speak. She descended on him like weather. "You will forgive an old woman, but my friend has waited in this box since yesterday and he does not wait well, he was always an impatient man, and the ground at the orchard cemetery is soft today and will not be soft tomorrow, and I am told your orders are to inspect goods, so inspect, but quickly, and do not touch the coffin, because the dead belong to God and the living belong to me, and I promise you the second is worse."

The sergeant blinked. I watched him try to find footing in the flood of her. Behind me, I felt Ilya go very still, the stillness of a man whose entire body has become a held breath.

"Insp—yes. Goods." The sergeant gestured at the cart. "What's in the crates?"

"Figs." Mama Sefu said it the way you'd say *bread*, or *rain*. "Funeral tribute. In our custom the family of the deceased sends fruit to the grave—it feeds the journey. You may look. You may even take some, God knows you people eat enough of ours without paying, but if you take from the tribute you take from the dead, and I will know, sergeant. Widows always know."

Something in the sergeant's face suggested he believed this absolutely.

He waved two men up onto the cart. One of them lifted the nearest crate—our crate, our crate, the one with the second half of the ledger sleeping under its third layer of figs—and set it on the tailboard. The whole world narrowed to the size of that crate. I heard my own pulse. I thought, absurdly, of my father counting breaths underwater, thirty strokes down, thirty strokes back, and how he must have learned to hold terror the way you hold air, carefully, knowing it was finite.

The soldier pried at the lid. Straw. Figs, dark and gleaming, packed tight. He pushed his hand in to the wrist, stirred, brought out a handful, ate two, offered one to his companion who shook his head. He looked into the crate the way men look into wells—seeing depth, seeing nothing, seeing only more figs descending into fig-darkness.

He did not dig. Of course he did not dig. Nobody digs beneath the third layer of figs.

"Ripe ones," he said, almost apologetic, to the sergeant.

"It's the season." Mama Sefu's voice could have curdled milk or blessed it. "Are you finished?"

The sergeant looked at the coffin. For one horrible moment his eyes rested on it, and I understood that the entire plan balanced on the universal human reluctance to open boxes containing grandmothers' friends. He took a step toward it. The keening behind him rose, effortlessly, wordlessly, six women becoming twelve women becoming a tide. Somewhere a child began genuinely crying, whether from grief or theater I never learned, and the sergeant stopped, visibly recalculating his life.

"Move them through," he said.

The bearers hoisted the coffin. The cart rolled forward. I kept the mule at a walking pace through sheer iron discipline, because a man who hurries his mule past a checkpoint is a man with something in his crates, and we had nothing in our crates but figs and figs and truth.

Only when the harbor road curved away, when the checkpoint was a smudge of smoke behind us, did Ilya exhale—a long, shaking breath, like a diver surfacing.

"She fed them figs," he murmured. "From the tribute. She fed the sergeant the dead man's figs."

"He'd have liked that," Mama Sefu called from behind, catching up on foot with terrifying energy for a woman of seventy. "Tomi hated waste. And he hated soldiers. This way he got to poison nobody and annoy everybody." Her eyes found mine over the cart rail, and for a heartbeat the humor dropped away and I saw what was underneath: an old woman who had just smuggled ten years of stolen names past four rifles, riding on the back of her oldest friend's coffin. "Don't look so grim, Vesper. He carried heavier loads than this. So have we."

The cemetery sat on the hill above the orchard quarter, white stones in green grass, the sea beyond glittering like it had never drowned anyone. We buried Tomi Adeyemi with full custom—the prayers, the earth, the women singing—and Mama Sefu wept real tears, because whatever else she was, she had loved him sixty years, and grief does not check whether it's convenient.

Then the tribute crates were carried to the far corner of the cemetery wall, where a fish-cart waited with a driver named Beko who owed Mama Sefu his life and possibly his boat, and the false bottoms came up for one final inspection by lantern light in the shadow of the tomb, and there it all lay: the ledger, the confession, the lists of names, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling faintly of figs forever now.

Beko would run the coastal road north tonight, past the blockade, to the mission school at Tern Point where a printer with sympathies had agreed to hold the copies until—who knew. Until the world changed. Until someone with a historian's conscience read them. Until.

Ilya stood over the crates a long time, his hand resting on the oilcloth like a man blessing a child.

"Twenty years I wrote those numbers," he said. "I thought the paper was the prison. Turns out the paper was the door."

We nailed the false bottoms down for the last time. Beko loaded the cart, clicked his tongue at the mule, and rolled off down the hill into the dusk, a fish-cart carrying the truest documents in Iloran under a cargo of herring, and I stood at the cemetery wall and watched until the road took him.

It was done. Out of the flames, carried by the dead, delivered to the living.

Mama Sefu put her hand through my arm. "Come home," she said. "You haven't eaten since yesterday, and tomorrow—" she glanced back toward the harbor, where the counting house stood dark against the burning sky, three days from the fire "—tomorrow we'll need our strength. Whatever happens next, Vesper Okonkwo, remember that we got them out. Everything else is only what they do with what we saved."

But as we walked down through the orchard quarter, past the dark windows and the shuttered stalls, I saw a lantern moving where no lantern should be—one of the memory-circle children, running toward us through the rows of young trees, barefoot, breathless, his face white as bone in the light.

"Vesper," Kofi gasped, grabbing my sleeve. "They took Adaeze. Soldiers at the house—they're asking who's been copying the ledgers."
