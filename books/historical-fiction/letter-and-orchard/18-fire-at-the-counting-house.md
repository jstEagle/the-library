## 18. Fire at the Counting House

Edouard Marchand had attended three public burnings in his career as a historian, and he had learned that they were never about fire. They were about witnesses—about who stood where, and what their faces did, and whether anyone in the crowd was foolish enough to weep.

The fourth burning took place on a Tuesday morning, on the flat ground behind the harbor warehouses, and it was about a book.

They had built the pyre at dawn. Two army wagons had carried the counting house archives out in sealed crates, and Sergeant Diallo's transport company had unloaded them with the brisk efficiency of men who wanted to be somewhere else. Now the crates sat stacked in a wooden frame like an altar, and soldiers kept a perimeter that was less security than stagecraft: *nothing here is hidden, nothing here is feared.*

Marchand stood where Colonel Vasseur's adjutant had placed him—a chalked square on the cobbles marked HISTORIAN, which struck him as the most honest thing about the whole arrangement—and tried not to think about the draft of his official history sitting back in his quarters, forty pages long, polished twice, and hollow as a drum.

*The Meridian Trading Company, whatever its administrative imperfections, served as a stabilizing force in the Iloran coastal trade...*

He had written that sentence four times. Each time his pen had slowed at *imperfections*, the way a cart slows over a bridge it does not trust.

"You'll want to note the date and hour," said Lieutenant Brasse, the adjutant, a young man with the complexion of someone who had been fed exclusively on regulations. "For the record."

"I always note the date and hour," Marchand said mildly.

"And the purpose of the destruction."

"Which is?"

Brasse blinked at him. "Sanitation, sir. As announced. The archives were found to be a health hazard following the flooding last spring."

"The flooding," Marchand said, "that no one remembers."

"The flooding, sir." Brasse consulted his watch with the air of a man who trusted time more than people. "Colonel Vasseur will arrive shortly. There will be a brief address. You are to record it verbatim."

"Verbatim," Marchand agreed. It was a comfortable word. Verbatim meant you didn't have to think; you only had to copy, and copying, he had told himself for twenty years, was a historian's first duty. He had believed it once the way one believes in the honesty of mirrors—until one noticed that mirrors only show you what stands in front of them, and never what is behind your own back.

The crowd gathered slowly. This was the part Marchand watched most carefully. The army had not compelled attendance—that would have been crude—but word had gone round that shops would open late, that the harbor master expected all registered workers to be present, and so the town came the way water comes downhill, without enthusiasm but without resistance either. Fishermen with caps in their hands. Warehouse men. A knot of women from the canning sheds. And along the edges, at distances each had chosen individually, the divers—the sea-people, the ones whose names lived in the crates.

Marchand counted them out of habit. Eleven visible. Probably thirty. He had learned in Iloran that the true number of anything was roughly triple the number you were permitted to see.

One of them, near the warehouse corner, stood very still. A young man, broad-shouldered, with the calm of deep water. Marchand had interviewed him two weeks ago—"native witness, occupation: diver"—and had received the most perfectly empty answers of his entire tour. *Yes, conditions were adequate. No, he knew of no deaths beyond those reported. Yes, the Company paid fairly.* Every sentence correct, every sentence worthless, delivered in a voice like a locked room. Marchand had written *unreliable* in his notes and then, later, crossed it out and written nothing at all, because he could not decide whether the man was lying or whether he was the only one in Iloran telling the truth by refusing to say anything at all.

The young diver was looking at the crates now the way other men looked at coffins.

"Sir?" Brasse again. "Your pencil."

Marchand wrote: *9th of the month, 8:40 in the morning. Archives of the Meridian Trading Company destroyed by order of the Provisional Authority, on grounds of sanitation.* He underlined *sanitation* and felt the underline become, in his hand, something very close to a scar.

* * *

Colonel Vasseur arrived at nine, mounted, dismounted, and gave his address to the town and to history simultaneously, being careful to face the crowd while speaking toward the crates.

"Citizens of Iloran," he began, and Marchand's pencil moved. "Today we close a chapter of neglect..."

It was, in its way, a masterpiece. The Colonel spoke of the old Company's debts as though they had been weather—an act of God, regrettable, now passed—and of the new charter as sunshine. He spoke of progress, of modernization, of the victors' generosity in forgiving what could not be collected anyway. He did not speak of dead men. Not one name crossed the colonel's lips. Marchand realized, with a small cold shock, that this was the entire genius of the speech: it managed to describe ten years of Iloran's history without mentioning a single human being who had lived or died in it. The crowd was being offered amnesia as a gift, wrapped in flags.

"...and so these records, water-damaged and diseased, are consigned to the flames, that the town may begin clean." The Colonel stepped back and nodded to the firing detail.

A soldier came forward with a lantern oil can. The smell of it reached Marchand where he stood—sharp, industrial, the same oil that lit the harbor lamps, which meant that every night thereafter, when the lamps were lit, Iloran would smell faintly of this morning.

He wrote: *At approximately 8:55, ignition.* Then his pencil stopped.

Because the crowd made a sound.

Not a cry. Nothing so useful to a tribunal. It was smaller than that—a single collective intake of breath, the sound of thirty chests tightening at once, so quiet that the soldiers around the perimeter did not notice it, but Marchand heard it the way he had once heard, in another country, another war, a village hear a list of requisitions read aloud. It was the sound of a town watching its memory catch light and knowing better than to say so.

An older woman near the front—a widow, gray-headed, with orchard dirt still under her fingernails as though she'd come straight from her trees—did not gasp. She stood with her arms folded and her chin up, and she watched the fire take the crates with an expression that Marchand could only describe, in the privacy of his own mind, as *bookkeeping*. She was counting. He was certain of it. She was standing there adding up exactly what was being taken, and she intended the total to be remembered.

The oil caught. Flame went up through the crate slats with a soft rushing sound, almost gentle, and then the paper inside found its voice—pages lifting, curling, going up in black flakes that rose on the harbor wind and drifted out over the water like a flock of scorched birds.

Marchand's pencil hovered. *Official narrative*, said his instructions. He began: *The records burned quickly and without incident.*

Without incident. He stared at the words. Somewhere in that fire were columns of figures in a clerk's cramped hand—debt, interest, days of diving, cause of death, and that terrible third column Ilya Petrov had hinted at with a drunkard's sideways courage before disappearing into the fish market. Somewhere in that fire was the entry for a drowned man named Okonkwo, and the seizure of his house, and the word *settled* doing work that no decent language should ask of it.

"Sir." Brasse at his elbow, breathless with officiousness. "You're to collect your notes afterward for review. Standard procedure."

"Naturally."

"There's also the matter of your appendix on economic conditions. The Colonel wishes the casualty statistics to reflect the Company's final annual report. The figure of seven losses over ten years."

Seven. Marchand kept his face still. Sergeant Diallo's unofficial count—slipped to him ten days ago inside a folded laundry list, of all things—had been sixty-one. Sixty-one names, gathered by a woman who had no business gathering them, who had risked a flogging to write them down in pencil on the back of a ration form.

"Seven," Marchand repeated. "As reported."

"As reported," Brasse agreed happily, and moved off to supervise the burning of something else—perhaps the truth, if any scraps remained.

* * *

The fire burned for an hour. That was the cruelty of it; they let it burn slowly, publicly, so the town could watch the whole arithmetic of its grief go up page by page. Children were shushed. The fishermen turned their caps in their hands. The gray-haired widow never moved.

Marchand stayed at his chalked square through all of it, taking notes that were accurate about everything except meaning. *Wind from the southeast. Flames visible above the warehouse line. Crowd disperses by degrees.* His handwriting grew tighter and tighter until Brasse commented on his grip, and Marchand said something about the cold, though it was a mild morning.

Near the end, when the pyre had collapsed into a bed of orange coals and soldiers were poking it with poles to hurry the center pages along, Sergeant Diallo appeared at his shoulder.

She was in full uniform, cap squared away, expression set to neutral—the face she wore for officers. She stood beside him looking at the coals, and for a long moment neither spoke.

"They burn thoroughly," she said.

"So I observe."

"In my experience, sir, paper burns unevenly. Especially ledgers. The bindings are leather; the ink is dense in the margins. You often get fragments." She paused, precisely, the way she paused when delivering a laundry list. "Half-burnt pages blow about. On a wind like today's, southeast, they tend to drift toward the warehouse walls. Someone ought to collect them. For sanitation."

"Sergeant," Marchand said carefully, "are you instructing me in combustion?"

"No, sir. I'm performing my duty." She turned her head slightly—not enough for anyone watching to read it as anything—and her voice dropped below the crackle of the fire. "Warehouse wall, north side, by the drain grate. Ten minutes. If a historian happened to be walking that way, no one would think anything of it. Historians walk everywhere. It's known."

And then she was gone, back to her soldiers, calling out some order about raking the coals, and Marchand stood alone with his heart behaving in a manner entirely unbefitting a scholar of the Provisional Authority.

Ten minutes. He spent eight of them pretending to sketch the pyre's remains, an activity that gave him a legitimate reason to drift, and two of them walking north along the warehouse wall with his hands clasped behind his back in the manner of a man contemplating architecture.

The scrap lay against the drain grate, half tucked under it as though placed there rather than blown—which, he understood, it had been. One page. Torn at the spine, browned at one corner, but whole where it mattered. Ledger ruling, clerk's hand, the cramped vertical script Marchand had seen once through the counting house window before the seals went on.

He read it standing up, in plain view of anyone who cared to look, because the boldest hiding place is daylight.

It was a week of entries. Divers' names down the left margin—real names, given names, the names mothers used. Hours dived. Debt accrued. And in the right-hand column, in a different, hurried ink, annotations:

*A. Okonkwo — lost, day 3, swell high. Debt transferred to estate. House seized, value applied.*
*M. Ferreira — lost. Payment to widow, 40 crowns, condition of silence.*
*T. Anselm — lost. No payment. Family absconded (see disposal).*

*Absconded.* A drowned man, recorded as a runaway debtor.

Marchand read it twice. Then he did the thing that historians are trained never to do: he stopped being a historian. For one long moment he was simply a man standing in the smoke-smell of a burned town, holding a piece of a murdered book, understanding that the choice in front of him was not between careers. It was between fires. There was the fire behind him, which had been ordered, and there was another fire, the one that burns in a manuscript when a writer finally tells the truth in it, and he could feed either one, but not both.

He thought of his forty hollow pages. He thought of the young diver with the locked-room voice, and of Diallo's pencil-scratched count of sixty-one, and of the widow who had watched the flames like a woman auditing heaven.

He folded the page once, cleanly, and put it inside his coat, against his breast pocket, where a man carries letters from home.

"Sir?" Brasse, materializing. "Did you get everything? For the record?"

Marchand looked at the smoldering pyre, at the soldiers raking ash, at the town already turning back toward its work with faces smoothed over like water after a stone has sunk.

"Everything," he said. "I have everything I need."

What he needed was not in his notebook. What he needed weighed almost nothing—one torn leaf, sixty-one penciled names, and the growing, terrifying, exhilarating suspicion that somewhere in Iloran, despite the seals and the soldiers and the sanitized histories, someone had copied that ledger before it burned. Someone had stood in the dark and written it all down again, name by name, because paper burns unevenly but memory, properly tended, burns not at all.

That evening he opened the official draft, read the first sentence—*a stabilizing force*—and did not cross it out. Instead he laid a fresh sheet beside it and wrote a heading he had promised himself he would never use:

*Appendix: True Register of the Lost and Their Debts.*

Then came a knock at his door—two knocks, pause, one knock—and a note slid underneath, unsigned, in a careful hand:

*If you mean to print what you have, there is more than a page. Come alone. Bring nothing but your name. — A friend of the orchard.*

Marchand sat for a long while in the lamplight, the burnt page on his desk and the note in his hand, listening to the harbor lamps being lit outside, smelling the oil.

Then he put on his coat.
