## 19. Testimony

Edouard Marchand had been a historian long enough to know that history rarely announced itself. It arrived instead in the form of a clerk's cough, a misfiled page, a colonel who wanted his name spelled correctly. So when the door of his requisitioned office opened without a knock on the third morning after the burning of the counting house, he did not look up from the sanctioned draft.

"Office hours are posted," he said. "They are, I admit, generous."

"They are not for me."

He knew the voice before he raised his eyes. He had heard it once before, in this same room, giving answers so carefully empty that they might have been woven from fog. The diver. Okonkwo. He stood now just inside the doorway, and he was carrying something wrapped in sacking that he set down on Marchand's desk with the deliberate care of a man laying down something heavier than its weight.

Marchand looked at it. Then at the diver. Then at the door, which remained open onto a corridor full of soldiers who would be very interested to know what had just been placed on the desk of the victors' historian by an indentured native witness two days after the company archives had gone up in smoke.

"You understand," Marchand said quietly, "that I am required to report this conversation."

"Yes," said Vesper Okonkwo. "That is why I am here. Report it. All of it. Every word."

And he unwrapped the sacking.

What lay beneath was not one book but many—a sheaf of copybooks sewn together with waxed thread, pages dense with figures in several hands, some childish, some careful, one hand shaking so badly the numbers seemed to shiver on the page. Beside them: loose sheets covered in names. Hundreds of names. And beneath those, a signed confession on counting house letterhead, dated, witnessed at the bottom by an old woman's mark—an X beside the printed word *orchard*—and by a soldier's signature that made Marchand's breath stop in his throat.

*Amara Diallo.*

He looked up again. The diver stood with his hands at his sides, and there was nothing empty in him now. Ten years of silence, Marchand thought absurdly, like watching a dam decide.

"The original burned yesterday," Vesper said. "You watched it burn. You were ordered to watch it burn, and to write that what burned was paper of no consequence. I am here to tell you what it was."

"Mr. Okonkwo—"

"My father's name is in there." He touched the copybooks without opening them, the way a man touches a wound to confirm it still hurts. "Okonkwo, Emeka. Entry four hundred and eleven. Not *lost at sea*. *Debt settled by estate seizure.* They took my mother's house the week he drowned. That is what burned yesterday, and you were sent here to make sure no one remembered it correctly."

Marchand had prepared, over the course of his career, a great many responses to difficult moments. He had deployed them in ministries and archives and once, memorably, at a dinner party where a general's widow had accused him of slandering her late husband's cavalry. None of them came. What came instead was the memory of Sergeant Diallo sliding a scrap of paper across a table three nights ago—the true casualty count, contradicted by everything he'd been paid to print—and saying, in her flat conscript's voice: *You're going to have to choose eventually, historian. Everyone does.*

"I am listening," he said, and reached for a pen.

"No." Vesper put his dark hand flat on the desk, over the books. "Not your pen first. Your ears first. You will write after. I am going to say the names. All of them. It will take hours, and you are going to sit there and hear every one, because that is what the fire was supposed to prevent, and because—" and here his voice did something complicated, cracked and steadied itself, "—because no one has ever said them out loud. Not once. Not their families, not their priests, not the men who counted them. They lived as debts and died as entries, and if I do not say them aloud to a man whose job is writing things down, then the fire wins."

The office was very quiet. Somewhere below, a cart rattled past on stones; the army had been hauling ash out of the counting house ruins all morning.

"There are soldiers in that corridor," Marchand said. It was not a refusal. It was a logistics question, and both of them knew it.

"Then close your door," said Vesper, "and I will speak quietly."

Marchand rose, crossed the room, and shut the door. His heart was behaving disgracefully. He noticed, distantly, that his hands were not.

* * *

It began badly, which is to say it began honestly.

Vesper stood—he refused the chair—and opened the first copybook, and read. But reading was not enough; within ten names he abandoned the book entirely and spoke from memory, and it was then Marchand understood what he was actually witnessing. This was not recitation. It was resurrection. Each name came with its freight: *Bello, Yusuf, entered 1839, drowned off the second reef, age nineteen. His debt passed to his brother, who dove the same reef the following spring.* Each one delivered in a voice pitched low and even, the voice of a man who had learned that survival meant silence and was now spending a decade's savings of it all at once.

Hours, the boy had said. Marchand glanced at the window. The light was already changing toward afternoon.

Somewhere in the second hour, a fist struck the door. "Marchand! Open up!"

He knew that tread. Captain Verhoeven, charter enforcement, a man who believed enthusiasm could substitute for competence. Marchand crossed the room in three strides and opened the door only wide enough for his own shoulders.

"Captain. Forgive the mess, I'm drafting."

Verhoeven peered past him. "I heard voices."

"My own," said Marchand, with the serene confidence of a liar who had rehearsed. "I read my drafts aloud. A habit from the Sorbonne. My colleagues found it tiresome; the dead find it flattering."

Something flickered across Verhoeven's face—the expression of a man trying to determine whether he'd been insulted and failing for lack of the necessary education. "The diver boy was seen coming up the stairs. The indentured one. Company property."

"He was summoned for supplementary testimony," Marchand said smoothly, "regarding harbor labor practices. You may verify with the charter office, though I'd advise against interrupting me mid-deposition; it doubles the paperwork, and the paperwork comes to *you*, Captain."

The magic word. Verhoeven's face performed the small tragedy of a man confronted with forms. He withdrew. Marchand closed the door, leaned against it, and discovered that Vesper Okonkwo had not stopped speaking through any of it—not one syllable lost, not one name skipped. The boy simply raised his voice slightly while the door was open, as if daring the corridor to listen, and lowered it again when it shut.

"That was either very brave or very foolish," Marchand murmured, returning to his chair.

"It was Mama Sefu's idea," said Vesper. "She says a lie told calmly is worth ten told loudly. She also says historians are like fig trees—all shade and no fruit until someone shakes the branch." A ghost of something crossed his face that was almost, impossibly, a smile. "She has opinions about you specifically. She says you have the eyes of a man reading two books at once."

Marchand thought of the sanctioned draft sitting on the side table, and the scrap of Diallo's figures folded inside it, and said nothing, because the old woman was evidently a witch.

* * *

By the fourth hour the light had gone amber and Marchand's pen had fallen behind. He had stopped trying to transcribe every entry and begun instead marking the structure—the pattern of payments, the dates clustered suspiciously around storm seasons when deaths ran high and insurance claims ran low, the recurring notation in the margin of certain pages that Ilya Petrov must have copied in his shaking hand: *paid, family silent, see col. 2.* The ledger was not merely a record. It was an indictment wearing the costume of arithmetic.

Vesper paused only twice. Once for water, which Marchand poured from his own carafe without being asked, an act that surprised them both. The second time was at a name near the middle of the third copybook.

"Adaeze Okonkwo," the boy read. "Entered 1841. Deceased, fever, age thirty-one. Debt transferred to minor son, indentured, Meridian compound, term twelve years."

Silence filled the room the way water fills a hull.

"My mother," Vesper said, when he could. "They put her debt on me when I was ten years old. I have been diving to pay for her death since I was ten years old." He set the copybook down with exaggerated gentleness, the way one sets down something breakable, or explosive. "Do you know what I did this morning, Mr. Marchand? Before coming here? I ate breakfast. I ate breakfast and I thanked the cook. Because for eleven years, being polite and quiet kept me alive, and this morning I realized I don't need it anymore, and I don't know how to be anything else yet. So I am being quiet the way I know how—except now the quiet is full of names instead of fear."

Marchand found, to his considerable embarrassment, that his eyes had gone hot behind his spectacles. He removed them, polished them unnecessarily, and restored them.

"In my profession," he said, "we have a phrase. *Le devoir de mémoire.* The duty of memory. I have written it in three published works. It sounded very fine on the page." He tapped the copybook. "I never understood until this moment that the duty has a sound. It sounds like you."

"Don't flatter yourself," said Vesper, but without edge—it was almost tender. "Flatter yourself tomorrow, when you print it."

"And if I refuse? If I burn these and keep my career?"

Vesper considered him for a long moment, and there was no hatred in it, which was somehow worse. "Then you'll be the best-documented coward in Iloran," he said. "Because we made copies, Mr. Marchand. More than you'll ever find. The fire already lost. The only question left is whether you were standing with the fire or against it when it lost."

There it was. The branch, shaken. Marchand looked at the sanctioned draft on the side table—three hundred pages of benevolent company, grateful divers, regrettable but isolated accidents—and felt it become, in a single instant, kindling.

* * *

The sun was setting when the last name was spoken. Vesper closed the final copybook and stood swaying slightly, wrung out, ten years leaving him in a single day. Marchand's hand ached. His inkwells were empty. He had filled seventeen pages of notes in shorthand that no clerk but himself could read, and he looked at them and felt the strangest sensation: that for the first time in his professional life, he had written something true.

A soft knock. Two taps, evenly spaced—the rhythm of a soldier, but a particular soldier. Marchand opened the door on Amara Diallo, who took in the scene in one glance: the exhausted diver, the littered desk, the historian with ink to his knuckles.

"The shift changed an hour ago," she said. "Verhoeven's been asking why your lamp burned all day. I told him you were a slow writer." Her eyes went to Vesper, and something passed between them that Marchand, watching, could not parse and did not try to. "The boy needs feeding. There's a mess tent that owes me a favor."

"He also needs an escort home," Marchand said, "and I need—" He stopped. Started again, and heard his voice change into something he didn't recognize, something younger than the ministry and older than the draft. "I need a day. Perhaps two. To do this properly."

Diallo nodded slowly. "The reckoning hearings start next week. The commission's already here—nobody told you?" A thin smile. "Nobody tells historians anything, apparently. They're collecting testimony on the company charter. If a certain historian were to present certain documents, in certain order, with certain witnesses..." She let it hang.

"With witnesses," Marchand repeated, looking at Vesper, "who can recite four hundred names from memory."

"Four hundred and six," said Vesper. He gathered the copybooks into his arms, then paused at the threshold. He did not turn around, but Marchand saw his shoulders square, and heard him speak the last words of the day quietly, to the doorframe, to the corridor, to the ruined counting house beyond it, to a drowned man named Emeka:

"Papa. Someone wrote it down."

Then he was gone, swallowed by Diallo's escort, and Marchand stood alone in his office among the wreckage of the sanctioned draft.

He picked it up. Three hundred pages. He carried it to the stove in the corner—the office was cold in the evenings now—and stood holding it over the grate for a long moment, savoring the choice, turning it over like a coin.

Then Edouard Marchand, historian of the victors, fed the official history to the fire, page by patient page, and sat down at his desk with fresh ink to begin the true account.

Outside, the last light left the harbor, and somewhere across town, in an orchard that smelled of figs and woodsmoke, a boy was eating supper with soldiers and telling an old woman, at last, everything.
