## 20. The Reckoning of Names

The printing house on Cordwainer Lane smelled of ink and hot metal and, faintly, of the sea that never quite left anything in Iloran. Edouard Marchand stood at the compositor's shoulder and watched the galley come together, line by line, letter by letter, until the page before him read:

*Appendix C: Register of Divers Lost Under Company Contract, 1841–1853, With Restored Names.*

Restored. He had argued over that word for two days with himself alone, pacing his rented rooms while the gulls argued outside the window. Struck-through was what the ledger had called them. Absconded was what the company had called them. Lost was what the town had been permitted to call them. But restored—restored was what he intended them to be.

"There," said the compositor, a broad woman named Hetta whose forearms were black to the elbow with a decade of ink. "Last sheet of Appendix C. Want to read it through before it locks?"

He did not want to. He made himself do it anyway.

One hundred and nine names. He had counted them so many times he could recite them like a rosary, though they were not his rosary and never would be. Okonkwo, Tomas — *lost at sea, debt settled by estate seizure*. The entry Vesper Okonkwo had laid on Marchand's desk beside a copied ledger heavier than any Bible, and then spoken aloud for six hours while Sergeant Diallo guarded the door and Marchand's sanctioned draft sat in the wastebasket where it belonged.

"Lock it," Marchand said.

Hetta locked it. Somewhere in the back of the shop the press began its slow iron heartbeat, and Marchand thought: *now it is out of my hands, and into everyone's.*

It was the strangest feeling of his professional life, and he had once published an account of a siege that got him chased through three provinces by men with very poor tempers.

* * *

Vesper heard the presses from the orchard.

Mama Sefu heard them too, or claimed to. She was up her ladder in the fig rows with a basket hooked over one arm, and she called down without turning around: "That's your book being born, boy. Go listen to it."

"It's not my book."

"It's got your father's name in it and your mouth behind it. That makes it yours enough." She plucked a fig, tested it with her thumb, dropped it in the basket. "Bring me down those shears before you go, and don't let them give you any of that official paper nonsense. If they hand you a form, ask them who signs the forms for the drowned."

The shears were handed down. The fig basket was set under the wall where it always went. And then Vesper walked down through Iloran with his hands in his pockets because if he did not keep them there they would shake, and he did not want anyone seeing them shake, least of all himself.

The printing house door stood open. Inside, Hetta looked him up and down with the frank appraisal of a woman who judged people by whether they'd stain.

"You're Okonkwo," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Hm." She jerked her chin toward the press. "Marchand's been standing there since dawn like a man outside a church. Go stand next to him. Misery likes witnesses."

Marchand did look like a man outside a church — pale, rumpled, holding his hat as though he'd forgotten hats went on heads. When he saw Vesper he straightened, and something moved across his face that might have been relief and might have been apology and was probably both.

"The first copies come off within the hour," Marchand said. "I wanted—" He stopped. Started again, which Vesper had learned was how honest sentences began with this man. "I wanted you to see it before anyone else. It seemed right that you should."

The press fed another sheet through its rollers. It came out warm. Marchand took it carefully, the way Mama Sefu took eggs from under a brooding hen, and held it so Vesper could read.

*Appendix C.* Column after column. Names, dates, vessels, causes — *causes*, plural now, no longer only the sea but also the rope, the cold, the company's arithmetic. And beside each name, in smaller type, the disposition of the estate: seized, auctioned, transferred, forgiven. Forgiven appeared exactly four times. Vesper had checked those four names himself, twice, against Ilya's reconstructed figures.

"Tomas Okonkwo," Marchand read quietly, "page forty-one." He offered the sheet.

Vesper took it. The paper was still warm from the press, and his father's name sat in the middle of it in clean black type, unstruck, unhidden, spelled correctly. He had imagined this moment for weeks and found, standing in it, that he had nothing prepared. No speech. No great feeling. Just warmth in his hands and a strange urge to laugh.

"He'd have hated it," Vesper said finally. "Being in a government document. He couldn't sign his own name. He'd have said paper was how they caught you."

"Paper is also how we caught *them*," said Marchand.

"That too." Vesper turned the sheet over. Blank on the back. Waiting. That felt about right.

Hetta cleared her throat. "First bound copy comes off the line tonight. You'll want it, I expect."

"I'll want twelve," Vesper said. "One for the orchard. One for the fish market wall. One for every family that gets called to a hearing, so they can walk in knowing the record already agrees with them."

Hetta grinned like a shark. "Twelve it is. First customer I've ever had order books before they existed."

* * *

The charter revocation came down on a Tuesday, read aloud from the harbor steps by a colonial assessor with a voice like wet gravel and a delivery like a man reading his own grocery list. Vesper stood in the crowd with Mama Sefu on one side and little Adaeze — daughter of diver Joseph Mbah, struck through in 1849 — on the other, and listened to eleven years of the Meridian Trading Company dissolved in four minutes flat.

"—all assets held in trust pending restitution proceedings—"

A cheer went up. It started small, near the fish stalls, and rolled outward through the crowd like weather. Adaeze grabbed Vesper's sleeve with both hands and jumped. Mama Sefu did not cheer. She folded her arms and watched the assessor with an expression Vesper had learned to fear slightly less than he used to but not much.

"Trust," she said, when the crowd noise allowed. "*In trust.* You know what happened last time the company held things 'in trust,' boy?"

"I was there."

"Mm." She unfolded her arms. "Well. Then let's go make sure they hold it properly this time."

The restitution hearings opened the following month in the old customs hall, before a panel of three: the assessor, a judge sent from the capital, and — to everyone's surprise including his own — Edouard Marchand, whose appendix had apparently become required reading in certain offices. The families came one at a time. Some came with documents. Most came with memory, which was precisely why Vesper and the others had done their work, and why the printed ledger lay on a table in the corner of the hall like a witness that never slept.

Ilya Petrov testified on the fourth day.

He had cleaned himself up for it — coat brushed, hair attempted, boots polished to a shine that fooled nobody about the rest of him — and he walked to the table like a man walking to a scaffold he had personally requested. His voice shook. His figures did not. He recited the second column from memory: payments, dates, recipients, sums, the quiet money that had bought the silence of forty families. When he finished, the hall was so quiet Vesper could hear the harbor bells.

"Why?" asked the judge. "Why speak now?"

Ilya's hands were flat on the table. He looked at them as though checking they were still his.

"Because I wrote 'absconded' beside a dead man's name and went home to dinner," he said. "And because a boy fetched me rope once and looked at the ledger longer than he should have been allowed, and I let him. That look has been following me for a year. This is the only way I know to put it down."

They gave him eighteen months. Conspiracy, falsification of records, embezzlement — the charges were real; the money had passed through his hands, however unwillingly, and Ilya insisted on pleading guilty to all of it, refusing every lawyer's suggestion that he might shift blame upward.

"They can have my freedom," he told Vesper through the bars, on the last visiting day before transport to the capital jail. "I've been unfree for years anyway. At least this way it's honest." He smiled, thin and tired and somehow lighter than Vesper had ever seen him. "Eighteen months. I've served worse sentences. I once spent six years pretending to believe the Meridian accounts balanced."

"Six years," Vesper said. "You should get a medal."

"I should get a cell. I'm getting one. The mathematics of justice are improving but they remain approximate." He gripped the bars. "Listen. The hearing schedule — the assessor's clerks will drag their feet. They always do. Make lists. Bring the children. Let them watch the ledgers being checked. Nothing keeps an office honest like an audience of drowned men's daughters taking notes."

"I know," Vesper said. "We already started."

Ilya laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him — and then pressed his forehead briefly against the bars, and Vesper understood it was goodbye, or close to it, and gripped his hand through the gap, clerk's fingers and diver's calluses, until the guard said time.

* * *

The pardon came eight months later, signed by the new governor, whose position depended rather heavily on the goodwill of a public that had read Appendix C and formed opinions.

By then Vesper had a routine. Mornings he dove — not for the company, which was ash and litigation, but for himself, for the pearl beds the freed divers had leased among them, cooperative now, splitting shares on a slate instead of a ledger. Afternoons he sat in Mama Sefu's orchard with whichever children needed letters, or figures, or simply somewhere that wasn't the rebuilt counting house, which the town had voted to convert into a school and a records office, and which Vesper still could not walk past without counting his breaths.

The pardon arrived on a day the figs were coming ripe. A courier in government gray found him knee-deep in the rows, and Mama Sefu intercepted the envelope with the speed of a woman defending her kitchen.

"Petrov, Ilya," she read, squinting. "'Full and unconditional. Effective immediately.' Well." She looked up. "He'll be on the coastal packet Thursday. Somebody ought to meet him."

"Somebody," Vesper agreed.

"Somebody with a cart," she said, handing him the envelope, "because that man owns nothing but shame and good handwriting, and shame doesn't pack well."

Thursday's packet came in on a gray swell, and Ilya came down the gangway thinner than the law strictly required, blinking at Iloran as though expecting it to have changed its mind about him. Vesper raised one hand from the cart seat. Ilya stopped halfway down, gripping the rail, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

"You came," Ilya said finally.

"You expected the harbor master?"

"I didn't expect anyone. Expecting things is how I got here." But he came the rest of the way down, and took the hand Vesper offered, and climbed up onto the cart beside the empty crates like a man boarding a ship to a better country.

They drove up through the town in silence for a while. Then Ilya said, "Is it true about the counting house? A school?"

"School downstairs. Records upstairs. The town voted."

"And the ledgers? The originals, the ones that survived the fire — the ones Diallo saved off Marchand's desk—"

"Filed. Catalogued. Cross-referenced with our copies and the printed appendices." Vesper flicked the reins. "There's a plaque. *So that the record shall not again depend upon the mercy of the powerful.* Mama Sefu suggested the wording. There was some argument about whether to include the word 'mercy.'"

"What won?"

"'Mercy,'" Vesper said. "She argued the town wasn't ready for her stronger drafts."

Ilya laughed, then sobered, looking at the harbor sliding past, the boats, the water, the light. "Eighteen months is nothing," he said quietly. "But it was the first time in my life I ever paid for what I did instead of just carrying it. Do you understand the difference?"

Vesper thought of his father's marker, buried at last beneath the orchard wall with its true words cut deep. Thought of ten years of breath-counting. Thought of the weightless strangeness of freedom, which he had not yet explained to anyone because he hadn't found the words.

"Yes," he said. "I think I do."

* * *

Sergeant Amara Diallo came to the orchard in the autumn, out of uniform, with a knapsack and the careful posture of a guest who knows she may be unwelcome.

Mama Sefu met her at the gate, looked her over from boots to braid, and said, "You're the one who falsified the manifest."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And slipped Marchand the true count."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And got a commendation for it, which is the most insulting thing I've heard all year."

Diallo's mouth twitched. "Yes, ma'am."

"Hm." Mama Sefu stepped aside from the gate. "Figs are ripe. Don't pick the ones on the low south branches, those are spoken for."

That evening the whole improbable family gathered under the orchard wall: Vesper and the widow, Ilya with his recovered good handwriting and his bad coffee, Adaeze and Kofi and little Sam and the rest of the memory-circle children, grown taller now, several of them reading fluently and correcting each other's figures with a ferocity that made Ilya misty-eyed. Diallo sat stiffly on a crate until Sam, age nine, climbed up beside her and demanded she explain the army, and by the end of the night she was laughing at something — Vesper missed what — with her head tipped back toward the early stars.

Later, when the children had gone and the lamps burned low, she found Vesper by the wall where the grave-marker stood.

"I'm leaving the service," she said. "There's talk of a commission — a proper inquiry board, going port to port, auditing every company charter on the coast. They want someone who understands how soldiers are used to guard paper." She paused. "I told them I knew a town where the paper fought back."

"Did you."

"I may have mentioned names." She glanced sideways. "Yours, specifically. They'll want testimony. More of it. Months of it. Traveling, speaking, putting your face to the record in every port where Meridian kept books."

Vesper looked at the marker. *Tomas Okonkwo. Diver. Father. His name endures.* He had planted a young fig beside it in spring; it had taken, stubbornly, sending up two green shoots against all advice.

"They'll want the children's names too," he said. "The memory-circle. Their testimony is half the record."

"They do. And I told them those children would decide for themselves, with their guardians' blessing, and not one word otherwise."

"Mama Sefu will negotiate terms."

"I assumed." Diallo almost smiled. "I've heard about the low south branches."

Above them the fig tree rustled, and beyond the wall, far off in the dark, the harbor bells rang the change of watch — the same bells, the same water, the same town that had once taught him that silence was survival. But the silence was different now. It was the silence of a thing healed enough to rest, not a thing hiding. He was still learning to tell them apart. He suspected he would be learning for years.

"Tell them yes," Vesper said. "For my part."

Diallo nodded and went to find the lamp oil, and Vesper stayed a moment longer in the dark, one hand resting on the cool stone, listening to the orchard breathe — roots in ground where debt had once been counted, fruit swelling in the dark where grief had lived.

Inside, Mama Sefu's voice rose over the children's laughter, scolding somebody about the south branches, and Vesper Okonkwo, free man, keeper of names, went in to supper.
