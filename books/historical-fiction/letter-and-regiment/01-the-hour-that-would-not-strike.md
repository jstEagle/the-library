## 1. The Hour That Would Not Strike

The town of Marlow Fen had learned, over seven years of diligent practice, not to look at me. It was a skill I respected, in the way a locksmith respects a good lock. They had drilled it into themselves the way one drills any habit—awkwardly at first, then with the smooth efficiency of the well-rehearsed. The butcher's boy no longer glanced at my shop window. Mrs. Pettibone, who had once crossed the street to avoid passing my door, now simply walked past it as though it were a stretch of blank wall, which, given my shutters, was nearly true. Even the vicar had perfected a nod so shallow it might have been a muscle spasm.

I had taught them this. That is the part nobody understands about invisibility: it is a collaboration. You cannot vanish alone. You need witnesses willing to forget you, and Marlow Fen had been generous with its forgetting.

So when Aldous Crane came down from the observatory hill that grey Tuesday morning and stood in my shop asking—in a voice pitched, deliberately, too loud—for the clockmaker, I understood at once that something had gone wrong with our arrangement.

"The master clock's stopped," he said. "Dead since Saturday night. Director wants it seen to before the armistice services."

"I'm an apprentice," I said. "My master died. There's been no one since."

"You're the only hands in the fen that touch clocks," Crane said, and his eyes did the thing I hated most, which was to look directly at me, taking inventory: the ink-stained fingers, the collar worn soft, the face people described as *unreadable* because they had never bothered to read it. "Mr. Galloway. Please."

He said *please* the way men say it when they've been sent by someone else and would rather not examine by whom.

I should have refused. Seven years of craft argued for refusal—the careful architecture of my days, each hour load-bearing, nothing left loose enough to rattle. But there was a second voice underneath, the one that had apprenticed me to clocks in the first place, and it said what it always said: *a stopped clock is a small grief in the world, and you know how grief behaves when it isn't attended.*

"Tuesday rates are double," I said. "War surcharge."

"There isn't a war on my hill."

"There's a war everywhere. It just hasn't reached your hill yet." I took up my tool roll and my oil tin. "Show me."

* * *

The walk up Observatory Hill took twenty minutes and cost me more than the climb should have. The town watched me go. I felt it the way you feel weather changing—not sight exactly, but attention, a barometric pressure of eyes behind lace curtains and half-lowered blinds. Ansel Galloway, walking uphill, carrying tools, visible. It had been years since I'd permitted myself to be seen moving through the town with purpose, and the strangeness of it sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

The war had made everyone in Marlow Fen an expert in looking away. We'd had four years of practice averting our faces from telegrams, from the lists pinned outside the post office, from mothers who stood too long at their windows in black. Looking away was the town's chief industry after wool. My own contribution had been merely personal. After Mother died and Uncle Merrick's letters thinned to formal cards, after the funeral where he shook every hand but mine, I had simply... folded myself away, like a watch put in a drawer still running, ticking to no one.

Crane talked the whole climb. He was the observatory's assistant director, a nervous man built like a folded rule, and he filled silence the way some men fill pipes.

"—stopped dead, mind, not wound down. Both trains wound, weights fully drawn, and yet nothing. Mr. Prewitt swears he heard it strike eleven on Saturday night, but the wrong eleven, if you follow—struck eleven times at ten o'clock, or some such nonsense—and then it simply quit. Won't be moved. And with the armistice services—" He caught himself. "If there is to be an armistice. Talk, all of it, talk."

"Talk usually arrives before its subject," I said. "Like weather from the west."

"You don't think—"

"I think clocks stop for mechanical reasons, Mr. Crane. Rust, wear, a bent tooth. They are machines, not omens." I said it firmly, because I believed it, mostly, and because the alternative—that a clock could refuse, could *choose* its hour—was a thought I kept locked in the same drawer as everything else I didn't examine.

The observatory dome rose against a low sky the colour of unpolished pewter. Inside, the stair smelled of cold stone and lamp oil, and the great clock room opened above us like the inside of a held breath.

I have stood in many clock rooms. I will tell you what none of the manuals tell you: a master clock is not furniture. It is an animal. A tall-case regulator nine feet of oak and brass, pendulum swinging in its case like a slow heart, escapement ticking out seconds with the patience of something that has agreed, long ago, to keep time for creatures who cannot. When it stops, the room doesn't go quiet. It goes *deaf*. Silence with weight to it. You feel the absence of the tick the way you feel a missing tooth.

This one had stopped mid-swing. The pendulum hung at perhaps fifteen degrees off centre, arrested, and the minute hand stood at twelve minutes past eleven—which was itself impossible, unless the clock had run fast, and master regulators do not run fast; that is the entire point of them.

"It struck wrong," Crane whispered, though we were alone with the machine. "Prewitt says it struck eleven and then—" He didn't finish.

I set down my tool roll and knelt at the case, and that is when I saw the satchel.

It was tucked into the corner beneath the great wheel housing, canvas, military pattern, mud-faded, the kind issued to infantrymen and buried with them often enough. Someone had wedged it deep, deliberately, where only a repairer kneeling at the base of the clock would find it. My first thought—my shameful first thought—was that Crane had staged something, some test of the reclusive clockmaker, and I looked up at him with the beginning of anger.

But Crane was staring at the satchel as though it were a snake.

"That wasn't there Saturday," he said. "I'd swear it wasn't—Mr. Galloway, what is that?"

"I was going to ask you the same question."

And then, because I am a professional before I am anything else, I turned back to the clock, and I saw what I should have seen first: the movement was not jammed. Someone had *stopped* it. Deliberately. A steel wedge, hand-cut, driven between the escape wheel and the pallet—clean work, someone who knew mechanisms, or at least knew enough to imitate one—and beside the wedge, curled into the warm hollow where the weight channel meets the floor of the case, asleep among the gears like a kitten in a hayloft, was a child.

* * *

She was perhaps six years old. Small for it, I judged, though my sample of children was thin—Marlow Fen's children were also expert at not looking at me, having learned it younger and better than their parents. She wore a man's army greatcoat belted twice around her middle, sleeves rolled in thick cuffs, and her hair was dark and cut badly, in the manner of someone cut by an amateur in a hurry. Her cheek was pressed against the brass of the weight channel and had left a smudge on it. In her fist, even in sleep, she gripped the strap of the satchel like a handle on a moving train.

"Prewitt swept this room Sunday morning," Crane breathed. "Swept around the clock case. He'd have seen—"

"He'd have seen what he was trained not to see," I said quietly. "We're all trained, Mr. Crane. Four years now. The town's very good at it."

The child stirred. Her eyes opened—not all at once but in stages, the way animals wake, wary of light—and they found me kneeling there, and she did not cry out. That was the thing I noticed first, later, when I had time to notice things: she looked at me, a stranger, a strange man kneeling over her in a tower, and she did not cry. She assessed. Six years old and already fluent in the local language of looking away, and she looked at me anyway, and decided.

"Are you the clockman?" she asked. Her voice was roughened, a small dry thing.

"I'm the clockmender. There's a difference. Menders get paid less."

She considered this. "It stopped."

"It was stopped. By someone who knew how." I nodded at the wedge. "Do you know who?"

Her face closed. Not guilt—something older and more practised, a door pulled shut on tiny hinges. She looked at the satchel instead, and tightened her grip on it.

"Is there anyone waiting for you?" I asked. "Anyone looking?"

"No one's looking," she said, with such flat certainty that something went cold along my spine. Children say things like that either as lies or as reportage, and she was not lying. No one was looking for her. Somewhere in my chest, under seven years of dust and deliberate disuse, an old mechanism shifted a single tooth.

"Mr. Galloway," Crane said behind me, in the voice of a man watching his quiet Tuesday curdle, "perhaps we ought to send for the constable."

"And tell him what? A child slept in a clock tower. The magistrates will want to know why the observatory's harboring waifs during services week." I was surprised by my own voice—how quickly it assembled a lie, how smoothly. Some crafts don't rust. "I'll take her down to the village. There's tea at my shop. Then we'll sort out whose she is."

Whose she is. As though she were a parcel. As though the war hadn't spent four years teaching us all that people could become parcels, forwarded, returned, marked undeliverable.

I lifted the wedge first—I couldn't help myself, professional deformity—and the escapement sighed, the escape wheel turned one tooth, two, and the pendulum, hanging these three days at its unnatural angle, shivered and began again. Tick. Tick. Tick. Eleven minutes past eleven forever, until someone reset the hands. The sound filled the deaf room, and the child flinched at it, then settled, listening to it, the way you settle when a familiar argument resumes downstairs.

I held out my hand for the satchel. She hugged it to her chest.

"It's heavy," she said. Which was not a no.

"Then let's find out what's worth its weight." I crouched to her level, and I did the thing I had not done in seven years, the thing the whole town had conspired to let me stop doing: I looked at her properly, face to face, and let myself be looked at back. "What's your name, then?"

She studied me with those grave, unsleeping eyes. Behind her, Crane hovered. Above us, the clock ticked its wrong eleven into the empty dome, and somewhere below, far off across the fen, the town went about its business of not seeing.

"Wren," she said finally. "Just Wren. He said—" and then she stopped, jaw setting, and I understood I had reached the wall.

"He said what?"

She looked at the satchel. She looked at me. And she recited, in the sing-song of words memorised without understanding, a sentence that would unpick the next month of my life stitch by stitch:

"He said, give it to the clockman, and forget the face. Forget the face, forget the face. That's all he said."

The pendulum ticked. The stone stairwell breathed its cold. And I, Ansel Galloway, who had spent seven years perfecting the art of being forgotten, knelt in a stopped clock's shadow holding a soldier's satchel and a child's instruction, and felt the first crack run through the glass of my invisible life—thin, high, unmistakable, the sound a mechanism makes just before it breaks, or just before it begins.
