## 2. Instructions in a Dead Man's Hand

There is an art to opening other people's mail. I learned it at fourteen, from my mother, who could slit an envelope so cleanly the seal might have failed of its own accord. She called it *reading what the house needed reading*, and she did it with the serene efficiency of a woman who believed that all correspondence was either a bill or a betrayal, and that one ought to know which before breakfast.

I thought of her as I turned the first letter over in my hands, because my own attempt was clumsy — a thumbnail tearing the paper where a blade should have gone — and because she would have known exactly what to do with a satchel full of sealed letters found beside a sleeping child. She would have read them. Then she would have burned them. In that order, and without losing any sleep.

I had not slept much either, but then, sleep had been negotiating hard terms with me since the moment I'd lifted the child off the observatory floor and felt how little she weighed. Seven years I had practiced the trade of being unremarkable. A town learns to leave you alone if you give it nothing to hold onto: no opinions louder than the weather, no visitors, no face that does anything interesting in church. I had been excellent at it. I had been, if I'm honest, something of a master.

And now there was a child in my workshop, sleeping in a chair built for winding springs, with a satchel of letters on my bench that smelled of mud and carbolic and something sweeter underneath — the particular staleness of paper carried against a dying man's chest.

The child had woken once in the night, looked at me with eyes too old for the rest of her, and said, in a voice like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years:

"He said wait for the clock."

Then she went back to sleep, which struck me as the most sensible thing anyone had done in this affair so far.

* * *

Morning came up grey through the workshop's one window, the colour of dishwater, the colour of every morning since the war started, as though the sky itself had been conscripted and couldn't be bothered anymore. The child — Wren, the note pinned to her coat had said; *her name is Wren, forget the face* — sat on my stool eating bread I'd toasted badly, holding it in both hands as though bread were a thing that could be taken back.

"Can you tell me who brought you here?" I asked, for the fourth time.

She chewed. She considered. This was, I was learning, her method: she did nothing quickly, including answering, and I began to suspect that whatever had been done to her recently had taught her the wisdom of slowness.

"A soldier."

"What did he look like?"

Another long consideration. "Bandages."

"Where?"

"Face." She touched her cheek, her jaw, the place over her ear. "He said — don't look at him. He said look at the bag instead. So people remember the bag."

Forget the face. Look at the bag instead. Whoever he was, he'd understood the mechanics of memory better than most novelists do. You don't hide a thing by making it dark; you hide it by putting something brighter next to it.

"And did you know him? Before?"

She shook her head. Then, after the bread, after a silence long enough for me to reseat a pallet wheel and pretend to be busy doing it, she added: "He cried. But quiet. Like rain inside."

I have repaired clocks for grieving widows, for mothers of the missing, for men who came home without the parts of themselves they'd left with. I know the sound of quiet crying. It is the loudest thing in a room, if you've trained your ears on mechanisms. Everything in a clock tells you what's wrong by how it moves.

The child would tell me nothing else. Children are like that — they keep their accounts in a currency adults can't count. I set her up with some brass gears too worn to be useful and let her arrange them into what she insisted was a garden, and I took the letters to the back bench, where the light was worst and the sightlines worse, because I am a coward in small practical ways even when I am being brave in large impractical ones, and I opened them.

* * *

There were nine letters. Eight were sealed; the ninth had been opened and resealed with such carelessness that I understood immediately it was meant to be found first — the cover of the book, the title page of a very short and very ugly story.

The hand was a soldier's hand, the kind they teach you in the trenches when your fingers don't work right anymore: block capitals, pressed hard enough to emboss three sheets down. It began without preamble, because dying men don't waste ink on weather.

*If you are reading this, I did not make it home, and neither, probably, did the man who carried it for me. His name was Teague. Remember his name even if you forget mine. My name is Corporal Elias Brandt, 4th Battalion, and according to the rolls I died in March.*

I sat down on my stool. Not out of drama. My legs simply filed a request and were granted it.

*I am not dead,* the letter continued, *which has been inconvenient for certain parties. I was listed among the fallen after Passchendaele — a clerical error, they told my sister, when she wrote asking why no effects came. Clerical errors are strange things. This one had a signature on it, and beneath the signature, a payment schedule.*

The next pages were copies — careful, laborious copies, the handwriting degrading as the writer's strength did — of ledger entries. Dates. Names. And against each name, a sum.

I am a clockmaker. I understand systems the way priests understand sin: intimately, professionally, and with a certain weary admiration for their craftsmanship. What was laid out in those copied entries was a system. A berth on a hospital transport home cost money — real money, more than a private made in a year. But a dead man, it turned out, cost nothing at all. Strike a living soldier from the rolls as killed, and his allotted passage home fell vacant. Vacant berths could be sold. The dead man's pay stopped, his rations stopped, his existence stopped — on paper, where it mattered, which during this war was the only place anything mattered.

Eighteen names in the copied entries. Eighteen men erased from existence by a fountain pen, their berths sold to men whose families could pay. And at the bottom of each entry, authorizing each erasure, the same initials, written in a hand I knew better than my own, because I had seen it on birthday cards and Christmas letters and one apology, never repeated, dated seven years ago.

*M.V.*

Merrick Vane. Colonel Merrick Vane. My mother's brother. The man who had held my hand at her funeral with a grip like a vice and a voice like warm honey, and told me that family was the only country worth serving, and then, when I needed that country most, had let me vanish from his own roll of the living without so much as a clerical error to show for it.

I laughed. I want to be honest about that, because the memoirs will surely soften it otherwise. I sat alone in my workshop with a dead man's letters and I laughed until the child looked over from her gear-garden with alarm, and then I stopped laughing quite abruptly, because the alternative to laughing was the other thing, and I had work to do.

* * *

The remaining eight letters were addressed to eight different people — a Member of Parliament, a newspaper editor whose name I recognized from obituaries of conscience, two chaplains, a barrister, the War Office's Board of Inquiry, a woman in Yorkshire identified only as *my sister, who deserves to know I am alive even if the Army disagrees*. Each contained the same core testimony, tailored slightly to its recipient. Each named Vane. Each named dates, sums, transport numbers. Each ended with a version of the same sentence:

*I have signed these and dated them and I am sending them out by different roads so that at least one arrives. Truth should travel like a good courier — dispersed, redundant, impossible to intercept entirely. If you receive this, I ask only one thing. Read the names aloud. The dead cannot object to being resurrected; I have checked.*

Corporal Elias Brandt had, before dying of wounds he'd evidently carried for weeks, composed eight identical acts of insurrection and entrusted them to a bandaged stranger along with somebody's daughter. I tried to reconstruct it: Brandt, hiding in a barn or a cellar or a ditch, knowing the Army's own post would bury him twice, hiring or begging some disfigured comrade — Teague? Was Teague the crier, or was Teague another name to remember? — to carry the satchel inland, away from the ports where Vane's system lived. And Teague, face ruined, choosing a clockmaker's door. Choosing, specifically, the observatory. Choosing the one building in town where a stalled clock was already a scandal, where strangers arriving would be explained by the repair summons.

Or perhaps he chose it because it was far from the barracks. Or because the tower was tall and visible. Or because God moves in mysterious ways and soldiers stop believing in Him but not in arrangements.

It didn't matter. What mattered was sitting in my hands.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about silence. I had spent seven years perfecting mine — the silence of the shop, the silence of lowered eyes, the silence that says *nothing here, move along.* I had believed it was a virtue, or at least a neutral act, like oiling a wheel. But a mechanism is never neutral. Every escapement either releases energy or holds it, and mine had held, and held, and held, while somewhere across the Channel men were being subtracted from the world with a pen, and the subtraction was possible precisely because everyone in the chain — clerks, officers, sisters who asked questions and were answered with *clerical error* — had chosen, each in their turn, the quiet.

Silence, it turns out, is not the absence of action. It is a weapon, and someone else had been holding mine.

Wren tugged my sleeve. I hadn't heard her cross the room. She pointed at the letters with a finger still greasy from toast.

"Bad news?" she asked. She had clearly heard adults say this. She deployed it the way children deploy borrowed tools — correctly, but with no idea of the weight.

"The worst kind," I said. "The kind that's true."

She considered this, nodded with terrible seriousness, and went back to her garden of gears.

* * *

By afternoon I had read everything twice and memorized most of it, and I had reached the decision point that the letters had been carrying toward me all along like a weight driven up a tower. Eight addresses. Eight chances. Post them, and the truth travels — dispersed, redundant, impossible to intercept entirely. That was Brandt's design, and it was a good design, and it required nothing of me but a walk to the pillar box and a steady hand.

Except.

Except that the eighth letter — the one to the sister in Yorkshire — had come loose from its envelope in the night, and tucked behind it, in a different hand, smaller, hurried, was a note I had missed on the first reading. It said:

*Brandt died Tuesday. I am the last who knows all of it. They will be looking for these. If you have a choice, do not trust the post. Trust no one who benefits from the war continuing quietly. — T.*

Trust no one who benefits. And I stood there in my dusty shop with my inherited name and my estranged uncle and my seven years of practiced invisibility, and I understood that the letters were not a parcel to be posted. They were a fuse, and the man who lit them was dead, and the child who carried them was asleep in my chair, and somewhere in a gilded office a colonel with my mother's grey eyes was signing things.

Tomorrow, I decided — tomorrow I would go and see him. Family duty. Sunday call. Bring the good biscuits.

After all, I reasoned, as I banked the fire and hung up my apron: who in this whole rotten business would ever suspect the clockmaker of looking?

The clock on the mantel ticked its agreement, and lied, as clocks do, about how much time there was.
