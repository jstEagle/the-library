## 3. What the Family Kept Polished

I spent the better part of Sunday deciding whether to go, which is to say I spent the better part of Sunday not going, and then at four o'clock put on my good coat anyway, because a man who has found a satchel of damning letters in a clock tower and done nothing with them for two days is a man who needs either an answer or an alibi, and Uncle Vane could supply both.

The house on Corvane Row had been the finest on the street before the war and had managed, through some alchemy of grief and money, to become finer since. Black crepe in the windows, but new crepe. A garden kept by hired hands. The brass knocker polished to a shine so aggressive it seemed to dare you to touch it. Everything about the place said: we have suffered beautifully.

Merrick Vane opened the door himself, which surprised me. A colonel with two citations and a houseful of servants does not answer his own door unless he wants something from the visit, or wants to control it, and I have learned — slowly, expensively — that Merrick always wanted to control things. It was his one inheritance from my mother's side that none of us ever named aloud.

"Ansel." He said my name like a man testing the weight of a coin he suspected was clipped. Then his face did what his face always did: it warmed, all at once, like a stove when the damper opens. "Seven years. Come in before the cold gets in behind you."

* * *

The parlor was a museum of mourning arranged to look like a museum of glory. Medals in a velvet case on the mantel. My mother's photograph, small and silver-framed, positioned so that anyone sitting where I was placed would have to look at it while speaking. A fire built just large enough to be picturesque.

He poured whisky without asking whether I drank. He remembered, of course, that I didn't — but pouring it anyway gave him something to do with his hands and gave me something to refuse, and refusal, between us, had always been a kind of conversation.

"You look well," he said. "The shop suits you. Mother would have been proud — she never understood the clocks, mind, but she'd have liked that you made something steady of yourself." He smiled. "In a town that shakes apart a little more every year, a man who keeps time is worth ten who keep opinions."

"And yet," I said, "you've sent for me twice this year and I only came now."

"I sent for family. You came for something else." He sat, folding his long frame into the chair opposite mine, and looked at me with those pale eyes that had watched men die and somehow come home unbothered. "So let's not insult each other with the weather. What brings my nephew out of his burrow?"

There it was. Seven years of practiced invisibility, undone in one sentence by a man who had known me as a boy who couldn't stop asking questions. I took the coward's road and told half the truth.

"The observatory clock stopped. They asked me to repair it."

Something moved behind his face. Not much. A flicker, like a draft under a door. But I've spent my life reading movements in mechanisms smaller than a human face, and I saw it.

"Did they," he said pleasantly.

"The master clock. The one that governs the chimes."

"Yes, Ansel, I know what a master clock is; I was educated before the army ate the rest of my brain." He laughed, and it was a good laugh, warm and easy, the laugh of a man everyone liked. That was always the terrible thing about Merrick: everything about him was genuinely likable. The charm wasn't a mask over rot. It was a coat of lacquer over wood that might have been sound once and might not be now, and there was no way to tell without cutting into it.

"Trouble up there lately," he said, swirling the whisky he knew I wouldn't take. "Vagrants. Some of these demobilized fellows drifting back wrong in their heads. The caretaker woman complains of break-ins." He said it lightly, the way you'd mention bad weather. "If you're working up there, keep your eyes on your tools and not much else. There are people in this town — good people, tired people — who don't need agitators stirring the mud. The war's over soon enough. Let the dead rest where we buried them."

"Where exactly did we bury them?" I asked, before I could stop myself.

The room went very quiet. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a clock — a small one, domestic, harmless — struck the quarter hour, and I thought absurdly of the great silent machine in the tower, waiting for me.

Merrick set down his glass with exaggerated care. "That's a strange question."

"It's a clockmaker's question. Everything that stops has to be somewhere."

He studied me for a long moment, and I watched the charm work like a ratchet — click by click, tooth by tooth — settling back into place over whatever had moved underneath. When he spoke again his voice was gentle, almost tender, and that gentleness frightened me more than anger would have.

"You were always the sensitive one. Your mother's boy through and through." He leaned forward. "Listen to me, because I'll say this as family and not as an officer. There's a paper shortage, a coal shortage, and a truth shortage in this country, and only one of those is being hoarded by gentlemen who can afford it. If someone hands you a story about the regiment — about casualty rolls, about transport berths, about men whose names got mislaid — you bring it to me first. Not the constable. Not the newspaper. Me. Because I love you, Ansel, and nobody else in this town loves you enough to protect you from what happens to people who repeat stories they don't understand."

It should have sounded like a threat. It didn't. That was the horror of it — it sounded like kindness. It sounded like the voice of every Christmas of my childhood, every time he'd carried me on his shoulders above the crowd, every letter he'd written my mother that she read aloud with her eyes shining. Love and menace braided into a single cord, and no way to find where one strand ended.

* * *

He walked me to the door himself, dismissing the servant with a flick of two fingers, and stood with me on the step in the blue evening cold. Down the street, gas lamps were coming on one by one, each with its small private pop of ignition.

"He's proud of you," Merrick said suddenly. "Hale. My sergeant. He asked after you at the shop — bought bread twice this week, I hear. A good man. Lost a brother out there." He paused, adjusting some invisible imperfection in my collar, a gesture he'd been making since I was nine. "Strange times make strange shoppers. Watch yourself."

Then, as I turned to go, he caught my sleeve — lightly, the way you'd catch a sleeve if you wanted it to seem accidental.

"One more thing." His voice dropped, and for just a moment the lacquer thinned and I saw grain underneath, old and dark. "The child. At the observatory. Red hair, doesn't talk much." His smile was warm as banked coals. "Children wander off, Ansel. Children get found. It would be a kindness to everyone — especially her — if she were found quickly. By the right people."

I walked home through streets that had learned not to look at me, and for the first time in seven years I wished somebody would look, anybody, because I needed confirming that the world outside my own skull still behaved by ordinary rules. It did not help. Every lamp I passed threw my shadow ahead of me like a messenger running late with terrible news.

Here is what I understood, climbing my own stairs with my heart going like a faulty escapement: I had gone to Corvane Row looking for a crack in the lacquer, some flaw I could press until the whole gilded surface gave way and showed me the uncle beneath the colonel. And instead I had found the thing I feared most. There was no seam. The charm and the corruption were the same wood. He loved me — I believed that, God help me, I believed it absolutely — and he would have Wren taken away by breakfast if it suited him, and both of those facts would sit inside him comfortably, side by side, like works in a case.

Back in the workshop, Wren was asleep in the chair by the stove, Mrs. Odell's knitted shawl around her shoulders, the satchel of letters locked in my bench drawer under three false bottoms I'd installed that afternoon. On the wall beside her, in chalk, someone small had drawn two figures holding hands under a round white face with rays coming off it. A sun, presumably. Or a clock.

She stirred as I hung up my coat, and looked at me with those grey eyes that missed nothing and reported nothing.

"He knows about you," I told her, because she deserved the truth even if she couldn't answer it. "And tomorrow a sergeant who buys bread is going to come asking questions, and I am going to have to become a much better liar than I have ever been in my life."

Wren considered this. Then she picked up her chalk and added a third figure to the wall, small, standing between the two larger ones.

I looked at it a long time.

Then I went and checked the locks on the drawer, all three of them, and began planning how to hide a child inside a clock tower full of weights.
