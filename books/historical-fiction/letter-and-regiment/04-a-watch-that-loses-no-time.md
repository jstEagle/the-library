## 4. A Watch That Loses No Time

The trouble with being invisible for seven years is that you forget what it feels like to be seen. I was reminded on a Tuesday, when the bell above my shop door rang and Sergeant Hale walked in as though he'd been walking into it all his life.

"Morning," he said. He had the kind of face that made a lie of itself—honest brow, honest jaw—and eyes that had learned to look elsewhere. "Bread, please. The dark loaf."

I wrapped him a loaf with hands steadier than my pulse. "We don't get much custom from the garrison."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You don't."

He paid in exact change, counted out like a confession, and left. Through the window I watched him pause across the street, examine our shuttered neighbor's window as if it might contain a war, and move on. Not away. On. Toward the observatory road.

* * *

From Hale's side of it, the assignment sat poorly, like a boot worn on the wrong foot.

Colonel Vane had given the order over brandy, in that warm voice he used for orders he didn't want remembered as orders. "Just keep an eye on the Galloway boy. Family business. Nothing sinister." Then he'd smiled, and Hale had thought: *nothing sinister, said by a man who knows exactly what sinister looks like.*

Hale had been adjutant long enough to know when a colonel's interest in family was family-shaped. And he knew the observatory clock had been stalled for a fortnight, and he knew a child had been seen there—a child nobody would claim.

So he bought bread. Every morning, the dark loaf, exact change. He asked about the weather, the flour shortage, the price of coal. And on the fourth day, leaning on the counter with the easy slouch of a man making conversation, he asked:

"Heard you've got a little one staying with you. Niece? Godchild?"

The question landed in the shop like a dropped gear. I let it sit a moment before answering, wiping flour from my hands with great ceremony.

"A visitor," I said. "Briefly."

"Briefly." He nodded, absorbing it. "Children are brief. They grow." A smile that didn't reach anywhere important. "My brother grew up fast. War did it quicker."

I said nothing. Silence had been my trade for seven years; I could produce it wholesale.

He took his loaf and went, and I stood in the quiet afterward doing arithmetic. Four days of bread. One question about Wren. The conclusion required no craftsmanship at all.

* * *

That evening I carried Wren up the observatory hill myself, her hand cold in mine, the satchel of letters sewn into the lining of my coat where it had lived since chapter one of this misfortune.

Mrs. Odell kept the caretaker's cottage below the tower—had kept it since before the war, back when her eyes were merely failing rather than gone. She opened the door before I knocked, which blind women do, having better things to listen with than the rest of us.

"You've brought the girl," she said.

I hadn't mentioned Wren to Mrs. Odell. I stood there recalibrating everything I thought I knew about privacy.

"She hears the clock through her floor," Wren informed me solemnly, as if explaining the mechanics of the universe.

"I hear everything through my floor," Mrs. Odell said. "Come in, both of you. It's turning cold."

Inside smelled of bread and lamp oil. She fed Wren honeyed milk without asking, the way people do who have raised children and cannot stop the habit. And while the girl drank, Mrs. Odell turned her ruined eyes toward me and said, quietly:

"Now tell me why a sergeant has walked past my gate twice today, counting windows."

* * *

Hale wrote his reports honestly, which was its own kind of treachery.

*Subject keeps regular hours. Shop opens six, closes six. No visitors but the baker's boy. Child not observed since Tuesday.*

He read them back and heard how they sounded: the report of a man protecting something. Because he was. Because every day he stood at that counter and looked at Ansel Galloway—thin, watchful, hands of a craftsman and face of a man waiting for weather—and saw his brother Tom, who had also been thin and watchful and had also waited for weather that came as shrapnel at Passchendaele.

Tom's name was on a casualty roll. Hale had checked it eleven times. *Killed in action, 9 October 1917.* There was no body. There was no witness. There was a telegram in a colonel's elegant hand and a grave in France containing, so far as anyone could prove, nothing at all.

And now here was a satchel of sealed letters floating loose in the world, and a colonel sweating politely about an apprentice clockmaker, and Hale began—slowly, the way rust works—to do sums of his own.

* * *

Wren settled into the cottage the way water settles into low ground. She helped Mrs. Odell by describing things—the color of the kettle steam, the shape of clouds—which the old woman received like gifts. In three days they had built a language between them that excluded me entirely, and I confess it stung more than the surveillance.

"It's safer," I told myself, walking down the hill each dusk. Safer. A word I had polished until it shone like brass and weighed nothing at all.

But Hale was at the shop every morning now, and once outside it, and once—I'm quite sure—outside the church where I'd gone only to stand somewhere with high ceilings and pretend to pray. He never pressed. That was the horror of him. He bought his loaf, asked his gentle questions about flour and children, and departed, and each visit cost me something I couldn't itemize.

On the ninth day he lingered.

"They say the master clock's been stopped a month," he said, examining the bread rack as though auditing it. "Town misses the chimes. Colonel misses them most—he does love a thing that runs on time." A pause, delicate as a hairline crack. "Funny, isn't it. A clockmaker whose own clock won't strike."

"The escapement's rusted through," I said. "Parts are hard to come by. There's a war on."

"There is," Hale agreed. "There is indeed a war on." He paid. He left. And through the glass I watched him stand a moment too long in the street, and I understood that we were both men performing competence at jobs we despised.

* * *

That night I climbed to the cottage and found Wren asleep and Mrs. Odell awake, which was her permanent condition after ten o'clock.

"Sit," she said. "You walk like guilt with boots on."

I sat. The fire ticked. Somewhere above us the dead clock hung silent in its tower, and I thought: even it gets peace.

"My son Elias," she said abruptly. "They sent me a telegram in June. Killed in action near Ypres. I had the parish put his name on the memorial wall. Paid for the cutting of it myself." Her hands lay open in her lap, palms up, as though she'd set something down long ago and was still checking it was gone. "And last month a man came selling insurance at the door—couldn't see him, mind, but I know a voice that wants money—and he says, 'Your condolences, ma'am, terrible business, your lad was a fine soldier.' And I say, how would you know. And he says, 'Served alongside him. Shared a berth on the transport home.'"

The fire ticked. My blood stopped.

"Shared a berth," she repeated softly. "*Home.* You can't share a berth on a transport home if you're dead in Flanders, Mr. Galloway. Either the man was mad, or my boy is alive, or—" she turned toward me, and her blind eyes found me with terrible accuracy, "—or somebody sold my son's death, and he bought his life back out of it."

I said nothing. Seven years of craft, and it failed me completely; my silence had a shape, and she heard it.

"What's in that satchel you carry?" she asked.

Everything in me said *protect her*. Everything in me said *you're seventy years old and this will break you*. But I had spent seven years learning what protection actually costs, and who pays it, and it was always someone like her.

"Names," I said finally. "Payments. Dates. And a list of berths—one per signature. The last one's reserved for the armistice transport."

She was quiet a long time. Then: "Is Elias's name among the dead ones?"

"Yes," I said. "Mrs. Odell—Elias's name is among the sold ones. As far as these papers show, your son may be alive."

Her breath went out of her slowly, like air leaving a bellows, and when she spoke again her voice was level in the way that costs the most.

"Then you'll do something with those papers besides carry them," she said. It wasn't a question. "Because I have cut my boy's name into stone, Mr. Galloway. I have mourned a living man for five months. Whatever you're afraid of—it is smaller than that."

* * *

Hale filed his Friday report at dusk and then sat in his billet with the brandy he wasn't drinking, looking at Tom's photograph on the desk.

*Child not observed since Tuesday,* his report said. He wondered how long a man could write true sentences that amounted to a lie. He wondered whether Tom's name was in whatever the colonel was so gently desperate to find. He wondered what a sergeant's pay bought in the way of forgiveness.

Then he took out a fresh sheet of paper, and instead of a report, wrote four words:

*Search planned. Before dawn.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he folded it small, and thought about bread, and about how in the morning he would buy his loaf as always, and leave it on the counter with something tucked inside the wrapping—and how either way it went, one of two families was about to find out what he was made of.

Down the hill, in a cottage beneath a silent tower, a blind woman slept with her door unlocked for the first time in months, and a child dreamed of clocks that struck, and I lay awake listening to the town hold its breath—knowing that dawn was coming, and that dawn would bring company.
