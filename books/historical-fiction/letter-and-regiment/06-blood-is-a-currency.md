## 6. Blood Is a Currency

My uncle sent for me the way he did everything: beautifully.

The note arrived on cream stock, edged in black, written in a hand so confident it seemed to lean back in its chair. *Ansel — come to dinner Thursday. We have years to mend. — M.V.* No address needed. No question mark. Colonel Merrick Vane did not issue invitations; he issued verdicts, and the verdict was that we were family again, and resistance would be noted in the file.

I read it three times at my workbench, a loupe still screwed into one eye, and I confess that for three full seconds — long enough for a hairspring to settle, not long enough to be forgiven — I wanted it. Wanted the cream stock and the leaning-back hand. Wanted someone to say *we* and mean me inside it.

Then Wren asked from the doorway whether the colonel was the man on the photograph, and I put the note in the drawer with the letters, under the false bottom I'd cut from a drawer of spare mainsprings, and said, "He's nobody. He's a photograph."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Photographs can't eat dinner."

"No," I agreed. "They generally can't."

"Are you going?"

I am a competent liar. Seven years of practice; I can sell a dead movement as restorable, a cheap case as heirloom. But she looked at me the way children look — straight through the dial to the works — and I heard myself say, "Yes," which was true, and "because he's family," which was also true, and both of them together were the most dangerous sentence I'd spoken all year.

* * *

Vane's house sat on the hill above the parade ground like a watch fob on a uniform: small, ornamental, positioned to catch light. A woman I didn't recognize opened the door — new staff; his old housekeeper had been dismissed in the spring, Mrs. Odell told me later, for asking about the post — and led me through a hall hung with medals arranged not in rows but in *compositions*, each one lit by its own little lamp like saints in a side chapel.

He received me in the study with a fire already lit though the evening was mild. Grief had gilded him, these seven years. Everyone said so. The colonel who lost his wife and never took off his mourning band; the colonel who wrote personal letters to every bereaved mother in his district. I had grown up believing that grief made men honest, the way cold makes metal brittle — it shows you where the flaws are. It does. But it also gives a man perfect cover, because who searches a shrine?

"Ansel." He said my name like a watchmaker saying *genuine*. "Sit. You've your mother's way of entering a room — apologizing to the furniture."

"She taught me well."

"She taught you nothing, God rest her, which is why you went to the clocks." He poured whisky without asking, two fingers, slid it over. "You look like her when you're frightened. You should know that. You've looked like her since you walked in."

"I'm not frightened."

"No." He smiled, warm as banked coals. "You're careful. Different animal entirely. Frightened men run. Careful men stay to take measurements." He raised his glass an inch. "So measure."

And there it was, laid out between us without ever being named: he knew. Not guessed — *knew*. The satchel, the letters, the child, or some portion thereof, and he wanted me to know he knew, because the first move of any negotiation is showing the other player you've already seen his hand.

I drank. Good whisky. Of course it was good; corruption has excellent taste.

"You came to the observatory in September," he said pleasantly. "You've been going back. Odd hours. And there's been a girl seen on the hill road — small, dark-haired, carrying bread wrapped in a napkin, walking up toward Mrs. Odell's cottage as regular as church." He turned his glass slowly on its base. "I mention it only because the town talks, Ansel. The town always talks. And there are men whose business is listening to what towns say, and they report to me, and I protect this district, and everyone in it, including clockmakers who've spent seven years teaching themselves to be furniture."

The word landed where he meant it to. Furniture. That was the whole of my life's strategy, named aloud by the man I'd spent seven years not thinking about.

"The letters," I said. Might as well.

"Yes." He didn't flinch, didn't glance at the drawer, didn't perform surprise. "You'll want to understand them before you do anything with them. Most people don't. They see the word *dead* next to the word *sold* and they stop reading, because outrage is comfortable and arithmetic isn't." He stood, crossed to the window, hands clasped behind him — the posture from every photograph. "Do you know how many men I've brought home? Alive? Three hundred and eleven. Men whose names were on casualty lists, men whose regiments had written them off, men rotting in hospitals no one visited. I found them. I moved them. I bought their berths with money that had to come from somewhere, because the War Office doesn't fund miracles, and the families who could pay — the ones desperate enough to sell a dead son's identity to a living stranger so a living son could come home wearing it—" He turned. "Those families thanked God for me. They still do. Every Sunday."

"And the actual dead men?" I asked. "The ones struck off to make room?"

His face did something terrible, which was nothing at all.

"The rolls are ink, Ansel. Ink and assumption. Half those boys were gone before anyone confirmed it — lost at sea, buried by strangers, blown past recognition. A name on paper versus a boy breathing in Dover. I know which one I chose." He came back and sat, leaned forward, voice dropping into the register he must have used on mothers. "But you don't care about my arithmetic. You care about blood. So let's talk about blood."

He let the silence work. It was very good silence. I recognized it; I sold silences myself, the pause before telling a widow the repair will cost more than the clock.

"You are the last of us," he said. "Your mother's line ends with you and begins with me. When I'm gone — and I'm fifty-eight, Ansel, and tired — the name goes in the ground unlived-in unless you take it up. The shop, the house, the standing. Your mother's portrait back on the wall where it belongs, instead of wherever you've hidden it. Legitimacy. Belonging. Everything you starved yourself of out of pride, restored in a signature." He opened the desk drawer and set a single sheet upon it. Not orders. A simple declaration of guardianship — mine, pre-drawn, awaiting my name — and beneath it, a second paper already signed: transfer of the Vane properties, contingent on nothing but my silence.

"Burn the letters," he said gently. "Keep the girl if she's grown on you — I'm told children attach like burrs, and there's no shame in charity. Sign, burn, and Thursday next you dine here as family instead of arriving as a suspect." He pushed the pen across. "Or don't sign, keep your papers, and learn what happens to men who traffic in stolen documents during wartime. There are tribunals for that sort of thing. There are cells." A pause, exquisitely weighted. "There are cells with girls in them, too, when the paperwork requires that a certain child not exist."

I want to tell you I stood up then. That I saw him clearly and walked out.

What actually happened is that I looked at the pen.

It was a good pen, silver-barreled, and my hand knew the weight of good instruments, and some starved animal in me — the one that had eaten invisibility for seven years, the one that had watched my uncle's door close at nineteen and told itself it hadn't hurt — that animal uncurled and smelled meat. *Family.* The word glowed. My mother's name spoken warmly for the first time since her funeral. A chair at a table. Someone to notice if I stopped coming home. I swear to you the pen was warm, as if he'd held it waiting, and I reached—

And saw, in memory as plainly as if it hung on his study wall, the chalk drawing.

Wren had done it a week ago on the workshop's whitewashed end-wall, in stubs of chalk she stole from the school slate pile. She drew badly, magnificently — a crooked tower, a crooked clock, and two figures on the hill beneath it, one tall with wild hair (hers) and one smaller with spectacles and an enormous key in one hand (me; the key was the size of a ladder). Above us she had written, in letters that wandered uphill and down, *ANSEL AND WREN KEEP THE TIME.*

Not Vane. Not Galloway. Not legitimacy. Just our names, joined by a conjunction, keeping time together on a wall that belonged to neither of us and therefore to both.

That was the inheritance on offer. Not his. Hers. Drawn in dust, worth exactly nothing, and the only thing in seven years I had ever been included in.

I set the pen down. Laid it parallel to the papers, neat as a jeweler, because my hands wanted occupation.

"No," I said.

He studied me for a long moment, and the warmth went out of him the way heat leaves a room when a door opens somewhere far away — gradually, then all at once, until what remained was just architecture.

"Pity," he said. "She's a pretty little thing. Very observant. Children who observe things grow up to testify, and testimony, Ansel, is such a fragile commodity." He rose and rang the bell, and somewhere downstairs a door closed that wasn't the front door. "Think about it until morning. Sergeant Hale will see you home — he insists; the roads are restless lately. Restless roads make for restless cargo."

At the threshold I turned. "Uncle Merrick. The 311 men you brought home. Was any one of them worth a name you erased to do it?"

He smiled, and it was the smile from the medals, from the photographs, from every Sunday sermon of my childhood, and it was the loneliest thing I have ever seen.

"All of them," he said. "Every single one. That's what makes it unforgivable — I'd do it again."

* * *

Hale drove me down in a requisitioned motorcar with his cap pulled low and his jaw doing the work of a clenched fist. We passed the darkened shops, the boarded bandstand, the town learning its evening curfew. Neither of us spoke until the observatory hill rose ahead, black against a sky just beginning to consider stars.

"He means it," Hale said abruptly, eyes on the road. "About the cells. About the girl."

"I know."

"There's a search coming. Soon. I don't know when — he doesn't tell me the shape of things, only the pieces." His hands tightened on the wheel. "Clockmaker. If I were you, I'd stop keeping important things in obvious drawers."

The motorcar stopped. I got out. Before I'd shut the door he added, quietly, to the windscreen rather than to me:

"My brother's name was Thomas. It's on a memorial wall in France. I used to think that was the worst thing that could happen to a name." He engaged the gear. "Goodnight, Mr. Galloway."

I stood in the lane watching his taillights shrink, and then I climbed the hill fast, taking the steps two at a time, because somewhere above me a child was sleeping among the gears, and dawn — whatever dawn carried — was coming for us both.
