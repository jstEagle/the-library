## 7. Rust in the Escapement

I had learned to read men the way I read movements in a watch—the hesitation before the tick, the drag of a worn tooth. So when Sergeant Hale came into my shop that evening and bought a loaf he had no intention of eating, I knew something had shifted in his mechanism.

He stood at the counter turning the bread over in his hands like it was evidence.

"Quiet night," he said.

"It usually is."

"You'll want to keep it that way." He set down coins—too many, I noticed, one more than the loaf cost—and did not take the change. His eyes went to the back room door, where Wren's chalk drawing hung on the wall where I'd told myself she'd never be allowed to draw. A clock with too many hands. Both of us beneath it, stick figures, her hand in mine. "Colonel's ordered a search of the observatory. Dawn tomorrow. Officially it's deserters. Unofficially—" He stopped himself, jaw working.

"Unofficially it's a satchel," I said.

His gaze snapped up to mine, and for a moment neither of us pretended anymore. It was almost a relief, dropping the pretense. Like setting down a weight you've carried so long you've forgotten your arm is supposed to hang differently.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Hale said, which was the most honest thing he'd said in three weeks of buying bread.

"No," I agreed. "You don't."

He lingered at the counter, and I saw the war happening behind his face. I had seen that war before—in the mirror, every day for seven years, since Mother died and left me nothing but an uncle who sent cards at Christmas and a name I preferred not to use. The war between what you know and what you can afford to have known.

"My brother Thomas," Hale said suddenly, to the bread rather than to me. "Listed dead, spring of '17. Second Argylls." He swallowed. "We buried an empty box. My mother still puts flowers on it."

I said nothing. There was nothing honest to say that wasn't also dangerous.

"He writes to me now. From a farm outside Perth. Can't sign his own name to the letters." Hale's laugh came out cracked. "Can't sign his own name. Do you understand what that is? That's not mercy. That's erasure with a pension attached."

The shop ticked around us. Forty-one clocks, all keeping honest time, and not one of them could tell this man what to do.

"Dawn," he repeated. "The search party musters at half five. They'll want the tower first—it's the only place with rooms above the ground floor." He picked up his loaf, put it down, picked it up again. "If a man were hiding something in that tower, he'd best move it tonight. And if there were a child involved—" his voice caught, just barely, on *child* "—she shouldn't be there when they come."

"Why are you telling me this?"

He looked at me then with an expression I recognized from my own mirror: a man asking himself the same question and not liking any of the answers.

"Because somebody signed my brother off a roll," he said, "and I've spent two years telling myself it was clerical error. Clerical error doesn't buy bread from the same baker every morning for three weeks hoping the baker will look at him like he's worth looking at." He straightened. "Goodnight, Mr. Galloway."

He didn't call me Mr. Galloway because he believed it was my name. He called me that because he understood exactly how much a name can cost, and was choosing, for now, to let me keep mine cheap.

* * *

Sergeant Hale walked the perimeter twice before the muster, which was once more than diligence required and once less than his conscience demanded. The fog lay along the parade ground like a sheet pulled up over a sleeping town. Behind him, six men stamped their boots and joked about breakfast. Ahead, the observatory stood black against the graying sky, its dome a closed eye.

Hale had done his arithmetic in the small hours and it would not balance. If Galloway had moved the satchel—as instructed, as begged—then Hale was complicit in whatever came next. If Galloway had not moved it, then Hale was complicit anyway, and worse, a fool. There was no column on either side of the ledger marked *innocent*. He had checked.

"Right," he said to his men, louder than necessary. "Tower first. Ground floor, workrooms, then up. Nothing leaves the building until we've been through all of it. Understood?"

They understood. Soldiers always understood; it was understanding the wrong things that got men killed.

Inside, the smell of oil and cold brass. Hale had been in clock towers before—every garrison town had one—and knew their grammar: the shaft going up, the weights coming down, the long dark throat of the mechanism where a man could hide anything if he were clever and desperate enough. He posted two men at the base of the stairs and took the rest up himself, because if there was a moment he needed to witness personally, it was this one.

The workroom was swept clean. Too clean—he noted it the way you note a bed made by someone hiding under it. A cot in the corner with a blanket folded into a shape too neat to have ever held a body. No satchel. No letters. No child.

But on the wall, in chalk: a clock with eleven hands, and two figures underneath holding hands, drawn by someone four feet tall who believed the world was a place where people held hands.

Hale looked at that drawing for a long time. One of his men asked if he wanted the wall searched for hollows.

"It's chalk," Hale said. "Leave it."

* * *

Ansel

We went up through the weights.

It was Mrs. Odell's idea, though she phrased it as scripture rather than suggestion. "My Thomas used to say the tower's got two ways in and one way out," she told me, tying Wren's bootlaces with fingers that read the knots by touch. "And the way out is the way nobody watches, because nobody wants to stand under a falling ton of iron."

So at four in the morning, with the fog still thick enough to drink, I carried Wren up the maintenance ladder inside the weight shaft—a wooden gallery bolted to the stone beside the great descending drives, close enough that I could feel them breathing, the slow groan of cable and drum. Wren rode against my chest in a sling Mrs. Odell had sewn from a flour sack, her breath warm on my collar, absolutely silent. Children learn silence the way soldiers do, I suppose. Someone teaches them without meaning to.

At the top of the shaft, behind the great wheel housing, there was a space the builders had left for a man with a lantern and a wrench. I had found it years ago, when the clock was merely broken and not yet a refuge, and I had spent two nights making it ready: a blanket, a flask, a candle stub in a tin because I would not risk even that much flame near the mechanism. And the satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, wedged where the beam met the joist—not hidden so much as *installed*, given a home among the works, as if the truth were simply another component of the machine, waiting for its hour.

I set Wren down on the blanket. She looked up at me with those grave dark eyes and whispered, "Are we hiding?"

"For a little while."

"Is it a game?"

"Yes," I said, and hated how easily the lie came. Seven years of practice. "The game is quiet. Whoever stays quietest longest wins."

"I'm good at quiet," she said, with a small fierce pride that broke something in me I'd thought already broken.

"I know you are." I touched her hair. "Mrs. Odell will come for you when the game's done. She knows the ladder. You remember the ladder."

"I remember."

"And Wren—if anyone finds you, you're Mrs. Odell's granddaughter visiting. Nothing else. Forget everything else."

She considered this with the terrible seriousness of the very young. "Like forgetting faces?"

My blood went cold. "What?"

"The paper said forget the face." She traced a circle in the air with one finger. "On my paper. When I came here."

I had never shown her the note. I had never told her about the note. Which meant the note had been written *to her*, or she had read it herself, four years old and literate enough—or someone had read it to her before carrying her through the dark to my door.

"Forget the face," she repeated, patient, as if instructing a slow pupil. "Whose face, Ansel?"

"I don't know," I said. And then, because lying to her had begun to taste like rust: "Maybe mine."

She shook her head, satisfied, and burrowed into the blanket. "Yours isn't forgettable," she informed me, and closed her eyes.

Below, faint through forty feet of stone and timber, I heard the search party arrive. Boots. Voices. Hale's voice, pitched to carry orders and nothing else.

I pressed my back against the wheel housing and waited, one hand on the oilcloth bundle, one hand on the sleeping child, while the machinery of my uncle's empire climbed the stairs to look for both.

* * *

Hale

The upper chamber smelled of dust and candle smoke, and it was empty.

Empty, except for the master clock itself, ticking away overhead with the patience of mountains, and the weight shaft breathing its slow iron breath at the chamber's edge. Hale posted his men to the corners and walked the room slowly, letting his torch find what it was supposed to find: bare floorboards, a workbench, tools racked in order, nothing.

A corporal poked into the caretaker's storeroom below. Another tested floorboards with a bayonet point. One of them, a private named Cully, drifted toward the weight shaft and stood at its mouth, peering up into the dark.

"What's up there, Sarge?"

"Cables," Hale said. "Drums. Enough iron falling to cut a man in half. You want to climb it, write to your mother first."

Cully laughed and stepped back, and Hale turned away before his face could report him. That was the trick of it, he was learning—the body keeps its own ledger, and every lie is a withdrawal. His voice had been steady. His hand on the torch rail had not.

They searched another hour. They found dust, a dead mouse, and a copy of the parish gazette folded to the shipping news. At seven minutes past six, Hale called it.

"Nothing here," he reported by telephone to the adjutant's office, and heard Vane himself come onto the line, smooth as oiled brass. "Nothing? You're certain, Sergeant."

"Certain, sir. Deserters haven't used the tower. Dust undisturbed."

A pause on the line, long enough to measure. Then, warmly: "Diligent work, Hale. The Colonel is pleased. Come by the house this afternoon—we'll discuss your advancement."

Hale set down the receiver and stood a moment in the gray light of the telephone alcove, feeling the word *advancement* settle on him like snow. Promotion. A career purchased with a blind eye, payable in installments, interest compounding.

His brother had a farm outside Perth and no name to sign. Somewhere in this town a child was learning to play a game called Quiet. And Colonel Merrick Vane was pleased with him.

Hale looked out the window toward the observatory dome, just visible above the rooftops, and gave it—stupidly, privately, like a boy touching wood—a small nod. *Hold*, he told it. *Hold till eleven.*

Then he buttoned his coat and went to collect his reward.

* * *

Ansel

They left at noon. I heard the last boots descend, heard Hale dismiss his men in the yard, and then I climbed down the shaft with shaking arms and lifted Wren out of the dark, and she blinked at the daylight and announced, "I won."

"You won," I agreed, and my voice did something embarrassing, and I let it.

That afternoon I sat at the workbench with the letters spread before me, and I did the arithmetic Hale couldn't. The search had failed—but searches that fail get retried, and next time the searcher wouldn't be a man who owed his brother's ghost a debt. Next time it would be someone thorough, or worse, someone sincere.

Vane had offered me the last berth once already. He would offer again, and the offer would have teeth. Armistice was coming—everyone felt it in the weather now, the way animals feel a storm—and with it the eleventh hour, and with it the transport, and with it the final signature on the final page.

The master clock hung above me, repaired in every particular save one: the striking train, which I had deliberately left disengaged, its lift levers resting idle, a heart with a held beat.

One chime remained to build. Eleven blows, on the eleventh hour, in front of the whole town, with the letters open in my hands.

I looked at Wren's chalk clock on the wall—eleven hands, all pointing nowhere—and I picked up a file, and I began.
