## 8. The Last Berth

The summons came by a boy in a corporal's coat too big for him, who stood in my shop doorway breathing November through his teeth and would not come inside. "Colonel's compliments, sir. He'd have you at the house tonight. Eight o'clock."

"Tell him the shop closes at six."

"He said you'd say that." The boy shifted, embarrassed for both of us. "He said to say it's family."

Family. Seven years I had survived on the absence of that word, and now it was being pressed into my hands like a summons to debtors' court. I looked past the boy at the street—grey, wet, empty except for a man reading a newspaper under the chemist's awning, a newspaper he had been holding right-side up but not turning pages of since I'd opened the shutters.

"Eight o'clock," I said.

The boy saluted the wrong way and left, relieved, and I went back to a watch that wanted nothing more of me than a clean pivot and a drop of oil, which was more than could be said for anyone else in this town.

Wren was upstairs cutting paper soldiers from an old catalogue, arranging them on the table in ranks she had invented herself—she gave them all her own surname, or what she imagined was her surname, since no one had been able to tell her. *Wren, Wren, Wren,* a whole regiment of them, marching across the flour sack toward some campaign only she understood. She did not look up when I told her Mrs. Odell would want company for supper. Children learn quickly which conversations are not for them; they learn it the way they learn walking, without anyone teaching, and it broke something in me every time I saw her do it.

"You'll be back before the lamps go out?" she asked. Not a question. A contract, with terms.

"Before the lamps go out," I agreed, and lied to her as gently as I knew how, because the lamps were already lit and we both knew it.

* * *

My uncle's house sat above the town like a face turned away from it—the tall windows dark, the drive raked, the gateposts painted so recently the smell of the paint still hung in the cold air like a boast. A sentry let me through without asking my name. That was its own kind of message: he knows you're coming; you were always coming; there was never any other road.

Hale opened the door himself. No adjutant's posture now, no clipboard of questions. He looked tired in a way that had gone past fatigue into something geological. "Mr. Galloway." He stepped aside to let me pass, and as I did he said, very quietly, without moving his lips much, "The study. He'll offer you a drink. Take it. It makes him generous, and you want him generous."

"And why do you want that, Sergeant?"

His eyes held mine for exactly one second longer than regulation allowed. "Because I've seen the other version of this evening. Once." Then he was the adjutant again, announcing me, and the door closed behind me on whatever else lived in his face.

The study smelled of pipe smoke and lemon polish, the particular scent of a room where grief has been curated rather than felt. My mother's photograph stood on the mantel between two campaign medals, and seeing her there—in Vane's keeping, framed in his silver, dusted by his soldier—I felt the old ache and the old anger arrive together, twins, as they always did.

"Ansel." Merrick Vane rose from behind his desk, and I will grant the man this: he was beautiful when he wanted to be. Silver at the temples now, the war carved into him not as ruin but as architecture. He came around the desk with his hand extended and his voice pitched to the register he must have used on dying men's mothers, warm and certain and utterly without floorboards beneath it. "You look like your mother. Do you know that? Every year more. She'd have been proud of you."

"She'd have asked why the observatory clock stopped."

A flicker. Quickly smoothed. "Sit. Whisky?"

I took it, because Hale had told me to and because my hand needed occupation. He poured his own and did not sit behind the desk but beside me, in the matching chair, close enough that we might have been two brothers-in-law at a funeral, which is precisely the geometry he intended.

"I'm told you've been keeping odd company," he said.

"The dead keep excellent hours. Someone has to."

"Ansel." My name in his mouth was a tool, worn smooth with use. "I'm going to speak plainly, because we are family, and because the hour is late, and because I believe you have more sense than your mother ever had and less sentiment, which is a better trade."

"How flattering."

"It isn't flattery. It's arithmetic." He set down his glass. "There is a transport leaving on armistice eve. There is one berth left on it. One. And I am offering it—to you, or to the child. Either. Your choice. Passage south, papers in order, a name that opens doors instead of closing them. By Christmas you could be in Bristol, or Lisbon, or anywhere the war's long arm doesn't reach. Start again. Properly. With money."

I made myself sip the whisky. It was good whisky, which offended me. "And in exchange?"

"The letters burn. All of them. Tonight, here, in that grate." He nodded at the fire as though suggesting we burn a draft of a will. "Whatever you were given, whoever gave it—they're beyond caring now. But names have weight, Ansel. Dead men's names especially. You'd be doing them a kindness, letting them rest."

"There's a name on those letters that isn't resting," I said. "Thomas Odell. Listed dead in September. Alive as of last week, unless Mrs. Odell has been writing letters to a ghost."

The silence lasted three seconds. I counted, because counting is my trade.

"You've read them thoroughly," he said at last, and the warmth had drained out of his voice the way water leaves a lock when the sluice opens. Good. I preferred the lock. At least a lock is honest about wanting to hold you.

"Thoroughly. Dates, payments, signatures. You have tidy handwriting, Uncle. Mother always said so. She said you wrote thank-you notes like a bishop."

Something moved behind his eyes—not guilt; I want to be clear about that; I searched for it and found none—but recalculation. The sound of a man re-pricing a commodity. "Your mother," he said softly, "was the only person whose opinion ever mattered to me. When she died, that account closed. Everything since has been bookkeeping." He leaned forward. "But you—you're still open. I can see it. You want to belong to something. Everyone does. Even you, hiding in that little shop with your gears, pretending invisibility is a virtue. It isn't. It's just poverty with better manners."

* * *

Here is what I have not told anyone, and what I told him then, because he had earned it: for seven years I had practiced not mattering. After Mother died and the family closed over her grave like water, after Vane sent one card and then no cards, I had learned to be a hinge that never squeaks, a clock that keeps time without being noticed. It was safe there. It was also, I understood now, exactly the condition he required of everyone around him—a town full of people trained not to look, each silence another entry in his ledger.

So when he said *you want to belong*, the terrible thing was not that he was wrong.

The terrible thing was that he was right, and he knew the price of it, and he was willing to pay it, and part of me—that small, starved, unforgivable part—stood up inside my chest and said *take it, take the berth, take the name, burn the letters, you owe these strangers nothing.*

I thought of Wren asleep at Mrs. Odell's among the smell of lavender and boiled wool, her paper soldiers ranked on the table, waiting for a lamp that would not go out. I thought of Thomas Odell's name struck through in a clerk's neat hand, erased for the price of a signature. I thought of my mother, who had once told me that the Galloways were people who kept their word even when it cost them, and who had married into a family, evidently, that kept other people's words instead—kept them, sold them, spent them.

One berth. Him or the child. Me or Wren. He had built the choice like a trap builds itself: either way, someone is abandoned, and either way, the papers burn, and either way, Merrick Vane walks out of this war with his medals polished and his ledger balanced.

Unless.

"One berth," I said slowly. "Either of us. Papers in order. And the letters destroyed tonight."

"All of it. You have my word."

"Your word." I turned my glass in my fingers, watching the light bend through it. "Uncle, I repair clocks for a living. I know an escapement when I see one. The tick that lets the wheel advance one tooth at a time—that's all this is, isn't it? Let one go, and the next follows, and the next, until the whole train runs down. First the letters. Then Odell. Then whoever else remembers too loudly."

He smiled, and it was almost fond. "You always did think in mechanisms."

"So do you. That's what frightens me." I set the glass down on the table between us, deliberately, the way one sets down a finished instrument. "I'll give you my answer tomorrow. Armistice morning. Eleven o'clock. At the observatory."

"That's not how negotiations conclude."

"No," I said, standing, taking my coat from Hale's silent hands at the door. "That's how they detonate."

* * *

He walked me to the door himself, all charm restored, the gilded man again, and pressed something into my palm as we shook hands: a railway ticket. Third class, armistice eve, one seat. "Whichever of you travels," he murmured, "the other should be ready to travel further. Think of the child, Ansel. Think of what's kindest."

It was the truest sentence he spoke all evening, and it was meant as a knife.

Outside, the fog had come down off the hills, and the sentry at the gate looked through me as though I were weather. Behind me the house stood tall and dark, every window blind, a face that had finally shown me what was underneath the polish.

I walked home through streets that had learned not to see me, and for the first time in seven years I resented it. Tomorrow the town would see everything. Tomorrow the clock would strike eleven and I would stand in the tower with a dead man's letters in my hands and a living child's future balanced on the escapement, and there would be no invisible hinges, no quiet pivots, nowhere in all the mechanism of this town for me to hide.

At Mrs. Odell's door I paused. Through the window I could see lamplight, and Wren's silhouette against it, still awake, still waiting, one small head bent over her paper soldiers.

One berth. He had offered me one berth, thinking it a mercy, thinking it a trap, thinking it the end of the negotiation.

He had forgotten, the gilded fool, that I was a clockmaker—and that a clockmaker knows better than any man alive that time does not run on his ledger. Time runs on springs, and springs, wound tight enough, break loose all at once.

Eleven o'clock tomorrow, I promised the fog, the house, the dead.

And God help us both, I intended to keep it.
