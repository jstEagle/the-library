## 9. Eleven O'Clock

I had always believed the hardest part of any repair was the diagnosis. I was wrong. The hardest part is the last hour, when you know exactly what's broken and you must simply decide whether to fix it.

At half past nine on the eleventh of November, 1918, I stood in the clock room of the Ashcombe Observatory with a screwdriver in one hand and a satchel of a dead man's letters at my feet, and I made my decision the way all cowards make their bravest decisions: because there was no graceful way left to run.

The escapement had been sabotaged—there was no other honest word for it. A sliver of brass filed to the width of a hair, wedged into the pallet where it would slow the train imperceptibly, then jam it entirely. A clockmaker's trick. Which meant someone had hired a clockmaker, or had once been one's apprentice and remembered enough to be dangerous. I thought of Hale's broad, apologetic hands and could not make them fit the work. I thought of Vane's hands, which fit everything.

It didn't matter now. The sliver came out in two minutes. What remained of the morning belonged to me.

* * *

Let me be precise about what I had planned, because precision was the only courage I owned. The master clock drove the observatory's great dial, and the great dial governed the town's electric time-ball and the bells of St. Aldric's below, which were wired—by some municipal enthusiasm of 1902—to strike in sympathy when the observatory hour rang true. If the master struck eleven, the whole town would hear eleven. Every bell, every whistle, every man on the parade ground checking his watch against the tower. And if a voice spoke from the tower gallery while that iron chorus held—

Well. You cannot unring a bell. That is rather the entire point of bells, and the reason I had spent seven years avoiding them.

I carried the satchel up the winding stair to the gallery, past the dial's enormous pale face, its hands already crawling toward the quarter hour. From the gallery you can see all of Ashcombe: the market square filling early, the parade ground where the regiment was drawn up in their dress best for the photographs they'd been promised, the little crowd already gathering because Mrs. Odell had told the fishmonger, who told everyone, that the observatory clock would speak at eleven and that anyone with sense ought to come listen. Mrs. Odell, blind as a mole and twice as cunning, had done more recruiting from her doorstep than the King did in four years of posters.

Wren was not there. I had put her on the morning coach to my mother's cousin in Harrowgate with a note, three pounds, and instructions to say she was sent by Ansel Galloway, who would explain everything later. She had gone with the terrible obedience of children who have learned that adults leave. I will not pretend I felt noble about it. I felt like a man amputating his own shadow.

The stairs behind me creaked.

* * *

Sergeant Hale came up through the hatch with his cap in his hands like a schoolboy called before the headmaster, which, given what he'd come to do, was either tact or terror. Behind him, on the lower stair, were two of the colonel's men whose names I never learned and whose faces I declined to study closely.

"Mr. Galloway." He said it the way he said *good morning* when he bought his bread: an apology wearing a uniform. "The colonel requests you come down."

"The colonel," I said, "requests many things."

"He does." Hale looked at the satchel under my arm, then deliberately out the gallery rail at the gathering square. "There's a transport leaving tonight. Last berth's still open. He'll give it to you. Or—" and here his voice dropped into something almost gentle, "—he says the girl needn't come back from Harrowgate at all, if the papers burn. Everyone goes home. Nobody's hurt. That's the offer."

"That's the offer," I repeated.

"He asked me to tell you straight, because he thinks we're friends." Hale's jaw worked. "And because he knows I won't lie to you. Not anymore."

"Then tell me one thing straight, Sergeant." I set the satchel on the rail. "Your brother. Thomas. Second Battalion, listed killed at Passchendaele."

Hale went very still. Below us, the crowd had grown; I could hear the fishmonger's bell and a child laughing, and beneath it all the master clock ticking in the room below like a patient heart.

"He isn't dead," Hale said quietly. "I've known since spring. I've known, and I've saluted the man who sold him off the rolls for thirty pounds, every goddamn morning, and smiled at him over breakfast." His knuckles were white on the rail. "So don't ask me what's right, Mr. Galloway. Ask me why I haven't stopped you yet."

"I was going to ask that next."

He looked at me then, really looked, and whatever he saw—a coward with a screwdriver, a traitor with a satchel—he nodded once, turned to the men on the stair, and said: "Wait below. Mr. Galloway's coming down presently." And they went, because orders are orders even when they're wrong, and Hale's rank bought him perhaps ninety seconds of wrongness.

When their boots faded he said, "You'll have until the quarter. Then I have to report you tried to escape across the roof, and failed, and are being escorted down under guard. Loudly."

"Loudly?"

"It's armistice day. There'll be cheering. No one will question a scuffle in a tower." He almost smiled. It was the first smile I'd ever seen him spend, and he spent it like a man paying a debt. "Make it count, sir."

* * *

What followed was not brave. Let no one later write that I stood serene above the town while truth rang out. My hands shook so badly I dropped the first letter down into the works of the clock and had to fish it out with a bent pin, sweating, while ten o'clock became twenty past and my heart performed percussion far less reliably than the escapement ever had.

But the quarter struck—ten forty-five, one clear note rolling down through St. Aldric's wires—and I climbed onto the gallery rail's step, opened the satchel, and began to read.

I read Private Edmund Odell's name first. I chose it deliberately; a town will believe a name it knows, a mother it loves, a boy it watched grow up stealing apples. *"Edmund Odell, struck from the roll, berth purchased, payment received, 4 March."* I read it twice. I read the dates. I read the sums. And then I read the sentence that the dying soldier had written in a hand like a spider drowning in ink, the sentence I had copied out and slept beside for a week:

*"Colonel M. Vane sells the dead to buy the living home, and calls it mercy. Ask him whose death paid for his medals."*

Below me, the square went quiet the way snow goes quiet—all at once, everywhere, total. Two thousand people not breathing. On the parade ground, the regiment stood in ranks while their own officers' crimes fell on them like weather.

Then the noise started. Not cheering. Something older than cheering—the sound a crowd makes when it has been holding a suspicion for years and finally sets it down. It rolled off the rooftops and came back doubled, and I confess that standing inside that sound, I understood for the first time why men go to war for ideas. It is not the idea. It is the moment the silence ends.

A door banged below. Boots on the stair, several pairs, moving fast.

* * *

Vane came up through the hatch himself. Of course he did. He would never delegate the killing blow; delegation was for clerks.

He looked magnificent, I will grant him that much forever. Full dress, medals throwing light, silver at his temples arranged by grief or a good barber—I never learned which. He crossed the gallery unhurried, as though visiting a nephew, and only his eyes moved, cataloguing: the satchel, the letters in my fist, the crowd, the clock.

"Ansel." My name in his mouth still sounded like family. That was the horror of him, always: he made betrayal sound like a christening. "Come down. Whatever you think you're doing, it's done better downstairs, over tea, with lawyers. You're my sister's child. Nothing said up here unsays that."

"Edmund Odell is alive," I said.

Something crossed his face—fast, professional, gone—and that flicker convicted him more surely than the letters. A innocent man hears a dead man lives and feels joy. Merrick Vane heard it and recalculated.

"He's a deserter by the book," Vane said mildly. "By the same book that will hang whoever's been reading other people's post."

"The book wrote him dead, Uncle. You signed it." I held up the letter. Below, the crowd saw the paper catch the light and roared again. "Two hundred signatures. One berth each. The last one booked for tonight—you offered it to me this morning. There are witnesses."

"There are always witnesses," he said. "That's why one hires adjutants." And he raised his voice without turning his head, without a flicker of effort: "Sergeant Hale. Remove Mr. Galloway from the gallery. Confiscate those papers. That is an order."

The stair was silent.

"Hale." Still mild. Still magnificent. "Sergeant."

And Hale stepped through the hatch, capless, and stood between the colonel and me, and said in a voice that carried to the parade ground clear as a bugle:

"No, sir."

Just that. Two letters and a lifetime.

Vane looked at him for a long moment, and I watched a man realize that his empire had been built out of other people's silences, and that silence, given one good reason, stops being load-bearing. "You'll be court-martialed," Vane said softly.

"Yes, sir," Hale agreed. "Alongside you, I should hope."

Below us the church bells began—not eleven yet, but the ringers climbing their towers anyway, pulled by rumor and rope. Somewhere in the crowd a woman's voice, unmistakably Mrs. Odell's neighbor, was reading my read-aloud letter back to people who couldn't hear the tower, passing it hand to hand like bread.

Vane turned back to me, and for just a moment—for the length of one tick of the master clock beneath our feet—the gilding fell away and I saw my uncle, my mother's brother, the man who had taught me to wind my first watch, looking at the ruin of the only currency he'd ever believed in.

"You were nothing," he said. "Invisible. I gave you seven quiet years."

"You gave me nothing," I said. "You took seven. They end at eleven."

The hands of the great dial above us crawled to five-to. The bells gathered themselves below like breath before a shout. And Colonel Merrick Vane, decorated hero of two campaigns, did the one thing I had not planned for and should have: he moved toward me, fast, reaching for the satchel with both hands—

—which is precisely when Sergeant Hale caught his arms from behind, and the gallery filled with boots, and the minute hand touched the hour.

Eleven o'clock began to fall on Ashcombe like the voice of God with excellent timing.
