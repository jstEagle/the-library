## 10. Keeping Time

The bells went on longer than anyone expected. That was the thing about a town that had spent four years holding its breath — when it finally exhaled, the exhale took a while.

I stayed in the tower and let it wash over me. Below, the parade ground had filled with people who had no idea what they'd just heard, only that something had broken open, and that the sound coming down from the observatory was not the usual eleven strokes but a voice, my voice, reading names into the cold November air. Names of men sold home before they could die for free. Names struck from rolls so that berths could be signed away like seats at a dinner party. Colonel Merrick Vane's name, spoken by me last of all, hanging over the square like weather nobody could argue with.

When I came down the tower stairs, my legs were shaking so badly I had to hold the rail. Wren was waiting at the bottom with Mrs. Odell's hand wrapped around hers, and she looked up at me with the grave, unblinking attention she gave to anything mechanical or true, and said, "You sounded funny. Like a bell."

"I felt like one," I said. "Rung."

* * *

They arrested him in the shadow of the monument, which I have always thought had a certain literary neatness to it, though I take no credit for the staging.

Vane did not run. I'll say that much for him. When Captain Ashworth and two officers of his own regiment crossed the square toward him — officers whose faces wore the particular gray expression of men doing arithmetic they'd been avoiding for months — Vane stood very still, as though he'd heard this chime before and knew precisely how many strokes remained.

He saw me across the crowd. The crowd parted around him the way crowds do, without being asked, and for a moment we had our old family silence back, the kind that had lived between us since my mother died, the kind I had mistaken all these years for respect. He looked tired. He looked, absurdly, relieved.

"You might have come to me," he said, and there was no anger in it. Only an old man's bewilderment at a door that would no longer open.

"I did," I said. "You offered to buy me instead."

Ashworth took his arm then, gently, the way you handle a clock you're afraid of dropping. Vane went without protest. As they walked him past me he paused — half a second, no more — and looked at my hands.

"Mind the third wheel," he said. "In the master escapement. It's been wearing uneven since '14."

And that was all. That was the whole inheritance. A technical note, delivered in the voice he'd used to teach me to file brass at nine years old, before everything else got polished over.

They took him away through the crowd, and the crowd closed behind him, and I stood there understanding that grief does not check whether its object deserves it. It simply arrives, punctual as anything I ever built.

Wren found my hand. She didn't ask questions. Children that age live entirely in the present tense, which is either a mercy or a discipline I've never managed to learn.

"Is the clock fixed now?" she asked.

"It is," I said. "It'll need winding every week."

"Can I help?"

"You can hold the crank," I said. "That's the most important job there is."

She believed me. God help me, she believed me, and I decided right there in the square, with the bells still dying away and my uncle disappearing into custody, that I would spend whatever years I had left making sure she never learned to stop believing me the way everyone else in that town had learned to stop believing each other.

* * *

The paperwork took three weeks, which surprised me; I'd assumed erasure was faster than restoration, having recently watched both performed.

A tribunal sat in the county seat. Hale testified — testified, mind you, against the man whose bread he'd bought from my shop and whose orders he'd followed for six years — and his testimony was the hinge on which the whole thing turned. He read out the ledger entries in a flat voice that never once cracked, though I saw his hands, and his hands were another story entirely. The falsified casualty records were struck down. The sold names were restored, one by one, to the rolls where they belonged.

Thomas Odell's name went back onto the list of the living on a Tuesday. Mrs. Odell heard it from the postmistress before she heard it from any official document, because that is how news actually travels in towns like ours, whatever the gazette likes to pretend. She came to the shop that afternoon. She didn't speak. She stood in the doorway with her hat in her hands, blind eyes aimed somewhere near my left shoulder, and then she reached out and touched the frame of the door the way you touch a wall to confirm the house is still standing.

"He wrote," she said. "From the transport. He wrote that he didn't know why they'd changed their minds about letting him come home."

"No," I said. "He wouldn't have known."

She nodded slowly. Then: "You'll bring the child Sunday?"

"We'll come Sunday."

"She should learn to read braille numbers," Mrs. Odell said, turning to go. "Clock faces are no use to people like me. But a watch that speaks — that's worth learning."

I watched her go down the street, hand trailing the railing, and thought about her son listed dead on a colonel's ledger for eleven months while he carried crates in a depot in Calais, alive, writing letters home that no one had stopped sending because even fraud has a sentimental streak. There is no clean way to say what that did to me. The closest I can manage: I understood, finally, that silence isn't neutral. Silence is inventory too. Someone is always counting it.

* * *

Restitution came in a manila envelope in March. A solicitor's letter, very crisp, informing me that as next of kin to Colonel M. Vane I was entitled to claim certain assets pending liquidation, including the house on Corvus Lane, a sum of four hundred pounds held in trust, and various effects.

I wrote back on shop stationery, under the little engraved clock face I'd ordered when I was nineteen and full of ambition:

*I decline. Distribute it among the families on the restored rolls. They kept accounts longer than I did.*

The solicitor wrote twice more. I declined twice more, the second time with less patience and more ink. On the fourth attempt he sent a form requiring only a signature, and I returned it blank except for one line added beneath the signature block: *The estate may consider its debt paid in time served.*

I don't know if he appreciated the pun. Solicitors rarely do.

What I kept — the only thing I asked for — was nothing of Vane's at all. I kept the letters. All of them, the dead soldier's hand and every page after, sealed now in a tin box on the top shelf of the workshop behind the spare mainsprings. Not hidden. Kept. There's a difference, and I've become a student of it. Hidden things are waiting to be found. Kept things are waiting to matter again someday, if someone needs them to.

Wren knows about the box. I told her when she turned seven, in the plainest language I could manage, that some stories in it were sad and some were brave and all of them were hers if she ever wanted them. She looked at the tin for a long moment and said, "Like the clock. You keep it wound even when nobody's listening."

Exactly like that, I told her. Exactly like that.

* * *

Guardianship was granted in April over the objection of exactly no one, which tells you something about how thoroughly Vane's fall had emptied that branch of the family tree. The magistrate — a dry, decent man named Prewitt who had bought a carriage clock from me in 1913 and never once haggled — signed the order, stamped it, and peered at Wren over his spectacles.

"And you understand what this means?" he asked her.

"That Ansel keeps me," she said.

"That is... substantially the legal effect, yes."

"Good," she said, and swung her feet off the chair, done with law forever.

We walked home through the spring streets and she held my hand and chattered about a cat she'd seen, and somewhere around the corner of Bell Lane I realized my eyes were stinging and attributed it to pollen, and Wren, who misses nothing, said kindly, "Your face is leaking," and offered me her sleeve, and I used it, because pride is just another mechanism that needs regular servicing and mine had been due for years.

* * *

Every Saturday morning, rain or shine, war or peace, we climb the tower together.

She's taller now. She takes the stairs two at a time and waits for me at the landings with the theatrical impatience of the young, calling down that I'm slow as a calendar. The weights have to be raised by hand, the great drum cranked until the cable sings, and she holds the light while I work, standing exactly where she stood the first night, all those years ago, asleep among the gears with a satchel of other people's sins tucked under her chin.

I think sometimes about the note that came with her. *Forget the face.* Whoever wrote it — the dying soldier, perhaps, or someone he trusted with his last errand — meant it as protection. Erase the child, save the child. It was the only kindness the war had left them, and it was made of the same material as everything else the war touched: disappearance dressed up as mercy.

But here's what I know now, at the end of it, winding this clock that once refused to strike: forgetting is a craft, and I was apprenticed to it for seven years, and I renounced the trade. Remembering is harder. Remembering has no escapement, no governor, no clever arrangement of wheels to regulate the release. You just have to carry it, all of it, the uncle and the sergeant and the boy who came home to a mother who'd mourned him, and the girl who chose me as surely as I chose her, though neither of us ever said the word *chose* aloud because we didn't have to. Some mechanisms run on springs. Some run on weights. Ours runs on something else entirely.

Last Saturday, at the top of the tower, with the town spread out below us getting on with the ordinary miracle of its days, Wren put her hand over mine on the crank and helped me raise the final weight.

"Eleven o'clock," she said, when the hour struck, clear across the rooftops.

"Every week," I said. "As long as we're here to wind it."

And below us the bells answered, and the town went on, keeping time.
