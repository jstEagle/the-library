## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

The bridge closed on the fourth of November, and Silas Underhill learned of it the way she learned of most things in Marrow's Ford — secondhand, from someone who assumed she already knew.

"The ferryman pulled the planks at dawn," said Corporal Reyes, shaking sleet off his cap in the doorway of the dressing station. "Warden's men came down with mules and took the whole span apart. You could hear them all morning. Sounded like a giant taking the river's spine out one bone at a time."

Silas did not look up from the splint she was binding. Her hands went on working. That was the first law of her trade: hands that stopped moving invited questions.

"Then no one crosses until thaw," she said.

"Nobody official," Reyes agreed cheerfully, and went to warm his hands at the stove, satisfied with the weather as a topic and unaware he had just read her a sentence of imprisonment.

She finished the splint, checked the boy's circulation, told him to come back in three days, and only when the ward had emptied of everyone but the snoring and the dying did she allow herself to stand very still by the window and feel it arrive — not panic, nothing so useful as panic, but a slow cold pooling under the breastbone, like meltwater finding a crack in a wall.

The wrong side of the river. She was on the wrong side of the river until spring.

* * *

By day, Silas Underhill was the least remarkable object in the garrison town. She was thirty-nine years old and looked like what she was: a woman who had spent twelve years within earshot of other people's pain. She had the flat, unhurried walk of someone who has never once been surprised by blood. Her face was the kind that officers' wives forgot five minutes after being introduced to it. She wore grey because grey showed stains least, and she kept her hair pinned so tightly it seemed less a style than a decision. When she passed, soldiers made room without noticing they were making room, the way water makes room for a stone.

This invisibility was not an accident of personality. It was a craft, and she had practiced it as diligently as any surgeon practices suturing. She had learned early that the safest place in any army is the exact center of unremarkable, and she had built her life there, brick by deliberate brick, for reasons that would have taken too long to explain to anyone and would have been fatal to explain to almost anyone.

Because by night — or rather, in the forty minutes between the last lamp going out and the first cough of the morning shift — Silas Underhill was something else entirely. She was the keeper of a ledger that did not exist.

It lived beneath the floorboard beside her cot, wrapped in oilcloth, in a space barely wider than a hymnal. Two hundred and fourteen names. Not names she had collected the way collectors collect — casually, acquisitively — but names she had *taken*, extracted from the mouths of the dying and the terrified and the officially forgotten, each one entered in a hand so small and so disciplined that the whole archive occupied fewer than sixty pages. Beside every name: rank where known, unit where known, date, place, manner of death, and — this was the part no historian would ever ask for but she recorded anyway — the name of whoever had told her, so that the dead would always be carrying their witnesses with them like coins sewn into a coat lining.

Two hundred and fourteen men whose deaths appeared nowhere in the reports. Men listed as transferred, deserted, lost to clerical error, or — the formulation she hated most, because it had the ring of bureaucratic tenderness — *unaccounted for*.

Tonight, after the ward slept, she knelt on the cold boards and lifted the plank.

She did it the same way she did everything: without hurry, without ceremony, listening to the building breathe around her. The oilcloth came away with its familiar whisper. The ledger opened to the ribbon marking her last entry — September, a boy named Aldous Wren, drowned at the ford during a retreat nobody admitted ordering, according to a corporal who had wept while telling her and begged her never to write his own name down. She had honored that. There were four entries in the ledger with blank witness lines, and those blanks were a kind of scar tissue she touched sometimes with her thumb, the way you touch a tooth that aches.

She read for twenty minutes. She always read. It was not sentiment; it was maintenance, the way a lock is oiled. A thing unread becomes a thing forgotten, and a thing forgotten becomes a thing that never happened, and a thing that never happened cannot be testified to. She turned the pages slowly, saying each name once inside her head, and when she reached Kessel Ravine — pages nine through seventeen, forty-one names in her smallest, tightest script — she lingered, as she always lingered, on the entry dated the eleventh of October, 1913.

*Whole companies unrecorded. The report says "engagement inconclusive." The ravine says otherwise.*

Then she wrapped the ledger, lowered it into the dark, set the plank, and dragged the trunk back over it by exactly the distance she had dragged it away. She lay down on her cot. Outside, the wind coming off the river carried a sound she had been hearing all day without letting herself hear it: the groan of new ice forming, thickening, closing over the water the way a wound closes — or the way a mouth closes.

Spring, then. Five months. Five months trapped in a town of two thousand souls with the men whose signatures had turned her two hundred and fourteen into nobody at all.

She found, examining herself honestly in the dark, that she was not afraid. What she felt was worse and more patient than fear. It was dread wearing routine's clothes — the sensation of having walked for years along a cliff path in fog and now, in a single clear afternoon, seeing exactly how far below the sea was.

* * *

In the morning she went about being invisible.

There was a frostbitten foot to debride, belonging to a sentry who had stood his post too long out of a stubbornness his sergeant praised and Silas privately diagnosed as despair. There was a fever case, a hernia, a boy who had fallen from the granary roof showing off for a girl and would bear the limp forever. She worked with the economy of long habit, spoke only when speech was medicine, and let the town's picture of her assemble itself undisturbed: Nurse Underhill, competent, tireless, dull as a hymn. The matron trusted her with the keys. The doctors trusted her with the truth of prognoses, because she never repeated what she heard. The officers trusted her most of all, which was the irony she swallowed daily with her tea: they trusted her precisely because they believed she noticed nothing.

At noon, crossing the yard between the dressing station and the kitchens, she saw the bridge's absence for herself.

The river ran wide and slate-colored below the bluff, and where the seasonal span had stood since her arrival in October there was now nothing but the cut stubs of pilings and, on the far bank, small and permanent-looking, the road south. Ice sheets drifted down from the north, colliding and grinding with sounds like furniture being moved in an enormous room overhead. On the near shore, a warden's man was driving the last stake out of the mud. The work had the cheerful efficiency of a job well done and thoroughly lawful.

"Shame, isn't it." The cook, a broad woman named Hedwig, had come out to dump potato peelings and stood beside her squinting at the river. "My sister's over in Tarnow. Won't see her till April."

"Mm," said Silas.

"Course, some say it's good the ice'll be solid soon. Old-timers say you can walk across by January if the cold holds. Though I wouldn't. Cousin of mine knew a man who tried it at Vell's Crossing." She tipped the peelings out in a steaming arc. "River keeps what it catches."

*The river keeps what it catches.* Silas thought of forty-one names in pages nine through seventeen, and of how many of them the ravine itself had kept, until a farmer she had never met had buried what the army declined to claim. She said, "They should put up a notice," and went inside to her patients, her heart beating with the particular steadiness she had trained into it, the steadiness of a woman who has decided that if the ground must give way beneath her, it will do so while her face shows nothing whatsoever.

But that evening, three things happened, each ordinary alone, and together like stones dropped one after another into a well she couldn't see the bottom of.

First, the adjutant's clerk came round with the winter duty roster, and Silas saw that her name had been moved from the transport detail — the runs that crossed the ice road to Tarnow once it froze — to permanent garrison posting. *No bridges at all*, the roster effectively said. She thanked the clerk, who was a boy named Pell, nineteen, conscientious, ink-stained, kind to the dying in a way that made her chest ache; she thanked him and smiled, and noted, filing it away in the room of her mind where she filed everything, that the change had been made before the bridge closed. Someone had known the roster would matter. Someone had wanted her anchored here.

Second, a letter arrived for Colonel Vane — she saw the envelope because she was sorting the mail, which was one of her duties and one of her disguises — bearing the seal of the Allied Historical Commission. The wax was deep green and heavy. She held it a moment longer than necessary, feeling through the paper the weight of whatever machinery it represented, then dropped it in the officer's tray with all the interest of a woman sorting mail.

And third, at dusk, walking back past the chapel, she met Marta Okafor coming from the charity ward with her husband's washing bundled in her arms, and Marta looked at her — really looked, with an expression caught somewhere between hope and terror — and said, "He's asking for you again. He won't tell me why. He says, *when the river closes, send for her.*"

Silas stood in the falling sleet with her bag in her hand and understood that the season had not merely closed a bridge. It had shut her in a box with everything she had spent twelve years keeping buried, and somewhere in that box, something had already begun knocking.

"When the river closes," she repeated.

"That's what he said. Weeks ago. I thought it was the morphine talking." Marta's eyes were red-rimmed and level. "Was it the morphine, Nurse?"

"No," Silas said quietly. "I don't think it was."

She walked on toward the ward through the blue hour, past the frozen pump and the chapel with its one lit window, and inside her, beneath the calm she had worn so long it had grown into the bone, the ledger stirred like something sleeping that had heard its name.

That night she did not lift the floorboard. There was no need. She lay in the dark reciting names instead — silently, in order, all two hundred and fourteen — the way other women said prayers, and she ended, as she always ended, on the forty-one of Kessel Ravine, and on the newest entry of all, the one not yet written:

*Tomas Okafor, Private, still living. For now.*

Outside, the ice on the river groaned — a long, low sound, like a door being tested in its frame — and settled, and held.
