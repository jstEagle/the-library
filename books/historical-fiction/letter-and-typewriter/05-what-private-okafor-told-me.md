## 5. What Private Okafor Told Me

The charity ward of Marrow's Ford smelled of carbolic and boiled turnips, a smell I had known in twelve years of field hospitals from Flanders to the southern passes, and which I had never once mistaken for cleanliness. Cleanliness had no smell. This was the smell of things being managed rather than mended.

I came on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays the ward received its weekly delivery of lint and laudanum and no one questioned an extra pair of competent hands. I carried a basket of bandages I did not need and wore the grey apron of my old service over civilian wool, and in that disguise — if it was a disguise; perhaps it was only the truest thing I owned — I passed the ward sister with a nod and was waved through as though I were furniture.

Tomas Okafor lay in the third bed from the window, beneath a card bearing his name in a clerk's careful hand: *T. OKAFOR, PVT., 2ND RIFLES — WOUNDS RECEIVED IN ACTION, 1916.*

The clerk had gotten the year right and everything else wrong.

He was thirty-four years old and looked sixty. The wounds at Kessel Ravine had taken his left leg above the knee and left him with a chest that filled with water every winter like a cellar in spring. Three years he had been in this ward, dying by inches on the town's charity, while the report signed at divisional headquarters recorded the action at Kessel Ravine as *a minor engagement resulting in the orderly withdrawal of all units, casualties light.*

Casualties light. Two hundred fourteen names in my ledger said otherwise. Forty-one of them had been men who walked into that ravine under Tomas Okafor's section.

I set down my basket beside his bed and he turned his head — slowly, the way the very ill turn, as though their skulls were full of wet sand — and looked at me for a long moment before recognition arrived.

"Sister Underhill," he said. His voice was a whisper with gravel in it. "They told us you were dead."

"They tell people a great many things."

"So they do." He tried to smile. It cost him something. "So they do."

* * *

I had nursed Tomas Okafor for nine days in October of 1916, in a dressing station four miles behind the ravine, while the shelling came and went like weather. I had held the basin when the gas in his lungs made him vomit black fluid. I had written his mother's address on his forehead in indelible pencil so that, if the station took a direct hit, someone might know where to send what remained.

Then he had been evacuated west, and I had gone north, and neither of us had spoken of Kessel Ravine since. Not because we had forgotten it. Because there are certain things two people who were present at the same horror do not need to say to recognize each other, the way two sailors know a storm by the way the other one's hands move.

But I had not come to reminisce. I had not come, in truth, entirely knowing why I had come. For three days since Edmund Hale's arrival with the commission I had carried the knowledge of it around Marrow's Ford like a stone in my boot — the historians installed at the Crown Hotel, the maps unrolled, Colonel Vane's staff officers fetching documents with the reverence of altar boys — and I had told myself I was merely curious, merely observing, merely an aging nurse taking her winter exercise past a hotel she had no business passing.

And then I had found myself walking here instead.

"You look well," Tomas said, which was a lie, and I let him have it, because dying men should be permitted their small fictions.

"And you," I lied back.

He laughed, and the laugh became a cough, and the cough went on long enough that I reached for the basin out of twelve years' habit, and when it passed he lay back gray-faced and said, "Still quick with the basin, Sister. You always were the fastest hands in the corps."

"There were faster."

"Not that I ever saw." He closed his eyes. Opened them again. "Why are you here, Silas?"

Not *Sister*. He had never once used my given name in the field; rank and habit forbade it. But we were neither of us in the field now, and the word fell between us like a coin dropped on a floor.

"The bridge closed," I said. "I'm stranded until spring."

"I heard. Whole town heard." His eyes moved to the window, where the river ran gray and locked between its banks. "Bad luck."

"Or good luck. I haven't decided."

"No?" He studied me with eyes that had grown very patient, the way the eyes of the long-ill do, because they have nothing left to be impatient about. "You came across town in the cold, wearing an apron you retired three years ago, carrying bandages to a man who has more bandages than skin. That's not indecision, Silas. That's a woman working up to something."

I had forgotten how much soldiers see. Or perhaps I had never forgotten; perhaps I had simply spent twelve years hoping they would not turn that sight on me.

"Tomas," I said, "what do you remember about the morning of the ninth of October?"

* * *

What he remembered was nearly everything, and he gave it to me in pieces over the following hour, between the coughs and the visits of the ward sister and a long silence after lunch was brought and taken away untouched.

He remembered the order coming down at half past four in the morning — verbal, delivered by Captain Reyes, who would die at Kessel with a bullet through the throat and therefore could not testify that any such order existed — that the Second Rifles would descend into the ravine by the goat path and hold the mill crossing against a force estimated at two companies.

He remembered that the estimate was wrong by a factor of five. That the intelligence officer who prepared it, a Major Aldous Crane, had relied on a reconnaissance flown eleven days earlier, after the enemy had already reinforced the sector. That Crane had been told this by a scout named Ferro, and had struck Ferro's report from the file, and had gone to breakfast.

He remembered going down into the ravine anyway, because orders were orders, and because the men believed — as men must believe or they cannot function — that someone above them had done the arithmetic honestly.

He remembered the machine guns opening from both rims at once, at first light, when the whole column was in the throat of the gorge with the river on one side and a cliff face on the other and nowhere in the world to go.

"Two hours," he said, looking at the ceiling. "It lasted two hours, Silas. Do you know how long two hours is? I've lain in this bed three years and I still can't make two hours fit inside anything."

"I know," I said. "I've counted it too."

He turned his head and looked at me then, really looked, and I understood that he knew what I meant. That somewhere in those two hours our paths had crossed without either of us knowing it — me receiving the wounded at the mouth of the ravine, him lying among them — and that the ledger I kept had probably held his section's dead before it had ever held his own name among the living.

"How many got out?" I asked, though I knew. I had written the number down the week it happened, cross-checked twice.

"Ninety-one," he said. "Of four hundred and six." A pause. "You know that already."

"Yes."

"Then why ask me?"

"Because I want to hear it from you. Because your voice is worth ten times my ink. Because—" I stopped. I had rehearsed nothing, which was unlike me, and now the reason arrived in my mouth unbidden, naked, true: "Because a commission has come to Marrow's Ford to write the official history, and they will write it wrong unless someone makes it impossible to write it wrong."

* * *

For a long moment Tomas Okafor said nothing at all. The stove at the end of the ward ticked. Somewhere a spoon rattled in a cup. Outside, a cart went over the frozen ruts of the street, bells jingling, a sound so ordinary it was almost obscene.

"A commission," he said finally. "Historians?"

"Three of them. Under alliance authority. They're at the Crown Hotel."

"And they'll write about the ravine?"

"They'll write whatever the army gives them to write. And the army will give them the report that says casualties were light."

"The report." He pronounced it carefully, the way a man handles a knife that has cut him before. "Signed by—"

"Hale," I said. "Captain Edmund Hale, divisional intelligence staff. He drafted it."

I watched his face do several things at once. There was no love in it. But there was something else, something complicated, and I understood it, because I had spent six years understanding the same thing myself: Edmund Hale had been the officer who signed the requisition that kept the dressing station supplied through that autumn, who had bent regulations twice to get plasma to men the system had marked as unsavable. Half the survivors of the Second Rifles owed pieces of themselves to Edmund Hale's signature. It was a strange and terrible thing to owe your life to a man whose pen had also erased the men who died beside you.

"He was decent," Tomas said at last, grudgingly. "He came through the wards sometimes. Remembered names."

"I know."

"You know a lot, Sister." He shifted against the pillows, and the movement dragged a thin sound of pain out of him that he swallowed before it finished. "You always knew more than you said. We used to wonder. In the ward tents, when the officers' reports came through and didn't match what we'd seen with our own eyes — there was talk. That somebody somewhere was keeping the real count." His eyes fixed on me. "Was it you?"

Twelve years of discipline rose in me like a wall. *A ledger is a confession you haven't been caught making.* Marguerite's voice, older than the war, worn smooth as a river stone.

"Yes," I said.

The wall came down all at once, the way walls do — not brick by brick but all together, catastrophically, with the dust still hanging in the air afterward.

* * *

He cried. I will write it plainly because he asked me to write everything plainly: Private Tomas Okafor, veteran of Kessel Ravine, lay in a charity bed in Marrow's Ford and wept for the better part of ten minutes, silently, the tears running sideways into his pillow, while I sat beside him and did not touch him because some men cannot bear to be touched while they grieve, and he was one of them, and I knew it, and so I sat with my hands folded in my lap like a woman at church and let him do it alone in company.

When it was done he was exhausted and lighter, the way men are lighter after, and he said:

"Two hundred and fourteen."

"That's the count."

"Ferro. Say Ferro back to me."

"Elias Ferro, scout, First Company. Recorded the reinforcement, reported it, saw his report struck from the file by Major Crane. Survived the ravine. Killed at Verchow Pass, June 1917. Your ledger has him."

"My ledger." He laughed, wetly. "Say it again."

"Your ledger has him."

"God." He lay back. "All this time I thought the numbers died with us. That we'd carry them and then we'd die and that would be that — the war would get away with it clean, like a thief leaving no fingerprints." He turned to me, and there was something new in his face, something that frightened me a little even then, though I told myself it was only the fever-light from the stove. "Silas. Make mine count."

"What do you mean?"

"My account. What I saw. What Crane did, what the order was, what the guns did to four hundred and six men in two hours. Write it down. Get it sworn. Get it in front of those historians before they leave." His hand came off the blanket and caught my wrist — a dying man's grip, which is stronger than a healthy man's grip, because it knows it will not get a second chance. "I'm not afraid of them finding out. I'm afraid of dying in this bed and having it count for nothing. Having the last version of me be a line in a falsified report. *Casualties light.*" His voice cracked on it. "Make it heavy, Silas. Make it weigh what it weighs."

"Tomas—"

"Sworn statement. Like the lawyers do. Date it, sign it, witness it. I've thought about this for three years, every night, three years in this bed with nothing but the ceiling and the memory. Ask me anything. Ask me the color of the sergeant's puttees. Ask me which rim the guns fired from first. Ask me what Ferro said when he came back and found out nobody had read his report." The grip tightened. "Ask me, and write it, and I will sign it so hard the pen goes through the paper."

* * *

I began that afternoon.

I sent Marta Okafor — his wife, a small iron-eyed woman who took in sewing three doors from the ward — for a proper notebook from the stationer's, telling her only that her husband wished to dictate a letter. Then I sat on the charity stool beside his bed with my knees at the height of his mattress, the way I had sat with a thousand dying men, and I asked him the questions in order, and I wrote down the answers in my flat, fast, nurse's shorthand, and the room filled gradually with the shape of a morning three years dead.

The order. The descent. The false strength report. Ferro's struck testimony. The machine guns on both rims, firing by prearranged signal — which meant the enemy had expected them, which meant the intelligence failure was not failure at all but negligence dressed as failure. Captain Reyes going down at the ford with the section colors wrapped around his body so they wouldn't be taken. The ninety-one who came out, and the manner of their coming out, crawling along the river shallows with their boots filling with ice water, because the goat path was swept by fire.

At half past four the ward sister came for the evening lamp-round and found me writing and asked no questions, because nurses writing at bedsides are furniture, and furniture is invisible, and I had built twelve years on that invisibility and had never once felt it as a weight until this moment, when I felt it begin to crack.

By seven o'clock I had eleven pages. By eight, Tomas's voice was failing, and I read the pages back to him in full, and he listened with his eyes shut, correcting me twice — the time of the signal flare, the spelling of Sergeant Brandt's name — and when I finished he said, "Now the statement. The formal one. Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow," I said. "You rest tonight."

"Rest." He smiled at the ceiling. "Sister, I haven't rested since October 1916. But I'll lie quiet. Bring your pen tomorrow. Bring a good one."

* * *

Marta Okafor walked out with me into the dark. She had sewn all evening in the visitor's chair and had heard every word, because charity wards have no privacy and she had not pretended otherwise, and at the door she stopped me with a hand on my sleeve.

"He hasn't talked like that in three years," she said. "Three years he lies there and says nothing, and I thought — I thought it was the illness eating him from inside. But it wasn't the illness. It was the silence." Her grip tightened. "Whatever you're doing, Mrs. Underhill — whoever you are, and I think you're somebody — finish it. Finish it while he's alive to see it done."

"I intend to."

"Do you?" She looked at me with those iron eyes, and women who take in sewing for a living see everything, because everything passes through their hands. "Then mind yourself. Men like the ones at the Crown Hotel don't send soldiers to do their work anymore. They send letters. They send favors. They send polite men." She released my sleeve. "My Tomas was polite once. Look where politeness got him."

I walked home through the frozen streets with the notebook inside my coat, against my ribs, where I had carried ledgers for twelve years, and I noticed — for the first time noticing it consciously — that I was no longer hiding it. That I had walked out of the ward with it visible in my posture, in my stride, in the set of my jaw, and that Marta had seen it, and that Tomas had seen it, and that tomorrow I would sit in a public ward and take a formal sworn statement naming Major Aldous Crane and a falsified report signed by Captain E. Hale, and that anyone with eyes would see what I was doing.

Anyone with eyes.

Anyone like the young ward clerk who had copied Tomas Okafor's admission card, and who had watched me all evening from his desk by the door with the particular attentive blankness of a boy who has been taught to notice things and to pass them along.

I stopped under the lamp at the corner of the square and stood in the cold, feeling the notebook rise and fall against my ribs with my breath, and I understood that something had changed in me today, and that the change had a direction, and that the direction was not safety.

Purpose, kindling. And kindling, whatever else it is, is fuel.

Across the square, the windows of the Crown Hotel burned yellow behind their curtains, and behind one of them, I knew, Edmund Hale was dining with historians, and the official record was being planned like a menu.

Tomorrow, I thought. Bring a good pen.

Behind me, far down the frozen river, the ice groaned — a long, deep sound, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.
