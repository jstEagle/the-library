## 6. The Typewriter in the Window

The pawnshop on Cordwainer Lane smelled of brass polish and other people's emergencies. Silas had passed it a hundred times in her years of coming and going through Marrow's Ford — the winter she'd nursed the garrison's fever ward, the autumn she'd waited out the rains with Okafor's battalion camped outside the walls — but she had never gone in. Pawnshops were where hope went to be weighed and priced, and she had never had anything she was willing to part with.

Now she stood before the window with her breath fogging the glass, looking at a typewriter.

It sat among the wedding rings and hunting rifles and a child's christening gown folded like something dead, and it was an ugly thing: a black iron Remington with chipped paint along the carriage, a ribbon spool wound crooked, one key — the *E*, naturally, the most worked letter in any language — worn nearly smooth as river stone. A card leaned against its base in the pawnbroker's slanted hand: *Works fine. Owner gone to the capital.*

*Owner gone to the capital.* Everything in this town, Silas thought, was owned by someone who had left or someone who could not.

She pushed open the door. The bell above it gave a single flat note, like a spoon tapped against a cup.

"Underhill." The pawnbroker, Herr Brandt, looked up from his ledger without surprise. He was a broad, slow man with a jeweler's squint and the particular patience of someone who had spent thirty years watching people decide what their grief was worth. "Thought you were the doctor's sort, not mine."

"I'm nobody's sort," Silas said. "How much for the machine?"

Brandt came around the counter at the unhurried pace of a man who knew that urgency was leverage surrendered. He lifted the typewriter down from the shelf and set it on the glass case between them, and his thick fingers moved over it with unexpected tenderness, checking the carriage, pressing keys, tilting it to read the serial number stamped beneath.

"Belonged to a schoolteacher," he said. "Good woman. Typed letters home for half the regiment that couldn't write their own names. Then the commission recalled her north." He shrugged, a small avalanche of shoulders. "People leave things. That's most of my trade, when you boil it down. Leaving."

"How much?"

"Fifteen marks."

Silas had eleven in her purse and four more sewn into the lining of her coat, money she kept there the way other women kept lockets. She counted it out onto the glass without haggling, and Brandt watched her do it, and something shifted behind the jeweler's squint — not warmth exactly, but recognition. He had seen enough people buy back pawned wedding rings to know what it meant when a customer paid full price without blinking. It meant the thing being bought mattered more than the money. It meant desperation wearing the mask of decision.

"You'll want a fresh ribbon," he said. "And paper. I've both." He fetched them without being asked, wrapped the typewriter in sacking with the competence of a man dressing a wound, and held it out to her across the counter. Then, instead of letting go, he held on.

"Sister Underhill." His voice dropped to the register men used in this town when they said things they did not want repeated. "I typed nothing myself, those years. But my wife did. For the boys in the transit hospital. Four hundred letters, maybe more." He let go of the machine. "Whatever you're going to write on this — write it true. We got enough of the other kind already."

Silas carried the typewriter home through streets going blue with early dusk, the sacking bundle heavy against her hip, and felt — absurdly, dangerously — light.

* * *

Her room above the dispensary was narrow as a coffin stood on end: a bed, a washstand, a chair, a window overlooking the yard where the laundry froze in ropes. She set the machine on the table beneath the window, unwrapped it, and then, after a moment's thought, dragged her blanket from the bed and hung it over the curtain rod so that the window showed only wool.

Anyone passing in the yard would see nothing. That was the point. That was also, she understood, the beginning of the lie — because a thing hidden was a thing known to be dangerous, and she was about to make the most dangerous document in Marrow's Ford more real than it had ever been.

She threaded the ribbon by lamplight, fingers remembering a skill she hadn't known she possessed until it surfaced — two weeks in 1913, a training course, an afternoon with a machine in a bright room while the world was still arranged sensibly. The spool seated itself. The carriage rolled. She inserted a clean sheet of the pawnbroker's paper and sat with her hands in her lap for a long moment, listening to the house settle around her, the pipes ticking, the wind working at the shutters.

Then she began to type.

The first line was Tomas Okafor's statement, the one she had taken down in shorthand three nights ago in the charity ward, his voice thin as paper but steady as a rail:

*I, Tomas Okafor, private, 4th Battalion, state as follows concerning the events at Kessel Ravine on the ninth of October, 1917—*

The keys struck. The little typebars leapt and fell like a flock of small black birds. The bell rang at the end of each line — a bright, domestic, almost cheerful sound — and the carriage slid back with a clatter, and the words appeared on the page in rows as even as soldiers on parade.

She stopped after the first paragraph and stared.

For twelve years she had written in ink. Ink was private. Ink was a nurse's hand, quick and slanted, legible to perhaps three people alive. Ink could be denied — *that's not my writing; anyone can forge a hand* — and had been, would be, if it ever came to that. Ink was a secret kept in a body, and bodies could be searched, broken, made to forget.

But this. This was different. These letters stood up off the page with mechanical impartiality. They belonged to no hand. They could belong to anyone — or everyone. A page typed like this didn't look like a memory. It looked like a record. It looked like the kind of document that got bound into volumes and cited by name and page number, the kind that outlived the people who made it.

The ledger under her floorboard had been a confession she hadn't been caught making. This was something else. This was testimony wearing its Sunday clothes.

She typed until midnight, and then past it. Okafor's account, all nine pages of it, cleaned of her shorthand, expanded where his coughing had broken sentences, marked where he wished to add detail when his strength returned. Then the statements of Corporal Reyes and Lance-Corporal Adeyemi, whom she had interviewed quietly over the past week — men whose names were not yet in danger because no one yet knew their names mattered. Then a fair copy of the Kessel Ravine pages from the ledger itself, transcribed from memory and cross-checked against the original twice, the dates, the unit designations, the count.

Two hundred fourteen. Her pen had written the number hundreds of times; her fingers now struck it key by key, *two hundred fourteen dead at Kessel Ravine, names as follows,* and the list unspooled down the page in cold perfect rows, and reading them in type was like seeing the men themselves in uniform for the first time — not ghosts in her private dark, but soldiers, presentable, undeniable, standing at attention in a language the century could hear.

Near three in the morning she fed in a fresh sheet and typed a heading she had not planned:

*Concerning the official report of the action at Kessel Ravine, signed E. Hale, Captain, and countersigned R. Vane, Colonel, which states that the engagement resulted in forty-one fatalities, all combatant.*

The keys rang. The carriage returned. She sat looking at Edmund Hale's name standing there in type, black and level and impossible to unsay, and felt the floor tilt gently beneath her chair.

He had been warm. That was the ruin of it. Six years, and he had crossed the commission office to take her hand in both of his and say *Sister Underhill, I have thought of you a thousand times,* and he had meant it — she was certain he had meant it, that was what made everything else so unbearable. Men who lied coldly you could fight. Men who had built a warm room around their lie and lived in it honestly —

She pulled the sheet from the platen, folded it once, and put it away. Not tonight. Tonight there was work, and work was a kind of mercy, and she would take mercy where she found it.

* * *

Over the following days the rhythm of the machine became the rhythm of her life.

By day she was Sister Underhill of the dispensary: dressings changed, fevers charted, the colonel's aide's infected blister lanced with professional indifference. She passed Vane in the street once and he touched his cap and smiled, and she smiled back, and the smile cost her something she pretended she could afford.

By night she typed.

She learned the machine's moods the way she had learned wounded men's — which key stuck when the room went damp (the *T*, always the *T), how much pressure the worn *E* needed, the precise angle at which the paper fed true. She learned to type softly, cushioning the space bar with her thumb, though at midnight with the wind up she doubted anyone could hear her over the river groaning in its channel. She typed Marta Okafor's first careful answers to questions about her husband's letters — *he wrote every week, sister, he wrote things I didn't understand then, I kept them because they were his* — and stopped herself from typing ahead of what Marta had actually agreed to say. A record was only as honest as its discipline. She would not put words in a living mouth. There were too many dead mouths already filled by other people's words.

On the fifth night, the pawnbroker's daughter came.

Silas heard her on the stairs — light steps, deliberate, a knock timed like an appointment. Greta Brandt was nineteen, sharp-faced, with her father's squint and none of his patience, and she stood in the doorway holding a paper-wrapped parcel of spare ribbons and said, without preamble:

"Papa says you bought the teacher's machine. Papa notices who buys what." She glanced past Silas at the blanket-hung window, and her eyes were quick and knowing. "I can keep books. And I can watch a stairway. And I don't gossip, because gossip is for people with nothing better to sell."

"Why would I need a stairway watched?"

Greta looked at her the way the young look at the old when the old are pretending. "Because you hang a blanket over your window in a town where the commission has taken the good rooms at the hotel, and you carry paper home in quantities no nurse needs, and last Tuesday you asked me about the postal schedule to the capital as if it were a medical question." She set the parcel on the table beside the machine. "I'm not asking what you're doing. I'm saying the hotel has clerks, and clerks have evenings free, and evenings are when people walk places they shouldn't."

Silas studied her. In another life she might have sent the girl away. But the ledger had taught her the arithmetic of witnesses: every name was a door, and doors opened in both directions.

"Tuesdays and Fridays," Silas said finally. "One hour after dark. If anyone asks, you're bringing me sewing."

"I don't sew."

"Then learn."

Something flickered at the corner of Greta's mouth — not quite a smile, but the place a smile would go when it grew in. She turned to leave, then paused at the door.

"The teacher," she said. "Before they recalled her, she told my father the machine was the only honest thing she'd ever owned. Said it couldn't flatter anybody." The girl's chin lifted. "Don't prove her wrong."

The stairs took her down, light and quick, and Silas stood alone with the covered window and the waiting machine, and felt the strangest sensation rise in her chest — not fear, though fear was there, a constant low hum like the ice on the river. It was something adjacent to joy. Twelve years of solitary ink, of being the sole custodian of other people's erased lives, of carrying two hundred fourteen names in a box under a floorboard like a disease she dared not show a doctor — and now there was a girl on the stairs, and a pawnbroker's blessing, and pages multiplying in fair copy, and a machine that could not flatter anybody.

She lifted the blanket from the window. Below, the yard lay blue with moonlight on frost, empty, ordinary, safe. Across the town the commission's lamps burned late at the hotel, and somewhere among them Edmund Hale was asleep, or not asleep, believing in the mercy of silence.

Silas lowered the blanket again, sat, rolled in a fresh sheet, and set her fingers on the keys.

*Statement of Marta Okafor, continued.*

The bells rang. The carriage sang back. Outside, far off, the river muttered in the dark like something turning over in its sleep, and Silas typed on into the small hours, exhilarated and unafraid, not yet knowing that the stairway Greta watched had already been climbed once — quietly, competently, by someone who knew exactly which floorboard to lift.
