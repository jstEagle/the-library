## 7. Colonel Vane's Courtesy

The knock came at four in the afternoon, when the light through my window had gone the color of weak tea and I was mending a blanket that belonged to no one in particular. Three knocks, evenly spaced, unhurried. Not the knock of someone who needed anything. The knock of someone who had decided to give you something — his time, mostly.

I opened the door to a tall man in a greatcoat the gray of river ice, cap under his arm, snow melting off his shoulders in two dark crescents. He was perhaps fifty-five, with a face built for portraits: strong jaw going soft at the edge, eyes pale as pewter, a mouth that had practiced kindness until it fit him like a glove fits a hand — perfectly, and with nothing inside it but a hand.

"Sister Underhill." He said it as though the name were a small gift he'd brought for me. "Colonel Ambrose Vane. I don't believe we've had the pleasure."

"We haven't," I said.

"No. A failure of the war, not of manners. May I?" He was already coming in.

That was the first thing I understood about Colonel Vane: he did not wait for permission because permission was, to him, a formality observed by people who needed things from each other. He crossed my room the way a surveyor crosses land he intends to own. His eye went over everything — the narrow bed, the basin, the chair by the window, the cold stove — with an appraiser's patience. It did not linger anywhere. That was its own kind of message. Whatever he was looking for, he already knew where it wasn't.

"Forgive the intrusion," he said, removing his gloves finger by finger. "I'm attached to the historical commission as liaison from the General Staff. A dull post. I stand between the historians and the army and make sure neither embarrasses the other." He smiled. "It is rather like standing between two dogs who both believe they are right."

I said nothing. Twelve years had taught me the arithmetic of silence; I knew how much a pause could hold.

"You'll wonder why I've come." He set his gloves on the sill, squared them precisely parallel, and turned. "I've come to thank you."

"To thank me."

"For your service. Six years at the front, they tell me, and before that the typhus wards. You were mentioned twice in dispatches — did you know? No, of course you didn't. They never tell the nurses anything. The officers get their ribbons read aloud in Parliament and the women who held the men together while they died get a line in a file no one opens." He shook his head with what appeared to be genuine regret. "I have made it something of a personal mission to correct that. The republic of gratitude owes debts it has never audited."

He spoke beautifully. That was the second thing I understood about him. Every sentence arrived pre-wrapped, like parcels tied with string, and you had to trust there was nothing rattling loose inside.

"That's very kind, Colonel," I said. "But I'm only passing through Marrow's Ford. When the bridge reopens—"

"The bridge." He nodded slowly, as if the word contained whole volumes. "Yes. The bridge. An inconvenience, isn't it? Being on the wrong side of the water. Though I suppose a woman of your profession knows better than most that rivers are only inconvenient for those trying to leave." He lowered himself into my one good chair without appearing to sit so much as occupy it. "You'll be here some months yet, I should think. The ice won't go before March, and March is optimistic."

"I can find work. There's a charity ward—"

"There is indeed." Something moved behind his eyes, quick as a fish turning. "St. Aldric's. You've been visiting."

"Tomas Okafor is a patient of mine from the war. He has no family in the town except—" I stopped, because Marta Okafor was not mine to give away, and Vane's stillness told me he had heard the stop.

"Private Okafor. Ravine man." He said it lightly, the way one says *Sunday rain* or *bad teeth* — a thing common enough to be unremarkable. "A great many ravine men in this town, come to think of it. The 3rd Battalion was recruited locally, you know. Whole companies of brothers and cousins. It makes for fine morale and terrible mathematics." He laced his fingers over his knee. "Tell me, Sister — how is he?"

"Dying," I said. "Slowly. Shrapnel in the lung. It's a question of weather now, more than medicine. Cold nights are hard on him."

"Yes. Yes, they would be." He gazed out the window at the river, gray-white and locked, the far bank a pencil line. "Do you know what I admire most about nurses? Their memory. Everyone imagines the surgeon holds the life of the patient in his hands, but truly it's the nurse — she remembers which leg, which dose, which allergy, which lie the man told the intake clerk about his age. Memory is the entire profession." He looked back at me. "And yet memory must be *managed*, mustn't it? Even in nursing. We decide what a patient needs to remember of his pain and what he is better off forgetting. Morphine is a kindness partly because it takes the night shift away. We do it for them."

"Sometimes we do it to them," I said.

His smile deepened by a degree. "Precisely. Sometimes to them. One hopes one can tell the difference at the time. One usually cannot."

The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below us a door slammed and boots went down the stairs of the billet. Vane let the quiet sit between us long enough for me to feel it as weight.

"I'll be direct with you, Sister Underhill, since directness is the privilege of age." He leaned forward slightly. "The commission downstairs — three distinguished gentlemen with three fountain pens and a combined forty-seven years of education — will spend this winter writing the history of the campaign. It will be a fine history. Accurate in its way. Wars are too large to be remembered whole; any honest account is a series of merciful omissions stitched together with dates. That is not cynicism. That is craft."

"Or tailoring," I said. "Cutting the coat to hide the stain."

"If there is a stain." He spread his hands, all reasonableness. "I have read the official reports. All of them. Kessel Ravine reads as a fighting withdrawal under fire — costly, gallant, misreported in no particular that matters. Now, you were at Kessel Ravine, I understand."

The room became very still, or I did.

"You were at the dressing station above the ravine," he continued, before I could answer. "August, six years ago. Your name appears in the casualty returns — not the wounded lists, the *working* lists. Someone thought you worth recording, once." He tilted his head. "So you saw what there was to see. And I have no doubt that what there was to see was ugly. War is ugly at the granular level; that is its signature. But ugliness is not falsity, Sister. A battle can be accurately reported and still be unbearable to recall. The report records the shape of the thing. Memory insists on the screams. Both may be true. Only one of them can be taught to schoolchildren."

"And the other one," I said, "gets buried with the men."

"With the men, yes." He said it gently, almost tenderly, as though I had offered him a comfort and he was accepting it. "With the men. Which brings me — and forgive me, I am a roundabout old soldier — to my purpose."

He rose then, and went to the window, and stood looking down at the street with his hands clasped behind him, so that I looked at his back and the map of the town beyond him and understood that he wanted me to watch him think, or pretend to.

"A nurse of your record should not end her years in charity wards and borrowed rooms," he said. "There is a matron's post opening in the spring. Salta Basin. A convalescent hospital — clean air, modern building, a staff of thirty. Warm climate. The doctors are adequate and the patients are grateful and nobody there has ever heard of Kessel Ravine, because half of them have never heard of this war." He turned. "I can have the appointment signed before Christmas. References arranged. Passage paid. A pension supplement for your twelve years — thirteen by spring, isn't it? — of service. It is, frankly, more than you could ever earn by staying where you are."

"By staying where I am," I repeated.

"Marrow's Ford is a small place in winter," Vane said. "Small places run out of work for people whose skills are... specialized. A nurse who has spent the war in field hospitals finds garrison duty dull, I'm told. Finds *towns* dull. Begins, out of idleness, to involve herself in things that are finished. Inquiries, correspondence, reminiscences." He picked up his gloves from the sill and tapped them once against his palm, softly, like a man beating time to music only he could hear. "The commission will be here through March. After that, the record closes. Literally — the volumes are bound and sealed and shipped, and whatever was left out stays out, forever, in the most permanent way imaginable. Until then, a person of conscience might feel tempted to... contribute. To write letters. To collect accounts." He met my eyes at last, and for one unguarded instant the kindness drained out of his face and left something patient and bottomless, like a well you could drop a stone into and never hear it land. "Sister, I say this with real respect: a woman who collects accounts is a woman who becomes one."

The words hung in the cold air of my room.

Then the warmth returned, seamless, as if it had never left. "But that is gloomy talk, and I didn't come to gloom you. I came to offer you Salta Basin. Think of it as a bridge, if you like — the kind that actually opens. By spring you could be somewhere the sun shines and the past is properly buried, doing work that matters, honored for it." He put out his hand. "May I count on you considering it?"

I took his hand. It was dry and warm and entirely steady. "I'll consider it, Colonel."

"Wonderful." He retrieved his cap from the chair arm, settled it with two precise touches, and paused at the door with the expression of a man remembering a pleasant errand. "Oh — one more thing. Small matter. The ward clerk at St. Aldric's, young Pell — a bright boy, isn't he? Quick with figures. He has a brother in a penal company on the eastern works. Hard posting. The kind men don't come home from." He adjusted his glove. "Transfers can be arranged, of course. For boys with helpful connections." The smile again, brief and warm as a struck match. "Good evening, Sister. Do mind the ice on the stairs. It's treacherous this time of year. So much depends, in winter, on knowing exactly where one steps."

His boots went down the stairs, evenly spaced, unhurried.

I stood in the middle of my room for a long while after the outer door closed, listening to the river. Under the ice it was still moving — it is always still moving — and it made a sound like breathing in another room.

He had named everything. The ward, Okafor, Pell, the ledger's shape if not its existence, the exact geography of my guilt. He hadn't threatened me once. He had *praised* me, offered me warmth, promotion, sunlight, and wrapped around each gift the same quiet clause: *places with no bridges.* He had even shown me the price of refusal without naming it — a dying man's widow, a boy's brother, a clerk's future — so that every harm to follow would be, by his accounting, self-inflicted. Mine. My choice. My stain.

I sat at the window until the light failed completely and the far bank disappeared and there was only the river's black breath rising off the ice.

Then I lit the lamp, pulled the floorboard, and counted the pages again. All there. Two hundred fourteen names, and beneath them, in my smallest hand, the beginnings of a list I had never intended to make:

*witnesses.*

Tomas first. And tomorrow, God help me, I would need to know why young Pell had looked at me across the ward desk that morning as if he wanted to confess to something and couldn't afford to.
