## 8. The Stolen Pages

The cold told me before my eyes did.

I had left the window latched — I always latched it, the way I always counted the instruments back into their tray, the way I always counted everything, because counting was the one habit twelve years of war had never managed to break in me. But standing on the landing outside my room at Mrs. Aldous's boarding house, with the smell of boiled cabbage rising from the kitchen and the floorboards groaning under my boots, I felt a thread of colder air against my knuckles that had no business being there.

I stood very still in the corridor and listened.

Nothing. The house breathed its evening breaths around me: Mrs. Aldous's wireless murmuring downstairs, a door closing somewhere on the second floor, the pipes ticking as they cooled. All the ordinary sounds of an ordinary house full of people who had no idea what slept beneath their feet.

I turned the handle and went in.

At first glance, nothing was wrong. That was the first thing I noticed — not what was disturbed but how little of it there was to disturb. My blanket lay smooth on the bed. My uniform hung where I hung it. The washbasin sat square on its stand, the pitcher aligned to its rim. A room searched by a clumsy man looks like weather has passed through it: drawers half-opened, papers fanned across the mattress, the small archaeology of someone else's hands. This room looked exactly as I had left it.

That was when my stomach went cold all the way down, because I knew only two kinds of people who could search a room like this: professionals, and people who already knew precisely where everything was.

I crossed to the hearth. The loose brick at the base of the chimney — third from the left, the one that rocked on a bed of old mortar — was back in place. It even looked right. But when I pressed it, it gave too easily, sliding out without the resistance of mortar that has been settled for months, and behind it the oilcloth bundle that should have weighed as much as a sleeping cat weighed almost nothing.

I unwrapped it with hands that did not feel entirely mine.

Half.

They had taken half. Not the whole ledger — half of it, and not the front half or the back half or any half a thief would choose. They had taken pages 40 through 118. Seventy-eight names gone, cut cleanly from the binding with a blade, and I knew without looking further which seventy-eight they were, because I knew every page of that book the way a mother knows her children's faces.

Kessel Ravine.

Every name from Kessel Ravine. Every date, every hour, every note in the margins about who had been alive when the shelling stopped and who had been left where they fell because the order to withdraw had come and the colonel's signature had already been drying on a report that said the engagement had resulted in no significant casualties. Gone. Lifted out of my keeping by someone whose hands were steady enough to re-lay a brick so that it fooled me for four full seconds.

Four seconds. In four seconds they had stolen six years of my life.

* * *

I do not remember sitting down. I remember coming back to myself in the chair by the window with the remaining pages spread across my knees and the light going blue-grey in the glass, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that frightened me more than fear itself: *count*. So I counted.

Two hundred fourteen names recorded over twelve years. One hundred thirty-six still in my possession. Seventy-eight taken. Of those seventy-eight, how many existed anywhere else in the world? Private Okafor's memory, and Tomas Okafor was dying in the charity ward with lungs that filled like wet paper. Marta Okafor's letters — if she still had them, if she would ever give them up, if she could even read them now without her heart stopping. The farmer at the ravine, if he was still alive, if he remembered, if anyone had reached him before whoever took these pages did.

And Edmund Hale. Edmund's signature on the false report, which meant Edmund knew what the true report should have said, though knowing and admitting were separated by a distance I was only beginning to measure.

I sat in the dark and made a list, because making lists was what I did instead of screaming.

Who knew about the brick?

Mrs. Aldous did not. She thought I kept sewing in the chimney space; she had once remarked that I was the only lodger whose mending never crept onto the parlour table. The pawnbroker did not know me well enough to know where I lived, let alone where I hid anything — and besides, I had bought the typewriter from him only days ago, cash, under the plain name of Underhill, and he had asked nothing. The ward clerk, Peter Voss, knew I kept notes — everyone knew nurses kept notes, that was unremarkable, that was invisible — but he had never been here. He had never seen this room.

Had he?

I tried to reconstruct every conversation I'd had in the past three weeks the way I reconstructed a wound: probe gently, follow the track, don't stop until you find the source. There had been the evening in early December when Okafor's fever spiked and I sat with him until midnight, and Voss had brought me tea and stayed talking in the corridor, and I had been tired, so tired, and I might have — had I mentioned the ravine? Had I said something about needing to check my records? I couldn't remember. That was the thing about silence: you practiced it for twelve years and you began to believe it was seamless, and then one careless sentence in a lamplit corridor came back to cost you seventy-eight names.

And there was the other possibility, the one I turned over last because turning it over hurt most.

Edmund Hale had come to Marrow's Ford with the commission. Edmund had dined with Colonel Vane three nights running. Edmund knew me — knew that I was methodical, knew that I kept things, knew me better perhaps than anyone living knew me, because he had lain in a field hospital cot in 1913 watching me write in a notebook by candlelight and had asked me, with the frankness of a man who believes he is dying, *what are you writing?*

*I'm writing your name,* I had told him. *So somebody knows you were here.*

He had smiled. He had been twenty-six years old and full of holes and grateful beyond speech, and I had believed, all these years, that the smile meant the secret was safe.

But men change. Men get promoted. Men sit at tables with colonels who say *the record must be settled before spring* and discover that the woman who once wrote their name in a notebook is now an inconvenience wearing a nurse's apron.

I didn't want to believe it. That was the trap, wasn't it — the oldest trap in war. You didn't want to believe it, so you didn't look, and while you weren't looking the knife went in. I had watched wives refuse to believe husbands dead because believing required rearranging the entire architecture of a life. I refused to believe Edmund capable of this because believing it required rearranging mine.

So I did what I always did with a wound I couldn't bear to probe. I dressed it, and I moved on to the ones I could.

* * *

Marta Okafor lived in a narrow house on Tanner's Row with lace curtains that had gone grey and a stove that smoked, and she opened the door before I finished knocking, which told me she had been watching the street. Women whose husbands go to war learn to watch streets.

"You've heard," she said.

"Heard what?" My voice came out wrong. Flat. "Marta, what's happened?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw in my face made her step aside and pull me into the hallway, into the smell of beeswax and coal smoke and dried herbs hanging from the ceiling beams. She shut the door. She bolted it, which she had never done in all the years I had visited.

"A man came yesterday," she said quietly. "In a good coat. Said he was from the pension office. He wanted to know if Tomas had letters from the war. Said the commission was verifying claims." Her hands were working at each other, wringing. "Silas, I gave him nothing. I lied. I said the mails lost everything, we got maybe two letters the whole war. But he —" She stopped. Started again. "He looked at the dresser. He looked at the dresser the way you look at a thing you intend to come back for."

My blood went thin and cold. "Did he give a name?"

"Brenner. He said his name was Brenner." She laughed, one dry syllable with no humor in it at all. "Tomas used to talk about a Brenner. A staff officer. The one who typed up the casualty lists after Kessel."

The stove ticked. Somewhere down the row a dog barked twice and quit.

"Marta." I took her hands and held them still between mine. Hers were rough and small and shaking. "Listen to me. Did Tomas keep copies? Did he keep anything — a diary, duplicates, anything at all?"

"The letters." Her eyes were wet and fierce at once. "You know about the letters."

"I need them tonight. Or a copy. Or —" I caught myself, because I heard what I sounded like, a woman demanding, and Marta Okafor owed me nothing except her husband's trust, which was worth everything and which I had no right to spend. I softened. "I wouldn't ask if it weren't — Marta, my ledger has been robbed. Everything from Kessel Ravine. Someone came into my room with a key or a pick and a great deal of patience, and they took exactly those pages, nothing else, and that means someone told them where to look, and I don't know who, and until I find out I can't protect anyone. Not Tomas. Not you. Not myself."

She stared at me. And then, horribly, she began to laugh — a real laugh this time, cracked and exhausted, the laugh of a woman who has spent years pretending ignorance and finds the pretense collapsing all at once.

"Do you know what Tomas says when the fever takes him?" she said. "He says, *tell the nurse, tell Silas, tell her it counts for something.* Six years I've listened to that. Six years I've brought you his soup and his clean shirts and pretended I didn't know what the two of you whispered about when I left the room." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "I'll get the letters. But you'll take them tonight, and you'll take them out of this house tonight, because that man in the good coat will come back, and I have a daughter upstairs who is eleven years old, and I am done pretending I don't know anything. Pretending didn't save Tomas and it won't save me."

She went to the dresser — the one the man had looked at — and knelt, and pulled out the bottom drawer entire, and worked loose a board beneath it with her fingernails. From the cavity she lifted a tobacco tin, rusted at the seams, and pressed it into my hands. It was heavier than it looked.

"Forty-one letters," she said. "He mailed them through a chaplain who owed him money. Dates and places in every one. He said —" her voice caught and steadied — "he said paper was cheaper than a grave marker."

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the river road, because the long way was darker and darker meant fewer faces, and because I needed the sound of the ice. It groaned out in the channel like something enormous shifting in its sleep, and I found that sound steadying in a way I couldn't have explained to anyone. The river was holding its dead under all that black ice. Come spring it would give some of them up. Rivers always did, eventually. It was only men who didn't.

The tin rode inside my coat, against my ribs, warm from my body. Forty-one letters. I would transcribe them tomorrow on the typewriter, three copies, and hide them in three places, and never again would the truth live in a single basket, however cleverly bricked.

But the other question walked beside me the whole way, matching my stride, breathing my breath.

*Who.*

Vane's people — certainly Vane's people had done the taking; the precision spoke of military intelligence the way a scalpel speaks of surgery. But precision was the instrument, not the hand that aimed it. Someone had drawn the map. Someone had said: *she keeps papers, she hides them behind the hearth brick, third from the left.* And the candidates arranged themselves in my mind with terrible neatness.

Peter Voss, the ward clerk, whom I had trained myself, who carried my tea and laughed at my jokes and had a brother in a penal unit whose release papers might be worth a few careful sentences overheard in a corridor. I hated myself for putting his name on the list. I put it there anyway.

Mrs. Aldous, who asked no questions, who charged fair rent, who had once mentioned — had she mentioned? — that a gentleman caller had asked after me in November, some official-sounding man, and she'd told him I lodged on the third floor, corner room, facing the river. Corner room. Facing the river. The room with the chimney.

Or Edmund.

I stopped walking. Out on the ice, a pressure ridge cracked and boomed, a sound like artillery remembered from far away, and I stood in the dark with my hand pressed flat against the tin through my coat and let myself think it all the way through for the first time.

Edmund had signed the report. Edmund had come back with the commission. Edmund had sought me out within a day of arriving — sought me out warmly, gratefully, asking after my health, my work, my *papers*, laughing and saying he remembered how I was always writing, always counting, that I hadn't changed a bit.

*Haven't changed a bit.* How easily a compliment becomes reconnaissance.

No. No — I made myself be fair, because fairness was a discipline like any other. If Edmund had wanted the ledger, why come to my door with warmth instead of sending Brenner with a crowbar? Why warn me — for surely the visit from Vane's man, the polite questions about my recollections, had been a warning of a kind? Unless the warning was the reconnaissance. Unless they measured what I would surrender voluntarily before deciding what to steal.

I had no way to know. That was the poison of it, the thing no one tells you about betrayal: not the loss of the thing taken, but the permanent infection of everything left. I would never again open a door without a small cold voice saying *who told them you'd be here*. Twelve years I had survived by trusting almost no one, and the almost had finally collected its fee.

One thing I knew, though. One thing I was certain of, the way I was certain of a pulse.

They had taken the pages, but they had not yet taken Okafor. And Tomas Okafor, dying by inches in the charity ward, was the last living mouth that had spoken the whole account aloud — the withdrawal order, the abandoned wounded, the numbers that did not match the report. Whatever else was uncertain, that much stood clear as a bone on an X-ray plate: if they had gone to the trouble of cutting seventy-eight names out of a brick wall, they would not leave the witness breathing indefinitely.

I climbed the stairs of Mrs. Aldous's house, locked my door, set the tin on the table, and lit the lamp. Then I took the surviving ledger pages from my coat and counted them again, twice, because counting was the one thing the war had never broken in me.

One hundred thirty-six names, and forty-one letters, and one promise scrawled in a feverish hand six years ago that said *it counts for something.*

Tomorrow I would begin typing. Tonight, before anything else, I would go back to the ward — I told myself it was only to check Okafor's dressing, only routine, only the ordinary rounds of an ordinary nurse — and I would sit beside him until morning if I had to, because something in my bones had begun to whisper that whatever time remained for telling his story was time I was spending faster than I knew.
