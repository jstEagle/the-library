## 10. The Widow's Testimony

The house on Tanner's Row was the color of old ash, its shutters painted once and never again, its windows fogged with the breath of a stove that had been burning the same damp coal for years. I had passed it perhaps two hundred times on my rounds without once letting my eye rest on it. That was the discipline of Marrow's Ford: you learned not to look at what looking would cost you.

I looked now.

Marta Okafor opened the door before I knocked. She was smaller than I remembered from the ward visits — Tomas had been brought in three years ago with his lungs full of ravine dust and his leg full of shrapnel, and she had come every Thursday with bread and clean linen, sitting by his bed with her hands folded like a woman at church who is not praying but waiting for God to finish speaking. She had never once asked me about the war. I had understood that as kindness, or as fear, and I had respected it the way one respects a closed door.

Now the door stood open and she stood in it, and her face said she had been standing there a while.

"Nurse Underhill," she said. "You came."

"You wrote to me." Her note had been four lines, no signature beyond an initial, delivered by a child who ran off before I could tip him. *They say he died of his wounds. He told me things before he died that were not wounds.* "May I come in?"

She stepped aside. The room beyond was immaculate in the manner of rooms where cleanliness is the only form of control left to a person: floor scrubbed pale, crockery aligned, a photograph of Tomas in uniform on the mantel beside a candle burned down to a thumb's width. She saw me notice the candle.

"I light it when he was dying," she said. "Every evening since Thursday. I don't know when to stop."

"There's no rule," I said. It was true and useless, and she nodded anyway, because we had both spent years learning to live inside rules that did not exist.

* * *

Three days after the funeral that wasn't a funeral — the ward doctor signed the certificate in eleven minutes and Vane's adjutant sent a wreath with a card reading *In grateful memory of faithful service* — Marta had sat alone in this room and done something she had not done in six years.

I know because she told me, and because she showed me the evidence, and because Marta Okafor did not lie. Not anymore. Perhaps not ever; perhaps only the silence had been hers, and silence is not a lie, though it does its work all the same.

What she had done was open the tin box under Tomas's spare trousers in the bottom drawer, the box she had dusted around for six years and never touched, and take out forty-one letters.

* * *

"He wrote every week," Marta said, pouring tea I could see her hands shaking over, so I took the pot from her and poured for both of us, and she let me, which told me how far past ceremony she was. "Even at the front. Even after. He said a man who stops writing home has decided he won't go home, and he wasn't going to decide that. Those were almost his words exactly."

"When did they change?"

She didn't pretend not to understand. "After the ravine." She said it plainly, the way you'd say the name of a river, which is what it was, which is what they had made it into — geography, scenery, nothing. "For two weeks nothing came. Then a letter came and it was his handwriting and none of his words. Warm, short, careful. 'All is well here. The men are in good spirits.' His letters before were never careful. He wrote me pages about the mud, the lice, a sergeant who sang off-key. After, he wrote me paragraphs that could be read aloud to a colonel."

"Because they were," I said. "Read aloud, I mean. They censored them."

"I know that now." She set her cup down with a small precise click. "At the time I thought — I'll tell you what I thought, Nurse Underhill, because everyone will tell you I knew nothing, and it isn't true. Nobody knows nothing. I thought he had done something he was ashamed of. That's what I believed for six years. That my husband had been part of some ugliness and couldn't say it, and I lay next to him night after night in our old flat wondering what kind of man I'd married." Her voice didn't break. It went flatter, which was worse. "And then he came home wounded and dying slowly, and he still wouldn't say it, and I thought, whatever it is, it must be very large, if it can't be said even to a wife who has promised to stay."

"And then?"

"And then a week ago he started saying it." She looked at the photograph on the mantel. "He'd made his peace with dying quietly, I think. He used to say the dead have lawyers enough — meaning God. But then you began coming to the ward with your questions, and I watched him sit up straighter than he'd sat up in a year, and I understood he'd found something better than peace. He'd found a use."

* * *

Here is where I should record what happened in that room on Tanner's Row with precision, because everything else in my ledger is precise and this deserves no less.

Marta Okafor went to the bottom drawer. She took out the tin box. She untied the string — the string had been retied at some point, I noticed; the original knot would have rotted — and she lifted out a bundle of letters bound in butcher's twine, and beneath them a second bundle, thinner, tied with ribbon that had been white once.

"The first ones are ours," she said. "Love letters, mostly. Complaints about the food. You don't need those." And then she put the second bundle in my hands and sat back, folding her hands in her lap, and waited — actually waited, hands folded, spine straight — for me to read.

I read.

* * *

The first letter was dated nine days after Kessel Ravine. I will set down what it says, because Marta gave it to me for exactly that purpose, and because a widow's courage should not be wasted on a private drawer.

*My dearest Marta —*

*I am going to write you something and then I am going to ask you to do something hard, and I am sorry for both.*

*On the 14th we were ordered into the Ravine to clear it. There were people in it. I want to write the number but I keep stopping my own hand. Two hundred and more. Most of them had put down their arms already or never had arms to put down. I saw it. Sergeant Reyes saw it. Corporal Adeyemi saw it, and he is dead now, and Private Lund saw it and he has shot himself, and I am telling you this because someone has to hold it somewhere and I cannot hold it in my head alone any longer.*

*Do not answer this letter. Do not show it to anyone. Burn it if you are afraid — I will understand, I swear to you I will understand, being afraid is the one thing nobody can fault you for. But if you keep it, keep it for me. Keep it so that I know it exists outside my skull. A thing that exists in one place can be killed. A thing in two places has to be hunted.*

*I love you. That is not careful writing. Let it stand.*

*Tomas*

There were eleven more in that vein. Eleven more dispatches from the country of the erased: names, dates, the order as it was given and the order as it was denied afterward, the officer who countermanded the burial detail, the wagonloads taken out under tarpaulins at night toward the lime pits. In the fourth letter he described a lieutenant weeping while he supervised the loading. In the seventh he named the signature at the bottom of the report that called the ravine *an engagement resulting in acceptable losses during a clearing operation.*

Edmund Hale's name. In Tomas Okafor's handwriting, dated six years ago, sealed under a widow's bed linens while I was writing the same facts in a ledger under my own floorboards, neither of us knowing the other held a piece of it.

I set the letters down. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I had expected them to shake.

"Marta," I said. "These men will destroy these if they learn they exist."

"They tried already." Something moved through her face then, quick and cold. "Two days ago. Before the funeral even. A very polite young clerk came to express condolences on behalf of the garrison and asked, among other small kindnesses, whether Tomas had left papers, as the commission might wish to collect his effects for the historical record. Very reasonable. Very gentle." She looked at me. "I told him Tomas left nothing but debts and a prayer book. He thanked me and tipped his cap and walked away whistling. Whistling, Nurse Underhill. Like a boy."

It was the clerk. I knew it the way you know a face across a street — the shape of it, the trained gentleness of it. The boy I had taught to file and fold and keep confidences. He had searched my room, and now he had come here, and either he had reported that the widow kept nothing, or he had reported that she lied. I filed the thought away where I keep the thoughts that hurt.

"Why did you lie?" I asked. "To him, I mean. Why not give them up and be safe?"

Marta was quiet for a long moment. The stove ticked. Somewhere down the row a cart went by, runners hissing on packed snow.

"Because I practiced," she said finally. "Six years of pretending not to know anything — you get good at it. Lying turned out to be the one skill all that pretending was training me for." She picked up the topmost letter, Tomas's first, and smoothed it against her knee as though ironing a crease out of time itself. "And because he asked me to keep them. Burn them or keep them, he said, but choose. I chose. I'm not going to un-choose because a boy whistles."

"What do you want, Marta? Truly. Because there are things I can do with these and things I cannot undo, and once they leave this room they stop being yours."

"That's just it." She leaned forward, and for the first time her eyes were not tired; they were lit, the way coals are lit, from underneath and stubbornly. "They were never mine. That's what I understood sitting here after they buried him. He kept me as a second place — a place where it couldn't be killed. But a hiding place isn't a purpose. He wanted them to count. His last week on earth, he sat up in that charity ward and begged you to make his account count for something. Well. I'm his account too. Every word he ever sent me." She pushed the bundle across the table toward me. "Take them. Copy them. Do whatever your typewriter does. And when you speak the names — and you will, I can see it in you the same way I saw it in him — speak his first. He'd hate the attention and he'd deserve it."

I gathered the letters. Twelve envelopes, each weighing almost nothing, together heavier than anything I had carried in twelve years of carrying.

"One condition," Marta said, as I rose.

"Name it."

"When you make your copies — make me one. Whatever happens to you, to me, to all of us. I spent six years as a hiding place. I'd like to spend whatever's left as a witness." She almost smiled. "Tomas always said I was the brave one. I thought he was being kind. He was being accurate."

* * *

I walked back through the town as the light went blue and the lamps came up one by one along the river road. The ice groaned under the bridge — early this year, earlier than anyone could remember, the thaw pushing up from underneath like something impatient to be born — and I stopped at the rail and listened to it, because it sounded the way I felt: solid-seeming, and cracking, and running underneath with water that would not be stopped much longer.

Twelve letters against my chest, sewn into the lining of my coat where the ledger's surviving half had lived. Forty-one letters total in the tin; I carried twelve; Marta would carry the rest, and now, tonight, I would sit behind my curtain with the battered typewriter and give her back every word of them in clean copy, triple-struck, before anyone could take them from either of us.

But it was the clerk I thought of on the bridge. The polite young voice asking after papers. Either he had failed to find them and reported failure, or he had seen something in Marta's face — and Marta, bless her exactness, had said she lied well, but she had lied to a trained questioner, and trained questioners count hesitations the way I count pulses.

By morning, someone would know the widow had lied.

And by morning, someone would know that I had visited her the evening before — the town was small, the snow held footprints, and Colonel Vane's courtesy extended, I had no doubt, to watching the doors of the recently bereaved.

I tightened my coat and walked on, faster now, the letters pressing warm against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Behind me the ice boomed once, long and low, a sound like a door opening somewhere under the world.

Ahead, in my darkened window, I could see the outline of the typewriter waiting behind its curtain — and beside it, faintly, the shape of a figure standing across the street, still as a lamppost, watching my window watch the street.

Someone had gotten there first.
