## 11. Edmund Reads the Ledger

I went to him at dusk, when the lamplighter was making his rounds and the commission's offices had gone quiet. I told myself it was strategy — that a tired man defends less, that night strips a man of his official armor the way sleep strips him of his rank. But that was only half true. The other half was simpler and less flattering: I could not carry the surviving pages one more day alone.

The ledger's remains weighed almost nothing. Forty-one pages out of two hundred and six, wrapped in oilcloth inside my coat, pressed against my ribs like a second heart. Vane's men had taken the rest with a burglar's neatness, but they had not found everything, because there are hiding places in this world that no search can reach: the ones you prepare for years before you need them. I had sewn pages into the lining of my winter coat in 1917, when the shelling came close enough to the dressing station to make me think about fire. Habit, my mentor would have called it. A confession you haven't been caught making, kept warm against your body for six years.

Edmund Hale lodged in the east wing of the garrison house, in a room that looked out on the river. The sentry at the door knew me by sight — half the town did; the nurse who mends what officers break — and waved me through with the incurious courtesy of men paid not to wonder.

He opened the door himself. He was out of uniform, in a grey sweater gone soft at the elbows, and for a moment we were simply two people who had once stood together in a tent that smelled of carbolic and snow, and I nearly turned around and went home.

"Miss Underhill." He stepped back to let me in. "I hoped you'd come. I've been hoping since the tea."

"You shouldn't have," I said. "But thank you."

* * *

The room was spare in the way rooms are when their occupant expects to leave them: a cot made with military corners, a writing desk, his own portable typewriter under its canvas cover like a sleeping bird. On the desk lay the commission's working papers, squared and weighted with a stone from the riverbank. I recognized the report at the bottom of the stack without touching it. Kessel Ravine. His signature at the foot, steady as a fence line.

"I want to show you something," I said, "and then I want to ask you something, and I want you to answer before dawn, because after dawn I may lose my nerve or my freedom, and possibly both."

He sat down slowly. "Silas."

"Not Silas. Not yet." My hands were shaking. I unwrapped the oilcloth on his desk, beside his river stone, beside his false report. "Read this first. Then ask me whatever you like."

Forty-one pages. Names, ranks, units, dates of death, causes of death, and where each man fell — coordinates I had taken from dying men's mouths, from the pockets of the dead, from farmers who buried strangers and remembered the fences they died against. Two hundred and fourteen names, and I still carried most of them in memory even where the paper was gone. But these forty-one pages included the ravine. The ravine was here. Every hour of it, from the first shell at 0430 to the last man crawling toward the wire at dusk.

Edmund read the way he did everything — thoroughly, silently, his jaw tightening at intervals like a man walking on ice he didn't trust. I watched the lamp burn down an inch. Then another inch.

At page nine he stopped. "This is Corporal Reyes' account. How do you have Corporal Reyes' account?"

"He gave it to me. In the dressing station, three days before they moved him. He asked me to write it down because the officers wouldn't take it and he couldn't hold a pen." I kept my voice level. "He said someone should know where he'd been."

"He died in the evacuation."

"He died telling the truth to a nurse. It was the best available option."

He looked up at me then, and I saw the thing I had been afraid of and hoping for in equal measure: not disbelief. Recognition. He had known. Some part of him had always known what the report said and what the ground said, and the distance between those two things had been living in him for six years like a splinter too deep to dig out.

"How many names?" he asked quietly.

"Two hundred and fourteen. That's the surviving copy. There were more pages once."

"Once."

"They're gone. Taken from my room ten days ago, with a precision that suggests friends." I let that sit between us. "I'm not accusing you. If I thought it was you, I wouldn't be here."

"No," he said. "You'd have come with witnesses and a lawyer and the full weight of your terrible competence." Almost a smile. It died quickly. "Vane's adjutant has been asking questions about you. I heard it secondhand and said nothing, because I told myself it was routine." He passed a hand over his face. "It seems I've developed a habit of saying nothing."

"Now you understand my twelve years."

* * *

He read until midnight. I sat by the window with my coat over my knees and watched the river, black between its banks of ice, carrying broken moonlight downstream. Somewhere below, the ice groaned — a long, deep complaint, like a door in an empty house. The thaw was early this year. Everyone said so. The commission was already packing crates.

At some point Edmund got up without a word and made tea, badly, the way men do who learned in the field and never improved upon it. He set a cup at my elbow. I drank it. It was terrible. Six years ago I had held a basin for this man while a surgeon dug shrapnel out of his chest, and he had gripped my wrist hard enough to bruise and said, *don't let them forget me if I go under,* and I had said, *no one forgets anyone on my watch,* meaning it as comfort, discovering later it was a vow.

"Page twenty-two," he said finally. His voice had changed. It was flatter now, stripped. "The second lieutenant who ordered the withdrawal that never came. You have his name, his order, the time it was given, and the fact that it was countermanded an hour later by someone above him."

"Yes."

"And the countermand is what killed them. Not the enemy. The countermand put them back into the open."

"Yes."

"The name above his is redacted in the official file." He wasn't looking at me anymore. He was looking at his own signature. "I requested that redaction myself. March 1919. On the grounds that naming him would complicate pension settlements for forty surviving men of the 2nd Battalion, whose widows' allowances depended on the action being classified as engagement with the enemy rather than command failure."

The room was very quiet. Down on the river the ice groaned again, longer this time, a sound like grief learning language.

"Say it plainly," I said. "I've earned plain speech."

"I signed a false report," Edmund said, "because I believed the truth would take bread out of the mouths of forty families. And I have spent six years telling myself that was mercy." He set down the pages as though they were hot. "And every year the story has gotten easier to live inside. That's the thing no one warns you about, Silas. A lie doesn't get harder. It gets comfortable. It gets furniture. You hang pictures in it. You invite people to tea in it." He looked at me. "You did invite me to tea in it."

"Yes. I did."

"Why?" And there it was — not anger, but the real question underneath all his warmth and gratitude, the question he'd been carrying since our first meeting. "Why come back into my life now? Why not let me keep my comfortable house?"

"Because Tomas Okafor is dying in the charity ward," I said, "and he begged me to make his account count for something. Because Marta Okafor handed me letters her husband mailed her that the army never read. Because a farmer walked me along the ravine last week and showed me where he buried thirty-one men whose tags he has kept in a tobacco tin for four years, waiting for someone to ask." I stood. My legs had gone stiff and cold. "Because I promised two hundred and fourteen men that no one would be forgotten on my watch, and I am apparently incapable of breaking a promise, even a stupid one, even one that ruins me."

"It will ruin you," he said. "You understand that. Vane will bury you. Not with a bullet — nothing so honest. With a diagnosis. Rest facility, war hysteria, a quiet train to somewhere with high walls. I've seen him do it to a signals officer who questioned the casualty figures at Ansel. The man is alive somewhere. Probably. No one remembers."

"I know what he does. He sent me a courtesy visit and a compliment."

"Then why—"

"Because the alternative is what you've been doing." I said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Six years of comfortable furniture. Edmund — I pulled shrapnel out of you. I held your hand while you bled. I have loved you the way one loves the wounded: entirely, and without any illusions about my own good sense. So believe me when I tell you I did not cross this river to destroy you. I crossed it because you are the only man alive whose signature sits on that report and whose conscience might still be reached."

* * *

He didn't answer right away. He went to the window instead, and we stood there together watching the ice move in the dark, great plates of it shifting and grinding as the water beneath woke early from its winter.

"When I was under the table," he said at last, "in the field hospital. Before you. They'd given me up — I heard the surgeon say so. And you argued with him. I don't remember the words, only your voice. It never rose. It just refused." He turned. "You have that voice tonight."

"I know. I hate using it on you."

"What happens at dawn?"

"At dawn I request a formal hearing. Vane will refuse and offer me a closed session instead, and I'll take it, because a closed session is still a room with a record being made, and I have learned to work with very poor materials." I gathered the pages back into the oilcloth — and then stopped, and set them down again, all forty-one, square in front of him. "Keep these until morning. Read them again. If you decide to stay in your comfortable house, return them to me at the hearing and I'll fight with what I have. But if you decide otherwise—" I looked at his typewriter under its cover. "You're a historian, Edmund. You know better than anyone what an official record is worth. And what it costs when it lies."

"You're asking me to break an oath."

"I'm asking you to keep a bigger one. The one you made lying on a table in 1918, gripping my wrist. Don't let them forget me. No one gets forgotten on my watch." I took my coat from the chair. "I've kept my end for six years. All I ever wanted was for you to keep yours."

I was at the door when he spoke.

"Silas."

I turned. He was standing behind the desk, and the lamplight caught his face, and I saw that the splinter had finally been found — not dug out yet, but found, which is the beginning of every painful surgery.

"The name at the top of the countermand," he said. "The one I redacted. You have it in these pages. Do you know what it will mean when it's spoken aloud?"

"I know exactly what it will mean."

"It's Vane's brother-in-law."

The floor seemed to tilt slightly, the way the ice shifts when the current underneath changes direction. I stood very still and let the information settle into place — Vane's courtesy call, the stolen pages, Okafor's timing, the whole elegant machinery of protection clicking suddenly into view.

"Then tomorrow," I said, "we speak it very carefully. And very loudly."

Behind me, out on the black river, the ice cracked like a rifle shot — the first true crack of the thaw, sharp and final, the sound of a season deciding to end.
