## 13. The Night of Copies

The pawnbroker's daughter came for me at half past nine, hood up, breath smoking in the lamplight, and said only, "She's ready. Bring the machine."

I had spent the day doing what I always did — folding bandages, counting stores, answering Vane's adjutant's questions about the charity ward's linen requisitions with a face like unmarked paper. All day my hands had been steady because they had to be, and all day beneath the steadiness ran a single thought, repeating like the tick of a clock no one else could hear: *tonight, tonight, tonight.*

I packed the typewriter in its case at dusk, laid the surviving ledger pages inside my coat lining where they sat against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and told the matron I was going to sit with Marta Okafor, whose grief was now a matter of public sympathy. It was even true.

* * *

The pawnbroker's shop smelled of oil and old metal and the particular dust that accumulates on things people have stopped wanting. Herr Brandt — he had given his name finally, after months of "the pawnbroker," as though naming himself were a transaction too costly to make casually — had cleared the counter at the back and set three lamps around it, their shades draped with dark cloth so the light fell downward and did not touch the windows.

"You understand," he said, not looking at me, arranging typebars and ribbon spools with the tenderness other men reserve for children, "that if they come, I sold you the machine in October and have not seen you since."

"I understand."

"And if they ask what became of the copies—"

"They will not ask you anything, Herr Brandt. You are a subject of a neutral consulate. They will be very polite and go away." I hoped it was true. In Marrow's Ford in the winter of 1919 politeness was the uniform everyone wore over whatever they were actually doing.

He grunted, unconvinced, and turned to his daughter. Anneliese was nineteen, sharp-boned, quick-eyed, with ink stains on her fingers from helping him keep his books, and she had appointed herself to this night with a ferocity that had surprised us both. When I had first come to buy the machine she had watched me count out coins with the expression of someone reading a letter held upside down. Later she said: "You type at night with the curtain drawn. My father says mind your business. But I can read through a curtain, Frau Underhill. I read everything."

Now she took up station at the front window with a view down the frozen street, a cup of coffee going cold beside her, and announced, "Nothing moves. A dog. The lamplighter. Nothing."

Marta Okafor sat on the stool behind the counter with Tomas's letters spread before her in careful chronological order, as she had arranged them every night for a week, and her sewing in her lap though we both knew she would not sew. She had insisted on coming. "He dictated those letters to me before his hands got bad," she'd said. "I know them better than he did. If your machine eats a word, I'll know which word it was."

Herr Brandt showed me how the ribbon advanced, how to clear a jammed key without bending it, and then withdrew to his back room, though I noticed he did not close the door fully. A man can disclaim knowledge of a thing and still want to hear it happen.

I rolled in the first sheet.

For a moment I could not begin. The keys rose under my fingers like small pale teeth, and the page lay white and expectant, and I understood suddenly why I had written the ledger by hand for twelve years. Ink is private. Ink is the scratch of one person talking to herself in the dark. But a typed line — a typed line looks like an official document. A typed line looks like something that will survive being read aloud in a room full of men who did not want it written. I had spent twelve years keeping secrets in the most concealable form available, and now I was about to convert them into the least deniable form there was, and my hand shook so badly I struck the wrong key on the first attempt.

Marta reached over and steadied my wrist, once, without a word, the way you steady a nurse whose patient is bleeding, and let go, and I began.

*Statement of Silas M. Underhill, nursing sister, taken and recorded by her own hand...*

* * *

We worked until the coffee was gone and the candles had burned down twice and been replaced.

It went slowly at first, faster after. The machine demanded a rhythm — strike, carriage, return, the bell ringing its small silver note at the end of each line — and once I found the rhythm I found I could not stop finding it, that it carried me the way a march carries feet that would otherwise refuse. There is a reason armies drum. There is a reason churches chant. Rhythm takes the thing out of the trembling hand and makes it larger than the hand.

I typed Tomas Okafor's statement from the notes I had taken across four visits to the charity ward, his voice rising off my shorthand in phrases I knew I would hear for the rest of my life: *They marched them into the ravine two hundred at a time and the reports say the column was redirected. Redirected. I stood in the ravine, Sister. There was no redirection in that place.* I typed the widow's testimony, Marta dictating in a low level voice while Anneliese spelled her difficult words aloud from the doorway between watches. I typed the farmer's account — I had gone back to him that afternoon, walking the road past the picnic ground with its new benches, and he had met me at his gate with the tin box already in his pocket, having decided, apparently sometime between my last visit and this one, that identity tags belonged to families more than they belonged to fear. I typed the dates. I typed the numbers. I typed Kessel Ravine in capital letters, eleven times, once per document, and each time the keys struck it felt less like writing a forbidden word and more like driving a nail.

Somewhere past midnight I realized I was crying and typing at the same time, and that the tears were not slowing me down at all. Twelve years of handwriting, and here it was, converted into something with margins.

Anneliese relieved the window at one o'clock and Marta took the watch. The girl stretched, drank the cold coffee, and asked, "What happens to the copies? Three, you said?"

"One goes into the wall of the chapel where Tomas worshipped — behind the loose stone under the third pew, left side. One gets mailed to an editor in the capital who owes a debt to a dead man I nursed. And one—" I fed another sheet into the platen "—one travels with me."

"Under the floorboard?" Anneliese asked. She knew about the floorboard. Of course she knew; she read through curtains.

"No," I said, and heard myself say it as a decision rather than a discovery. "No more floorboards. The floorboard is how they took half of it. This one stays where I am. If they take it, they take me with it."

Marta, at the window, said nothing, but I saw her shoulders ease, just slightly, as though some weight she had been carrying on Tomas's behalf had been redistributed.

* * *

Around three in the morning the cold deepened and the shop's stove gave up and we worked in our coats, our breath ghosting above the paper. It was around then that the fear came.

Not the sharp fear of the searched room or Vane's courteous voice — that fear I knew, that fear had a shape and a schedule. This was a slower dread that crept in with the cold, sitting down beside me like a visitor with no appointment. *You are producing evidence,* it said. *Evidence has two edges. Every name you type tonight is a name someone will be asked about tomorrow. The boy in the penal unit. The ward doctor who signed a death certificate he may not have believed. The farmer with his tin box. Edmund Hale, who will stand in a room and be asked to call himself a liar in front of his own commission.* The keys kept moving but the dread spoke over the rhythm, patiently, reasonably, the way Vane spoke. *Silence kept them safe for twelve years. You are typing the end of safety.*

I stopped. My hands hovered.

From the window Marta said quietly, "Keep going."

"You should sleep," I said.

"Tomas waited six years for someone to write it down. He can wait six minutes while you remember why you're doing it. Keep going, Sister." She turned back to the street. "Besides, I've buried a husband. Fear doesn't frighten me anymore. It just visits."

So I kept going. And somewhere around four o'clock the dread did what dread does when refused — it thinned, and then it changed, and what remained in its place was not comfort exactly but something harder and better: defiance worn smooth by repetition into ritual. Strike, carriage, return, bell. Each completed page I lifted out and laid flat, and Anneliese stacked them into three piles with the solemnity of a sacristan laying out vestments. Copy for the chapel wall. Copy for the mail. Copy for me. By lamplight the stacks were thin, absurdly thin — a few hundred sheets, a kilo and a half of paper, that was all twelve years and two hundred fourteen lives weighed when you finally picked them up.

Two hundred fourteen names. I typed the list last, all of it, straight from memory, because the list itself had been in the stolen half of the ledger and I was the only copy of it left alive. Rank, surname, initials, date, manner of death, where the body lay or was believed to lie. The bell rang at the end of every line, small and silver, like a bell over a door, like a bell in a chapel, like a bell tolling one for each man as his name went down onto paper that could not be unpapered. When I finished the last name — Private E. Sandoval, 14th Column, drowned, Kessel ford, 9 October — I sat back and the silence rang louder than the bells had.

Marta came and stood over the pages. She read the names with her lips moving, and when she reached the O's she stopped, and pressed her fingers flat against the paper over her husband's name, briefly, the way you'd touch a cheek.

"There he is," she said. "In print. Like a person who existed."

* * *

Dawn was still an hour off when Herr Brandt appeared in the doorway of his back room, dressed, coat on, as though he had never gone to bed. He looked at the three stacks, at the machine, at the three of us women grey-faced in lamplight, and something in his merchant's caution visibly lost an argument with something older in him.

"The chapel stone," he said. "Third pew, left side. I know the church. I will deliver it myself today, after the early service, when a man carrying a parcel is invisible." He held out his hand for the first stack before I could decide whether to trust him, which I suppose was his way of settling the question for me.

Anneliese folded the second stack into a parcel, wrapped it in brown paper, and wrote the address herself in her neat clerk's hand — the editor's name, the newspaper, the capital — saying, "If it's in my handwriting they'll think it's stationery. Nobody opens stationery." Then she looked at me. "And the third?"

I packed it into the typewriter case, beneath the machine, against the wooden base where the case's false bottom — a pawnbroker's invention, he swore, used for smuggled watch movements — made a space exactly the depth of two hundred and fourteen names. Then I closed the lid and buckled it.

"The third goes wherever I go," I said. "Starting tomorrow, if I can get it done. Starting whenever the ice lets me."

Outside the glass, the sky along the river had begun to turn the colour of dirty pewter, and beneath the silence of the street I heard it — faint, arrhythmic, unmistakable. A groan from the direction of the water. Then a crack, long and splitting, running out under the ice like something turning over in its sleep.

Anneliese heard it too. Her eyes widened. "Is that—"

"That's the thaw," I said. My heart was slamming. "Early. Weeks early."

Marta gathered Tomas's letters back into their bundle with quick hands. "Then Vane leaves when the bridge opens," she said. "And so do you. And so does everything that hasn't happened yet."

I stood in the grey light of the pawnshop holding a typewriter case with a life's testimony hidden in its false bottom, and listened to the river breaking itself apart in the dark, and understood that whatever time I had, I had already spent most of it.
