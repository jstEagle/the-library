## 14. The Informant

The pawnbroker's daughter found me first.

I had gone to the ward to sit with Tomas Okafor's body for an hour — not to pray; I had no gift for prayer — but because someone ought to be with him who knew what he had carried. The charity ward smelled of carbolic and wet wool and the particular sweetness of a man three days dead whose bed had already been stripped for the next occupant. They kept the bodies in the back room at Marrow's Ford until the ground softened enough to dig, which meant April, which meant Tomas would wait in a cold annex while the commission wrote its clean history upstairs in the heated reading room.

I was still there when Ada Brandt came through the doors with snow melting off her shoulders, her breath ragged, her eyes doing something I had never seen them do. Ada was nineteen and unafraid of anything — rats, drunk orderlies, the colonel's adjutant when he came to count beds. Now she stood in the doorway of the annex wringing her mittens like a woman arriving too late to a deathbed, and she said, "Miss Underhill. You have to come now. He's going to do something stupid."

"Who is?"

"Piotr." Her voice cracked on the name. "Piotr Wozniak. He's written you a letter but he can't wait for you to read it, he's going to walk out onto the ice, I know him, Miss Underhill, I've known him since we were children —"

The world rearranged itself around one word. Not *who* — though that came, cold and complete, half a second later — but the shape of it. Piotr Wozniak. The ward clerk. The boy I had taught to keep a duty log in a hand straight enough to survive inspection. The boy who had brought Okafor his morphine on schedule for eleven months and stayed afterward to play draughts with him because Tomas complained the other orderlies treated dying men like furniture.

"He took the pages," I said. It was not a question. My voice came out of me flat as a slate.

Ada's face answered anyway. She nodded once, miserably, and told me everything she knew while we walked — fast, because there was no time, because a boy with guilt in him and a frozen river in front of him is a clock ticking down.

* * *

Here is what I learned on that walk, and I set it down exactly, because exactness is all I have ever had:

Piotr Wozniak had a brother. Antonin. Two years older, conscripted in '16, convicted in '17 for striking a sergeant who — according to every account but the official one — had earned the blow twice over. Antonin was sent to a penal unit on the eastern approaches, where men were given the work nobody wanted done and the ammunition nobody trusted, and where the survival arithmetic was simple: a man lasted four months, five if the winter was kind. Antonin had lasted fourteen. Nobody could say how. Then, in December, a review board had convened at Marrow's Ford, and a clerk from Colonel Vane's office had come to the ward office where Piotr worked evenings, and the conversation had been brief.

His brother's transfer out of the penal unit — commutation of sentence, home by spring, alive — in exchange for information about the nurse in Room 4 of the nurses' quarters. Where she kept things. When she was absent. What she was working on.

"They told him it was nothing," Ada said, hurrying beside me through the blue afternoon, our boots punching holes in the crusted snow. "That's what he keeps saying over and over, they told him it was nothing, just papers, just an old woman's papers, that it wouldn't hurt anyone. And he believed them because wanting to believe is easy when it's your brother." She stopped walking so abruptly I went two paces before I understood. "Miss Underhill — I helped him carry the floorboard up. I didn't know what was under it. I thought — we all thought you kept your sewing down there."

I looked at her. Nineteen years old, cheeks scalded red with cold and worse than cold, and I understood that I could spend my fury on her or I could save it for the man who deserved it, and there was no time for either, because somewhere ahead of us a boy was standing on river ice deciding whether the world needed him in it.

"Then you'll help me bring it back," I said. "Come on."

* * *

We found him at the old ferry landing, downstream of the bridge, where the townspeople no longer went since the new span went up. The ice there was grey and ridged, groaning in long low notes as the current beneath it shifted — the first true sounds of thaw, weeks early, and every one of them audible across the flat white distance.

He stood at the edge with his coat unbuttoned and his cap in his hands, which was somehow more frightening than if he had been at the water itself. A boy about to drown himself dresses carefully. A boy about to live has thrown his cap into the river already.

"Piotr," I called, and did not run. Running sends a message. I walked toward him the way I had walked toward a thousand frightened men in twelve years of wards — unhurried, hands visible, voice pitched to carry without carrying alarm. "Piotr Wozniak. Turn around."

"I can't," he said. His voice was wrecked. "You're here. God. You're actually here." He turned, and his face was the face of a child who has looked into the machinery of the adult world and seen the gears. "They killed him, didn't they. Mr. Okafor. The night before he was going to sign. That wasn't his wounds. Everyone says it was his wounds but everyone always says that."

"Yes," I said. There was no kindness available in any other direction. Kindness that lies is just cruelty wearing gloves.

He sat down then, right there on the ice, legs folding under him like a puppet with cut strings, and began to cry with the terrible efficiency of the very young — no performance in it, no appeal, just the body emptying itself. Ada dropped to her knees beside him. I stood over them both and felt the ledger's absence against my ribs like a missing tooth. Half of it. Gone. Two years of names, two years of the ravine, taken with a floorboard lifted so cleanly that I had suspected everyone in this town except the boy who swept my ward and sharpened my pencils and asked, only last month, with shining eyes, whether I truly remembered every name.

"The review board," I said. "When did your brother's transfer go through?"

He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "It hasn't. They said after. After you were — handled. That's the word the clerk used. Handled." He laughed, one dry broken note. "Handled. Like cargo."

"And if I'm handled badly enough — committed, reassigned, discredited — does your brother still come home?"

The silence stretched. On the ice beneath us something vast shifted, and the whole river spoke in a groan that rose through our boot soles.

"No," Piotr whispered. "No, they'd never —"

"They would," I said. "Because you have now seen their faces and heard their word, and a boy who knows what a review board costs is worth more to them drowned than delivered. Think, Piotr. You are cleverer than they assumed. That is why they chose you — clever boys believe they are being let inside the machine instead of fed into it." I crouched down to his level, knees cracking in the cold. "Listen to me. Your brother comes home if the truth gets out, not if it stays buried. Men like Vane keep their promises exactly as long as keeping them costs nothing. Once I am gone — once I am *handled* — you are a loose end with a guilty face. But if I testify, if the record breaks open, then your bargain becomes worthless paper and your brother becomes what he actually is: a soldier whose release can be granted quietly, without anyone asking why a penal unit man was suddenly worth a colonel's favor."

"You don't know that."

"No," I said. "I don't. I know almost nothing anymore except what is written down. But I will tell you what Edmund Hale taught me six years ago in a dressing station, bleeding, certain he was dying: that a lie told for love is still a debt, and debts collect interest." I held out my hand. "Give me whatever you have left. Pages, copies, the letter you wrote me. Everything."

He stared at my hand. Then he reached inside his coat and brought out a folded packet — thin, heartbreakingly thin — and laid it in my palm. Three pages of the ledger. Copies, made in the ward office after hours, clumsy and smudged. The letter he had never sent. And underneath, folded separately, worn soft at the creases: a photograph of a grinning young man with his arm around a smaller, grimmer version of himself, both squinting into a summer that existed before any of this.

"Antonin," Piotr said. "Before."

I looked at the photograph a long moment. Then I put it back in his hands.

"That one you keep," I said. "The rest I take. And tomorrow morning you will come to the chapel annex at nine o'clock, and you will tell me the name of the clerk from Vane's office, and the date of the conversation, and every word you remember of it, and you will sign your name under it."

His head came up. "Sign — you want me to *sign*? After what I —"

"Especially after what you did," I said. "That is how records work, Piotr. They don't only hold the innocent. That is the entire reason they terrify men like Vane."

* * *

I did not sleep that night. I sat in my violated room with the floorboard loose beside me and typed by lamplight behind the curtain, because my hands needed the work and because the rhythm steadies me the way prayer steadies other women. I typed the account of the ferry landing while it was fresh: date, hour, temperature, the state of the ice, the words exchanged. I typed what Ada had told me on the walk. I typed, slowly and with great care, a paragraph I had never expected to write:

*The informant was Piotr Wozniak, aged nineteen, ward clerk, trained by myself in record-keeping in March of 1918. He traded knowledge of my papers for the promised release of his brother, Antonin Wozniak, from a penal unit on the eastern approaches, where he had served fourteen months of a sentence imposed for striking a superior officer. I do not record this to excuse him. I record it because a history that contains only the crimes of the powerful is not a history; it is a mirror angled to flatter. If my book survives me, it will contain this page, and Piotr Wozniak will be in it — not as a footnote, not as a caution, but as a boy who loved his brother and was shown the price of that love by men who would never pay anything themselves.*

Mercy, I have decided, is not a feeling. Feelings arrive when they please and leave when they're needed most. Mercy is a discipline, like bandaging: you do it correctly whether or not you feel gentle, because the wound does not care about your mood, and neither does the record.

Somewhere past two in the morning I heard footsteps in the corridor — the night orderly's round, unhurried — and I stopped typing and sat in the dark listening until they faded. Vane's people knew where I slept. Whatever came next would come soon. Outside, the river groaned again, longer this time, a sound like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

At nine o'clock precisely, Piotr Wozniak came to the chapel annex with his cap in his hands and his eyes swollen and steady, and Ada Brandt came with him, uninvited and unbudging. He gave me the clerk's name — Hessler, a civilian seconded from the provincial archives, which meant Vane had reached outside the army for this, which meant premeditation — and the date, and the hour, and the exact phrasing he had carried in his head for eleven days like a swallowed stone. I typed it as he spoke. When he finished, I turned the page toward him and set the pen in his hand.

"Read it first," I said. "Every word. Then sign."

He read it twice. At the line recording his own crime he went white, but he did not look away, and when he signed, his hand did not shake.

"There's one more thing," he said, capping the pen. "Hessler keeps his files in the reading room, bottom drawer of the commission's own cabinet — he said so, boasting, the way small men boast. The original pages you lost. They're not destroyed, Miss Underhill. Vane doesn't destroy evidence. He *files* it." A ghost of something crossed his face — not quite a smile, but its cousin. "He thinks paperwork is safety. He told me that. He thinks whoever holds the paper holds the power."

I sat very still, hearing the river groan beneath the words.

"Well," I said. "So do I."

By evening the thaw had begun in earnest; the eaves dripped all night like a house counting down, and Marta Okafor arrived at my door with her coat still buttoned and a look on her face that meant she had made a decision of her own — one I would need, and sooner than either of us knew.
