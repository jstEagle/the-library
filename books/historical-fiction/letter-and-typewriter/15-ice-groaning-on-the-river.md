## 15. Ice Groaning on the River

The thaw came early that year, and it came dishonestly.

I had known the river all my life as a thing of two moods — the hard silence of January, when the ice lay over the water like a lid on a box, and the loud surrender of late March. What I had never heard before was the sound it made in the last week of February: a groaning, a long complaint that rose up through the ground itself and into the floorboards of Mrs. Aldery's boarding house, so that I woke at three in the morning with my hand already reaching under the loose board where my ledger had lived, finding nothing there but dust and the memory of dust.

The river was speaking. And everything it said was *soon*.

* * *

By daylight you could see it happening. The ice had gone grey at its edges, then rotten in its middle, the white skin blistered and darkened like old paint. Water stood in pools along the shore road. The ferryman's boy walked the bank each morning with a pole, sounding the ice the way a doctor sounds a chest, and each morning he came back shaking his head a little more.

"They'll want to cross before it goes," he told me when I stopped at the landing to listen. "Commission folk. There's a steamer coming up from Corvel as soon as the channel opens below the bend. Captain says five days, maybe six."

Five days.

I made myself smile at him. "Then they'd best finish their writing."

"Oh, they're finished," he said, cheerful in the way boys are when the news isn't theirs to carry. "Saw their clerks loading crates yesterday. Papers all sealed and ribboned. Colonel Vane had them counted twice." He spat companionably toward the water. "Whole war fit in eleven boxes, they say."

Eleven boxes. I stood at the rail of the landing and watched the ice shift and grind against the pilings, and I understood with a clarity that felt almost physical how a record dies. Not by burning. Not by decree. It dies by packing crate — by being sealed, ribboned, counted twice, and carried away across open water while the people who could contradict it stand on the shore with their mouths full of words no one has scheduled time to hear.

Eleven boxes were leaving Marrow's Ford in five or six days. Two hundred fourteen names were not in any of them.

* * *

Marta Okafor saw it too. She came to my room that evening without knocking, which she had earned the right to do, carrying a loaf she did not need an excuse to bring.

"The pawnbroker's daughter says there are men asking after you," she said, setting the bread down. "Not soldiers. Civilians. Well-dressed. They asked at the ward where you kept your hours. They asked at the chapel. This afternoon they asked at this house."

"What did Mrs. Aldery tell them?"

"That you were a quiet lodger who paid on time." Marta sat down across from me, her hands folded in her lap the way Tomas used to fold his, as if stillness were a discipline learned young. "She thought she was protecting you. Silas — what do well-dressed civilians ask after nurses for?"

I told her about the rest facility then. All of it. The letter had come that morning on commission stationery, signed by a name I didn't recognize beneath a line that said it conveyed the *gracious concern* of Colonel Vane himself. A convalescent home at Harrowmere, it said, in the southern hills, where the air was gentle and distinguished ladies of the nursing service might recover from the accumulated strains of field duty. Passage would be arranged. A carriage would call. The commission wished to express its gratitude for my cooperation.

*I have not cooperated,* I wanted to write back. *I have survived, which is a different verb.*

"Recover," Marta repeated. She said the word the way one handles a bone found in a stew. "From what? From remembering?"

"From remembering out loud," I said. "That's the diagnosis they're settling on. It isn't madness unless someone hears you."

She was quiet a moment. Outside, the river groaned — a long, deep note, like a door being forced somewhere under the world.

"My husband died the night before he was to sign," she said at last. "You told me that plainly, and I believed you, though the doctor wrote *wounds* on his paper and everyone nodded. Now they come for you with a carriage instead of a pillow. Do you see it, Silas? It is the same sentence. Only the punctuation changes."

I did see it. That was the trouble. I had spent twelve years learning to read the grammar of official kindness, and every clause of that letter was correct.

"I need time," I said. "Two days to type the final copies—"

"You've been saying two days for a week."

"And now I have fewer of them." I looked at her, and I will confess here what I could not say aloud even to her: that I was afraid in a way I had not been afraid at Kessel Ravine or in any dressing station since. Fear at the front is a clean animal. This fear was administrative. It arrived on good paper, with a courteous salutation, and it would take me away not in violence but in *care*, and no one — not Edmund, not the commission, not history itself — would ever be obliged to notice.

* * *

Edmund came the next afternoon, unannounced, and I knew before he spoke that he brought bad news, because he had dressed carefully. Men dress carefully before difficult conversations the way soldiers clean their rifles before retreat.

"Harrowmere is real," he said, standing in the middle of my small room as though unsure whether to sit. "It isn't a prison. I checked — I went so far as to look at the prospectus. Gardens, a lake, a piano. But the admissions papers are drawn up, Silas, and Vane has the medical endorsement he needs. One signature from a magistrate and you'd be on that carriage within the week."

"On what grounds?"

"A nervous condition arising from prolonged front-line service." He couldn't quite meet my eyes as he said it. "Hysteria, in the older reports. They've revived the word for cases like yours."

"Cases like mine." I laughed, and the laugh came out ugly. "Women who count."

"Silas—"

"No. Say it plainly, since we're being plain. Cases like mine are women who remember things the commission has already packed into eleven boxes."

He flinched. Good, some uncharitable part of me noted. Let him flinch.

"There may be a way to delay it," he said. "If you gave the commission your materials voluntarily — not testimony, just the pages, quietly, for review — Vane loses his pretext. A woman cooperating with the historical record cannot plausibly be called unhinged for wanting to contribute to it."

"And the pages would go into a crate. Sealed. Ribboned. Counted twice."

"Possibly consulted. Possibly—" He stopped himself. That was the whole of it, standing in the gap of that unfinished sentence: possibly never. Possibly filed under a number in an archive the size of a cathedral, in a drawer that would not be opened until everyone who remembered Kessel Ravine was dead of ordinary time.

"You're asking me to trade the only weapon I have," I said, "for the promise that someone might, someday, open the drawer."

"I'm asking you to stay free long enough to fight another day." His voice roughened. "I watched you pull men back from death with your hands, Silas. Don't let them bury you alive in a garden with a piano."

After he left I sat by the window a long while, listening to the ice. It sounded different now than it had a week ago — less groaning, more cracking, short sharp reports like distant rifle fire, the river testing its own strength. The thaw was accelerating. Everyone in town could feel it: the commission's departure date moved up twice in two days, first to Friday, then to Thursday. The steamer from Corvel was coming early. Time, which had lain frozen all winter, was running underneath everything now, fast and dark and going somewhere.

* * *

That night I typed.

I had moved the typewriter twice already — once behind the wardrobe, once into the false bottom of my linen chest — and now I worked with Marta keeping watch at the window and the pawnbroker's daughter, Lena, sitting in the hall with a novel she wasn't reading. We had developed a system. One knock meant a passerby. Two meant someone stopping at the gate. Three meant Mrs. Aldery, whose loyalties were honest but whose discretion was a colander.

The keys struck in the cold room like small determined footsteps. *Name. Rank. Unit. Date witnessed. Account.* Forty-one names remained to copy — the pages Vane's men had missed, hidden in the chapel since the theft, retrieved only two nights past. My fingers were stiff. The ribbon was wearing thin and printing half its letters pale, and I pressed harder to compensate, so that the finished pages looked strangely urgent, the early lines faded and the later ones struck black, as if the document itself were gathering voice.

Near midnight, Lena knocked once, then paused, then knocked once again — our signal for something new.

Marta lifted the curtain a fraction. "A carriage," she whispered. "Slow. No lamps. It's passing the house — it's stopped at the corner."

We put out the lamp. In the dark I sat with my hands on the keys, feeling the machine's warmth like the warmth of a living shoulder, and listened to the river crack and boom beneath the groaning. The carriage waited at the corner for perhaps ten minutes. Then it turned, unhurried, and rolled away toward the commission house.

They weren't taking me tonight. They were measuring the distance. Learning how long the drive would take, how many turns, whether the bridge — the bridge, which would be open to wheeled traffic any day now — would bear the journey to the southern road.

"They know about the typewriter," I said into the darkness. "Or they suspect. That visit wasn't random."

"Then we stop typing," Marta said.

"We type faster."

* * *

In the morning the summons came.

Not a carriage this time — a note, hand-delivered, on commission paper. Colonel Vane requested the pleasure of my attendance at the commission offices at four o'clock, regarding *final administrative matters pertaining to personnel records*. Beneath it, in smaller script, a line that managed to be both courtesy and leash: *Your cooperation in these closing days is noted with appreciation.*

I showed it to Marta. She read it twice.

"It's a trap," she said.

"Of course it's a trap. But it's also a door." I held the note up to the grey light. "They think they're closing the record on Thursday. Which means tomorrow is the last day anyone in this town can be heard by that commission — unless someone forces the question. Unless someone walks into that office and asks, formally, in front of witnesses, to give evidence."

"They'll laugh at you."

"They'll try to commit me. That's different from laughing. Laughing leaves a person standing."

Marta was silent. Then she reached into her coat and brought out a packet wrapped in oilcloth — Tomas's letters, the ones he had mailed home from the front in the months before the ravine, the ones I had read so often I knew his handwriting better than my own.

"If you go into that room," she said, "these go with you. And I go with you. He waited his whole soldier's life for someone with rank to ask him what happened. He's not waiting any longer. Neither am I."

Outside, the ice gave a tremendous report — not a crack this time but a shifting, a vast slow movement, as though something enormous had turned over in its sleep and decided to wake.

Thursday, the steamer. Tomorrow, four o'clock, the colonel's office. And underneath it all, day and night, the river counting down.

I laid the letters on the table beside my typed pages, and I began, calmly, in the fading light, to type the last of the names.

There were nine left. I intended for every one of them to be spoken aloud before the ice let go — and if the commission would not hear them in its fine sealed room, then I would read them on the steps, or the landing, or the bridge itself, wherever there were ears; because a record that lives only in drawers is a grave, and I had dug enough graves.

At four o'clock tomorrow, I would walk into Colonel Vane's office carrying two hundred fourteen reasons why he should have been kinder to me.

And God help the man who stood between those names and the air.
