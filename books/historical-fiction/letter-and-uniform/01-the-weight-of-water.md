## 1. The Weight of Water

The tide went out at five and we went out with it.

I had been walking the same mile of seabed since I was nine years old, and my feet knew it better than the floor of our house. The mud gave under each step with a sound like a slow kiss, and then the water closed around your ankles, your knees, your waist if you were lucky enough to reach the deep beds before the light came up fully. Lucky was the wrong word. The deep beds paid more. That was all luck meant on this coast: how much water you could stand in before your hands stopped working.

We worked in lines, forty women strung along the bar like beads on a string that someone had dropped and never bothered to pick up. Each of us carried a basket, a knife, and a tally stick. The company's tally man walked the shore end of the line with his book under his arm, dry as paper, counting what we brought up. Everything came up out of the sea already counted. That was the first thing you learned here — not how to dive, not how to pry an oyster loose without cracking the pearl inside, but that nothing belonged to you, not even the minutes of your own morning.

My mother had stood in these beds for twenty-two years.

She was standing in them still, in a way. Her lungs were full of the coast now, silted like a channel nobody dredges. The doctor in Port Calloway had used a word for it — *the water got into her*, he said, as though she'd swallowed the whole bay and couldn't give it back — but my mother had her own word for it too, said once, flatly, while I spooned broth into her: *debt*. She'd meant the company debt, of course, the sum written against Sandoval, M. in the great brown books at the office, growing a little every season, fed by rice and lamp oil and the year the roof failed and the year her chest began its long collapse. But lying there at night listening to her breathe — that wet, laboring sound, like oars through heavy water — I understood she'd meant both things at once. She owed the company her labor, and the sea had collected the interest early.

I dove for both of us now.

* * *

By seven the sun had cleared the headland and the water turned from pewter to something almost warm-looking, which was a lie, and the tally man rang his bell for the first count. I waded to the shallows with my basket riding high against my hip. Forty-one oysters, six of them seeded, one holding a pearl the size of a peppercorn. The tally man — Reyes, a narrow man with ink-stained fingers even out here, as if the ledger followed him everywhere like weather — weighed my basket against the brass standard and chalked the number on my stick.

"Sandoval," he said. He always said it. Not because he didn't know me. Because the number needed a name attached, and mine was the shortest one on the line.

"Forty-one," I said.

"Forty-one." He wrote it down. Somewhere in a brown book in an office that smelled of camphor and old glue, Marguerite Sandoval became a column entry again, and would remain so until dusk, when the day's total was read back to me and subtracted, never added, from what I owed.

I don't know when I stopped resenting the arithmetic. There must have been a time — my mother said I screamed at the tally man when I was small, tried to bite him over a basket of oysters he declared short-weighted — but resentment is heavy, and everything heavy gets left in the beds eventually. You learn to carry only what floats. The numbers weren't cruel; they were just true, the way the tide chart was true. You owed eleven pesos and change. A day's good diving earned four centavos against it. Do the division. The answer was years. Everyone on the line could do that division in their heads without slowing their hands, the way a sailor knows his depth by the color of the water. We didn't talk about it. Talking about it would have been like complaining about salt.

Old Marta worked two baskets down from me, her wrists wrapped in rag strips against the shells. She'd been diving since before the company built its warehouse, back when the beds were open to anyone strong enough to hold their breath, and she was the only one who ever spoke about the old days, and only in fragments, the way you'd hand someone pieces of a broken plate.

"Young hands today," she said, nodding at my fingers, nicked and gray-puckered from the shells. "Enjoy the cuts. They're honest ones."

"Marta."

"Honest cuts heal clean." She prised an oyster off the bed rock and flipped it into her basket without looking. "It's the other kind you want to watch for."

I didn't ask what other kind. She wouldn't have answered, or she'd have answered with one of her fragments, and anyway the bell rang for the second count and the morning folded itself back into work. That was the mercy of the beds. Your hands were too busy to think. It was only afterward, hauling the baskets up the shingle to the drying racks, that the mind started moving again, and I kept it moving on purpose, kept it busy with the evening's list — rice, oil, the mending for Señora Ferran, whether my mother needed the last of the willow bark tonight or could stretch it to tomorrow — because a busy mind was a shallow-water mind, and anything that sank too deep down there stayed down.

Except some things floated back up no matter how you weighted them. They waited until my hands were occupied and my guard was down, and then they surfaced.

* * *

The boy came back to me most often in the drying shed, of all places, with the smell of brine and low smoke and the racks ticking as the timber expanded. Ten years ago this spring. I was nine. The monsoon had come early that year and the company had ordered the beds worked anyway, because the buyer's ship was in harbor and buyers do not wait for weather, and I had gone out past the marker stake after a flash of nacre I thought I saw in the deep channel. I remember the moment the bottom fell away beneath my feet — literally, the shelf of sand dropping off into the channel's cold throat — and the water going from waist-deep to endless in half a heartbeat, and the way the sky looked from underneath, wobbling and white and very far away.

I did not struggle. That's the detail that comes back clearest, the one I've turned over so many times it's worn smooth. I didn't struggle because I was nine and I had already learned the first law of the coast, which is that the company owns everything, and somewhere in the deepest part of my child's mind I believed the sea was the company's too, and struggling against it would be theft.

Then a hand closed on my hair.

Not gentle. There was nothing gentle about it. He hauled me the way you'd haul a net, furious, efficient, and when we broke the surface he shoved me toward the shallows and shouted at me in island speech, fast and contemptuous, words I didn't know yet but understood completely: *what are you doing, why did you go out, are you stupid, are you trying to die.* Then he caught my eye and switched to trade-talk, the broken pidgin we shared with the fishing families who beached their proas on the north spit every season.

"The channel eats people," he said. "Everyone knows. Even company children should know."

He was maybe twelve, brown as the mangrove roots, thin enough that his shoulder blades stood up like folded wings when he wrung out his shirt. His family fished the outer banks — I'd seen their boat, painted with an eye at the bow so it could find its way home. He walked me back along the bar without another word, carrying my basket, which I found so astonishing I nearly cried. Nobody carried anything for anybody on this coast. Everything was counted.

On the shingle, before we parted, he crouched and picked up a broken shell — a pearl shell, cracked clean in half, its insides gone the color of dawn. He pressed one half into my hand and kept the other.

"So it's witnessed," he said, in that serious, half-grown way children imitate from their elders. "You owe me. And I—" he hesitated, working out how to say it, "—I owe you the warning. If ships come someday, ships with soldiers, I'll come tell you. And if ships come for *us*—" he tapped the shell half in my fist, "—you come tell us. That's fair. That's even."

Tomas. I hadn't known his name for another two seasons, when I finally worked up the courage to ask one of the fishwives whose boy he was. *That one? That's Salazar's son, the eldest. Tomas.* I had held the shell half for years after, kept it in the seam of my sleeping mat, taken it out at night when the house was quiet and my mother's breathing filled the dark. I knew exactly what I'd promised. Children keep promises the way they keep shells — carefully, seriously, without any idea of the weight involved.

Then we grew. The fisher families shifted their grounds further out after the bad season, then further again after the company posted new rules about the north spit. I saw him twice, three times, across the width of the market, and the last time he raised his hand — just slightly, the shell gesture, half a wave — and I didn't raise mine back, because Reyes was standing beside me and the tally book was open, and I had learned by then what it cost to be noticed.

I told myself the promise had been a child's thing, like the shell itself: pretty, incomplete, cracked. The sea had changed shape since then. So had I.

But I still knew where the shell half was. Under the third board of the floor, wrapped in waxed cloth. Some things you stop believing in and cannot throw away.

* * *

Evening came down on the town the way it always did, soft and salt-heavy, and I made the walk up from the racks to the company store with my chit clutched in my fist. The store was the tallest building on the coast except the warehouse, and the factor had built it tall on purpose, so that every soul in town could see it from their door and be reminded, gently, constantly, of where they stood. Inside it smelled of kerosene, dried fish, and the particular sweetness of cheap sugar stacked in hundred-weight bags. The shelves behind the counter went up higher than a man could reach, and above them, bolted to the wall, hung the framed portrait of the company director, a silver-bearded gentleman in London whom none of us would ever meet and all of us served.

The store clerk took my chit, licked his pencil, and consulted a slate where my account stood chalked in the corner reserved for the worst debts. My name wasn't on the worst corner anymore — my mother's death last autumn had transferred the full weight onto my papers alone, and somehow the transfer had come with a small administrative mercy, a few pesos forgiven as a "consolidation fee" waived. Generosity, the factor called such things. I called it what it was: they didn't forgive debt, they restructured it, the way a current restructures a sandbar. Same amount of water. New shape.

"Rice, oil, thread," I said. "And the willow bark if you've got it."

"We've got it." He weighed things out slowly, enjoying the slowness, the way small men enjoy whatever power drifts within reach. "Working the deep beds now, I hear. Alone, since your mother passed."

"My aunt's with me part of the season." This was true and also a deflection, and he knew it, and let it go, because there was no profit in me. That was the other lesson of the coast, the second one: being unprofitable was a kind of armor. The company pursued what it could gain and ignored what it couldn't. A girl with a modest debt, a strong back, and no kin worth seizing — I was beneath notice, and I had spent ten years making sure I stayed there. Useful. Unremarkable. Present for every count, absent from every conversation that mattered.

I carried my parcel out into the blue dusk. Lamps were coming on along the waterfront, and beyond the breakwater the harbor lay flat and pewter-dark, the moored luggers knocking gently at their buoys. On the north spit, faint with distance, I could see the cooking fires of the fishing camp — smaller this year than last, fewer fires, the families drifting outward year by year like sediment settling away from a disturbance.

I stood a moment longer than usual, looking at those fires. I don't know why. Habit, maybe. Or the shell, surfacing again.

Then I saw the light on the horizon.

At first I took it for a star sitting low, but stars don't sit, and this one moved — a slow, steady crawl along the edge of the world, north of the shipping lane, where no buyer's ship had any business being. It was too far out to make out a hull. But I had grown up reading water lights the way Marta read weather, and I knew the spacing of a steamer's running lamps when I saw them, and I knew, too, that steamers bound for our harbor announced themselves to the factor by signal flag hours ahead, and no announcement had been made.

The light crawled. It did not turn toward the harbor. It held its course, parallel to the coast, patient as the tally man's pencil.

Behind me the store clerk laughed at something, a customer's joke, ordinary human noise. The fishwives were calling their children home off the shingle. Somewhere a dog barked at the tide. Everything continued exactly as it always continued, and I stood there with my parcel of rice and willow bark, watching a stranger's light move along the rim of the sea, feeling — without reason I could have named, without anything I could have put into words for Reyes or Marta or anyone — the first faint shift of the ground under my feet, the sensation, familiar from the deep channel, of the bottom quietly giving way.

I went home. I lit the lamp. I fed my mother's memory its medicine and sat by the cold stove doing the night's arithmetic, and the whole time my eyes kept going to the window, to the north, to the place where the light had been.

Three days later, the gunboats came.
