## 2. Ledger Days

The company office smelled of camphor and dried squid, and by the third week Marguerite had stopped noticing either. What she never stopped noticing was the quiet. After the beds—after hours of cold water pressing on her chest and the scrape of shells under her nails—the office was a different country. Here the sea did its work without getting anyone wet.

She copied accounts.

That was all they told her she did. Copying: the factor's figures into the fair books, column after column of names and numbers in her careful schoolroom hand, which was better than the clerk's own and everyone knew it. Señor Anselmo had eyes that were going soft at the edges, and a tremor in his wrist that made his sevens look like drunken little flags, and so three mornings a week Marguerite Sandoval sat at the second desk with a blotter, a steel-nibbed pen, and the entire economic life of the coast spread open before her like a patient waiting for surgery.

She learned fast what a person was worth.

Not worth—owed. That was the word the ledger used, and it was more honest. The Delgados owed eleven pesos and four reales against the 1908 season, carried forward, interest accruing. Old Bautista owed his lungs, though the ledger didn't have a column for that; it had only the figure for the cough medicine he bought on credit from the company store, and the next year's figure, and the next, each one larger, a staircase descending. The Reyes family owed their boat, then their boat's replacement value when the boat "was lost in weather"—Marguerite knew there had been no weather; she'd been on the beach that night—and then their labor, priced at whatever the factor decided labor was worth that season. She copied the entry twice because her hand had slipped the first time, not from fear but from something hotter she had to swallow down before the nib would behave.

Every family on the coast was in those books. Every birth was an asset entered at the moment of christening, every death a line struck through with the date and the balance transferred to survivors. A man could be born owing and die owing, and between those two events he would dive a thousand times into water that wanted to kill him, and the whole of it would amount to a figure in a column, and the figure would be wrong anyway, because the interest was calculated in the factor's head and Marguerite had checked the arithmetic herself, once, carefully, and found that the Delgados were owed back four pesos and change if anyone had bothered to run the sums honestly.

No one bothered. She put the four pesos in the fair copy where the original said they should be, and she thought about it for days afterward, the way you think about a stone in your shoe.

* * *

It was Anselmo who taught her the geography of the office without meaning to, or perhaps meaning to exactly as much as he meant everything, which was just enough to deny later.

"The originals stay on the shelf," he said, the first time her fingers drifted toward the fat leather volumes chained—actually chained, a hasp and padlock through iron rings—to the standing desk by the window. "The fair copies are yours. The daybooks are mine. You do not touch the daybooks."

"Why not?"

He looked at her over the spectacles he wore for reading and removed for scolding. "Because a copy is a courtesy. An original is a truth. And truths in this building belong to the company."

"That doesn't answer me."

"No." He almost smiled. His face was built out of dried river clay, all cracks and patience, sixty years of monsoon seasons pressed into it. "It doesn't. It answers the question you'll be asked, though, if anyone ever asks. *Why did you touch them?* is a question with no good ending. Better never to have to begin it."

She went back to copying. But something had shifted in her, the way a boat shifts when cargo settles wrong. She had spent seventeen years learning to be unremarkable—her mother's first lesson, delivered in whispers over a mother who couldn't shout anymore: *be useful, be small, be the girl who works and says nothing*—and here in this room was the machinery of unremarkableness itself, and it ran on paper. Not force. Not soldiers. Paper. The Reyeses hadn't lost their boat to weather; they'd lost it to an entry, made by a man at this desk, in ink, while the rain came down outside. The entry had done everything. The rain had merely been present.

She began, without deciding to, to read what she copied. Not skim—read. Names she'd known her whole life became legible in a new way. The fishwife Rosa Ibarra, who sang while she mended nets and gave Marguerite dried fish on feast days, was also *Ibarra, R.—advances against catch, 14 pesos 2 reales, guarantor: none*, and the *none* was its own kind of biography, because everyone else had a guarantor and Rosa had refused one, and Marguerite understood without being told that Rosa had refused it the way you refuse a collar: visibly, so everyone could see the refusal, and alone, so no one else would pay for it.

And then, on a Thursday, in the box of loose instruments filed under *S*, she found her own name.

Her indenture papers. Her mother's signature—or the mark that stood for it, a wobbly cross, since her mother had never been taught letters—and the factor's countersignature, and the terms: *Sandoval, M., minor, bound to the service of the Company for a term of seven years, wages applied against household account, said account standing at—*

The number. She stared at the number. It was larger than she expected, large enough that she did the arithmetic twice, and both times the sum said the same thing: that if she dove every working day until the term expired and paid nothing toward food or lamp oil or the medicine that kept her mother breathing, she might, *might*, walk free at twenty-four with nothing owed. Any sickness, any storm, any season of bad beds, and the term would extend itself like a tide coming in.

She had known this. Of course she had known this. She had lived inside this number her whole life the way a fish lives inside water, without seeing it. But seeing it written down—in her own future tense, in ink that was already dry—was different. It was the difference between knowing the sea is deep and looking down through the green light at the bottom.

"Found yourself, have you."

Anselmo had come up beside her without a sound. He didn't take the papers away. He looked at them with her, his hands folded behind his back like a man regarding a painting.

"How long has my account been growing?" she asked.

"Since before you could walk. Your father opened it." He said it without cruelty, the way you report weather. "He signed for a boat. The boat drowned him. The debt stayed afloat. That is how these things work—they are built to float, child. Everything else sinks."

"And nobody checks the sums."

"The sums are correct."

"They aren't. The Delgado entry is off by four pesos in the company's favor. I've checked it three times."

Something moved behind his eyes—not surprise, she realized later, but a kind of tired confirmation, the look of a man who has been waiting years for someone to say the thing out loud. He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, slowly, giving himself somewhere to look.

"Do you know why I let you copy?" he said. "There are boys in this town who can write faster than you. There is a factor's nephew who wants the seat. I keep you here because your hand is steady and because—" he set the spectacles back on his nose— "because you are the kind of person who checks the sum three times. And I am going to tell you the most valuable thing I know, and it will sound like nothing, and someday, if you live long enough, it will sound like everything."

He leaned close enough that she could smell the camphor on him.

"Power does not live in the pearls," he said. "Or in the boats, or in the guns the factor keeps in the back room, God help us. Power lives in paper. Whoever holds the paper holds the truth, and whoever holds the truth owns the people in it. The company knows this. That is why the daybooks are chained. That is why your own name is kept in a locked box under *S*. That is why I am old and careful and have never once, in forty-one years, corrected a figure that favored a debtor." He straightened. "Because the day I do, I stop being the keeper of the truth and become a man with a motive. Do you understand?"

"You're afraid."

"I'm employed," he said. "It looks similar from the outside."

* * *

She worked late that Thursday, finishing the fair copy of the season's advances, and the office emptied around her the way offices do—Anselmo first, then the factor's boy, then the lamplighter passing in the street, his pole tapping the cobbles. She trimmed the wick herself. The light went gold and small, a private sun, and the shadows in the corners grew long and friendly, and she sat with the finished pages squared in front of her and did not leave.

Instead she looked at the daybooks.

Chained. Padlocked. Forty-one years of Anselmo's careful, cowardly, complicit handwriting in them, and beneath his entries the entries of the men before him, going back past the founding of anything she knew, the whole coast's memory in leather and iron. Everything true was in there. Everything that mattered. The copies she made were shadows of it, permitted shadows, and she suddenly hated them—hated the neatness of her own hand, the obedience of it, the way it reproduced error without comment, four pesos at a time.

She got up. She crossed the room. She stood before the standing desk with the lamp held high, the way you hold a lantern over a well, and she looked down at the daybook lying open to the current week, and she read her own page.

*Sandoval.* There it was in Anselmo's failing script, the weekly entry: wages credited, advances charged, the balance marching in the wrong direction with beautiful consistency. Below it, in an older hand, faded brown: *bond of rescue witnessed, see instrument file, ref. T.*

Ref. T.

She stood very still. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself. Somewhere outside a dog was barking at the tide, and the harbor water slapped the pilings below the window with a patience older than the company, older than the town, older than any paper that claimed to own either.

She remembered, briefly—a flash, salt and panic and a small brown hand closing on her wrist—remembered and put it away, the way she always did, the way you fold a letter and slip it under the lining of a suitcase you don't open.

*T.* For Tomas. For a promise made by a nine-year-old girl over a broken shell, with the sea still dripping out of her lungs, witnessed by no one but two children and the moon. Witnessed by no one—and yet here it was, entered in the company's own book, because the company recorded everything, because even kindness, once it touched the coast, became an entry, a reference, a fact that could be looked up.

Someone had written it down. Someone had thought it worth writing down. Which meant it existed in the way that only paper made things exist—which meant it could be found, and read, and used.

Marguerite returned to her desk. She banked the lamp. She squared her pages, capped the ink, and left the office the ordinary way, locking nothing, taking nothing, touching nothing, her heart going like a diver's pulse in deep water.

But walking home along the harbor road, past the dark warehouses and the sleeping boats, she caught herself doing something new. She was memorizing. The lock on the daybook chain—brass, three levers, worn at the shoulder. The hour the factor's boy left—always before dusk on Thursdays, because he had a woman in the next village. The shelf where the instrument files slept, and the exact position of the box marked *S*.

She stopped at the end of the pier, where the water breathed below her, and looked north along the coast to where the mangroves began, black against the last light.

Knowing too much, Anselmo had warned her, was the danger. But he was old, and old men mistake knowledge for a weight you carry. Standing there in the dark with the tide coming in, Marguerite felt it differently. Knowledge wasn't weight.

It was ballast. And ballast, properly placed, was the difference between a boat that capsized and a boat that ran before the wind.

Three nights later, smoke rose from the mangrove side of the bay—thin, controlled, cooking-smoke, the kind islanders made and no one else—and Marguerite, watching from her doorway with her mother's shawl around her shoulders, understood that the past had come to find her.

She just didn't know yet that the army was coming to find it too.
