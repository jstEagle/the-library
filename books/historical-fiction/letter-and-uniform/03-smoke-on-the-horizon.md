## 3. Smoke on the Horizon

The tide went out far enough at midnight that a man could walk the mudflats below the drying racks without leaving a print worth reading. Tomas knew this because he had spent ten years learning every way a body could move through this coast unseen — first as a boy running messages for his father between the island camps, later because being seen had stopped being safe.

He came up from the waterline with his trouser legs dark to the knee and the smell of the flats on him, salt and rot and something older underneath. The town slept in the shape the company had built it: rows of company houses, company lamps, company dogs that knew which strangers to bark at. But the dogs were his mother's cousins' dogs, three generations back, before anyone thought to draw a line through the water and put a word like *enemy* on one side of it. They lifted their heads when he passed and lay down again.

Marguerite Sandoval's house was the last one before the sea wall, small enough that the company had nearly forgotten it existed. A lamp burned behind the shutter. She was still awake. She was always still awake; he'd learned that much in the two nights he'd come here already, standing in the dark below her window like something out of a worse story than theirs, working up the nerve.

Tonight he didn't work up the nerve. Tonight there wasn't time.

He tapped the wall twice, then once more — the old signal, shell against hull, the rhythm they'd made up on the sandbar the summer they were nine. He heard the lamp set down. The shutter opened an inch.

"Go away," she whispered.

"I need you to hear me first."

"I said go *away*, Tomas."

"And I'm asking you to remember a thing," he said. "That's all. Remember it, and then send me away if you want."

A long silence. Somewhere down the row a baby cried and was hushed. Then the shutter closed, and he heard the bar lift inside, and hated himself a little for what he was about to make her carry.

* * *

She wouldn't let him past the doorway. She stood with her back against the inner wall, arms crossed, wearing a man's coat over her shift because the nights had turned cold early this year. Her hair was loose and she hadn't expected anyone and she looked, for a moment, so exactly like the girl on the sandbar that his chest hurt.

"You shouldn't be here," she said. "If anyone sees you—"

"No one sees anything after dark on this coast unless they're paid to."

"That's not comforting, Tomas. That's the opposite of comforting." But she glanced past him at the empty street anyway, and stepped aside just far enough that he could stand in the doorway, half in, half out, like a draft of cold air she hadn't managed to shut out.

He put his hand in his pocket and brought out what he'd carried six miles through the mangroves: a piece of pearl shell, cracked clean across, its nacre gone grey with age. He held it out flat on his palm between them.

She looked at it the way people look at a snake.

"Put that away."

"It's ours."

"It's *nothing*. It's a broken shell. Ten years old. It doesn't mean anything anymore."

"You kept your half," he said quietly. "I know you did. Your mother sewed it into your coat lining when we were twelve and you wore that coat until the seams gave out, and I'd bet my boat you transferred it to the new one yourself, stitch by stitch, telling yourself it was sentiment and meaning nothing by it."

Her jaw tightened. He'd seen her do that in the office window once, years ago, copying figures under the clerk's eye — a girl building a wall out of her own face. She'd gotten very good at it since.

"What do you want?" she said.

"The truth is coming whether you want it or not. So I came to give it to you first, while it can still do some good." He turned the shell over in his fingers. "There are gunboats assembling at Port Anselm. My cousin works the pilot station up the strait — he counted seven masts yesterday, steam, not sail, flying the colonial ensign. They're taking on coal and marines. And the factor at Anselm has been buying rice in quantities no trading post needs. You know what that means better than most. Armies eat before they march."

"They're always drilling somewhere," Marguerite said. "There's always some show of force. It keeps the shareholders calm."

"This isn't a show." He made his voice flat, the way his father delivered bad weather. "The archipelago councils refused the new licensing terms in the spring. All of them. Every island. My father's council among them. The Resident wrote back saying the matter would be resolved by year's end. Marguerite — nobody resolves a licensing dispute with seven gunboats and forty tons of rice. This is the war. It's decided. It's only waiting for the season."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the door. He watched her watch it, watched her do the arithmetic he knew she couldn't help doing — the same arithmetic she did with everything, debts and tides and days, everything reduced to what could be counted and carried.

"So warn them," she said finally. "Warn the islands. That's your family's business, not mine. I copy accounts in an office. I mend nets. I don't have any part in—"

"You have the oldest part there is." His voice came out harder than he intended, and he saw her flinch, and pressed on anyway because softness would let her off the hook and the hook was the whole point. "You swore. Over that shell, on the sandbar, with the sea in both our ears. You said: *if ships ever come for us, I will warn your family.* Not if it was convenient. Not if the timing suited. If ships came."

"I was nine years old."

"Yes."

"Children say things."

"Children mean things," he said. "It's adults who learn to stop."

* * *

She turned away from him then, went to the window, put her hand flat against the shutter as if checking whether the world outside was still where she'd left it. He waited. He was good at waiting; the sea had taught him that too.

"My mother drowned slowly," she said to the shutter. "Did you know that? Everyone thinks drowning is fast. Hers took eleven years. The beds took her lungs a spoonful at a time, and the company docked her pay for every day she couldn't dive, and charged interest on the medicine, and by the end we owed them more for her dying than she'd earned alive in twenty years." She laughed — a dry, terrible sound. "I tell you this so you understand what I am now. I am a woman who has learned that promises are a luxury item. Sold in the company store, priced beyond reach. I survived by being small and useful and forgetting things. That's not cowardice, Tomas. That's a skill. It kept a roof over—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I owe the company four hundred pesos. If I lose the copying work, they take the house, and I go into the sheds, and the sheds kill women slower than the beds but just as surely."

"I'm not asking you to burn anything down."

"Aren't you? Listen to what you're actually saying. Gunboats are coming. War is coming. And you want me to stand in front of it holding a promise I made barefoot in the surf, like that counts for something against a marine battalion."

"It counts," Tomas said. "It's the only currency on this coast that isn't theirs."

She looked at him then, really looked, and he saw the fight going out of her posture before it left her face — the wall still up but someone knocking on it steadily, patiently, from the other side.

"If I'm seen talking to your family," she said, "if one fishwife mentions it, if the clerk notices I've been walking the flats at night — Tomas, they keep records. Everything on this coast goes in a book eventually. Who owes, who's born where, who signed what. There'll be lists. There are always lists, and lists turn into arrests, and arrests turn into boats going north, and I have seen the shipping manifests for those boats. Do you know what 'indentured labor, destination unspecified' means in the fine columns?"

"I know what it means."

"Then don't ask me this."

"I'm not asking you to fight," he said. "I'm asking you to remember. There's a difference, and right now it's the width of the world." He stepped forward and set the broken shell on her table, beside the lamp, in the light, where she couldn't pretend not to see it. "Ships are coming for us, Marguerite. Real ones, with guns. All I need from you is whatever warning you can manage, however small, whenever you can manage it. A note. A look. Gossip in the market. You told me yourself — the fishwives carry more intelligence than the Resident's entire staff, and nobody watches them because nobody thinks women gossiping about herring matters. Be invisible. Be small and useful. Use the skill that kept you alive." He moved toward the door. "Just point it at us this once."

* * *

At the threshold he stopped. Behind him she hadn't moved from the window, and in the lamplight her face was doing something complicated — fear and guilt wrestling for the same ground, neither winning.

"Tomas." Her voice was low. "Why me? Why not the priests, or the harbor master, or—"

"Because you're the only person on this coast who ever promised," he said simply. "And because you of all people know how paper works. When the lists get written — and they will get written — I wanted you to see your own name on them ahead of time. To know what side of the ink you're standing on before someone else decides for you." He pulled the door open onto the cold and the smell of the tide. "My father says the camps will hold through the season. After that, we scatter to the outer shoals, and finding us becomes hard even for people who love us."

"And if I do nothing?"

He looked back at her one last time. He didn't answer with words. He touched the table where the broken shell lay, once, lightly, the way you'd touch a grave marker or a sleeping child, and stepped out into the dark.

The mudflats took his footprints behind him as the tide began to turn.

* * *

He did not go straight home. He walked out along the sea wall instead and stood where he could see the whole town laid out below him — the warehouses, the company store with its painted sign, the office where a lamp still burned because the clerks worked late during inventory season. Seven gunboats at Anselm. Forty tons of rice. By year's end.

Somewhere in that office, he thought, there was a page with his father's name on it. Fishing rights, deed-papers, the license renewals the council had refused — everything his family was on this coast, reduced to entries in a book locked in a building full of sleeping men. His father believed paper was sacred, that rights lived in documents the way souls lived in bodies. His father had never watched the company write and rewrite a debt until a dead woman's children inherited it.

Tomas had watched that happen. He'd been nine years old, standing on a sandbar, watching a girl nearly drown, and he'd learned it young: the sea keeps no ledger. Only men do. And what men write, other men can change — if they have access, and a steady hand, and a reason.

He thought of Marguerite's face in the lamplight, the wall and the knock upon it. She would say yes or she would say nothing, and either way she would lie awake tonight doing her arithmetic, and somewhere in the counting the promise would surface, because the past on this coast was like the oyster shells under the mud — you could walk on it for years, but it never went deeper than a boot's length down.

Far out on the black water, where the horizon should have been empty, he thought he saw a smudge of smoke. Coal smoke, low and broad, moving south toward Anselm. Or perhaps it was cloud. Perhaps it was nothing.

He stood a long time anyway, watching it, memorizing its heading.

Three days later, the gunboats anchored in the harbor — seven of them, exactly as his cousin had counted — and the soldiers came ashore at dawn, and the first thing they requisitioned was not the warehouses, not the stores, not the boats.

It was the ledgers.
