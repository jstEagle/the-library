## 4. Requisition

The gunboats came on a Tuesday, which felt obscene. Disasters should arrive on Sundays, when the town was already dressed for grief.

Marguerite heard them before she saw them—three long notes from the harbor bell, rung by old Ferro who rang it for storms and for ships flying colors he didn't recognize. She was at the company office early, copying the quarterly accounts in her small even hand, and she set down her pen and went to the window because everyone did.

Three gunboats sat low in the harbor like gray animals come to drink. Not trading vessels, not patrol cutters with their peeling paint and bored crews. These had the clean, hungry lines of things built for one purpose, and their guns—she counted without meaning to—were trained not outward toward the open sea but inward, toward the town, as if the town itself were something to be subdued.

"They're early," said Mr. Anselm, the factor's deputy, coming up beside her. He said it the way a man remarks that rain has arrived ahead of forecast—as though the war were a weather event someone else had scheduled.

"Early for what?" Marguerite asked, though she knew. Everyone on the coast had known for months, the way you know a storm is building past the horizon: rumor first, then prices, then silence. The archipelago was enemy now. The word *enemy* had been printed in newspapers that came north twice a month, and men in the cantina had begun saying it carefully, tasting it, learning how it fit in the mouth.

"Early," Anselm repeated, as if repetition were an answer, and went downstairs to put on his good coat.

* * *

By nine o'clock the harbor road was full of soldiers.

They came ashore in launches, rank after rank of them, boots finding the planks of the jetty in unison so that the whole structure seemed to breathe. They were young, most of them, sunburned and serious, carrying rifles with the exaggerated care of men who had drilled with them but never fired at anything that could fire back. Officers moved among them in white uniforms already going translucent with sweat, calling out numbers, streets, names of buildings they could not yet have learned—and yet they knew them. That was the thing that made the hair rise on Marguerite's arms. They knew the town. Someone had drawn its map, listed its warehouses, counted its doors, and none of that work had happened here.

A major with a red-striped cap stood on the jetty reading from a document while the town gathered at the respectful distance crowds always keep from power. His voice carried flat across the water.

"—by authority of the Provisional Military Administration, all port facilities, storage facilities, vessels exceeding five tons, dwellings designated by requisition order, and commercial records are hereby placed under military jurisdiction—"

*Commercial records.* The phrase slid past most of the crowd like a fish too quick to net. But Marguerite saw Mr. Voss—the clerk, old Voss, who had taught her her figures and her place in equal measure—go still in a way she had never seen him go still. He stood near the office door with his keys in his hand, forty years of keys, and he watched the soldiers the way a man watches water rising around his feet.

She had worked beside him for three years. She knew every mood he owned: the dry irritation of audit season, the sour satisfaction of catching a debtor's arithmetic error, the rare, brief warmth he showed when she copied a page exactly to his standard. This was none of those. This was a man watching his life's work being reclassified as cargo.

* * *

They took the warehouse district first, because warehouses are simple: padlocks changed, sentries posted, chalk marks made on doors. Then they took the drying sheds and the boatyard, and then, in the afternoon, they came for the office.

Two lieutenants and six soldiers filed into the counting room where Marguerite sat among her neat columns. One of the lieutenants was young and polite and apologized for the intrusion in a voice that had been trained to apologize the way other men are trained to shoot. The other said nothing. He walked the shelves with his hands behind his back, reading spines.

"The ledgers," he said finally. "All of them."

Voss came forward so quickly he nearly stumbled. "Sir, those are proprietary records of the Pearl Coast Trading Company. I have instructions from the home office regarding—"

"You have new instructions." The silent lieutenant held out his hand for the requisition order, glanced at it as if he needed no glance, and handed it back. "Census rolls, debt registers, indenture papers, correspondence files. Everything. You'll be provided a receipt."

"A receipt," Voss said, and the word came out cracked down the middle.

It took them four hours.

Marguerite was ordered to assist—her knowledge of the filing system made her useful, and usefulness, as always, was her safest garment—so she spent those hours carrying armloads of the company's memory to crates lined with sacking. She handled the debt registers she had copied from, the ones where every family on the coast was reduced to sums: the Cortez brothers, eleven pesos against next season's catch; the widow Palma, her husband's diving gear held as collateral; page after page of lives measured in what they owed.

And then she lifted the book she had been dreading since the lieutenant said the word *ledgers*.

The master register. The original. The one Voss had told her never to touch, back in her second week, when her fingers had strayed toward it and his hand had come down over hers like a lid. She knew what was inside it—not just debts but births, kinships, papers signed between island families and shore families in the years when such papers had been prudent rather than treasonous. She knew, because she had once caught a glimpse of a page while reaching for a file, that somewhere in that book was an entry with her name beside it. *Sandoval.* A witness notation. A bond sworn.

Ten years old, and she had never spoken of it, and neither had Voss, and she had allowed herself to believe—because survival required believing—that paper forgotten was paper harmless.

Now two soldiers carried that belief out the door in a crate marked with chalk.

She watched from the doorway as the crates went onto a handcart, then onto a launch. The harbor light lay flat and gold on the water. The master register rode on top of the stack, its leather corners worn soft by forty years of Voss's hands, and a soldier sat beside it with his rifle across his knees, guarding her name the way he would guard ammunition.

"There goes everything we are," Voss said behind her. He had come to stand in the doorway too, and his voice had gone very quiet, very old. "Every debt. Every birth. Every promise anyone ever wrote down. In a crate."

Marguerite said nothing. Promises, she thought, were mostly kept by not writing them down. Her own best one lived only in memory—in salt water and a boy's brown hand closing around her wrist—and paper could not hold what it had never touched.

But she had seen the ledger's spine pass within arm's reach, and she understood, with a cold settling feeling, that the army now possessed the difference between what people remembered and what could be proven.

* * *

That evening the town held its breath and lit its lamps anyway.

At the company store, where Marguerite queued for rice behind a dozen neighbors, the talk ran low and fast. The garrison would occupy the customs house. Boats over five tons were impounded—Ferro's among them, though Ferro swore his leaked worse than a basket. Curfew at dusk. Soldiers paid in scrip the store had been ordered to accept, which meant the army had found a way to make even its money a form of requisition.

"They'll want divers," said the widow Palma, shifting her bundle. "Island water or not. My boys are strong swimmers, and strong swimmers get conscripted."

"They'll want lists more than divers," said old Marta, who sold dried fish and missed nothing. "You mark my words. Guns are for after. First comes the knowing who everyone is."

Marguerite kept her eyes down and her basket full and said nothing at all, which had kept her alive for twenty-six years. But walking home along the harbor road, past the customs house where lamplight burned late in windows that had been dark for a decade, she felt the invisibility shift under her like sand.

For ten years she had been nobody: the clerk's quiet copyist, the drowned mother's daughter, a woman whose debts were modest and whose face opened no doors. Nobody had served her well. Nobody had kept Tomas's night visits unnoticed, kept the fishwives' gossip flowing around her like water around a stone.

But the army had a crate full of pages that said otherwise. Somewhere in the customs house, under a soldier's rifle, lay a line of ink with her name attached—a child's oath recorded in a clerk's careful hand, sworn over a broken pearl shell she could still feel between her palms when she let herself remember.

Lieutenant Harrow, the men were already saying—the officer in charge of the records, a young man with a reputation from the capital for finding things in files that files didn't know they held.

He would read the whole book. Men like him always did.

Marguerite climbed the path to her house above the tideline and stood a moment looking down at the black water where, ten years ago, a boy had pulled her gasping into the air and made her swear. The sea gave nothing back, as usual. It never promised anything either.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would ask Voss exactly what the entry said. Word for word. Because a woman cannot defend a page she has never read—

and because, deep in the marrow where dread keeps its accounts, she already suspected that the entry named not only her, but him.
