## 6. Busy Hands

The army posted its notice on the company store door the morning after the gunboats' searchlights had kept half the town awake. Marguerite read it twice, standing in a queue of women waiting for flour they could no longer afford at the old price.

*Laborers required. Laundry, mending, kitchen work. Apply to Sergeant Doss, garrison annex. Wages: one shilling per day.*

One shilling. The company paid three for a full day on the beds, and the beds were closed now anyway—declared off-limits until "security conditions improved," which was the army's way of saying the oysters would rot in their cages while officers decided who owned what. A shilling was robbery. It was also the only coin in town that didn't pass through the factor's hands anymore, and more than that, it came with a pass through the garrison gate.

Marguerite took her place in the line of applicants and made herself small. This was not difficult. Ten years of practice stood behind it: shoulders rounded, eyes down, the face arranged into the mild blankness of a woman whose thoughts never rose above soap and thread. She had learned young that the invisible were left alone, and she had built a life inside that lesson like a hermit crab in someone else's shell.

But invisibility, she was beginning to understand, had two edges. The same quality that kept you safe kept you useless. And there were people now—a boy in the mangroves, a family with no papers, an old clerk dying by degrees behind his desk—who needed her to be something other than safe.

Sergeant Doss looked up from his list when her turn came. He was a heavy man with sunburned ears and the air of someone who found all paperwork distasteful but all women interchangeable.

"Name?"

"Sandoval. Marguerite Sandoval."

"You any good with a needle?"

"I've mended sails since I was eight, sir."

"Sails." He wrote something. "Laundry detail's six days a week. Mending's whatever comes off the uniforms. You'll be working out of the annex building, east wing. You cause trouble, you steal so much as a button, it's the stockade. Understand?"

"Yes, sir."

He waved her off without looking up, and Marguerite walked back through the gate she'd just come through, this time wearing a paper chit that said *AUTHORIZED*, and felt the strangest sensation in her chest—not fear exactly, though fear was there too, but something closer to the moment a diver slips beneath the surface and the noise of the world goes away.

She knew where the east wing was. Everyone did; it had been the company's record annexe before the requisition, which meant the records room—the room where the ledgers lived now, under army lock instead of company lock—was forty feet from where she'd be boiling sheets.

Forty feet. She could cross it in twenty steps. She intended to learn every one of them.

* * *

The first week taught her the shape of the place the way her palms had once learned the shape of an oyster knife.

The garrison had swallowed the waterfront whole. The warehouses held grain and rifle crates now; the drying racks had been torn down to build a parade ground that flooded at every tide because no soldier thought to ask the fishwives about drainage. Soldiers moved through it all in patterns, and Marguerite discovered that patterns were a language anyone patient could read.

There were four sentry posts on the perimeter. The north gate post changed at dawn and again at midday, and the relief always ran ten minutes late because the corporal who walked the change stopped at the cookhouse for coffee. The east wall post—the one facing the annex—was manned by a private named Whitlock, or at least the others called him Whitlock, a gangly boy of perhaps nineteen who spent the slow hours between rounds picking at the mortar between the wall stones. At dusk the entire watch doubled for two hours, then thinned again after the officers' mess closed and the last lanterns went dark over by the factor's seized residence.

None of this was secret. That was the thing that surprised her most, sitting over the wash-tubs with steam rising and her hands going red and raw: soldiers talked. They complained about their rotations within earshot of the very women scrubbing their collars. They grumbled about Lieutenant Harrow's inspections, about the records room being locked even to the adjutant's own clerk after seven bells, about Harrow himself—"reads everything, that one, sleeps with the ledgers under his pillow, my missus says"—and they said it all in the flat, unguarded voices of men who had decided the laundry women were furniture.

Furniture with ears. Marguerite filed every word.

She worked beside five other women. Old Petra, whose fingers were too knotted with salt-rot to feel a needle but who could smell a lie at forty paces. The Consuelo sisters, who bickered constantly and forgave instantly and between whom information moved faster than water downhill. A girl called Nena, sixteen, who had been promised in marriage to a soldier and hated it and therefore hated all soldiers equally and indiscriminately. And Berta Almeida, broad-backed and silent, whose brother-in-law had been taken in the first arrests at dawn—that memory still sat in Marguerite's chest like a stone she couldn't cough up—and who had come to the garrison, Marguerite suspected, for reasons much like her own.

They did not speak of it directly. Nothing was spoken of directly. But on the third evening, as they wrung out sheets together in the fading light, Berta said, without looking at her:

"The north gate runs late of a morning. My sister's boy delivers ice to the mess. He says nobody counts the carts before seven."

Marguerite wrung the sheet, watched the gray water spiral away. "Ice carts go in and out all day," she said. "Nobody looks at an ice cart."

"No," Berta agreed. "Nobody does."

That was all. That was everything. The fishwives' network had run gossip and grievance along the coast for generations—prices, pregnancies, which husband drank, which company overseer took bribes—and it absorbed the new business the way the tide absorbs rain, without effort, without visible seam. By the end of the week, warnings Marguerite passed at the wash-tubs were reaching the mangrove camps by way of daughters and cousins and a deaf grandmother who read lips with terrifying accuracy: *the sweeps are moving to the south shore; the patrol boat runs the channel at slack water; the stockade took eleven more on Thursday.*

And somewhere out in the green tangle beyond the town's edge, Tomas would hear it. She had no way to send word to him directly—his family had gone to ground after the arrests, deeper into the mangroves, and she hadn't seen him since the night on the jetty—but the network carried things whether you asked it to or not, and she had to believe it reached him. Had to. Belief, these days, was less a feeling than a load-bearing wall.

* * *

On the ninth day, the mending brought her close enough to touch the door.

It was an accident, or seemed one: a sack of uniforms needing repair was carried into the annex corridor rather than the workroom because the workroom was flooded from a burst pipe, and so Marguerite sat on a stool outside the records room itself with a basket of trousers at her feet, stitching name-tapes while a corporal stood guard ten feet away and pretended not to fall asleep.

The door was oak, older than the army's presence here. Company oak. The brass plate had been unscrewed—she could see the four pale holes where it had been—but the lock was new, iron, military, with a keyhole that gleamed from use. Above the lintel, a faded stencil in the factor's neat hand still read: *LEDGER ANNEXE — NO ADMITTANCE.*

She stitched. Her needle moved in its steady rhythm, and her eyes stayed on her work, and her mind laid the corridor out like cloth on a cutting table. Door faces east; light from the high window falls across the lock until mid-afternoon. Corporal stands to the left of the door, back to the wall, sightline blocked by the corner to the south. The corridor runs sixty feet to the yard door, which is barred but not locked, because the yard is inside the perimeter. Seven bells, the soldiers had complained, and the room seals till morning. Seven bells was nine o'clock by the church clock, and the church clock ran fast.

"Good work," the corporal said suddenly, and Marguerite's heart slammed once against her ribs before she understood he meant the stitching.

"Thank you, sir."

"My missus can't sew to save her life. You do the buttons reinforced, eh? These island shirts, the buttons tear clean off."

Island shirts. She looked down at the garment in her lap properly for the first time: light cotton, cut loose, dyed a deep indigo—the uniform of the laborers the army had already conscripted from among the islanders rounded up in the first weeks, men who now unloaded grain and dug latrines within sight of water they weren't permitted to reach. Someone had torn the name-tape from the collar. She could see the stitch-holes where it had been.

"They don't get names?" she asked, keeping her voice mild, curious, empty.

Corporal shrugged. "Numbers. Easier to shout a number."

She finished the shirt. Before folding it onto the pile, she turned the cuff back once, briefly, and pressed her thumbnail hard into the hem—a habit, a signature, the kind of tiny irregularity a laundress leaves without meaning anything by it. Then she folded it like all the others.

That night she lay awake doing arithmetic. Not debt-arithmetic; she had given up on that ledger long ago. This was different: minutes and footsteps, bell-times and tide-times, the distance from the wash-tubs to the corridor measured in seconds if you walked it carrying a basket, unhurried, invisible. Forty feet. Twenty steps. She counted them against the ceiling until sleep took her, and dreamed of doors opening onto nothing at all.

* * *

The warning came on the twelfth day, and it nearly came too late.

Nena brought it in from the market, breathless, disguised as complaint. "You should see the price of rice, it's criminal, and the sergeant's wife bought up half the stock because—" a glance toward the doorway, where a soldier passed and faded— "*they're moving the sweep up. Friday. The south shore camps, all of them, boats and everything.*"

Friday was two days off. The south shore camps held maybe thirty souls, Tomas's family among them, hunkered in the mangroves where the channels narrowed and the patrol boats drew too much water—if the boats stayed out. But the army had commandeered shallow-draft fishing launches, and everyone knew it, and the mangroves were no longer sanctuary, merely delay.

Marguerite kept washing. Her hands moved through the motions—scrub, twist, wring, hang—while her mind ran ahead like a current finding its channel. Two days. The network could move a warning to the south shore overnight if the weather held and if the cousin with the skiff wasn't drunk, both uncertain. Moving thirty people further, inland or up-coast, in two days, past patrols that now searched every cart—

"It's the rice," she said aloud, to no one, in the tone of a woman whose mind never rose above provisions. "Everything's short since they came. My mother used to say war is just hunger wearing a uniform."

Old Petra snorted. The Consuelo sisters laughed. The sentence went nowhere and everywhere, the way such sentences did, and by nightfall it had been handed down the chain of mothers and daughters and stall-keepers until it reached the ear it needed to reach, dressed in new clothes each time, carrying its cargo intact.

What cargo? She didn't know yet herself. Warn them, yes—but warn them toward what? The open water was watched. The interior was patrolled. Every road out of the town now ended in a checkpoint or a bayonet, and the ledger sat in its locked room cataloguing every family that might run, and somewhere behind that oak door Lieutenant Harrow was reading, patiently, the way he did everything, matching names to faces one page at a time.

She understood, hanging the last of the sheets in the dark, that warnings alone were finished. A warning tells you the flood is coming; it does not build you a boat. Sooner or later—and she felt it coming the way divers feel the pressure change before the storm—someone would need papers. Permits, passes, travel writs bearing stamps and signatures. Paper, the clerk had told her once, was the only power worth having on this coast, and paper was locked behind an iron keyhole forty feet from her wash-tub.

She pressed her raw hands flat against the cool line, and looked across the dark yard at the annex window, where a single lamp still burned. Late. Past seven bells. Someone was working in the records room, and through the frosted glass the shadow bent over the desk with the particular stillness of a man reading something he found interesting.

Harrow. Reading. Always reading.

Marguerite turned back to her laundry and began folding, fold by fold, the army's linens, thinking about keys, about ink, about the exact width of a travel permit and how many steps it took to cross forty feet—and wondering, with a cold clarity that felt almost like calm, how long it would be before the man behind the lamp began wondering about her.
