## 8. Names Called at Dawn

They came with the tide, which was the army's first mistake and the town's last mercy. Anyone who had worked the beds knew the hour the water turned—knew it in the body, before the mind woke—and so when the boots began hitting the shell road before first light, half the houses on the strand were already awake behind their shutters, listening.

Marguerite heard them from the loft above the company office, where she had slept badly on a pallet of sacking since the requisition. She counted footsteps the way she counted oyster shells: by weight. Soldiers moved heavy, boots full of harbor sand, but they moved in rhythm, and this was a rhythm of eight or ten men, no more, moving not toward the warehouses but up the strand, toward the houses where the fishing families lived.

She was dressed before she finished thinking about it.

Outside, the dawn was the color of unbleached linen, that thin gray that makes everything look already surrendered. A corporal stood at the corner with a paper in his hand and a lantern held low, reading names aloud in an accent that flattened every vowel into something the sea would not recognize.

"Delacruz. Delacruz, widow, and household."

The Delacruz door opened. Old Sefa Delacruz came out with her shawl over her nightclothes and her grandson's hand in hers, and she did not argue, did not weep, did nothing but look down the length of the strand as if measuring the distance to some water only she could see. The boy was crying quietly, the way children cry when they have learned that noise makes things worse.

Marguerite stood in the shadow of the office doorway and did the arithmetic she could not stop herself from doing. *Sefa Delacruz. Her son married an island woman, dead now two years. The ledger will have it. The ledger has everything.*

The soldiers went inside and came out with the household's papers wrapped in oilcloth, and one of them kicked the door shut behind him because doors are not the army's concern once they have what they need.

* * *

By full light there were eleven families in the stockade yard behind the old customs house, sitting on the ground in the arrangement of people who have been told to sit. Marguerite saw it all from the laundry line, where she had gone because the garrison's washing needed doing and because the washing line faced the customs house and because a woman with wet hands and a lowered head is furniture.

She watched the neighbors being processed. That was the word she'd heard Lieutenant Harrow use the day before, through the open window of the records room: *processed*. He'd said it the way the clerk said *entered*, the way her mother had said *weighed*. Everything in this coast's history arrived as a verb for making a living thing into a figure.

And then she saw Rosa Ibarra among the eleven.

Rosa, who kept the drying racks two doors from Marguerite's own childhood house. Rosa, whose sister had married an island man fifteen years ago and whose sister's children spoke two languages and caught fish with nets woven in both traditions. Rosa, who three nights ago had come to Marguerite's window in the dark, smelling of smoke and fear, and whispered, *"You told me ships were coming. You told me to get my sister's boys inland."*

And Marguerite had said: *Wait. Not yet. If you move too soon and someone sees—wait until I know how they're choosing names. Wait until I can move you clean.*

Rosa had waited. Rosa was good at waiting; the whole coast was good at waiting, it was the first thing the company ever taught you. And now Rosa sat in the dust of the stockade yard with her sister's two boys pressed against either side of her like bookends, and the wait had ended without her.

Marguerite's hands stayed in the wash water. They had to stay there. If she took them out, they would do something, and whatever they did would be seen.

A guard walked the line of the seated families with a list, checking off. Behind him, at the customs house door, stood a tall officer in a uniform too clean for the salt air, holding a ledger under one arm the way a farmer carries a hen he intends to wring. Harrow. Even at forty yards Marguerite knew the posture—the slight forward lean, the head tilted as though the world were a document he was pleased to be reading.

He stopped beside Rosa. He bent slightly. He said something Marguerite could not hear, and Rosa looked up at him, and Marguerite watched—from forty yards, through steam and soap and the flat morning light—a woman decide whether to lie.

Rosa shook her head.

Whatever the question had been, she shook her head, and Harrow smiled—not cruelly, that was the worst of it, he smiled the way a clerk smiles at a well-kept account—and made a small mark on his paper and moved on.

Marguerite wrung out a sheet hard enough to hurt her wrists.

* * *

The fishwives found her at noon, or rather arranged themselves around her at noon, in the shade behind the boiling sheds where the gossip network ran its deepest channels. Nobody said anything dangerous aloud. That was the discipline of it. Ten years of company rule had taught the women of the strand to conduct entire conversations in the placement of baskets and the direction of a spitting gesture.

Old Marta set down a basket of mending an arm's length from Marguerite and said, to no one, "Eleven households. And the Delacruz boy is nine years old."

"He'll be counted as household," said another woman, whose name Marguerite had never let herself learn too well. Learning names was a debt now. "Households don't get transported. Households get held."

"Held where?"

"The stockade. Until processing."

The word again. Marguerite picked up the basket of mending and began sorting it, shirt from shirt, because her hands needed categories.

"They're saying," Marta continued, in the tone of a woman reciting weather, "that the lists came from the company books. Every marriage, every godparent, every debt owed across the water. They're saying the factor handed them over without a stamp of protest."

"The factor handed over his own ledgers," Marguerite said quietly. "You think he argued about ours?"

Silence, of the kind that agreed with her.

Then Marta leaned close, close enough that the words barely crossed the air between them, and said: "The Ibarra woman asked for you. Before they took her phone—" she corrected herself, "—before they took her in. Asked that word reach you." A pause, measured out like rope. "She said: tell her the water doesn't wait for clean."

Marguerite sorted shirts. Her fingers were steady. Somewhere beneath her ribs, something was not.

"I don't know what she means," she said.

"No," Marta agreed. "Nobody knows anything. That's why we're all still here."

The women gathered their baskets by ones and twos and dispersed along paths that looked aimless and were not, and Marguerite was left alone with the mending and the sound of the tide going out, taking the morning's cover with it.

* * *

She told herself, walking back along the strand, that caution had been correct. That moving Rosa's family blind, before knowing the selection criteria, would have delivered them to the stockade a week earlier with the whole network exposed. That slow was safe. That invisible was alive. These were the truths that had kept her breathing for twenty-six years, and she recited them like the multiplication tables, and they did not help, because underneath the recitation ran a different current, cold and clear:

*Rosa came to your window. You told her to wait. You wanted to be sure—sure of the risk, sure of your own safety, sure that the cost wouldn't land on you. And while you were being sure, the soldiers read a name off a page, and a nine-year-old boy is sitting in the dust.*

She stopped at the tideline. Below her feet, the wet sand held the morning's boot prints, dozens of them, overlapping, all pointing inland. The tide would take them by evening. The tide erased everything; that had always been the sea's kindness and its cruelty both.

Her mother had drowned slowly, lungs filling year by year with the brine of the beds, and had never once blamed the water. *The water does what the water does,* she used to say. *It's the boats that choose where to go.* Marguerite had thought it fatalism when she was young. Standing on the boot-printed sand, she understood it was an accusation, aimed precisely, at everyone who steered.

Harrow was steering. But he had never pretended otherwise. It was the ones who claimed to be only drifting—the clerk with his keys, the factor with his stamps, Marguerite with her careful, patient, murderous *wait*—who let the ships arrive.

* * *

That evening she climbed to the office loft with her supper of rice and dried fish and ate none of it. Through the single window she could see the customs house, lamplit now, a square of yellow against the blue dark. Silhouettes crossed it at intervals: guards changing watch. She had begun, without deciding to, keeping count. Four posts. Relief at the second bell and the sixth. One officer, irregular hours.

On the desk below her lay the copies she made by day—fair accounts, harmless accounts—and beyond them, in the locked cabinet, the originals. The clerk's key hung on a nail by the door, as it always had, because the cabinet had never needed guarding from anyone the company trusted. That was the whole architecture of the place: trust built on need, need built on debt, debt built on the certainty that nobody with something to lose would ever touch the books.

Rosa Ibarra had something to lose now, and it was locked in a cabinet.

Marguerite put down the untouched food. She thought about the arithmetic of it the way she thought about the beds: depth, current, what the tide would give and what it would take. Eleven families today. The sweep Harrow had hinted at—the one the records room whispers spoke of—would take more, and soon, and among the next names, if the pattern held, would be islanders' kin who had gone quiet in the mangroves, waiting for a warning that a certain careful girl had not yet sent.

Tomas had come to her window twice now. Twice she had given him half an answer. There would not be a third asking. She understood that suddenly and completely, the way you understand a rip current: the family in the mangroves would not wait for her courage to ripen. They would run blind, or they would be found, and either way the promise she'd sworn over a broken pearl shell ten years ago would end as ink on a page in a crate in a records room—witnessed, recorded, worthless.

Below her, the lamp by the clerk's nail flickered in the draft from the door he'd left ajar, as he always did now, forgetting, or pretending to forget, that anyone might be watching.

Marguerite got up. She went down the ladder without lighting her own lamp, and she stood in the dark office with the smell of paper and ink and old salt, looking at the key on its nail.

It was six bells. The relief at the customs house would change at the second bell. She had, by her own count, most of an hour.

Her hand closed around the key, and it was colder than the sea had ever been.
