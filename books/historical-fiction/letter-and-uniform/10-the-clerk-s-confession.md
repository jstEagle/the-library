## 10. The Clerk's Confession

The clerk's house stood where the town ended and the salt marsh began, a plank-and-tar paper box on stilts above the mud, with a view of nothing but the gray water and the gray sky and the gray line between them. Nobody visited the clerk. Nobody had ever visited the clerk. He was one of those men who seemed less like a person than a fixture of the office itself—like the inkwell or the squeaking shutter or the smell of camphor that lived in the walls—and it had taken Marguerite years to learn that he had a name (Aurelio) and a history (a wife who had gone back north twenty years ago and never written) and now, apparently, a house you could die in.

She went at dusk, after the garrison lamps were lit and before the night patrol made its first round. She carried bread and a jar of the fishwives' ointment for his chest, which was what she'd told herself she was carrying. She knew better. She'd known since he coughed blood into his handkerchief behind the counting desk on Tuesday, folded the cloth away without looking at it, and said, in the flat voice he used for figures: "Marguerita. Come see me when I'm not at the desk."

Not Marguerite. Marguerita. The name her mother had used, the company had shortened and stamped and filed away until only the ledger version remained.

The door opened before she knocked. He must have been watching from the window all evening, waiting, and something about that—the patience of a man arranging his last days like a column of numbers, each entry balanced—made her throat tighten.

"You look terrible," she said, because he did, because there was no point being gentle with a man who dealt only in exactitudes.

"And you look like a girl carrying bread she doesn't intend to be eaten." He stepped aside to let her in. "Come. Sit. The chair by the stove is the good one."

* * *

The house smelled of lamp oil and medicine and, beneath both, of paper—that dry sweet dust of old ledgers that Marguerite realized, sitting down, she had come to think of as the smell of safety, though she could no longer have said why. A single room. A bed against one wall. A desk against the other, and on the desk, incongruous as an altar, a locked writing case of black lacquered tin, the kind clerks used for documents too valuable for the general files.

He lowered himself into the bed with the care of a man handling wet cargo, and for a while they simply looked at each other across the small room, two people who had spent eleven years within arm's reach of each other six days a week and had never once spoken of anything that mattered.

"The army took the ledgers," she said finally. It wasn't a question. They both worked forty feet from the crate they'd gone into.

"They took the ledgers," he agreed. "They did not take my key."

Her heart did something complicated. She kept her face still. Eleven years of copying accounts in front of a man who missed nothing had taught her that.

"Every lock in that office has two keys," Aurelio said. "One hangs on the factor's belt, so everyone can see how important he is. One is mine, so the work can actually be done." He began to cough then, deep and wet, and she rose without being asked and poured him watered wine from the bottle on his shelf, and he drank it and lay back and watched her with eyes that were still, even now, doing arithmetic.

"How long?" she asked.

"Does it matter?"

"No."

"Then don't ask questions whose answers change nothing. That's the first rule of good bookkeeping." He almost smiled. "You'll want the second rule too. Write everything down. Memory is a thief. Paper is honest—or rather, paper is whatever its keeper makes it, which is more useful than honest."

"Aurelio—"

"I'm going to tell you a story," he said. "You will sit there and let me finish it, because I've been rehearsing it for three days and I haven't got breath to spare for interruptions." He settled himself against the pillows. "I came to this coast thirty-one years ago. Young man, quick hand, letters of recommendation. The company liked me because I never asked what the figures meant. A man owes twelve pesos; the company takes his boat; the man owes fourteen pesos for the storage of his own boat. I wrote it down. I never once asked why the numbers always moved in one direction. Do you understand? Thirty-one years. Every family on this coast reduced to a column in my hand. The Salazars. The Ochoas. Your mother."

Marguerite's hands closed in her lap.

"Your mother signed her indenture papers with a mark," Aurelio said gently. "Because she couldn't write. I witnessed it. I wrote beside the mark: *signed by X, her mark, attested A. Ferreiro, clerk.* And every year after, when the interest was added, I added it, and when your father drowned and his debt passed to her, I entered that too, and I never asked why a dead man's debts outlive him. You know what I told myself? I told myself: *the ledger is not mine.* I keep it. I do not make it. There is a difference, I said. A clean distinction. Very tidy. Very satisfying." His voice roughened. "There is no such difference. A ledger is kept into existence, Marguerita. Day after day, entry after entry. The keeping is the making."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft she couldn't feel. Outside, the tide was turning; she could hear the mud sucking at the pilings below them.

"Why are you telling me this?" she said.

"Because Tuesday I coughed blood into a handkerchief, and Wednesday an army lieutenant sat where your stool sits and asked me to explain the cross-reference conventions of volume four, and I explained them beautifully, because explaining things beautifully is the one thing I have ever done well." He held up a trembling hand before she could speak. "No. Let me finish. He asked me—I remember his words exactly, I always remember words exactly—he said: *Which entries would matter most to a man trying to find friends of the enemy?* And I showed him. I walked him through it like a tour guide through a garden I had planted myself. Bond-sworn entries. Witnessed friendships. Native-born registers. I handed him every name on this coast that ever meant anything to anyone, arranged alphabetically for his convenience."

The word arrived in her chest before she allowed it into her mind: *Sandoval.*

"He read for an hour," Aurelio went on. "He reads the way a snake drinks—slowly, completely, without hurry. Then he marked certain pages with a slip of green paper, and thanked me, and said I had been most helpful. Most helpful." He laughed, and it turned into coughing, and she waited it out with her fists pressed against her knees. "And I went home and thought: I am dying anyway. So the question is not whether I am complicit. The question is what I am complicit *in*, between now and the end. And I found—" he reached toward the desk, and she understood then that the whole story had been a road leading here, laid down entry by entry like his life's work, "—I found that the distinction I built my whole career on was available to me after all. Not the one I claimed. The real one."

His fingers rested on the black tin case but did not lift its lid.

"The ledger can be altered," he said.

Marguerite did not move. Some animal part of her, the part that had survived her father and the sea and eleven years of the company's arithmetic, wanted very badly to stand up and say she didn't know what he was talking about and go home. She stayed seated.

"Ink can be lifted," Aurelio said. "Oxalic acid lifts iron-gall ink cleanly if you're patient. Or a blade, if the paper is thick—but blades leave ghosts, and ghosts invite questions. Acid leaves nothing but blankness, and blankness invites pity: *oh, poor file, damaged by damp.* This coast is damp. Everyone believes damage here." He turned the case slightly, so it faced her. "Entries can also be added. A steady hand, the right ink, the right nib—a skilled copyist can become any signature on this coast. I taught you to copy fair hands for eleven years. I did not do it for the company's convenience. I did it because I watched you at fifteen, ruling lines freehand straighter than mine, and I thought: there, but for the accident of birth, is a clerk. And now—" the cough again, shorter, angrier, "—now I think: there, thank God, is a clerk."

"I copy accounts," Marguerite said. Her voice sounded very far away to her. "That's all I am."

"That is all you were *permitted* to be. There's a difference, and you know it, because you've been mapping the guard rotations at the garrison in your head for three weeks, and you think I don't notice things." He smiled at her expression. "Marguerita. I noticed you the day you arrived. I noticed everything. It was the only power I had, noticing, so I hoarded it like a miser. Well." He pushed the case across the desk toward her. It slid with a soft whisper of tin on wood. "Here is my hoard. Spend it better than I did."

She didn't touch it. "What's in it?"

"My key. A flask of oxalic acid. Blotting paper. Three nibs of different weights, matched to the factor's hand, the paymaster's, and mine. A stick of sealing wax in the company color, because permits without seals are just paper. And a list—in my hand, dated yesterday—which names every page Harrow marked green, so you needn't guess." He paused. "Your page is among them. I'm sorry. I checked twice, hoping I'd remembered wrong. Sandoval, M. Witness to islander rescue. Bond sworn, 1901. Witnessed by me."

Ten years ago. Ten years of being useful and unremarkable, and a boy on the water had made her remarkable with a single line of ink, and she hadn't even known until the army came to collect it.

"If you take it," Aurelio said, "you take everything that comes with it. Not the danger—I won't insult you by pretending you haven't already accepted the danger, whatever you tell yourself. The *burden*. Every name you strike, someone else keeps their freedom. Every name you fail to strike, you'll carry. There is no clean distinction. There never was. That's the inheritance, child. Not the key. The arithmetic."

"And yours?" she asked quietly. "Who carries yours?"

"I carried it thirty-one years," he said. "I'm setting it down. That's what dying is for, if you use the time properly." He lay back, suddenly exhausted, as if the speech had been paid for out of some final account. "Take it. Go home. Don't come again—it's safer for us both, and I've said everything worth saying. If you decide you can't, put the case under my floorboard where you found it and let the lieutenant inherit my sins along with my effects. God knows he's earned them."

* * *

She stood at last and picked up the case. It weighed almost nothing. That was the terrible thing—she had expected it to weigh something, the way a promise weighs, the way a drowning boy's arm around her ribs had weighed—and instead it was light as an empty lunch pail, and it fit inside her market basket beneath the bread.

At the door she stopped.

"The oath over the shell," she said, not turning around. "In 1901. You witnessed it. What exactly does the ledger say?"

"It says what I was told to write." Aurelio's voice came slow and careful from the bed. "*Bond sworn between T. Cabral of the island settlement and M. Sandoval, minor, witnessed and entered this day.* It doesn't say what was sworn. Ledgers record that promises exist, Marguerita. Only people remember what they were."

Outside, the marsh had gone dark and the first stars were out over the water, cold and indifferent as punched holes in a filing card. She walked home the long way, along the tideline, feeling the case shift against the bread with every step, and behind her the little stilted house shrank into the dark with its dying tenant, and ahead of her the garrison lamps burned over the warehouse where the ledgers slept in their crate—every debt, every name, every bond on this coast, guarded by boys with rifles who could not read.

She had access. She had a steady hand. By Friday, Lieutenant Harrow had scheduled the sweep of the mangrove camps for the following week, and the tide would not wait, and neither, anymore, would she.
