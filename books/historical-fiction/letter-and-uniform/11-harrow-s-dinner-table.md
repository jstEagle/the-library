## 11. Harrow's Dinner Table

The factor's house was the finest building in the town, which was to say it was the only one with glass in its windows. Lieutenant Harrow noted this the way he noted everything: precisely, without appetite, and with an eye for what it revealed about the man behind the door.

Factor Aldous Grebe received him in a dining room that smelled of lamp oil and tinned beef, both of which had been shipped a thousand miles to simulate civilization on a coast that wanted none of it. The table was laid properly—white cloth, three forks, a decanter of port that had traveled farther than most of the soldiers in the garrison. A Chinese houseboy moved at the edges of the light, silent as ballast.

"Lieutenant." Grebe extended a damp hand. "An honor. We don't often entertain the military."

"We rarely merit entertaining," Harrow said, and took the chair indicated.

He had dressed for the occasion, which is to say he had done nothing at all. His uniform was correct by default; it required no effort and permitted no improvement. He wore it the way other men wore their faces.

Grebe talked first about the weather, then the price of shell, then the scandal of the new customs duty, each topic abandoned the moment it threatened to require knowledge. Harrow listened with the particular attention he gave to all information, sorting it into two piles: useful and decorative. Almost everything Grebe said was decorative. That itself was useful. It told Harrow the factor understood nothing about why the army had come, or worse, understood and did not care, so long as his ledgers balanced and his shipments left on schedule.

The soup arrived. Grebe tasted it and made a face of mild suffering.

"You'll forgive the fare. Since your requisition—" he said *your* the way a man says *plague* —"we've been rather stripped. Half my stores gone to the garrison. And the ledgers, Lieutenant. I confess I still don't understand what the army wants with ten years of accounts receivable."

"The army wants everything the island has ever written down," Harrow said pleasantly. "Yours happened to be the only writing worth taking."

Grebe laughed, uncertain whether he'd been insulted. He decided against it. A man who owned this much glass could not afford enemies in uniform.

* * *

Harrow let the meal proceed. He was good at letting meals proceed. He had learned early that men reveal themselves not under questioning but under comfort—that a second glass of wine loosened more than any strap ever had. He asked about the oyster beds. He asked about the labor system, the indentures, the arithmetic of debt that bound half the town to the company store. Grebe warmed to the subject like a stove.

"A simple machine," the factor said, dabbing his mouth. "Every man owes us. Every man knows he owes us. A man who owes you will work for you, vote for you, inform for you. Debt is better than law. Law can be appealed. Debt sits in the chest."

"And in the book."

"In the book," Grebe agreed happily. "The book is the chest."

Harrow turned his glass slowly on the cloth. "Your clerks are careful men?"

"Careful? Old Anselm has kept those books since before I came out. Thirty years. He rules his columns like a bishop rules a diocese. Why do you ask?"

"No reason yet," Harrow said. "I'll tell you when there's one."

That was a lie. There was a reason already, and it sat folded in his breast pocket like a knife wrapped in linen. He had carried it all day: a single line from the ledger, copied out in his own hand because he did not yet trust the original to stay where he'd found it.

*Sandoval, M.* — entered at age nine, daughter of debt-holder Sandoval, R., deceased. Indentured to company service. And beneath the accounting, in the clerk's smaller hand, a note that should never have survived thirty years of tidying:

*Witness, rescue from water, islander child T. Bond sworn by parties juvenile. Terms unrecorded.*

Bond sworn. Unrecorded terms. Between a company child and an islander boy, witnessed by no adult, filed by a clerk who either could not bring himself to erase it or could not resist recording it. Harrow had read the entry eleven times. Each reading produced the same pleasure, the small cold pleasure of a lock yielding to the right key.

Because here was the thing about bonds sworn between children over drowning: children grew. The islander boy would be a man now, twenty perhaps, living somewhere along this coast or on the water itself. And the girl—the girl was still here. Marguerite Sandoval. Nineteen years old. Employed in the company office until the requisition, currently assigned to laundry detail at the garrison, described in the census roll as *native-born, mixed parentage, unremarkable.*

Unremarkable. Harrow had underlined the word twice.

* * *

The fish course came and went. Grebe drank steadily and talked about himself, which was his favorite subject and his longest. Harrow fed him questions the way one feeds a fire—small pieces, evenly spaced—and learned that the factor feared three things: the customs office, his superiors, and the sea in that order.

"And the islanders?" Harrow asked. "Do they frighten you?"

Grebe waved a fork. "They're fishermen. They've fished these waters longer than anyone's been counting. They keep to the mangroves, trade through intermediaries, cause no trouble worth the name. Frankly, Lieutenant, I've always found them useful. Cheap labor, honest weights. Some of the older families have fished the same beds under company license for generations. Deeds and such, all quite regular."

"All quite regular," Harrow repeated. "And their sympathies?"

"Their what?"

"When the war was declared. When the archipelago was named enemy territory. Where did their hearts sit?"

Grebe hesitated for the first time all evening. Harrow watched the hesitation with interest, the way a naturalist watches a specimen choose between two doors.

"They're... island people," Grebe said carefully. "They have cousins across the water. Everyone does, in that direction. But hearts aren't cargo, Lieutenant. They don't come through my warehouse."

"No," Harrow agreed. "But names do. Names pass through your books every day. And a heart, Mr. Grebe, leaves a paper trail just the same—it signs things, swears things, witnesses things. It's remarkably bureaucratic, the human heart. You simply have to know where to look."

He took out the folded page then—not dramatically, not with flourish, but the way a man takes out a pipe—and unfolded it beside his plate, weighting one corner with the salt cellar.

Grebe read it upside down, frowned, turned his head. "What is this?"

"One line from your magnificent ledger. A bond, sworn between a company child and an islander child. Witnessed, dated, filed. Ten years ago." Harrow sipped his wine. "Tell me, Factor. In your years here, have you heard of a drowning? A girl pulled from the water by an islander boy?"

Grebe's face did something complicated. "There was a Sandoval girl," he said slowly. "Years back. The mother worked shell for us, the father drowned in debt, if you follow me. The girl went into the water once—I remember the fuss of it, the mother weeping at the office. Islander lad brought her out. There was some talk of a reward. Nothing came of it."

"Nothing came of it," Harrow murmured. "Except a line in your ledger. Except a sworn bond whose terms were never recorded." He tapped the page. "A promise, Mr. Grebe. Made by a girl who is presently washing shirts forty yards from my records room, to a boy who is presently a man, on an enemy coast, with family in the mangroves."

The houseboy refilled the glasses. Neither man spoke until he had withdrawn.

"You think she'd act on it," Grebe said. "After ten years. A child's promise."

"I think promises made over drowning don't drown," Harrow said. "I think they wait. I think the girl was nine years old and someone saved her life and she swore something in the terrible solemn way children swear things, and I think she has spent ten years being unremarkable so that no one would ever ask her to pay it." He smiled without warmth. "People like that, Mr. Grebe—they keep their debts better than your customers do. She owes a debt that isn't in any book. Those are the only ones people honor."

Grebe had stopped eating. "You're saying she's a sympathizer."

"I'm saying she may be a channel. One girl, one bond—but bonds attach to families, and families attach to boats, and boats attach to water, and water attaches to the archipelago." Harrow folded the page away again. "It's a thread. I pull threads."

* * *

Dessert was a preserved fruit compote that had crossed an ocean to taste of sugar and regret. Grebe, fortified by the port, had recovered his authority and was explaining the proper grading of pearl shell when Harrow interrupted him with the ease of a man laying down a winning card.

"How many islanders camp within a day's walk of this town?"

Grebe blinked. "I—dozens of families. Perhaps two hundred souls in the mangrove camps and the fishing villages. Why?"

"Because within the week," Harrow said, "there will be a sweep. A full cordon of the coastal camps. Identification papers examined, kinship lists checked against the rolls, detentions where warranted." He said it in the tone of a man discussing drainage works. "The order came down yesterday. I'm telling you because your warehouse will be needed for processing, and because your fishing licenses will be affected—several families hold deeds, you said. Quite regular. Those deeds will need reviewing."

Grebe set down his spoon. "Reviewing how?"

"Honestly, I hope. Though honestly is a flexible instrument." Harrow leaned back. "You see my difficulty, Factor. Somewhere in those camps is a young man who once pulled a drowning girl out of the sea. If he's there when the sweep comes, I'll find him, and I'll match his face to the ledger line, and the thread will pull clean. If he's warned—if the girl keeps her promise—then the sweep finds empty camps, and I'll know exactly which thread to pull next, and where it leads." He spread his hands. "Either way, the sweep tells me something. That's the beauty of it. Even failure is intelligence."

"You're using the operation as bait," Grebe said, with the flat tone of a man realizing the dinner had been about him all along.

"I'm using everything as bait. It's the only honest use for anything." Harrow finished his port. "One more thing, and then I'll release you to your evening. The old clerk—Anselm. He's been ill, I understand."

"Dying," Grebe said. "Months, the doctor says. He came to protest the requisition last week. Shaking like a leaf. Very protective of his books."

"Yes. Protective." Harrow stood, and Grebe scrambled up after him, too late to be graceful. "Watch him for me, would you? Men near death develop sudden appetites for honesty. They want to settle accounts. And your clerk has thirty years of accounts, and one of them"—he touched his breast pocket lightly—"doesn't balance."

At the door, Grebe ventured, "You enjoy this work, Lieutenant. Forgive me, but—you do."

Harrow considered the question seriously, which was his habit with all questions.

"I enjoy correctness," he said. "Everything in this war is misfiled—loyalties, borders, names. Someone has to put the papers in order. Good night, Factor. Thank you for the dinner. The fish was excellent."

* * *

Outside, the night smelled of salt and woodsmoke, and the harbor lay black except for the riding lights of the gunboats. Harrow walked back through the town without hurry, boots ringing on the coral road, past shuttered houses behind which he imagined the town holding its breath.

Two hundred souls in the camps. A sweep within the week. A girl with steady hands and an unremarkable face, washing uniforms forty yards from the records room.

Let her hear of it. Let the gossip network do what gossip networks do. Let her run.

He found, turning the thought over as he walked, that he hoped she would. A trap that caught nothing taught nothing. But a trap that was *escaped*—that told him everything: routes, accomplices, loyalties, the whole hidden anatomy of the town laid bare in a single desperate motion. He did not want to stop a promise. He wanted to watch it being kept, and catch everyone who helped keep it.

In his quarters, he lit the lamp, opened the requisitioned ledgers to the Sandoval pages, and read them again, unhurried, while somewhere out in the dark the tide turned and the mangroves breathed and a clock he alone could hear began, very quietly, to run.
