## 12. Forging Tides

The clerk's key was heavier than it looked.

Marguerite had carried it against her palm all the way from his sickroom above the drying sheds, wrapped in a scrap of sailcloth so the metal wouldn't ring against anything, so no one would hear what she already knew in her bones: that a key was a kind of confession, and carrying one was a way of saying yes to something before you'd even named it. Old Ferro had pressed it into her hand with fingers gone soft as kelp, and he had looked at her the way drowning men look at shore—not begging, exactly. Just measuring the distance.

"The ink is iron gall," he'd said. "It eats into the paper. You can't lift it once it's set, so don't try. Cut around it. Paste over it. Write new where you must, but write small—Harrow's eye is good, and he reads ledgers the way priests read scripture." He'd coughed then, long and wet, the sound of a man whose lungs were filling the way Marguerite's mother's had filled, and when he could speak again he'd said the thing she would carry up the stairs of the records annex at midnight like a stone: "I kept those books clean for forty years. Clean as a lie can be. Whatever you do in there, girl, do it better than I ever did."

* * *

The records room smelled of lamp oil and salt rot and the particular sweetness of old paper—the smell of every debt in the coast bound into one place. She had let herself in through the coal hatch at the rear, the one Ferro had shown her years ago during an inventory, saying *remember this* without explaining why, and now she understood that he had been leaving her a door the way other men leave money.

She lit the lamp low. One wick. The flame stood no taller than her thumb, and its light reached only the desk in front of her, leaving the shelves of ledgers rising up into darkness on either side like a congregation at prayer—or a jury.

Volume Seven sat where Harrow's clerks left it each evening, squared to the edge of the shelf, spine out, because Lieutenant Harrow believed order was a form of virtue. Marguerite had watched him enough by now to know this. He squared his papers. He aligned his pens. A man who loved straight lines would notice a crooked page faster than a missing one.

She opened the book to the Sandoval page first. Not to alter it—to look at it. Her own name in her father's hand, twenty-two years old, the ink browned to the color of dried blood: *Sandoval, M.—witness to islander rescue, bond sworn.* Beneath it, in smaller script, the note some long-dead clerk had added: *bond unredeemed.*

Unredeemed. As if a promise were a debt of coin. As if she owed the company rather than the boy on the water.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed it the way you notice rain starting—one drop, then the understanding that everything is about to be wet. She pressed them flat against the desk until the tremor ran down into the wood instead of into the pages, and she made herself think of Delia Cruz, who had been taken from the stockade road with her children because Marguerite had waited three days too long to pass the warning. Three days. She thought of the fishwife Anselma's face in the market, the way she had not wept, only nodded, when they told her her sons' boat was impounded: *then we go again,* she'd said, and that nod was the thing Marguerite set between herself and fear now, a plank to walk on.

She turned back four pages to the Reyes entry. *Reyes, E.—islander kin by marriage, bond sworn, account current.* Account current meant nothing anymore; the army did not care about accounts. The army cared about names beside names. She took the fine bone folder from her apron pocket, the one she used for lifting oysters from their shells whole, and she slid it under the corner of the slip pasted over the Reyes account—a company trick, slips pasted over old entries when terms changed, and under slips, always, was paper that could be persuaded.

She worked slowly. The lamp flame leaned when her breath touched it, and twice she stopped breathing altogether just to keep the light steady. The slip came away clean, taking a layer of the old page with it, and beneath was blank paper where a sympathetic name had lived. She cut a slip of matching stock from the flyleaf of a damaged volume destined for pulping—Ferro had taught her that too, that every office keeps a body-donor—and wrote in a small, dry hand she had practiced for six nights against her knee: *Reyes, E.—account settled, removed to mainland, 1908.* Settled. Removed. The past tense of a living man, sent safely backward in time to a year when nobody was looking.

Then the Ocampo page. Then the Batalla cousins, both entries. Then, hardest, the page belonging to Anselma herself, because Anselma was already in the stockade and a clean ledger might mean a shorter sentence, or none. Marguerite wrote the widow off the books entirely, erased her into the mainland, gave her a fictional departure date and a fictional settlement and felt, with each stroke of the pen, something in her chest that was not guilt exactly. It was closer to exhilaration, and that frightened her more than the footsteps she kept imagining on the stairs.

Because this was power. She had copied these figures for six years and never once understood what she was copying. Now she understood: the ledger did not record the town. The ledger *was* the town. Whoever held the pen held the people, and tonight the pen was hers, and her hand had stopped shaking somewhere around the second name and had gone steady as a tide table since.

She should have been horrified. Instead she felt awake.

* * *

The permits took longer. Travel chits were kept in the locked drawer of the duty desk, but the drawer's lock was older than the war and younger than Ferro's key, and it opened to the same small persuasion. Each chit bore the garrison stamp, and the stamp lived in Harrow's outer office, which meant walking past the sergeant's cot in the anteroom, which meant the plan she had made at dusk now required her feet to carry her through the dark heart of the building while her mind screamed the arithmetic of risk.

She counted her way there, the way her mother had taught her to count waves between breaths. Twelve steps down the corridor. Four to the outer office door—it was unlocked; officers locked papers, never doors; doors implied someone worth locking against. Six more to the desk. The stamp sat in its wooden cradle like a sleeping animal, and she inked it, pressed it, and heard the wet kiss of it against paper sound, in the silence, like a shout.

Nothing moved. She stamped five chits. Cargo run, north anchorage, legitimate destination, the fishwives' cover story written into the government's own paperwork. In the last box on each chit, where the issuer signed, she forged Ferro's hand—his letters were cramped and leaning, easy to fake if you were tired, and everyone assumed a dying man's documents were tired documents. She was giving the dead clerk work to do from beyond the grave, and she thought he would have laughed at that, coughing, wiping his eyes.

She was relocking the drawer when the floorboard spoke.

Not the corridor—the anteroom. One board, one creak, the small sound of a boot shifting weight, and Marguerite went cold from the scalp down. Someone was in the building. Someone had been in the building, perhaps for some time, listening to the silence she had mistaken for emptiness, hearing the same silence broken by nothing—except that a moment ago she had stamped five chits and the world had been loud with it, to anyone with ears in the dark.

She killed the lamp with two fingers. The dark came down complete.

Footsteps now, unhurried, crossing the anteroom toward the outer office. Not a patrol's rhythm—patrols walked in pairs and announced themselves out of boredom. This was a single walker, and single walkers at midnight in a requisitioned office meant one man. She knew his step already from a week of laundry duty, from passing the records room door while he worked late: measured, even, the pace of a man reading while he walked.

Lieutenant Harrow.

He stopped at the threshold. She could see nothing, but she could feel him seeing—the doorway framed against whatever faint gray came through the shuttered window, and her own body pressed behind the duty desk with the lamp still warm under her hand and five forged chits inside her bodice lying against her heart like a second heartbeat.

"Odd," Harrow said to the empty room, in the mild tone he used for interesting problems. "I'm certain I left the drawer locked."

Marguerite did not breathe. Her mother's voice, from ten years drowned: *count the waves, little one, count them, don't fight them.*

A match scraped. Light bloomed in the outer office doorway, throwing Harrow's shadow long across the floorboards—across her, a stripe of it, and she watched that shadow lie over her own hand and did not move. He stood considering the records room with its dark shelves, the lamp dead on the desk, the drawer locked, everything squared and innocent.

Then, softly, he laughed. Once. It was the worst sound yet, because it was not suspicion. It was amusement.

"Moths," he said, to no one, and struck another match, and began—she could hear it—the small domestic business of relighting the wall sconce in the outer office, settling himself at his own desk with his own papers, apparently prepared to work until dawn ten feet from where she crouched with the evidence of treason folded against her skin.

She understood then, with terrible clarity, what he was doing. Whether he knew or merely sensed—he was staying. Making the night inconvenient for whoever might be hiding in it. Letting the dark grow expensive.

But he had not come in. And the coal hatch was behind her, and the window shutters in the records room faced the seaward alley, and Ferro had not left her one door but two, and she had spent her whole life being unremarkable, invisible, a woman the eye slid past—and invisibility, she now knew, was not a temperament. It was a discipline. It was a craft. And tonight she would practice it better than she had ever practiced oyster-opening or figure-copying or any of the small survivings of her life.

She rose without sound. The chits she left against her heart. The ledger lay closed on the desk, altered, damning, squared neatly to the edge because Harrow liked order and she wanted him to see order.

At the window she eased the shutter one inch, and the sea air came through the gap smelling of tar and coming storm, and beyond the roofs she could see the harbor, black water, and riding at anchor in it the dim running light of the fishwives' drying-boat—the boat that would carry Tomas's family out if the chits in her bodice said yes and the patrol boats said no, and the squall line on the horizon said the decision would not wait for anyone's courage.

Behind her, Harrow turned a page of his papers. The sound was very loud.

Marguerite slipped through the window like a name leaving a ledger, pulled the shutter closed behind her, and dropped into the seaward alley—where a figure detached itself from the shadow of the rain barrels and caught her arm, and a voice she knew said, low and urgent:

"They've moved the sweep up. Tomorrow at dawn. Marguerite—we launch tonight, or not at all."
