## 13. The Fishwife's Net

It began, as most dangerous things in this town did, over fish.

Not the pearl oysters that made the company rich—those went to the factor's warehouse under lock and ledger—but the plain catch the women netted at dawn and sold in the salt market for coin the company never counted. Mackerel, sardines, the silver trash-fish that no clerk bothered to weigh. The army bought what it wanted from the company and left the rest alone, and so the fishwives kept their own economy running beneath the soldiers' boots like water under ice.

I had been passing warnings through that network for weeks now, folded into gossip the way a smuggler folds opium into bales of cotton. *They took the Reyes brothers from the point.* *The stockade took forty more on Tuesday.* *Harrow is asking after anyone who signed friendship papers before the war.* Each warning traveled mouth to ear to mouth, and I had told myself it was enough—that warning was all my promise required of me.

Tomas had stopped believing that. So, it turned out, had the women.

* * *

Rosa Ibáñez summoned me with a basket of mackerel and a look that meant business. Rosa was sixty if she was a day, built like the breakwater she'd been born beside, and she had buried two husbands and one son to the sea without ever once letting her voice crack where a creditor could hear it. She sold fish at the third stall from the corner, and everyone knew her prices were honest because everyone feared her temper more than they loved a bargain.

"Girl." She thrust the basket at me. "You'll take these to Consolación. Tell her they're for drying."

Consolación lived at the far end of the strand, past the last of the drying-racks, in a house that leaned toward the water like an old woman listening for a boat. She was a fishwife too, widowed, childless, and she dried fish on racks that stretched along the beach in long gray rows like the ribs of some beached leviathan.

I carried the basket and understood nothing until I got there and saw the boat.

It sat on rollers above the tideline—a drying-boat, broad-bellied and shallow-drafted, the kind used to run fresh catch out to the curing sheds on the far side of the bay. Its hold was open to the sky, its boards salt-bleached, its sail furled and patched. Ordinary. Except that Consolación was standing beside it with a carpenter's rule and an expression I recognized from my own mirror: the look of a woman measuring something against her conscience.

"You're the Sandoval girl," she said.

"I'm Marguerite."

"You're the girl who's been sending warnings up the coast through Rosa's fish. Don't bother looking surprised; Rosa has known since the second message. She's known everything you've done, and she's told me, and between us we've told four others, and here we are." She said it the way she might report the tide tables. "Now come and look at this hold and tell me if a family of five can lie flat in it for six hours."

* * *

There were seven of us in Consolación's kitchen by dark, and I want to set down who they were, because for ten years I had believed myself alone in this town, invisible, unremarkable, useful only to the company—and then in a single evening I looked around a lamplit table and saw that invisibility had been holding meetings without me.

Rosa Ibáñez, who controlled the salt market and therefore every basket, every price, every errand that moved through town without a soldier's glance.

Consolación, who owned the boat and the racks and, more importantly, the legitimate cargo license that let a drying-boat cross the bay twice a week without inspection.

Petra Flores, whose husband drove the company's coal cart and who could tell you, to the minute, when every sentry post changed watch, because she'd been counting since the gunboats arrived—not from fear, but the way other women count stitches.

Old Maruja, who washed for the garrison and heard the officers' shirts talk, if you understand me—the stains on a collar, the mud on a trouser knee, the rank of the man who'd sweated into them.

Luz and her daughter Inés, who mended nets within sight of the mangrove channels and could mark, with a knot in a net-line, anything that needed marking.

And me, with my head full of guard rotations and ledger pages and a key hidden in the lining of my coat that still felt warm as a live coal against my hip.

"We need a date," Rosa said, once the door was barred and Maruja had confirmed the street was empty. "The family can't stay in the mangroves another week. The boy came again last night, didn't he?" She looked at me. "You don't have to say it. Your face says it. They're eating raw shellfish and drinking rainwater off leaves, and the father's coughing."

"Tomas says his mother won't last another week in the wet," I said. It cost me something to admit it—to hand these women my fear like currency—but the whole design of the evening was that I stop carrying things alone. "And Harrow's sweep—"

"Harrow's sweep is Thursday," Petra said flatly. "Coal cart goes to the stockade Wednesday night with extra load. That means extra men. Extra men mean the sweep's moved up or reinforced. Either way, your islanders are out of time."

"How do you know about Harrow's sweep?"

"Maruja washes his shirts." Petra shrugged. "He writes his orders at dinner and drops ink on his cuff. Ink on the cuff Wednesday, extra coal Wednesday night. Every week the same. The man is punctual. Punctual men are easy."

I looked around the table and felt something shift in my chest, some plate of old habit cracking. All those years I'd thought survival meant knowing things alone. These women had simply divided the knowing among themselves, each holding one piece, none of them ever seeing the whole picture—which meant no arrest, no interrogation, no informer under torture could give up more than a fragment. A net isn't one rope. It's a thousand knots, and cut any one and the net holds.

"The boat," Consolación said, bringing us back. "Friday there's a legitimate run: dried fish to the curing shed across the bay, and beyond it, if the weather holds, the neutral anchorage past the point. My papers cover the run. The soldiers check the deck cargo—they always do, they take a share, that's their toll—and they never check the hold below the false floor, because why would they? Nobody dries fish in a hold. Nobody hides in a hold. It's four feet high, it stinks of brine, and there's no light."

"There's no air either," Luz said. "Six hours is too long. Five, maybe, with the hatch cracked under the tarpaulin."

"So we go on a short day. Weather will help—" Consolación glanced at me. "You've watched the water longer than any of us. When's the next squall?"

"Saturday morning, early, if the glass keeps falling. Rain thick enough that the patrol boats hug the harbor."

"Then Saturday. Squall cover, five hours' run, hatch cracked, tarpaulin weighted." Consoláció laid it out stroke by stroke, and I marveled that she'd had this in her head already, that the boat on its rollers hadn't been idle carpentry but rehearsal. "Deck cargo: forty baskets dried fish, which the soldiers will count and tax and grumble over, and while they're counting—"

"While they're counting," Petra said, "the coal cart breaks an axle at the harbor gate. Right at the gate. Blocks the road twenty minutes, no way around, and every sentry comes to gawk or help or shout, and not one of them is looking at the water."

"And the fishwives," Rosa said, "will be selling on the strand that morning, as we always do, a dozen of us, loud as gulls. Loud is a wall, girls. You put enough noise between a soldier and his suspicion and he hears nothing at all."

* * *

We argued about the father.

It was Luz who raised it, gently, the way you raise a thing you already know will draw blood. "The deed-papers. Tomas's father won't board without them. Everyone knows it. If the man plants himself in the mangroves over some papers locked in a requisitioned warehouse—"

"He'll get in the boat," I said.

"Will he?"

"Yes." And then, because lying to this table felt like spitting into church: "Or I'll make him. I'll sit in the mud beside him until the tide turns if I have to. He doesn't have to love leaving. He just has to leave."

Maruja snorted. "Pride. My second husband drowned of pride. Refused to reef his sail in a rising wind because the younger men were watching." She looked at me with eyes gone pale as fish-scale. "You can't argue a man out of pride, girl. But you can drown it. You tell him this: the papers are paper. His wife is flesh. Paper floats, mind—it floats right out of your hands the moment the water decides. Flesh sinks. Let him choose which he'll gamble."

I wrote it down in my head, the words exactly, because I would need them word for word in the mangroves, delivered with Maruja's gravel behind them.

The rest was detail, and the detail went fast because each woman already held her piece. Inés would knot the warning into the net-lines visible from the mangrove channel: three loops for Friday, four for Saturday. Rosa would salt the market with chatter about a big curing shipment, ordinary news, giving the run its public face days in advance. Petra would see to the axle—her husband owed her, and she named the favor in a tone that suggested the debt was older than the war. Maruja would wash extra uniforms Thursday, keeping the garrison's laundry slow and its men complacent. Consolación would provision the hold: water in sealed jars, sacking to lie on, and—I insisted—a lamp and matches lashed inside oilcloth, because five hours in the dark does things to children, and Tomas's youngest was six.

That left me. They all looked at me when the assignments circled back, and I understood that they'd known my piece from the start and had saved it for last out of courtesy.

"The permits," Consolación said. "Travel passes for the crew list. Four names, stamped, dated Saturday. Real ones, or good enough that Harrow himself couldn't swear the difference."

"The records room key turns at nine," I said. "The night clerk drinks. The alteration takes me twenty minutes if the lamplight holds."

"And if Harrow checks the register against the original entries?"

"Then I'm finished, and so are you, and so is the family." I said it plainly because they deserved plainness. "But Harrow trusts paper the way a priest trusts scripture. He reads it. He believes it. That's his religion, and I'm going to worship at it until Saturday."

Rosa reached across the table and closed her hand over mine—dry, hard, strong as a cleat. "Ten years you've been nobody," she said. "Tonight you're one of eleven knots in a net. Remember how a net works, girl. You pull one rope, the whole web feels it. But you cut one knot—" her grip tightened, not cruelly, just honestly, "—and the rest of us hold. Whatever happens to you, the rest of us hold."

I had spent my whole life learning how to disappear. It had never once occurred to me that disappearing could be a thing we did together.

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the tideline, because I wanted to see the boat.

Moonlight lay on the water like spilled milk. The drying-boat sat on its rollers above the reach of the tide, patched sail furled, deck swept, innocent as Sunday. Somewhere out in the black mangroves a family lay breathing shallow in the wet, waiting on a signal knotted into fishing nets, trusting a plan built by women whose names appeared in no ledger anywhere in this town—because the company had never thought to record what fishwives did, and the army had never thought to ask, and the whole machinery of ownership and requisition and numbered debt had rolled over them the way the tide rolls over pebbles, without once stopping to pick one up.

Invisible. We had all of us been invisible, and I had hated it, and tonight I understood it differently. A net is invisible in the water. That's what makes it work.

I was halfway home when I noticed the man.

He stood near Consolación's racks, in the shadow where the beach met the cart road, and he wasn't doing anything—that was the trouble. Men on the strand at night were fishermen checking gear, or lovers, or drunks, and all of them did something. This one stood with the stillness of a paid hour. As I passed he turned away, unhurried, and walked toward the town, and the moon caught him for half a second: a thin face I half-knew, a man who sold shrimp and small secrets down by the salt market, a man who had been standing close enough to Consolación's kitchen window to hear a lamplit table talking.

He didn't hurry. That was the worst of it. Men with guilty consciences hurry.

By the time I reached my door my heart was going like a fishline with something heavy on it, and I stood in the dark of my own kitchen and made myself think it through: he might have seen nothing. Heard nothing. The window was shuttered; Consolación was careful. It might be nothing.

But Saturday was four days off, and Harrow read paper like scripture, and somewhere between the strand and the salt market, a man with a thin face was walking toward whoever paid best.

I put my hand in my coat pocket, around the key, and began, in my head, moving up the timetable.
