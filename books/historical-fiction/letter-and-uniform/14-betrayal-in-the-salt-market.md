## 14. Betrayal in the Salt Market

The informer was a man named Quill, though nobody in the salt market called him that. They called him nothing at all. He was one of those figures who existed in the gaps between other people's conversations—a lean, soft-footed man who sold dried fish of uncertain origin and listened with his whole body while pretending to weigh eels. Marguerite had seen him a hundred times and had never once thought about him, which in hindsight felt like the town's collective failure. Invisibility, she knew better than anyone, was never neutral. Somebody always owned it.

She learned afterward—from the fishwives, from the boy who swept the market steps—that Quill had gone up to the garrison on Tuesday evening with his cap in his hands and a story in his mouth. What he'd sold Harrow was simple: a boat, a date, a hold that smelled wrong for its cargo. Drying-boats ran fish to the mainland twice a month; everyone knew their schedules the way they knew tides. But this run, Quill said, was carrying more than fish. He didn't say islanders. He didn't need to. The word *smuggling* did all the work, and the army's imagination filled in the rest.

Marguerite heard none of this on Tuesday. On Wednesday morning she stood at her stall among the salt-crusted women, haggling over a basket of oysters she no longer needed to sell because the garrison contract paid better, and felt the day go wrong before anything visibly went wrong at all.

It began with the gulls.

They rose off the harbor in a single ragged sheet, all at once, the way they do when something below them has decided to move. She looked up out of habit and saw the patrol boat already cutting across the anchorage, its wake a white seam stitched straight toward the drying-docks. Not heading out to sea. Heading in.

Her hands kept working. That was the training of a lifetime—keep the hands busy, keep the face dull—and so she counted change for old Marta while her eyes tracked the boat and her mind ran ahead of it like a dog straining a leash. The *Cormorant* was supposed to load tonight. Tomorrow night, actually—the tide tables said the small hours, the dark water, the long slack before dawn. It wasn't supposed to be anywhere near this. Unless someone had moved it up. Unless someone had panicked. Unless—

Two soldiers came down the market steps at a walk that wasn't running but wanted to be.

"Close your stalls," one of them said, loud enough for the whole row. "By order of the garrison commander. Nobody leaves."

The words went through the market like cold water through cloth. Stalls didn't close here; stalls were the heartbeat. Fishwives froze mid-haggle, hands still curled around coins, and a silence spread outward from the soldiers the way oil spreads on wet stone. Marta's fingers tightened around Marguerite's wrist—not dramatically, just enough to say *I'm here*, which was the only currency the two of them traded in anymore.

"What's happened?" someone asked, and nobody answered, and the not-answering was worse than any answer.

Marguerite lowered her eyes to her basket and became, with the ease of long practice, a woman interested entirely in oysters. Inside, everything in her had gone very quiet and very fast, the way it does when you fall.

***

They took the boat at the drying-docks in full view of half the town.

There was no subtlety in it, no apology. Six soldiers waded out to where the *Cormorant* sat low on its moorings, and they boarded her with rifles slung, and they began pulling out her cargo and stacking it on the dock in the gray morning light: baskets of dried fish, jars of brine, coils of netting. And then—Marguerite saw it happen from thirty yards away, through the gap between two warehouses, her heart going like a bird against a window—they pulled up the false floor of the forward hold.

Empty. Empty, empty, empty. Tomas had insisted the family wouldn't board until the final night, would wait in the mangroves until the last possible hour, and his caution sat there now like a miracle: an empty cavity, some old canvas, a smell of bilge. Nothing damning. A smuggler's compartment, perhaps. A fisherman's dishonesty. The kind of thing every boat on this coast had in some form or another, and the soldiers clearly knew it, because they kept searching, methodical as ants, turning the *Cormorant* inside out while the crowd on the docks grew and thickened and pretended not to be a crowd.

Then they brought out the crew.

Old Benito first, the owner, his white hair unbound and blowing, protesting with both arms raised—not guiltily, Marguerite noted, but loudly, in the fluent outrage of a man who had paid bribes to three governments. Behind him came his son, Raul, and Raul's wife, Delphine. Delphine, who had carried messages folded inside loaves of bread for three weeks without once letting her face change. Delphine, who had taught Marguerite which guard drank on duty and which one prayed.

They walked them down the dock with their wrists bound in front of them, and the crowd parted, and the crowd was careful to look like nothing more than weather.

Marguerite stood in it. She had positioned herself deliberately, back against a stack of crab pots, shawl up, eyes down—the whole grammar of disappearance—but she made herself watch. She owed them that. If she let herself look away, if she let herself become the woman who studied her shoes while her people were marched past, then she was nothing but the ledger with legs, and she refused.

Delphine's eyes swept the crowd as she passed. Once, quickly, professionally—the same sweep she gave the garrison yard. Marguerite watched her do it and watched her find nothing, because there was nothing to find, because Marguerite Sandoval was a woman holding a basket of oysters, and Delphine's chin lifted slightly, almost imperceptibly, in what might have been relief or might have been contempt, and then they turned the corner toward the stockade and were gone.

Benito was still shouting. His voice hung over the water after the rest of him had vanished: *thirty years I've fished this coast, thirty years, ask anyone, ask the company, ask God himself—*

Nobody asked anybody. A lieutenant Marguerite didn't know posted a paper on the warehouse door declaring the vessel impounded under military authority pending investigation. The ink wasn't dry before children were reading it aloud to their mothers.

***

She found out about Quill by evening, and she found out the way the town found out everything: through the fishwives, whose gossip network was older than any ledger and would outlast every army that ever anchored in that harbor.

"He sold them," Marta said, in the dark of Marguerite's kitchen, both women sitting close to the single lamp as if the light itself could be arrested. "Quill. Went up Tuesday with his hat off like a beggar. They gave him paper for it—real paper, army scrip. Rosa saw him counting it behind his stall. He bought a new scale." Marta's mouth twisted. "A scale. To weigh his filthy fish honestly, now that he's sold men for money."

"Did he name names?" Marguerite asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that and sickened by it in equal measure. "Or just the boat?"

"Just the boat, near as anyone knows. But—" Marta leaned closer, lowering her voice beneath even the lamp's hearing. "Rosa says the soldiers came back for him today. Took him to the records building. Not the stockade—the records building. Like a guest."

The records building. Where the ledgers lived now, under lock, under lamplight, under Lieutenant Harrow's patient hands. Marguerite felt the floor tilt gently beneath the room. Quill hadn't just informed; he'd been *debriefed*. Somewhere in that building, right now, a careful man was writing down everything Quill knew, and everything Quill knew was everything Quill had seen, and Quill had spent twenty years seeing everything on this coast.

"They'll want more than the boat," Marguerite said quietly. "An informer who gives one name is worth scrip. An informer who gives ten is worth a salary."

Marta reached across and put her rough hand over Marguerite's. "Then we move faster than he can talk."

"How? The boat's gone. Benito's in the stockade. Every hull in this harbor will be searched from now until Doomsday."

"There are other boats," Marta said. "There are always other boats. And there's you."

"I'm nothing," Marguerite said. "That's the point of me."

"No." Marta's grip tightened, and her old eyes caught the lamplight and held it. "You're the one who knows the papers. You're the one the clerk trusted. Don't you dare tell me what you are, girl. We've all known since the arrests started. We've known, and we've said nothing, and we've left bread where bread was needed. That's what invisible means here. It doesn't mean alone."

Marguerite stared at her. Ten years she had built that invisibility plank by plank, and in one sentence an old woman had told her the whole town had been standing on it with her, holding it up, keeping it secret, the way the sea holds up a swimmer who thinks she's swimming alone.

She should have felt exposed. Instead she felt—for the first time since the gunboats came—less alone than afraid.

***

She walked home past the stockade because she couldn't help it, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The compound wall was new timber, army-cut, and beyond it she could hear voices—low, many, the sound of a village compressed into a yard. Somewhere in there were Benito and Raul and Delphine, and the six families taken in the dawn arrests, and every day the walls seemed to grow taller.

A sentry paced the walk above the gate. She kept moving. At the corner of the street she stopped, ostensibly to fix her bootlace, and let her eyes do one quick pass along the wall's base—and that was when she saw the chalk mark.

Small. Waist-high. Three short lines scratched into the timber, fresh, the dust still clinging. She knew the sign; the fishwives had revived it from their mothers' mothers, a signal older than the war that was trying to kill it. Three lines meant: *watched. careful. wait.*

Someone had marked the stockade for the network. Someone inside or someone passing, someone who had seen what she'd seen and wanted the others warned off. Which meant the network still breathed. Which meant Delphine—or someone like her—had gotten word out even from inside.

But it also meant the enemy knew the network existed. Why else mark the stockade at all?

She straightened, shouldered her basket, and walked on with the slow, unhurried tread of a tired woman going home to supper. Behind her the stockade wall stood black against the last light, and beyond it, out in the mangroves, a family waited in the mosquitoes and the dark for a boat that was now sitting impounded under armed guard, its hold gutted, its owner in chains.

Three days. Maybe four. That was how long Harrow had given the sweep, according to the dinner-table whispers that had reached the fishwives through the factor's own cook. Three days, and the *Cormorant* rotting at its moorings, and an informer in the records building telling a patient man everything he'd ever seen.

And Quill had seen her. Of course he had. Twenty years of weighing eels and watching—you didn't survive that trade by being blind. Had he named her? Did a description of her exist somewhere now, in a neat officer's hand—*woman, young, seen speaking with Delphine at the fish market, seen near the docks*—waiting to be matched to a page in a ledger where her name already sat beside a promise she'd made in childhood?

She climbed the path to her door as the first stars came out over the water, and she understood, with terrible clarity, that the cost of the plan had just outrun the plan itself. Two helpers in chains. A boat under guard. An informer fed and housed by the enemy. And out in the mangroves, Tomas's father still refusing to move without his deed-papers, still insisting on the past, while the present closed around them like a net.

Tomorrow she would go to Tomas. Tomorrow she would tell him the truth: there would be no deed-papers, no proper cargo, no clean escape. There would be whatever she could forge in one night and whatever hull the women could find and whatever luck remained, and after that, nothing but water.

She lit her lamp. Sat down. And began, with a steady hand, to draw up the papers for a family that might already be dead.
