## 15. Interrogation by Lamplight

Lieutenant Harrow had learned long ago that fear was a blunt instrument. It broke people, yes, but it broke them into fragments—shards of confession that fit nothing together, edges that cut the interrogator as often as the prisoner. What he wanted was not fear. What he wanted was thread.

So when they brought the fishwife in at nine o'clock that evening, he did not shout. He had a chair set for her by the stove, because her hands were blue from the holding cell, and he poured tea himself, and he sat down across from her with his notebook closed on his knee.

"Mrs. Cabral," he said. "Or do you prefer Petra?"

She looked at him the way a gull looks at a fisherman: patient, calculating, entirely unimpressed. "Petra's fine. Everyone calls me Petra."

"Everyone." He smiled. He let the word sit there between them, small and harmless as a coin on a table. "You're well known in the salt market."

"Known enough. I sell what I catch and my husband dries it, and I've done so since before you were born, Lieutenant, if the face on you means anything."

"It usually does." He opened his notebook now—not to write, just to have it open, the way a man opens his hands to show he carries no stone. "I'm going to be honest with you, Petra, because I find honesty moves things along faster than anything else I might try. Two of your friends were arrested yesterday. You know that. The boat was impounded. You know that too, or you'll know it by morning, because this town has one nervous system and everything travels along it faster than I can walk."

Petra said nothing. She held her tea in both hands but did not drink.

"The charge is conspiracy to move persons out of the territory without permits. Persons of island origin, specifically. In wartime, that isn't smuggling. That's aiding the enemy." He paused, watching her eyes, which were brown and steady and gave away exactly nothing. "Now. Here is what honesty gets you. If you tell me how the network works—who organized it, who carried messages, who paid for the boat—I will treat you as someone deceived by cleverer people. Deceived women go home, Petra. Organizers go somewhere else."

"And if I tell you nothing?"

"Then I keep asking questions until I stop being polite, and you'll wish we'd had this conversation by the stove instead of wherever it ends up." He said it without heat, almost gently, and that gentleness was the point. Cruelty promised made men brace themselves. Cruelty implied kept them soft all night.

Petra drank her tea after all. He counted that as a small victory—the body accepting comfort from the hand that threatened it.

* * *

He worked her for two hours, and she gave him nothing whole, but she gave him threads without meaning to, the way a worn sleeve sheds fibers.

It began with the boat itself. The impounded drying-boat was registered to a widower named Costa who had died fourteen months ago, and yet its cargo manifest, filed three days before the arrests, bore a signature in a woman's hand—neat, educated, the loops of the capital letters closed like little locks. Harrow had the signature photographed and pinned above his desk before he went down to the cells again.

"Someone signed for you," he said to Petra. "A woman. She writes beautifully. Better than most clerks."

"Plenty of women write."

"Not plenty of women in this town. This town writes its name and stops. This signature writes like someone who spent years copying other people's accounts." He watched her face for the flicker and saw only the sea looking back at him. "Who does accounts here, Petra? Think carefully, because I already know the answer, and every question I ask that you lie about is a question I'll ask twice."

"There's the company office," she said slowly. "There's old Mr. Anselm. There's the girl who copies for him sometimes."

"The girl."

"Sandoval. Marguerite Sandoval." Petra shrugged, an elaborate performance of indifference. "Half the town can tell you that. She's been in and out of that office since she was a child. Her mother sold oysters; the company took the debt; the company takes the daughter. That's not a secret, Lieutenant, that's Tuesday."

It was true, which was what made it useful and useless at once. Everything true about Marguerite Sandoval was also ordinary. She was poor, indentured, half-orphaned, unremarkable—a woman the ledger itself described in figures so small they barely warranted ink. And yet.

And yet Harrow had spent three weeks with the requisitioned ledgers, and he had developed, in that time, something like intimacy with the handwriting inside them. The clerk Anselm wrote like a dying man—his letters had been decaying year over year, the descenders shortening, the pressure failing, a slow collapse visible across two decades of columns. But certain pages, certain entries, certain travel permits issued in the last six years showed a second hand. Younger. Steadier. A hand that copied Anselm's style the way a good musician plays another composer's music—faithfully, but with a pulse underneath that wasn't quite his.

He had noticed it first on a permit dated four years back, allowing an island-born fisherman to travel to the mainland market—a routine document, except the fisherman had never arrived anywhere, and the permit's date fell on a day Anselm's log showed him bedridden with fever. Somebody else had written that permit. Somebody with access, skill, and a reason.

Harrow laid these pages out now on the table between himself and Petra Cabral, five documents fanned like a card hand.

"Look at the writing," he said. "Don't touch them. Just look."

She looked. He watched her look, and here at last came the flicker—small, quickly buried, but real. Recognition. Not surprise. Recognition, which meant she had seen the hand before, recently, probably in connection with the forged signature on the boat manifest.

"Well?" he said.

"I'm not a judge of writing. I dry fish."

"You're a magnificent judge of writing. You simply prefer not to be." He gathered the pages back, unhurried. "Here's where we are, Petra, so you can follow along. Someone altered company records. Someone forged travel papers, possibly for years. Someone signed a false manifest to move islanders by sea. And the same evening those islanders were meant to sail, your friends were loading them into a hold. Now—" he leaned forward, voice dropping, confiding, almost kind— "either you are part of that, in which case you should talk to me while talking still buys something. Or you are not part of it, in which case you should be terrified, because whoever organized this used people like you the way a current uses driftwood, and when it runs aground, the driftwood takes the damage."

Something moved behind her eyes then—not fear exactly. Grief, maybe. Or anger wearing grief's coat.

"You talk pretty," Petra said. "For a man who burns villages."

"I've never burned a village."

"No. You read ledgers and let other men burn them. That's the modern way, isn't it. Paper does the killing now, and the paper men sleep well."

Harrow laughed despite himself—a short, genuine sound that startled them both. "You'd have made a fine officer, Mrs. Cabral."

"I'd have made a terrible officer. I'd have told the truth."

* * *

At midnight he sent her back to the cell—not the cold one; he ordered her moved to the room off the kitchen, because a comfortable prisoner talks in her sleep and a frozen one only curses—and he sat alone with the files, and he built the thing he was building, thread by thread, on the wide table in the records room.

The network, as far as he could reconstruct it, ran through the fishwives' gossip the way water runs through limestone: invisibly, shapelessly, surfacing only where it wore a hole. Warnings had reached the island families before each sweep—someone always knew the schedule a day early. His own patrol rotations, he now suspected with professional humility, had been read aloud in the salt market within hours of his issuing them. He had begun issuing false schedules, and two nights ago the false schedule had produced exactly nothing, which confirmed rather than refuted the leak: the real information was flowing through a channel his forgeries couldn't reach.

Which meant the channel wasn't gossip. Gossip would carry poison as readily as truth. No—someone was filtering. Someone close enough to him, or to the records, to know which schedules were real.

He spread the documents again. The decayed hand of Anselm. The young hand beneath it, faithful and pulsing. The manifest signature, neat, loops locked shut.

He wrote *Sandoval, M.* on a fresh card and set it in the middle of the table, and then he sat looking at it for a long time, feeling the particular pleasure and unease that comes when a pattern completes itself too easily.

Because there was a problem, and the problem was the ledger.

Three days ago, doing his cross-referencing, he had pulled the original bond entries—sworn debts, witnessed promises, the paper trail of obligation that bound this coast together. He had found the entry he'd flagged weeks before: *Sandoval, M.—witness to islander rescue, bond sworn.* A child's promise, witnessed and recorded, that she would warn the family of the boy Tomas against ships coming for them. Curious, he had thought. Charming, even. The sort of sentimental residue that paper preserves and life forgets.

But tonight, comparing the entry against his memory of the page, he found something wrong. The ink was right. The hand was right—it matched the young copyist's hand precisely. And yet the entry sat on the page in a way that bothered him the way a picture hangs crooked: the spacing above it was tight, the spacing below generous, as though a line had been removed, or added, or shifted. As though someone had gone back into the book and touched it.

Two possibilities presented themselves, and he turned them like a pearl between finger and thumb.

The first: Marguerite Sandoval had altered her own entry, deepening or disguising the bond, and her carelessness with spacing was the seam showing. Guilty, and sloppy under pressure.

The second: someone else had altered the entry—to protect her, or to frame her, or merely to tidy a record—and the young copyist was exactly what she appeared to be: a tool used by older hands. Anselm's hands. Anselm, who was dying, and who had keys to everything, and whose conscience, if the man had one, must have been waking up late in life like an old dog hearing thieves.

Harrow disliked problems with two clean solutions. Clean solutions were usually both wrong.

* * *

At two in the morning he went down to the kitchen, where the guard reported the prisoner asleep, breathing slow and even on her cot. He stood in the doorway a moment, watching her, thinking about driftwood and currents.

Then he climbed to the factor's veranda, where the night watch kept the signal lamp, and he looked out at the black harbor and the darker line of the mangroves beyond it. Somewhere out there, if the pattern held, the remaining islanders were waiting on the tide. Somewhere in this town, a woman with beautiful handwriting was deciding, tonight, whether to run or to try again.

He thought about her file. Poor. Indentured. Useful and unremarkable—that was how the company's own assessments described her, in the flat language of inventories. Useful and unremarkable. It was, he reflected, the perfect description of a spy, and the worst possible cover, because nobody suspected the invisible, and therefore nobody watched the invisible, and therefore the invisible could do anything.

He took out his card—the one bearing her name—and after a moment he wrote beneath it, in his small precise script: *Ask her to copy something.*

Because there was one test left, simple and cruel as all his best tests. Tomorrow he would summon Marguerite Sandoval to the records room, put a blank page and a pen in front of her, and ask her to make a fair copy of a damaged shipping entry—an innocent task, a chore, the sort of thing she'd done a thousand times.

And he would watch her hand.

If it shook—if the loops trembled, if the pulse underneath the borrowed style faltered—he would know everything the ledger couldn't tell him.

And if it didn't shake, he would know something worse: that he was dealing with someone whose nerve matched his own.

Harrow pocketed the card, turned down the lamp, and stood a moment longer in the dark, listening to the tide come in over the beds where the pearls grew around their wounds.

In the morning, he decided, he would send for her himself. Politely. With tea.

The way one gentleman asks another for the time before taking his watch.
