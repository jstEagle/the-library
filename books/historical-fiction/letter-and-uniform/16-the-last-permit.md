## 16. The Last Permit

The lamp burned low and the ink went down like a confession.

Marguerite had learned the ledger's handwriting the way other girls learned hymns—by repetition, by candlelight, by the slow accumulation of small devotions. Old Ferro's hand had a particular character: the *f* crossed with a flourish that never quite lifted from the page, the numbers leaning east like men walking into wind. For three nights she had practiced it in the margins of a ruined account book, filling pages with names that did not exist until she made them exist. *Alvarez, R.* *Costa, P.* *Serrano, M.* Fishermen. Deckhands. Free men with permits to travel to the northern grounds for the season.

By the fourth night her hand no longer shook when it wrote them. It only shook afterward, when she set the pen down and looked at what she'd made.

She sat in the back of the drying shed where the fishwives kept their nets, wrapped in the smell of brine and tar, with a board across her knees for a desk. Outside, the garrison's drums beat the evening watch. Two days. Harrow's sweep was two days away—she'd heard it from the laundress who heard it from the corporal who washed his own socks, and the chain of women was more reliable than any dispatch. Two days, and then the soldiers would walk into the mangroves with bayonets fixed and lists in their hands, and everything she had forged, everything Ferro had given her his key and his shame to make possible, would come to nothing if the boat wasn't gone before dawn on the third day.

One set of papers left. She saved it for last because it was the hardest.

*Permit of passage — Serrano family, seven persons — fishing grounds, northern waters, return by end of season.*

Seven persons. She counted them the way she counted everything now: Tomas. His father, old Rafael, whose pride weighed more than his nets. His mother, Luz, who had once pressed a dried fig into Marguerite's hand at the market and said nothing, which among island people was its own kind of blessing. Two younger sisters. A grandmother who spoke no trading tongue. A cousin, sixteen, who fished like he was arguing with the sea.

Seven souls in the hold of a drying-boat that smelled of salt fish and would smell of fear by morning.

She signed the permit. Not Ferro's name this time—the factor's, which she had copied twice already and could do in her sleep, the heavy downstroke of the *F*, the little hook of self-importance at the tail of the signature. Then the company stamp, inked onto a cut potato carved by the cooper's wife, who had laughed for the first time in weeks when Marguerite showed her the design. *Even treason needs good stationery,* the woman had said, and then stopped laughing, because it was too true to be funny.

Marguerite blotted the page. Held it up to the lamp. It was perfect. It was beautiful. It was a lie with seven lives folded inside it, and she loved it more than anything she had ever made.

Then she blew out the lamp and went to find Tomas.

* * *

He came to her at the tide line below the old salt pans, where the mangrove roots made a lattice dark enough to hide two people who had learned to move like shadows. He was thinner than he'd been even a month ago—living on what the mangroves gave, mud crabs and oysters poached from the beds that used to be his family's, sleeping in shifts, listening all night for boots. When he stepped out of the root-shadow her chest tightened with an old grief that had nothing to do with the war. Ten years since he'd pulled her from the water, and he still moved through it like it was his first language.

"You have news," he said. He always read her first, before she spoke. It should have frightened her, being so legible. Instead it felt like being known in a world that knew her only as a number in a book.

"The papers are ready. All of them. Travel permits, ration chits, a letter of consignment for the cargo." She kept her voice flat and practical, because if she let anything else into it she would not be able to say the rest. "The *Saltera* loads at the north quay tomorrow after the second watch. Marta has the crew bought. You go aboard as deckhands taking the seasonal run."

His face opened—and then closed again, because he was his father's son, and he already knew what was coming.

"And the deed-papers," he said.

"That's why I'm here."

"They're in the requisitioned warehouse. Third shelf of the records room, green folio, marked with our village name." He recited it like a prayer he'd been saying nightly. "Rafael will not go without them. I told you—he says if we leave the papers, we leave the proof we ever owned the water, and then we're refugees instead of fishermen, and his sons' sons will beg harbors instead of working them. He buried two brothers under that claim, Marguerite. He will die on that shelf of paper before he walks away from it."

"I know what he'll do. That's why I'm telling you how it has to be." She made herself look at him. Made herself say it plain. "You cannot wait for them. The boat goes tomorrow night, or it doesn't go at all."

The silence between them filled with the sound of the tide working the roots.

"Harrow sweeps the mangroves in two days," she went on, and now she couldn't keep the heat out, and didn't try. "I've forged every paper I can forge. I've spent Ferro's key and his conscience and my own sleep, and I've put six women in this town at risk of the stockade, one of whom already has a son behind wire because I was too careful, too slow. And none of it—not one line of ink—is worth anything if your father decides his pride is heavier than seven lives." Her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed through anyway. "Tomas. There is no version of this where I get the deed-papers out of that warehouse. Harrow sleeps fifty feet from that room. Every day I go near it is a day the net draws tighter around all of us. If I tried, I would lose the papers, the boat, and everyone in both."

"So we tell him," Tomas said slowly. "We show him the permit. Seven names. We tell him the boat leaves tomorrow and the papers stay."

"Can you?"

"He's my father."

"I know exactly what he is," she said. "He's a man who taught his son to swim by throwing him off the canoe, and who swore over a broken pearl shell that the sea keeps honest accounts. I remember him, Tomas. I've remembered him for ten years while pretending I remembered nothing. But the sea isn't keeping accounts right now. Harrow is. And Harrow's arithmetic has no room for honor."

Tomas turned away from her. Walked three steps along the tideline, stopped, stood with his back to her and the water pulling at his heels, and she watched him carry the argument to a man who wasn't there, and lose it, the way she had already lost it for him in the drying shed with the lamp burning low.

"My mother will go," he said finally. "Luz will take the girls by the ear and put them in that hold herself. And my grandmother. It's Rafael alone."

"Then don't fight him. Don't argue pride with pride—you'll lose; he's had sixty years of practice." She came closer. The tide came with her, cold around her ankles, and she thought absurdly of a girl going under ten years ago, of hands closing on her wrist, of the oath sworn dripping and half-drowned with a shell cutting both their palms. *If ships ever come for them, you warn my family. You swear it on the water.* She had sworn it on the water. The water had never released her since.

"Tell him the truth instead," she said. "Tell him the papers were never his to save. Tell him—" and here her voice did something strange, dropped low and steady, the voice of the ledger room, the voice of a woman who had spent years copying other people's debts in someone else's hand— "tell him I will write him new ones. Whatever waters you reach, whatever harbor takes you, I will draw up the claim myself and sign it in the company's own hand, and no clerk alive will know it isn't real. He won't have lost the proof. He'll be carrying forgery instead of memory, and forgery floats."

Tomas looked at her then, really looked, and something moved across his face that she had seen once before, years ago, when he hauled her gasping over the gunwale: the particular astonishment of a person watching someone refuse to drown.

"You'd do that," he said. "You'd falsify a land claim across open ocean, on faith, for a promise you made as a child."

"I'd do worse. I'm doing worse." She almost smiled. "Ask the ledger."

* * *

They went together through the mangrove channels to where the family hid—a low hummock of roots and hammocks and banked cook-fire smoke, seven people folded into a space the size of a chapel. Luz saw Marguerite coming and rose from the fire pit, and for a moment the older woman just looked at her, at the company girl in her patched dress standing in the mud with salt in her hair, and then Luz took both her hands and pressed them, hard, the way you press a wound to stop it bleeding. No words. Among these people, no words.

Rafael sat apart on a root buttress like a figurehead that had been unshipped and set ashore, whittling nothing in particular out of a stick of driftwood. He listened to Tomas lay out the plan—the quay, the watch, the papers, the timing—with his jaw set in a line Marguerite recognized from the market, from every negotiation where an islander was being offered less than he was worth. When Tomas reached the part about the deed-papers, the knife stopped moving.

"No."

"Papa—"

"The folio stays with us or we stay with the folio. That is the whole of it." He said it not loudly, not cruelly. That was the worst of it. He said it the way the tide says it comes in.

Marguerite knelt in the mud in front of him. It was not a gesture she had planned; her body simply decided, the way it had decided to sink toward the bottom of the sea ten years ago, and the way it had decided, lately, to stop sinking toward anything.

"Old man," she said—because she had earned the rudeness, or hoped she had—"when I was nine years old your son pulled me out of the channel and I swore an oath to him over a broken shell. I have kept that oath badly and slowly and at cost to people who deserved better from me. So hear me now, because I am done being slow." She held up the permit, seven names, the factor's forged signature, the potato-carved stamp. "This is real enough to kill us all if it fails, and it fails the moment you choose paper over people. Your fishing rights—your brothers' graves' worth of rights—I will restore them. On forged stock, in true ink, wherever you land. I copy accounts for the company that owns this coast. I know exactly how claims are made. They are not made of deeds, Rafael. They are made of what people agree to believe, and what they're afraid to question. Give me your belief. Leave the paper."

The whittling knife hung still. Somewhere out in the channels a bird called, three notes, like a question asked twice and once more for certainty.

"You would forge us a country," Rafael said at last. Not mocking. Wondering.

"I'd forge you a door," Marguerite said. "Walking through it is your work."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at his wife, who had not stopped holding Marguerite's hands and whose face said everything the trading tongue had no words for. Then at his daughters. At the grandmother, ancient and silent, who spat once into the fire—Marguerite would learn later that this was approval, the highest grade of it—and at the cousin, sixteen, who was already looking at the boat in his mind, already halfway to the quay.

"It is a hard thing," Rafael said quietly, "to ask a man to abandon the proof of what he is."

"It is a harder thing," Marguerite said, "to make your children carry it to the stockade."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, the tide had turned in his face; she could see it, the way she could read weather now, the way she could read ledgers, the way she could read men who believed themselves unreadable. He laid the driftwood down unfinished.

"We load tomorrow night," he said. "And girl—if your forgery fails us in some northern port, I will come back across the whole sea to haunt you personally."

"If it fails," Marguerite said, "there won't be enough left of me to haunt."

Luz laughed then—one short, cracked note, the first laughter in that camp of roots—and pulled her up out of the mud, and for a moment the whole hidden family was holding her, seven pairs of arms smelling of smoke and salt, and Marguerite let them, and did not say that she might not be there to see whether the door held. Some truths were cargo for another crossing.

* * *

She walked back alone along the tide line with the last light going copper on the water. Tomorrow night the boat would go. Tomorrow night the mangroves would empty, and the sweep would find nothing but cold fires and bent reeds, and Harrow would stand in the records room with his lists and his patient eyes and understand that something had slipped through his fingers—and he would begin, then, in earnest, to look for the hand that had opened the lock.

She had maybe two days of freedom left. Maybe less. She found she was not afraid of that the way she expected to be. What frightened her was smaller and stranger: that when they arrested whoever they arrested, she would feel relief—that ending it would be easier than continuing to carry it. That was the thing about invisibility. It was warm, down there under the surface. The pressure was steady. A person could stay under a long time and call it living.

At the bend where the tide line met the seawall she saw a lantern coming toward her along the embankment, and her heart performed its old trick of stopping entirely.

It was only Marta, the fishwife, shawl black against the last light, walking fast.

"Marguerite." Marta caught her arm, breathless, and the words came out in the flat rush of bad news arriving ahead of itself. "The cooper's wife just came from the quay. They've moved the inspection forward—Harrow's men are boarding every boat leaving the harbor starting tonight, tonight, do you hear me, not tomorrow. And there's a patrol posted at the warehouse gate that wasn't there this morning." Her grip tightened. "Child, whatever you've planned—it has to be now."
