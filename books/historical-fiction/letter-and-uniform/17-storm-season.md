## 17. Storm Season

The sky had been wrong all morning.

Tomas felt it before he saw it—a pressure behind his ears, a flatness to the light, the gulls flying low and silent over the mangroves as if the air itself had grown too heavy to carry them. His father would have called it *bagyo weather*, the season's first true storm, and in another year they would have stayed ashore, mended nets, told stories. But this was not another year. This was the last morning the tide would serve them, and Harrow's sweep was coming at dawn, and so they went out into a sky that looked like bruised fruit.

The launch was smaller than Tomas had hoped. One of the fishwives' husbands had brought it around the point in darkness, an old cargo runner with a patched hull and an engine that coughed twice before it caught, and now it sat low in the brown water off the mangrove channel, riding its mooring line like something tired of waiting.

"Twelve souls," the boatman had said, counting with his eyes. "Twelve and whatever you're carrying. No more."

They were carrying more than that. They were carrying everything.

Tomas stood at the gunwale while his mother handed down the bundles: blankets wrapped in oilcloth, a clay pot of rice gone hard at the edges, the small wooden saints his grandmother had carried across three seas in her lifetime and refused to leave to the soldiers or the salt. His sisters came next, silent, their hair bound tight against the wind. Then the old ones—his uncle who could no longer swim, his aunt whose hands shook too badly to hold a line—and then the neighbors from the far camp, four families' worth of fear folded into one small boat.

His father came last.

He came down the mangrove mud slowly, deliberately, the way he did everything, and Tomas saw immediately what was wrong. The bundle under his father's arm was not a blanket. It was not rice. It was flat and heavy and tied with the red string that meant paper that mattered, and Tomas knew what it was before his father set foot on the deck.

The deed-papers.

* * *

They had fought about it for three nights, in the whisper-voice his father used instead of shouting, which was worse than shouting because it never ended.

"The papers are the proof," his father had said, kneeling over the tin box in the dark of the stilt-house, holding the documents up into the lamplight like a priest elevating the host. "Forty years our family has fished those grounds. Forty years, written down, signed by men who are dead now but whose signatures still speak. Without them we are squatters. Without them we are nothing but people running."

"Papa." Tomas had kept his voice even, though his chest was full of broken glass. "Without *us* there is nothing to prove. The soldiers are sweeping the camp in days. Marguerite says—"

"Marguerite says." His father said her name the way he said the names of typhoons. Not with hatred. With the particular contempt of a man for weather he has survived before. "The company girl. The one who writes her name in their books."

"She is the reason we know when the boats come. She altered the ledger, Papa. She forged the permits. She has risked—"

"I know what she has risked." His father had closed the tin box, and the click of it had ended the conversation, as clicks do. "And I am grateful to God for her. But I will not run like a thief from my own water. We go out with our papers, or we do not go out as ourselves."

And so the papers were coming, forty years of signatures in oilcloth and red string, riding in the same boat as twelve lives and a storm.

Now, pushing off from the mangroves, Tomas watched his father settle into the bow with the bundle held on his knees like a sleeping child, and he thought: *we are going to die for paper.* He did not believe it fully. But he believed it enough that his hands would not stop moving—checking the fuel line, checking the bilge, checking the faces of the women and children huddled under the canvas awning—for work was the only prayer he trusted anymore.

* * *

They made the point by midmorning, and that was where the sea changed.

Beyond the headland the water went from brown to green to a deep, cold blue, and the wind arrived all at once, the way storms arrive on that coast—not building but simply *present*, as if it had been waiting offshore for permission. The launch began to pitch. The canvas snapped. Somewhere beneath the deck plates the engine found a new note, higher and less certain.

The boatman shouted over the wind: "We make for the shipping lane! If we reach the lane, we're just another boat! Patrols don't stop neutral traffic in open water!"

"And if we don't?" his aunt asked, and nobody answered, because everyone already knew.

It was half past ten when Tomas saw the smoke.

It rose off the southern horizon, thin and gray at first, and his heart lifted stupidly—he thought, for one whole breathless second, *a steamer, a merchantman, cover for us*—before his eyes finished the arithmetic. The smoke was moving against the wind. Smoke only moved against the wind when it was pushed, and things were pushed by engines, and engines on that coast in that season belonged to exactly one navy.

"Patrol boat," he said. He didn't shout it. There was no need. Fear travels through a crowded boat faster than any voice could carry it.

The launch became very quiet. Under the canvas, his youngest sister began to cry without sound, the way children cry when they have learned that sound is dangerous, and their mother pulled her face into her shoulder.

Tomas looked at his father.

His father was looking at the smoke too, and his face—the face that had argued for three nights about papers, the proud face, the stubborn face—was doing something Tomas had never seen it do. It was calculating. Not the pride. The arithmetic. Twelve souls against one engine. A loaded boat against a chasing one. Every bundle, every pot, every saint and blanket and tin box, weighed in the balance against the distance between them and the shipping lane.

The patrol boat turned toward them.

Not sharply—it didn't need to be sharp; it had speed to spare—but with the unhurried confidence of something that has already decided how the morning ends. Through the spray and the gray light Tomas could see figures at its rail, and the glint of glass where a man raised binoculars, and he understood with terrible clarity that they were being read. That somewhere behind that glass a lieutenant was matching what he saw against what he knew, names to faces, faces to families, the way he matched everything.

"We can't outrun it," the boatman said. "Not with her sitting this deep. She'll take us inside twenty minutes."

"Then throw out weight," someone said, and hands began moving—blankets going over the side, the clay pot, a bundle of drying fish, the small wooden saints—

"No!" His grandmother's voice, cracked and furious, snatching the saints back. "Not them!"

And through all of it his father sat motionless in the bow, the red-stringed bundle on his knees, watching the patrol boat come.

* * *

Later, Tomas would try to remember the exact order of what happened next, and he never quite could. Memory gave it to him in pieces, like wreckage washed up separately on different mornings.

He remembered the first wave that broke green over the bow and the cold slap of it, the boat suddenly lighter and lower and slower, shipping water. He remembered the boatman screaming about the pump. He remembered his mother's arms around his sisters, making one shape of them, one target.

But mostly he remembered his father standing up.

He stood the way old men stand in boats—feet wide, knees bent, body answering the sea's argument automatically after fifty years of practice—and he unwound the red string from the bundle with fingers that were not shaking at all.

"Papa." Tomas reached for him. "Papa, no—"

"Your grandfather signed these papers with a thumbprint," his father said, "because no one had ever taught him to write. Do you understand? He pressed his hand into ink like an animal, because that was what they allowed him, and he did it anyway, so that his son would have something to hold." He looked at the bundle, and then out at the water, and then at Tomas, and what was in his face was not defeat. Tomas would spend the rest of his life being sure of that. It was not defeat. "I said we would not run like thieves. I was wrong. A thief runs to keep what he stole. These—" he lifted the papers, "—these were always ours. No soldier can give them to us and no soldier can take them from us. The paper was only ever the *shadow* of the thing. And you cannot drown a man by sinking his shadow."

"Papa, we can put them in oilcloth, we can—"

"Tomas." Quietly. The whisper-voice, but gentle now, gentler than Tomas had ever heard it. "Look at me. I spent three nights telling your girl Marguerite she didn't understand ownership. Let me tell you what I understand instead." He stepped up onto the thwart, into the wind, into the spray, holding the papers against his chest. "What I own, I am taking with me." He touched his own chest. Twice, then—once for himself, once, Tomas realized, for his father, and his grandfather, the thumbprint man, the whole drowned line of them. "Everything else is ballast."

And he threw the deed-papers into the sea.

The oilcloth caught the wind for a moment—forty years of signatures lifting once, like a bird deciding—and then the water took it. Tomas watched the red string go under last, a thin bright line unspooling into the blue, and felt something tear in his chest and something else, underneath the tearing, let go. He could not have said which sensation was grief and which was freedom. They came from the same place. They wore the same face.

The launch rose in the water. Only inches—but the sea noticed. The boat answered the wheel differently, the engine's strained note eased, and the boatman shouted "That's it! Keep her light! Everything that floats, off!" and the throwing-over became general then, blankets and baskets and the weight of every possession except the saints and the people, until the launch ran shallow and quick as the thing she'd been built to be.

Behind them, the patrol boat kept coming. Of course it did. It had not been chasing their cargo.

* * *

The shipping lane appeared at noon as a long glittering road of swells, and on it, miraculously, absurdly, a tramp steamer flying a neutral flag, wallowing north under a smudge of coal smoke. The boatman put the launch directly into the steamer's wake, into the churn and clutter of her passage, hiding among the big ship's ripples like a minnow sheltering beside a whale.

The patrol boat slowed.

Tomas watched it happen. Watched the gray hull fall off its pursuit, watched it circle once at the edge of the lane with the patience of a dog at the end of a rope, watched the binocular-glint swing across them one last time—counting, recording, *remembering*—and then turn away, back toward the coast, back toward the harbor, back toward the town and the stockade and the records room and the ledger.

Back toward Marguerite.

The relief hit him so hard he had to grip the rail. Around him the boat exhaled—his aunt crossing herself, his mother finally letting his sister cry aloud now that sound was safe, the old uncle laughing in the breathless way of men who have been certain they were drowning and have just discovered otherwise.

They were through. They were through, and the water ahead ran clean to neutral sea, and his family sat in a boat that carried nothing now but themselves, and somehow that was the richest the boat had ever been.

Then Tomas thought it through, the way his father would have, and the relief curdled.

Harrow had seen them. Had counted them. Had let the binoculars rest on their faces long enough to *know* them—and then had turned away. A hunter does not turn away from prey unless he has better prey. Unless the net behind him is more valuable than the fish escaping in front of him.

Unless the escape itself was only ever evidence, and the evidence pointed home.

"Harrow let us go," Tomas said quietly, to no one, to the wind. "He wanted us to run. A chase makes a report; a report makes questions; questions make the factor nervous. But a boat that gets away—" his stomach dropped as the sentence finished itself, "—a boat that gets away means someone helped it. Someone on shore. Papers. Forged papers, in a ledger room, in a garrison, alone at night—"

His mother touched his arm. "Tomas. What is it?"

He looked south, at the shrinking coastline, at the smudge of the harbor he could no longer distinguish from the storm cloud gathering over it. Somewhere under that gray, a woman who had torn her own name out of the company's books was sitting in a records room, and the man who read ledgers the way priests read scripture was walking toward it with everything he needed already in his hands.

She had bought their crossing with herself. He understood that now, completely, the way you understand a thing only after the price has been paid and cannot be refunded. She had known, when she forged the last permit, when she sent them into the storm—she had known exactly what she was doing, and she had done it anyway.

"Turn the boat," he said.

The boatman stared at him. "What?"

"Not toward the harbor." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "There's a cove past the point—half a day's sail, inside the storm line. Put us ashore there and I go the rest of the way on foot." He was already pulling the oilskin from under the thwart, already binding his hair back, and his mother was saying his name in the voice mothers use when they already know they've lost, and his father—who had thrown forty years of paper into the sea an hour ago and stood lighter for it—was looking at his son with something that was almost approval.

"You'd walk back into the net," his father said. It wasn't a question.

"She walked us out of hers." Tomas tightened the last knot. "There's a debt. And I know how she feels about debts."

Ahead of them the storm front came on like a closing door, and beyond it, small and gray and getting smaller, the coast waited—with its stockades, its ledgers, its lamplit rooms—and somewhere inside it, a woman named Marguerite Sandoval, who had given away every name she had, was about to learn whether anyone would come to give one back.
