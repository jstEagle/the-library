## 20. Departures

The news came the way all important news came to our coast: sideways, wrapped in something ordinary.

I was at the transport dock before dawn, three days after I signed my name to my own ruin, waiting with the other labor conscripts for the muster whistle. The army had given us numbers instead of names—mine was stenciled on a canvas kit bag that smelled of tar and someone else's sweat—and we stood in a loose line in the grey light while a corporal counted us twice because he couldn't count us once correctly.

A fishwife named Adelita worked her way along the crowd selling coffee from a dented pot, and when she reached me she poured without being asked and said, low, under the hiss of the urn: *They made it. Past the point, past the patrol line. Neutral water by moonrise Tuesday.*

She moved on before I could answer. That was the discipline of it. That was what we had learned.

The coffee scalded my tongue and I didn't care. I looked out at the harbor—at the gunboats riding their anchors, at the drying-racks stripped bare, at the mangrove line dark against the east—and I let myself feel it for exactly as long as it took to finish the cup: Tomas's mother alive, his father alive, the little ones breathing salt air that belonged to no ledger. Alive, and gone, and never coming back, which was the shape of every victory this war would allow us.

Then the whistle blew, and I put down the cup, and I became number 114 again.

* * *

They gave the conscripts one day to settle affairs. One day, as if a life could be folded up like laundry and stowed.

I spent the first hours of it doing what I had sworn not to do: walking past the company office. The shutters were open. Inside, a young clerk—a boy really, sent from the capital, with soft hands and a copybook hand—sat at old Ferro's desk, stamping requisition forms. The ledgers were gone, crated and shipped north weeks ago, but the boy stamped anyway, because stamping was what desks were for. He glanced up at me standing in the doorway shadow, and I saw him take in the labor corps armband, and look away. A woman marked for the army's own service was not a person you met the eye of. We were the town's bad arithmetic now, its sum carried forward into the next column.

"Looking for work?" he asked, because the silence embarrassed him.

"No," I said. "Saying goodbye."

"To whom?"

And there it was—the whole question of my life in three words. To the desk where I'd learned that power lived in paper. To the old man who'd died holding his key and his shame. To the girl whose name had sat in those pages beside a promise she'd actually kept, which was more than most entries could claim.

"To the light," I said. "It comes through that window nicely in the afternoon."

He frowned at the window, at nothing, and went back to his stamping, and I walked away before my face could tell him anything useful.

* * *

In the afternoon I went to the beds.

The oyster grounds lay a half-mile out, and I rowed myself there in a leaky skiff I borrowed from a widow who didn't ask why, since everyone in town knew by then who was leaving and who was staying and which kind of leaving each was. The water was flat and green-gold, the season turning toward storm, and I shipped the oars over the spot where my family's lease had been before the company absorbed it, before my mother's lungs filled with brine and her debt transferred to me like any other inheritance.

Ten years I had worked these beds. Ten years of cold hands and cut palms and the long slow arithmetic of shells: so many bushels to the company, so much deducted for tools, for rice, for the doctor who bled my mother and killed her faster, for interest compounding like rot. I had been good at it. Being good at surviving is a skill nobody congratulates you for, and I had gotten so fluent in it that I sometimes forgot there had ever been another language.

I trailed my fingers in the water. Somewhere below, in the dark, the oysters were making pearls around their wounds the way they always did—taking an irritation and coating it, layer by layer, until the hurt became something smooth enough to keep. I used to think that was beautiful. Now I thought it was just what living things did with damage, and that beauty was what other people called it afterward, at a safe distance.

"Tomas pulled me out right about there," I told the empty water, because there was no one left to tell it to properly. "Right there. And I've been swimming ever since."

The tide took my voice away south, toward neutral water, and I pulled for shore before the light went.

* * *

My mother's grave sits on the rise above the fisher quarter, where the ground is too poor to farm and too rocky to build on, which is how the poor get their cemeteries. No headstone—we couldn't afford stone—but a wooden marker Ferro had let me carve on a company lathe, back when borrowing company property for grief still felt like a small crime rather than a habit. Her name, her dates, and underneath, because I was nineteen and angry and hadn't yet learned to hide it: *She Paid.*

The army had not taken the graveyard. There was nothing in it worth requisitioning.

I knelt in the dry grass and pulled weeds, not because the dead need weeding but because kneeling gives your hands something honest to do while the rest of you says what it has to say.

"Mama," I said. "I'm going north."

The wind off the water moved the grass in long combed strokes, the way she used to comb my hair when it was still long, before I cut it short for the beds.

"You'd want me to tell you it was for a good reason. It was. A whole family's alive because of what I did, and I'm not sorry, and I know you'd have done the same, because you're the one who taught me that a promise isn't paper—it's what's left when the paper burns."

A gull screamed somewhere behind me, greedy and indifferent.

"But I'll say the hard part too, since you can't interrupt me anymore, and you always did." I pressed my palm flat on the warm earth over her. "I kept the promise and I lost everything you built. The beds are gone. The name Sandoval is going north on a prison transport. If there's anything left of us after this, it won't be here. I'm sorry. I did the math and I chose the people over the property, and if you haunt me for it, I'll sit still for the haunting."

The wind died. The evening came down soft as flour sifting. And in the quiet I heard—not with my ears, but in the older way, the daughter's way—something that might have been her saying: *Good girl. Terrible arithmetic. Right choice.* My mother had never once told me I was right in her life. She saved it, apparently, for after.

I laughed until I cried, or cried until it turned into laughing; at the graveside they sound the same. Then I stood, brushed off my knees, and left the marker standing. Let the storms take it whenever they liked. Everything she was, I was carrying north in the only vault that couldn't be requisitioned.

* * *

Last, though I hadn't planned it, I went to find Tomas.

I had told myself I wouldn't. What was there to say? He was a name on a successful escape, a family whole on foreign sand, and I was a conscript with a departure at dawn. Some endings are better left unwritten; I had spent a career forging papers and knew better than anyone that a clean document is a kind lie.

But my feet, which had carried me through drowning and debt and arrest and bargain, refused the lesson, and brought me to the drying-yard at dusk where the fishwives' network had first learned to speak in cargo manifests. He was there alone, mending a net that belonged to no one now, his hands moving with that quick sure island grace the soldiers had never learned to read.

He saw me and set down the shuttle.

"They told you," he said.

"Adelita told me." I stopped an arm's length away. Close enough to touch. Far enough that neither of us would. "Your mother sends word she's keeping my letter. Your father says—" I smiled. "Your father says the sea owes him nothing now, and he owes it nothing, and that's the richest he's been in twenty years."

"That sounds like him."

We stood in the smell of salt and tar and drying fish, two people who had been children together in the water ten years ago and would be, in some permanent way, children together forever, because that was where the bond had been forged and no document could move it.

"I'm going north," I said. "Labor corps. Indefinite term."

"I know." His jaw tightened. "Marguerite—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't offer to wait. Don't swear anything over shells. We've both seen what oaths cost when the tide turns." I looked at him—at the boy who had breathed life back into me on a beach, at the man who had thrown his father's deed-papers overboard to outrun a gunboat—and I gave him the only thing left in my inventory: the truth. "I don't regret it. Any of it. You should know that, wherever you land. When they ask me, years from now, whether it was worth it—I'll already have answered."

"And when the war ends?" His voice was careful, the voice of a man carrying something breakable across ice. "Wars end. Even these wars."

"Then write to me. Care of the Northern Labor Corps, depot four. They let conscripts receive letters." I almost smiled. "I'll be writing letters for the others anyway. I've got a reputation to maintain."

He laughed—one short breath of it, surprised out of him—and then he did touch me, briefly, pressing his forehead to mine in the island fashion, the greeting of kin, the greeting that means *you are of my house wherever you sleep*. His skin was cool and salt-stung. Neither of us closed our eyes. We weren't saying goodbye, whatever the word for it was. We were filing it somewhere it could be found again.

"The tide will bring you back," he said. "Or bring me to you. Water always settles its accounts."

"Not according to any ledger I've ever seen."

"Then it's time you saw better ledgers."

I stepped back before either of us could make it harder. "Go home, Tomas. Be with your people. That's the payment. That's the whole payment, and it's enough."

I walked away and did not look back, because looking back is for people who believe in endings, and I had finally learned better.

* * *

Dawn came grey and hot, the horizon stacked with storm-clouds the color of wet slate. The transport *Coronel* sat low in the harbor, a rust-streaked freighter converted for bodies, and we conscripts climbed her gangway in single file while the townspeople watched from the quay in silence. No waving. No weeping. Our town had learned its manners under occupation: feeling was contraband, displayed only indoors.

But as I crossed the gangway plank, I heard it begin—soft at first, then gathering, passed mouth to mouth along the dock like a current finding the sea. Not a cry. Not a slogan. Just the fishwives' working song, the one they sang hauling nets, the one whose verses were mostly counting and whose meaning was mostly defiance: *one for the taking, two for the keeping, three for the water that gives back its own...*

They sang us out of harbor. All the way past the point, thinning with distance but never stopping, until the town itself was only a smudge and the song was only memory and the memory was enough.

I found a place at the rail, number 114 among forty others, and watched my whole life shrink to a brown line on green water.

Home surrendered. Survival achieved. Both true at once, which was the last and hardest lesson of the coast: you rarely lose one thing at a time, and you never win cleanly, and the best you can hope for is to choose which losses you'll carry and which you'll let the sea have.

Ahead lay the north: the labor camps, the years, the unknown. In my kit bag, sewn into the lining where even a quartermaster wouldn't search, I carried three things. A broken pearl shell, worn smooth by a decade in a pocket. A scrap of ledger page with one line struck through and one name written free above the strike—my own, unowned, the first document I had ever issued to myself. And a blank sheet of paper, folded twice, on which I intended, when the mood took me and the sea allowed, to write the opening of a letter.

The *Coronel* swung her bow north. The storm clouds opened a seam of gold along the horizon ahead of us, brief and untrustworthy, like all promises.

I turned my face into the wind and began, in my head, with the salutation.
