## 22. Letter and Uniform

The letter came on a Tuesday, folded inside a government envelope with a stamp so new the ink still smelled of the press.

*Peace declared. Hostilities concluded by treaty. All labor corps personnel to be processed for release within ninety days. See enclosed certificate.*

Marguerite read it twice, standing in the doorway of her stall, letting the harbor wind lift the corners of the paper the way it lifted everything here—loose, unclaimed, free to go where it pleased. Behind her, a fisherman waited with his cap in his hands, needing a letter written to a brother in the north. She made him wait another moment longer.

Ninety days. Four years had taught her that governments counted time in increments designed to outlast hope, and yet here was a number small enough to hold in one hand.

"You'll want it short?" she asked the fisherman, folding the certificate away.

"Short as you can make it, miss. He can't read much past his own name."

"Then we'll give him something worth reading slowly."

She dipped her pen. The stall was nothing—a plank table, an oilcloth awning, three bottles of ink ranked like soldiers—but it stood at the mouth of the harbor road where every boat came home, and in four years she had written perhaps two thousand letters out of this exact spot. Complaints to the company. Petitions to the magistrate. Love letters, most of them, because love letters were what people actually wanted once they worked up the courage to ask.

She wrote the fisherman's brother four lines about the catch and the weather and their mother's bad knee, and signed it not with the fisherman's mark but with his name, spelled out whole, because that was the service she sold: *your name, entire, on paper.* Then she charged him two coppers and watched him walk away lighter than he'd come.

The war had ended the way wars end—not with the sound everyone expected but with paperwork. Treaties signed in rooms she would never see, between men who had never smelled drying fish. The archipelago was declared neutral waters now. The company's charter had been "reviewed," which meant the ledger system was being dismantled, page by page, by clerks in the capital who thought they were doing something unprecedented. Marguerite had read about it in a newspaper and laughed until the woman at the next table asked if she was well.

They were burning the old debt books, someone told her. Publicly, as ceremony.

She hoped the fire was hot enough to reach the ink.

* * *

Tomas came back on a Thursday, though she did not know it was him at first.

She knew the boat—she knew every boat in the harbor by its particular brokenness—and this one flew no flag and rode too high in the water for how heavily it sat in Marguerite's chest the moment she saw it. A neutral trader's pennant. Island-built hull. And standing at the rail, a man in a plain canvas jacket, older than the boy who had pulled her from the water, thinner, with a scar along his jaw she didn't recognize and didn't need to.

He didn't come straight to her. That was the thing she noticed first, and understood best. He paid the harbor fee. He helped tie off. He spoke to the harbormaster, nodded to the customs man, walked the length of the quay like a person to whom the quay belonged and always had. Fourteen years ago he had been a boy with salt-wet hair holding a broken pearl shell over two children's heads. Now he was a man coming home from a war that had tried to erase him, and he was walking slowly, deliberately, letting the ground confirm itself under his feet.

Only then did he turn toward the stall.

For a long moment neither of them said anything. The harbor filled the silence with its ordinary noises—halyards, gulls, a fishwife arguing about ice—and Marguerite found that all the speeches she had composed over four years of letters she never sent had evaporated entirely.

"You're writing letters now," Tomas said.

"I'm writing names." She gestured at the sign above her awning, which she had painted herself and which said, simply, LETTERS. "The letters are incidental."

He smiled. It changed his whole face, and underneath the scar and the years she saw the boy completely, unmistakably. "My mother says you got them out. All of them. She says it every time anyone mentions the coast, which is constantly, because she's my mother."

"They got themselves out. I pushed some paper around."

"My father burned the deed-papers in open water." He said it lightly, but his hand went briefly to his chest, to some pocket that wasn't there anymore. "He talks about it now like it was his idea all along. Like he wasn't dragged onto that boat half-drowned in pride."

"And your family? The fishing rights?"

"Gone with the papers. But we're alive, and the treaty recognizes islanders' claims in the old grounds." He looked out at the harbor, at the beds beyond, dark under the morning light. "It turns out we were never owed paper. We were owed the water."

Marguerite came around the stall. It seemed suddenly important not to have anything between them—not a table, not an oath, not a ledger line.

"I kept your page," she said. "From the ledger. I couldn't alter it—Harrow had it memorized by then—so I took the penalty for it instead. But when they released me, they gave me my file, and your name was in it. *Witness to rescue, bond sworn, Sandoval, M.* I've been carrying it around for four years like a stone I refused to put down."

"What did you do with it?"

She reached into the drawer beneath the ink bottles and brought out a single sheet, yellowed, stamped with the company seal and crossed through with army cancellation marks. She held it out.

"Burned it would be cleaner. But I thought—" Her voice caught, steadied. "I thought you should be the one. It's your name on it. It was always more yours than mine."

Tomas took the page. He read it all the way through—the childish oath transcribed by a clerk's indifferent hand, the figures assigned to a friendship, the bond sworn over a shell—reading it the way you'd read a letter from someone dead. Then he folded it once, carefully, and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, which was apparently still there after everything.

"We could burn it tonight," he said. "On the water. Where it started."

"It should be where it started."

"That's not why I came back, Marguerite."

She had known that. She had known it since the boat cleared the point, maybe since the first unsent letter four years ago, maybe since a boy's hands closed around her wrists in green water and refused to let go. But knowing a thing and hearing it are different countries, and she had learned to live in both.

"Why did you come back?"

"For the same reason you stayed." He looked at the stall, the ink bottles, the queue of neighbors waiting their turn with their caps off. "You spent four years in a labor camp writing letters for people whose names the army took. I spent four years finding out that the oath was the only part of my childhood that ever belonged to me. We were both paying down the same debt." He paused. "I don't think we owe each other anything anymore. That's what I came back to say."

The wind came off the water and moved between them, patient as tide.

"So say it," Marguerite said.

"The oath is finished. Done. Paid in full, witnessed by the sea, canceled by the treaty, whatever words you want." He took her hand—not the way he had taken it in the water, gripping, desperate, saving, but lightly, openly, an offering rather than a rescue. "I'm asking for something the ledger has no column for."

* * *

They burned the page at dusk, off the point where the beds began, in a tin dish on the stern of his boat while the first stars came out over neutral water. The oath went up in a breath of flame and ash that the wind carried inland, toward the town, toward her mother's grave, toward everything that had been survived.

Then they sat in the dark with their shoulders touching and talked until the lamps on the quay were lit, about small things—his mother's garden, the fisherman's brother, whether the company's replacement clerks were honest or merely new—and none of it was a promise and none of it needed to be.

In the morning, Marguerite opened the stall as always. But she repainted the sign first, adding a second line beneath LETTERS in careful block capitals:

*NAMES RESTORED — REASONABLE RATES — NO DEBT REQUIRED*

And when the first customer of the day arrived—a young woman, island-born, holding a birth certificate she wanted copied before the new registry office could lose it—Marguerite dipped her pen and asked, the way she asked everyone now:

"What's your name? Your whole name. Let's start there."
