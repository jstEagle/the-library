## 1. The Ledger Opens

The ferry *Marigold* came around the bend of the river at half past seven in the morning, and Lior Ashcombe was already standing at the rail with her valise at her feet and her ledger strapped shut under her arm, watching the town assemble itself out of the mist.

She had been told its name was Harrow's Bend. She had been told a great many things about it in the three days since she'd received her assignment, most of them from the ferry clerk in Corvane, who had said it with the particular flatness of a man delivering bad news he had delivered before. *"Harrow's Bend? You're going to Harrow's Bend?"* And then, when she'd confirmed it: *"They don't much care for strangers there. Last census man left early. Didn't finish."*

Lior had thanked him and written nothing down, because some things a person carries better unrecorded.

Now the town rose through the fog in layers: first the smoke of morning fires, gray against gray; then rooftops, slate and shingle, pitched steeply; then the church spire with its weathervane turning slowly, as if unsure; and last—emerging all at once, the way a face emerges when you finally look directly at it—the observatory on the hill above everything, round-shouldered and domed, its copper gone green with years of rain. Beneath the dome, faint even at this distance, stood the pale circle of a clock dial. Its hands were still.

That was the first thing Lior learned about Harrow's Bend, before she ever set foot in it: the observatory clock did not run. Every other detail would come later, filed and cross-referenced like a good clerk's work, but that one arrived first and stayed.

"Passenger for the Bend?" The ferryman had come up beside her, a broad man with rope-scarred hands and the resigned patience of someone who made this crossing twice a day regardless of weather or consequence.

"I'm the census taker," Lior said. "Lior Ashcombe." She offered her hand; he looked at it, considered, and shook it once, briefly, as though completing a form.

"Huh," he said. "Thought you'd be a man."

"The office doesn't specify."

"No," he agreed. "I suppose it doesn't." He picked up her valise without being asked and carried it toward the gangway, which Lior understood to be either courtesy or a way of controlling where she went first. "Dockmaster'll want your papers. Then it's Front Street up to the square, and the trustees' hall if you've business with the town. Which you do, I expect."

"I do."

"Well." He set the valise down at the foot of the ramp. "Good luck to you, miss."

It was not an encouraging sentence. Lior had learned to hear the ones that weren't.

* * *

The dock smelled of tar and cold water and fish scales drying in the sun that was only now burning through the fog. October had come down the river valley like a slow tide, gilding the sycamores along the bank and putting a bite in the air that promised frost within the fortnight. Lior stood a moment at the top of the ramp with her ledger open on her forearm—the habit of ten years' service, the book held like a waiter's cloth—and wrote the date, the hour, the number of souls disembarking (one), and one line of observation:

*Town appears orderly. Clock at observatory stopped.*

Then she closed the book and went up Front Street to present herself to the trustees.

The walk took eleven minutes, which she knew because she counted her paces out of professional discipline rather than anxiety, though she was honest enough with herself to admit the two had become difficult to separate over the years. Front Street was brick below and clapboard above, shopfronts with their awnings furled, a barber's pole, a chandler's, a dry goods store whose window displayed bolts of wool arranged with more ambition than customers seemed to warrant. A dog followed her for half a block, lost interest. Two women sweeping their steps paused to watch her pass, and their pausing had weight to it—not hostility exactly, but the particular attention a small town pays to anything it cannot immediately sort into a known category. Lior was thirty-one years old, plainly dressed, carrying a leather ledger, walking alone. She could feel them sorting.

At the trustees' hall—a squat stone building wedged between the church and what appeared to be a feed store—she was received by a clerk named Mr. Pettibone, who read her credentials twice, checked the seal against a printed specimen he kept in a drawer, and then led her down a corridor to a room where four men sat around a table beneath a portrait of some deceased benefactor.

They were expecting her, but they were not pleased about it, and the distinction showed itself in every detail: the chairs pulled slightly back from the table rather than offered, the single cup of coffee poured for Mr. Pettibone alone, the silence that lasted exactly long enough to communicate something before the eldest of the men gestured her to stand rather than sit.

"Miss Ashcombe." He did not rise. He was perhaps sixty, silver-bearded, with the build of a man who had once been formidable and now administered his formidability through others. "Aldous Crane. Chairman of the board of trustees. You have our cooperation, of course. The law requires it."

"Thank you, Mr. Crane."

"But I'll be plain with you, because I find plainness saves everyone time." He laced his fingers on the table. "Harrow's Bend has had a difficult decade. We lost half our trade when the railway went through Vell instead of here. We lost our mill. We lost forty-three men to the fever year, and God rest them. What we have left, we are protective of. There are those in this town who will see a government man"—his eyes moved over her, correcting himself—"a government officer, with a book full of questions, as an intrusion. I'd ask you to be quick, and discreet, and to remember that whatever you write down becomes the town's business forever."

"I write down what the law requires, Mr. Crane. Names, ages, occupations, household composition. Nothing more."

"And you'll submit your sheets to this office when complete?"

"To the district office, as required. Copies are available to the town upon request." She said it evenly. She had learned, too, that the best defense against a man like Crane was to know the regulations better than he did, and to deliver that knowledge without apology.

Something flickered behind his eyes—reassessment, perhaps, or the recalibration of a man who had expected less resistance. "Very well. Pettibone will give you the ward maps. Boarding at the Ferryman's Rest, I assume?"

"That was my intention."

"It's clean enough." He gathered his papers, a clear dismissal. "One piece of advice, gratis. The townsfolk will answer your questions faster if you lead with the families they respect. Start at the church end of the square and work outward. And—" he paused, and for the first time his voice shifted into something almost genuine, "you'll get no argument from anyone in this town except possibly old Marta Kessel, who argues with the weather. But there's one house you may find closed to you. The clockmaker's."

Lior kept her pencil still. "There's a clockmaker in Harrow's Bend?"

"Thackeray. Edmund Thackeray." Crane said the name the way one sets down something fragile. "His father founded the workshop, made the observatory clock, God rest him too. The son keeps to himself. Doesn't attend church. Doesn't come to market—you'll notice we hold market Thursdays, and in ten years he's not once set foot in it. Does his trade by post, mostly. Repairs, fine work, they say, though none of us would know." He shrugged, a gesture meant to seem careless and achieving the opposite. "He won't welcome you. But he's a ratepayer and he sleeps in the town, so I suppose he counts."

"He counts," Lior said quietly. "Everyone counts, Mr. Crane. That's rather the entire point of the exercise."

Crane looked at her for a moment longer than the remark required. Then he nodded once, curtly, and turned back to his papers, and Pettibone walked her out with the ward maps rolled under his arm and a warning about the state of the lane behind the tannery after rain.

* * *

The Ferryman's Rest was clean enough. Her room was on the second floor, overlooking the street, with a bed, a washstand, a chair, a window that stuck, and a mirror spotted with age. Lior unpacked with methodical economy: two spare shirts, writing materials, her reference copies of the census schedules, a tin of pencils, a small tin of tea, a photograph of nobody, because there was nobody. She hung her second-best coat on the peg and stood a moment looking at the room as though assessing it for hazards, which in a sense she was. Rooms were temporary. This was the eleventh she had occupied in six years of district work, and she had stopped pretending to herself that any of them was home.

She ate supper downstairs among drovers and a pair of commercial travelers, and it was there, over a bowl of barley soup and bread that fought back, that the talk found her—or rather, she let it wash past her until a fragment caught, the way fragments do.

"—telling you, the man's a ghost. My cousin delivers brass stock up the hill twice a year and he's never once been asked inside. Takes payment through the door slot like a toll bridge."

"Ten years, though. Can't fault the work. The Vell station clock, that was his father's, but the repairs these last years—my brother-in-law swears it's kept better time than the railway's own."

"Keeps better time than anything in this town except the church bell, and that's God's business, isn't it." Laughter, brief. "Still. A man who won't show his face at market. Won't take a drink at the Anchor even at Christmas. You wonder what a person's hiding, living like that."

"Maybe nothing," said the first voice, unexpectedly. "Maybe he just likes his own company."

"Nobody likes their own company *that* much."

The conversation drifted on to the price of coal. Lior finished her soup and went upstairs, and by lamplight she opened the ledger to a fresh page and began, as she did every night of an assignment, to prepare.

This was her private liturgy, never taught, never spoken of: the night-before ritual of setting down what she knew and, more importantly, what she didn't. Names to confirm. Households to verify. Questions to phrase carefully, because a question asked bluntly in a suspicious town could close doors that took weeks to reopen. She drew the map of Harrow's Bend in miniature from the ward plans—Front Street, the square, the church, the lane behind the tannery—and marked the households in pencil, ninety-four of them by Pettibone's count, each one a small architecture of lives she would be permitted to enter for perhaps twenty minutes apiece.

And at the edge of the map, up the hill road near the observatory, she marked a single house and wrote beside it: *Thackeray, E. Clockmaker. Rumored reclusive. Verify occupation; confirm household.*

She sat looking at those words for a while. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the publican locked the front door and wished the last drover a good night, and the building settled into the kind of silence Lior had come to think of as the true national language—everyone fluent, no one discussing it.

Loneliness was not a thing she permitted herself to name. It was inefficient; it produced nothing; it could not be entered in a ledger under any column the schedule provided. But it was there tonight, the way it was there most nights in a new place, sitting across the table from her like a companion who paid half the lodging. Ten years in the service, and what did she have to show for it? Eleven rooms. Ninety-four households, soon to be ninety-five, each of which she would enter and count and leave, taking nothing but figures, leaving nothing but a knock on the door remembered for an afternoon.

*Everyone counts,* she had told Crane, and meant it, and yet there were nights when the arithmetic ran the other direction—when she felt less like a counter of people than a woman whom no one had thought to count.

She blew out the lamp before the thought could finish itself. That was the discipline. That was the whole trick of the service, if she was honest: you kept moving, kept asking, kept the pencil sharp, and the loneliness never got a word in edgewise.

* * *

Morning came hard and bright, frost on the window glass in fern patterns, and Lior began at the church end of the square, exactly as advised, because advice that cost nothing and worked was advice worth following.

The day established its rhythm quickly and she surrendered to it gratefully, because rhythm was armor. Knock. Introduce herself with the credential card held ready. Ask the household questions in the prescribed order—name of each person, relation to head, age, occupation, birthplace—and record the answers in her small, exact hand. The families of Harrow's Bend were wary for the first three minutes and voluble for the next twenty, because people were people everywhere, and once they understood she wanted facts rather than judgments, most of them gave her facts and opinions besides, unprompted and unsolicited and occasionally useful.

She learned that the Widow Harnett's lodger was officially her nephew and unofficially something else, which she recorded as *lodger* because the schedule had no column for unofficial. She learned that the tannery employed nine men and owed three of them a month's wages. She learned that the fever year had hollowed out certain houses so thoroughly that a woman named Mrs. Collier answered every question about her household composition in the present tense, describing sons who had died a decade ago, and Lior wrote down the living and left the dead alone, because the schedule had no column for that either, and because some mercies were simply the absence of further questions.

By Thursday she had covered the square and both sides of Front Street. Market day confirmed Crane's account: stalls of wool and root vegetables and one knife-grinder working a wheel, the whole population of the town compressed into two loud hours in which everyone saw everyone. Lior walked the rows with her ledger under her arm, counting stallholders, and felt the attention of the town settle on her again—not hostile, merely heavy, the collective gaze of a community deciding whether she was weather or climate.

And she noticed what Crane had promised she would notice: the absence. In a crowd where every face belonged to somebody's cousin, there was no lean figure in workman's clothes, no man slipping through with his collar up. She asked the knife-grinder, casually, whether the clockmaker ever came down for supplies.

"Thackeray?" The grinder didn't stop his wheel. "Never seen him at market in my life. Sends his orders down with payment wrapped in oilcloth. Honest money, mind." He squinted up at her, sparks drifting off the blade. "You're the census woman. You going up there?"

"It's on my list."

"Good luck to you, then." The same words the ferryman had used, and the same tone, and Lior was beginning to understand that in Harrow's Bend, *good luck* was not a pleasantry but a diagnosis.

Friday she climbed the hill road. The frost had thickened overnight and the ruts held ice in their shadows; her breath went out ahead of her like a scout reporting danger. The observatory dome caught the early sun and threw it back greenish and dull, and below it, set into the hillside with its back to the wind, stood the workshop—a low stone building with deep-set windows, a chimney breathing a thin thread of smoke, and above the door a painted sign gone soft with weather: *THACKERAY & SON, CLOCKMAKERS.* Someone had maintained the paint on the lettering even as the surrounding wood greyed, so that the sign seemed to hover, freshly inscribed, on a surface aging around it.

Lior stopped ten paces from the door and allowed herself the pause she usually denied herself—the pause where she admitted she was nervous. Not of the man. Of the pattern. Six years of doors, and she knew how this one would likely go: opened a hand's width, questions answered in clipped monosyllables, the interview conducted as an extraction, the door closing on her like water closing over a stone. She was tired, in a bone-deep, unledgered way, of being an intrusion. And yet the law required the count, and she believed in the law's reason for requiring it—that a town unseen is a town that can be forgotten, that the forgotten are always the first to be taken advantage of. Even here. Especially here.

She knocked.

For a long moment, nothing. Long enough that she raised her hand to knock again—and then heard, from somewhere inside, footsteps crossing a floor, and a voice, low and even, saying through the wood:

"If it's about the Vell clock, it ships Monday."

"It's not about the Vell clock," Lior said. "My name is Lior Ashcombe. I'm the census taker for this district."

Silence. The kind of silence that has machinery in it—she could almost hear thinking on the other side of the door, deliberate and wary, like a mechanism deciding whether to engage.

"The census," the voice said at last.

"Yes. It takes perhaps fifteen minutes. Name, age, occupation, household. It's required by law, and—" she hesitated, then abandoned the official phrasing for something plainer, "—and I'd rather do it properly than do it twice."

Another silence. Then bolts—two of them, she counted—and the door opened.

Not a hand's width. All the way, which surprised her, though the man in the doorway did not: tall, narrow-shouldered, somewhere past thirty, dressed in a leather apron over a dark shirt, with ink on his fingers and calluses she could see from where she stood, and eyes of a color she couldn't fix in the flat morning light—gray or green, deciding later. His hair was cropped short and unruly, as if cut by his own hand with scissors and a mirror and little patience. He looked at her the way she imagined a man looks at an incoming tide: measuring it, not fearing it, calculating exactly how high it would reach.

"You'd rather do it properly," Edmund Thackeray repeated. Something in his voice suggested the phrase had landed somewhere it wasn't aimed.

"It saves us both time," Lior said. "May I come in?"

He stood aside. "Fifteen minutes," he said. "I have work."

Inside, the workshop was dim and warm and smelled of oil and brass and cedar shavings, and every surface—this she registered in the first three seconds, with the involuntary precision of her profession—every surface was in order. Tools racked by size. Drawers labeled in a small severe hand. On the bench under the window, disassembled into a constellation of wheels and springs, lay the heart of some clock, laid out as neatly as surgery.

Edmund Thackeray pulled out a stool, sat, folded his ink-stained hands, and looked at her with an expression of perfect, guarded courtesy—the expression, Lior thought, of a man answering questions he had spent ten years preparing for.

"Ask what you need to ask," he said. "I'll answer."
