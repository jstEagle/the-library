## 2. A Question of Households

The Thackeray workshop stood at the end of Cooper's Lane like a sentence left unfinished, its shutters the gray of old pewter, its sign—*E. THACKERAY, CLOCKMAKER & REPAIRER*—swung on a bracket so rusted that Lior half expected it to drop while she watched. She had walked past it twice already that morning, once by accident and once with intent, telling herself she was simply ordering her route through the town's eastern quarter. The truth was less orderly. She had been circling.

Census work rewarded patience, her supervisor in the district office had told her, and punished hesitation. *Get the answers, write them clean, move along.* Six years she had followed that instruction through mill towns and market villages, through kitchens that smelled of tallow and parlors that smelled of camphor, filling columns with names and ages and occupations until the pages looked less like lives than arithmetic. She was good at it. That was the trouble with being good at something lonely: you did more of it.

She climbed the three steps to the workshop door and knocked—a census knock, two raps, official without apology.

From inside came a sound she felt before she understood it: a ticking. Not one clock but many, out of phase with one another, so that the whole building seemed to breathe in small irregular pulses. Then footsteps, measured, and the door opened.

The man who stood there was younger than she expected and harder to look at than she liked to admit. Not handsome in any way the word usually meant—too sharp at the jaw, too pale from years indoors—but composed the way instruments are composed, all lines drawn to a purpose. He wore a leather apron over shirtsleeves rolled to the elbow, and his eyes went first to the ledger under Lior's arm, then to her face, then back to the ledger, cataloguing her the way she supposed he'd catalogue a broken movement brought in for repair.

"Mr. Ashcombe," he said. "The census man."

"You know me?"

"The ferryman's daughter mentioned a government fellow asking questions up and down Front Street." He stepped back from the doorway—not warmth, exactly, but the absence of refusal. "You'd better come in. The light's better."

It was true. Inside, the workshop glowed. Late September sun came through windows grimed with brass polish and fell across a long bench crowded with tools laid out in ranks so precise they might have been counted—and then, when Lior's eye traveled the wall behind the bench, she saw that they had been. Every plane, every file, every burnisher hung on an outline painted to its own shape, and where a tool was missing from its outline there was a small paper tag with a date written on it, as though even absence had been entered into evidence.

Clocks covered everything else. Shelf clocks, wall clocks, carriage clocks, movements stripped to their skeletons and suspended in frames like anatomical diagrams. And beneath all of them, layered and slightly out of step, the ticking she had heard through the door—dozens of hearts, none of them agreeing on the hour.

"Sit anywhere that isn't a vise," said Edmund Thackeray.

Lior sat on a stool by the door and opened her ledger, squaring it on her knee. "This won't take long. A dozen questions, most of them dull."

"Dull questions are the honest kind." He took up his place at the bench but did not resume whatever he had been working at; instead he turned a small brass wheel between his fingers, around and around, a fidget or a habit or a way of keeping his hands occupied while his attention stayed elsewhere. "Go on."

"Name?"

"Edmund Thackeray."

"Age?"

"Thirty-one."

"Occupation?"

"Clockmaker. Journeyman, though I've not taken the guild examination. Repairer, if we're being precise about what pays the rent."

Lior wrote. *Precise.* That was the word this man kept choosing for himself, and she noticed that precision, offered unprompted, often meant the same thing as a locked drawer: something worth protecting inside it. She had learned to hear locks in people's speech the way sailors hear weather.

"How long have you lived at this address?"

"All my life. My father held the shop before me. I was born in the room above it."

"And your father? He's passed on, I take it—the ferryman said the shop changed hands some years back."

"He died ten years ago this winter. The workshop passed to me under his will." The brass wheel stopped turning. Started again. "There were no other heirs."

"No mother living?"

"She died when I was small. I've her eyes, my father used to say, though I confess I don't remember them well enough to say whose they look like."

Lior wrote *widower's child, sole heir,* and found the phrase sat oddly on the page. There was something in the way he spoke of his parents—fluent, rehearsed almost, each answer arriving fully formed like a piece machined to tolerance. She had interviewed hundreds of people by now, and most of them answered badly. They guessed at dates, argued with their spouses about ages, invented occupations to seem grander than they were. Human imprecision was the tax every census taker paid.

Edmund Thackeray paid no such tax. Every figure arrived correct to the digit, and the correctness itself began, after a while, to feel like a kind of noise.

"Are you married?" she asked.

"No."

"Widowed?"

"No."

She let the pause sit for a moment longer than the question required. He met it without flinching, turning the little wheel, watching her with those pale level eyes, and Lior understood that he would answer anything she asked so long as she asked it directly—that indirection was the only thing he couldn't abide, perhaps because indirection was the one instrument missing from his bench.

She moved down her columns. Place of birth—he gave the parish, the county, the year, unhesitating. Religious denomination—"None," and then, catching something in her face, "I keep the Sabbath in my own way. Clocks don't observe holidays, and neither does whoever winds them." Whether he could read and write—"Yes." Whether he was deaf, dumb, blind, or lunatic, idiotic, imbecile, or feeble-minded—here he almost smiled, the first crack in the surface, dry as a hairline fracture in porcelain.

"That question gets asked of every man who works alone too long," he said. "Solitude is the national suspicion."

"It's the column, not me."

"I know. I'm not offended. Write 'none of these,' and underline it if it pleases you."

She underlined it, and found she was smiling too, and stopped, and wrote the next question before the smile could mean anything.

Number of rooms in the dwelling. Number of families. Whether any member of the household was sick or disabled. Whether any children between five and sixteen were absent from school. He answered them all, each one placed like a gear into its seat, and the interview ran so smoothly that Lior caught herself relaxing, which she never did on duty, because relaxed census takers made mistakes.

Then she came to the last line of the schedule, and read it aloud in the flat voice she used for all of them:

"And is there anyone else—any other person—who sleeps in this house?"

The ticking filled the room.

It had been there all along, she realized; she had simply stopped hearing it the way one stops hearing rain. But now, in the silence after her question, every clock in the workshop seemed to speak at once, dozens of small voices disagreeing, and Edmund Thackeray's hand had gone still on the brass wheel.

He did not answer.

Three seconds. Five. Lior had conducted enough interviews to know what a pause meant, and this was not the pause of a man counting children or recalling a lodger's name. This was the pause of a man standing at a door he had not expected anyone to knock upon. She watched his thumb press the wheel flat against the bench, hard enough that she wondered it didn't bend.

"Anyone else," he repeated, as if tasting the words for spoilage.

"Who sleeps here," Lior said. "Family, lodgers, servants, apprentices. It's only for the count. It doesn't signify anything beyond the number."

"Nothing signifies anything beyond the number," he said quietly. "That's rather the point of numbers."

And then he took up the pen that lay beside the ledger's edge—a pen she hadn't offered him—and wrote, in a small severe hand, a single character on the schedule's final line.

*No.*

He wrote it without looking up. That was the detail Lior carried away afterward, the one that would not smooth itself out no matter how she tried: he did not look at her, did not look at the line, did not look at anything except the grain of the bench beneath the pen nib, as though the answer existed somewhere in the wood and he was only copying it out. His hand did not shake. It should have shaken, she thought irrationally; a lie that steady was worse than a trembling one.

"There," he said, and set the pen down with the care of a man returning a borrowed instrument. "Is that everything?"

Lior looked at the page. One word, written in another man's hand on an official document, which was itself against every instruction she had ever been given—*entries in the enumerator's hand only*—and she found she lacked the will to strike it out and ask again. Perhaps because striking it out would require a second asking, and she had seen what the first asking cost him. Or perhaps, said a colder voice in her head, because she wanted to see whether he would spend the same coin twice.

"That's everything," she said.

He stood, and the interview was over the way a clock is finished—abruptly, all the running parts stopping at once. He saw her to the door with a courtesy that had reassembled itself so completely she might have imagined the pause entirely, except that her ledger weighed heavier going out than coming in, the way documents do when they hold more than their paper can carry.

At the threshold she paused. It was against her practice to editorialize; she had learned that commentary invited conversation, and conversation invited error. But the lane was empty, the morning was bright, and something in her—something she did not examine closely—wanted to leave the door open behind her rather than close it.

"The clocks," she said. "They're all ticking at different times. Doesn't that bother you?"

Edmund Thackeray considered her for a moment, and this time the look lasted long enough that she saw something move underneath it—surprise, maybe, or its cousin.

"A stopped clock tells the truth twice a day," he said. "A wrong one never tells it at all, but it never stops trying. I find I prefer things that keep trying."

He closed the door, gently, and the latch clicked home with a sound like a very small verdict.

* * *

She did not go straight back to Front Street. Her route sheet said the eastern quarter next anyway, six households between Cooper's Lane and the tannery, and she told herself the detour was economy, not avoidance. But her steps slowed past the cooperage, and by the time she reached the widow Halloran's gate she was still turning the interview over, the way one turns a stone to see what's sheltering underneath.

The widow Halloran gave her tea and contradictory information about her son's age and talked for forty minutes, and Lior wrote it all down gratefully, because imprecision suddenly felt like honesty and precision felt like a locked room. *Everyone else in this town answers wrong in the ordinary way,* she thought. *Everyone else lets the mess show.*

At the bottom of the Halloran schedule, in the space for remarks, her pen hovered. She wrote nothing. Remarks were optional. But that evening, back in her rented room above the chandler's, with the ferry lights wobbling across the river and her completed sheets spread out for review, she found herself reading the Thackeray entry again. All of it. Line by line.

*Edmund Thackeray. Age 31. Clockmaker. Lives alone.*

Her own handwriting, except for one word. She held the page to the lamp and studied the single character at the bottom—*No*—in that small severe script, and asked herself the question she should have asked in the workshop, out loud, where it could be answered or refused properly:

Why would a man who answered thirty questions like a machine hesitate over the one that mattered least?

The number of sleepers in a house. No inheritance turned on it, no pension, no law. It was the emptiest column on the page, and it had been the only one that cost him anything.

Unless it wasn't empty. Unless the question had landed somewhere she couldn't see, on something the house held besides furniture and clocks—some sleeper she'd been told didn't exist, or some absence dressed up as presence, or some third thing she had no category for yet. Lior knew how towns worked. She knew that a man who lived alone above his dead father's shop, who never attended church, who answered questions like a confession in reverse, would accumulate stories whether or not there was anything to tell. The ferryman's daughter already had one: *the clockmaker who talks to the machines.*

She squared the sheets, tied them with the official string, and set the Thackeray page on top of the stack where she'd have to move it first thing tomorrow. Rules were rules. Entries in the enumerator's hand only. She would come back, she decided—there was a clerical matter anyway, a duplicated entry she could legitimately claim required correction—and she would ask the question again, plainly, and watch his hands this time instead of his face.

Because faces could be trained. She'd proven that herself, six years of knocking on doors with a neutral expression nailed on. But hands told on everyone. Hands had held that pen steady as a spirit level while writing a word that cost him something, and Lior Ashcombe, keeper of numbers, had discovered that she could not stop wondering what number he was protecting.

Outside, the river kept its own time, and somewhere down Cooper's Lane, behind a shuttered window, dozens of clocks ticked on—out of step, disagreeing, refusing to stop trying.
