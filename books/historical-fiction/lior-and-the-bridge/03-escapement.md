## 3. Escapement

The escapement was lying to her.

Edmund Thackeray knew it the way she knew rain coming off the river—by a pressure in the air, by the wrongness of a sound that had been right for years. She stood on the scaffold platform inside the observatory tower with her ear an inch from the master clock's case, listening. The tick had gone thin. Somewhere in the train of wheels above her head, a tooth had chipped or a pivot had begun to wear its bushing oval, and the escape wheel was releasing its seconds unevenly—a hair of hesitation before each beat, so small no one in town would ever notice.

She would notice. She would hear it every day until it was mended, and the hearing itself would be a kind of itch under her skin, and so there was nothing to do but climb down, fetch the lamp and the tools, and begin.

The observatory smelled of cold stone and machine oil and pigeon droppings from the belfry above, where the birds came in through louvres no one had repaired since before the war. It was October, and the tower held the chill of the river like a cupped hand holds water. Edmund lit the lamp, hung it on its hook, and opened the clock's trunk door wide enough to work. The movement emerged from the dark by degrees as the lamplight reached into it: the great barrel, the going train, the pendulum hanging dead still because she had stopped the clock at noon to keep it from running crooked while she diagnosed.

A stopped master clock was a small scandal in a town like Harrow's Bend. The dial faced the square; half the town set its watches by it, grumbling, and the other half set its grievances by it, which amounted to the same discipline. She had three days before the trustees noticed and sent someone up the hill to ask why. Three days. She flexed her hands—scarred knuckles, the left forefinger permanently nicked where a wheel had bitten her at fourteen—and began.

* * *

Her father had taught her to listen to mechanisms before he taught her anything else about them. Not look—listen. *Eyes can be fooled,* he'd said, his beard against her ear as they both leaned over a customer's mantel clock gone wrong. *Ears can't. A mechanism tells you what ails it if you shut your mouth long enough to hear.*

She had been eight. She could still feel the warmth of him behind her, the smell of tobacco and brass filings, the astonishing safety of being small next to someone who knew how everything worked.

*Keep the name, lose the self.*

He had said it eleven years ago, in this same workshop, though not in these words—not then. In those last weeks he had said ordinary things: *mind the glue pot, don't let it boil*; *the Hollis account is paid through Christmas*; *you have your mother's patience, thank God, because you have my eyes and neither of us ever learned to wait.* It was only at the end, when the coughing had eaten him down to a voice like paper tearing, that he had taken her hand—the hand already roughened by ten years of apprenticeship, because he had never once made a distinction between what a son's hands did and what a daughter's might—and pressed something into it. The indenture papers. His own apprenticeship indenture, signed when he was twelve, folded soft as cloth from handling.

"You'll take the shop," he'd said. It was not a question. He had no son. He had her, and he had spent her whole childhood treating that fact as a fact and not a tragedy, and now the ledger had come due.

"Father—" she'd started, meaning *I'm here*, meaning *don't*, meaning a hundred things a daughter says.

"Listen." And she had listened, because that was the first thing he ever taught her. "The name stays. Thackeray keeps the bench, keeps the guild standing, keeps the customers. Understand me? The name is the tool. Tools don't feel. You do the feeling somewhere else, somewhere deep, and you lock it away, and you let the name do the work." His grip had been terrible in its weakness, a bird's bones wrapped around hers. "I'm sorry for it. I'll be sorry past the grave. But the world we're given isn't the world we'd have built, and I won't have them take the bench from you because God gave you the wrong shape."

He had died two days later with her hand still in his, and she had done exactly as he instructed. She had buried Elias Thackeray, told the town her father's last wish was that his apprentice carry on—which was true, if you understood that the apprentice was herself, indentured in everything but ink—and she had put on his spare coat, cut her hair with the shop shears, and lowered her voice by degrees over six months until speaking felt like operating a machine she'd built inside her own throat.

Ten years. The town knew Edmund Thackeray as a quiet man, competent, solitary, indifferent to church and market both. The town did not know him well enough to know anything else, because she had made sure of it. Solitude wasn't loneliness. Solitude was armor, fitted plate by plate, and she had worn it so long she sometimes forgot it weighed anything at all.

Sometimes. Standing at the open clock case with the lamp throwing her shadow huge across the tower wall—a shadow with broad shoulders and a man's stance, because she had taught even her shadow to lie—she remembered the weight perfectly.

* * *

The fault, when she found it, was in the third wheel's arbor: the pivot had worn a shoulder, letting the wheel drift a hair sideways, and the drift was stealing a fraction of drop from the pallets. Not broken. Worn. That was worse. Broken things announced themselves; worn things lied politely for years and then failed all at once, usually at the worst hour.

She sat back on her heels and considered it. Proper repair meant making a new pivot—turning down the old steel, fitting fresh, polishing to a mirror finish so the oil would stay where oil belonged. Two days' work if she didn't rush it. She didn't rush. Rushing was how men ruined clocks and reputations alike, and Edmund Thackeray's reputation was the only part of him that had never once had to lie.

Her father had kept a drawer of failures. That had been another lesson, unspoken until she found the drawer at nine and he caught her holding a gear train fused together by solder poured in panic. Most fathers would have snatched it away. Elias had sat down beside her instead and gone through it piece by piece: *this one, I overheated the bluing and the spring lost its temper; this one, I forced a fit that wanted filing; this one, I was angry at a customer and let it get into the work.* Every failure labeled in his small precise hand, dated, annotated. *A craftsman who hides his mistakes will repeat them. A craftsman who records them owns them.*

She had thought of that drawer often in the past ten years, and wondered what her father would have written on a label for her. *This one: I asked my child to disappear.* Would he have owned it? She believed he would have. Believing it was the only thing that kept the instruction from curdling into cruelty. *Keep the name, lose the self*—but he had also taught her that mistakes recorded were mistakes owned, and somewhere in the arithmetic of his dying there must have been a column where the cost was written out honestly, even if he never spoke it aloud.

She wished—she caught the wish and crushed it, the way she crushed all wishes larger than a Tuesday—she wished he had spoken it aloud. Once. Just once, *my daughter*, so that the words existed somewhere outside her skull, so that on bad nights she could tell herself she hadn't invented the tenderness, hadn't embroidered a deathbed into absolution.

Bad nights were frequent lately. The census man had come Tuesday.

Lior Ashcombe. She turned the name over the way she'd turn a strange part found loose in a case—examining it from all sides, looking for the mark of its maker. A government man, young-ish, with ink-stained fingers and a way of asking questions that sounded like courtesy and landed like probes. *How many persons reside at this address? Does anyone else sleep in the house?* She had answered everything precisely, because precision was her native tongue, and then the last question had come and she had written *no* without looking up, and the silence after it had lasted one beat too long—one beat, in a life measured in beats, and she had heard it the moment it escaped her, the way she heard a chipped tooth in a wheel.

Had he heard it too? He had a listener's face. That was the trouble with him—he didn't talk the way officials talked, filling space with their own importance. He waited. Waiting men were dangerous. A waiting man gave you room to hang yourself.

She shook it off and went back to the lathe. There was comfort in the work, real comfort, the kind she trusted. Steel doesn't care what name you give it. Brass doesn't ask whether the hands turning it are a man's or a woman's. A file cuts true or it doesn't. For ten years, every honest sentence she had spoken had been spoken to metal, and metal had kept her secrets better than any confessor, because metal cannot repeat what it hears—it can only show the marks your tools leave, and those marks say only *skilled* or *careless*, never *woman*.

She mounted the wheel arbor in the lathe and set the graver. Outside, dusk came down over the river valley, and somewhere below a dog barked at the ferry whistle, and the tower grew dark around her single lamp until it felt less like a building than a lantern floating in black water.

* * *

By nine she had the new pivot turned and was polishing it with progressively finer emery, breathing through her mouth to keep grit off the work, when the memory arrived—the one she kept locked deepest, because it was the only one that hurt more than it helped.

She had been seventeen. Her courses had come late and hard that autumn, and she had doubled over at the bench with cramps so violent she couldn't hold a file, and her father had looked up from across the room and known instantly—not from anything she said, because she said nothing, ever, about such things—but from the way she stood, the way any craftsman knows a warped board by sight alone.

He had sent her home—home being the rooms above the shop, thirty feet of stairs—and an hour later climbed up himself with a hot brick wrapped in flannel and a cup of willow bark tea, and set them down without ceremony, saying only, "Your mother used to swear by the brick. Said tea was superstition but drank it anyway." Then he had stood there a moment, big and awkward in the doorway of her narrow room, and what he finally said was: "It's not fair, Nell. None of the arrangement is fair. But unfair isn't the same as impossible. Fair is a country nobody lives in. We live here." And he tapped the floorboards twice with his boot, meaning the shop below, meaning the world, meaning *this is where we are and here we make do.*

Nell. He had called her Nell that once and never again—had caught himself, had visibly closed the door on the name—but she had kept it, hidden in the strongbox of her chest alongside everything else he'd locked away for safekeeping. Eleanor. Her mother's name, her grandmother's, a chain of women reaching back past memory. When the cramps passed and she went down to the bench the next morning, she was Edmund again, and Elias greeted her with a job list and no glance wasted on anything but the work, and that was the mercy of it, and that was the wound of it, both at once, forever.

She blew a curl of emery dust from the pivot and held it to the lamp. The polish was good. Near-mirror. Her reflection floated in the curved steel, tiny and distorted, a pale smear with dark eyes—no more recognizable than the shadow on the wall, and just as carefully constructed.

* * *

Midnight came and went. She reassembled the train by lamplight, each wheel back in its place, bridges seated, screws snug, and then came the moment every repairer lived for and dreaded equally: the test. She weighted the pendulum, gave the barrel three careful turns, and released the escapement.

*Tick.*

She bent close. *Tick.* Listened through twenty beats, fifty, a hundred, counting against the pulse in her wrist, hunting the hesitation. At beat sixty-three she heard it—or imagined she did—and closed her eyes and listened harder, the way her father had taught her, shutting her mouth, shutting her mind, becoming nothing but an ear.

Even. Or near enough that only she would ever know. Near enough that the town below could go on setting its watches and its grudges by a dial that told the truth, whatever else in Harrow's Bend did not.

She allowed herself the smallest exhale, the one that was almost a laugh, the one that in another life might have been accompanied by someone else's voice saying *there, you've done it again*—and stopped that thought at the gate, as trained. Ten years of practice. The gate held.

But tonight, oddly, the gate strained. Tonight the tower felt less like a fortress and more like a cell with excellent joinery, and she sat back on the scaffold planks with her arms around her knees and admitted, in the privacy of her own skull, where admission cost nothing: *I am tired.*

Not of the work. Never the work. Tired of the doubling. Tired of translating herself daily into a language with no word for what she was, tired of the binder beneath her shirt pressing ribs flat, tired of pitching her voice down into that gravel register by morning and letting it creep back up by midnight when no one could hear. Tired of the arithmetic—every conversation a sum, every acquaintance a variable, every single day computing survival to two decimal places without error, because error meant ruin, ruin meant the bench taken, the craft taken, the last true thing taken.

Her father had bought her ten years with his dying instruction. She wondered, watching the pendulum swing in the lamplight—steady, metronomic, indifferent—how many more years the coin would buy. She wondered what happened to a person who locked everything away so deep that eventually even she couldn't find the key. She wondered whether the key still existed, or whether she had, in some sense she refused to examine closely, filed it down and melted it into the works of the clock, where it told time honestly forever and never spoke a human word again.

The lamp guttered. Below in the dark, the river moved, carrying its boats and its secrets to towns downstream that would never know either.

And then—because the mind is a mechanism too, and runs its trains whether you wind them or not—her thoughts went, traitorously, to Tuesday. To the census man's patient face. To the question *does anyone else sleep in the house*, and her pen scratching *no*, and the silence afterward, one beat too long, hanging in the workshop air between them like a wheel suspended off its pivot.

He was coming back. She had seen it in his face before he left—not suspicion, worse than suspicion, interest. Men like that returned. Records bred errors and errors bred revisits; she knew how bureaucracy worked, its escapements and its trains, its slow inevitable advance. Some part of her that should have braced merely sat very still, the way she sat before a delicate repair, hands clean, breath shallow, waiting to see whether the mechanism before her would prove honest or false.

Three days until the trustees noticed the stopped clock. Sooner, perhaps, until the census man climbed the hill again.

Edmund Thackeray trimmed the lamp, packed her tools with the ritual care of ten years' habit, and pulled the trunk door nearly shut—nearly, because a clockmaker never seals a movement fully until she has watched it run a full day. Through the gap, the pendulum swung on, keeping the only honest time in Harrow's Bend.

She descended the tower stairs into the dark, one hand on the wall, listening to the tick follow her down like a pulse that wasn't hers.
