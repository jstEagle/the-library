## 4. The Second Visit

The error announced itself on a Tuesday, in Lior's own handwriting.

She had spread the week's census sheets across the desk in the surveyor's office she'd been lent—a room that smelled of lamp oil and other men's tobacco—and was checking each entry against her field notebook, as method required, when she found it. The Thackeray household, line fourteen: *Occupation of head of house.* In the notebook she had written *clockmaker* and beneath it *apprentice to late E. Thackeray (father), workshop on Mill Lane.* On the official sheet, transcribed at night by lamplight, she had written only *clockmaker.*

It should not have mattered. The occupation was correct; the relationship to the trade's deceased master was supplementary detail, useful but not required. Any sensible clerk would let it pass.

But Lior Ashcombe did not survive on the goodwill of sensible clerks. She survived on the certainty that her sheets could withstand any scrutiny whatsoever—the magistrate's eye, the auditor's pen, the slow bureaucratic machinery that ground through counties like a millstone through grain. A sheet with an inconsistency between source and record was a sheet that invited questions, and questions were the one currency she could not afford. The town already watched her with narrowed eyes; she'd felt it at the market, at the ferry landing, in the way conversation died when she entered the tavern's common room. An outsider with a government satchel was suspicion enough without giving anyone cause to doubt her diligence.

So: she would have to go back to Mill Lane.

She told herself the flutter beneath her ribs—quick, birdlike, unwelcome—was nothing but dread of another rebuff. The clockmaker had answered her questions precisely, correctly, and with the warmth of a ledger page. There had been no rudeness in it, which somehow made the memory worse. Rudeness she knew how to answer. That flat, exact courtesy had left her feeling like a door knocker on a door with no one behind it.

*You are going back for a transcription error,* she reminded herself, buttoning her coat against the river wind. *Nothing more.*

* * *

Mill Lane climbed away from the water in a series of crooked steps worn smooth by a century of feet. The workshop stood at its top, wedged between a shuttered cooperage and a wall of ivy gone rust-red with autumn, its windows glowing faintly gold even in daylight—the light of lamps kept burning over work. A wooden sign above the door read **E. THACKERAY, CLOCKMAKER** in letters that had been repainted recently enough to shame the peeling paint around them. Beneath the name, smaller: *Repairs. Regulators. Observatory work undertaken.*

Lior knocked, and the sound went into the building and kept going, swallowed whole.

For a long moment there was nothing. Then footsteps—not hurried, measured, as though whoever walked had counted out the distance from bench to door so often the count had become habit—and the door opened.

Edmund Thackeray looked down at her the way one looks at weather: assessing, mildly inconvenienced, prepared to endure it. Up close, in the slanting October light, Lior saw details the first visit hadn't given her. The apprentice clockmaker was younger than she'd sounded—young enough that the ten years since old Thackeray's death must have begun in boyhood. Dark hair cropped close and practical. A jaw shadowed with the particular roughness of a razor applied hastily, or perhaps applied by someone who resented every stroke of it. And eyes the color of strong tea, watchful, set deep under brows drawn together now in something that wasn't quite a frown.

"Miss Ashcombe." Not a question. Of course he remembered her name; he'd remember the number of her questions too, probably, and the order she'd asked them in.

"I'm sorry to trouble you again." She held up the sheets. "I've found a discrepancy in my transcription. It requires correction while the record is still open."

Something moved behind those tea-colored eyes—Lior couldn't have said what. "A discrepancy."

"Your occupation is listed, but not your apprenticeship under your father. It's supplementary detail, but I keep my records consistent. If you'd confirm the dates—I have them as 1883 until his death, but I'd rather hear it from you than trust my own notes."

For three full seconds Edmund Thackeray said nothing, and Lior had the strange sensation of watching someone perform arithmetic very fast behind a calm face. Then: "That's right. Eighteen eighty-three. Come in, if you're coming in. The draft off the lane gets into everything."

It was not hospitality. Lior recognized that immediately—it was the same instinct that made a farmer bring in the harvest before frost, a matter of protecting what lay within. Still, she stepped over the threshold into warmth and lamplight and the sound of ticking.

* * *

She had noticed the ticking on her first visit and told herself she'd stop hearing it after a minute. She hadn't stopped. The workshop's front room was lined with clocks—carriage clocks on shelves, a tall-case regulator standing sentry in the corner, movements in various states of undress laid out on baize like surgical patients—and none of them agreed. Each ticked at its own tempo, dozens of small stubborn hearts keeping separate time, so that the room didn't tick so much as murmur, a congregation of voices all speaking at once about the same subject.

"Sit anywhere that isn't covered in brass filings," Edmund said, already moving toward the back, where a kettle sat on a iron plate above a low fire. "I'll make tea. You'll want to say I needn't, and I'll say I'm making it anyway, and then we can skip to the part where you drink it."

Lior blinked. It was the longest speech she'd heard from him, and it had almost been—what, wit? She sat carefully on the one chair that appeared free of debris, arranging her skirts and her satchel, and watched the clockmaker's back at the fire.

There was something in the set of those shoulders she kept returning to. Narrower than they first appeared. The waistcoat hung loose on a frame that seemed built to be overlooked—everything about Edmund Thackeray was built to be overlooked, Lior thought suddenly. The plain clothes. The cropped hair. The voice pitched in a register that gave nothing away, neither youth nor age nor anything else. Even the way he stood, slightly angled away, presenting the smallest possible surface to the world.

She put the thought down the way one puts down a hot tool. It was none of her business. People arranged themselves however they needed to; heaven knew she did, with her spectacles and her brisk handwriting and her air of belonging exactly where she was sent.

The tea came in two cups that didn't match, one chipped. Edmund set hers down with a precision that bordered on ceremony and took the stool opposite, elbows on knees, hands wrapped around his own cup.

"The dates," Lior said, uncapping her pen. "You began at thirteen?"

"At thirteen, yes. My father believed a child's hands learned faster than an adult's. He may have been flattering himself about the quality of instruction available." A sip of tea. "Eighty-three to ninety-one. Seven years, plus the four since. Call it eleven."

"You use the word *call it,*" Lior observed, writing. "Most people would say *eleven years exactly.*"

"Exactly is a word for finished things. Nothing in this shop is finished."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere deeper in the building, a larger clock struck the quarter hour with a sound like a coin dropped into a well.

"It must be strange," Lior said before she could decide not to, "keeping your father's shop under your own name. Working beside his tools. His marks will be on everything."

"They are." For a moment the cup paused halfway to Edmund's mouth. "Every file has his grip worn into the handle. Every bench dog has his knife cuts. You learn to work around a dead man's habits. Or—" the cup completed its journey, "—you learn to make them yours. I haven't decided which."

Lior wrote the date, closed the notebook, and ought to have left. The correction was made; obligation discharged; the door eight steps away. Instead she heard herself say, "May I ask you something that isn't for the census?"

"That depends entirely on whether you intend to write it down."

"No."

Edmund studied her over the rim of the cup, and Lior had the uncomfortable feeling of being the one assessed, weighed, entered into some private ledger whose columns she could not see. "Then ask."

"You never attend church. You don't trade at the Saturday market. Half the town thinks you're either a hermit or hiding from debtors, and the other half won't say what they think at all. Why does a man live like that?"

The silence that followed had texture. Lior felt it press against her like deep water. She'd asked bluntly—blunter than her training allowed—but something about this place, this person, made bluntness feel like the only honest instrument available, and honesty, she was beginning to suspect, was the one thing Mill Lane traded in.

"Records," Edmund said at last. "You believe records tell truth. It's your profession. So tell me—does a record tell the truth about a man?"

Lior set down her cup. "A record tells the truth about facts. Name, trade, household. Whether a man attends church isn't a fact I collect."

"But it's a fact the town collects. They keep their own ledgers here—attendance, tithes, who bought what at market, who nodded to whom. And a man who appears in none of them becomes a blank space, and blank spaces frighten people. They fill them in." The tea-brown eyes lifted. "I keep my blanks deliberately. A man who gives the town nothing to write has given them nothing false. I'd rather be an empty page than a wrong one."

"That's not living," Lior said quietly. "That's hiding in plain sight."

"And what is a census," Edmund returned, "but hiding in plain sight? You walk through towns collecting the shape of everyone's life and leaving nothing of your own. Do they know where you sleep, Miss Ashcombe? What you left behind to take a post nobody wanted, in a river town nobody wanted you in? You know my blanks. I know yours. We're both empty pages walking."

The words landed harder than they should have. Lior felt them sink past the professional armor she wore like her coat, into whatever was underneath—which was thinner than she liked to admit. Three postings in four years. A sister's letters growing shorter. A father who still signed his notes *for my son the clerk* because he'd never reconciled himself to *daughter* meaning anything different.

"I collect facts," she said, and heard how thin it came out. "Facts are honest. Facts don't care whether we're comfortable."

"No," Edmund agreed softly. "They don't. That's the trouble with them."

And then Lior made her mistake—or her discovery; she was never afterward sure which. Edmund reached to set his cup on the bench, turning it to align the handle with the edge with a small compulsive exactness, and his sleeve fell back from his wrist, and his hand came fully into the lamplight.

She had seen workingmen's hands all her life. These were workingman's hands beyond argument: calluses mapped along the palm in the specific geography of files and lathes, a white scar crossing the base of the thumb, nails cut short and stained at the edges with the gray-black of metal polish. But it wasn't the labor that caught her. It was what the labor sat upon. The fingers were long—longer than any man's she'd met at the benches, tapered, fine-boned at the tips, the kind of fingers that belonged on a pianist or a surgeon. The wrist above them was slender, the tendons delicate under the skin, and the skin itself—

She looked up. Too slowly. Edmund was watching her watch, and the expression on that guarded face was not anger. It was something far worse: a kind of weary recognition, the look of someone who has seen this exact moment coming for years and is almost relieved it finally arrived, and almost grieving, and determined to survive both.

"Files wear the hands down," Edmund said evenly. "Ten hours a day. You end up with a worker's hands and a fiddler's fingers, and neither quite fits."

"Yes," Lior said. Her heart was behaving strangely, a quick uneven tick she suspected no clock in the room shared. "Yes, of course."

Neither of them believed it. Both of them let it stand.

* * *

She gathered her things with hands that felt borrowed. At the door she paused, the afternoon cold waiting outside like a creditor.

"Thank you for the tea. And the correction."

"The record's welcome." Edmund stood in the doorway behind her, half in lamplight, half in the gray of the lane. "Miss Ashcombe. Your sheets—they'll be filed with the county office?"

"In due course. Copies to the registrar, originals retained per the Act."

"Retained where?"

"Why?" The question came out sharper than intended. "Worried about your blanks?"

Something flickered across Edmund's face—there and gone, quick as a escapement slipping a tooth. "Worried about accuracy," he said. "It's a professional interest of mine. Things that are supposed to keep true records and don't."

He closed the door with a click as precise as any mechanism, and Lior descended the crooked steps into the wind with her notebook against her ribs and one thought circling her mind like a fly she couldn't wave off:

*I have seen those hands before. Not those hands—that shape. That shape of hand, on a woman I once knew, and I looked too long, and he let me look, and neither of us said a word.*

Behind her, high on the hill above the town, the observatory dome caught the last of the light, and inside it the great master clock waited, silent, broken, keeping no time at all.
