## 5. What the Clock Keeps

The knock came at half past four on a Thursday, and Edmund knew before she opened the door that it would be the census woman.

She had learned the sound of Lior Ashcombe's footsteps in the week since the second visit—a particular rhythm, brisk but not hurried, the walk of someone who counted things for a living and could not help counting her own steps too. Most people in Marlow Bend walked like they were trying to get somewhere without being seen arriving. Lior walked like she was arriving whether anyone liked it or not.

Edmund opened the door with the excuse already built. "The observatory," she said, before Lior could speak. "You wanted to see how the work proceeds. For your records."

Lior blinked. Her satchel hung from one shoulder, and behind her the October light came down the street at a long angle, gilding the puddles left by last night's rain. "Did I say I wanted—"

"You wrote it down." Edmund stepped back to let her in. "Last week. You asked if I'd permit an inspection of progress, since the observatory is town property and the trustees have been asking." She had not written it down. Edmund had invented it at two in the morning, lying awake and turning over the memory of tea gone cold between them, of hands nearly touching over a ledger page, of the way Lior had looked at her fingers as though reading something written there she couldn't quite decipher. Invention was safer than invitation. Invention gave them both somewhere respectable to stand.

"Did they?" Lior said. "Ask?"

"They ask about everything." It was true enough. Crane had sent his man twice that month to stand across the street and copy down who came and went, which was no one, which was precisely the point.

Lior hesitated on the threshold the way she always did, as if entering were a formality requiring signature. Then she came inside, and Edmund closed the door on the light.

* * *

The path up Observatory Hill was mud and gravel, and they climbed it mostly in silence because the climb took breath. Edmund carried the tool roll; Lior carried nothing but her questions, which Edmund could feel accumulating like water behind a dam.

At the tower door, Edmund fitted the key her father had cut for her ten years ago—the only key, kept on a cord beneath her shirt where no one saw it—and let them both into the stairwell. The air changed immediately: colder, older, smelling of stone dust and machine oil and, faintly, of brass, which has a smell the way rain does, imperceptible until you know it.

"Forty-one steps," Edmund said, because saying so filled the silence. "The clock room is above the telescope chamber. My father designed the mounting so the vibration of the mechanism wouldn't travel down into the instruments."

"Your father built all this?"

"The original train, yes, thirty years ago. The rest is repair upon repair." She climbed. "Towns think a clock is finished when it's installed. A clock is never finished. That's what most people don't understand about them. They're not objects. They're arguments the maker is having with time, and time always gets the last word unless you keep arguing."

Behind her, the scratch of a pencil. Lior was writing it down. Edmund felt something turn over in her chest—not displeasure. Something nearer to alarm, though she couldn't have said why being recorded should frighten her more than being watched. Being watched she was practiced at.

* * *

The clock room occupied the top of the tower like a heart in a chest of stone. The master clock stood against the north wall, seven feet of oak case opened wide, its movement exposed to the waist: wheels within wheels, the great barrel wound taut, weights hanging on their cables like held breaths. Through the slit windows on four sides the afternoon came in thin and gold, laying bars of light across the gears so that the whole assembly seemed lit from within, some patient furnace burning nothing but hours.

Lior stopped just inside the doorway.

Edmund had seen people look at the clock before—trustees, aldermen, once a photographer from the county seat—but they looked at it the way they looked at monuments, flatly, wanting it to signify rather than to be. Lior looked at it the way she looked at everything: as though it might be hiding a discrepancy.

"It's louder than I expected," Lior said finally. "From outside you can't hear it at all."

"That's the point. Everything loud happens here so that everything outside can be quiet." Edmund set her tools on the bench and began unrolling the canvas, each instrument in its place, worn leather loops darkened by forty years of her father's hands and ten of her own. "A regulator clock doesn't chime. Doesn't strike. Nobody in town knows it exists except when they glance at the dial on the observatory face and adjust their watches by it. It governs every other clock in Marlow Bend without any of them knowing they're governed."

"That sounds like a metaphor," Lior said, and there was the pencil again, moving.

"It's an observation." Edmund pulled the lamp closer even though daylight still held; lamplight showed wear better. She nodded toward the escapement, the small ferocious heart of the thing where anchor teeth caught and released the escape wheel, tick, tick, tick, fifty years without missing except when broken. "Come here. You'll want to see this part. It's why the clock keeps honest time."

Lior approached, careful of the floor, of herself, and stood where indicated—closer than Edmund had calculated, close enough that Edmund could smell the ink on her fingers and the cheap soap of the boarding house. She did not step back. She noticed that she did not step back, and made herself continue as if the distance were deliberate, professional, nothing.

"This is the escapement." She touched nothing yet; first you showed, then you handled. "Every clock in the world is a weight or a spring trying to unwind all at once. The escapement is what stops it. It lets the power out one tooth at a time—one, and stop. One, and stop. Fifty times a minute, sixty thousand times a day. The gear train wants to run away and the escapement says no, says again, says wait." She reached in and, with a fingertip, stilled the pallet just enough to show the engagement, the precise kiss of steel on steel. Under her touch the ticking paused, resumed, paused. "It's the only dishonest-seeming thing in the whole mechanism. It interrupts. But the interruption is what makes the truth possible. Without it there's no time at all—just power spent in a rush, meaning nothing."

Lior was watching the pallets, not her face, which was a mercy. "And that makes it honest?"

"A clock can't lie. That's what I meant to tell you, the other day, before we argued instead." She withdrew her hand and the ticking steadied. "Everything else in this world will bend for you. Ledgers bend. Testimony bends. People bend themselves into whatever shape the moment requires—I'd know. But a going train either agrees with the sun or it doesn't. Set the noon gun against the transit of the star and the error shows to the tenth of a second, and no amount of wishing moves it. The mechanism reports itself exactly. Every wheel tells you what it is and what it's done. Wear shows. Repair shows. Fraud shows." She heard her own voice climbing and brought it down. "I've always thought that was a kind of mercy. Somewhere in the world, something that can't be talked into being other than what it is."

The pencil had stopped.

When Edmund glanced over, Lior was looking at her—not at the clock—with an expression Edmund had no category for. Not suspicion. Not the polite blankness townspeople wore around her. Something more like recognition, the look of a person finding a door in a wall she'd believed solid.

"You talk about it differently than you talk about anything else," Lior said. "Have you noticed?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"With me, at your shop, you answer in measurements. Yes, no, the date, the number of persons in the household. Like a man paying a debt he resents." Lior turned back to the movement, but gently now, the way one approaches an animal that has decided not to bolt. "But right now you're talking like the work is yours. Like you love it."

The word landed in the clock room and rang there. Love. The great wheel seemed to tick it around, tick, tick, distributing it through all the gearing.

"My father taught me," Edmund said, which was true and was also an evasion, and she found, standing in the honest machinery, that she was tired of the second half. "He used to say a clockmaker's confession is written into the work. You can't help it. Your patience shows in the finish. Your haste shows in the burrs. Whatever you refuse to say, the file says for you." She picked up a loupe and held it, not putting it to her eye. "Ten years I've worked alone because the work is the only conversation I can have without editing myself first. Every sentence I speak to another person goes through inspection. Is it safe. Does it fit the name. Will it hold weight. But when I'm cutting a wheel, there's no name. There's just the pitch of the teeth and whether my hand is steady."

Silence. The escapement counted it out—one, and stop, one, and stop—and Edmund understood suddenly that she had said more in three minutes than she had said aloud in ten years, and that she had said it to the one person in Marlow Bend whose profession was writing things down.

She should have been terrified. She examined herself for the terror and found instead a strange, loosening relief, like a mainspring let down after years at full tension.

"Show me the rest," Lior said quietly. "If I'm to record the condition of the works, I ought to understand what I'm recording."

So Edmund showed her.

* * *

She showed her the great wheel, six feet of spoked iron, and explained how its mass was not brute force but storage, a reservoir of steadiness against which all the smaller urgencies could be measured. She showed her the maintaining power that kept the clock running while it was wound, so that even in the act of renewal it never stopped telling the truth. She showed her the pendulum, and here she made Lior hold the lamp while she demonstrated the rating nut, and their hands overlapped on the lamp handle for longer than the task required, and neither of them moved, and the pendulum swung above them in its long patient arc, indifferent and exact.

"Why a pendulum?" Lior asked. Her voice had gone low, conversational, the register of two people in a room rather than a clerk and a subject.

"Because a pendulum doesn't care how heavy the load is. Wheels speed up and slow down with friction, with temperature, with wear. But the swing of a pendulum depends almost entirely on its own length. Gravity is impartial. Hang a weight on a rod of a certain length and it will take exactly as long to fall and rise as it took yesterday and will take tomorrow. It's the nearest thing to a promise the physical world makes." Edmund looked up at it, the bob gleaming dull gold in the lamplight. "My father said a pendulum is a prayer that got answered so reliably the church lost interest."

That startled a laugh out of Lior—a short one, quickly caught, but real. Edmund stared at her. She had never heard Lior laugh. She found she wanted to hear it again the way a man dying of thirst wants the next swallow, and she turned away to hide the wanting and busied her hands with the winding crank.

"You loved him," Lior said. It wasn't quite a question.

"He was the only person who ever spoke to me as though my mind were a fact rather than a claim." The crank was cold under her palms. She began to wind, slowly, keeping her eyes on the barrel. "Other fathers gave their sons a trade. He gave me—" Careful. Careful. "—a standard. Nothing less than correct, and nothing done for show. I hated him for it when I was young. Now I understand it was the only inheritance worth having. Money gets audited." She heard the word leave her mouth a half-second too late, heard how it sounded, and covered the flinch by hauling the crank through another quarter turn.

Lior didn't seem to notice, or pretended not to. "And your mother?"

"Died when I was six. I remember her hands. Nothing else. Just that they were always working at something." Ten years, and the lie had worn smooth as a river stone; it came out without friction. But today, in this room, under this light, the smoothness disgusted her. She set the crank down harder than necessary.

"You do that," Lior said.

"Do what."

"Stop mid-sentence. When a question comes near something." Lior had not moved from beside the pendulum; lamplight and window-light divided her face. "I notice it in records constantly. The entry that's too neat. The household list with a name missing. Gaps are where people keep what they can't afford to lose." She said it mildly, professionally, and then, quieter: "I'm not asking. I want you to know that. I'm just—telling you that I see the gap. That's all."

The ticking filled the tower. Edmund stood with her back half-turned, one hand resting on the oak case the way you'd rest a hand on a shoulder, and felt the choice arrive the way weather arrives—pressure first, then the certainty of it. She could close the case. She could make a joke about the hour, walk the census woman down forty-one steps, resume the arithmetic of survival: yes, no, one person in household, no wife.

Or she could open one more wheel.

"There's a part I haven't shown you," Edmund said. "The motion work. The dial train. It's the reason the whole clock exists at all, and everyone ignores it."

She knelt at the base of the case and unlatched the panel beneath the movement, and drew out the small elegant cluster of gears that translated the slow rotation of the hour cannon into the visible sweep of hands across a face. She held it in both palms, offered up like something in a story her mother might have told.

"Everyone thinks the soul of a clock is the escapement. The beating heart. But the escapement only counts. This is what makes the counting mean something." She turned it so the lamplight ran along the wheels. "The motion work takes one revolution per hour and divides it—twelvefold—so that a hand can point at a numeral and a person looking up from the street can read their own life in it. When to meet. When to grieve. When the ferry leaves. None of it is the clock's business, and all of it passes through these little wheels. The mechanism doesn't know what it's saying. It just says it truly." She looked up then, and found Lior kneeling too, closer than the work required, their faces level, the gears between them like a held breath. "I used to think honesty was a luxury. Say the true thing and lose everything. But a clock convinced me otherwise. The truth isn't the expensive part. The expensive part is all the machinery you build to avoid it—the false faces, the silent hours, the names that aren't yours. Truth is just what's left when the escapement lets go."

Lior's eyes had not left hers. "And what happens when it lets go?"

"Then the hands move," Edmund said, "and there's no stopping them until they've said what they were wound up to say."

Neither of them moved. The pendulum swung. Somewhere below, the river went on doing what rivers do, carrying things downstream whether or not anyone consents to be carried.

It was Lior who broke it, rising, brushing dust from her skirt, retrieving the clerk's voice like a coat from a hook. "I should note the works are in excellent condition," she said. "For the record."

"For the record," Edmund agreed, and latched the panel shut over the motion work, over the truth-telling wheels, and felt them ticking away in the dark of the case regardless—unseen, unstoppable, keeping their count toward whatever hour they had been wound to announce.

They descended the stairs together. At the tower door, in the last of the light, Lior paused with her hand on the iron latch.

"Mr. Thackeray," she said, and then, oddly, did not finish whatever the sentence had been. She stood a moment in the doorway with the wind pulling at her collar, looking at Edmund with that same unreadable door-in-a-wall expression, and Edmund understood that the gap she had admitted to seeing was widening in her notes, in her mind, in the space between them—and that sooner or later a question would come along narrow enough to fall straight through it.

"Good evening, Miss Ashcombe," Edmund said.

Lior went down the hill. Edmund climbed back up the forty-one steps to be alone with the clock, which was the only company she had ever trusted—and found, for the first time in ten years, that she wished for other.

Two days later, passing the trustee hall on her way from the brass merchant's, she saw Aldous Crane come out onto the steps with a folded paper in his hand, speaking low to the hall keeper, and the paper, when he gestured with it, bore the printed heading of the census office.
