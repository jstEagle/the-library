## 6. The Trustees' Meeting

The invitation came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy of perhaps twelve who smelled of lamp oil and had the practiced boredom of someone paid by the errand rather than the hour. He thrust a cream-colored envelope at Lior across the boarding house threshold, waited just long enough to confirm she wouldn't tip him, and vanished down Merchant Row before she'd broken the seal.

*Mr. Aldous Crane requests the attendance of Miss Lior Ashcombe, Census Enumerator for District Fourteen, at the monthly session of the Board of Town Trustees, Thursday next, seven o'clock, in the upper hall of the Courthouse. Your services as recorder are respectfully requested.*

Lior read it twice. *Respectfully requested.* In her experience—and her experience now spanned three river towns and a mill settlement—the phrase was never respectful and never quite a request. It meant that some man with a title had decided her pen would be useful and had not troubled himself to imagine she might decline. She considered declining anyway, purely on principle, then looked at the envelope again. Crane. The name had surfaced twice already in her first weeks here: once from the ferryman, who'd spat it like a seed husk, and once from Mrs. Halloran at the boarding house, who'd said it in the particular hushed tone reserved for men whose houses were larger than their churches.

She went. She told herself it was because a census taker who antagonized the trustees made her remaining work harder, and this was true, but it was also true that she wanted to see the machinery of the place—wanted to know what moved beneath the town's surface politeness, what gears turned when no one was counting.

The courthouse stood at the top of the hill like a man who'd stopped climbing halfway and decided the view from there was sufficient. Its upper hall had been built for grander occasions than monthly trustees' meetings: a vaulted ceiling gone gray with stove smoke, tall windows whose curtains had been washed to a uniform shade of nothing, and a long table of dark oak at which seven chairs sat waiting for seven men. A clerk's desk had been shoved against the wall for her benefit—a smaller table with an inkwell, two pens, and a stack of foolscap ruled by hand, which suggested they'd expected her and had prepared for her the way one prepares a room for a tradesman.

Seven o'clock came and went. At seven minutes past, the doors opened and the trustees filed in, talking among themselves in the easy overlapping way of men who have shared a table for years and no longer hear each other. Lior caught names as they settled: Hargrove, who farmed; Pettibone, who owned the grain warehouse; Dray, the physician; old Mr. Sallow, who appeared to be asleep before his chair finished accepting him. And at the head, Aldous Crane.

He was not what she'd expected. She'd pictured bulk, bluster, some outward sign of the appetite that rumor attributed to him. Instead he was narrow and precise, a man assembled entirely of edges—high collar, high cheekbones, a jaw shaved so close it gleamed under the gaslight. His hair was iron-gray and parted with geometric exactness. When he sat, he sat all at once, like a door closing. He acknowledged her with a single nod, no more, and Lior understood immediately that the nod was not courtesy. It was inventory. He was noting that she existed, that she had arrived, that she could be counted upon. Everything in the room, she suspected, was counted upon by Aldous Crane. That was presumably how he'd gotten the room built around him.

"Minutes of the September session," Crane said, before anyone else could speak, "are approved as circulated." A murmur of assent. "New business."

There followed forty minutes of town business so relentlessly ordinary that Lior's pen nearly slowed: a dispute over the drainage ditch behind the cooperage; a petition from the schoolmistress for coal allotments; the question of whether the bridge lanterns should be converted to the new-style burners. She recorded it all faithfully, dates and figures and the small grumbling asides of farmers' representatives, and began to believe the evening would be exactly what the envelope promised—tedium with a title.

Then Pettibone mentioned the observatory clock.

It came up sideways, the way these things do. The town's lease arrangement with the observatory trust required maintenance reports, and the maintenance report for the master clock had come back with a note: repairs ongoing, completion anticipated within the season, work conducted by E. Thackeray, clockmaker. Pettibone read the note aloud in the tone of a man reading someone else's laundry list, and added, "Thackeray's been up there better part of two years, hasn't he? Clock's still not keeping time."

"The mechanism suffered considerable damage," Dray said mildly. "I'm told the escapement was near destroyed. These things take—"

"These things take money," Crane said.

He said it quietly, which somehow made it worse than shouting would have been. The room adjusted itself around his voice the way water adjusts around a dropped stone. Even Mr. Sallow opened one eye.

"The observatory trust disburses funds against invoices," Crane continued, drawing a folder from beneath a stack of papers with the smooth economy of a man who has already rehearsed the gesture. "Invoices signed E. Thackeray. I have reviewed eleven months of them." He did not open the folder. He didn't need to; the folder was furniture, a prop in a play whose script lived entirely inside his head. "And I find myself asking a simple question. Who is E. Thackeray?"

Silence took the room and held it. Lior's pen hovered above the foolscap.

"The late Josiah Thackeray's son," Hargrove said, frowning. "Apprenticed to him since—what, boyhood? Everyone knows Edmund Thackeray."

"Everyone knows of him," Crane corrected, with the delicacy of a fencer turning a blade flat. "Which is not the same thing. Consider, gentlemen, what we actually know. A workshop passes by inheritance to a son. The son appears in public perhaps four times a year, always at hours when the streets are empty. He does not attend church. He does not attend market. He has taken no wife, joined no guild, signed no public roll under any full name. He communicates with this board exclusively through paper." Crane let the folder rest flat on the oak and placed two fingers upon it, lightly, as if taking its pulse. "Eleven months of invoices. All in the same cramped hand. And now a note informing us the work continues, indefinitely, funded by monies drawn on an estate whose disposition rests on a will no one outside that household has ever seen."

Dray shifted. "Aldous, the man keeps to himself. Half the craftsmen in this county keep to themselves. My own—"

"Your own what, Doctor?" Crane asked pleasantly. "You were about to cite an example. Let us have it examined with the same rigor we'd apply to any other irregularity."

Dray did not finish his sentence. Lior watched him fold back into himself, watched the other trustees study their hands, and felt something cold begin to move through her—not at what Crane said, but at how he said it. There was no anger in him. No relish, even. Only procedure. He spoke of a man's life the way an auditor speaks of columns that refuse to balance, and that, she thought, was more frightening than malice. Malice could be argued with.

"Gentlemen," Crane said, "I move that this board commission a formal audit of the Thackeray estate and its disbursements, citing irregularities in the execution of the late Josiah Thackeray's will—specifically, the circumstances under which the apprenticeship and inheritance transferred, and the identity and standing of the current holder. Further, that the audit be empowered to request supporting documents from any office of record, municipal or otherwise."

Any office of record. The words went through Lior like a splinter finding the seam of a glove.

Seconded by Pettibone, who seemed relieved to be agreeing with someone. Discussion followed, brief and lopsided, mostly Hargrove objecting that it seemed a poor way to treat a craftsman mid-repair—"the clock'll stop, and then we'll all be explaining to the observatory trust"—and Crane answering each objection with the same unanswerable patience. An audit was not an accusation, he said. It was hygiene. A town that failed to examine its records deserved what grew in them. By the time he called the vote, only Dray abstained, and he abstained while looking at the table.

"Aye," said five voices.

Lior wrote it down. *Motion carried, five in favor, one abstention.* Her hand was steady. She noticed that her hand was steady and hated herself a little for noticing, the way one hates oneself for being calm in a house where someone is dying.

* * *

The hall emptied slowly, trustees gathering coats and continuing arguments in murmurs, until only Crane remained, reviewing the foolscap she'd filled. He read quickly, precisely, his eyes moving down the page like a shuttle through a loom.

"You record well," he said, without looking up. "Clean hand. You don't editorialize."

"It's not my place to editorialize."

"No." He set the pages square against the desk's edge, aligning them with a small tap of his fingertips. "It's your place to be accurate. Which is rarer, and worth considerably more." Now he looked at her, and his eyes were pale gray and entirely without warmth, though nothing in them was cold enough to be called cruelty either. They were simply thorough. "Miss Ashcombe. How long have you been in Harrow's Bend?"

"Six weeks."

"And in that time you've visited every household in the district. Every dwelling. You knock on doors, you ask your questions, you write down what people tell you." He said it without accusation, laying out facts like instruments on a tray. "Occupations. Ages. Marital status. Persons residing under each roof."

"Yes."

"Tell me—when you visited the Thackeray workshop, what did the census yield?"

The question landed in the room and lay there. Lior heard her own pulse. Somewhere below, in the lower hall, a door closed, and the sound traveled up through the floorboards like a warning sent ahead of itself.

"That information is confidential," she said. "Census returns are sealed. They're compiled into aggregate figures—population, occupation, density—for the state tables. Individual entries aren't published. They aren't available."

"To the public," Crane agreed. "I'm aware of the statute." He gathered his folder, his papers, his precise gray self, and rose. "But statutes are written by governments, Miss Ashcombe, and audits are commissioned by courts, and courts can subpoena anything a government has ever bothered to write down. If it comes to that—if the examination finds it necessary—I imagine your sheets will speak very clearly on their own. You seem like a woman who writes down what is true." A pause, thin as a knife laid flat. "That will serve the board admirably. Good evening."

He left. Lior sat alone in the emptied hall with her foolscap and the guttering lamps and the particular silence of a room after a decision has been made inside it.

* * *

She walked home the long way, along the river road, because the direct route passed the foot of Observatory Hill and she did not want to look at it. The night was October-cold and enormous, stars scattered overhead with the indifference of spilled salt, and the black water beside her kept its own counsel, moving without opinion toward the sea.

Her satchel held the completed sheets for the eastern district. Forty-one households, recorded in her clean, non-editorializing hand. She knew every entry on those pages. She had asked every question herself, written every answer herself, and somewhere in that stack—she could see it without looking—was a sheet headed *Thackeray, E., clockmaker,* with lines ruled and waiting:

*Lives alone. No wife. No other persons residing at the premises.*

She had recorded it on a morning when the light through the workshop window had been gold and thick with dust motes, when Edmund Thackeray had answered her questions with a precision that had struck her even then as armor, when she'd noticed the pause—too long, a beat too long—before the word *no*, and had written it down anyway, because writing things down was what she did, because accuracy was her craft and her protection and her whole architecture of safety in towns that did not want her.

*Accuracy is rare, and worth considerably more.* Crane had praised her for the exact quality that now made her dangerous. Her honesty was the instrument; his audit was merely the hand that would pick it up. And the worst of it—the part that made her stop walking, stand still on the dark river road with her breath smoking in the lamplight—was that she had known. Not known-known, not in any way she could prove or articulate, but some animal part of her had registered that pause, that too-long silence, and had chosen not to pull the thread. She had let it lie because it wasn't her business, because curiosity in a stranger is a trespass, because ten years of moving between towns where she was tolerated at best had taught her that everyone carries locked rooms and decency means walking past their doors.

Decency meant walking past. But she hadn't walked past, had she? She'd knocked. She'd asked. She'd written it down. And now her careful, honest, professional record sat folded in a satchel on her arm like a loaded thing, waiting for a court to point it at whoever the court decided needed pointing at.

Complicity, she thought, doesn't wait for consent. It arrives ahead of you, sits down at the table, and takes minutes.

She thought of Edmund's hands—she had thought about those hands more than she cared to examine—scarred and ink-stained and impossibly sure of themselves, turning a gear in the lamplight as though the gear were listening. She thought of the pause before *no*. She thought of a man—or whatever truth lived behind that name, the shape of it she couldn't yet look at directly—who had answered every question with precision except the one that mattered, and who had trusted, perhaps, that the census was a formality. That the state's interest ended at numbers.

Crane knew otherwise. Or Crane suspected, which was worse, because suspicion needed only a document to become certainty, and she was the document.

By the time she reached the boarding house, her mind had done what her mind always did under pressure: it had begun drafting alternatives. She could alter the sheet—scratch the line, re-enter the household as ambiguous, *refused to answer*—and a falsified return was a crime with her signature on it, discoverable, prosecutable, ruinous. She could submit as-is and let events proceed, telling herself the law was the law and she was only its pen. She could warn Edmund—but warn of what, exactly, on what evidence, having admitted what knowledge?

Mrs. Halloran had left a lamp burning in the front parlor. Lior climbed past it in the dark, let herself into her narrow room, and set the satchel on the desk without opening it.

Through the window, past the rooftops, she could just make out the observatory dome against the sky, and below it the faint warm smudge of a lit window in the workshop—someone awake at midnight, working, unaware or trying not to be aware that the town's machinery had begun, quietly and with perfect procedure, to close around them.

Lior sat down at the desk. She did not take out the sheets. She put her head in her hands instead, and stayed that way a long time, listening to the river she couldn't see, thinking: *I could have written anything on that page. I wrote the truth. Why does it feel like the first dishonest thing I've ever done?*

Tomorrow, she told herself, she would decide what kind of recorder she intended to be.

Tomorrow, she would go back to the workshop.
