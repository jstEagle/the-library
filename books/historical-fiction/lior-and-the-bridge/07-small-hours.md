## 7. Small Hours

The escapement would not forgive her.

Edmund knew it the way she knew weather in her knees—some old certainty that lived below thought. She had been at the bench since nine o'clock, and now the candle had burned down to a stub swimming in its own wax, and the pallet wheel still refused its rhythm. *Tick. Tick-tick.* A hitch, like a man with a stone in his boot pretending to walk well.

She set down the tweezers and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until sparks bloomed in the dark.

Outside, October was doing its work on the town. Wind came off the river and worried the shutters of the workshop, and somewhere down the hill a dog had been complaining since midnight, a thin exhausted sound like a hinge no one would oil. The lamp over her bench threw its small yellow circle onto the bench board, onto the scattered wheels and the open throat of the master clock's movement, and beyond that circle the room went to blackness, and beyond the blackness was the whole sleeping town, and somewhere in it Aldous Crane was presumably asleep too, dreaming in ledger lines.

She should not think of Crane. Thinking of Crane made her hands unsteady, and unsteady hands were the one sin she could not commit.

*Keep the name,* her father had said, ten years ago, in a room that smelled of camphor and lamp oil. *The name is the workshop. The workshop is the craft. Lose anything else you must.*

He had said it as arithmetic, and she had done the sum faithfully for a decade. Bind, flatten, lower the voice to its bottom shelf, sign *E. Thackeray* in letters that grew more masculine each year until they were nothing like the girl's hand she'd learned with. She had become so fluent in the lie that sometimes, alone at this bench at two in the morning, she caught herself thinking in it—the lie had gotten inside her language, like water in a clock case, swelling the wood.

And now there was a census taker who looked at people as though they were sentences she was trying to parse.

Edmund reached for the pallet fork again and stopped herself. No. A tired hand ruins good work. Her father's rule, second only to the first. She took up instead the piece she could do blind: polishing the escape wheel teeth, one by one, with a stick of boxwood and rouge, the motion so worn into her muscles that her mind could leave entirely.

It left. It went where it always went when she let it—to the observatory, to the master clock hanging silent in its tower these three weeks, its great dial blank-eyed over the river while the whole town arranged itself around the absence of the hour. She had promised the trustees completion by the first frost. Crane had smiled when he said *first frost*, the way a man smiles when he knows something about frost that you don't.

Tick. Tick-tick.

She polished. The wind worked at the shutters. The dog gave up sometime after three, or fell asleep, or died; she didn't go to the window to learn which.

Her father had known. That thought arrived without warning, the way the worst ones did. Not the deathbed arithmetic—she'd had ten years to wear grooves in that—but the other thing, the thing she kept discovering in the margins of everything: the way he had never once asked why his daughter wanted to be apprenticed under her brother's name, dead Ned, poor drowned Ned whose name fit the guild rolls better than any living girl's ever would. He had simply drawn up the indenture papers in his careful draftsman's hand and said, *Mind your oil levels,* and that had been the whole conversation, conducted across thirty years of shared silences at a workbench, and she had believed it meant he understood.

Or it meant nothing. Or it meant he couldn't bear to look directly at what he was helping her do. Grief and love look identical from behind.

The candle guttered. She trimmed the wick without looking, and the light steadied, and she went back to the wheel, tooth by tooth by tooth, and if her eyes stung she could blame the rouge dust, and did, and kept working.

* * *

Dawn came the way it does in river towns—not arriving so much as leaking in, grey seeping upward out of the water until the sky admits it. Edmund noticed it finally because the light through the east window began to compete with her lamp, making her shadow double and waver on the bench board like a bad pendulum.

She sat back. Her neck cracked when she rolled it. Six hours, and the escape wheel was finished—every tooth bright—and the pallets adjusted twice and still that hitch, that tick-tick, that stutter she could hear now even in her memory.

There was coffee in the tin on the stove, yesterday's, and she drank it cold standing at the window, looking down the hill toward the ferry landing, and that was when she saw the figure on the riverbank path.

Grey coat. Census satchel. Standing at the rail of the old fish pier doing absolutely nothing, which at half past six in the morning marked a person as either mad or sleepless, and Lior Ashcombe did not look mad. She looked like someone who had lost an argument with her own bed and come outside to finish losing it in the cold.

Edmund watched her for a full minute before deciding she hadn't decided anything. Then she put on her coat, took the spare lantern though dawn made it foolish, and went down the hill.

The path was slick with the year's first real frost. Lior heard her boots and turned, and Edmund watched the woman assemble her professional face piece by piece—there, the neutral mouth; there, the level gaze—as though Edmund might demand an accounting of her presence.

"You're up early," Lior said.

"So are you."

"I couldn't—" Lior stopped. Started differently. "The ferry horn wakes me. Even when there's no ferry. I hear it anyway."

"There's no ferry till eight," Edmund said.

"I know." And then, with the honesty of the very tired: "I've been here since five."

They stood in the cold a moment. The river ran brown-green and unhurried beneath the pier, carrying last night's leaves seaward in ragged little fleets. Somewhere upstream a mill began its day, a deep rhythmic knocking like a giant heart.

"I have coffee," Edmund said, "but it's cold and terrible."

"Is there more of the terrible kind?"

"My neighbor sells beans. Terrible ones. Fresh every week."

Lior almost smiled. Almost—it was the almost that undid something in Edmund's chest, some small latch she hadn't known was set. "Then we should walk," Lior said, "before either of us becomes sensible."

They walked. The riverbank path ran west along the water, past the rotting pilings of the old coal wharf, past reed beds gone bone-white and rustling, and the frost made the world quiet in that particular way, sound arriving clean and close as though the air itself had been polished. Their breath hung in front of them and tore away downstream.

"The clock," Lior said, after a quarter mile of companionable silence. "How goes it?"

"It stutters."

"A clock? Stutters?"

"Pallets out of true by a hair. A hair is a country, in my trade." Edmund turned her lantern off and hooked it to her belt; the dawn had won. "Everyone believes a clock is about telling time. It isn't. It's about lying well. You build a machine that lies the same amount, over and over, forever, and everyone agrees to call the lie *correct*."

Lior glanced sideways at her. "That's a bleak theology for a clockmaker."

"It's an honest one." Edmund kicked a frost-stiffened clod of earth off the path and watched it break apart on the shingle below. "What about you? Is a census about truth?"

Lior was quiet long enough that Edmund thought she wouldn't answer. The mill's knocking faded behind them; ahead, gulls argued over something on the mudflats.

"When I was training," Lior said finally, "my instructor told me the census is the only document most people will ever appear in. Think of that. A farm wife in Nebraska, a dockhand, a girl in a boarding house—one page in one ledger, and a hundred years from now that page is all anyone can know. Name, age, trade. Whether they can read." She looked out at the river. "So I try to write it truly. And every day I find out how much truth doesn't fit in a column."

"What doesn't fit?" Edmund asked, and heard her own voice come out too carefully, too interested, and hated it.

"This town doesn't fit." Lior laughed shortly, without much humor. "I record forty households on Ferry Street. Forty. But I've counted the chimneys smoking. Fifty-three. Where are the other thirteen families? Boarders, lodgers, a woman and her sister sharing a house since their husbands died—who writes *that* down? There's no column for how people actually survive." She hugged her arms against the cold. "In the city it was worse. Tenements with nineteen souls behind one door. You'd ask the head of household and he'd give you twelve, because nineteen meant fines, and fines meant eviction, and my true page could burn down somebody's life. So." A shrug that carried a great deal of weight. "You learn which truths are safe to hold."

"And which aren't?"

"That depends," Lior said, turning to look at her directly, "on who's asking."

The gulls rose all at once, a white scatter against the grey, and Edmund used the interruption. She walked on a few paces before answering, watching her boots find the frozen ruts.

"I came here from Lowell," she said. "After my father died. Everyone in this town thinks Thackeray has always been here, because Thackeray behaves as though he has. But I was born in a mill city, and I escaped it the way you escape a burning house—by never once looking back, because looking back is how you turn around." She kept her eyes on the path. "Lowell taught me that a person can be completely surrounded by people and entirely unknown, and that unknown is the safest country there is. You can live there for years. I did. Ten of them."

"Why leave it, then?" Lior asked softly. "Unknown?"

Edmund stopped walking. Below them the river slid past, indifferent, going about its enormous business. She found that she was holding her own wrist behind her back, the old habit, the binding habit, keeping herself together.

"Because unknown starts to feel like erased," she said. "Eventually."

The words came out smaller than she intended. Lior didn't rush to fill the silence, which Edmund was grateful for and unnerved by in equal measure—everyone else rushed, everyone else needed the silence filled with reassurance or questions or noise, but this woman simply stood in the frost and let it be, as though silence were a room she knew how to keep.

"Where did you come from?" Edmund asked, to move the weight off her own chest. "Before the census office."

"Boston. Before that, a town in Vermont so small it isn't on the railroad maps anymore." Lior resumed walking, and they fell into step without arranging to. "My father was the town's records keeper. Town clerk, justice of the peace, keeper of the vital statistics—births, deaths, marriages, all of it in his hand, all of it in ledgers he ruled himself because store-bought lines were too far apart for his liking." A pause. "When I was sixteen I found a page where he'd recorded a birth wrong. Deliberately. A child born four months after a wedding, and he'd written it eleven months, smoothed it over, given the family cover. Neat as anything. His best hand." She shook her head slowly. "I stood there in his office hating him and understanding him in the same breath, and I couldn't decide which was worse, and I left within the year. Took a clerkship in Boston. Then the census. I told myself I was the honest version of him."

"Are you?"

"I don't know yet." Lior said it plainly, without performance, and Edmund felt the strange vertigo of hearing someone refuse to lie even about their virtue. "I keep waiting for my page. The one where smoothing things over seems like kindness instead of theft. Some days I think it already happened and I just haven't checked the book."

The path bent inland around a stand of bare cottonwoods, and the wind found them there, sharp with frost and coming off the water, and both of them turned their faces from it in the same motion, and for a moment they stood shoulder to shoulder against it, close enough that Edmund could smell woodsmoke in Lior's coat, could see the fine exhaustion around her eyes.

"My father kept secrets too," Edmund said, when the wind passed. "In his shop. Every lockbox, every false drawer. I used to think it was guilt—something hidden, something owed. Now I think it was just... care. He kept things safe by keeping them secret. It's the same skill. It's the same *disease*." She laughed, and the laugh cracked a little in the middle. "Look at us. Standing on a frozen riverbank at dawn confessing our fathers to each other."

"Is that what we're doing?" Lior asked. There was no alarm in it. There was something almost like relief.

"I believe it is. God help us both."

They came around the bend and the town opened below them—rooftops silvered with frost, smoke rising straight up from fifty-three chimneys in the still air, the dark bulk of the observatory on its hill with its silent dial facing the water like a closed eye. The mill knocked. The gulls resettled. The world proceeded.

"I should get the fresh terrible beans," Edmund said. "Before my neighbor sells out to people who want good coffee."

"Yes," Lior agreed. But neither of them moved for another moment, standing there above the river, and Edmund understood suddenly that they were both doing the same thing: postponing the moment the ordinary day resumed and this strange thaw refroze. She had spent ten years becoming excellent at refreezing. It startled her how much she didn't want to practice it now.

"Thackeray." Lior said it as she finally turned to go, and something in the name—her name, the borrowed one, spoken in this new careful voice—made Edmund's chest tighten. "The stutter in your clock. When you fix it—if you need a second pair of hands for anything. Holding, lifting. I'm clumsy, but I follow instructions."

Edmund looked at her. The grey light lay flat on the water and flat on Lior Ashcombe's patient, guarded, wide-awake face, and Edmund heard herself say, before caution could catch it:

"Come by this afternoon. There's a gear train that wants three hands, and I only have two."

Lior nodded once, and held her eyes a moment longer than a nod required, and then walked back along the frosted path toward the ferry landing and whatever official day waited for her there.

Edmund stood watching until the grey coat disappeared among the rooftops. Then she turned uphill toward the workshop, toward the cold coffee and the stuttering clock and the long arithmetic of the day, and found that her hands, for the first time in weeks, were steady.

She did not examine why. She was saving that, she suspected, for later—for this afternoon, when the door would open and three hands would reach into the works of a machine built to tell the truth.

She only hoped, climbing the hill with the frost melting off the rails beside her, that she would recognize the moment the gears engaged. She had a feeling it would sound exactly like a stutter, cured.
