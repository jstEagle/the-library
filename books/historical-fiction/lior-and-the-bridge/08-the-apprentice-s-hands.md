## 8. The Apprentice's Hands

The morning of the third Thursday in October arrived with fog so thick the river seemed to have swallowed the town whole. Lior stood at the window of her rented room above the chandler's shop, watching the mist erase the far bank, and told herself she had come to the workshop for professional reasons only.

She had, after all, unfinished business with her own ledger. The clerical error from her second visit had been corrected weeks ago—Edmund Thackeray, occupation: clockmaker, household: one—but there remained the matter of the observatory clock itself, which the census instructions required her to note as a public works structure under maintenance. A technicality. A formality. She had walked past the observatory four times this week without going in.

Today she went in.

The workshop smelled of brass filings and lamp oil and something faintly sweet she couldn't place—perhaps the beeswax Edmund used to dress the wooden gear trains of the old regulators. The door stood open as it always did now, and Lior let herself into the front room where the benches crowded together like passengers on a ferry deck. Edmund was not at her usual station by the window. Instead, Lior found her crouched beneath the great worktable in the back, wrestling with a crate that had arrived on the morning packet from the city: new-cut wheels for the master clock's going train, wrapped in oilcloth and straw.

"You're early," Edmund said, without looking up. Her voice came out muffled and slightly strained. "Or late. I've lost track of which."

"It's half past eight."

"Then early." She emerged from beneath the table with a wheel cradled against her chest—a disc of brass the size of a dinner plate, its teeth cut fine as the edge of a coin. She held it the way one holds an infant or a wounded bird, and Lior saw again what she had noticed on her first visit and could not stop noticing since: those hands. Broad across the palm, scarred at the knuckles, ink worked permanently into the creases around the nails—and yet the fingers themselves were long, almost delicate, moving with a precision that seemed at odds with their strength. A worker's hands. But something else besides. Something she couldn't name and had stopped trying to name aloud, even in the privacy of her own thoughts.

"I need these mounted before the fog lifts," Edmund said. "The light's better for checking the teeth when it comes through the east windows, and once the fog burns off the sun moves too fast." She nodded toward the assembly frame at the center bench—an iron scaffold of clamps and arms, currently empty, waiting. "I can't do it alone. The train has to go in all at once or the tension fights me."

"Is that why you're asking me?"

"That's why I'm asking *you*," Edmund said, and there was something almost wry in it, almost warm. "You're the only person in this town who walks past my door without crossing the street first. And your hands are steady. I've watched you write."

Lior set down her satchel. "What do I do?"

"Hold this arm here—" Edmund guided her to the frame, positioned Lior's left hand against a clamp lever, "—and don't move it. Whatever you hear, whatever happens, don't move it. If the train slips while I'm seating the fourth wheel, the mainspring unwinds and takes my thumb with it."

"That's a reassuring thing to tell someone."

"I'm telling you because it's true," Edmund said. "Everything I tell you is true. It's the only currency I have."

Lior didn't ask what she meant by that. She had learned, over these weeks of visits and arguments about records and truth, that Edmund Thackeray said things that sounded like confessions and then went silent before they could be examined. It was a habit Lior recognized, having practiced some version of it herself—the art of saying exactly enough and never a syllable more.

They worked. Edmund fed the wheels into the frame one by one, each seated on its arbor with a tap of a brass-faced hammer, and Lior held the clamp arm rigid while the assembly grew between them: wheel above wheel, the going train taking shape like a small mechanical tree. The fog outside thinned by degrees, and the east light came through in long pale blades, catching the brass until the whole frame seemed lit from within.

It was exacting work, and it required them to stand close. Closer than Lior had ever stood to anyone in years—not counting handshakes with strangers at doors, the transactional touches of her trade. She could smell the lamp oil in Edmund's hair, could see the fine scarring on her—on *his*, she corrected herself automatically; she always corrected herself—cheek where a slipping file had caught him years ago. She kept her eyes on the mechanism because looking anywhere else felt dangerous.

"The fourth wheel is the heart of it," Edmund murmured, absorbed. "Everyone thinks the escapement is, but it isn't. The escapement only decides. The fourth wheel delivers. Every second of every day, this wheel hands the motion down the line. It never decides anything. It just keeps giving."

"You talk about it like it's alive."

"It's more honest than most living things." Edmund lifted the fourth wheel from its wrapping—a smaller disc, pierced with elegant spokes—and fitted it to the arbor with both hands. "Hold steady. This is the moment."

Lior held. And as Edmund leaned in to seat the wheel, guiding it with fingertips on either side of the rim, her right hand brushed Lior's where it gripped the clamp arm—the backs of two fingers against the backs of two fingers, a contact of perhaps a square inch of skin.

Neither of them moved away.

It should have lasted half a second. The wheel needed seating; the task demanded attention; any sensible person would have withdrawn. But Lior's fingers stayed where they were, and Edmund's stayed where they were, and the wheel hung half-seated on its arbor between them while four seconds passed, then five, then six—the longest six seconds Lior had ever counted, though counting time was supposedly her profession too, in her way, names and numbers and durations, everyone fixed to their proper place in the record.

The workshop was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, a regulator ticked. Outside, the fog was lifting off the river, and a gull cried somewhere over the water, and none of it mattered at all.

Edmund withdrew first. Not abruptly—she finished seating the wheel, gave it the smallest trial turn, and stepped back with her eyes still on the assembly. But her ears had gone red above the collar, and her voice, when she spoke, was carefully level.

"There. Fourth wheel's in. You can let go."

Lior let go. Her hand felt strange, emptied, as if it were still holding something.

"It's beautiful," she said, meaning the clock, meaning nothing else, refusing to mean anything else.

"It's a machine." Edmund began wiping her hands on the rag at her belt, not quite looking at her. "Beauty comes later, if the beat's true. Come and see it run when I've got the escapement on. A week, maybe less."

"A week." Lior retrieved her satchel. At the door she paused, and heard herself say, before she could decide whether to say it: "Thank you for trusting me with it."

Edmund looked up then, directly, and for a moment something moved behind her expression—something unguarded and startled, a door opening onto a lit room and immediately shut. "You hold things steady," she said. "Not many people do."

Lior walked home through the burning-off fog with her heart behaving badly and her mind constructing explanations: surprise, fatigue, the intimacy of shared work, nothing, nothing at all.

* * *

That evening she sat at the little desk by the window with her census sheets spread out before her, doing what she did every evening now that autumn was deepening: reviewing, cross-checking, preparing the final fair copies for submission to the county office. It was methodical work, and she loved it—or had loved it once, before this town, before this assignment, before a clockmaker's hands had made her doubt her own steadiness.

Household by household she worked through the district. The Hollis family at the ferry landing, seven souls. Widow Pruen and her lodger. The schoolmaster, his sister, his sister's three children. Names, ages, occupations, places of birth, the whole architecture of a town rendered in her careful clerk's hand.

She reached the sheet for the Thackeray workshop near the bottom of the stack, and her pen slowed.

*Thackeray, Edmund. Age 31. Clockmaker (master, by inheritance). Place of birth: this town.*

And below it, in the space reserved for remarks, the note she'd written herself during that first visit, recording what the neighbors had told her, what the town believed, what she had observed:

*Lives alone; no wife.*

She read it twice. Three times. There was nothing remarkable in it—thousands of entries across the country would read the same, bachelors and spinsters and widowers, the whole quiet army of the unattached that every census recorded and no one questioned. It was a fact. It was a fact she had written down without a second thought, the way she wrote down everything, faithfully, mechanically, believing that facts were innocent things.

So why did the words sit wrong on the page tonight?

*Lives alone.* True enough. No one else slept in that house; she'd asked directly, and the pause before the answer had lasted long enough to measure. *No wife.* Also true, presumably. Thirty-one years old, established in trade, respectable inheritance—and never married. Never courted either, so far as the town knew, and the town knew everything about everyone except, apparently, Edmund Thackeray.

She thought of the trustees' meeting last week—Aldous Crane's smooth voice, the word *irregularities*, the way his gaze had drifted to her own papers on the table as though they were tools laid out for his use. She thought of how a census record could be subpoenaed, could become evidence, could be turned inside out by a man who wanted a workshop and knew precisely which questions to ask of a document. *Why does he live alone? Why has he never married? Why does no wife appear, no sweetheart, no scandal even—what kind of man keeps himself like that?*

And beneath that thought, arriving unbidden and unwelcome, another thought entirely, one that had been circling her for weeks like a moth around a lamp:

*What kind of man indeed.*

Her pen hovered over the remark. One stroke could amend it—one clean line through *no wife*, a marginal correction, *unverified*, the honest annotation of a careful clerk who could not vouch for secondhand information. No one would ever know. Records were amended constantly; that was what review drafts were for.

But that was the crime, wasn't it? That was the exact shape of the thing Crane would do with her sheets if she let him—not falsify, merely *curate*. Choose which truths to keep. And she had sat in that meeting and said nothing, taken her notes, gone home, and told herself complicity arrived before choice, as if that excused anything.

She put down the pen. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes.

The trouble—the real trouble, deeper than Crane, deeper than the audit—was this: she had written *lives alone; no wife* as a neutral fact, and now she could not read it neutrally. Now the words carried weight they shouldn't carry. Because she had stood close to him today—close enough to smell the oil in his hair, close enough that their fingers had touched and neither had pulled away—and some part of her, some part she refused to examine, had registered the touch not as accident but as *answer*. As if the question had been asked weeks ago, in the pause before *no*, in the redness of his ears, in the way he said *everything I tell you is true* and then went silent, and today the mechanism had finally delivered the motion down the line.

And suspicion, her oldest professional instinct, whispered the other reading. That the pause had been a lie. That the silence had a shape. That a man who lived ten years alone in a dead father's house, who avoided church and market and guild rolls, whose hands were strong and fine and somehow *wrong* in a way she couldn't articulate—who kept himself like that—was keeping something. People kept things. She had documented enough households to know that every ledger had its blank spaces, and blank spaces were never truly empty; they were where people hid what they could not survive writing down.

Desire and suspicion, turning together like two wheels on a single arbor. She couldn't speed one without speeding the other.

Lior looked at the entry again. Then, deliberately, she picked up her pen and left it untouched. The record would stand as written—true, incomplete, dangerous. Let Crane make of it what he would. She would not begin the business of curating truth, not for Edmund Thackeray's protection, and not for her own.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the town settled around her: the river muttering against its pilings, a dog somewhere on Ferry Street, the faint far-off ticking that she could almost convince herself came from the observatory hill—that great broken heart of a clock, waiting for the man who had promised to finish it.

A week, he'd said. Come see it run.

She lay awake a long time, her fingers remembering a contact of one square inch, her mind turning over a single written line—*lives alone; no wife*—and wondering, with a dread that felt disgracefully like hope, what else in that neat and faithful record was waiting to be understood.
