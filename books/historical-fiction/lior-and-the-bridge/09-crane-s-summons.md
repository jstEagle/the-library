## 9. Crane's Summons

The bell above the workshop door had not rung in three days, and when it rang at last—two sharp notes, brass on brass—Edmund knew before looking up that it would be trouble. Customers came in hesitantly, letting the bell trail off into silence. This visitor let the door swing shut behind him with the flat authority of a man who had never once entered a room he did not intend to own.

Aldous Crane stood in the doorway of Thackeray & Son, Watchmakers and Repairers of Fine Instruments, holding his hat against his chest as though the workshop were a church and he a reluctant communicant. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with the ruddy, well-fed complexion of someone who had never missed a meal or an opportunity, and his eyes moved over the benches and shelves the way a creditor's eyes move over a ledger—itemizing, discounting, summing toward a total.

"Master Thackeray." His voice filled the room without effort. "I hope I find you at your work."

"You find me at my work," Edmund said. She did not rise from the bench. Her loupe was still screwed to her eye; she left it there a moment longer than necessary, finishing the thought she'd been having about the pallet stones in front of her, and then set it down on its leather rest with a small deliberate click. "Mr. Crane. The trustees don't usually call in person. There's a post office."

"There is indeed. But some matters want a face attached to them." Crane advanced into the shop, and Edmund watched him read it: the smell of oil and metal filings, the racks of unfinished movements hanging like cured hams, the great plate-glass case where finished watches slept under lock. Ten years she had kept this room. She knew exactly what he saw—a workshop, well-kept, prosperous enough—and she knew exactly what he was looking for, which was everything the room could not show him.

She wiped her fingers on the rag that lived by the bench, a gesture so habitual it required no thought at all, and only afterward registered what the rag revealed: her hands, blackened at the knuckles, nicked across two fingers, the nails cut blunt and square. A working man's hands. She folded them under the bench's edge.

"To what do I owe the visit?" she asked. "If it's the assessment for the road levy, I paid it in September. Receipt's in the drawer."

"It isn't the levy." Crane smiled, and the smile was worse than the eyes, because the eyes at least pretended to nothing. He drew a document from inside his coat—a folded paper, worn soft at the creases as though it had been carried for days, taken out and looked at and put away again. "It's a matter of record. Town records, guild records. You know how I am about records, Master Thackeray. I believe we share the acquaintance of Miss Ashcombe, the census taker—she tells me you're a man who appreciates precision."

Edmund felt the sentence settle into her chest like a cold coin. *She tells me.* So the census taker's name had already passed through Crane's mouth, already been turned over and examined for its usefulness. She made herself lean back in the chair, make her shoulders loose, arrange on her face the mild boredom of a craftsman interrupted over trivialities.

"Miss Ashcombe asked me eleven questions," Edmund said. "I answered eleven questions. If the town wants to know how many chickens I keep, she'll tell them: none. Chickens require getting up early, and I get up early enough."

Crane laughed—a good laugh, warm, practiced, the laugh of a man who had learned that disarming people was cheaper than fighting them. He pulled out the stool by the second bench, the one apprentices used, and sat down without being invited. That too was a message. In this room, only two people had ever sat on that stool: Edmund's father, and Edmund herself.

"Do you know what this is?" He unfolded the paper and smoothed it against his knee.

Edmund looked. For one vertiginous half-second the room tilted, because she knew the document before she'd finished reading the first line—not this copy, but the thing itself. The indenture. Her father's careful hand, the guild stamp faded to the color of dried blood, and at the bottom, in ink she had ground and mixed herself at thirteen years old because her father liked his own formula:

*This is to certify that Edmund Thackeray, son of Josiah Thackeray, master clockmaker, is bound apprentice to the said Josiah Thackeray for the term of seven years...*

"A copy," Crane said, answering his own question. "Made from the guild archive last week. I've taken a certain interest in the Thackeray file."

"The guild keeps files on every man who's ever held a card in this county. I'm flattered mine is interesting." Edmund reached for the movement on her bench and picked up her tweezers. It was either that or let him see her hands shake, and she had ten years' practice at choosing correctly. "Seven years served, journeyman's examination passed, certificate on the wall if you'd care to look at it. It's the one behind the regulator clock, in the frame. Father had it done up proper."

"I've seen it." Crane laid the indenture flat on the corner of her bench, squaring its edges with the wood, an oddly fastidious gesture from such a heavy man. "Fine certificate. Fine signature on it too—the examiner's, old Mr. Pell, God rest him, though I'm told his eyesight was failing badly by 'eighty-four. And your father's countersignature, of course. Your father signed a great many things in his last years, didn't he? When his hands were bad. I remember—he couldn't hold a pen without the shakes by the end. Sad thing. My own mother went the same way."

The tweezers hovered over the pallet bridge. Edmund set them down.

"What is it you want, Mr. Crane?"

"Want? Nothing, yet." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and became suddenly, terribly reasonable. "I want to understand some things that puzzle me, and I find that asking directly saves everyone a great deal of unpleasantness later. Indulge an old committee man. You apprenticed in—what does it say here—'eighty-one. You'd have been, what, fourteen?"

"Fifteen."

"Fifteen. And seven years makes eighty-eight, and the examination was held that autumn, yes, I have the date here somewhere." He patted the paper as though the date might fall out of it. "So you're twenty-seven, twenty-eight now. A young man established in a good trade, owning freehold property outright—no mortgage, I checked the land registry too, forgive me, thoroughness is a disease with me—owning property, earning respectably. And yet." He held up one finger. "No wife. Not even a courtship that anyone can recall. Not a single name whispered over fences in ten years."

Edmund shrugged. "Clocks are better company than most wives. They only complain when they've cause."

"Mm." Another finger. "And no children, obviously. And—" the third finger rose, and here his voice dropped into something almost gentle, almost kind, which was the most frightening register of all— "no signature. That's the one that puzzles me most, Master Thackeray. The guild rolls. Every journeyman signs the roll when his certificate issues. Full name, in his own hand, witnessed. Old Pell insisted on it, superstitious about it, called it the whole point of the trade—a man putting his name under his work. And the roll for autumn 'eighty-eight has an entry all right. *E. Thackeray.* Initial and surname. No given name. The only entry in forty years of rolls like it."

"The roll is in the observatory office," Edmund said. "You could have asked me this by letter."

"I told you. Some matters want a face." Crane spread his hands, all innocence, all reasonableness, a man merely tidying loose threads in public view. "Perhaps it's nothing. Perhaps your father wrote your entry himself—you were grieving, I understand, the examination came barely six weeks after his death, hard timing—and perhaps he simply wrote the initial because writing the whole name cost him too much that day. Grief does strange things to a man's pen. I'd accept that explanation entirely, if you gave it to me."

*Then why ask,* Edmund thought, *if you'd accept it.*

Because he wouldn't accept it. Because every question he had asked was a stone laid in a wall, and the wall was nearly closed, and the last stone was waiting in his coat pocket next to the indenture. She understood men like Crane the way she understood mainsprings: they stored force quietly, coiled, patient, and released it all at once when the mechanism reached its appointed hour. He was not here to learn anything. He was here to watch her lie, and measure how it lay.

She gave him the explanation. She heard her own voice deliver it—steady, slightly dry, touched with just enough weariness to sound true—and it was a good performance, maybe the best of her life, and some detached part of her stood behind her own shoulder taking notes on it the way she took notes on a difficult repair. *My father signed it. I was not fit to hold a pen that month. You may verify the dates of his death yourself; I won't perform grief for your benefit.* She even produced the small roughness of genuine irritation, let it sharpen two or three syllables, watched it land. Crane nodded along, sympathetic, satisfied, a man collecting exactly what he had come to collect.

"And the wife question?" he prompted, when she paused.

"No wife. I told you."

"Never wanted one?"

"Mr. Crane." Edmund let the silence stretch until it creaked. "I buried my father, I repaired the observatory clock through two winters when the trustees' own contractor quit, and I have kept this shop solvent while half the riverfront went to the bank. If the town's concern is whether I've married, I'll thank it to concern itself instead with whether I've paid my taxes. I have. Both years."

Something flickered behind Crane's eyes—not anger. Appetite. She recognized it too late, the way one recognizes a gear meshing wrong only after the tooth has stripped: she had just shown him force, and force was what fed him. He rose from the apprentice stool, unhurried, and took up the indenture, folding it along its soft old creases.

"Well. I'm satisfied," he said warmly. "Grief and a solitary disposition—I'll note it that way. Though—" he paused at the door, hat going back onto his head, and delivered the last of it lightly, almost as an afterthought, which was how men like Crane delivered their worst blows— "the trustees are reviewing the Thackeray inheritance next month. Routine housekeeping, nothing more, a formality left over since your father's death. It would ease everyone's mind enormously, Master Thackeray, if the guild file were complete before then. Signatures and so forth. A man's name is a small thing, but it's remarkable how much trouble its absence can cause. Good day to you."

The bell rang twice as the door closed behind him.

Edmund sat very still. The workshop ticked around her—the dozen clocks on the wall, each keeping its own stubborn time, none of them agreeing perfectly with any other, the way clocks never do. She listened to them the way another person might listen to breathing.

Then her hands began to shake.

Not at once. First a fine tremor in the right, the one that had held the tweezers steady through Crane's entire performance, and then the left, and then both forearms, and she stood up because sitting still made it worse and walked the length of the shop and back, and then again, past the glass case, past the certificate in its frame with its incomplete signature, past the stool Crane had sat on. She opened the door to let the river air in and stood in it, gripping the frame, watching the water move without seeing it.

*Lies fluently,* she thought. *That's what you are now. Ten years of practice and you lie the way you file—smooth, even, no marks left behind. And afterward you shake like an apprentice.*

Her father's voice came back to her, unbidden, from the last winter of his life, when his hands had grown too unsteady for the lathe and he had begun talking to her across the bench as though dictating a will: *Keep the name, Edmund. Keep the name and the shop and the craft. The rest of it—the rest is yours to bury, and I'm sorry for it, and I'll say no more.* He had said no more. He had died saying no more, and she had spent ten years inside that silence, and today she had felt it tighten around her ribs like a spring wound past its safe limit, coil over coil, and Crane had stood there turning the key.

*A man's name is a small thing.*

He knew. She could not say how she knew, but she knew—the way she knew a movement was sick before the symptoms showed, from some accumulation of details no single detail explained. The census sheets. The missing signature. The questions arranged like teeth in a wheel. Aldous Crane suspected, and suspicion in Crane's hands did not need proof; it needed only time and a hearing, and the hearing was already scheduled.

And Lior Ashcombe's sheets were in that hearing's path. Eleven answers, precise and honest, given by a woman who believed records should tell the truth. Edmund thought of her standing in this doorway three weeks ago with her ledger and her terrible earnest eyes, and felt something twist that was not fear—or was fear, but fear braided through with something warmer and far more dangerous, because Crane had said *she tells me*, and whatever Lior had written, it had already been read by the wrong man.

Edmund went back to the bench. She picked up the tweezers. Her hands had steadied—that was the discipline her father had built into her, grief hardened into skill, trembling postponed until the work allowed it—and she seated the pallet stone, adjusted, tested the beat, listened to it catch.

The escapement ticked: release, lock, release, lock. Each tooth of the escape wheel paying out its tiny measured portion of the spring's force, the mechanism surviving precisely because it gave away its power a fragment at a time. She had built her whole life on that principle. Today, for the first time, she understood how it ended: the spring runs down, and every clock stops, and the only question is whether the maker is there to wind it again.

Outside, far off toward the trustees' hall, a church bell struck four. On the bench, the folded ghost of the indenture seemed still to lie where Crane had smoothed it, though she had put it nowhere—only in her memory, where it would stay.

Next month, the trustees would review the inheritance. Before then, a census taker's sheets would go into the public record.

Edmund reached for the loupe, screwed it to her eye, and bent over the movement as if bending low enough could hide her from the sound of the spring tightening in the dark.
