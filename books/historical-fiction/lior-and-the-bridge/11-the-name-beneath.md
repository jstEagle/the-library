## 11. The Name Beneath

The strongbox had not been opened in nine years.

Edmund knew this because she was the one who had closed it, on the morning after the funeral, when her hands were still shaking and the house smelled of lilies and boiled linen and the particular staleness of a room where a man has died. She had locked it with her father's key—her key now—and put the key at the bottom of her tool chest, beneath the pinion gauges and the jeweling set, beneath everything she touched daily, so that finding it would require intention. So that opening it would be a decision.

She had made no such decision in nine years. The box sat under the floorboard behind the lathe, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and every year she told herself she would deal with its contents, and every year the clock took precedence. There was always a clock. There had always been a clock. That was the arrangement she had made with grief: you may have my nights, my Sundays, my capacity for tenderness; in exchange I will give the hours to the work.

But Crane had come to the workshop three days ago with his copy of the apprenticeship indenture, smiling his thin smile, asking why Edmund Thackeray had never signed the guild rolls under full name, never married, never done any of the hundred small things a man does that leave a paper trail wide as a road. And he had said, in parting, *I'll want to see the original will, of course. For the audit.* As though it were a courtesy. As though he did not already know what was in it—or worse, as though he suspected and wanted to watch her face while she lied about it.

So tonight, after the shop was shuttered and the lamp turned low, after the master clock above had been wound and left ticking its slow patient count through the dark, Edmund pried up the floorboard.

The oilcloth came away stiff with cold. The strongbox was smaller than she remembered—a merchant's cash box, black iron, dented at one corner from some accident before she was born. Her hands, which could hold a balance wheel steady enough to poise it to within a grain's tolerance, shook fitting the key into the lock.

Inside: papers. A deed to the workshop. Two passbooks, their ink faded to brown. A bundle of letters tied with string—correspondence with a brass supplier in Sheffield, nothing more, she could tell by the handwriting. And beneath these, flat and official-looking, a document folded twice, sealed with wax that had cracked along one edge.

The will. Or a will. She unfolded it with the care she'd give a rusted mainspring and read it by lamplight, her lips moving slightly, the way her father used to when he read contracts aloud to himself, checking each clause against the last.

It was what she expected. It was what she had memorized years ago, standing in Proctor Wilmot's office while the old man's eyes kept sliding to her jawline, her throat, the width of her shoulders, and sliding away again with an expression she had spent a decade learning not to name. *To my child, Edmund Thackeray, whom I acknowledge as my sole apprentice and heir, I leave the premises known as the Clockmaker's Workshop, together with all tools, stock, patterns, and unfinished works therein...* Sole apprentice. Sole heir. No mention of a son or a daughter, no Christian name beyond the one she wore like a borrowed coat, no pronoun anywhere in the entire document. She had thought, at nineteen, that the omission was an oversight. Later she had understood it must have been deliberate—her father had been too precise a man, too exacting a craftsman, to leave anything ambiguous by accident.

*Whom I acknowledge as my sole apprentice and heir.* He had built the sentence like a bridge over a gap, wide enough for whatever walked across it.

She set the will down on the bench beside the lamp, next to a half-finished remontoire, and looked back into the box.

There was one thing left. An envelope, unsealed, unstamped, addressed in her father's hand—the same upright, economical script that filled his work logs—in two words only:

*My daughter.*

Her breath stopped somewhere below her ribs and stayed there.

The workshop was utterly silent except for the faint transmission of sound through the floorboards, the observatory clock counting out the seconds above her like a pulse she could feel in the soles of her feet. Ten past eleven. The river fog pressing at the windows. Somewhere a dog, far off, giving up on barking.

She picked up the letter the way she would pick up something hot.

*My daughter.*

He had written those words and then, apparently, folded the letter and put it in the strongbox and never sent it. Never given it to her. Never spoken whatever was inside. Nine years it had lain under the floorboards, six inches from where she knelt every night to wind the shop regulator, and she had never known.

For a long moment she simply held it, feeling the weight of a single sheet of paper, which is almost nothing, which is heavier than any gear train ever cast.

Then she opened it.

* * *

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead and you have finally gone looking, or you have come looking because someone is making you. Either way, forgive me the state of these pages. I have begun this letter eleven times and this is the twelfth, and precision, which has served me all my life in metal, fails me entirely here.*

*I do not know how to say what I mean to say, so I will say it plainly and let plainness stand in for eloquence.*

*I have known since you were twelve years old.*

The words swam. She read them again. *I have known since you were twelve years old.* The lamp flame leaned in a draft and righted itself. Above her, the clock counted. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen.

*You thought you hid it well. You hid it as well as anyone could. But I taught you to file brass, and a man who has spent forty years watching metal remember every stroke of the file knows what it looks like when a thing is being reshaped against its nature. I saw how you watched yourself in the shop glass. I heard you practicing your own voice in the stairwell when you believed the house was empty, lowering it note by note like tuning a bell wrong on purpose. I noticed that you stopped saying certain words. I noticed that when the neighbors' daughters came by you stood like a man being fitted for a coat he hated.*

*I said nothing. Understand why. Not from shame—I want that written clearly—not from shame, and not from disapproval. I said nothing because I was afraid. This is the confession of a coward, and I make it without excuse. I was afraid of the world outside this door, and I believed—I persuaded myself—that silence was shelter. That if we did not speak of it, it could remain a private thing, a family thing, ours. I told myself you would understand someday that everything I did not say was said anyway.*

*That was vanity. Silence is not shelter. Silence is just silence, and it leaves a person alone in a room with a locked door, guessing at what stands on the other side of it. I know you guessed. I watched you guess. Every time you brought me tea and waited, afterward, a moment too long before leaving the room—waiting, I think, for me to open the door myself—I drank the tea and let you go. God forgive me. I chose the workshop's safety over your certainty, and I called it prudence.*

*And then there was the other matter, the practical one, the one I handled the way I handle everything—by mechanism rather than courage. When I understood that the guild would never admit you, that the law would take the shop from you the hour my death was registered under your true name, I made my arrangements. The indenture names you Edmund. The will acknowledges my child Edmund, my sole apprentice, without word of daughter or son, because I wanted the paper to fit you however you needed it to fit. Let them call it a quirk of an old man's drafting. Let them puzzle over it. The documents are built to hold. I checked every clause the way I check a escapement, and they will bear your weight.*

*But hear me, and this is the whole of what I have failed to say for twenty years: the documents protect the shop. They cannot protect you. Only you can do that, and you should not have to do it alone, and if my silence has made you believe you must, then that is the worst thing I ever made, and I have made some poor work in my day.*

*You are my daughter. You were my daughter the day you were born, and the day you first held a graver, and every day since. Whatever name you wear to keep what we built, the name underneath is yours, and it is good, and I loved it before I ever knew it. If I could have found the way to say so while living, I would have spared you ten years of guessing. I could not find the way. A man can build a clock that keeps perfect time and still miss the moment entirely.*

*Finish the tower clock. Not for me—for you. And then decide what you want the rest of your life to weigh, and let no trustee, no proctor, no guild roll, and no ghost tell you otherwise.*

*Your father,*
*Josiah Thackeray*

The letter ended. The lamp burned. The clock counted.

Edmund discovered she was on her knees on the cold floorboards, the letter held in both hands at arm's length, as if it might ignite, as if it might dissolve, as if reading it a second time might undo the first. She read it a second time. Then a third, slower, her lips moving over phrases until they lost their meaning and became only shapes, only marks her father's pen had pressed into paper on some night she had slept through, believing him asleep too, both of them awake in the same dark house, separated by one floor and an ocean of unsaid things.

*I have known since you were twelve years old.*

Twelve. The year she had stood at the shop window watching the fair come upriver, and her cousin Ada had laughed and said *you look just like your mother did at that age,* and Edmund—Eleanor, then, though the name already felt like a garment worn indoors where no one could see—had felt something crack quietly and permanently, the way a pivot cracks: no noise, no visible damage, only a mechanism that would never run quite true again. The year she had begun practicing her voice in the stairwell. She had thought herself invisible. She had been seen, note by lowered note, by a man sitting below with his mending and his silence, letting her tune her own bell wrong on purpose because he could not find the courage to stop her.

Or—to be exact, because her father had prized exactness above nearly every virtue—because he had chosen a different cowardice: to let her reshape herself rather than fight the world for her. And then, having chosen it, to spend the rest of his life building her a fortress of paperwork and calling it love, and dying before he could say the word itself.

*The name underneath is yours, and it is good, and I loved it before I ever knew it.*

She did not cry gracefully. There was nothing in it of the tidy grief of novels, nothing becoming or restrained. She cried the way a mechanism fails—suddenly, totally, all the tension released at once after years of being wound. She knelt on the floor of the workshop among brass filings and oil rags and the scattered contents of a strongbox, and she wept over gears for the first time since she was a child, since the funeral, since before, and the sounds she made were ones she did not recognize as her own, neither the voice she spoke with nor the voice she had buried, but something older than both, something from before the arithmetic of survival began.

Nine years. Nine years she had believed herself alone in her knowledge, the sole keeper of the secret, carrying it the way Atlas carries—alone, and by necessity, and without witness. And all that time the proof of her father's knowledge had lain six inches beneath her feet. He had known. He had known and loved her anyway—he had loved her *because*, not despite; he had drafted his will around her truth the way he'd once cut a new escape wheel to accommodate a flawed arbor, working with the flaw, honoring it, making the whole mechanism stronger for the accommodation.

She thought of the pause that lasted too long, three weeks ago, in this very room, when the census woman had asked whether anyone else slept in the house. *No,* she had written, without looking up, and felt the decade of silence settle over her like a shroud being fitted. She had thought then that her father's silence and hers were the same silence, inherited, load-bearing.

They were not. His had been a failure of nerve. Hers—she was only beginning to see—was becoming something else, something she would have to answer for differently.

She wiped her face on her sleeve, leaving a smear of oil across her cheekbone, and laughed—one broken, wet syllable—at the sight of herself: kneeling in the wreckage of a strongbox, crying like a girl, filthy as any apprentice, holding a dead man's letter like a reliquary. Outside, the fog had thickened until the windows gave back nothing but the lamp's reflection and her own blurred shape, doubled and indistinct, which seemed suddenly less like concealment and more like company.

*Let no trustee, no proctor, no guild roll, and no ghost tell you otherwise.*

But ghosts could not stop trustees. Letters could not stop subpoenas. Crane had the indenture, and soon he would have the census sheets, and somewhere between those two stacks of paper lay the discrepancy that would unravel everything her father had engineered. The will would bear her weight—she believed that now, believed it in her bones—but only if she stood on it openly. Only if she claimed the name it protected. Otherwise it was just paper, and Crane was very good at reading paper.

She gathered the strongbox contents back with steady hands—the steadiness returning now, as it always returned, the craft reasserting itself over the flesh—and paused with the letter in her fingers.

She did not put it back in the box.

Instead she folded it along its original creases and carried it to the drawer beneath the bench where she kept her most delicate tools—her father's loupe, the burnisher with the walnut handle, the things that mattered—and laid it flat beneath the velvet lining, where she would know it was, where it could not be lost again, where it would wait for whatever came.

Then she climbed the stairs to the observatory, unlocked the tower door, and stood in the great silent room where the master clock hung in its case like a heart in a chest of oak and glass. The moonlight through the south dial fell in a pale wedge across the floor. She put her hand flat against the wooden case and felt the movement answer, tick and tock and the long slow breath of the strike train gathering itself for the hour.

Midnight came. The clock struck twelve, each note falling through the tower and out over the sleeping town, over Crane's house and the trustees' hall and the ferry landing and the census office where a certain woman was presumably asleep with her finished sheets in a locked drawer—sheets that could save Edmund or destroy her, depending on what Lior Ashcombe decided to do with the truth she had almost certainly already seen.

Edmund stood in the moonlight with her father's letter beneath the floorboards of her heart and understood, with the clarity of a mechanism finally assembled true, what she would have to do.

She could not hide in the will's careful language. She could not let Crane define her by his reading of the record. Somewhere, sooner than she wished, she would have to say the name aloud—the one beneath, the good one, the one her father had loved before he ever knew it—and let the town do what it would.

Above her head, the great dial faced the dark, and kept time, and waited.
