## 13. Interlude of Ordinary Days

Lior told herself it was diligence that kept bringing her back to the workshop.

The census was finished—every household in the town entered in her careful hand, every name accounted for except the ones she had chosen not to look at too closely—but there were always corrections to make, she reasoned, always a stray birth or a death unrecorded, and a census taker who cared about her work did not simply pack her ledger away because the season's task was done. So on the third evening after the confession at the observatory, she climbed the hill with her satchel over one shoulder and a loaf of bread from the baker's cooling shelf under her arm, and she knocked on the workshop door as though it were any other call of professional business.

Edmund opened it wearing a leather apron gone soft at the edges, brass filings glittering in the creases of her sleeves like spilled light.

"You've no reason to be here," Edmund said.

"Corrections," Lior said. "There are always corrections."

They both knew it was a lie. They let it stand anyway, the way one lets a clock run a few seconds fast because correcting it would mean stopping it entirely, and neither of them could bear to stop anything just now.

She stayed for supper. It was meant to be brief—a plate, an hour, the ledger open between them like a chaperone—but the stew Edmund made from salt pork and river onions was better than anything Lior had eaten since the ferry brought her here, and the lamp burned low and golden, and when the meal was done Edmund did not clear the table but instead drew the ledger aside and set down in its place a small brass movement, half-disassembled, its gears laid out on green felt like organs on a surgeon's tray.

"Do you know what this is?" Edmund asked.

"A clock," Lior said.

"A clock is the case, the dial, the hands people stare at while they wait for something. This—" she touched the naked movement with one fingertip, almost tenderly, "—this is what keeps time. The rest is costume."

Lior looked at the small bright parts and then at Edmund's face, lit from below by the lamp, and thought: *I have been staring at the costume.* But she said nothing. She only watched the hands move among the gears, sure and quiet, and thought that ten years of silence must teach a person to speak with their fingers, and that she could listen to this speech forever.

When she left, near eleven, Edmund stood in the doorway and said, "You'll want supper again Thursday. I buy pork on Thursdays."

It was not a question. Lior walked home through the cold with her breath smoking and caught herself smiling at nothing, at the dark windows, at the smell of coal smoke and river mud, and she thought: *This is borrowed. Every hour of it. Borrowed against a debt I can't see.*

She decided not to count the interest.

* * *

Edmund worked better those two weeks than she had worked in years.

She noticed it first in her hands—they moved without hesitation, without the small stuttering pauses where fear used to live. For a decade her craft had been a fortress: she had built walls of precision around herself, filed every surface of her life smooth so that nothing could catch on it, nothing could snag and tear and show what lay beneath. Precision as armor. Perfection as camouflage.

Now the same hands, doing the same work, moved differently. Lighter. As if some weight had been redistributed—not lifted, never lifted, but shared—and the escapement of her own body ticked more evenly for it.

She caught herself humming once. An actual tune, half-remembered, something her mother had sung before the fever took her. She stopped immediately, alarmed, listening for footsteps on the stair. There were none. She hummed it again, quietly, and filed brass to the rhythm until the candle guttered out.

Lior came Thursdays, then Sundays, then a Tuesday when the rain made the roads impassable for anyone with less excuse than a census correction. Edmund began buying pork on Tuesdays too.

On the fifth Sunday, Lior arrived carrying a book.

"It's nothing," she said, setting it on the workbench as though apologizing for it. "A novel. I found it in the ferry office left behind by some traveler. I've been reading it evenings."

"I don't read fiction," Edmund said. "Fiction is a kind of lie, and I'm short enough of honest hours."

"So am I," Lior said, "which is why I need it. A lie you know is a lie is just—rest. Read me a page and tell me it isn't restful."

Edmund wiped her hands, took the book, and read aloud. Her voice was still the low trained register she wore like a coat, but reading softened it somehow, rounded the edges, and by the middle of the page Lior had closed her eyes and Edmund had forgotten to perform the voice at all. It slipped upward without her noticing, into a range she hadn't permitted herself since she was nineteen years old, and when she reached the bottom of the page she heard where it had gone and stopped as though scalded.

"Don't stop," Lior said, eyes still closed.

Edmund looked at her—at the trust of those closed eyes in a room full of secrets—and turned the page, and let her voice stay where it wanted to be.

After that, the book lived on the workbench. Lior read while Edmund filed; Edmund read while Lior shelled peas or copied addresses into her private notebook. They got through forty pages of a story about a sea captain's widow that neither of them would ever remember the plot of, and it didn't matter. The plot was not the point. The point was the sound of a voice in the room that wasn't alone.

Once, near the end of the second week, Lior looked up from shelling peas and found Edmund watching her instead of the gear in her hand, with an expression so unguarded it hurt to witness—something raw and wondering, like a person seeing sunlight for the first time after long convalescence. Their eyes met. Neither spoke. Edmund bent back to her work, and the file whispered against brass, and outside a cart went by on the cobbles, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, which was the most extraordinary thing either of them had known in years.

* * *

The clock was nearly whole.

Edmund stood before the great mechanism in the observatory tower each morning now, checking the train of wheels, testing the gravity escapement her father had designed and she had rebuilt tooth by tooth, listening to the beat of it the way a doctor listens to a chest. *Tick. Tick.* Even. True. Two weeks ago it had faltered every fourth swing, a hesitation in the pallets she'd traced to a worn pivot, and she had re-cut the pivot herself, working three nights straight, and now it swung clean.

A fortnight more and she could deliver it. A fortnight more and the town would have its master clock, keeping time for church bells and market days and trains that hadn't come yet, and almost no one would ever know whose hands had made it true.

Almost no one. One person would know. One person knew everything now, and had not flinched, and kept coming up the hill with bread and novels and thin excuses, and Edmund found that she had built her whole life on the assumption that being known meant being destroyed, and here was the proof that she might have been wrong all along, arriving ten years too late to save her anything but these two weeks.

She did not examine this. Examining it would mean asking how long it could last, and the audit papers sat in Crane's office like a loaded spring, and the hearing date would come, and—

She picked up the file. She worked. That was the arithmetic she trusted: hours in, work out, the clock closer to done. Everything else was weather.

But in the evenings, when Lior was there, Edmund found herself doing things she had never done. Leaving the good chair for her guest. Laughing—actually laughing, twice, once until her ribs ached, at Lior's deadpan account of a farmer who had insisted his twelve chickens be counted individually by name. Telling stories about her father that were funny rather than holy, about the time he'd fallen asleep mid-argument with the mayor and woken agreeing with himself on both sides. Small excavations of a self she'd buried, each artifact passed across the table like contraband, received with both hands.

"You should have known him," Edmund said one night, and then heard what she'd said—the *should*, the grief folded inside the grammar—and went quiet.

"He sounds as though I do know him," Lior said gently. "Through you."

Edmund turned back to her work so Lior wouldn't see her face. Through me. Ten years of erasure, and here was this woman telling her she was legible—that everything Edmund thought she'd hidden was in fact a kind of inheritance, readable, alive. She filed brass until her eyes stopped stinging, and Lior, mercifully, pretended not to notice, and read aloud about the sea captain's widow standing on her empty dock.

Neither of them mentioned Crane. Neither mentioned the sheets in Lior's satchel or the will in Crane's drawer or the word *hearing*, which hung over the town like frost on a window—present everywhere, touching everything, admitted by no one. They had agreed on this without agreeing. It was the one dishonesty in the house, and they kept it carefully, the way one keeps a cracked dish at the back of the cupboard: usable, so long as no one looks directly at it.

Some nights Edmund lay awake and felt it there, waiting. Then she would get up and go downstairs and find the lamp still warm and the novel open where Lior had left it, and she would read three pages in her own voice, the real one, alone in the dark, and go back to bed.

* * *

On the last evening of the fortnight, Lior stayed later than usual. The rain had come again, a proper autumn deluge, drumming on the workshop roof like applause from a very large invisible audience, and walking home through it seemed a foolishness neither of them proposed. So Lior sat by the stove with her feet near the coals while Edmund soldered a bracket, steam rising off their wet wool in two slow ghosts, and the conversation had long since run past the point where conversations needed running.

"Lior." Edmund said it suddenly, as though the name had escaped custody. "Why census work? Truly. You fled somewhere—I know that much. What was it?"

Lior considered the fire. Outside, water sheeted off the eaves in silver threads.

"There was a man," she said at last. "In my town. He decided what everyone was. Who was respectable, who was ruined, who was lying. And he was wrong about nearly everyone, but he was loud, and loudness wins in small places. My sister believed him about herself, and it—" Lior's hands closed briefly in her lap. "It cost her. When the census job opened I took it because counting people honestly seemed like the opposite of him. A record that asks only what is true and writes it down and lets everyone be exactly as complicated as they are." She laughed, short and dry. "And then I became him. A little. I wrote things down that weren't mine to write, and someone may use them to destroy someone, and I sit here eating your stew."

"You didn't know what the sheets would become."

"I know it now. That's worse." Lior looked up. "Does it frighten you? What's coming?"

Edmund set down the soldering iron. In the pause, the rain filled the room.

"Yes," she said. "Every day. But I've been frightened for ten years, and I can report from experience that fear makes a poor companion and keeps terrible hours. Whereas you—" she stopped herself, visibly, at the edge of the sentence.

"Whereas I?" Lior asked softly.

"Keep excellent hours," Edmund said, and turned back to the bracket, ears burning, and Lior smiled at the fire and said nothing at all, and the clock upstairs ticked through the storm like a heart that refused to be troubled.

Later, at the door, Edmund handed Lior an umbrella that was really a broken tripod stand with canvas stretched over it, hideous and perfectly functional, and Lior carried it down the hill grinning like a fool.

* * *

Edmund woke before dawn on the fourteenth morning with the certainty, cold and complete, that the interlude was over.

She could not have said how she knew. Nothing had changed overnight—no letter under the door, no footsteps on the gravel. But she lay in the grey dark and felt the borrowed weeks settle around her like a garment being taken back by its owner, and she rose, and dressed, and bound herself as she always did, and climbed the tower stairs to the clock.

The mechanism waited, whole and patient, brass glowing faintly in the pre-dawn. She wound the great weights, checked the beat, wrote the day's figures in her father's old logbook in her father's old habit. Eleven days of work remained. Perhaps fewer, if the summons came early.

Down the hill, the town was beginning to smoke itself awake. She saw a figure on the river road—small, hooded against the damp, climbing toward the observatory with a satchel—and knew the walk of it before she could see the face, and felt the ridiculous lift in her chest that two weeks had taught her, dangerous as a habit.

Then, behind the figure, further down the road: another. Two men in dark coats, moving at the unhurried pace of men on official errands, turning in at the foot of the lane that led up to the workshop.

One of them carried a document case.

Edmund stood very still in the tower doorway, the wind pulling at her coat, the clock beating steadily behind her like a second conscience. Eleven days, she had calculated. The spring was releasing early.

She went down to open the door before they knocked.
