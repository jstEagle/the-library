## 14. The Subpoena

The order came on a Tuesday, folded inside an envelope that smelled of pipe smoke and legal paper, delivered to the census office by a boy who would not meet Lior's eyes.

She knew what it was before she opened it. She had known since the trustees' meeting, since the moment Aldous Crane's gaze had drifted—casual as a man checking his watch—to the folio of completed sheets resting on the table beside her, and she had seen the arithmetic move behind his eyes like gears engaging. *Household of one. Lives alone. No wife.* Eleven words in her own careful hand, and every one of them a nail.

She read the order standing at the counter while Mrs. Pellworth sorted returns behind her and the morning light came gray through the office's single window.

*COURT OF THE PROBATE DIVISION. In re the Estate of Josiah Thackeray, deceased. Whereas irregularities have been alleged...* The language was dry as August grass, but its meaning was simple: the originals were wanted. All household schedules pertaining to the Thackeray premises, Bridge Street, to be surrendered within seven days for inspection by counsel for the trustees. Failure to comply would constitute obstruction of a lawful proceeding.

Seven days. She had until the following Tuesday.

"Bad news, dear?" Mrs. Pellworth asked without looking up from her stack.

"A subpoena," Lior said. Her voice sounded like someone else's—flat, administrative, competent. "For records I hold."

"The Thackeray business." Mrs. Pellworth clucked her tongue. "Whole town's talking about it. Shame, that. Good clockmaker, whatever they say about him." She paused over a sheet, pen hovering. "You'd know better than most, I expect. You went out there twice."

Lior felt the question settle over her shoulders like a wet coat. "I conduct the enumeration," she said. "That's all."

"Mm," said Mrs. Pellworth, who had lived in Marrow's Bend for forty-one years and missed nothing worth missing.

Lior took the order back to her lodgings above the chandlery, where she sat with it spread on the small table beneath the lamp while the river fog pressed against the glass. The paper did not change. The seven days did not lengthen. Somewhere across town, in a workshop that smelled of brass and oil, Edmund was working—she could picture it exactly: sleeves rolled, the loupe screwed into her eye, those calloused hands moving with a certainty no one else in the world was permitted to see. Edmund did not know yet. Or perhaps she did; Crane was not a man to keep his ammunition quiet when displaying it might do half the work.

The order had come addressed not to the census office but to *Miss L. Ashcombe, Enumerator,* care of the district registrar. Someone had looked up precisely which clerk held the sheets. That kind of precision took time. It meant this had been coming for weeks, longer than Lior had understood, longer maybe than she had been in the town at all—or so it seemed now, looking backward through the distorting lens of everything she'd learned since.

She thought: *I could tell them.*

The thought arrived wearing reasonable clothes. Tell the registrar. Explain the conflict—that the enumerator had become personally acquainted with the subject of a contested audit, that her testimony regarding the records' accuracy ought properly to be given by someone impartial. Recuse herself. Hand the sheets over clean-handed and let whatever followed follow.

And then what? Then Crane would lay the schedule before the hearing, tap his finger against the entry—*household of one, lives alone, no wife*—and set it against the apprenticeship indenture, the guild rolls, the tax assessments, ten years of paper in which Edmund Thackeray existed as a solitary male householder, and ask the court to explain the discrepancy. And the discrepancy would be explained. Not gently. Not privately. In front of the whole town, in front of the guild examiner whose certification was the last wall between Edmund and ruin, in front of everyone.

There was another version. A version where Lior sat down tonight with fresh foolscap and copied the schedule, changing eleven words. *Household of two. Boarder present.* An error, discovered later—a clerical mistake, regrettable, common enough. Enumerators made mistakes constantly; the office was drowning in them. Who could prove otherwise? And if the duplicate were submitted in place of the original—if the original were quietly lost, misfiled, destroyed—

She got up and stood at the window with her arms wrapped around herself, watching the fog eat the far bank of the river.

Her father had been a customs officer in Hull. She had grown up watching him weigh cargo and record tonnage with a scrupulousness that bordered on religion, and once, when she was nine, she had watched him refuse a shipowner's bribe—an envelope thick enough to buy their house—and say, afterward, over supper, in the mild tone he used for weather: *A record is a promise made to people you'll never meet.* She hadn't understood then. She understood now, standing in a rented room with a court order on her table and forgery assembling itself in her mind with terrible fluency. She had already composed the altered entry. Twice. The ink was imaginary but the sentence was complete.

*Household of two.*

It would take twenty minutes. Twenty minutes of writing, and Edmund's name would be safe, and the workshop would stand, and the clock—the great unfinished clock in the observatory tower, the one Edmund spoke of the way other people spoke of prayer—would get finished, and no one would ever have to know except the two of them, carrying it between them like a coal passed hand to hand.

Twenty minutes. And then every record Lior Ashcombe touched for the rest of her life would be worth exactly what a forged thing is worth. Nothing. And every person she documented after this—every widow, every laborer, every family crowded into rooms the landlords pretended didn't exist—would be recorded by a woman who had proven, once, under pressure, that her ledger bent.

She thought of Edmund's hands. She thought of Edmund saying, in the clock tower, in the dusk: *Ten years of being accurate about everything except myself. You have no idea how heavy that is, Lior. To be precise all day long about matters that don't matter, because precision is the only place I'm allowed to live.* And she thought of Edmund saying, quieter: *Don't you dare lie for me. I've spent a decade building my life out of lies and look what it's cost. If it comes apart, let it come apart on truth. Promise me you won't forge anything. Promise me, or I can't—*

She had promised. In the tower, with the escapement ticking between them like a third heart, she had promised.

But promises made at dusk are cheap. It was promises made at midnight, alone, with a court order and a blank sheet of foolscap and a bottle of ink, that told you what you were.

* * *

On Wednesday she drafted it anyway.

She told herself she was only testing the thing—seeing whether it could be done convincingly, whether the alteration would pass. She cleared her table, laid out fresh foolscap the same weight as the office stock, ruled it to match, and began to copy the schedule in her best enumeration hand, the hand she used for official work, slightly more upright than her natural script. Name of head of household: Thackeray, Edmund. Occupation: clockmaker. She wrote the columns with mechanical care, and when she reached the line that would undo everything, she dipped her pen and hesitated only a moment before writing:

*Number of persons in household: 2.*

*Relation: boarder (male), laborer.*

It looked right. That was the horror of it—it looked entirely right, sitting there in her tidy figures, plausible as any of the three hundred schedules she had completed that autumn. A boarding laborer explained the second bed she had never officially noticed, the second cup she had never officially drunk. The lie was well-made because she had built it out of true materials: there *was* a second presence in that house, had been for weeks, a presence that slept badly and drank tea strong and laughed, rarely, in a voice Edmund probably believed no one alive had heard.

Lior set down the pen and looked at what she'd done, and felt something in her chest turn over slowly, like a mechanism seizing.

She picked the page up. The paper was still faintly damp at the columns. She held it under the lamp and examined her own handwriting as though it belonged to a stranger—as though she could catch the forger at it, confront her, demand an accounting. But the forger was her. The forger had excellent penmanship and a steady hand and had thought, even as she wrote, *boarder (male)*, about which word would survive scrutiny longest. She had committed fraud with craft. She had done it *well.*

She put the page face-down on the table and walked to the window and stood there until her legs ached.

Below, the street lamps were coming on one by one along the quay. A cart rattled past toward the ferry landing. Somewhere a dog barked itself hoarse at nothing. Ordinary evening sounds, the sound of a town going about its business, a town that would gather in the courthouse hall in five days' time to watch a woman be dismantled in public unless something intervened. Unless Lior intervened. Wasn't that the argument? Wasn't that the whole architecture of the lie she'd just built—not cowardice but rescue? What was a little false ink weighed against a human life? Her father's maxim rose up and she pushed it down. *A record is a promise made to people you'll never meet.* But Edmund wasn't a stranger. Edmund was the opposite of a stranger. Surely the rule couldn't mean she should feed Edmund to Crane's lawyers for the sake of an abstraction.

Surely.

But she remembered the feeling of writing *boarder (male)* and how easy it had been, how smooth the pen had moved, and that ease frightened her more than anything Crane could do. Because if lying were this easy tonight, it would be easier tomorrow. And the woman who lied easily was not the woman Edmund had trusted in the clock tower. She was someone else—someone softer, someone who would learn to call every betrayal mercy.

She went back to the table. She stood over the draft for a long time, her hand flat on the page, feeling the faint tooth of the paper under her palm.

Then she took it to the stove.

The chandlery below sold tallow by the barrel; her landlady kept the parlor stove fed and the grate was warm even now. Lior knelt, opened the iron door, and held the draft over the coals. For a moment she couldn't make herself let go. The firelight moved across the columns, across *Thackeray, Edmund, clockmaker*, across the two small words she had added, and the heat came up against her fingers, and she thought: *this is the last moment in which both futures are possible.* One future where she surrendered the true sheets and trusted Edmund to bear what came. One future where she became, permanently, the author of a convenient fiction.

Edmund had chosen truth in a courthouse once already, in effect—had chosen it every day for ten years by refusing the easier erasure, the quiet disappearance, the running away to some city where nobody asked questions. She had stayed. She had stayed and bound her chest and lowered her voice and answered every census question with perfect precision because staying was the honest shape of her love for her father's workshop. She would not thank Lior for saving the workshop with a lie. She had said so in almost exactly those words, lamplight on her face, exhaustion and terror in equal measure.

*I've spent a decade building my life out of lies. Let it come apart on truth.*

Lior let go.

The page caught at the corner first, curling brown, then went up all at once with a soft rushing sound, the ink flashing dark and gone. She watched the columns burn—the name, the occupation, the forged count, the relation that had never existed—watched the fire take her neat, criminal competence and reduce it to a scrap of black lace that collapsed into ash on the coals. Smoke curled up the flue. The room smelled briefly of scorched paper.

She stayed kneeling until the grate cooled enough to close, and then she closed it, and sat back on her heels on the rag rug, and found that she was crying—not loudly, not dramatically, just the steady silent leaking of someone who has finally stopped pretending a decision hasn't been made. She cried for the draft, which had been the last exit. She cried for Edmund, who deserved better than a rescuer's forged mercy. She cried a little, shamefully, for herself—for the clean career, the unblemished register, the tidy civil servant she had been six months ago on a ferry with a satchel full of blank forms, believing that accuracy was a small virtue, an easy one.

*A record is a promise made to people you'll never meet.* Yes. And Edmund was a person she *had* met, and loved, and the way to honor both facts was not to falsify the first promise but to keep it and stand beside its consequences. If the sheets condemned Edmund, then Lior would testify to what the sheets could not say: that the woman behind the name Edmund Thackeray was the finest craftsman she had ever seen, and that the town should be ashamed of itself. Testimony was not perjury. Truth, freely offered, was the one weapon Crane's paper war could not confiscate.

It was thin armor. She knew that. She put it on anyway.

* * *

Thursday morning she presented herself at the workshop with the court order in her satchel and her mind made up, and found the door unlocked and the shop empty—empty of Edmund, that is; it was full of everything else, the familiar clutter of lathes and drawers and the smell of oil that she now associated, helplessly, with safety. A note on the bench, in Edmund's compressed drafting hand: *Gone to the observatory. Back by noon. Help yourself to tea if you're here, though you always do anyway.*

Despite everything, Lior smiled. Then she sat down at the bench to wait, took out the court order, and smoothed it flat on the scarred wood where Edmund's elbows wore the grain pale, and turned it so it could be read from the stool across the bench, and waited for the door to open.

Outside, the river ran high with autumn rain. Across the roofs she could see the observatory dome, gray against the grayer sky, and she thought of the master clock waiting up there in the tower—weeks from finished, its heart half-built, keeping nothing yet, promising everything. Five days. Whatever happened next would happen fast, and loud, and in front of everyone.

She heard footsteps on the cobbles, unhurried, and the latch turning, and stood up with the paper in her hands.

"Edmund," she said, when the door opened. "We need to talk. They've served me."
