## 15. Broken Escapement

The sound woke her before she understood it.

Not a tick. Not the clean, metronomic heartbeat she had spent three months coaxing back into the master clock's chest, but something wrong—a stutter, a scrape, a note of metal dragged against metal that no healthy mechanism ever made. Edmund Thackeray lay in the narrow bed above the workshop and listened to it the way a physician listens to a failing heart, and by the second repetition she was already out from under the blankets, already reaching for her trousers in the dark, because she knew—she knew with the certainty that lived in her hands rather than her head—that something had broken in the night, and nothing broke in that clock without cause.

The workshop below was cold. October pressed against the windows like a beggar at a door. She lit the lamp on her father's old bench, turned up the wick until the flame stood tall and steady, and climbed the iron stair to the observatory floor where the great clock occupied its tower of mahogany and brass like a patient king upon a throne.

She opened the case.

For a long moment she simply looked. Her mind refused the evidence; it sorted and resorted it, hunting for an innocent explanation the way a drowning man hunts for footing. The escapement—the anchor escapement she had rebuilt tooth by tooth over six weeks, filing each pallet to tolerances finer than a hair, the work she had called, only last evening, the finest of her life—that escapement hung ruined. The left pallet arm was bent back at an angle no wear, no fatigue, no honest failure could produce. The escape wheel beneath it showed gouges across four teeth, deep crescents bitten into brass that had taken her hours apiece to polish. And there, snagged in the crutch wire, a sliver of steel that did not belong: a fragment of a cold chisel, snapped clean, its broken face bright as new coin.

Edmund took the sliver down and held it to the lamp. A quarter-inch chisel. The kind sold in every hardware stall in the market square. The kind any man might carry in his coat pocket, and only one kind of man would carry it up forty steps into a locked tower in the dark of night.

The tower door had been locked. She checked it twice, though she already knew what she would find: the lock unmarked, the hasp undamaged, the latch released with a key. There were three keys to the observatory. One hung on its hook beside her bench. One lived in the curator's drawer at the town office. And one belonged to Aldous Crane, trustee of Harrow's Bend, chairman of the audit committee, keeper of keys and of ledgers and, apparently, of chisels.

She did not weep. That surprised her afterward, when she could think about it at all. She had imagined grief arriving as it had arrived at her father's deathbed—as a flood that took the legs out from under her. Instead it came as arithmetic. She sat down on the iron stair with the broken pallet arm in her palm and counted.

Six weeks for the pallets. Four days for each wheel tooth, if the wheel itself held true—and she would have to test every spoke under glass before she trusted it again. Nine days until the hearing. The guild examiner would arrive on the morning of the eighth day to inspect the clock, and if the clock did not run—if the clock lay dead in its case like a shot bird—then Crane would not need his census sheets or his insinuations or his copy of the indenture. He would need only to point at the silent dial and let the whole town draw its conclusion: the apprentice could not finish the master's work, therefore the apprentice was a fraud, therefore the workshop passed to worthier hands.

He had not come to steal a part. He had come to steal the proof.

* * *

Morning came gray and slow over the river. Edmund worked through it without stopping, because stopping was the danger. If she sat still, if she let the lamp go out and the silence gather, then the other thought would arrive—the thought that had been circling her all night like a hawk over a field.

*Let it go.*

The thought wore reasonable clothing. It said: you are tired, you have been tired for ten years, and what is the clock to you now? The hearing will likely go against you regardless. Crane holds the ledger, the town holds the prejudice, and the examiner is one man against a roomful of trustees. Finish or don't finish—you lose either way. Pack your tools while there is still daylight to travel by. Go somewhere they have never heard the name Thackeray. Start small. Repair watches in a back room. Be nobody, safely, for whatever years remain.

It was the voice of survival, and she had obeyed that voice for a decade, and obedience to it had kept her breathing. But sitting on the stair with her father's ruin in her hands, she heard the voice clearly for what it was—not reason but habit, not wisdom but fear wearing wisdom's coat.

She tested the escape wheel under the loupe. Two teeth salvageable with careful stoning. Six needed rebuilding from stock—she had blanks in the drawer, good German brass, enough if she worked without error, which meant working without sleep, which meant—

Which meant nothing. It meant the arithmetic didn't close. Nine days minus six teeth minus two hands minus one body that had been running on will since spring. The equation failed on every side. Even her father could not have made these numbers balance, and he had balanced numbers all his life.

She put down the loupe. She went downstairs. She did not go to the bench; she went to the strongbox in the corner, the one she had opened once in August and never dared open again, because of what waited inside it.

Her hands were steady until they touched the lid. Then they shook.

The letter lay where she had left it, wrapped in oilcloth, weighted under a spare mainspring barrel. She unwrapped it with the same care she gave a movement she intended to keep running another century. Her father's hand crossed the page in strokes that sloped forward like a man leaning into wind:

*My daughter —*

*I am a coward in most things, and I will die a coward in this one, because I cannot say aloud what I am able to set down here in ink where no voice can fail me. You have taken my name and my trade and my place in the world, and I have let you do it, and I will not insult you by pretending I did not understand why, or that I have not lain awake counting what it costs you.*

*A name is a tool, Eleanor. Nothing more. It opens doors and it closes them, and a craftsman who forgets this will worship the tool instead of the work. Let them have the name. Keep the hands. The hands are the truth of you, and truth keeps better time than reputation ever did.*

*Finish the clock. Not for the town—they have not earned it. Finish it because it is yours, because the making of it is the truest sentence you will ever speak, and I want the record to show that my daughter finished what she began.*

*Forgive me the silence. I chose the shape of it badly, but I chose it so that you would have a workshop instead of a grave, and I find I cannot be sorry.*

*Your father,
Josiah Thackeray*

She read it twice. The third time she did not read it; she listened to it, the way one listens to a clock one knows by heart, hearing not the words but the cadence underneath them—the cadence of a man who had loved clumsily and too late and had known it.

*Truth keeps better time than reputation ever did.*

She folded the letter along its worn creases. Outside the window, the river ran brown and indifferent past the town that would take everything from her in nine days unless she fought. And she understood, suddenly, completely, with the clarity of a balance wheel finding its beat after long disorder, that Crane had miscalculated. He believed he had broken her clock. He believed despair was the natural product of sabotage, that a woman cornered for ten years would finally do what cornered creatures do—flee, or fold, or die quietly in some interior way that left the paperwork tidy.

But he had never watched her work. He had never seen what happened to Eleanor Thackeray when something stood between her and a finished mechanism. He knew Edmund, the quiet apprentice who answered questions precisely and signed nothing and wanted nothing visible. He did not know the girl who had taught herself to file brass at eleven years old because her father's hands shook too badly to hold the graver, and who had not stopped learning since.

Despair was a luxury. Defiance was a discipline. She had learned discipline the way other people learned prayers.

* * *

She washed her face, ate bread and cheese standing at the counter like a journeyman between tasks, and cleared the bench of everything except what the work required. Then she laid out the campaign the way her father had taught her to lay out a difficult repair: parts first, sequence second, sentiment never.

The bent pallet arm could be straightened—but no. Straightened metal remembered. It would carry the injury in its grain and fail within the year, and the clock deserved better than a healed bone set crooked. She would make a new arm from scratch. Steel rod, annealed, filed, hardened, tempered to a straw-blue, polished, fitted. Eighteen hours of work if her hands stayed true. The six damaged wheel teeth were worse; cutting new teeth into an existing wheel meant indexing the blank with absolute precision or the train would bind and she'd lose a day discovering it. She built the index plate first, from an old protractor and a scrap of boxwood, because the tools must precede the work the way the foundation precedes the house.

By afternoon the light through the observatory windows had gone the color of weak tea, and her shoulders ached in the deep muscle where ten years of bench work had settled like sediment, and she had produced exactly one annealed steel blank and a great deal of swarf. Progress measured in shavings. It was enough. It had always been enough.

She heard the footsteps on the tower stairs at dusk—she knew everyone in Harrow's Bend by their tread, and these were light, quick, uneven, pausing twice on the landings the way they always did when the climber was talking herself into the ascent.

Lior Ashcombe appeared in the doorway with her satchel still slung across her shoulder and her hair coming loose from its pins, and Edmund saw at a glance that she had run part of the way.

"I heard," Lior said. "The curator's boy told me the clock had stopped. He said—" She stopped. She was looking past Edmund at the open case, at the gutted escapement, at the index plate clamped in the vise, and Edmund watched understanding move across her face like weather. "Someone did this."

"Someone with a key." Edmund held up the chisel fragment. "Left behind. Either carelessly, or because he wanted me to know."

Lior came closer. She did not touch anything—she had learned that much about a bench in their evenings together—but she bent over the wrecked escapement with the same narrowed attention she brought to a column of figures, and Edmund felt the strangeness of being witnessed in this room, in this hour, by someone whose instinct was to look carefully rather than away.

"Nine days," Edmund said. "Eight, really, by the time the examiner arrives. I've inventoried it. New pallet arm, six teeth, full re-test of the train, regulation, and three days of running-in before I'd trust it in front of a stranger. It doesn't fit. The numbers don't close, Lior. They don't close even if I never sleep."

Lior was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the flat steadiness Edmund had come to recognize as the census-taker's professional register—except that it wasn't professional at all anymore, hadn't been for weeks, and both of them knew it.

"How many hours does it take?"

"For the whole repair? A hundred and twenty. Perhaps more. Perhaps considerably more, if anything goes wrong, and things go wrong."

"And how many waking hours do you have?"

"Lior—"

"How many?" Lior set down her satchel. She untied her cuffs, rolled her sleeves to the elbow, and looked around the tower with the expression of a woman surveying a household she intended to run whether it consented or not. "Because I make it roughly two hundred, if a certain clockmaker allows herself eight hours' rest a night, which she won't, which leaves us a surplus. I can file. I taught myself bookbinding; my hands are neater than they look. Tell me what a pallet is and I'll hold whatever needs holding."

"You'll ruin your official duties. Crane is watching you—he's watching everyone connected to me. If he learns you've been up here with lamp-oil on your fingers—"

"He already intends to subpoena my sheets, Edmund. My discretion is already spent; the only question left is what I spend it *on*." Lior said it without drama, the way one states a figure already carried forward in a sum. Then, quieter: "You told me once that a mechanism is honest even when its maker isn't. Well. I'm done being dishonest in either direction. Show me the work."

Edmund looked at her—at the ink stains on her fingers that matched his own, at the stubborn set of her jaw, at the lamplight caught in her eyes—and felt the last of the hawk-circle thought fall away like a snapped-off chisel tip dropping to the floorboards.

There was no innocent explanation left between them, and neither of them reached for one.

"The pallet arm first," Edmund said, pulling the second stool to the bench. "Watch my hands. We'll work in shifts—I sleep at two, you wake me if the lamp gutters, and God help either of us if you file past the scribe line."

Outside, the river darkened and the town lit its windows one by one, and above it all the dead clock waited in its case like a heart awaiting the surgeon's knife—while below, in the yellow circle of the lamp, two pairs of hands began the impossible arithmetic of putting it right before the town came to watch it fail.
