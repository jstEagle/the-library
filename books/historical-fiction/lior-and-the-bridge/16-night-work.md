## 16. Night Work

Lior had told herself she would only stay an hour.

The lie lasted until the lamplight caught the first broken tooth of the escapement wheel, and then it dissolved entirely, the way all her careful resolutions had been dissolving since the day a ferry carried her across the gray water into this town. She stood at Edmund's workbench with her coat still buttoned and her census satchel still slung over one shoulder—the satchel that held the originals, the ones Crane's subpoena would come for within days—and watched Edmund turn the ruined wheel in the light like a physician examining a wound.

"It wasn't wear," Edmund said. Her voice was flat in the way Lior had learned meant the opposite of calm. "Look here. The pallet faces are scored. Someone let grit into the oil—or put it there." She set the wheel down with exaggerated gentleness, the way one sets down something that might otherwise be thrown through a window. "Six weeks of work. The train leaves for the hearing in four days."

"Could you order a replacement from the city?"

"A replacement." Edmund laughed once, without humor. "For a master clock escapement? I'd have to make it. That was always the point—Father made his own wheels when no supplier could be trusted. He used to say—" She stopped. Her jaw worked.

"He used to say what?"

"That a clockmaker who can't rebuild what's broken deserves to lose the shop." Edmund picked up the wheel again, turned it, and Lior saw her decide something, saw the grief compress itself down into that familiar discipline, coal into diamond. "So I'll rebuild it. Tonight. All of it, if I have to."

"You can't cut and finish an entire escapement in one night. Even you."

"I've done worse with less." Edmund was already moving, pulling stock brass from the drawer, lighting the second lamp, dragging the small lathe from its corner with a screech of wood on floorboards. "You should go home, Lior. You have sheets to prepare. Depositions. Whatever it is they'll want from you."

It was true. It was precisely, damningly true, and every professional instinct Lior possessed rose up and said it: go home, protect yourself, keep your hands clean, surrender what you must surrender and grieve later. The census office expected the completed town ledger by week's end. Crane would expect her compliance. The law expected her to be an instrument, not a person.

She took off her coat instead. She hung it on the peg by the door, beside Edmund's worn leather apron, and rolled up her sleeves.

"What are you doing," Edmund said. Not a question. A warning.

"Teach me."

"Lior—"

"You need four hands and you have two. You haven't slept—I can see it in your face, you haven't slept properly since the part was found broken. You'll make mistakes after midnight alone. So teach me. I file documents for a living; my hands are steady and I follow instructions exactly. It's the only skill I have, but tonight it might be enough."

Edmund stared at her across the littered bench, lamp flame doubled in her eyes, and for a long moment Lior thought she would refuse—that pride or fear or both would close the workshop door between them as it had closed against the whole town for ten years. Then Edmund pulled a stool to the bench's far side and set a raw bar of brass before it.

"All right," she said quietly. "But you do exactly what I tell you. Nothing more, nothing less. Clockwork forgives almost nothing."

* * *

The first hour was failure.

Lior learned that brass fights you when you rush it. She learned that a file has teeth that cut only on the push stroke, that pressing harder doesn't press truer, that the difference between a finished surface and a ruined one is measured in strokes she couldn't count and couldn't undo. Edmund corrected her grip twice, her angle four times, her patience continuously.

"You're treating it like paper," Edmund said, standing behind her left shoulder, close enough that Lior could feel the warmth of her through the lamplit air. "Paper takes an impression. Metal takes a negotiation. Slow. Let the tool do the work. Feel when it stops arguing with you."

"And how will I know when that is?"

"You'll know." A pause. "Everything honest tells you, if you pay attention long enough."

Lior filed. The sound filled the workshop—a fine whispering rasp, rhythmic as breath—and somewhere in the middle of the second attempt she stopped counting failures and started feeling the metal instead: the way resistance softened under a correct angle, the tiny shudder of agreement when the edge came true. When she held the blank up to the lamp and Edmund examined it without touching it, and then touched it anyway, tracing the cut face with one ink-stained fingertip, and said nothing at all for three full seconds—

"That will do," Edmund said finally, and her voice had gone rough. "That will do very well."

They fell into a rhythm that felt older than either of them. Edmund worked the lathe, turning the escape wheel's new arbor, and called out measurements; Lior marked them, held the workpiece, passed tools before they were asked for. Somewhere past midnight the tea went cold in its pot and neither of them noticed. Somewhere past two o'clock Lior stopped being aware of her aching shoulders, because her hands had entered a country where there was only the next stroke, the next measurement, the next small act of precision performed side by side with another person breathing the same lamplight.

"Do you remember," Edmund said at some black hour, not looking up from the depthing tool, "when you asked me whether records tell truth? Our second visit. You were furious with me."

"You were infuriating. You answered every question about your household like a witness avoiding perjury."

"I was a witness avoiding perjury." Edmund's mouth curved faintly. "But I've thought about it since. What I wanted to say and didn't. Records don't tell truth. They tell what someone dared to write down. Truth is—" she lifted the partially assembled escapement, held it to the light, and the brass threw small gold reflections across the ceiling beams— "truth is whether the mechanism runs. Whether it keeps time when no one's watching. Everything else is testimony."

Lior thought of her sheets in the satchel by the door. Of the line she'd written in a fair hand ten weeks ago: *lives alone; no wife.* Of everything that line contained and concealed, like a seed case around a living thing.

"Then we're building testimony," she said. "Right now. This clock will say what actually happened here, long after everyone who could lie about us is dead."

Edmund looked at her then—fully, directly, in a way she almost never permitted herself—and whatever was in Lior's face made her look back down at the work quickly, as though she'd seen too much sun. "Hold this steady," she said, voice unsteady, and pressed the pallet frame into Lior's palms, and their fingers overlapped on cold brass, and stayed.

Neither moved for longer than the work required.

"The third wheel needs seating," Edmund said finally, barely above a whisper. "If we're going to finish."

"We're going to finish," Lior said. It did not feel like a statement about clocks.

* * *

At half past four the escapement hung complete in the movement, and Edmund would not test it.

She stood before the open clock case with the winding key in her hand and didn't move, and Lior understood without being told: six weeks of work had died once already at someone else's hands. This moment was the whole war. If the escapement ran, Crane's sabotage had failed. If it failed now, in front of them both—

"You do it," Edmund said suddenly, and held out the key. "I can't be the one. If it breaks I need—" She stopped, swallowed. "I need to watch it survive. Do you understand?"

Lior took the key. It was warm from Edmund's hand.

She fitted it to the great winding square and began to turn. The mainspring took up tension beneath her palm, coil after patient coil, and the whole tower seemed to lean into her effort like a living thing waking. At full wind she withdrew the key, and set her hand flat against the case, and looked at Edmund.

"Now release the escapement," Edmund whispered.

Lior tripped the retaining catch.

A hesitation—one heartbeat, two—and then the new escape wheel turned, and the pallets caught, released, caught, released, and the tick came up through the floorboards and the walls and Lior's own bones: steady, unhurried, incorruptible. Tick. Tick. Tick. The sound of something that could not be bribed or subpoenaed or burned.

Edmund made a sound Lior had never heard from her—not quite a laugh, not quite a sob—and pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth, and stood in the ringing dark of the observatory while the master clock told the hour to a town still hours from waking.

Outside, the east windows had gone from black to iron to the first thin silver of dawn. Below them the river showed pink along its far bend. A milk cart rattled somewhere down on Mill Street. Smoke rose from the bakery chimney, straight in the windless cold.

They climbed down together and walked out onto the observatory steps, and the town lay below them just beginning to stir—shutters opening, lamps going out one by one as daylight made them unnecessary. Ordinary people beginning ordinary mornings, none of them knowing that above them, in the tower, two women had spent the night building a machine that would testify on their behalf.

"We finished it," Lior said. It seemed necessary to say aloud.

"You finished most of the filing." But Edmund's shoulder was warm against hers, and her voice held none of its usual armor. "Four days until the hearing."

"I know."

"They'll take your sheets, Lior. They'll read that line aloud in front of everyone—*lives alone; no wife*—and ask you to explain it, and whatever you answer will either destroy you or destroy me, and possibly both." Edmund said it evenly, watching the smoke rise. "You could still resign. Take the morning ferry. Be gone before Crane—"

"No."

"Lior."

"I abandoned my official duties tonight to build clockwork by lamplight with a woman the trustees are trying to ruin," Lior said. "Do you imagine I'm going to run now that the hard part's done?" She turned, and dawn was coming up full behind the observatory dome, and she let herself look at Edmund the way she had wanted to look at her for weeks—openly, without pretext, without any instrument in her hands except the truth. "Whatever happens at that hearing, I'll be standing where you can see me. That's all. That's my whole testimony."

Edmund looked back at her for a long time. Down in the town, the first church bell rang the hour, and above them, in the tower, the master clock rang it too—two voices, nearly matched.

"Come inside," Edmund said at last. "I'll make tea. Real tea, hot, before you have to go surrender yourself to your paperwork. And Lior—" she paused on the threshold, hand on the door, the new escapement ticking steadily behind her like a heart that refused to stop— "whatever you wrote in that ledger, whatever they make you give them—when the moment comes in that hearing room, I've decided something. And I think you already know what it is."

Lior did already know. She followed her inside.
