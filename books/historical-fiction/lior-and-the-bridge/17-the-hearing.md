## 17. The Hearing

I did not sleep the night before the hearing. I had meant to. I had banked the workshop fire at nine o'clock as a man does when he intends to rest, and I had lain down in my father's bed in my father's room with my boots set square beside the door like two small sentinels, and I had stared at the ceiling beam where he had once carved a crude sun with a penknife and been scolded by my mother for defacing good oak. The sun was still there. It had watched over ten years of my lying still, and it watched now.

At midnight I got up and went down to the bench and took out the escapement — the new one, the one Lior and I had rebuilt by lamplight while the town slept, our four hands black with oil, our shoulders touching — and I held it to the window where the moon could find it. Brass does not lie. That is the first thing my father taught me, before I could hold a file properly. A gear is either true or it is not; a tooth either meshes or it does not; there is no almost in a train of wheels that must keep time for a hundred years. Men lie. Women lie. Institutions lie so fluently they forget they are lying. But brass — brass can only be what it has been made to be.

I polished the escapement until three, not because it needed polishing but because my hands wanted occupation more than my heart wanted courage, and then I dressed in my best coat — the grey one, brushed, the collar turned down straight — and I bound myself one last time in the manner of Edmund Thackeray, and I looked into the small mirror above the washstand and practiced being him.

*Edmund Thackeray, journeyman clockmaker.* The mouth made the shapes easily; the mouth had made them ten thousand times. But something behind the eyes would not cooperate this morning. Something behind the eyes kept saying: *this is the last time you will be asked, and you will be asked under oath.*

The observatory clock struck seven from across the river, its voice carrying over the water the way it had carried for sixty years, through my father's apprenticeship and mine, through frost and flood and the year of the fever. I had promised that clock I would finish it. Whatever happened in the trustees' hall, the clock was finished. Whatever else they took from me, they could not take the work back out of the tower. A thing truly made belongs to itself.

Lior was waiting at the corner by the chandler's shop. She wore her census office coat, the plain dark one, and she stood with her hands clasped behind her back like a woman guarding something fragile against her own spine. When she saw me she did not smile. We had agreed on no words, and we needed none. She fell into step beside me and matched my pace exactly, neither leading nor following, and I understood that this was her way of holding my hand in public: to walk beside me at the same speed, as if we were one mechanism moving on a single train.

"You slept," she said. It wasn't a question, quite.

"I worked."

"Good." A pause of four steps. "Eleanor."

My own name, spoken aloud on an open street in morning light, went through me like a current through a wire. Ten years, and only she had ever said it. Only she had ever known there was anything to say. I kept walking. "If I falter in there," I said, "don't look at me with pity. Look at me the way you look at the clock. Like something that works."

"That's how I've always looked at you," she said.

We crossed the bridge.

* * *

The trustees' hall had been a grain exchange once, before the river trade thinned, and it still smelled faintly of dust and old wheat and the particular staleness of rooms where men make decisions about other people's lives without ever having to live inside them. Seven chairs on the dais, six occupied. A clerk's table. A rail. Benches for the public, half full — word travels fast in a dying town, faster than the ferry, faster than gossip deserves, because there is so little else moving.

Aldous Crane sat at the center of the dais like a man who had measured the room and found himself proportionate to it. He wore black broadcloth and a watch chain of heavy gold, and I knew that watch — everyone in town knew it — a repeater, Swiss, striking the quarters with a sound like a tiny bell being strangled. He had bought it in the city. He had never once taken it to be cleaned. I could tell by the drag in its tick when he'd wound it near me at the guild supper years ago, the hesitation of a movement running dry, and I had thought then, with the uncharitable precision of craft: *there is a man who owns time and cannot keep it.*

On the table before him, squared to the edge, lay a single sheet of paper.

I knew it before I saw the header. I knew it by its fold, by the way the light caught the iron-gall ink. Census schedule, 1890, household of E. Thackeray, clockmaker, premises adjoining the old observatory road. *Lives alone. No wife.* My handwriting answering his questions; his hand recording my answers. The document that had begun as a stranger's polite intrusion and become, over suppers and arguments and one long night of lamplight and gears, the strangest courtship I have ever heard of — and now lay flattened under Crane's palm like a specimen pinned to cork.

The chairman — old Mr. Halloway, whose gout I had once treated with a clockwork foot-warmer he never knew I built — called the session to order. Crane rose.

He did not rush. That was his gift, if you could call it one. He let the silence do half his labor, standing there with the paper in his hand while the room settled into the particular hush of people preparing to enjoy something shameful. Then he read aloud.

"'Household of Edmund Thackeray. One occupant. Lives alone.'" He lowered the sheet. "Gentlemen. Ten years ago this board ratified the transfer of the Thackeray workshop and its guild standing to the son of the late Josiah Thackeray, upon evidence of a lawful apprenticeship and a will of record. Since that time, questions have arisen. Irregularities." He pronounced the word carefully, savoring its Latin bones. "The apprentice indenture bears a name. The guild rolls bear a signature. Neither name appears in any parish register of baptisms in this county. No schoolmaster remembers him. No physician attended his childhood. And the census taker of this very year—" a gesture toward the benches, and every head turned toward Lior, and I felt her go rigid beside me like a mainspring at full wind— "recorded this household with her own hand. One occupant. Alone. Yet witnesses will attest to a woman seen entering these premises after dark. Witnesses will attest to voices. To two teacups, gentlemen, on the sill of a house where one man officially lives alone."

He turned to me. "Mr. Thackeray. Will you account for this discrepancy?"

* * *

Here is what I had prepared.

I had prepared it the way I prepare everything: completely, in advance, with tolerances measured. If pressed on the parish registers, I would say my mother bore me at her sister's house in Harrowgate and the curate there drank. If pressed on the woman, I would say my late father's cousin comes to cook and mend and prefers her privacy, as do I. If pressed on the census, I would say the census taker came twice and confused herself, and I am a man of few words and fewer corrections. Three lies, fitted together like wheels in a train, each driving the next, smooth enough that no one would feel the mesh.

I had rehearsed them in the mirror at dawn. My mouth had made the shapes easily. The mouth had made them ten thousand times.

But lying, I have learned, is a mechanism too, and every mechanism wears. Ten years of binding my chest before dawn. Ten years of lowering my voice into the register my father coached me toward, syllable by syllable, the way he taught me to true a wheel — remove only what must be removed, and stop. Ten years of signing *Edmund* with a small deliberate flourish to disguise the hand, of flinching from my own reflection, of letting the whole world call me by a dead boy's borrowed name and thanking them for it. The mainspring was wound to its limit. I could feel it in my wrists, in my teeth, in the place behind my sternum where my heart worked double-time inside the bindings like a balance wheel beating against a case too tight for it.

Crane waited. The room waited. Somewhere in the rafters a pigeon shifted its weight, and the sound came down soft as falling flour.

I looked at the census sheet in his hand. *Lives alone. No wife.* Both statements false, both written in my voice, recorded by the one person in this hall who knew precisely how false and loved me anyway — who had lain awake, I knew, drafting falsifications and burning them, choosing the crime or the truth and finding both unbearable, and who had come here this morning anyway, unarmed, in her official coat, to stand beside whatever I decided.

That was the thing. That was the tooth that finally meshed. Everyone in this room believed the record was the truth and I was the fraud. They had it backward. The record had always been the fraud — a decade of forged entries in the ledger of myself, each one countersigned by fear. And the truth had been standing in this town the entire time, filing brass, winding the master clock, keeping the observatory's heart beating, wearing my father's name like a case worn hollow from the inside.

Brass does not lie. I had spent ten years trying to prove my father's oldest lesson wrong, and the mechanism had finally, inevitably, run down.

"My lord chairman." My voice came out in Edmund's register, steady, and I let it, one last time, the way a man lets a worn-out tool make its final cut. "You ask me to account for a discrepancy. I will account for it."

Halloway nodded. Crane allowed himself the smallest adjustment of his cuffs, the gesture of a man closing his watch case over a finished movement.

"The discrepancy," I said, "is not between the census and the truth. It is between the truth and everything else I have ever signed."

A murmur moved along the benches like wind through wheat. Crane's hand stilled on his cuff.

"You want the parish records. There are none, because no parish baptized me under the name Edmund Thackeray, because no such child was born. You want the schoolmaster. There is no schoolmaster, because no such boy was schooled. You want to know why I never married, never signed the guild rolls in full, never stood for the portrait the guild commissioned in '88." I unbuttoned my coat. Not dramatically — I am not a dramatic person; I am a precise person — but because my body had decided, ahead of my mind, that the bindings were finished. "I can tell you why. I can tell you in one sentence, and then this board may do what it likes with the workshop, and the law may do what it likes with me, and I will say nothing further to defend what was never real."

I looked past Crane, past the dais, to the benches, and found Lior's eyes. She did not look at me with pity. She looked at me the way she looks at the clock.

"My name," I said, and the bindings broke, and the voice that came out of me was the voice I had buried at nineteen beneath my father's coffin — higher, lighter, unmistakably a woman's, ringing off the grain-exchange rafters with a clarity no disguise had ever permitted it, "was Eleanor Thackeray. I am Josiah Thackeray's daughter. I served the apprenticeship he drew up for his son, because there was no son. Every gear in that observatory tower was cut, filed, and fitted by my hands. I am the clockmaker of this town. I have been its clockmaker for ten years under a stolen name, and I am done stealing."

The silence afterward was total. Even the pigeon held still.

Then Crane said, softly, silkily, the way a man speaks when the trap has closed exactly as designed: "Madam. You confess, before this board, to a decade of fraud against the guild and the estate of your late father. You understand what follows."

"I understand," said Eleanor Thackeray, "precisely what follows. I have understood since I was nineteen years old. What I did not understand until this moment is that I would rather lose everything under my own name than keep everything under his."

She turned from the dais — I turned from the dais — and walked down the aisle with my spine straight and my freed ribs aching with breath that was suddenly, extravagantly, mine, past the benches where the town that had bought my clocks and eaten my suppers stared at me as at a ghost walking, and out into the white morning light where Lior Ashcombe was already rising to follow.

Behind us, the chairman's gavel fell like the first stroke of a hour being struck.

And somewhere across the river, indifferent to verdicts, the master clock began to count it.
