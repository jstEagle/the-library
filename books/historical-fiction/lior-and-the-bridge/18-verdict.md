## 18. Verdict

The courthouse had never been built for a crowd.

It was a squat brick building on Cordwainer Lane, raised in the town's brief prosperous decade and never enlarged, with a hearing room meant for a dozen trustees and perhaps twenty spectators. By nine o'clock that morning, the corridor outside held better than two hundred people, and by half past, the heat of their bodies had fogged the tall windows until the river beyond was only a gray suggestion. Lior stood pressed against the back wall with her notebook in one hand and her other hand curled so tightly around the pencil that she could feel the hexagonal facets printing themselves into her palm.

She had not slept. She suspected no one in this room had.

The trustees sat at a curved table beneath the county seal: five men, four of them farmers or merchants she'd counted in her rounds—Hobbs of the feed store, Merrick of the grain elevator, old Prewitt who'd answered her questions through a door he never fully opened. And at the center, Aldous Crane, whose collar was whiter than anyone else's in the room and whose face wore the particular serenity of a man who has already counted his winnings.

Eleanor Thackeray sat alone at a small table facing them, her back to the room.

Lior had watched her walk up the courthouse steps an hour ago and had understood, watching, that she was seeing something she would remember for the rest of her life. Eleanor had dressed as Edmund had always dressed—the dark coat, the plain cravat, the boots scuffed at the toe from years at the lathe—but she wore them differently now. Loosely. Like a coat taken off and put back on for warmth rather than disguise. Her hair was bound at her nape without apology. She had signed the witness roll that morning in full view of the clerk: *Eleanor Thackeray*, the letters steady, neither hurried nor performed.

The room had made a sound when she did it. Not a gasp exactly. Something more like the settling of a house in wind—a collective shifting, hundreds of small recalibrations happening at once. A woman near Lior had whispered *that's the clockmaker* with the tone of someone identifying a ghost.

Now Judge Halloway—not a judge in any formal sense, but the county magistrate who presided over trust matters, a tired man with ink on his cuff and no appetite for what had been brought to his courtroom—struck the table twice with a small wooden mallet that seemed almost apologetic about its work.

"This hearing is convened," he said. "Matter before the board is the estate audit initiated by trustee Crane concerning the Thackeray workshop and apprenticeship. Mr. Crane, you have the floor."

Crane rose without hurry. He had documents. Of course he had documents; Lior had watched him amass them for weeks the way silt accumulates in a harbor—quietly, continuously, from sources she hadn't always been able to name. She knew one of those sources now. She had known it since the subpoena arrived at the census office ten days ago, since the morning she had stood in her supervisor's back room holding a court order that demanded the original household sheets for the Thackeray district, hers among them, in Crane's own careful hand.

Her sheet was lying on the trustees' table now, in a leather folder, and looking at it made her stomach turn over slowly, like something drowning.

"Members of the board." Crane's voice carried easily; he'd been speaking in rooms like this all his life. "I will be brief, because the facts are brief. Ten years ago, Josiah Thackeray died leaving his workshop, his tools, his guild standing, and his pending commission—the observatory master clock—to his apprentice. That inheritance was lawful under the terms of his will, which named his apprentice, Edmund Thackeray, as sole heir."

He let the name hang there.

"The person seated in this room today," Crane continued, turning slightly, presenting Eleanor to the board as though she were an exhibit, "has testified under oath—and I invite the board to consult the transcript—that her true name is Eleanor Thackeray, daughter of the deceased. That she was never apprenticed. That the indenture filed with the guild bears a signature obtained under false pretenses. That for ten years she has traded upon her father's grief and his dying wishes to hold property, income, and professional standing to which she had no legal claim whatsoever."

He opened the folder. He did not read from the census sheet aloud—he didn't need to; half the town had already heard the contents retold in three versions by Sunday—but he laid it flat where the board could see it, and where Lior, from the back wall, could see it too. Her own handwriting. *Occupation: clockmaker. Lives alone.* The little ruled boxes of her methodical, faithful, catastrophic diligence.

Lior had spent ten days hating that page. She had spent one terrible night drafting a duplicate with a single altered line, holding the pen over the paper until the nib had bent against her hesitation, and then she had burned the draft in the office stove and sat on the cold floor afterward and understood that whatever happened in this room, she would not purchase Eleanor's safety with her own corruption. It was not a noble understanding. It had felt, at the time, like watching the last door close. But it had been hers, and she had made it awake.

"The law," Crane was saying, "does not concern itself with sympathy. The will named an apprentice. There was no apprentice. The inheritance fails, the estate reverts, and the board must void the transfer. I move for judgment."

He sat down. Somewhere in the crowd a man said *amen* under his breath, and someone else hissed at him to shut up.

* * *

Halloway turned to Eleanor. "Madam—you'll respond as you see fit. Take your time."

Lior watched her rise. Watched her smooth her coat front in a gesture Lior had seen a hundred times at the workbench, the unconscious motion of a woman checking that her tools were where she'd left them. When she spoke, her voice was low and even, and it reached the back of the room anyway, the way certain voices do—not loud, but load-bearing.

"I don't dispute anything Mr. Crane has said," Eleanor began, and the room rippled. "The will named my father's apprentice. There was no apprentice. There was only his daughter, wearing his son's clothes, because in 1883 a girl could inherit a name in this town but not a trade."

"Madam, the legal question—"

"With respect," Eleanor said, and something in her voice made even Halloway stop, "the legal question is the only question you're permitted to ask me, and I've answered it. Void the inheritance if you must. I won't argue it. But before you do, I want the record to show what it is you're voiding, because Mr. Crane has been very careful to call it fraud, and fraud implies I took something that wasn't earned."

She turned then—not to the board, but to the room, to the two hundred faces—and Lior saw that her eyes went briefly, deliberately, to the back wall. To her. One heartbeat of contact, no longer. Enough.

"My father died in March of '83, with the observatory commission six months begun and no son to finish it. He asked me to keep the shop. I kept it. For ten years I have repaired every clock in this valley that nobody else would touch—I have the ledgers, you may read them, every entry in my hand. I rebuilt the harbor pilot clock after the flood of '86. I serviced forty-one watches last winter for families in this room. And I finished the master clock." Her voice did not rise, but it gathered, the way weight gathers on a wheel. "It ran its first full month without deviation three weeks ago. Its rate is within two seconds a fortnight. You are welcome to go up that hill and listen to it, because it is keeping your town's time right now, this minute, while we sit here, and it does not care what name you give the hands that built it."

Silence. The kind of silence that has texture.

"So take the workshop," Eleanor said. "Take the building and the benches and the sign. I won't beg for bricks." She paused, and Lior saw her hands close once at her sides and open again, deliberately relaxed. "But write into your minutes what actually happened here, because history is also a ledger, gentlemen, and someday someone will read yours."

She sat down.

* * *

The vote was not suspenseful. Hobbs and Merrick voted with Crane; Prewitt abstained with visible discomfort; the fifth trustee, a dry, precise man named Voss whom Lior had never managed to interview, voted against, saying only, "The work speaks for somebody," before falling silent under Crane's glance. Four votes of effect to one of conscience. Halloway read the determination in the flat voice of a man reading someone else's grocery list: the Thackeray inheritance was voided; title to the workshop premises, tools, and goodwill passed to the trust, of which Aldous Crane was senior trustee; possession to be surrendered within thirty days.

The gavel came down.

That was the sound Lior would keep—the sound itself, small and woody and final, a sound no bigger than a knuckle rapped on a door, carrying ten years of a woman's life inside it. Around her the room exhaled into murmur. Someone near the door began to cry quietly. Two of the watchmaker's customers—the Kessler brothers, whose father's chronometer Eleanor had restored the previous spring—stood up and removed their hats, which made no parliamentary sense whatsoever and which everyone present understood perfectly.

And Crane was already gathering his papers with the satisfaction of a man closing a ledger column that balanced.

Then the doors at the far end of the hearing room opened.

The man who entered was sixty-odd, travel-worn, carrying a black valise and an air of having come a long way specifically to be unimpressed. Behind him, absurdly, came the observatory's young caretaker, breathless, still in his shirtsleeves, as though he had run the whole distance up from the hill.

Halloway frowned. "This session is concluded. State your business."

"Guild business." The stranger set his valise on the front table without waiting for permission. "Samuel Orenstein, examining officer, Worshipful Company of Horologists, second district. I'm told there's a certification matter outstanding in this jurisdiction." He looked across the room, found Eleanor, and inclined his head with the exact courtesy of one craftsman to another. "Miss Thackeray. Your caretaker wired me Tuesday that your master clock had completed its proving period. I'd have come sooner, but the river road is flooded past Marbury."

"You—" Halloway began.

"I attended the hearing from the corridor," Orenstein said placidly. "All of it. I heard the lady state her rate figures. I have spent the last two days with that clock, gentlemen, because I was already in the valley surveying another commission, and I'll tell you what I found, and then I'll tell you what I intend to do about it."

He opened the valise and produced instruments—a vernier gauge, a pocket chronometer of serious ancestry, a folio of notes in a hand like engraving.

"That movement is the finest piece of provincial horology I have inspected in nineteen years of service to the Company. The escapement is original in design—her design; I checked the drawings against the bench marks, they're hers, drawn at fifteen years of age if the dating papers are honest, and they are. The compensation curve in the pendulum solves a problem the Company has been arguing about in London for a decade. The finishing is beyond reproach. The clock keeps time to within two seconds a fortnight, which is what she claimed, and which I have verified independently, because I assumed she was boasting." He allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "She was understating."

The room had gone very still again, but it was a different stillness now—the stillness of people realizing the ground has shifted under them mid-step.

"Now." Orenstein closed the folio. "The Company certifies journeymen, not heirs. We certify demonstrated competence before a qualified examiner, under whatever name the candidate presents. The candidate before me presents as Eleanor Thackeray, and her work presents as first-rate. So." He looked at Halloway, then at the board, and his voice acquired, for the first time, an edge fine enough to draw blood. "If anyone in this room wishes to argue that the daughter of Josiah Thackeray lacks the standing to sit an examination, I invite you to make that argument to the Company directly, in writing, with your name attached. Otherwise—Miss Thackeray. You've already sat the practical; I've watched your bench work these two days. I have one further oral examination, standard form, and I propose we conduct it here and now, in front of these good people, so that no one leaves this room confused about what was decided today."

He looked at Eleanor. "Do you consent?"

Lior saw her stand. Saw the pause—three seconds, four—while Eleanor looked at the trustees' table, at the census folder, at Crane, whose composure had developed its first hairline fracture and was now visibly propagating.

"I consent," Eleanor said.

What followed took forty minutes and felt, to Lior, like watching a lock being picked from the inside. Orenstein walked her through train calculations and lubrication theory, through the behavior of metals under seasonal humidity, through the ethics of a repairman who discovers a colleague's concealed fault in a customer's watch. Eleanor answered everything—not fluently, not glibly, but with the deep unhurried precision of a woman who had lived inside these questions for ten years. Twice she corrected the examiner's terminology. Once she said, "I don't know," and Orenstein nodded as though that answer had been worth more than the others.

At the end he closed his folio, stood, and addressed the room.

"By authority of the Worshipful Company of Horologists, second district, I hereby certify Eleanor Thackeray as a journeyman clockmaker. First of her name to hold the certificate. The record will show it was granted in the town of Harrow's Bend, this day, following public examination." He produced a document, heavy cream paper with a seal in red wax, and set it on the table before her. "Sign the roll, Miss Thackeray."

Eleanor signed. *Eleanor Thackeray*, in the same steady letters as the witness roll that morning, and this time the sound the room made was not a settling—it was applause, starting ragged somewhere near the Kessler brothers and spreading like fire through dry grass, until even Halloway was striking his useless little mallet and failing entirely to suppress it.

Lior stood against the back wall with tears running freely down her face and her pencil forgotten, applauding until her palms burned.

* * *

Afterward, in the corridor, amid the press of bodies and the extraordinary noise of a town rearranging its opinion of itself in real time, Crane emerged from the hearing room with his folder under his arm and his face composed into something almost gracious.

"Congratulations on your certificate," he said to Eleanor, loudly enough for the corridor to hear. "You'll find the trust a reasonable landlord, should you wish to remain as tenant of the shop. Reasonable terms." A pause, weighted. "Assuming satisfactory conduct."

It was a kindness shaped exactly like a leash, and everyone heard it.

Eleanor looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached into her breast pocket and drew out a key—the workshop key, brass worn bright at the bow from ten years of her thumb—and held it out to him on her open palm.

"The building is yours, Mr. Crane. As agreed." Her voice was pleasant, almost light. "As for satisfactory conduct—I'd reflect on what happened in that room. You brought a hundred witnesses to my exposure, and every one of them watched the Company certify me instead. You might ask yourself whether the same arithmetic works in reverse." She closed his fingers over the key with both of her hands, gently, the way she handled a finished movement being returned to its case. "Good day."

Crane stood holding the key as though it had changed temperature in his hand.

Orenstein appeared at Eleanor's elbow then, valise recovered, and said something too quiet for the crowd; Lior caught only the words *London* and *the autumn examinations* and *there are workshops*, and watched Eleanor's face perform a complicated negotiation between hope and disbelief. Then the examiner tipped his hat to the room at large, announced that he had a coach to Marbury before dark, and departed in a wake of parted bodies, leaving Eleanor standing in the corridor holding a certificate and no longer holding a trade.

Lior pushed through the crowd to her.

For a moment they simply looked at each other, there under the fogged windows, with the whole town flowing around them like water around two stones.

"You knew," Eleanor said finally. "About Orenstein. The caretaker wired him—but you knew he was in the valley. You sent him."

"I wrote to the Company three weeks ago," Lior admitted. "When I saw the master clock finish its proving period. I told them there was work here worth an examiner's attention. I didn't know if anyone would come." She swallowed. "I couldn't give you the workshop, Eleanor. The law was going to take it no matter what I did, and I wouldn't lie on the record to stop it—I couldn't do that to either of us. But a certificate isn't a lie. It's the truth, certified. It's the one thing in this whole affair that nobody can ever void."

Eleanor looked down at the document in her hands, then out the window, where the fog was lifting off the river and the observatory hill stood clear above the rooftops, its dial catching the first clean light of afternoon.

"They gave you nothing," she said softly. "They took everything, and gave you nothing, and you're smiling."

"I'm not smiling because of them." Lior reached out and straightened Eleanor's collar, a small domestic gesture, absurd in a courthouse corridor, witnessed by half the town, regretted by neither of them. "Pack your tools tonight. Both of us. We're leaving on the morning boat."

Eleanor stared at her. "Where?"

Lior smiled properly then—the smile of a woman who has finally stopped auditing herself.

"Somewhere they've never heard of either of our names," she said. "We'll get to choose what they hear first."
