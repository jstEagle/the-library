## 19. Emptying the Workshop

The workshop had never seemed large until she had to empty it.

Eleanor stood in the doorway that first morning after the verdict, a crate at her feet and a roll of straw in her hands, and looked at ten years of her father's life and five of her own arranged along the walls like sentences in a language only two people had ever spoken fluently. The lathe under its leather cover. The bench with its row of pin vises hanging from nails her father had driven before she was born. The window over the polishing wheel, where the light came down amber through the old glass and turned everything it touched—the brass swarf, the oilstone, the dust of years—into something almost precious.

She had thought she would be angry. She had prepared herself for anger the way one prepares for weather, laying in stores of it against the long winter ahead. But standing there with an empty crate and a full room, what she felt was closer to tenderness, and that frightened her more.

*They took it anyway,* said a small voice inside her. *Let them have the walls. They cannot have the hands.*

She began with the wall of drawers.

Each drawer held its own small civilization. Number 1: files, sorted by cut, each handle wrapped in cord where the wood had split. Number 2: taps and dies, oiled and wrapped in waxed paper against rust. Number 7—she paused with her hand on the brass pull—number 7 held the pivots, hundreds of them, steel rods fine as grass stems, and beneath them, flat as a pressed flower, the practice piece she'd made at eleven: a tiny wheel with forty-eight teeth cut by hand, three of them wrong. Her father had kept it all these years. He had never told her he kept it.

She set it on the bench rather than in the crate. Some things deserved to be carried where she could see them.

The work went slowly because she made it go slowly. There was no sense rushing; the trustees had given her a fortnight to clear the premises, and Crane's man had already come by twice to stand in the doorway with his ledger and his little smile, counting what remained as though inventory were victory. Let him count. Let him learn, if he could, which of these objects were tools and which were relics, and how little difference there was between them.

By the third day she had filled six crates and uncovered, behind the great cabinet where the heavy stock lived, the strongbox.

She had not opened it since the night she'd found the letter. She had meant to take it with her whole, but now, kneeling in the cold corner with the draft moving across her neck like a hand, she thought: *no.* A box carried unopened is a debt carried unpaid. She lifted it to the bench and worked the lock with the key from her watch chain, and the lid came up on the smell of iron and old paper.

Her father's letter lay where she had left it, folded around itself like a sleeping bird.

*To my daughter, if she ever has cause to read this—*

She did not unfold it fully. She knew every line. She knew the way his handwriting slanted when he wrote quickly, knew the ink blot near the middle where his pen had hesitated too long, knew the last sentence better than any scripture ever composed by any faith: *I would rather lose the shop than the truth of you, but I am a coward, and so I leave you both, and ask forgiveness of whichever you choose to keep.*

Ten years she had chosen the shop. Ten years binding and buttoning and lowering her voice into its false cellar. And the shop had been taken from her anyway, in a single afternoon, by men who had never wound a clock in their lives—and she was still here, still breathing, still *her*, whatever that word had cost. The arithmetic she had built her life upon did not balance, and for the first time she saw that this was not failure. It was freedom of a terrible kind.

She folded the letter closed and set it aside.

Then she took a fresh sheet of paper from the drawer where her father had kept his order books, and she sat down at his bench, in his chair, worn to the shape of a body not quite hers, and she began to write.

*To whoever comes next—*

The pen stopped. Who would come next? Crane would install some clerk or cousin, someone who would keep books and call it craft, or perhaps no one would come next at all and the shop would stand empty until the roof went. It didn't matter. That was the strange discovery of the morning: it did not matter who read it. Letters like this one were not addressed to their readers. They were addressed to time.

*You will find this shop smaller than its reputation,* she wrote. *That is true of most things worth having.*

*The lathe sticks in damp weather; ease the tailstock with oil and patience, never force. The second drawer on the left of the bench is false-bottomed—I removed the bottom myself at fourteen to hide letters I was ashamed of, and I am telling you this because shame hidden becomes furniture. Check your own drawers.*

*The master clock upstairs wants winding every seven days without fail. It does not forgive neglect, and neither should you. Its escapement is my design, corrected four times, and it will keep time to within a minute a month if treated honestly. I say "my" without apology. Whatever else was decided about me in the meeting hall, the clock knows whose hands made it.*

*If you are reading this because you have been told the story—that the clockmaker Thackeray was a woman, that she signed her name in public and lost everything—consider what was actually lost. Walls. Consider what was kept. The work. The name. The leaving.*

*I do not know who you are. Perhaps you are a girl like the one I was, reading by lamplight, certain that no life exists for you beyond the one prescribed. I was certain of it for twenty years. I want you to know that certainty can be wrong. It can be wrong for decades and then, in a single season, entirely wrong.*

*Keep the name that keeps you.*

She signed it—not Edmund, not Eleanor, but both, one above the other, the way a clock strikes the hour twice to be sure it is heard:

*Eleanor Thackeray, journeyman*
*(known in this town as Edmund, and glad, finally, to be done with him)*

She folded it around her father's unsent letter, the two of them nested together like gears in mesh, and returned them to the strongbox, and pushed the strongbox back into the dark behind the cabinet. Let Crane find them or let him not. Some inheritances could not be voided by any board of trustees.

* * *

The crates multiplied through the week. Seven, then nine, then twelve, stacked by the door like a family waiting for a coach that might never come. Word got around the town in the way words do, and people began to appear at the workshop door with offerings that were unmistakably apologies wearing the clothes of errands.

Mrs. Halloway from the bakery brought a loaf and stood turning her hat in her hands while she said, "My grandmother's mantel clock, it hasn't run since '81, and I always meant—" and couldn't finish, and Eleanor took the clock and said, "Bring it Thursday," because Thursday she would be gone and they both understood the kindness of an appointment that would never be kept.

Old Tam, the ferryman who had carried Lior across the river that first autumn morning, brought a jar of pickled eels ("Your father's favorite, though he pretended otherwise") and stayed an hour talking about nothing, which was his way of talking about everything. He was the only one who used her new name aloud, awkwardly at first, then easier, saying it three times in a quarter hour as though practicing a foreign phrase he intended to keep.

And once, at dusk, she looked up from the bench to find a boy of perhaps fifteen standing in the doorway—Crane's clerk's son, she realized, the boy who swept the meeting hall—with his cap in his fists.

"They're saying you finished the master clock," he said. "They're saying it keeps time better than anything in the county."

"It does."

"Could a person—" He stopped. Started again. "Is it something a person could learn? Without being born to it?"

Eleanor wiped her hands and considered him: the grease already permanent in the creases of his knuckles, the hungry way his eyes moved across the walls of tools, the particular stillness of a body trying not to hope visibly.

"A person could learn," she said. "Come Thursday evening. I'll show you the escapement before I go."

He nodded too many times and fled. She sat back down and looked at her hands, and understood that she had just done the only thing available to a craftsman who loses her shop: she had planted the craft somewhere it could outlive the ground.

* * *

Lior came on the sixth day.

Eleanor heard the ferry first—the change in its engine note as it swung toward the landing—and then footsteps on the lane, unhurried, and then the door opening without a knock, because knocking between them had died sometime during the night they rebuilt the escapement, when every other formality had burned away with the lamp oil.

"You've been crying," Lior said. Not a question.

"Packing." Eleanor gestured at the crates. "Packing is crying with your hands full."

Lior set down the satchel she carried and looked around the workshop with an expression Eleanor had learned to read over these months: the census-taker's face, recording everything, judging nothing. Her gaze traveled the tool wall, the bench, the strongbox corner, the stair to the tower, cataloguing a place she had entered as a stranger with a clipboard and was leaving as—what? Eleanor still had no word for it. *Companion* was too thin. *Colleague* too cold. The truth was a thing they had circled for months without landing on, the way a moth circles a flame without choosing the burning.

"I brought something," Lior said, and her voice did something Eleanor had never heard it do—it faltered. Lior Ashcombe, who could recite regulations from memory and stare down trustee Aldous Crane without blinking, faltered over a satchel.

She drew out an envelope and held it a moment before offering it, the way one holds something hot.

Inside were two train tickets. Eleanor read them twice. Wednesday morning, the eight-forty eastbound. Two seats, purchased together, side by side, all the way to the city—a word that in this town meant noise and anonymity and beginning.

"I resigned yesterday," Lior said. "From the office. I gave notice properly, which Mr. Petch said was the first professional courtesy anyone had shown that building in thirty years." A breath. "There's a reform society there. They document working conditions—women in workshops, mills, laundries. They need someone who can take testimony without flinching. I applied. They accepted." She was speaking faster now, the words tumbling like water over a broken weir. "It's honest work. It's the work, really—the same work I was doing here, except done right, with consent, with names given instead of taken. And there are guilds in the city. Real ones. The examiner who certified you sent a letter ahead—he says the eastern guild will honor it, he *insisted*—"

"Lior."

"—and there's a flat above a repair shop on Corvess Street, the widow who owns it takes lodgers, it's small but the light is good for close work, I asked specifically about the light—"

"*Lior.*"

Eleanor crossed the space between them and took the envelope gently out of her hands and set it on the bench, and then took Lior's hands, which were cold, which were always cold, the hands of a woman who had spent her life holding pens in unheated offices.

"You planned all this," Eleanor said.

"I had a week of watching you pack." Lior's jaw tightened, and Eleanor recognized the effort beneath it—the effort of a person who had rehearsed composure and was watching it fail in real time. "I planned to make a speech. On the ferry I drafted a speech. It had structure. Three points and a conclusion, like a deposition." She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "I've forgotten every word of it. I've been standing here for two minutes talking about *light* because I couldn't find the sentence underneath."

"Tell me the sentence underneath."

Lior looked at her. The workshop was quiet around them—the particular quiet of a room whose clocks have all been packed away, so that for the first time in ten years Eleanor could hear her own pulse, and suspected Lior could hear it too.

"I don't want to arrive anywhere without you," Lior said. "That's the sentence. I've been arriving places alone my whole life and I am *finished* with it. So I bought two tickets, and if you tell me you'd rather travel separately I'll tear mine up and follow yours on foot, but I very much hoped—"

"Yes."

"—that you'd—"

"Yes." Eleanor said it a third time, quietly, against Lior's temple, the word landing somewhere beneath the skin. "Yes. You elaborate *terribly* when you're nervous. Has anyone told you?"

"Frequently. It's why I became a census taker. Forms don't require speeches." But Lior was smiling now, the full unguarded smile that transformed her face from official to human, and her arms came around Eleanor amid the crates and straw and the smell of brass filings and machine oil, and they stood a long moment in the middle of the emptied shop, two women holding on in a room that was learning to be nobody's.

When they separated, Lior's eyes were wet and she made no attempt to hide it, which was its own kind of arrival.

"The tickets," Eleanor said, nodding at the envelope. "Wednesday."

"Wednesday."

"Then I have two days." Eleanor turned and surveyed the workshop—the bench, the lathe, the stairs climbing into shadow toward the tower where the master clock ticked on above them all, faithful as gravity. Something moved through her, slow and enormous, that was grief and gratitude braided so tightly they could not be told apart.

"There's something I have to do before we leave," she said. "Something for the town. For the clock." She picked up the winding key from its hook by the door, and its weight was familiar as her own heartbeat. "Will you help me carry the oil up the stairs? One last time?"

Lior took the can from the shelf without asking what for. Some questions answered themselves.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, the observatory dial caught the last of the daylight and threw it back gold, and Eleanor looked at it the way one looks at a face one is memorizing—and began, silently, to plan the ending she intended to give it.
