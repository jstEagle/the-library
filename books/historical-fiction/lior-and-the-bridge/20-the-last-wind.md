## 20. The Last Wind

The workshop was already half-empty when I woke on the last morning, and the emptiness had a sound to it.

Ten years of living inside these walls had taught me the building's vocabulary: the tick of cooling brass at night, the sigh of the floorboards when the river damp came up in autumn, the particular silence of a room where tools hung in their appointed places. Now that vocabulary was breaking apart. The pegs stood bare where my father's files had hung. The bench was stripped to its grain, showing the pale scars of a hundred years of cutting. Crates waited by the door with straw packed around the lathe like something wounded being carried off a field.

Lior slept on the cot we'd dragged down from the loft three weeks ago, after it stopped mattering who slept where. Her census satchel sat beside her boots, empty now, surrendered with everything else official about her. In the grey light her face was turned toward me, mouth slightly open, one hand curled under her chin like a girl's. I watched her breathe and thought: this is what I kept instead. This is the ledger I'm taking out of here.

I dressed without lighting a lamp. There is an old habit in dressing as a man—bind first, then collar, then waistcoat, each layer a sentence in a grammar I'd spoken so long I no longer heard it. But the hearing was eleven days behind me. My name was public property now, argued over in the taproom and the trustee minutes alike. *Eleanor Thackeray*, printed in the *Gazette* in letters small enough to deny and large enough to be true. When I buttoned the waistcoat over my flat chest it was only clothing again, not architecture. I could not tell you which felt stranger.

I let myself out through the workshop door, and the bell above it—the little shop bell my father had bought secondhand from a draper going under—did not ring, because I lifted the clapper with two fingers, the way I'd done for ten years whenever I left before dawn. Old habits are just love with nowhere to go.

The town was still asleep, or pretending to be. October had come down hard overnight; the river mist lay in the streets like spilled milk, and every lamp post wore a halo. My breath went before me. I walked up Church Hill with my collar turned against the cold, past houses whose windows I knew by heart—which shutters were painted, which curtains mended with flour-sack cloth, which chimney smoked crooked because the flue had never been right—and I understood, walking, that I was doing it deliberately. Reading the town one last time. Memorizing it the way you memorize a mechanism you will never hold again but might someday need to draw from memory.

They had rejected its maker. That was the phrase Lior used once, furious on my behalf, and I had laughed—not because it was wrong but because it was too grand for the truth. They hadn't rejected me. They had failed, collectively and almost gently, to imagine me. Aldous Crane hadn't seized the workshop out of malice; he'd seized it out of arithmetic, the same arithmetic I'd lived by for ten years, the cold summing of what a thing must be versus what it appeared. The difference between us was only that I had done my sums in secret and he had done his in committee.

The observatory stood black against the paling east, its dome furred with frost. The town had built it forty years ago on a wave of civic pride and railway money, and the pride had drained away faster than the money, until the only soul who tended the place was the one nobody invited anywhere. I had the key on a string around my neck. I have worn keys around my neck since I was nine years old; my father said a clockmaker should carry her authority close to the heart, and I had never decided whether he meant it as philosophy or joke.

Inside, the stair wound up through cold air that smelled of stone and oil and, faintly, of apples—someone's orchard nearby, or memory. The clock room at the top held the master clock in its glass-fronted case like a saint's relic, except that relics don't tick, and mine did. Mine had ticked through ten years of my life. It would tick long after.

I opened the case and put my hands into the works.

You want to know what it feels like, holding a great clock's movement? It feels like holding a sleeping animal's heart. The weight train hangs silent when it's run down—all that stored gravity waiting—and your fingers find the great wheels by touch, the going train, the striking train, the escapement I had rebuilt twice, once by design and once after Crane's man had broken it with a file in the dark. The pallets. The escape wheel with its teeth like a saw blade made patient. Everything machined true, everything fitted, nothing wasted. A clock is the most honest object humans make. Every part does exactly what it was made to do, forever, and asks nothing back but winding.

I had told Lior that once, standing exactly where I stood now, her head tipped toward the gears, lamplight catching the dust motes. *A mechanism can be honest even when its maker isn't.* She had looked at me then with an expression I couldn't read, and it had taken me months to understand that she wasn't puzzled—she was grieving. She'd recognized the confession for what it was before I did.

I wound it. Both barrels, the full turn, weight after weight climbing back into their promise. My arms knew the work; eight days of reserve in the great barrel, and I gave it all eight. Then I set the hands.

This was the ceremony of it, though no one had written it down. The clock had been stopped since the hearing—I had stopped it myself the morning I testified, superstitiously, the way a sailor won't start a voyage on a Friday. Now I brought the hour hand around slowly, past six, past seven, feeling the strike train gather itself behind each hour like a held breath. I set it to the time by my father's pocket watch, which I carried now in place of him.

Seven minutes past seven. A good hour. The hour the market opened, in the years when there was a market worth opening. The hour my father used to unlock the shop and say, *Right, Edmund, show me you learned something yesterday.*

He had known. All those years I'd believed I was deceiving him perfectly, the way children believe their disguises work, and the letter in the strongbox proved he had seen straight through me from the beginning—or near enough—and had loved me anyway, and had been too much a coward of his own era to say it aloud while he lived, so he'd said it to paper instead, addressed to *my daughter*, and sealed it, and died with it unsent. I had wept over it among the gears, alone, at thirty-one years old, crying for the first time in that room since I was a child. Grief and absolution arrived together, the way they usually do: too late to change anything, early enough to save what remained.

I stepped back from the clock and let the escapement do its work.

*Tick.*

The sound filled the tower the way water fills a vessel. One beat, and the whole building remembered its purpose. The seconds hand moved off its mark and began its rounds. Somewhere below, in the dial housing, the motion work began translating wheel-turns into the slow crawl of the great hands on the face that the whole town read without thinking, the way they read weather and gossip and each other.

I had promised to finish it. That promise had been made to a dead man and kept to a living town that had taken everything else from me—the shop, the name I'd built, the legal personhood of ten careful years—but could not take this, because I declined to give it. Let them wind it. Let some hired man come up the stairs every eight days with a crank and no understanding, and let the clock keep better time than any of them deserved. That was not bitterness. I checked it carefully, turning the thought over the way I'd check a wheel for warp. It was not spite either. It was simpler than both: a finished thing should run. Work outlives welcome. My father taught me that too, though he taught it by dying rather than saying.

I closed the glass case and latched it, and pressed my palm flat against the cool pane one moment longer than the job required.

Then I went down the stairs, and locked the observatory door behind me, and hung the key back around my neck, because that key at least was mine to keep. The trustees could have the building. The guild examiner's certificate—*E. Thackeray, journeyman clockmaker*, the first ever issued under any name to a woman, inked by a red-faced old man who'd spent three hours examining my escapement and finally said, gruffly, that he'd seen worse from men with twenty years on me—that certificate was folded in my breast pocket, and it weighed more than the key, and it was going with me.

* * *

Lior was awake when I came back down the hill. She stood in the workshop doorway with her coat over her arm and two cups of tea steaming on the bench among the packing straw, and she didn't ask where I'd been. She had learned, over these months, that I go to the clocks the way other people go to church or drink, and that asking is worse than waiting.

"The ferry's at nine," she said.

"I know."

"We could take the later one."

"And spend another day here watching the windows?" I picked up my tea. It was strong enough to stand a spoon in; she made it the way I liked without having been taught, which is one definition of love. "No. Better while it's misty. I'd rather remember the town blurred."

She nodded and looked at the crates. "The carter comes at eight."

"Then we've an hour."

We spent it badly and perfectly. She read to me from the newspaper she'd bought off the boy at the corner—parliamentary nonsense, a shipping notice, a small item noting that the trustees of Millbrook-up-River had resolved the question of the Thackeray estate in favor of Mr. Aldous Crane, who thanked the board for its diligence. No mention of the clockmaker certified that same week. Papers print losses and skip legacies; it saves space.

"Crane sent a note," Lior said, folding the page away. "It came last night. I put it in your coat."

"I know. I read it."

"Well?"

"He offers to retain me," I said. "To service the observatory clock. Wages quarterly." I let that sit. Lior's eyebrows climbed. "He thinks he's being magnanimous. He thinks hiring the woman he ruined will make the town forgive him for ruining her."

"Will you?"

I thought of my palm on the glass case. "No. The clock doesn't need me anymore. That's the whole point of finishing something—you get to leave." And then, because fairness is a habit I can't break: "Though I'll write out the winding instructions properly and post them to the observatory. The clock shouldn't suffer for its owners."

Lior laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of her—and crossed the bare boards and kissed me, tasting of tea and sleep, and for a moment the empty workshop was not empty at all but simply a room we happened to be standing in, and the future was a door rather than a wall.

The carter came at eight exactly, because carters are the most honest clockwork in any town. We loaded crates and trunks and the lathe wrapped in sacking, and my father's tool chest, which I carried myself and would have fought anyone who reached for it. The neighbors came out to watch, as neighbors do. Mrs. Petch from the chandlery, who had sold me tallow for ten years and called me sir for ten years, stood in her doorway with her arms folded, and our eyes met, and I could not read her face at all—which was, I suppose, fair payment for ten years of her being unable to read mine. Old Hobbes, the cooper, touched his hat brim and said nothing. Halfway down the street a woman I didn't recognize pressed a paper-wrapped loaf into Lior's hands and hurried off, and we stood looking at each other over the warm bread, neither of us able to say whether it was kindness or apology or both, and in the end it didn't matter. Bread is bread.

At the foot of Quay Lane I stopped once and looked back up the hill. The workshop's upper window caught the sun, which had finally burned through the mist, and for a heartbeat the glass blazed white, the whole facade lit like a signal. Ten years I had lived behind that glass, bent over brass, visible to anyone who cared to look and hidden from everyone who did. Someone else would look out of it now. Perhaps an apprentice. Perhaps, someday, if the world turned kindly, a girl with a secret and steady hands, and perhaps whoever kept the shop then would be wiser than the town had been. I had left my letter in the strongbox, beside my father's—two letters, twenty years apart, addressed to whoever comes next. It was the closest thing to an heirloom I had to give, and I gave it to the future, which has never once signed for its deliveries.

* * *

The ferry was late, which ferries are, so we stood on the quay with our trunks while the river ran brown and quick beneath the pilings. The town woke around us: smoke from chimneys, the market bell—my hour, seven minutes past seven rung wrong by a lazy sexton—the gulls arguing over fish guts. Children ran the planks. A dog stole a herring and was pursued with theatrical fury.

"You're doing it again," Lior said quietly.

"Doing what?"

"Memorizing."

I smiled. She knew me now well enough to catch me mid-theft. "One last inventory," I admitted. "Census-taker's training, I expect. You've infected me."

"Occupational hazard." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "What did you count?"

"Houses with crooked flues. The number of steps on Church Hill. Which gull is the bully." I paused. "How many people looked out their windows at us. Eleven."

"And how many looked glad to see us go?"

"None," I said. "That's the strange part. Not one glad face. Only curious ones." I turned my collar to the wind. "Perhaps that's all most people ever are. Curious. It isn't cruelty, mostly. It's just curiosity with no imagination attached."

The ferry came around the bend with its paddle churning, trailing gulls, and the deckhand swung the gangplank across, and we handed up our trunks and paid our fares, and I did not look back at the quay. I looked forward, upriver, at the water unspooling toward cities I'd only read about, where no one knew which silences had kept us safe and no one would need to. Lior found us seats on the leeward side, out of the spray, and sat close enough that her shoulder rested against mine, proprietary and unashamed, and I let my eyes close and listened to the engine's rhythm until I had identified it—simple oscillating gear, badly balanced, due for trouble within the year—and fell into something between waking and sleep, rocking, going.

I woke proper when the light changed.

The ferry had come about in the wide reach below the town, and the whole valley opened astern of us: the roofs, the church spire, the terraced gardens, and above them all the observatory on its hill, dome catching the new sun. And on the dial—small at this distance, a coin held at arm's length—the great hands stood at seventeen minutes past nine, and the gilt rim of the face flashed once, hard, like a struck match, like an eye opening, and held the light as the ferry carried us around the point.

Nobody on that boat knew whose work was keeping the town's hours. Nobody needed to. The clock would strike noon today whether anyone remembered my name or not, and next year, and after everyone who had voted in that hot little hearing room was themselves a name on someone's ledger. I had put eight days of wind in the barrel. It seemed a fair term: eight days of honest time, given freely, to a town that had paid me in none.

Lior's hand found mine on the rail. Neither of us spoke. The dial sank behind the trees, then the hill, then the mist took the valley entirely, and I discovered that my eyes were wet and could not honestly assign the reason—grief, relief, the wind, all three keeping time together—and I let them be wet without apology.

Ahead, downriver, the city waited with its smoke standing straight in the still air, and somewhere in it a room we had never seen, a bench we had not yet scarred, a contract with my own name waiting at the bottom to be signed.

I turned my back on the water and faced the way we were going.

"Tell me about it again," I said to Lior. "The reform society. The women you'll be writing down. Tell me what questions you'll ask."

She looked at me a long moment—she always knows when a question is really a vow—and began, quietly, counting on her fingers, and the river ran, and the paddle beat, and we went.
