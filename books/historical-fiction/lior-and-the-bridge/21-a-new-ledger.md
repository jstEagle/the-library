## 21. A New Ledger

The census office smelled of lamp oil and wet wool, and on the morning Lior Ashcombe resigned, it smelled of both more than usual, because the rain had come in off the river and everyone's coats were steaming on the pegs by the door.

She had dressed carefully for it. Not her good dress—that would have been a statement, and statements invited questions—but the gray one, the plain one, the one that said *I am here to conduct business*. She had rehearsed the speech on the ferry across, then again walking from the pier: brief, courteous, effective. Effective had become her watchword in the months since the hearing. She had learned from watching Eleanor what it cost to be ineffective at the wrong moment.

Mr. Halloway, the district supervisor, received her letter without reading it first. That was his habit; he read people before paper, weighing them the way he weighed everything, against the cost of replacing them.

"You're giving up a permanent post," he said, "for charity work."

"For work," Lior said. "The society pays."

"The society pays in gratitude." He set the letter down unopened. "Ashcombe, you were the best enumerator I had this cycle. Clean sheets. Legible hand. You didn't pad your numbers." A pause, and then, because Halloway was not entirely a fool: "This is about Thackeray."

The name still landed in her chest like a dropped tool. She had thought she'd armored herself against it. Apparently armor was something one renewed daily, like lamp oil.

"This is about how records get used," Lior said. "I wrote down ten thousand lives last autumn, Mr. Halloway, and I did it well. And then I watched one line of mine get read aloud in a room full of men who used it to take everything a person owned. I'm not going to pretend the pen is neutral anymore. It isn't. It never was."

Halloway looked at her for a long moment. Outside the fogged window, a dray horse clopped past through the rain, hauling coal toward the gasworks.

"You could just say you're tired of the road," he offered.

"I could," Lior agreed. "But I spent a year learning what happens when people say the easy thing instead of the true one. I'd rather not start again now."

He signed her severance without further argument. At the door, as she buttoned her coat, he said—quietly, not looking up from the ledger on his desk—"The society. The women workers thing. If they ever want someone who can read a payroll book and spot where the figures have been cooked, send them to me when I retire."

Lior smiled. "I'll put it in writing."

"Don't bother," he said. "I've seen your handwriting. It's too honest."

* * *

The Women's Industrial Aid Society occupied three rooms above a milliner's shop on Corvess Street, and it was there, two weeks later, that Lior discovered what it meant to ask questions and be permitted to receive answers.

Her first interview was with a girl named Tamsin Reeve, seventeen, a bobbin-winder at the Halvard Mills, who came in on her half-day with chapped hands and a cough that rattled like gravel in a tin cup. Lior had prepared a form—a new form, her own design, approved after argument by the society's committee—and the first question on it was not *name, age, occupation*, the way the census went, marching through a life like a bailiff through a house.

The first question was: *What do you want written down?*

Tamsin had stared at it. "Nobody's asked me that."

"Then nobody's been asking properly," Lior said. "Take your time. We don't print anything without your signature under it. If you want a line struck out later, we strike it out. If you want the whole sheet burned, we burn it in front of you."

"Even if it's about the fines?" Tamsin's voice dropped. "They dock us for thread breakage. Even when the thread comes to us already rotten. Missus Vane says it's in the rules. But nobody's ever shown me the rules."

Lior wrote it all down—the fines, the hours, the locked fire doors, the foreman who touched girls' arms when he checked their output—and when she finished, she turned the pages around and read them back aloud, every word, while Tamsin listened with her mouth slightly open, as though hearing her own life spoken by a stranger made it suddenly real, suddenly weighty, suddenly *hers*.

"That's me," she said at last. "That's actually me, on paper."

"That's you," Lior agreed. "Sign at the bottom only if you're willing. It may go before the factory commission in spring."

Tamsin signed. Her hand shook, but she signed.

Afterward, Lior sat alone in the little back room with its single window overlooking Corvess Street, and she held the signed sheets in both hands and understood, finally, what she had been reaching toward since the day she'd burned the falsified duplicate in her lodging-house grate.

A record could be a weapon. She had known that since Crane had stood up in the trustees' chamber with her census sheet in his fist like a warrant. But a record could also be a shield, a lantern, a lever. It depended entirely on whose hand held it, and whether the person inside the record had consented to be written.

She dipped her pen and began the next entry in her private log—not the society's log, but her own, the one she kept of things learned:

*A ledger is honest or dishonest exactly as far as the hand that keeps it. There is no such thing as neutral ink.*

Below it, after a moment, she added: *E. would say the same of gears. Everything a mechanism does, someone designed it to do. The trick is designing honestly.*

* * *

The Guildhall stood on Foundry Square, a soot-darkened pile of arches and windows that had watched over the city's tradesmen for a hundred and forty years, and on the morning of the third of March, Eleanor Thackeray walked through its doors wearing her father's old coat brushed clean and her best shirt collar, with her certificate papers folded flat in an inner pocket and her hands steady only because she had spent twenty minutes the night before teaching them steadiness, filing a scrap of brass until midnight purely for the practice of calm.

Lior was not allowed inside—the examination gallery was for members—but she waited across the square in the doorway of a coffee house, where she could see the doors, and she drank nothing, because her stomach had decided against it.

They had argued about it, of course. In the small rooms on Pell Street where they'd lodged since autumn—two narrow chambers, a shared stove, a view of a brick wall and one determined wisteria—they had argued about nearly everything, pleasantly, endlessly, the way two people argue who have learned that arguing is a form of trust. Eleanor had said the guild was a fortress built by men for men, that the word *journeyman* itself was a wall, that she should not beg at gates designed to keep her out.

And Lior had said: "You told me once that your father taught you a mechanism doesn't care who winds it. Let them find out."

"It's not winding," Eleanor had said, staring into the stove. "It's signing. They'll make me sign."

"Yes," Lior had said. "They will. And this time you choose what goes beneath the pen."

Now, through the Guildhall's high windows, Lior could see nothing but lamplight moving behind frosted glass—shadows of men crossing and recrossing, the examination proceeding somewhere above in a room she could not enter. She ordered a coffee she didn't drink and watched the light and thought about time, which she did often now, living with a clockmaker. How it thickened. How a minute in a waiting room outweighed an hour anywhere else.

An hour and forty minutes after the doors closed behind Eleanor, they opened again.

Lior set down the untouched cup and crossed the square at a pace she refused to call running.

Eleanor came down the steps slowly, which was unlike her—she climbed stairs and descended them like water, always, a habit of a decade of making herself unremarkable—and her face was doing several things at once, none of them settled.

"Well?" Lior said.

Eleanor stopped on the bottom step. Around them, Foundry Square went on with its morning: carts, cries, a knot of apprentices smoking by the fountain. Nobody looked twice at two women talking on church steps. The city's great mercy was its indifference.

"They tested me three ways," Eleanor said. Her voice was level but very quiet. "Theory, at the board. Then a blind mechanism—they took apart a regulator and gave me the pieces in a bag, no marks, no maker's name, and told me to diagnose it. Then the practical. Four hours at the bench, an examiner standing over my shoulder the whole time." She paused. "He was Master Corvin. Do you remember? From the hearing?"

Lior remembered him perfectly: a dry, precise man with spectacles like a pair of full moons, who had examined the master clock for six hours and then stood before the trustees and said, in a voice that cut through Crane's bluster like a graver through soft brass, *Gentlemen, whoever built this clock is worth certifying, whatever else you decide about the will.*

"He shook my hand afterward," Eleanor said. "In front of the other examiners. And he said—" She stopped. Her throat worked. "He said, 'Your escapement work is better than anything I've certified this decade, Miss Thackeray.' Miss. On purpose. In front of all of them."

The square tilted slightly. Lior gripped the iron railing.

"And the contract?"

Eleanor reached into the inner pocket and drew out a single folded sheet, heavy paper, embossed with the guild's seal—an escutcheon bearing a balance wheel crossed by calipers—and opened it with hands that were, at last, visibly trembling.

There was the standard language, the clauses and considerations. And there, near the bottom, above the examiner's countersignature, the line Lior had watched her practice writing a dozen times at the kitchen table, sometimes laughing, once crying:

**E. Thackeray — Journeyman Clockmaker.**

"Just the initial," Lior said softly. "Not Eleanor."

"Not yet." Eleanor traced the letters with one finger. "Corvin offered to write it in full. He said the guild had no rule against a woman's given name—only against an unqualified one. But I told him E. Thackeray was how my father signed his work, and E. Thackeray was how I'd sign mine. Let the town that knew me as Edmund read it however they need to. Let the rest of the world learn the whole of it slowly, on my schedule." She looked up. "Is that cowardice?"

Lior thought of Tamsin Reeve's shaking signature. Of her own form's first question. *What do you want written down?*

"It's authorship," she said. "It's the whole point. No one gets to hold your pen but you."

Eleanor laughed—a short, wet sound—and folded the contract along its practiced creases, and for a moment neither of them moved, standing on the edge of Foundry Square while the city streamed past, until Eleanor said, "You're late for the society. Vane's commission report won't write itself."

"How do you know I have—"

"You've had ink on your left cuff since breakfast and you keep touching your pocket where the interview notes are. You've been composing paragraphs in your head all morning; your lips move when you do that." Eleanor's mouth curved. "Ten years of reading people who might see through me, Lior. I see everything."

* * *

Spring came to the city in stages, the way it came everywhere—first as rumor, then as mud, then all at once, green and shameless.

The society's report went to the factory commission in April with forty-one signed testimonies bound in blue boards, and Lior's name appeared on the title page as compiler, her first appearance in print under circumstances she could respect. A clerk from the commission came to Corvess Street to verify signatures; Lior produced each woman's consent slip in its own envelope, filed and dated, and the clerk, a young man with an earnest face, said, "You know, most societies don't bother with this much paper."

"Most societies don't bother with the women, either," Lior said, "beyond counting them."

The commission's interim findings cited testimony number nine—Tamsin Reeve's—by name. By June, the Halvard Mills had posted its fine schedule publicly, and though the fines themselves remained, the rotten-thread dockings had stopped, because rotting thread could now be proven and someone had begun proving it.

Lior kept a clipping. She kept other things, too, accumulating on the mantel of the Pell Street rooms like sediment: the blue-bound report; a brass gear blank Eleanor had turned on her first day at Harrow & Sons, the repair shop that had taken her on within a week of the certification; a pressed sprig of wisteria from the determined vine outside the window. And, wrapped in flannel at the back of the shelf, the workshop key from the river town—which Eleanor had carried out in her coat pocket the day they left, saying nothing, explaining nothing, and which had migrated gradually, without discussion, to the place of honor among the ordinary objects of their life.

Some evenings, when the stove was lit and Eleanor worked at the small bench by the window—commission pieces now, a regulator for a shipping office, a striking clock for a widow on Bellamy Row—Lior would sit across from her with her own papers and the two of them would work in silence broken only by file-strokes and pen-scratches, and the silence was the richest thing Lior had ever owned.

One such evening in June, Eleanor set down her file and said, without preamble, "Corvin wrote again."

Lior looked up. Letters from Master Corvin arrived perhaps once a month, always brief, always containing some piece of guild news or a technical query designed, Lior suspected, less for information than for the pleasure of receiving Eleanor's answers, which were evidently better than those of most masters.

"He wants me to submit a piece for the autumn exhibition," Eleanor said. "Open category. Striking clocks." She turned a small brass wheel between her fingers, not looking at Lior. "He says the judges this year include two horologists from the Continent. He says—" she hesitated, and then recited, in the careful tone of someone quoting exactly, "'A maker of this caliber should not remain in a repair shop. The guild has funds for a journeyman's studio. Thackeray should apply.'"

"A studio," Lior repeated. "Her own."

"Our own, if you're asking the real question." Eleanor finally looked up, and the lamplight caught the corner of her smile. "Pell Street fits two benches badly and a stove worse."

Lior set down her pen. Through the window, the wisteria moved in the warm dark, and somewhere below on the street a cab rattled past, and the little striking clock on the shelf—the first piece Eleanor had finished under her own name, the one she'd wound every Sunday like a ritual—ticked on with the particular authority of a well-made thing.

"There's something else in the letter," Lior said. It wasn't a guess. Eleanor's voice had that hitch in it, the one that preceded either bad news or enormous news, and lately those two had started sounding alike.

Eleanor slid the second page across the table. "Read the postscript."

Lior read it. Read it again. Set the page down flat on the table between them, as though it might behave better under restraint.

*PS — An inquiry reached the guild this week from your former borough. A Mrs. Aldous Crane, if you can credit it, petitioning regarding the disposition of certain workshop effects. The trustees' clerk informs me she claims a family interest. I have replied that the guild holds no opinion on furniture. But you may wish to know the town talks of reopening the observatory, and a master clock wants a keeper. — F.C.*

The stove ticked. The wisteria scratched the glass.

"She wants the workshop," Eleanor said quietly. "Or what's left of it. Or—" She stopped, jaw tightening. "Or she wants to reopen the observatory and needs someone who can keep that clock alive, and there are perhaps four people alive who understand that movement, and she knows it."

Lior looked at the letter, and then at Eleanor's face, which had gone still in the old way—the way it had gone still in the workshop a lifetime ago, when Lior had asked whether anyone else slept in the house.

"Eleanor," she said gently. "What do you want written down?"

For a long moment the clock filled the room with its steady count. Then Eleanor reached out and turned the letter face-down on the table.

"I think," she said, "we need to talk about going home."
