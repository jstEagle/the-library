## 22. What Keeps Time

### Eleanor

The flat above Hargrove's repair shop had two windows facing the street and one facing the chimney of the dye works next door, and Eleanor had claimed the one facing the street for her bench because the light came in clean from eight until four, which was the honest part of the day.

She was fitting a pallet to an escape wheel when she heard Lior's boots on the stairs—not the hurried half-flight of a woman late for something, but the measured tread of someone who had learned, in the past year, that arriving on time and arriving calm were not the same thing. Eleanor did not look up. She listened instead: the pause at the landing where Lior always shook rain or dust from her coat, the creak of the third stair that she had never gotten around to fixing because the sound told her who was coming home.

"You've been at it since before I left," Lior said.

"I've been at it since Tuesday."

"It's Saturday."

"Then I've been at it since Tuesday." Eleanor held the wheel up to the window and turned it slowly, watching light run along the teeth. "It's not right yet. The fourth tooth has a burr you can't see but you can hear. It's like a man clearing his throat in the middle of a sentence."

Lior set down her satchel—the reform society's satchel, leather gone soft at the corners now, stuffed with interview notes—and came to stand behind her. Eleanor felt the warmth of her before she felt the touch, a hand settling on her shoulder, thumb moving once across the knob of her collarbone where the bone still sat closer to the skin than it used to. She ate better now. She slept. The binding had come off her chest eleven months ago in a rented room in a city where no one knew her name meant anything, and some mornings she still woke and reached for the linen strips out of habit, found them folded away in a drawer she never opened, and lay there breathing deep just to feel her ribs move.

"What does the commission say?" Eleanor asked.

"That Mrs. Okafor's clock will be ready when it's true," Lior said, "and not before." She had learned this phrasing from Eleanor and deployed it with the satisfaction of a woman quoting scripture she'd memorized herself. "She seemed satisfied. She asked if I could write down what you said about escapements. For the pamphlet."

"The pamphlet."

"On women in the trades. You said you'd contribute."

"I said I might."

"You said, and I quote myself verbatim, 'If Lior writes it, I'll read it.'"

Eleanor set the wheel down and turned on the stool. Up close, Lior had ink on her fingers again—the good kind, the chosen kind, the kind that came from writing what people actually said rather than what forms required them to say. A year ago those same fingers had held a census ledger, and Eleanor had watched them across a workshop table with her heart going like a broken mainspring, certain that whatever this stranger wrote down about her would be the instrument of her ruin.

Now the ledgers were different. Now the pages held Mrs. Okafor's account of running a laundry out of a cellar for nineteen years, and a girl named Prudence who wired telegraph poles and got paid half a man's wage, and forty other lives that Lior recorded with their consent, their names spelled the way they themselves pronounced them. Eleanor had read every page twice.

"There's tea," she said. "And bread that's only slightly stale. And a clock that wants finishing."

"In order?"

"In any order you like. It's your house too."

Lior paused at that, as she always did, as though the phrase were a small door she kept opening to check whether it still swung freely. It did. It always did. But she checked anyway, and Eleanor loved her for checking, and neither of them ever said so.

* * *

### Lior

In the evening, after bread and tea, Lior sat at the table by the second window with her notes spread out while Eleanor worked on at the bench, because Eleanor worked the way rivers ran—ceaselessly, quietly, without needing a reason beyond the going. The lamp between them threw two shadows against the wall, and Lior had long ago stopped finding it remarkable that she could sit in silence with another person for hours and feel no need to break it.

She was transcribing Mrs. Okafor's interview into fair copy when she came to the passage she'd flagged that afternoon.

*My husband died the winter of '81,* Mrs. Okafor had said, hands wrapped around the cup Lior bought her, *and everyone told me to sell the laundry, take in sewing, marry again if I could find a fool. Instead I hired two girls and taught them the boiling vats myself. You want to put that in your book? Put it plain. Don't dress me up as brave. I was angry. Anger keeps better than courage.*

Lior copied it plain. Then she looked up at Eleanor's back—at the slope of her shoulders under the shirt, the roll of muscle at her forearm where ten years of filing brass had built something the tailor's chalk had tried to hide and failed—and thought about anger keeping better than courage, and about how much of her own life had been run on both without her ever naming either.

"Lior," Eleanor said, without turning. "You're staring."

"I'm working."

"You're staring *and* working. I can hear the difference. Your pen stops when you stare."

"My pen stops when I think."

"Your pen stops when you think about me," Eleanor said, and there it was—that dryness that had once been armor and was now simply the grain of her humor, the way she'd grown comfortable enough to be playful in a register Lior suspected no living soul had heard before the census taker knocked on a workshop door in 1893.

Lior returned to her transcription. Her pen stopped twice more.

On the mantel above the small iron stove, the workshop key sat on its nail. They had argued about bringing it—Eleanor had wanted to leave it hanging in the empty workshop for whoever came next, a gift to strangers, and Lior had said nothing, and then at the last minute Eleanor had taken it down herself and pocketed it without a word, and they had never discussed the matter since. It was dull brass, unremarkable, the sort of key that opened ten thousand doors in ten thousand towns. On the mantel it caught the lamplight each evening like a coin placed over the eyes of something dead—or like a coin saved, Lior could never quite decide which, and had stopped trying to decide, because perhaps it was both, the way grief and gratitude were both, the way the loss of the workshop and the gaining of everything else had arrived in the same gavel-fall and never fully separated since.

"Eleanor," she said now. "Mrs. Okafor wants to know if you'd teach. Evening classes. Escapements, wheel-cutting, basic repair. The society would pay for the room."

The file stopped.

"They'd want you teaching under your own name," Lior went on carefully. "Eleanor Thackeray, journeyman clockmaker. First woman certified under—" 

"I know what I am," Eleanor said. Not sharp. Just level, the way she was when something mattered. She turned around on the stool. In the lamplight her face was all planes and shadow and that terrible, beautiful steadiness she carried like a tool she'd forged herself. "I spent ten years being nobody's daughter so I could keep my father's name. Then I spent one afternoon being my father's daughter so I could have my own. Do you know what I learned?"

"Tell me."

"That a name is like a signature on a movement plate. Anyone can scratch letters into brass. What matters is whether the work underneath will still be running in a hundred years." She picked up the escape wheel, held it to the lamp. "I'll teach. But I won't teach them to hide. Whatever else they learn from me, they'll learn that hiding costs more than it pays. I paid the full price. I can quote it accurately."

Lior wrote it down. *Anger keeps better than courage; honesty keeps best of all.* She made up the second half. Mrs. Okafor hadn't said it. But it belonged in the book, and books, unlike censuses, could hold things people chose to give them.

* * *

### Eleanor

Sunday morning, rain on the glass, and the Okafor commission finally true.

Eleanor assembled the movement on the bench with the patience her father had drilled into her before he'd ever told her, dying, to keep the name and lose the self—the old lie she had obeyed for a decade, the instruction she had finally understood differently only after reading his unsent letter, *my daughter*, in his own cramped and careful hand. He had known. All those years of *Edmund, lad, mind the pitch of the teeth*, he had known and said nothing, and the letter said everything, and between those two silences her whole life had been lived.

She set the balance, breathed on it, watched it wake. Tick. The sound filled the flat the way it filled every room she'd ever worked in, and she supposed it always would, and she found she didn't mind. Some inheritances were worth carrying even after you'd burned the paperwork.

Lior appeared at her elbow with coffee, hair pinned up badly, wearing the shirt Eleanor liked and pretending she didn't know why.

"It's done?" Lior said.

"It's true. Which is better than done."

They stood together and listened to it. Rain outside. Clock on the bench. Downstairs, faintly, Hargrove opening his shop, the bell over his door giving its one flat note.

"A year ago today," Lior said.

Eleanor did the arithmetic out of habit and found Lior was right. A year ago today she had been packing tools into crates with her father's letter in her breast pocket, and Lior had come up the workshop steps holding train tickets and no speech prepared, and had said, finally, only: *"The ferry leaves at nine."* And Eleanor had started the master clock one last time, wound its weights full, and walked out of the building that had eaten her whole life, leaving it ticking behind her for a town that had voted her out of existence.

"Do you miss it?" Lior asked. "The workshop."

"Yes," Eleanor said. "Not enough to go back. That's the strange thing about loss—it doesn't shrink, exactly. It just learns to fit alongside everything else." She touched the finished clock, feeling through her fingertips the tiny transmitted shudder of the escapement doing its honest work. "Do you miss the census?"

Lior laughed—a short, surprised sound. "No one has ever asked me that. No. I don't. I was good at counting people, and I hated it, and I couldn't have told you why until I met you. Now I count what people tell me, and only what they choose to tell, and it turns out that's a completely different profession wearing the same coat."

"Everything is," Eleanor said. "Same coat. Different person inside it."

Lior looked at her then, really looked, and Eleanor let herself be looked at—one of the small daily miracles she still catalogued, still marveled at, a year on: that she could be seen, entirely, by eyes that knew every fact, and that the seeing had not ended her. It had begun her.

"Mrs. Okafor's clock needs delivering," Eleanor said, to break the moment before it broke her open in front of the coffee. "Tuesday. And I promised Hargrove I'd look at his regulator."

"And I promised the society six pages by Friday," Lior said, "which I will not finish, and about which I feel no guilt whatsoever."

They grinned at each other like apprentices skipping lessons. Outside, the rain thinned toward a brightness that wasn't quite sun. On the mantel the old key hung in its little cage of lamplight and morning, opening nothing now, standing for everything: the door that closed, the door that opened, the hands that had turned it, the hands that had carried it here.

Eleanor wound the new clock fully, set its beat true, and left it running.

It would keep time. They all would—together, in this small flat above a repair shop, in a city that didn't know their story, in a book that would tell it right.
