## 3. The Name in the Wax

The letter sat in my coat pocket for two days before I could make myself look at it.

This is the thing about silence that nobody tells you: it is not empty. It fills up. It fills with everything you are not saying, and it grows heavy as wet sand, and you carry it anyway because carrying it has become the shape of your life. For two days I carried a folded piece of paper that weighed almost nothing and felt like an anvil.

Marta had burned her hand rather than let me touch it. That was the part I kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. She had grabbed the letter off the workbench with her bare fingers while Erik's soldering iron was still cooling beside it—no, not the iron, the kettle, the kettle had just come off the stove—and she had held on to it even as her skin hissed against the hot wax seal. She had looked me full in the face, this woman who never raised her voice, who communicated displeasure through the angle of a teacup, and said: *Some things are not yours yet, Oscar.*

*Yet.* I had turned that word over all night. Yet implied a later. Yet implied that someday the letter would be mine, or at least mine to know about. Marta was a careful woman; she did not spend words she didn't mean.

So on Thursday evening, when Erik took Linnea to the harbor to collect a delivery of clock springs and Marta went to market for candles and dried herring, I was alone in the workshop with the letter, and I told myself a story: that I was only going to hold it. That holding was not reading. That I would feel its weight, learn something from its texture, and put it back, and the family's careful machinery would go on turning as it always had.

I have since learned that apprentice clockmakers make excellent liars, because we understand escapements. A lie is just a truth with the release mechanism adjusted—one tooth at a time, so the wheel doesn't notice it's falling.

I read the letter.

* * *

It was written in Swedish, but a Swedish with a Russian accent baked into it, the sentences built slightly wrong, like furniture assembled by someone working from memory. The hand was small and slanted, economical, the writing of a man who had learned to write quickly and destroy quickly.

*Dear friend,* it began, and then stopped being dear very fast.

*I write this where no one will find it, which means I am trusting a clock, which is fitting, because clocks have been more honest to me than men. If you are reading this and your name is not Nystrom, then I am sorry for what comes next, though I suspect by then it will be too late for apologies.*

My skin prickled. I looked at the door. The workshop was quiet except for the regulator on the wall, ticking its patient two-beat, tick-tock, the sound I had fallen asleep to every night for five years, the sound that meant *home* more than any voice had.

*Erik,* the letter continued. *You will say peacetime ended our arrangement, and you will be right, and it changes nothing. The debt does not expire. Debts of blood do not carry interest, but neither do they carry mercy clauses.*

I read that sentence three times. My father—I mean Erik; I have never managed to call him anything else out loud, though God knows he has earned the word a hundred times over—my father owed someone a debt of blood. I set the letter down on the bench, next to a half-disassembled carriage clock whose guts lay open like a patient on a table, and I pressed both palms flat against the wood until the grain printed itself into my skin.

Then I picked the letter back up, because of course I did. You don't stop halfway down a stairwell that has already collapsed behind you.

*You know what happened in Riga. You were there when they came for him, and you hid him in your own cellar, and when they found the cellar you stood in front of the door and lied with such conviction that I have wondered ever since whether you might have missed your true calling. He lived eleven more years because of those lies. Eleven years in which he met a woman, and built a life, and made a son. I do not think you regret those eleven years. So do not tell me the account is closed.*

The room tilted. Not really—the regulator stayed on the wall, the bench stayed under my hands—but something in the floor's certainty gave way, the way ice gives, silently first and then all at once.

Riga. I knew Riga the way you know a word you've heard used but never defined. It was where my father had died—the other one, the first one, the man whose surname I wore like a borrowed coat. Papa, dead of fever in a port city, that was the story. Dead of fever, and Erik Nystrom, a Swedish clockmaker who had once delivered a regulator to a Latvian merchant house, had taken in the orphaned son without a moment's hesitation, and everyone in the neighborhood had called it charity and thought themselves lucky to know such a good man.

Nobody had mentioned cellars. Nobody had mentioned men coming for anyone. Nobody had mentioned lying with conviction.

*The arrangement is simple, as it always was,* the letter went on. *Your workshop receives parcels. Small ones. Documents, mostly, occasionally instruments of a delicate nature that certain parties prefer not to declare. You hold them. A man collects them. You never ask, and I never thank you, because thanks leave fingerprints. This is how it was between Stockholm and Riga before the war ate the sea routes, and this is how it must be again now, because the people I serve have found their way north and they trust nothing but old channels.*

*You will want to refuse. Refuse slowly, if you must, but understand: the name Nystrom is already in circulation. I could not keep it out. There are men in this city who know what your shop has been, and they are using that knowledge whether you consent or no. Better the channel runs through willing hands than seized ones.*

*Dmitri Volkov*

*P.S. Tell your wife the seal is the same as the one on the letters she burned in '09. She'll understand. She always understood more than either of us pretended.*

Volkov.

I said the name inside my head and it rang like a struck bell that wouldn't dampen. Volkov. I didn't know it. I was nearly sure I didn't know it. And yet some cellar-door deep in me swung open at the sound, and out of the dark came my father's voice—not Erik, the first father, the one I barely remembered except as a smell of tobacco and salt wool—saying something in Latvian-accented Russian to a guest whose face I could not see, laughing, the laugh of a man who owes someone his life and pays the debt in warmth.

I was four years old when we left Riga. Five when he died. Children remember laughter longer than faces.

I stood in the workshop for a long time. The regulator ticked. Somewhere upstairs the house settled its bones against the cold. Outside, a cart rattled past on the cobbles, and a fishwife shouted a price at somebody, ordinary sounds, the sounds of a city that had no idea my family's name was circulating somewhere like a counterfeit coin.

Here is the geometry problem I could not solve, standing there among the gears:

Erik Nystrom saved my life. Not dramatically, not with cellars and doors—he saved it the slow way, with bread and bed and trade and patience, with five years of teaching a mute grieving boy to file brass straight, and never once asking why the boy flinched at loud male voices, never once demanding the story. He had taken me in knowing my name. Knowing my *name*. He had known what the name meant, known what my father had been to Volkov, known perhaps that taking me in was itself a kind of signature on an old document. Every kindness he'd shown me had been rendered with full knowledge of the ledger underneath it, and he had never once let the ledger touch me. That was the silence I had grown up in. I had thought it was grief. It was armor.

And now the letter said the armor was compromised. Said men were using the family's name without—without what? Without consent? The letter implied Erik had refused, had declared the account closed. But the letter also existed, hidden in a clock, addressed to him, calling in a debt of blood, and debts of blood, the writer promised, do not expire.

So which was it? Was my family innocent, about to be drowned by a current they'd tried to escape? Or complicit, and merely careful? And which answer was worse?

Because here was the cruelest tooth of the whole escapement: if they were innocent, then the silence that had raised me was protection, noble and doomed, and I should help them keep it. And if they were guilty—if parcels had moved through the workshop under my nose while I oiled escapements above them—then the silence was still protection, still theirs, still wrapped around me like a blanket, and I should help them keep *that*. Either way, loyalty pointed the same direction: shut the door, burn the letter, say nothing, be safe.

Either way. And yet my hands shook, and not from cold.

Because there was a third point in the geometry, wasn't there? There was the man in Riga. The guest whose laughter I remembered. The man who had stood in a cellar doorway once, or hidden in one, or dragged someone out of one—someone whose survival had produced me, eventually, by some chain of grace I would never fully trace. Dmitri Volkov had saved my father's life, and my father's life, however briefly extended, had been the vessel that carried me into the world. I owed a man I had never met a debt I couldn't calculate, and he was now asking—or demanding, the letter wavered between the two—something from the family that had given me everything else.

A debt of blood on one side. A debt of love on the other. And me standing at the vertex, holding a letter that weighed almost nothing.

* * *

I heard the cart before I saw it—Erik's particular rhythm on the cobbles, the left wheel with its slight complaint—and I had the letter folded and into my shirt, against my chest, in the time it takes to swing a pendulum. Some skills you don't know you've learned until they save you. Five years of living among careful people had taught me to move carefully too.

The door opened on cold air and Erik's big frame, and Linnea behind him shaking snow out of her hair like a dog, and the ordinary evening resumed around me as if nothing had shifted. Erik grunted at the cold. Linnea announced that the harbormaster's clerk was an idiot and that she had defended my honor regarding a delivery slip, which I had not known needed defending. Marta came in with the candles and the herring and the particular way of looking at me that mothers have, the look that checks all your edges for cracks.

"Did the regulator behave?" Erik asked, nodding at the wall.

"It always behaves," I said. "That's the trouble with regulators."

He laughed, and Marta smiled, and Linnea rolled her eyes at both of them, and I stood in the middle of my family feeling the paper against my chest like a second heartbeat, and I understood that I had crossed a line from which there was no return crossing. I knew things now. Knowing cannot be unkilled. Whatever happened next—whatever parcels came or didn't, whatever men knocked or didn't—I would be standing in this kitchen knowing, forever, and choosing, every day, what to do with the knowing.

That night I lay awake listening to the house. Erik's snore through the floor, steady as a flywheel. Marta's slower breathing beside his. Linnea turning over twice, dreaming about whatever sharp-eyed girls dream about. The regulator downstairs keeping time for all of us, faithful as a heartbeat we'd stopped hearing years ago.

*The name Nystrom is already in circulation,* the letter had said. *Better the channel runs through willing hands than seized ones.*

And beneath my pillow, folded small, a dead man's—no, a living stranger's—handwriting waited for morning, and so did the question I hadn't dared finish thinking:

If someone was using the family's name without permission—then who had written *this* letter, sealed it in wax Marta recognized, and wedged it into the observatory's master clock where only a clockmaker would ever find it?

Someone wanted it found. And tomorrow, I intended to learn why the observatory's clock had been chosen as a hiding place—and why the jamming of its escapement had felt, to my fingers, less like sabotage than like a message.

* * *

Morning came gray and early, the way it does in Stockholm in November, arriving less like dawn than like a concession. I was down in the workshop before anyone else, ostensibly to start the fire, actually because I had slept maybe three hours and the letter under my pillow had seemed to grow heavier by the hour, as though it were absorbing the darkness.

I fed the stove and set the kettle on and tried to think like a clockmaker instead of a boy.

The letter had been wedged into the escapement of the observatory's master clock. Think about what that requires. First, access—the master clock lives in a locked housing, behind a door that three people have keys to, and none of them are named Nystrom. Second, knowledge—that a repair order would come to Hedlund & Sons, and that Hedlund & Sons would send *me*, specifically, alone, the apprentice who listened more than he spoke. Third, intent—to jam the escapement in exactly such a way that it would halt the clock, prompt the repair, and place the letter in the hands of someone connected to the Nystrom workshop without ever passing through the workshop itself.

It was elegant. It was careful. It was the work of someone who understood mechanisms and understood people, and who had wanted the Nystroms warned—or armed, or accused—without touching them directly. Whoever had done it had known Marta would recognize the seal. Had known she would burn her hand before letting me near it. Had counted on the family's silence to keep the discovery quiet.

Which meant the letter-writer knew my family intimately. Knew our habits, our silences, our reflexes. And had chosen a twelve-year-old's memory of laughter as the messenger.

The kettle began its low climb toward whistling. Behind me, on the stairs, a floorboard creaked—the third step, the one that announced everyone—and I slid the letter deeper into my shirt pocket and turned around wearing what I hoped was an apprentice's blank morning face.

Linnea stood on the landing in her shawl, hair unbraided, eyes already too awake.

"You're up early," she said.

"So are you."

"I'm always up early." She came down the last steps and looked at me, and my sister has always had the unnerving gift of looking at a person the way she looks at a movement that's running a minute fast—patiently, precisely, waiting for it to explain itself. "You've got soot on your collar," she said, "and you're standing between the fire and the bench like you're guarding it. And you didn't hear me come down, which you always hear, because you're part bat."

"I'm tired," I said.

"Mm." She poured herself water from the jug, unhurried. "When you're ready to say what it actually is," she said, "I'll be awake. I'm always awake."

She went back up the stairs, and I stood there with the kettle finally shrieking behind me, and thought: the letter says the name Nystrom is in circulation. It says nothing about the name *Oscar*. Perhaps that was vanity. But as I pulled the letter out and read Volkov's postscript one more time—*tell your wife the seal is the same as the one she burned in '09*—one detail snagged, and refused to let go, and turned the morning cold all over again.

1909. Marta had burned letters in 1909.

1909 was the year I arrived.
