## 4. What Father Owed

The road from Nynäshamn had been kind to Erik Nystrom, which was more than he could say for the road's opinion of his cart.

He had made the coastal delivery a hundred times in twenty years of marriage to this trade—brass fittings for the lighthouse keeper, a repaired barometer for the widow Lundgren, and a small mantle clock for the pastor, who always paid late but paid in salted herring, which Erik pretended not to enjoy because Marta said it was bad for his heart and he had learned long ago that a man may keep one small dishonesty if it is only about fish. The cart's left axle had developed a complaint somewhere past Ösmo, a low groan on every downhill stretch, like an old man asked to hurry. Erik had talked to it the way he talked to stubborn clocks: patiently, in Swedish, with occasional threats in Finnish that neither of them meant.

So it was nearly dark when he turned the cart into the courtyard behind the workshop, and he was thinking of nothing more complicated than dinner and whether the axle could wait until Thursday or would insist on Tuesday, when he saw Oscar standing at the workshop door with his hands in his pockets and his face arranged into the particular blankness of a boy guarding something.

Erik had raised two children who showed their feelings the way weather shows itself—Linnea announced her moods like thunder over open water, all warning and spectacle—and then he had taken in a third who did not. In five years, Oscar Nystrom had taught Erik more about reading silence than forty years of clockwork ever had. A clock that runs too quietly is not a healthy clock. It is a clock preparing to disappoint you.

"Good trip?" Oscar said.

"Fine trip. Axle disagrees." Erik climbed down, working the stiffness out of his back. "Pastor sent herring."

"Mother will be pleased."

"Your mother will disapprove loudly and then eat most of it, as is tradition." He handed the boy the reins. "Walk him, cool him down. I'll see what your sister has burned for supper."

"She hasn't burned anything," Oscar said. "She's been out."

"Out where?"

"Just out."

And there it was again, that too-quiet running. Erik looked at his son—his son, though no paper in Stockholm said so—and felt the small cold hand close around the back of his neck that had nothing to do with the December air. Something had happened while he was away. Something involving the observatory job, most likely, since that was the only new thing in their lives, and Erik had been uneasy about that job from the moment Hedlund assigned it. He had been uneasy in the specific way a man is uneasy when he suspects God or fate has left a message for him somewhere, and has chosen a clock case as the envelope.

"I'll walk the horse first," Oscar said.

"Take your time."

Oscar took the cart toward the stable at a pace that was slightly too quick, the way a man leaves a room when he does not want to be asked what he was doing near the drawer.

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of coffee and beeswax and Marta, which is to say it smelled of home, though Erik noticed she was polishing the same section of counter she had polished, by the look of it, since noon.

"You're limping," she said without turning.

"I'm walking thoughtfully."

"You're favoring your right knee."

"The knee and I are in negotiation."

She set down the cloth. Marta Nystrom was not a tall woman, and she was not a loud one, and people who mistook these two facts for softness generally only made the mistake once. She had hands that could coax a rusted mainspring back to life and a face that gave away exactly as much as she chose, which on ordinary days was warmth, and on days like this one was nothing at all, which was its own message.

"Erik," she said. "The letter."

The word went through him like a cold wire.

"What letter?"

"Don't." She came around the counter and stood in front of him and lowered her voice even though Linnea was out and Oscar was in the stable and there was no one in the world to hear them but the cat. "Don't do the face you do. The boy found it. In the master clock, wedged into the escapement. Deliberate, Erik. Someone put it there to be found, or someone hid it there never to be found, and either way—" Her jaw tightened. "Either way it had your wax on it. The seal you use for the shipping papers. The one from before."

Erik set down his bag very carefully, as if the bag were the thing that might break.

"Where is it now?"

"In the stove, mostly. I burned it." She held up her right hand, and he saw the fresh blister across her palm and fingers, and understood that she had reached into fire rather than let anyone else touch the thing. "I burned my hand doing it, and I'd burn the whole hand off, so don't ask me why."

"Marta—"

"He read it." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the word. "I don't know how much. I got it away from him, but Erik, he's not stupid. He was mute for a year and he still heard everything in this house. You think five years of quiet made him deaf?"

Erik sat down on the workshop stool, the good one, the one with his shape worn into it, and put both elbows on the bench, and looked at the wall of tools hanging in their careful rows—the chisels by size, the files by cut, everything in its place, everything ordered, everything controlled, because order was the religion of this household and they had all been devout for very good reasons.

"It should have been dead," he said finally. "That arrangement. It died with peacetime. I buried it myself, Marta, I buried it so deep I half convinced myself—"

"Half."

"All right. Seven-tenths."

She almost smiled. It passed over her face like sun through cloud and was gone. "Tell me what it said. What you remember of what it was supposed to say. Because our son is going to come through that door in ten minutes with a lie in his pocket, and I need to know how big the lie is."

"How do you know he'll lie?"

"Because he's yours," Marta said, "and mine, and he learned it here. And because when I took it from him he looked at me the way you look at me when you've broken something and haven't decided whether to confess. Now. Talk. While I wrap this hand."

* * *

She wrapped the burn and he told it, in the voice he used for difficult things, low and level and slightly too precise—the voice, he realized as he used it, that Oscar had inherited without knowing.

It began, as these things did, in Riga, and Erik hated beginning there because Riga was a country he had emigrated from rather than a city he had left; you did not visit it in memory, you defected.

"I told you some of it. When we took him in. The part that mattered for taking him in."

"You told me a father died and a man owed another man his life and the arithmetic was simple." Marta wound the linen snug. "I have been married to you nineteen years. I know when the sum has a remainder."

Erik turned his coffee cup a quarter turn on the bench, then another quarter turn, until it had made a full revolution and accomplished nothing.

"Dmitri Volkov saved my life twice," he said. "The first time he'd deny. That was 1891, the harbor, a crane cable let go—a crate of ship's iron, and he pulled me back by the collar like a kitten. He laughed about it for a year. Called me his slow Swede. The second time was not funny at all, and I don't tell it, and you've never asked, and I thank you for that."

"I'm asking now."

He was silent long enough that the kitchen clock—one of his own makes, honest as bread—ticked eleven times.

"There was a man," Erik said. "In Riga. A customs officer who decided I was moving more than clock parts on my manifests, and he wasn't wrong, though what I was moving wasn't contraband—it was letters. Letters for people who couldn't post letters. Exiles writing to exiles. Volkov was one of them, or adjacent to them; he was Russian, educated, half in love with ideas that get a man watched. The customs officer decided to make an example of me instead of just fining me. There was going to be a search. Not a normal search—a thorough one, the kind where things are found whether they're there or not, and then a trial, and trials in that empire ended the way they ended."

"And Volkov—"

"Volkov bought the officer," Erik said simply. "Bought him outright. Spent money he didn't have and called in favors that cost him more than money. By morning my file had been misplaced, and by spring I was on a boat to Stockholm with clean papers, and Dmitri Volkov had made himself a poor man and a marked one to keep a stranger's ledgers empty." He looked up. "That is the debt. Not money. Money I could have sent. The debt is that a man unmade his own safety to make mine, and never once, in thirty years, has he asked for anything back."

"Until now."

"No. Even now—not yet. The letter Oscar found, if it's the one I think it is, was written years ago. Nineteen-oh-eight, maybe '09. Back when the network still ran, before the war froze everything solid. It named this workshop as a relay point. Documents between Stockholm and the exile circles—Volkov's crowd, the ones the tsar's police hunted. Nothing dangerous in itself. Paper moves; paper is light. But it meant that for three years, without you knowing, without the children knowing, packages came through here addressed to names that weren't real, and I forwarded them, and I lied to you with my whole face every time, and you knew, and you let me, because you are a better woman than I am a man and you understood that some silences are load-bearing."

Marta sat down across from him. For a moment she looked tired in a way he rarely saw—tired down at the foundation.

"And after?" she said. "After the war started, after Riga fell, after all of it?"

"After all of it, the network scattered. Some dead, some fled, some swallowed by revolutions of one flavor or another. The letters stopped coming in 1912. I told myself the arrangement was over. I told myself Volkov was probably dead too, and grieved him privately, and kept the seal in the drawer anyway, like a man who keeps his father's watch though it doesn't run." He rubbed his eyes under his spectacles. "If that letter was hidden in the observatory clock, someone wanted it preserved. Or planted. And if it's been found—if Oscar read it—"

The door opened.

Oscar came in with the cold on his coat and the stable smell on his hands and the lie already settled on his face like snow, so well fitted that Erik might have missed it entirely if he hadn't been looking for it, and if Erik hadn't spent thirty years wearing the same one.

* * *

Supper was herring and potatoes and Linnea's account of her evening, which was detailed in proportion to how little of it she wished to explain. Erik ate and listened and watched his son.

Oscar ate the way he did everything—carefully, completely, without waste. He answered questions when asked. He asked none. And when Marta's bandaged hand came into view passing the butter dish, Oscar looked at it for one second too long, and looked away, and the look-away was the loudest thing at the table.

Afterward, Erik found him in the workshop, sweeping shavings that were already swept.

"The axle needs looking at Thursday," Erik said.

"I'll look at it tomorrow."

"Tomorrow you're at Hedlund's. The observatory clock won't fix itself." Erik picked up a brass gear from the bench, turned it, set it down. "Oscar. About today. At the observatory. Your mother says the clock was jammed with something that shouldn't have been there."

"A letter." Oscar's broom moved. "Old. Sealed. I didn't open it—I barely touched it. Mother took it. She burned her hand getting it away from me."

"That was careless of her."

"She was protecting us."

"From what?"

Oscar stopped sweeping. In the lamplight his face did the thing it did—went smooth, went still, became a closed case with excellent joinery. Erik recognized the craftsmanship because he had built it. Every rule of this house, every careful habit, every question-not-asked: Erik had laid those floors, and now his son stood on them and used them against him, and there was a terrible pride in that mixed with a grief that had no name in any language Erik spoke.

"I don't know," Oscar said. "I assumed it was nothing. Old correspondence somebody lost. Why? Is it something?"

The lie was good. Erik wanted to applaud it. It had structure, it had a load-bearing question placed exactly where a guilty man would place one, redirecting suspicion outward. Five years old when he arrived, and the boy had learned concealment the way he learned filing—by watching the masters.

Erik had a choice, and he knew it, and he chose the way he had chosen for nineteen years, the way he would probably choose at his own funeral: to protect the living by burying the past deeper.

"It's nothing," Erik said. "Somebody's idea of a joke, or a hiding place forgotten. If anything else turns up in that clock—anything—you bring it straight to me or your mother. Not to Linnea, not to Hedlund. To us. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

They shook on it, formally, absurdly, two liars sealing a pact of honesty, and Erik felt the handshake in his chest like a hairline crack in a mainspring—nothing today, nothing tomorrow, but someday, under load, a failure.

"Off to bed," he said gruffly. "You've got a master clock to save."

Oscar went. His footsteps on the stairs were even and unhurried, and Erik stood alone in the lamplight among his honest tools and thought: *he read it. He read enough.*

And then, because worry abhors a vacuum, a second thought arrived and stood beside the first like bailiff beside debtor:

*If the letter was in the observatory clock, someone put it there. And if someone put it there, someone knows it's gone. And if someone knows it's gone—*

Out in the dark courtyard, beneath the workshop door where no one would pass until morning, the snow lay smooth and unmarked.

Except, when Erik went to latch the outer shutter, except for one thing: a small chalked mark on the lower panel, a sign he had not seen in six years and had prayed never to see again—an old signal, from the years when paper moved through this house under false names, meaning only one thing in the whole grammar of that vanished world.

*The route is open. Expect a delivery.*

Erik stood in the cold a long time, looking at it, while behind him the kitchen clock ticked with perfect, merciless honesty, keeping time toward whatever came next.
