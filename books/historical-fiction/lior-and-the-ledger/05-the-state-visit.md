## 5. The State Visit

Linnea Nystrom had learned to read her family the way other girls read fashion plates: closely, and with an eye for what was being concealed beneath the surface.

It was not a skill she had chosen. It was thrust upon her at birth, or shortly thereafter, by virtue of living above a clockmaker's shop where every person she loved treated truth like a fragile porcelain cup—carried carefully, set down in safe places, never, ever jostled. Her father answered direct questions with weather. Her mother could deflect an inquiry about the household accounts with such grace that Linnea once watched a tax assessor leave the premises feeling grateful for the privilege of not knowing. And Oscar—

Well. Oscar was a different matter entirely. Oscar did not conceal things so much as he simply declined to exist in conversation, folding himself into corners of rooms and silences like a man who had been taught that taking up space cost money.

But this morning, over the porridge, Oscar had taken up space. He had knocked his spoon against the bowl twice without noticing. He had let Marta refill his coffee without the small nod of thanks that constituted his entire conversational repertoire before eight o'clock. And when Erik had asked him how the observatory repair was coming along, Oscar had said "fine" in a voice that meant *the opposite of fine*, and then he had looked at the letter rack by the door for four full seconds.

Four seconds. Linnea counted. Counting was another thing she'd been forced into early, growing up among people who measured everything.

She said nothing. That was the first rule of the house: say nothing, watch everything. But she buttered her bread with the particular deliberation of a girl filing something away for later, and if Oscar noticed—and he noticed most things, that was the trouble with Oscar—he gave no sign.

* * *

The city, meanwhile, had gone quietly mad, which suited Linnea's mood admirably, because it meant everyone else's strangeness would provide cover for her own.

The Russian delegation was coming. Not the Tsar himself—there had been a rumor of the Tsar, briefly, and Marta had gone pale in a way she thought nobody saw—but a delegation of ministers and naval men and at least one grand duke, arriving in ten days to review the fleet, inspect the fortifications, and be fed herring preparations until they wept. Stockholm had entered that peculiar state of municipal panic in which every lamppost was repainted, every beggar politely relocated, and every citizen reminded, via newspaper and pulpit and the postman's sighs, that the eyes of two empires would be upon them.

For the customs house where Linnea worked as a junior clerk—the only female clerk on the third floor, a distinction she had earned through competence and maintained through being slightly more frightening than the men who resented her—it meant one thing above all others.

"The manifests," said Herr Lindqvist, her supervisor, standing at her desk on Monday morning with the air of a man delivering terrible news about a relative. "Everything must be reconciled before the sweep begins. The harbor police will want records going back six months. Six months, Fröken Nystrom."

"Then I should begin immediately," Linnea said, "instead of listening to you explain it to me."

Lindqvist opened his mouth, reconsidered, and retreated to the safety of his office, where he could worry in private. Linnea allowed herself half a smile. Then she pulled the first ledger toward her and began.

Reconciliation work was, in theory, drudgery. In practice, Linnea had discovered it was the closest thing the customs house offered to detective fiction. Every ship that docked left a paper trail: cargo manifest, duty assessment, inspection certificate, each signed and countersigned and filed by clerks of varying sobriety. Most of it matched. Some of it didn't. The mismatches were where the interesting people lived—the captains who declared forty barrels of salted fish and landed fifty, the merchants whose Swedish handwriting mysteriously deteriorated when Russian goods appeared on the page.

She worked through the morning with methodical pleasure, and it was somewhere around eleven o'clock, with ink on her right ring finger and the harbor fog pressing gray against the windows, that the thought arrived—not fully formed, but circling, the way a gull circles before deciding whether to steal your lunch.

*Oscar is lying about something.*

Not the ordinary lying. Not the silence that wrapped around their house like insulation. Something specific. Something with a shape. He had come back from the observatory with parts of the master clock crated in the workshop below, and he had spent three evenings bent over them, and twice she had heard him stop working entirely—no file, no file, no file—and sit in the kind of stillness that even Oscar didn't usually achieve.

And there had been the letter. She hadn't seen a letter. But Marta had burned her hand on the stove iron the evening the crate came home—a burn far worse than carelessness explained—and had held that hand behind her back all through supper, and had looked at Oscar exactly once, across the table, with an expression Linnea had no word for except perhaps *recognition*. As though something had arrived in their house that Marta had been expecting for years.

Linnea turned a page. A shipment of machine oil from Lübeck. Duty paid. Signed, countersigned, stamped. Perfectly boring.

She made herself a small note in pencil on her personal memorandum pad, in shorthand of her own devising: *O. — observatory — M.'s hand — ?*

It was a beginning. Beginnings were her specialty. Endings tended to involve her mother saying *Linnea, really*, but you couldn't have everything.

* * *

That evening she put her plan—or the embryonic organism that would eventually become a plan—into its first motion.

The motion took the form of following her brother.

Not obviously. Linnea was many things, but she was not a fool, and following Oscar obviously would be like following a cat: he would hear you within thirty feet and vanish into some architectural impossibility. Instead she waited until Thursday, when Oscar announced at supper—he announced; the family exchanged glances as though a chair had spoken—that he would be working late at the observatory, calibrating the master clock ahead of the ceremonial signal.

"You'll take your coat," Marta said. It was not a question. Nothing Marta said was ever a question; questions invited answers.

"I'll take my coat," Oscar agreed, and did, and left.

Linnea gave him twenty minutes. She used them to wash the supper dishes faster than any dish deserved to be washed, and to inform her mother that she might visit Ingrid about the sewing circle, which had the advantage of being true for the first fifteen words of the sentence.

The fog had settled in for the night, thick and salt-smelling, the good kind of fog for walking unseen. She knew the route Oscar would take because she knew everything Oscar did, having catalogued his habits over five years the way he catalogued gear trains: uphill along Sturegatan, past the closed bakery where he always slowed down (she suspected he liked the smell of tomorrow's bread), then left toward the observatory hill. He walked fast tonight. Faster than calibration required.

She kept to the opposite side of the street, hands in her pockets, a plain dark figure among plain dark figures, and it struck her—not for the first time—that she and Oscar had learned the same trade from the same house. How to move through a city without being part of it. How to see without being seen to see. Their parents had raised two children, and somehow produced two watchers.

He didn't go straight up the observatory hill.

At the corner where the road forked—left for the observatory, right for the harbor terraces—Oscar stopped. Stopped completely, the way he did when listening, head tilted, and Linnea flattened herself against a garden wall and became, for approximately ninety seconds, part of the architecture.

Then he went right. Toward the water. Toward the docks.

Toward the docks, ten days before a police sweep that would turn every warehouse and tavern and suspicious shadow inside out, when every honest person in Stockholm was giving the waterfront the widest possible berth.

Linnea stood in the fog and felt the pleasant, dangerous click of a puzzle offering its first piece. Her brother, the boy who crossed the street to avoid conversation, was walking voluntarily into the harbor district at night.

She followed him as far as the top of Fjällgatan, and no farther, because below her the lamps of the old town wavered in the mist and the masts of the ships rose like winter trees, and she lost him somewhere between one lamp and the next. But she had seen enough. More than enough.

She stood there a long moment, breath fogging, thinking.

Here was what she knew, arranged on the table of her mind like tools on felt:

One. Something had come home in the clock crate from the observatory. Something that burned her mother's hand and silenced the whole table.

Two. Her father had returned from a coastal delivery two days later looking as though he had aged a season in forty-eight hours, and had since developed a habit of standing at the workshop window after dark, watching the street.

Three. Oscar had read whatever it was—she was certain of it now, certain the way you are certain of arithmetic—and it had sent him walking to the docks at night, alone, in fog, like a man paying off a debt or collecting one.

Four. In ten days, a Russian delegation would arrive, and with it police, and with the police, scrutiny, and their family's entire religion of careful silence was built on the assumption that no one would ever look.

Someone was about to look.

Linnea turned up her collar and started home. The sewing circle story would need reinforcing; she'd stop at Ingrid's window and borrow a genuine half hour of gossip as ballast. But underneath the domestic logistics, her mind was already running ahead, drafting lists, assigning tasks to future-Linnea. Tomorrow she would pull the harbor manifests for the past six months—all of them, not just her assigned quarter—and she would read them the way she read everything, closely, with an eye for what was hidden under the surface. If something touched her family, something would pass through the customs house. Paper always passed through the customs house. Paper was the one place in Sweden where lies were required to write themselves down.

At the door of the workshop, she paused. Through the window she could see her father at his bench, not working, just sitting with his hands flat on the wood, staring at nothing. Above him hung the wall of clocks they repaired and resold, and every one of them ticked at its own private rhythm, so that the room sounded like rain on a roof—dozens of small assurances that time was passing, that everything was proceeding normally.

But Linnea had grown up inside that sound, and she knew its secret: those clocks were not synchronized. They merely agreed to disagree politely, all day, every day. It was the same with her family. Everyone keeping their own time. Everyone pretending the hours matched.

In ten days, something would force them all onto a single dial.

"Well," she said quietly, to the fog, to the clocks, to herself. "Let's find out who's winding whom."

Inside, Erik Nystrom looked up sharply at the window—as though the glass itself had whispered—and Linnea smiled her sweetest, most uninformative smile, and went in to tell her mother about the sewing circle.

Every word of it, she resolved, absolutely true. For the first time in the history of this family, that seemed like the more dangerous choice.
