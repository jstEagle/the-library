## 6. Volkov's Shadow

The chalk mark appeared on a Tuesday, which Oscar would later decide was the most insulting part of it.

Tuesdays were delivery days. The workshop smelled of linseed oil and cold coffee, Erik was out along the coast road with a cart full of regulators bound for Norrtälje, and Marta had gone to the market with Linnea to argue with a fishmonger about the moral character of his herring. Oscar was alone at the bench, fitting a new mainspring into a carriage clock no larger than a hymnal, when he heard footsteps stop outside the door.

He had learned long ago that you could tell a great deal about a person by how they paused at a doorway. Customers hesitated hopefully. Neighbors slowed with intention. But this man stopped — stopped dead, the way a man stops when he is confirming an address — and then there was a small dry sound, like chalk whispering against wood, and the footsteps went away again at a pace just short of running.

Oscar set down his tweezers.

He crossed the workshop floor without hurrying, because hurrying was for people who wanted to be noticed, and eased the door open a hand's width. Ten meters up the lane, a figure in a gray coat was already turning the corner by the chandler's shop. He walked like a man who had somewhere to be and wished he didn't.

Oscar looked at the door.

There, at knee height, drawn in white chalk with a steadiness that suggested practice: a circle, bisected by a single vertical line, with two short ticks at the lower left like the beginnings of clock hands. A face without features. A clock without numbers.

He knew it. That was the terrible thing — he knew it the way you know a smell from childhood. Five years of living among the Nystroms had taught him their silences one by one, and one of those silences lived in this exact shape. He had seen it once before, scratched hastily and half-erased on the inside of the coal cellar door, and when he'd asked Erik about it, Erik had looked at him for a long moment and said only, "Old habit. From a friend who traveled," and had sandpapered it off himself that same evening, saying nothing else, whistling nothing at all.

Erik Nystrom did not sandpaper things without reason. Erik Nystrom considered sandpapering a form of conversation.

Oscar stood in the doorway with the November fog rolling up the hill from Saltsjön and thought about the letter wedged inside the observatory clock's escapement. He thought about the wax seal he had never been allowed to touch, about Marta's burned hand, about the way his father — his adoptive father, though the word *adoptive* had quietly fallen out of use sometime around 1910, worn smooth like an old coin — had come home from Riga five years ago carrying nothing but grief and a boy who wouldn't speak.

And he thought: someone has just told this house something, in a language the house was built to understand.

* * *

Here is what must be understood about Oscar Nystrom, aged nineteen, apprentice clockmaker, keeper of silences: he was not brave. He would have been the first to insist on this. Bravery, as far as he could tell from the novels Linnea smuggled home under her shawl, involved charging toward danger while declaring yourself. Oscar's entire education had run the other way. In the house of careful hands, the first lesson was that danger is best met sideways, quietly, preferably while holding a tool so that if anyone asks what you're doing you can say *this gear won't seat properly* and be believed.

So it was not bravery that sent him after the man in the gray coat.

It was, he would later admit, mostly spite.

Because the mark on the door hadn't been meant for him. It had been meant for Erik — or rather, for whatever network Erik had once belonged to and now, presumably, did not. Someone out there believed the Nystrom workshop was still a station on some invisible railway, still a place where a chalked circle meant *message waiting, safe hands, come after dark.* And that someone was wrong, and being wrong in that particular way could get his family arrested, and Oscar found — testing the feeling carefully, like pressing a thumb against a bruise — that he was angry about it. Genuinely, warmly, productively angry. It was almost a novelty. He decided to follow it wherever it led.

He put on his coat, took the precaution of pocketing a small screwdriver (a workman walking is invisible; a young man walking empty-handed is a question), and set the shop's regulator clock to note the hour. Then he stepped out into the fog.

Stockholm in late November is a city that keeps its own counsel, and the harbor districts keep it best of all. The fog came off the water thick and yellow-gray, smelling of tar and herring brine, and it swallowed distances. Lamplighters' posts became soft halos. Warehouse walls appeared and vanished like decisions reconsidered. Somewhere out on the water a ship's bell rang twice, unhurried, as if time itself had gotten lost in the weather and wasn't going to pretend otherwise.

The man in gray had perhaps ninety seconds' head start. In clear air that would have been hopeless. In fog, it was merely difficult — and difficulty, Oscar had found, was mostly a matter of listening, which was the one subject in which he had always excelled.

He caught him at the bottom of the hill, where the cobbles gave way to the timber planking of the fish market. Gray Coat had stopped at a lamp post and was doing something with his collar, and in the halo of light Oscar got his first real look: forty-ish, broad through the shoulders in a way that spoke of dock work or worse, with a sailor's roll to his stance even standing still. A Swede by his coat, possibly not by his vowels — when he muttered at the fog, the mutter had a Baltic flatness to it that Oscar couldn't place and didn't try to. Places, he reminded himself. Not faces. Faces could be wrong.

Gray Coat turned south along the water, past the shuttered stalls, past the customs house with its blind lit windows, and Oscar followed at the distance of one lamppost behind, matching his steps to the sound of a passing dray so his own footsteps would be swallowed. This was the sort of thing he had never done before and discovered, with mild alarm, that he was good at. Silence, it turned out, was a skill that transferred.

They came down to the quays where the fog was thickest, where the masts of moored colliers stood like the ribs of drowned cathedrals, and here Gray Coat did three things in quick succession that confirmed every suspicion Oscar had been trying not to have.

First, he checked over his shoulder — not the casual glance of a man in a strange neighborhood, but the practiced sweep of a man who expected to be followed and planned to be annoyed about it. Oscar melted behind a stack of herring barrels and studied a knot in the wood with great scholarly interest.

Second, he stopped at the office door of the shipping firm of Lindqvist & Co., agents and brokers, a respectable establishment whose name appeared on freight documents Oscar filed weekly — and chalked, low on the doorframe, the same circle-and-line, with the little clock-hands tick now pointing a different direction.

Third, he said, very quietly, to nobody visible in the fog beside the door:

"He'll want it tonight. Tell him the boy's name is on the manifests now."

Then he knocked a pattern on the door — two, pause, three — and was admitted by a light that opened and shut too fast to show anything but the smell of pipe tobacco and lamp oil.

Oscar stood among the barrels with the fog beading on his eyelashes and felt the ground do something structural beneath his feet.

*Manifests.* His family's name — Erik's name, Marta's name, the workshop's honest, careful, tax-paying, church-going, complaint-to-the-city-about-noise name — written onto shipping papers. By whom? For what? The letter in the clock had spoken of a drop point, of documents moving between Stockholm and a Russian exile named Volkov, of a debt owed since before the war scare, before everything. But this — this was different in flavor. The letter had been old ink asking an old favor. This was fresh chalk, tonight's tide, a stranger saying *the boy's name* with the easy certainty of a man reading it off paper.

Someone was not asking the Nystroms for help. Someone was dressing the Nystroms in the plot like a costume, whether the fit pleased them or not.

* * *

He waited by the barrels for the better part of an hour, which gave him ample opportunity to reflect that his feet were wet, his nose was running, and heroism was significantly colder than advertised in Linnea's novels. The fog did not lift; it deepened, taking on the blue-black weight of evening. Lamps were lit along the quay. A watchman passed, coughing, and nodded to the barrels as if they were colleagues.

At last the office door reopened and closed, and Gray Coat emerged — but not alone. With him came a second man, shorter, wrapped to the eyes in a dark overcoat, and the two of them stood close together in the lamplight speaking in voices pitched precisely below the level at which fog carries.

Almost below. Fog, like Oscar, was a creature of listening, and it betrayed them just enough.

"—not the old man's hand," the second man was saying. "That's the point of it. The old man's clean. That's why it works."

"And when they come asking?"

"They'll ask the workshop. And the workshop will say what workshops say." A thin sound that might have been laughter. "Nothing. Clockmakers are famous for it."

They moved off along the quay, toward the bridge and the city, and Oscar let them go. Following further had crossed from caution into stupidity, and stupidity, in the Nystrom household, was the one sin Marta would actually scold aloud.

He stayed among the barrels until his heart agreed to resume ordinary operations, and then he did something he had been turning over in his mind for the last quarter hour, the way you turn over a gear that won't quite mesh.

He walked to the office door of Lindqvist & Co., took out his handkerchief, breathed on the chalk mark, and wiped it away.

Then, from his pocket, he produced the stub of tailor's chalk Linnea used for marking hem lines, and beneath the spot where the signal had been, he drew — slowly, tongue between his teeth, with a craftsman's care — a small, precise, unmistakable drawing of a mainspring, broken in the middle.

It meant nothing. That was the beauty of it. It was pure nonsense, invented on the spot, and it would sit there on the doorframe puzzling anyone who came looking for the circle-and-line, telling them in effect: *the clock you think is ticking here doesn't run anymore.*

Or it meant everything, depending on who read it. Depending on whether whoever ran this network still had any respect at all for the house whose name they were stealing.

Either way, Oscar reflected, wiping chalk dust from his fingers, it was the first sentence he had ever written to a stranger. Marta would have called it rude. Erik would have called it dangerous. Both, he suspected, would have secretly approved of the draftsmanship.

* * *

The walk home took longer than usual because he took three streets he didn't need, doubled back once through the churchyard, and loitered for ten minutes in a tobacconist's doorway pretending profound interest in a window display of pipes he had no intention of buying. Paranoia, he was discovering, was simply thoroughness applied to oneself.

The workshop windows glowed warm when he finally came up the hill, and the smell that reached him at the gate was fried onions, which meant Marta was either celebrating something or preparing to interrogate someone, and with Marta these were frequently the same meal.

Linnea was in the kitchen, feet on the fender of the stove, a newspaper folded to the shipping notices on her lap — she read the shipping notices the way other girls read serial fiction, and had opinions about tonnage. She looked up as Oscar came in, and her eyes did the thing her eyes always did, the quick inventory: coat damp, shoes wet, cheeks fog-burned, chalk dust — she saw the chalk dust immediately, damn her — on the outside of his right hand.

"Evening," she said pleasantly. "Good fog for it."

"For what?" said Oscar.

"That's what I'm wondering." She turned back to her newspaper, radiating patience. "I'll want the whole story eventually, you know. I've cleared my Thursday."

"There's no story."

"Mm." She turned a page with great delicacy. "You've got chalk on your cuff, brother. And you've been crying, or the fog's been at your eyes, and I know which, because you don't cry, you leak silently and deny it, which runs in the family." She glanced up, and beneath the tease her eyes were sharp and worried and entirely awake. "Whatever it is — and it *is*, I can see it sitting on you like a wet cat — you should know Father's back. Came in an hour ago. He's in the parlor with Mother, and they've had the door shut, and when it opened, Father was holding the poker like he'd forgotten he was holding it."

Oscar stood in the kitchen doorway with onion smoke curling around him and the fog still in his bones, and thought about the poker. Erik Nystrom held tools the way other men held opinions — constantly, competently, and with total unconsciousness. But the poker came out only twice a year: when the fire needed building, and when something needed killing.

"Did he say anything?"

"He asked if you'd gone out. Mother said you'd gone for parts. Which was interesting," Linnea said, "because you didn't take your parts bag."

From the parlor, through the door, came the low murmur of Erik's voice, and Marta's answering murmur, and beneath both, faint as a heartbeat under wool, the steady tick of the workshop regulator keeping its perfect, indifferent time.

Oscar hung up his coat. Whatever had come through that fog tonight — chalk marks, stolen names, men in gray coats speaking of manifests and boys — it had reached the parlor ahead of him, or something like it had. The house that ran on silence was humming with it now, every gear loaded, every spring wound tight.

Tomorrow, he thought, he would go back to Lindqvist & Co.'s door. Tomorrow he would find out whose hand had written his family onto those forged papers, and why the handwriting felt — though he couldn't yet prove it, though it was only a prickle at the base of his skull, a clockmaker's instinct for a mechanism running slightly wrong — familiar.

But tonight there were onions, and a poker standing sentinel in the parlor, and a sister watching him over the top of a newspaper with the expression of someone who had already begun clearing her Thursday.

He went in to dinner.
