## 7. The Informant

Anton Reval had learned long ago that the secret to surviving a life of small dishonesties was to keep the honest parts of it immaculate.

His desk at the customs house was therefore the tidiest in the building. His ledgers balanced to the öre. His inkwell sat exactly two fingers from the blotter's edge, and his pen rested parallel to it, and if you had asked him why, he would have said — truthfully, because he lied so rarely about things that did not matter — that a man whose work is crooked ought to keep his desk straight, as an apology to the world.

He was forty-six years old, thin in the way of men who forget lunches, with a mustache that had gone grey unevenly, as though it could not quite commit either way. He wore the same brown suit three days out of five and the good grey one when he expected trouble, which lately was most days.

The trouble had a shape. It was roughly the shape of four hundred and thirty kronor.

"Reval." The chief clerk, old Blomberg, appeared at his shoulder the way weather appears over water. "Manifests for the *Svea* line. Before noon."

"Before noon," Reval agreed, and Blomberg moved off, satisfied, because Blomberg had never in twenty-two years checked whether Reval actually did anything by noon, only whether he agreed to.

Anton looked at the stack of manifests and felt the familiar arithmetic begin behind his eyes, unbidden, like a song stuck in the head. Rent: sixty-one kronor, due Friday. Elsa's shoes: eleven. The doctor for Henrik's chest: fifteen, unless the doctor had raised his rates again, which he had, twice since Michaelmas. And then the other debt, the one with no column in any ledger, the one that lived not on paper but in the damp voice of a man named Krüger who came by on Thursdays and smiled too much and never raised his voice, because he didn't need to. Four hundred and thirty kronor, and interest that accrued with the quiet confidence of a thing that knew it would be paid.

There were, Anton had calculated over many sleepless nights, exactly three ways out of such a hole. You could inherit money, marry money, or find money. He had no rich relatives and no prospects of any, having been married for nineteen years to a woman he loved, who was as poor as he was and considerably better company than money would have been anyway.

Which left finding money. And finding money, for a customs clerk in Stockholm in the autumn of 1913, meant selling what he had to sell.

What he had to sell was information. Not much of it, and none of it very good — arrival times, cargo irregularities, the names of ships' masters who watered down their manifests. But there was a market for even poor information, and Anton sold to two buyers. One was a shipping concern that liked advance notice of inspections; they paid poorly but reliably. The other was the police.

The police paid better.

* * *

The dockside café where he met his police contact was called the Ankaren — The Anchor — which was optimistic naming for an establishment that seemed held together mainly by nicotine and habit. It stood between a sailmaker's loft and a chandlery, close enough to the water that the floorboards sweated salt in warm weather, and in cold weather the stove smoked and everyone pretended not to mind because the alternative was standing outside.

Anton arrived at half past two on Thursday, ordered coffee he did not want, and took the corner table where he could see both the door and the window. This was not paranoia. It was habit. There is a difference, though it is narrower than most people think.

His contact was a police sergeant named Lundgren, a large placid man built along the lines of a wardrobe, who drank his coffee with three sugars and spoke of his wife's garden the way other men speak of their children.

"You look terrible," Lundgren observed fondly.

"I always look like this."

"No, you usually look merely bad. Today it's terrible. New trouble?"

"The same trouble. It has simply grown." Anton stirred his coffee without drinking it. "Krüger wants payment by month's end. All of it."

"Mm." Lundgren did not offer sympathy; sympathy was not in his budget, any more than it was in Anton's. Instead he slid a folded paper across the table under the pretense of wiping up a spill. Inside was a list — names, dates, a ship called the *Ehrensvärd* out of Åbo. "We're interested in paper moving east. Documents, mostly. Nothing heavy, nothing bulky. Somebody's been running a courier line through the harbor, and it isn't smugglers in the ordinary sense. Ordinary smugglers move brandy. This one moves letters that make important people sweat."

"And what do I get?"

"Fifty now. More if the name leads somewhere."

"A hundred."

Lundgren looked at him with mild reproach. "You've gotten expensive."

"My debts have gotten expensive. It's the same inflation affecting us all."

Lundgren sighed the sigh of a man negotiating with a beggar he secretly respected, and said, "Seventy-five, and I'll speak to Krüger myself about giving you until Christmas. He listens to the department. He knows we find you useful."

This was, Anton reflected, the closest thing to kindness the arrangement had ever produced: a policeman offering to lean on his loan shark. He accepted it gratefully and without comment, because commenting on kindness made it harder to come by.

"There is one thing already," he said, before Lundgren could leave. "I don't have a name yet. But I have a mark."

"A mark?"

"On the shipping papers. A stamp — a small gear wheel, struck in violet ink. It's been appearing on consignments that shouldn't exist. Goods received that no warehouse shows, fees paid that no ledger records. Someone with access to a proper seal has been making entries, and the entries are clean. Very clean. Whoever cuts that stamp knows his trade."

Lundgren's eyebrows went up a fraction, which from him was the equivalent of another man's open astonishment. "A forger."

"A good one. And here's the part worth your seventy-five kronor." Anton lowered his voice, though the nearest patron was a sleeping fish-porter who could not have been roused by cannon fire. "I know who cuts stamps around this harbor. There are perhaps four men who can do work that clean, and I buy my own little necessities from one of them. If the violet gear turns up in his handiwork — or if he's seen it, or been offered it — I'll hear. For a price."

"Everything for a price with you, Reval."

"Yes," Anton said. "That is the entire tragedy of my character, and I'd thank you not to summarize it while my coffee's still hot."

* * *

Gustav Ek kept a workshop above a rope-walk on Södermalm, reached by a stair that smelled of tar and cat. He was a small neat man with enormous careful hands — hands that seemed designed by a different God than the rest of him — and he greeted Anton the way a priest greets a familiar sinner: without judgment, and without hope of reform.

"The violet ink man," Anton said, once they were upstairs and the door shut. Ek's workshop was astonishingly orderly for a criminal enterprise. Stamps hung on pegs in rows like tools, each labeled in a tiny precise hand. Somewhere below, a cat complained about the stairs it had just climbed.

Ek went still, in the particular way of craftsmen considering whether to lie to a customer. "Who asks?"

"The police ask. I'm asking first, because I'd rather sell them the answer than let them find it themselves and wonder why I hadn't."

"They'd wonder," Ek agreed. He crossed to his bench, thought, then unlocked a drawer Anton had never seen opened. From it he lifted a strip of paper bearing test impressions — a dozen small stamps, trial strikes, the way a type-setter proofs his letters. Most were in black or red. Three were in violet.

And there it was. The gear wheel. Small, twelve teeth, crisp as a coin.

"That mark," Ek said, "is not mine. I want that understood and possibly written down and witnessed."

"But you've seen it."

"I've been *offered* it." Ek set the strip down and wiped his hands on his apron, though they were clean. "Three weeks ago. A fellow comes to me — polite, well-spoken, gloves, the whole performance — says he needs duplicates cut from this master. Twelve-toothed gear, violet ink, and a matching signature block. Offers me forty kronor a copy."

"And you refused, being an artist of principle."

"I refused because forty kronor is an insult for work that clean, and because—" Ek paused, and for the first time Anton saw genuine unease move through him like a draft under a door. "Because I recognized the style of the master. The cutting. The letter-spacing on the accompanying signature. I know that hand, Anton. It belongs to a man I trained myself, years ago, before I went respectable-ish. A young fellow. Quick fingers, quick temper."

"A name."

"Nystrom," Ek said. "Young Nystrom. Or that's the name he used then, and names on this waterfront are like hats — passed around, worn by whoever fits them." He tapped the violet impressions. "But the mark isn't his alone anymore, understand? The polite gentleman with the gloves took the design away when I turned him down, and God knows who's striking it now. What I can tell you is where the young man worked, back when I knew him. A clockmaker's shop. Hedlund & Sons, up past Slussen. Family business, or near enough. Quiet people. The kind nobody ever looks at twice."

Anton wrote the name down in his small straight hand, feeling the peculiar chill of a man who has just picked up a thread and felt, at its far end, the faint tug of something large beginning to move.

*Nystrom.* It sounded like nothing. It sounded like bread and woodsmoke and Sunday dinner. That was the trouble with names; the guilty ones sounded exactly like the innocent ones.

* * *

He went home through the early dark — the dark coming earlier every day now, October tightening its grip — and climbed the stairs to the flat on Katarina väg where the lamp was lit and the smell of onion and dill meant Elsa had beaten him home from her sewing work, as she always did, because she was faster at everything than he was and had been for nineteen years.

Henrik was at the table doing sums, fourteen years old and serious about it, his collar too big for him in the way of boys growing faster than their clothes. His chest had been weak since the winter he was nine, and every rattling breath he took was a debt of its own, entered in a ledger Anton kept nowhere and consulted constantly.

"Papa," Henrik said, not looking up, "if a man borrows two hundred kronor at six percent, how long before—"

"Longer than he thinks," Anton said, hanging up his coat, "and sooner than he hopes. That's how interest works. It's the only honest dishonesty in the world."

Elsa laughed from the stove. "Don't teach the boy economics at dinner. He'll end up a banker."

"Worse fates," Anton said, and kissed the top of his son's head, and sat, and ate, and told himself — as he told himself every evening, with diminishing conviction — that the thing he did at the docks had nothing to do with this table, this lamp, this boy. That the compartments were separate. That information about strangers' crimes was a commodity like coal or herring, and someone would sell it whether he did or not, so it might as well be him, so that Henrik could keep breathing through the winter.

It was nearly true. Most of his life was nearly true; that was what made the false parts fit so neatly.

Later, when Henrik had gone to bed and Elsa was mending by the lamp, Anton took out his notebook — the plain one, the one that lived behind a loose brick in his heart rather than in the flat — and added a line beneath his other jottings:

*Nystrom. Clockmakers, near Slussen. Violet gear. Ask at Hedlund & Sons.*

Then he sat looking at it for a long time.

Because here was the thing he had not put in the notebook, the thing he was not sure he wanted to know even in his own handwriting: the polite gentleman with the gloves — the one Ek described, the one shopping the forged mark around the harbor — had been seen twice more since. Once near the customs house itself. And once, according to a porter who talked too much for the price of a drink, asking questions at the office of the harbor pilot commission about a coastal delivery run that had come up from the south the week before.

Questions about deliveries. Questions about a clockmaker's shop. Questions wearing gloves.

Somewhere across the city tonight, Anton thought, there was a household that had done nothing yet, that perhaps had done nothing at all, that was at this moment eating supper by lamplight and complaining mildly about the price of coal — and he was holding the pen that could draw a circle around all of it.

He thought of Henrik's collar, too big, growing. He thought of the word *Nystrom*, sitting in his notebook like a seed in frozen ground, waiting for spring, waiting for money, waiting for him.

He closed the notebook and hid it, and told Elsa the office had given him a headache, which was true in spirit if not in letter, and went to bed and lay awake listening to his son breathe.

By morning, he had decided what a tired man always decides: not yet. Not yet. There would be time. There was always time, right up until there wasn't.

On Friday, he took the notebook to Sergeant Lundgren.

* * *

They met at the Ankaren again, out of schedule, which itself meant something. Lundgren had brought a second man this time — a lean inspector with careful eyes and a courteous manner that Anton distrusted immediately and completely, the way one animal distrusts another. He introduced himself as Dahl.

"Hedlund & Sons," Dahl repeated, when Anton laid out what he had. "Clockmakers." Something flickered across the inspector's face — not surprise; recognition, perhaps, of a piece arriving that he had already imagined the shape of. "And the observatory contracts its repairs to them, does it not? The master clock."

"I wouldn't know about clocks," Anton said honestly. "I only know about paper. And the paper says Nystrom."

Dahl and Lundgren exchanged a look over his head, the look of men dividing a pie he had baked.

"You'll keep buying from your forger friend," Dahl said. It was not a question. "Anything touching that violet mark, anything at all — names, dates, deliveries. In exchange, your situation with Mr. Krüger becomes... less pressing. Consider it settled by Christmas."

Consider it settled. Anton heard what was underneath the words, the way he heard everything, because hearing everything was his entire profession: *keep selling, keep quiet, and we will remember — and if it goes wrong, we will also remember.*

"Christmas," Anton said. "And fifty kronor against it. My son needs shoes."

"Your son," said Inspector Dahl, with a smile that was almost kind, "has expensive taste in footwear."

That night the warrant was drafted — unsigned, unhurried, a document lying in a drawer like a loaded gun in a respectable home. On it, in Dahl's small exact hand, was written a single name:

**Nystrom, Erik. Clockmaker.**

And in the drawer it lay, patient as winter, while across the city the lamps of a certain workshop burned warm and gold against the dark, and a family that had not yet heard the noose creak went about its evening — winding clocks, mending clothes, keeping its careful silences — entirely unaware that somewhere in a cold office by the harbor, someone was learning how to say their name.
