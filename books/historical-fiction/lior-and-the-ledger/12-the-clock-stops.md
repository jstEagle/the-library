## 12. The Clock Stops

There is a particular sound a master clock makes when it is dying, and I had heard it once before, in the spring of 1911, when the observatory's regulator caught its own pendulum and shuddered to a halt like a man remembering mid-sentence that he had left the stove on.

I was not there to hear it the second time. That is the thing about catastrophes—they prefer to happen while you are elsewhere, eating lunch, or in my case standing in the workshop arguing with a mainspring that had opinions of its own.

* * *

It began, as most of our recent troubles did, with an ordinary Tuesday.

"Oscar." Linnea put her head around the door of the bench room without knocking, which she never did, which meant something. "A boy from the observatory is at the counter. He says—" she paused, and I watched her choose between the dramatic version and the true one, as she always did, "—he says the clock has stopped."

I set down my pliers. The mainspring could wait; it had been waiting since October and showed no sign of taking offense.

"Stopped how?"

"Stopped stopped. As in not going. As in forty minutes before the ceremonial signal on Thursday, the most important clock in Sweden is sitting in the dark doing nothing whatsoever." She folded her arms. "The boy says Herr Lindqvist is beside himself. He asked for you by name."

By name. My name. I felt something cold settle behind my ribs, small and precise, like a screw dropped down the back of a case.

"They asked for me?"

"You're the one who fixed it last time. Apparently you've developed a reputation." Linnea's eyes narrowed slightly, the way they did when she was three steps ahead of everyone and enjoying it. "Which is odd, isn't it? For someone whose entire strategy in life is to be forgettable."

"I'm not *forgettable*, I'm *unobtrusive*. There's an art to it."

"Mm." She stepped aside to let me pass, and as I went by she said, quietly, so the apprentice boy at the counter couldn't hear: "Oscar. Be careful."

It was the kind of thing Marta said. Hearing it from Linnea made the cold spot behind my ribs spread by a full degree.

* * *

The observatory stood on its hill above the city like a patient old professor who had seen every kind of foolishness and expected more. By the time I arrived, huffing up the ice-slicked path with my toolbag, a small cluster of men had gathered around the entrance: Lindqvist the timekeeper, two assistants, and—my stomach dropped—a constable in a blue coat, stamping his feet against the cold and looking profoundly bored.

"Herr Nystrom," Lindqvist said, seizing my hand and pumping it as though I were a well itself in need of winding. "Thank God. Thank *God*. It simply—stopped. Forty minutes before the signal rehearsal, can you imagine? If it fails Thursday, when the delegation—" He couldn't finish. He didn't have to. Everyone in Stockholm knew what Thursday held: the Russian imperial delegation, the state visit, the ceremonial time-signal broadcast from this very room to telegraph offices across the capital. A stopped clock here wasn't a broken machine. It was a national embarrassment with an international audience.

"When did it halt?" I asked, already moving toward the clock room.

"Just after eleven. We heard it—the silence. You know how you hear it."

I did know. A running clock fills a room the way water fills a jar; you don't notice it until it drains away.

The constable followed us in. "Routine," he said, when I glanced at him. "Anything involving the delegation gets reported. Don't mind me."

I minded him. But I said nothing, because saying nothing was the first language I had ever learned fluently, and I opened the tall case of the master clock and looked at what was inside.

At first glance, nothing. The great weight-driven movement sat in its case, dignified and still. Then I knelt, brought my lamp close, and saw it.

The escapement was jammed. Not broken—jammed. And the object doing the jamming was wedged deliberately between the pallet and the escape wheel, positioned with a craftsman's care, so that when the wheel next turned it would bind, stall, and stop the whole train dead. Someone had done this on purpose. Someone had opened this case, reached past the pendulum, and placed that obstruction exactly where it would do the most harm at exactly the right hour.

And the obstruction was familiar. Horribly, specifically familiar.

It was a sliver of brass stock, filed to shape, with a maker's punch mark near one end. I knew that punch mark the way you know your own handwriting. I had cut a dozen blanks from the same bar myself, in the workshop on Regeringsgatan, under Erik Nystrom's approving eye.

Brass from Hedlund & Sons' own stores. Brass that had passed through my hands.

"Well?" Lindqvist hovered at my shoulder. "What is it? What stopped it?"

I had perhaps four seconds to decide whether to lie. I want it noted, for the record of my own conscience, that lying had recently become easier for me, and that I hated how easy it had become, and that I did it anyway.

"A piece of debris," I said. "Old solder, by the look of it. It must have worked loose and fallen into the train." I eased the sliver free and closed my fist around it, warm metal against cold palm. "The mechanism itself is undamaged. If you'll give me an hour, I can have it running again."

"An hour! Ha!" Lindqvist clapped the constable on the shoulder as though the man had personally repaired the clock. "You see? This is why we ask for Nystrom. The boy has golden hands."

The boy has golden hands. I thought of the brass sliver hidden in my fist, of the work order pinned to the workshop wall with my name on it in Erik's careful ledger script, of the constable now watching me kneel in the clock case with his hands in his pockets and his boredom beginning, very slightly, to sharpen into attention.

Golden hands. Yes. And someone had just used them to point a finger.

* * *

I worked for an hour with the constable three paces behind me, narrating my repairs aloud in the flat tone I used for customers, which is to say saying only true things in a way that invited no further questions. Cleaned the escapement. Checked the oiling. Reset the beat. The truth was convenient: the sabotage had damaged nothing but the schedule.

When the pendulum took up its swing again—that deep, patient heartbeat filling the dome—I stood and wiped my hands and let myself feel, for exactly three seconds, the simple animal pleasure of a machine brought back to life. Then I packed my tools and carried my problem home.

Not all of it. The brass sliver stayed in my pocket. The constable would have wanted it as evidence, and evidence was the one thing I could not let travel to the police station, where someone—someone with access to Hedlund & Sons' supplier lists, someone who knew our brass, our punch marks, our work orders—would find exactly what they hoped to find.

Because that was the shape of it. I had seen it clearly on the walk down the hill, the way you see the whole mechanism at once when you finally understand what a train of wheels is for.

Whoever jammed that escapement had used brass from our workshop. They had done it days ago, knowing the clock would stop close to the ceremony, knowing the police would be called, knowing the investigation would follow the material backward—and the material led straight through our supplier, our stock, our punch mark, to the repair order bearing the name Nystrom. My name. Signed out in the workshop ledger in ink that any clerk could match.

They hadn't sabotaged a clock. They had sabotaged *us*, using a clock as the delivery mechanism. The frame hadn't sprung yet—it was still closing, tooth by tooth, and the teeth were ledgers and shipping manifests and a customs clerk somewhere with a warrant draft on his desk.

Reval. I thought of Anton Reval, whom I'd met in the fog, tired-eyed and honest in the way of men who hate their own jobs, holding papers with our name forged across them. Was he part of this? Or was he, too, a gear in someone else's train, turning because he was placed there?

I didn't know. That was the worst of it. I had spent five years learning to read silences—Marta's hands stilling over dough, Erik's pauses before certain words—and now the silence I needed to read belonged to strangers, to a city, to people who moved pieces on a board I could barely see.

* * *

Marta met me at the door with the expression she wore when she had already heard bad news and was waiting to learn how much worse it was. News travels fast in a neighborhood; the observatory boy had probably told the baker, and the baker told everyone.

"It's fixed," I said. "Running fine."

"And?"

She always knew there was an *and*. I took the brass sliver from my pocket and set it on the kitchen table between the bread board and the salt cellar. She looked at it for a long moment. Her face didn't change, but her hands did—the small tremor she thought none of us ever noticed, though all of us had learned to watch for it.

"That's ours," she said quietly.

"The punch mark. Our stock. Whoever planted it wants the police to trace it here."

Erik came in from the front room, wiping glue from his fingers, and read the table the way he read everything—in silence, completely, before speaking. When he finally looked up at me, I saw something in his face I had rarely seen there: not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man meeting an old acquaintance he had hoped was dead.

"This is how it starts," he said. "In Riga, before the trouble, there was a warehouse fire. Arson, they said. And do you know what they found in the ashes?" He tapped the brass sliver without touching it. "Something belonging to a man they wanted gone. The fire was the message. The object was the signature. Nobody was ever arrested, because nobody needed to be. The man understood, and he left."

"You think this is a warning, then? To us?"

"I think a warning is what it is if we're lucky." Erik sat down heavily. "If we're unlucky, it's the first page of a case file."

Linnea appeared in the doorway, coat still on, cheeks pink from the cold, and I realized she had been out—not at the shop, not anywhere she'd said she'd be. She looked at the brass sliver on the table, then at me, and something passed between us, quick as a spark off a grinding wheel.

"The customs house clerk I know," she said carefully, "mentioned today that warrants are being drafted. Something about dock irregularities. He didn't say names." A pause. "He didn't have to. He looked at me strangely, Oscar. Like he was sorry."

Marta's hand found Erik's on the table. I watched my father turn his own hand over and hold hers, and I thought: this family, which survived on silence, which kept itself alive the way a banked fire survives the night—this family is being pulled apart link by link, and the tool being used is my own good reputation with clocks.

They asked me for Nystrom, Lindqvist had said. Asked for me *by name*.

I had signed the original repair order in September. Anyone with access to the observatory's files—anyone inside, anyone trusted, anyone whose job it was to keep the master clock running—could have known exactly whose hands the police should follow.

"Tomorrow," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, "I'm going back to check the clock's regulation. Officially. And I'm going to find out who requested me by name, and who had keys to the clock room, and who was alone with that case long enough to wedge our brass into its heart."

"Oscar," Marta began.

"No." I surprised all three of them, and myself most of all. "They've used our name, our brass, and my work order. Every hour I stay quiet, the frame closes another notch. Silence kept us safe for five years, Morsan. It won't keep us safe from this. This was built to be discovered by people who ask questions—and the only question that matters now is who built it."

The clock in the hall ticked into the quiet I'd left behind me. Outside, snow was starting again, fat slow flakes falling straight down, the kind of snow that muffles footsteps.

Linnea pulled out a chair and sat, deliberately, like a woman settling in for a long night's work. "Then we'd better start asking them," she said.

None of us knew yet that by morning there would be a knock at the door, and a courteous voice, and Inspector Dahl's card held between gloved fingers—and that Aino was asleep upstairs, breathing softly in the dark of the workshop loft, one floor above the man come to arrest us all.
