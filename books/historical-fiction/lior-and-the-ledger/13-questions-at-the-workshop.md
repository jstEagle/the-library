## 13. Questions at the Workshop

Erik Nystrom had faced down customs men in Riga, a cholera quarantine in Danzig, and one memorable night in 1904 when a horse collapsed on the Skeppsbron quay and he had talked three gendarmes into believing he had nothing whatsoever to do with it. He had, in fact, had everything to do with it. But those lies had been small ones, practical ones, the sort a man tells with his hat still on.

The knock came at nine minutes past ten on Thursday morning, and Erik knew at once that this lie would be different, because his hands were shaking before he even reached the door.

"Master Nystrom." The man on the step was fiftyish, gray at the temples, wearing an overcoat too fine for the weather and an expression of almost apologetic courtesy. "Inspector Dahl. Stockholm police. May I come in? It's bitter out here, and I promise I'm more comfortable than I look."

Behind him stood a younger officer with a notebook and the posture of a man who wished he were anywhere else.

"Marta," Erik called, and was proud that his voice came out level. "We have visitors."

* * *

They had known since dawn, of course. The observatory clock had stopped forty minutes before the ceremonial signal yesterday — the whole city had heard about it within the hour, and by evening every newspaper boy on Södermalm was shouting about sabotage and foreign agents as though selling papers were the same thing as understanding them. And when the police began asking questions at the observatory, they found, pinned neatly to the repair ledger like a fly in amber, the work order bearing the name Nystrom & Sons.

Oscar's work order. Oscar's name, entered in his own careful apprentice hand.

"He'll want to talk to you first," Marta had said that morning, braiding her hair with the unhurried precision she brought to everything, including, apparently, catastrophe. "You're the master of the house. That's how these men think."

"I haven't lied to a policeman since I was younger than Linnea."

"Then you're overdue for practice." She tied off the braid, looked at him in the little mirror, and added, not unkindly: "Erik. Don't try to be clever. You are many good things, but clever is not among them. Say less than you know. I will handle the rest."

It should have wounded his pride. Instead it steadied him, the way her voice always did — the way it had five years ago, when a mute boy stood dripping in their doorway holding a suitcase with a broken latch, and Marta had said *he stays* in exactly that tone and closed the subject forever.

Now there was another young person in their house who could not afford to be seen, and Marta had said *she stays* in the same tone, and Erik had discovered that at fifty-three years old he could still be overruled completely by a woman holding a kettle.

Which is why, at this moment, Aino — the courier, the snowstorm guest, the reason his heart had been climbing toward his throat for two days — was folded into the longcase clock in the front workshop.

It had been Marta's idea, delivered in a whisper while Dahl's footsteps were still audible on the stairs outside. The clock case was Erik's finest unfinished piece, a tall regulator commissioned by a shipping office, its movement not yet installed. There was room inside for a slim young woman to stand among the bare weights and pulleys, behind two false panels Marta had designed years ago for storing veneer — panels that had never once been used for anything dishonest until this week, which Erik felt said something about how a household goes wrong, namely all at once, between breakfast and lunch.

"She can't stay in there past noon," Erik had whispered back. "She'll faint, or sneeze, or—"

"Then we finish this conversation before noon." Marta had pressed his hand once, briefly, and gone to answer nothing, because answering doors was apparently now his job.

* * *

Dahl accepted coffee. This seemed, Erik thought, like a bad sign. Honest men offer coffee; policemen who accept it are settling in.

"Your apprentice — your son, the papers say son—" Dahl began.

"Foster son," Erik said, and heard himself say it too quickly, and watched the inspector's eyebrows perform a small, polite gymnastics. *Too fast, Erik.* He made himself slow down. "Oscar. He's been with us five years. He's a good worker."

"So I understand. His work at the observatory is well spoken of. Which makes what happened there stranger, doesn't it?" Dahl turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, precisely, like a man regulating an escapement. "A master clock stops forty minutes before the most important time-signal of the year. Sabotage, the newspapers say. And the last hands inside that clock case belonged to a boy from your house."

"The clock was failing when we sent him," Erik said. This much was true; this much he could lean on. "Worn pallets, dirty oil, a mainspring that had been complaining for a year. Anyone who knows clocks would tell you the same. If a cart horse dies, you don't accuse the farrier."

"No," Dahl agreed pleasantly. "But if the farrier has recently been seen talking to Russians—"

The word landed in the room like a dropped wrench.

"—one does begin to wonder," Dahl finished, and smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes.

Erik's mouth opened. Closed. In the silence he could hear the shop clock ticking, the kitchen clock ticking, Linnea's mended mantel clock ticking from the parlor where she sat — he hoped — keeping very still. Everything in his life ticked. Nothing in his life was helping.

"I don't know any Russians," he said.

And that was the moment Marta swept in with the coffee tray she had already refilled, laughing — actually laughing — and set it down and said, "Inspector, if my husband knew any Russians he'd have told me by now, mostly to complain about them. He complains about everyone. Ask him about the Danish importer who shorted us on brass stock, go on, ask, you'll get twenty minutes." She sat, arranged her skirts, and added warmly, "He's a wonderful complainer. It's his only talent besides the clocks."

"That's not true," Erik said, stung into speech. "I'm also adequate at accounts."

"You see?" Marta spread her hands toward Dahl, delighted. "Adequate. This is a man who cannot lie. He once told my sister her bread was 'an interesting texture' and then apologized for a week."

Dahl laughed — a real laugh, which frightened Erik worse than the eyebrow gymnastics had — and wrote something in his book anyway. Erik understood, dimly, that he had just watched his wife build a wall out of true things, brick by brick, each brick verifiable, the whole structure a fortress of misdirection. He resolved to study her method for the rest of his life, assuming he had one.

"Now," Marta said, pouring, "you wanted to ask about the observatory work. Ask Oscar. He did the repair, he keeps the notes, he can tell you to the minute when he left and what he touched. Although—" she paused, and here her voice acquired just enough weight to matter, "—I'd thank you to be gentle with him. The boy doesn't speak easily. He lost his father, you know. He listens more than he talks, and people mistake that for strangeness when it's really only grief learning manners."

Something crossed Dahl's face then — brief, quickly governed, but real. "I lost someone myself," he said. "Long ago. I remember how people talk." He clicked his pen shut. "I'll be gentle. But I will still ask."

"Ask away," said Marta. "Just not through our dinner."

* * *

Oscar came down when called, and Erik watched his son — his son, whatever the papers said — cross the workshop floor with his hands quiet at his sides and his face arranged into the blankness Erik knew so well, the look of a boy who had learned at eight years old that the safest expression is no expression at all.

*Say less than you know,* Marta had told Erik. Oscar had been living that instruction since before any of them met him.

"You repaired the observatory's master clock," Dahl said. "Tell me about the escapement."

"It was worn. I replaced the pallets, cleaned the train, re-hung the pendulum." A pause. "The clock was dying, sir. Not murdered. Just old."

"And yet it stopped anyway."

"Yes." Oscar's eyes did not move from Dahl's face. Erik, watching, saw what no one else in the room could see — the tiny tension at the corner of his son's jaw, the held breath, the mind running calculations behind a shuttered window. "I hadn't finished. I was coming back Friday to regulate it against the telegraph time. Whoever stopped it chose the gap in my schedule."

"Convenient for you," Dahl observed.

"Inconvenient," Oscar said. "My work order is in your ledger with my name on it. If I meant to stop that clock, sir, I'd have signed someone else's name." A beat, and then — and Erik nearly sat down with relief and pride in equal measure — with the ghost of dryness: "Or no name. Forgers generally skip the signature."

Dahl studied him for a long moment. "How old are you?"

"Nineteen."

"Hm." The inspector wrote. "When you opened the clock case — the full case, during the repair — did you find anything in it? Papers. Sealed letters. Anything that didn't belong to the mechanism?"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere in the front workshop, behind two false panels, Erik would later swear he heard a human being stop breathing entirely.

"No, sir," said Oscar.

"Nothing wedged in the works? Nothing tucked behind a plate?" Dahl's voice stayed mild as morning milk. "Clocks are marvelous hiding places. I've found things in clock cases that would curl your hair. Confessions. Love letters. Once, in Göteborg, a very small pistol."

"Clocks keep time," Oscar said evenly. "Not secrets."

"Ah." Dahl smiled again. "That would make a lovely line in a novel. In my experience it's the opposite."

He asked a few more questions — dates, hours, the names of observatory staff — and Oscar answered each one plainly, correctly, and without one unnecessary word, and Erik understood he was watching a masterclass conducted by a nineteen-year-old who had never taken a lesson except from life itself. When Dahl finally closed his notebook, Erik exhaled.

"One more thing," Dahl said, standing, settling his coat. "Routine. We're speaking with everyone connected to harbor trades this week. Your coastal deliveries, Master Nystrom — do you ever carry parcels for other parties? Documents, correspondence? Shipping men sometimes do."

"No," said Erik.

It came out wrong. He heard it come out wrong — a half-second too fast, pitched slightly high, the lie of a man whose conscience had never developed calluses. Marta's hand settled on his forearm, light as a falling leaf.

"He means no *paid* commissions," she said smoothly. "Though honestly, Inspector, half the docks owes him favors. Last winter he carried a widow's trunk to Vaxholm free because her son died on the ice. If you're looking for a smuggler, you've found a soft touch."

"A soft touch," Dahl repeated, and wrote it down, and Erik had the distinct feeling the phrase was going into the file under some heading he would not enjoy reading.

At the door, the inspector paused. "Thank you for your hospitality, fru Nystrom. Herr Nystrom. Young Oscar." He put on his hat. "Oh — one small thing. Purely administrative." He drew a card from his breast pocket and held it up between two fingers, unhurried. "In the course of the week we've been reviewing customs records. There's a clerk at the harbor house, a forger by trade, who's been rather helpful to us. Name of Lindqvist. Does the name mean anything to anyone?"

"No," said Marta.

"No," said Erik.

"No, sir," said Oscar, in perfect unison with them both, which under other circumstances would have been funny.

"Mm," said Dahl, and left the card on the hall table anyway. "If it comes to mean something. Day or night."

* * *

They waited until the younger officer's boots reached the street. They waited until the boots faded. They waited, on Marta's instruction, another full quarter hour, during which nobody moved and the three clocks ticked like a jury deliberating.

Then Erik opened the clock case, and Aino stepped out on legs gone wooden, gray-faced, her breath shallow, and Marta caught her elbow and steered her to a chair and put brandy in her before anyone said a word.

"They heard me," Aino whispered. "When the inspector asked about things in clock cases. I could hear everything. I'm sorry — I couldn't help hearing—"

"You heard what we heard," Marta said. "Drink."

Erik lowered himself onto the workbench stool, and found that his legs had opinions about the morning too. "Lindqvist," he said. "The forger. He told the police we're involved."

"Or he told them something close enough," Linnea said from the doorway, where she had materialized the way she always did, silent as her brother. Her chin was set in a way Erik recognized and dreaded; it was her mother's chin, armed. "Papa. He left the card. He *wants* us to run to Lindqvist, or panic, or both. But there's a third thing he wants, and it took me all morning to see it." She came forward, and her voice dropped. "Dahl said 'in the course of the week.' He said the forger's been *helpful*. Helpful clerks don't stay helpful, Papa. Men like that sell to both sides until one side gets tired of paying."

The workshop went cold around the stove's warmth.

"You think," Erik said slowly, "that whoever is using our name might decide Lindqvist has become expensive."

Linnea looked at her brother. Her brother looked at Linnea. And Oscar — quiet, careful Oscar, who had not spoken an unnecessary word in five years — said:

"Then somebody has to reach him first."

Outside, the snow had begun again, thickening over the rooftops, erasing the streets one flake at a time — and somewhere down in that whitening maze, near the water, a tired man with debts was counting the hours until either the police came for him or someone worse did.
