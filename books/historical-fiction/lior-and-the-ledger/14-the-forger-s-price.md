## 14. The Forger's Price

The forger's name was Gustav Lindqvist, and according to Linnea's sweetheart Henrik—who worked the customs house copying desk and had a memory like a well-oiled gear—the man kept a room above the Anchor & Chain tavern on Söder Mälarstrand. He did "documents of convenience," Henrik said: seamen's papers, bills of lading with the dates nudged forward or backward as required by conscience and commerce. Nothing political. Nothing dangerous.

That was before his hand had produced shipping manifests bearing the Nystrom workshop mark.

Oscar stood at the workshop bench that Thursday morning, ostensibly oiling a carriage clock for a widow in Kungsholmen who believed her late husband had wound it every Sunday and expected the tradition honored. In truth he was turning over what Henrik had told Linnea, which Linnea had told him at breakfast in a whisper pitched to carry no further than the butter dish.

"Gustav Lindqvist," she'd said. "Henrik says he drinks at the Anchor & Chain but never past ten. Says he's careful. Says he has a limp from an old fall off a gangway, so you can't miss him."

"Does Henrik know why I'm asking?"

"Henrik knows you're my brother and that I don't ask questions without good reasons. He's decided not to wonder about the rest." She'd smiled then, quick and crooked. "He's a sensible man. That's one of the things I like about him."

Erik had been in the back room, doing something loud with a hammer, which was how Erik listened to conversations he didn't want to admit he could hear.

* * *

The Anchor & Chain sat where the street gave up on respectability and surrendered to the harbor. Gulls argued overhead. The air smelled of tar, fish, and the particular sourness of beer spilled a decade ago and never fully forgiven by the floorboards. Oscar arrived at half past nine in the evening, wearing his second-best coat and the expression of a young man with nowhere better to be, which required no acting at all.

He had learned, in five years of being quiet in rooms, that silence is not the absence of speech. It is an instrument. You tune it. You hold it up to a conversation and read what it reflects back. In the workshop he used it to hear the tick of a movement through the casing. Here, among dockmen and clerks drinking away the cold, he used it to find the shape of a man everyone mentioned but nobody watched.

Lindqvist was easy to spot. The limp was unmistakable—a rolling hitch in his walk, left side dipping—and he sat alone near the stove with a glass of aquavit he treated less as a drink than as a colleague. Fiftyish, gray-stubbled, with ink stains on his right middle finger that no amount of scrubbing ever quite removed. A man whose hands told the truth even when his mouth lied for a living.

Oscar took the table nearest the stove and ordered what the man beside him was having. It proved to be terrible. He drank it anyway, slowly, and listened.

Lindqvist wasn't talking. He was waiting. Oscar recognized the posture because he wore it himself most days: the stillness of a person expecting someone. And beneath it, tonight, something tighter. Lindqvist checked the door four times in twenty minutes. Each time, the dip of his shoulder when he turned made the motion look almost comical, like a puppet on a slightly tangled string—and each time, whatever was behind his eyes got a little grayer.

At a quarter past ten, Lindqvist drained his glass, paid with coins counted out one by one, and hauled himself up.

Oscar gave him thirty seconds and followed.

* * *

The stairs to Lindqvist's room were outside, iron, and groaned a warning to anyone climbing them—which, Oscar reflected, was probably the point. He did not climb them. Instead he crossed the alley and stood in the shadow between a cooperage and a stack of barrels that smelled of herring's distant ancestors, and waited, the way he had once waited in harbor fog behind a stranger with chalk in his pocket.

It was cold enough that his breath betrayed him in small white puffs, and he learned to exhale into his collar.

An hour passed. Then a figure came up the alley—broad, unhurried, moving with the confidence of someone who owns the dark rather than rents it. The figure climbed the iron stairs without a single groan of complaint, which meant either great skill or great familiarity, and knocked twice, paused, knocked once.

The door opened. There was light inside, a lamp turned low. Voices—two voices, maybe three, low and indistinct, the way voices are when they have agreed beforehand to be indistinct. Oscar pressed deeper into the barrel-shadow and did the arithmetic he hated doing.

Then the door opened again, sooner than expected, and the broad figure came down the stairs, and passed within arm's reach of Oscar's hiding place, and stopped.

For three full seconds, the man stood in the alley breathing the same frozen air Oscar was breathing, close enough that Oscar could smell wet wool and pipe smoke. Oscar held absolutely still, the way he had learned to hold still over an escapement, when one tremor ruins everything.

"Boy," the man said quietly, to the darkness in general. "Go home."

Then he walked out of the alley and was gone.

Oscar did not go home. He also did not go up the stairs. He stood in the cold doing what he did best, which was listening—to his own heart, hammering; to the muffled sounds above, a chair scraping, nothing more; and finally, much later, to the long slow creak of springs as someone lay down to sleep, if sleep was what a man in Gustav Lindqvist's position could call it.

He went home at two in the morning. Marta was awake, of course. She didn't ask where he'd been. She set bread and cold ham in front of him and sat across from him with her hands folded, and the two of them shared the family silence, which had lately begun to feel less like armor and more like a wall they were both leaning against, hoping it would hold.

"You're getting thinner," she said at last.

"You're not sleeping," he answered.

"We're a fine pair." She almost smiled. "Eat your ham, Oscar. Whatever war you've enlisted yourself into, they'll feed you worse."

* * *

Friday morning, Oscar went to the observatory to collect tools he'd left from the master clock work—an excuse so thin it barely qualified as cloth, but the observatory had become, strangely, a place where he could think. The dome's silence was different from the workshop's. Up there, time itself seemed to be the thing under repair.

Assistant Halvardsson was at his desk in the calculating room, red-eyed, stamping forms. Halvardsson had been strange all week—not unfriendly, exactly, but watchful, the way a man is watchful when he has something in his drawer he doesn't want found.

"The director wants the clock's final certification by Tuesday," Halvardsson said, not looking up. "Before the delegation arrives. No loose ends."

"No loose ends," Oscar agreed mildly.

"How is the clock?"

"Running true. She'll keep better time than any of us."

Halvardsson's stamp paused, just for a beat, above the paper. "Everything keeps its own time eventually," he said, and stamped, hard.

Oscar carried his toolbox out into the gray morning and thought about that sentence the whole way down the hill. Everything keeps its own time eventually. Either it was the idle philosophy of a tired clerk, or it was a man talking to himself about a deadline. He filed it away in the drawer where he kept things he wasn't ready to look at. The drawer was getting crowded.

* * *

Saturday, Oscar went back to the Anchor & Chain at nine in the evening, intending to do nothing more dramatic than drink one terrible aquavit and confirm that Lindqvist was alive, limping, and as frightened as ever. He intended it the way one intends to only adjust a single gear and somehow ends up with the whole movement disassembled across the bench.

Lindqvist wasn't there.

The barman, wiping glasses with a rag older than the building, shrugged. "Hasn't been in since Thursday. Unusual. Man's regular as tide." A pause, and then, because barkeepers trade in more than drink: "You a friend of his?"

"A colleague," Oscar said. "Of sorts."

"Colleague of sorts." The barman snorted. "Half this city's colleagues of sorts. Well. If you find him, tell him he owes me for the bottle he took upstairs Wednesday."

Wednesday. Before Oscar had watched the broad man climb those stairs. Something cold moved through him that had nothing to do with the weather.

He crossed the alley again and stood in the barrel-shadow, though this time there was nothing to wait for except the confirmation of a feeling he already carried. The window above the tavern glowed dimly. The iron stairs creaked under no one. An hour crawled by, and another, and the glow never changed, and no lamp was lit, and no footsteps crossed the floor above.

A man who owed the barman for a bottle does not leave a bottle unopened for three days.

Oscar climbed the stairs. His conscience offered a brief lecture about breaking and entering; he thanked it and kept climbing. The door was locked. It was also, he discovered when he put his shoulder to it, old, and the frame around it was older, and the lock surrendered with a splintering crack that sounded, in the silent alley, like a cannon salute.

The room smelled of ink, tobacco, and something else underneath—something sweetish and wrong that his body understood before his mind did.

The lamp was there, unlit. The chair was there. And Gustav Lindqvist was there, slumped against the wall beneath the little window, his bad leg stretched out awkwardly, his face gray-blue and calm in the way faces go calm when all their appointments have finally been cancelled.

Oscar knew death. He was a clockmaker's apprentice; he had grown up in a house where grief lived in the walls. But knowing a thing and standing in a small room with it are different trades entirely. He knelt, because some part of him insisted on checking for breath, for warmth, for anything—and found the skin cold as the window glass, and the stillness total, and beyond repair.

On the desk lay a half-finished bill of lading, the Nystrom workshop mark half-drawn in the corner, abandoned mid-stroke. Whoever killed him had let him get that far. Had perhaps wanted him to get that far.

And pinned under the inkwell, deliberate as a signature, was a page torn from a customs ledger. Oscar recognized the column headings—he'd spent enough evenings lately studying Henrik's world through Linnea's reports. The page bore a manifest entry, dated three weeks hence. The observatory delivery schedule. The ceremonial consignment for the state visit.

Whoever wrote it hadn't needed Lindqvist anymore. They'd needed him blamed, and dead men blame beautifully.

Oscar's hands were shaking. He noticed them shaking with a kind of detached professional interest, as though observing a balance wheel run wild, and then he made them stop, because a clockmaker's hands obey.

He searched the room quickly, gently, apologizing to the dead man under his breath for every drawer he opened. In a tin box beneath the floorboard by the bed—under the floorboard, rather; Lindqvist had been a craftsman, after all, and hid his valuables like one—he found a slim packet of practice sheets: the Nystrom mark, drawn again and again, dozens of attempts, improving with each line. And on the last sheet, in a different hand entirely, the original mark itself, pressed clean and confident, the model Lindqvist had been copying.

Not copied from the workshop's actual stamps. Copied from a drawing. From a document someone had brought him and taken away again.

Someone inside. Someone with access to the observatory's paperwork or the customs house files, close enough to carry the mark out and far enough to send a broad-shouldered man up iron stairs instead of coming themselves.

Oscar stood very still in the dead man's room and let the fear arrive fully, let it sit down in his chest like a guest who intends to stay the winter. Because until tonight, the plot had been a matter of names and paper, of debts and dangers at one remove. Now it was a room that smelled wrong and a man with a limp who would never limp again, and the knowledge—arriving with the certainty of a striking clock—that whoever ran this had killed once and would not hesitate at a second time, or a third, or at a nineteen-year-old apprentice who had made the mistake of asking questions in taverns.

His eye fell on the half-finished manifest. Three weeks hence. The observatory consignment.

They weren't finished using the observatory. They were only beginning.

Oscar took the practice sheets and the torn ledger page, wrapped them in his scarf against the damp, and left the room as carefully as he'd entered it—though there was no longer any lock to betray his passing. On the iron stairs, the city spread below him: the harbor lights, the custom house, the observatory hill with its white dome holding the sky's exact time.

Somewhere down there was a man who owned the dark. Somewhere up there was a man who stamped forms with red eyes and talked about loose ends. Between them, stretched thin as wire, was his family.

He descended into the fog and walked home fast, and the fear came along, and he let it, because fear, he was beginning to understand, is only resolve that hasn't found its hands yet.

Marta met him at the door. She looked at his face, at the splintered wood dust still clinging to his sleeve, and she did not ask. She simply stepped aside to let him in, and called softly up the stairs for Erik and Linnea, and put the kettle on—for in the Nystrom household, war, funeral, or miracle, there would first be tea.

"Whatever it is," she said, setting down cups with hands that did not quite tremble, "you'll tell us all of it. Tonight we're done being careful."

Outside, the snow began again, softening the streets, covering footprints, keeping the city's secrets the way it always had.

But not, Oscar thought, sitting down at the kitchen table and laying the dead forger's papers flat in the lamplight, keeping his. Not anymore.
