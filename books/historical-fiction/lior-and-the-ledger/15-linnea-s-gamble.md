## 15. Linnea's Gamble

Linnea Nystrom had learned arithmetic the way other children learned prayers: at her mother's knee, with a ruler across the knuckles for errors. By nineteen she could total a column of shipping figures faster than any man at the Customs House, which was precisely why she'd been given the work and none of the credit. The men got titles. She got the ledger.

She had made peace with this, more or less, in the way one makes peace with winter. But lately the ledgers had begun to frighten her, and no amount of peacemaking helped.

Three nights ago, Oscar had come to her room after the household slept, carrying a candle stub cupped in his hand like a bird he didn't want to startle. He had told her everything—the letter in the clock, the debt to Volkov, the forged manifests bearing their family's mark, the courier hidden in the workshop while Inspector Dahl's questions hung over them all like smoke that wouldn't clear.

He had told her because she had demanded it, in the end. She had simply stood in the doorway of his little room above the workshop and said, "Either you tell me or I'll ask Marta, and you know how she is when she lies—she polishes the teapot." And Oscar, who could keep a secret the way a vault keeps gold, had looked at her for a long moment and then moved aside to let her in.

That was three days ago. Since then Linnea had been doing what she did best. Not clockwork, not courage—arithmetic.

The numbers were wrong. She had known it since autumn, in the small way one knows a sour note in a hymn before one can say why. Entries in the coastal manifest books, shipments logged through the Nystrom workshop that Erik had never received. Crates of "brass stock" arriving from ports her father hadn't visited. Signatures that were almost right—a careful, practiced *almost*—the E in *Erik* curled just slightly too generously, the way a man signs when he has signed someone else's name many times but never watched himself do it.

Her father's mark, on paper her father had never touched. Someone had written her family into a smuggling route the way a forger writes a will—confidently, and for money.

And now there was a warrant being drafted somewhere upstairs in the Customs House. Oscar had heard it from the harbor, from a man named Reval whom Linnea did not yet trust and could not stop thinking about. A warrant needed evidence. Evidence lived in the archives on the third floor of the Customs House, behind a door locked at six o'clock sharp.

Which meant Linnea needed Johan.

* * *

Johan Lindqvist was not a dramatic man. He was a second clerk with ink-stained cuffs, a cowlick that defeated every attempt at order, and a habit of humming when he added—which drove the senior clerks mad and which Linnea found, privately, endearing. He had walked her home from the Anderssons' music evening eleven times without once managing to hold her hand, and she had begun to suspect they would both die of politeness before anything so bold as an embrace occurred between them.

Now she intended to ask him to commit a crime. It seemed only fair to warn him first.

She caught him at lunch in the little café on Regeringsgatan where the coffee was weak and the pastries honest. He was reading a newspaper and brightened when she sat down opposite him, then immediately dimmed, because Linnea knew her own face when she was working up to something. Her mother said she had the look of a cat watching a mousehole.

"You want something," Johan said.

"I always want something. Today I want more of it."

"That's ominous."

"It is." She stirred her coffee though she took no sugar. "Johan. Do you remember the Söderhamn entries? The brass stock?"

His eyes flickered—he was a good clerk; he remembered everything—and something cautious settled over his face. "I remember Senior Clerk Bergström telling me to stop asking about them."

"Did you ask about them?"

"I asked why a workshop the size of Nystrom's would be importing four hundred kilos of brass through Söderhamn twice in one season." He lowered his voice. "Bergström told me some questions are above my pay grade. Which is his way of saying they're exactly the right questions, asked too loudly."

Linnea set down her spoon. "Then listen quietly. Those entries are forged. My father never shipped them. Someone is using our name to move goods—documents, possibly—for people who would see my family hanged rather than explain themselves. There may be a warrant drafted by week's end."

Johan stared at her. The newspaper slid forgotten from his fingers. "A warrant. For your father?"

"For my whole family, if it comes to that." She heard her own voice and was surprised by how level it was. Somewhere inside her, a girl who had spent nineteen years being careful was screaming. Linnea let her scream. Careful had gotten them this far; careful would not get them further. "I need to see the original manifest pages. The archive copies. Before whoever holds the pen upstairs decides the file is complete."

"The archive is locked after six."

"I know."

"And keys are issued to senior staff."

"I know that too." She looked at him steadily. "You have access to the archive during business hours. You have a legitimate reason to pull any volume I ask for—you're re-indexing the coastal series; everyone knows it. All I need is twenty minutes alone with those pages and your coat pocket, and a window when Bergström is at his committee meeting Thursday afternoon."

Johan sat very still. Around them the café clattered on—cups, chatter, a delivery boy whistling—but between the two of them the air had gone taut as wire.

"Linnea," he said finally, "if we're caught, I lose my position. Possibly worse—it's state papers. And you—" He stopped. She watched him do the arithmetic she'd already done, the arithmetic of what happened to a family under investigation, and his ears went red with anger on her behalf, which was somehow the most moving thing anyone had done for her in years.

"Yes," she said. "I know exactly what happens to me. That's why I'm asking you and not the reverse. If it goes badly, you're a foolish clerk misled by a pretty girl. I'm the daughter of smugglers; I was lost either way."

"Don't say that."

"It's true."

"It doesn't have to be." His jaw worked. He picked up the newspaper, put it down again, picked it up. Finally he folded it with tremendous precision, as though the fate of empires depended on the fold, and laid it flat. "Thursday. Bergström sits on the tariffs committee until half past three. I can pull the Söderhamn volumes onto the re-indexing cart at two. There's a records room off the archive—no windows on the door, nobody uses it since the telephone went in downstairs. You can be inside by ten past two if you come through the yard entrance and wear—" he glanced at her hat, a perfectly respectable hat— "something less noticeable than that."

"This hat cost me two months of pin money."

"Then it's a sacrifice we'll both have to bear." But he wasn't smiling, quite. He reached across the table, hesitated, and covered her hand with his—first time in eleven walks home, and it took a felony to manage it. His hand was warm and faintly inky. "Why me?" he asked quietly. "Truly. You must have thought about the risk to me. Why ask at all?"

Because I trust no one else, she might have said. Because you hum when you add and you asked the right question about Söderhamn before anyone told you to. Because when my brother came to my room with his terrible secret, I understood for the first time what our family has paid for its silences, and I am done paying it.

What she said was: "Because you're the only person I know whose signature I've never doubted."

And then, because honesty was clearly contagious and she had opened the bottle herself, she added: "And because I think I love you, and I'd rather find out what you're made of now than after we're married and it's too late to return you."

Johan made a sound like a man who has stepped into cold water. Then he laughed—one startled bark that turned a head two tables away—and squeezed her hand hard.

"Married," he repeated.

"Don't make it strange."

"You proposed conspiracy and marriage in the same breath over café coffee."

"One should bundle one's errands." But her heart was hammering, and he could surely feel her pulse through her wrist, and she found she didn't mind. Nineteen years of careful hands, of the family's old law—*see nothing, say nothing, want nothing openly*—and here she was wanting things openly in broad daylight on Regeringsgatan. It felt less like falling and more like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she carried.

"Thursday," Johan said. "Half past one, the yard gate. Wear the ugly hat."

"It's a *practical* hat."

"Wear it anyway. If they hang us, let them say we died sensibly dressed."

* * *

Thursday came in grey and cold, with a wind off the water that smelled of iron. Linnea wore the practical hat and a plain grey coat borrowed from Marta—who had asked no questions, which frightened Linnea more than any interrogation, because Marta's silence had lately acquired the quality of a held breath. At the door that morning her mother had straightened Linnea's collar with both hands and held on a moment longer than necessary.

"Come home," Marta had said. Just that.

"I always do."

"Not always," Marta said. "Only so far." Then she'd gone back to the bench and her lenses, and Linnea had walked out into the wind with her heart going like a flywheel run loose.

The yard gate was unguarded at half past one; the customs porters took their meal in the shed by the coal store, and Johan stood at the side door in his shirtsleeves despite the cold, looking like a man awaiting execution who has decided to be punctual about it.

"You came," he whispered, absurdly.

"You're holding the door. Where would I go?"

Inside, the Customs House smelled of dust, sealing wax, and decades of tobacco exhaled into officialdom. Johan walked her down a corridor of closed doors, past the great public counter where clerks stamped the day's papers with a rhythm like slow machinery, up one flight of stairs to the archive floor. He talked as they went, loudly enough to be heard through doors, about the re-indexing project and the lamentable condition of the 1911 volumes—performance for any listening ear, delivered with such earnestness that Linnea nearly believed it herself.

The archive itself was long and dim, shelves marching away into shadow under a skylight filmed with city grime. Two junior clerks worked at a far table, heads bent, pens scratching. Johan wheeled the re-indexing cart—six fat volumes of coastal manifests already stacked on it—took a slip of paper from the day-book, and announced to the room in general that these were wanted for collation in the lower records room.

Nobody looked up. Bureaucracy, Linnea reflected, was a machine of marvelous tolerance for anything done with a clipboard and a confident walk.

The lower records room was a cell of a place: one desk, one chair, one lamp with a green shade, shelving along one wall stuffed with obsolete tariff schedules. Johan shut the door, turned the key, and leaned against it, exhaling like a swimmer surfacing.

"Twenty minutes," he said. "Bergström left at half past. After that, God help us, he sometimes comes back early when the committee bores him, which is always."

"Then don't waste them talking." She was already at the cart, pulling the top volume toward her—*Kustmanifest, hösten 1913*, its spine cracked, its pages soft with handling.

Finding the entries was the work of minutes; she had memorized dates and page references from the duplicate books downstairs, where she copied fair versions daily. Here were the originals, in the hand of whichever clerk had first taken them down—and there, appended to each, the receiving signature.

*E. Nystrom.*

She spread three pages side by side under the lamp. And there it was, plain as a scar once you knew to look: the same almost-right handwriting on all three, the same generous curl on the capital E, the same hesitation-repair on the descender of the y where the forger's pen had paused a fraction too long. Three signatures, supposedly written on three different days in three different places, and identical down to the tremor. Her father's actual signature varied like any living man's—she had seen it hundreds of times on workshop papers, quick and slanted, the E clipped short because Erik Nystrom wrote fast and hated waste.

"These aren't even good forgeries," she breathed. "They're *lazy* ones. Whoever did this never met my father. Never watched him sign anything."

"But it's close enough for a file," Johan said quietly, at her shoulder. "Close enough for a warrant."

"Close enough for a court, if it comes to that." She turned the pages, hunting. "But a forgery leaves neighbors. Look—every genuine entry around these has a counter-signature. The examining officer at the dock. Who countersigned these?"

Johan leaned in. His finger stopped above the column, and she felt him go rigid.

"There isn't one," he said. "Every other page in the volume carries a counter-signature. These three are bare." He swallowed. "Linnea, that's not how the process works. No officer countersigned, no stamp, no arrival notation in the day-book margins. These pages weren't examined because they were never real cargo. They were written directly into the book."

"By someone with access to this archive." She looked up at him. "Or someone who borrowed access. Johan—who handled the coastal series this autumn? Before you?"

He was pale now. "Re-indexing was assigned in September. Before that—" He closed his eyes, recalling, a clerk retrieving his own ledger of days. "Senior Clerk Holm had it. He retired in August. And before Holm—" His eyes opened. "Oh. Oh no."

"What?"

"Bergström wants the coastal series personally whenever there's an inquiry. Any inquiry—customs disputes, police requests. He takes volumes to his office 'for reference.' Sometimes for days." Johan's voice had dropped to almost nothing. "In October there was a request from... I shouldn't know this, but the messenger slip crossed my desk—from the police liaison. An informant's query about coastal consignments."

An informant. Oscar's stranger in the fog. Reval.

"So the police asked, and Bergström fetched the volumes, and sometime in that window three false entries grew in the book like mold." Linnea pressed her palms flat against the desk, steadying herself. "We need proof it wasn't my father. This is proof—the missing countersignatures, the identical tremor. But it points upstairs, and upstairs won't thank us for pointing."

"Copy them," Johan said suddenly. "Quickly. Both the forged pages and clean pages for comparison—my father's hand, I mean your father's—do you have specimens?"

"In the workshop. Signed delivery notes going back years."

"Good. Copy these. Every flaw, every pause in the ink. And the volume numbers and folio marks, so the originals can't be quietly replaced without anyone knowing what was there."

So she copied. Her pen flew—she was the fastest copyist in the building and had resented it every working day of her life until this moment, when resentment turned to gratitude. Folio 214, folio 231, folio 247: the generous E, the hesitating y, the naked margin where a countersignature should have stood. Then, for contrast, folio 210 and folio 250, honest pages thick with stamps and signatures, the bureaucratic equivalent of a healthy heartbeat.

Into the lining of her coat, flat against the cardboard, wrapped in oilcloth from home. It made her silhouette lumpy. She decided she looked like a woman with poor posture, which was at least believable.

"Time," Johan murmured. "Fourteen minutes gone. We wheel the cart back, I reshelve, you go out through the yard the way you came."

"Johan." She stopped, hand on the door key. In the green lamplight his cowlick stood up like a flag of surrender, and she loved him so much it was frankly inconvenient. "When this is over—if it ends well—I intend to let you hold my hand the entire way home. Possibly for several streets."

"Several," he agreed gravely. "All of Skeppsbron, if it comes to that."

"It may well come to that."

She turned the key.

* * *

It nearly came apart at the last door. As Johan wheeled the cart back into the archive and Linnea slipped toward the stairs, a voice came down the corridor—Bergström's voice, unmistakable, complaining about committee coffee—and heavy footsteps approaching. Johan, magnificent improviser, raised his voice in mid-complaint about the 1911 volumes' binding, loud enough to fix Bergström's attention on the cart and the dutiful clerk pushing it, and Linnea descended the stairs in the noise of it with her heart slamming and her stolen pages flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

The yard was empty. The wind had risen. She walked—not ran, walked—out the gate and around the corner before she allowed herself to breathe, and even then the breath shook coming out.

By the time she reached Skeppsbron the light was failing and lamps were waking along the quay, gold reflections trembling on black water. She stopped at the rail, ostensibly to watch a steamer warping out, actually to confirm her cargo hadn't shifted. Oilcloth, flat, intact. Three forged pages riding against her ribs like contraband—which was exactly, deliciously, what they were.

She thought of her father, who lied badly because lying was against his nature. Of Marta, who lied beautifully because she'd had practice. Of Oscar, who had spent five years perfecting silence and was now preparing to shatter it publicly, at whatever cost. Of a dead forger by the docks whom no one would ever ask to testify. The family law had been *see nothing, say nothing*. Well. Linnea had seen, and she had said, and she had stolen government property besides, and tomorrow she would lay it all on the workshop bench under the good lamp and watch her parents' faces when they understood that the silence had ended—not broken, ended, retired with honors, replaced by something better.

Something with more people in it.

She was still smiling at the black water when she saw, further down the quay, a figure standing beneath a lamp who had no business standing anywhere: a tired-looking man in a customs clerk's overcoat, hat brim low, watching the same steamer with the fixed attention of a man who isn't watching the steamer at all.

Anton Reval. She knew him by sight from the building—everyone did, vaguely; he was furniture, like the coal stove. But Oscar had described him, and the description fit like a signature.

He had seen her come out of the Customs House at dusk, from the direction of the yard.

Reval turned. Their eyes met across thirty feet of quay, lamp-lit, unmistakable. He didn't call out. He didn't move at all, except to take one step toward her—and then stop, as if some private argument inside him had ended in a draw.

Linnea pulled her collar up, turned, and walked home as fast as dignity allowed, with another man's heartbeat folded against her own, knowing that by tomorrow—perhaps by morning—she would no longer be deciding whether to trust Anton Reval.

He would already have chosen.
