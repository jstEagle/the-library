## 16. Reval's Choice

Anton Reval had been standing in the harbor fog for forty minutes, and in that time he had formed three opinions.

The first was that Stockholm fog in November was not weather but a condition, a damp gray illness that got into the lungs and the ledger books alike. The second was that the warrant folded inside his coat felt heavier than any document he had ever carried, and he had once carried an entire customs seizure report on Russian tobacco that weighed four pounds. The third — and this was the one that troubled him most, because it arrived unbidden and would not leave — was that the boy he was waiting for probably already knew he was there.

Reval had spent eleven years as a customs clerk learning to read men the way other men read manifests. He knew the nervous shufflers from the liars, the drunkards from the desperate, and the desperate from the truly dangerous. And in his one brief encounter with Oscar Nystrom at the workshop door — the interview with the family, the courteous questions and the courteous answers — he had catalogued the apprentice as something rarer than all of those: a listener. A boy who stood very still and let you fill the silence until you told him things you hadn't meant to say.

Reval himself had filled rather a lot of silences in his life. It was how he'd gotten into this whole miserable business. Talk to the right man at the dockside tavern, mention that the customs house was short-staffed and that a man with debts might be flexible, and suddenly you were an informant, with a handler named Berg who smelled of herring and called you "our friend in the office" in a tone that made friend sound like a disease.

And now here was the fruit of all that flexibility: a warrant, drafted but unsigned, bearing the name Nystrom, and a fog thick enough to hide a cathedral, and instructions to find the apprentice and bring him in for further questioning before the delegation's investigators arrived.

"Further questioning," Reval muttered into the fog. As if the first round had been a pleasant conversation over coffee.

* * *

He heard the footsteps before he saw anything, which was the fog's only virtue — it made the whole world audible. Soft steps on wet cobblestones, unhurried, coming from the direction of the fish market. Of course. The boy would have come by the market route, where the early carts left wheel-ruts deep enough to follow by feel if you were the sort of person who noticed such things. Which he was.

"You walk like a clockmaker," Reval said, when the shape resolved into shoulders and then a face. "Quiet, but you can hear the mechanism."

Oscar Nystrom stopped perhaps two meters away. Up close, in the gray light, he looked less like a boy than Reval remembered — more tired around the eyes, more settled in the stance. Someone had taught him to stand like that, or grief had. Reval had seen the same stillness in widowers and in soldiers.

"You sent word through the girl at the bakery," Oscar said. "That I should come alone."

"I did."

"That was either very brave or very foolish of you."

"It can be both," Reval said. "In my experience it usually is." He pulled the warrant from his coat, partly to show it and partly because holding it made him feel like he was doing his job rather than committing one. "You know what this is?"

"A warrant." Oscar didn't look at it. He looked at Reval, which was worse. "Drafted. Unsigned. Inspector Dahl hasn't committed to it yet, which means someone above him is pressing for arrests before the delegation sails home, and Dahl is a careful man who doesn't like being pressed. Am I close?"

Reval stared at him. Somewhere out in the fog a gull complained about the state of the world, and Reval found he sympathized entirely.

"How old are you?" he asked.

"Nineteen."

"Nineteen." Reval laughed, a short unhappy sound. "I have clerks twice your age who can't read a signature the way you just read mine. Where did you learn that?"

"In a house where listening kept people safe." The boy said it simply, without self-pity, and Reval felt the strange sensation of a door opening somewhere inside him that he had nailed shut years ago.

"Then let me tell you what listening has gotten *me*," Reval said, because apparently he had no sense of self-preservation left either. "Eleven years in the customs house. A wife who died owing money to her sister. A son who wants to study engineering and whose school fees I pay by selling scraps of information to a man named Berg, who pays in small bills and large contempt. And now—" he held up the warrant "—a piece of paper with the Nystrom name on it that I am supposed to help turn into an arrest. That is my ledger, young man. Every entry written in my own hand. So before you look at me the way you're looking at me — before you decide I'm the villain of this particular chapter — understand that I know exactly what kind of man stands in fog waiting to trap a nineteen-year-old. I've been avoiding mirrors for months."

"I don't think you're a villain," Oscar said.

"No?"

"I think you're a man with a son." Something flickered across the boy's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one. "My mother says every person is forgivable if you can find the child they're working for."

"Marta Nystrom," Reval said slowly. "The one who lied to me so beautifully she should give lessons to the foreign ministry."

"She lies well," Oscar agreed. "It's how she loves."

* * *

They walked together along the quay, because standing still in that fog had become unbearable, and because — Reval realized with a start — the boy had simply started walking and expected to be followed, and Reval had followed, which told him something alarming about who was conducting this interrogation.

The harbor was a rumor of itself: masts like drowned trees, the shapes of lightermen moving with lanterns, water slapping against pilings somewhere below the edge of sight. Reval talked as they walked. He couldn't seem to stop. It was the effect the boy had on people, he suspected — you poured words into his silence and they came out truer than you intended.

"The forged manifests," Reval said. "Shipping entries with the Nystrom mark — the little anchor-and-wheel stamp your father uses on his delivery receipts. They've been appearing on cargo that never passed through legitimate hands. Documents moving south. I traced six of them. Whoever made them knows the workshop, knows the stamp, knows the routes Erik Nystrom runs to the coastal towns." He paused. "You knew some of this already. I can see it. You've been ahead of me for days."

"Not ahead," Oscar said quietly. "Only underneath. It's where our family does its best work."

"And the letter? The one from the clock?" Reval watched the boy's profile sharpen slightly. "Yes, I know about the letter. Berg knows too. That's the problem. That's why we're standing in a fog instead of my office — because the moment Berg learned a sealed letter had been found in that clock, everything accelerated. He doesn't want truth. Truth takes time and produces paperwork. He wants arrests. Names. A tidy scandal to hand the delegation's security people so everyone looks busy and competent."

"Then he'll love the Nystroms," Oscar said. "We come pre-accused. Adopted son, foreign blood, a father who runs deliveries to coastal towns nobody watches. We're the easiest story in Stockholm."

The flatness in the boy's voice made Reval stop walking. "You speak about yourself like a suspect."

"I've been one since the letter came out of the clock." Oscar turned to face him fully. In the fog his eyes were pale and steady. "So let's not pretend this is an ambush, Herr Reval. You came to catch me. Let me show you what you actually caught."

From inside his coat the boy drew a packet wrapped in oilcloth, and Reval's first instinct — eleven years of instinct — was to reach for it and seize the evidence, do his duty, hand it to Berg and go home to his supper. His hand actually moved. And then it stopped, halfway, because Oscar Nystrom was watching that hand with perfect calm, the way a man watches a dog decide whether to bite, and Reval understood that whatever happened next would depend entirely on what kind of man he chose to be in the next thirty seconds.

His hand came back empty.

"Open it yourself," Reval said hoarsely.

* * *

By the light of a lantern hanging from a bollard — the boy produced matches from somewhere; of course he did, he was the prepared sort — Reval examined the papers while the fog pressed in around them like an audience.

There were copies of the forged manifests. Six of them, each bearing the Nystrom anchor-and-wheel, each with dates and cargo codes in a clerk's hand. Beside them, Oscar had laid genuine delivery receipts borrowed — Reval didn't ask how — from the workshop files, and the differences were plain enough even to eyes as tired as Reval's. The stamp on the forgeries sat a half-degree off its true angle. The ink was too fresh, too uniformly black, lacking the brown fade of the workshop's iron-gall. And the hand that had written the routing numbers was not Erik Nystrom's cramped, patient script but a slanted, confident scrawl that Reval recognized with a cold drop in his stomach.

"I've seen that hand before," he said.

"At the dockside," Oscar said. "The forger. The one you've been buying information from."

"He's dead," Reval said, and then, because saying it aloud in the fog made it real: "Found Tuesday morning. Fell — or was helped — into the channel by the coal yards. I'm the one who reported the body." He looked up. "You know that too, don't you? You've been following my investigation the way I've been following your family."

"Somebody killed him to close the trail," Oscar said. "But trails have two ends, Herr Reval. He copied these documents for someone. Someone gave him the Nystrom stamp to work from, or access to it. Someone with authority to move cargo past customs inspection without question." The boy tapped the forged manifest with one finger. "Look at the inspection column. Cleared — initialled. Every single one. No customs clerk initials his own inspections six times in a month unless he owns the column."

"Or unless someone owns *him*," Reval said slowly. He took out his spectacles, breathed on them, wiped them on his scarf — a ritual that bought him ten seconds to keep his voice level — and bent closer to the initialled column.

The initials were *A.R.*

The fog seemed to contract. The lantern hissed. Somewhere behind them a ship's bell counted a hour Reval did not hear.

"That isn't my hand," he said. His voice sounded very far away. "I have never cleared those shipments. I have never—" He stopped. Because there it was, under the lantern light, undeniable: his own initials, rendered in that same slanted, confident scrawl, sitting in the inspection column of documents that led, entry by entry, toward an arrest warrant with his own informant's reports folded underneath it like a confession.

"They used you," Oscar said. Not cruelly. Almost gently. "The way they used us. Your initials clear the shipments, my family's name carries them, and when it all comes apart, there's a paper trail leading straight to a tired customs clerk with debts who's been selling information to the police. You were never their hunter, Herr Reval. You were the scapegoat they built into the plan from the beginning. The moment Berg presents this evidence, you're not the informant. You're the accomplice."

Reval sat down. There was a crate beside the bollard — fish crates, salt-crusted, smelling of herring — and his legs simply decided the matter for him, and he sat, and put his head briefly in his hands, and laughed, because the alternative was something worse than laughing.

"My son," he said, to the fog, to the lantern, to God if He was taking calls from Stockholm harbor. "Gustav. He wants to build bridges. Bridges! And his father can't even build a life that holds weight." He lowered his hands and found the boy crouched in front of him, watching with that terrible patience. "Do you know what the worst part is? When I saw my initials, my first thought wasn't fear. It was relief. Eleven years of selling scraps to Berg, telling myself I was keeping my head down, providing for my family — and some part of me has been waiting all that time for the bill to arrive. Well." He gestured at the papers. "Here it is. Itemized."

"Then let's contest the bill," Oscar said.

Reval looked at him. "You're mad."

"I'm practical. Think it through. You have access to the full manifest archive. I have the workshop records proving the stamp was copied. Linnea—" he caught himself, and something crossed his face that Reval, even rattled, filed away as *a sister, one who takes risks* "—a colleague of mine has been copying documents from the customs house. Between the three of us, we can trace every forged shipment back to whoever requested them. And the requests had to come from somewhere with authority over both shipping and the observatory, because that clock was sabotaged to bring police to my family's door, and the sabotage required someone with keys."

"The observatory," Reval said. The thought arrived whole, unwelcome, obvious. "Someone inside it. Or attached to it." He thought of the delegation arriving, the naval officers, the ceremonial time-signal that half the city's clocks would obey. He thought of Berg, smelling of herring, saying *give me names, Anton, just names.* "Oh, this goes higher than a dockside forger. This goes into uniforms."

"Which is why it needs a man inside the customs house," Oscar said. "Not a fugitive from it."

"And if I refuse? If I take this warrant to Berg tomorrow and do my duty?"

"Then in three weeks you'll be sharing a cell with my father, and Gustav will visit you through glass, and the men who forged your initials will drink schnapps with the delegation and be gone by spring." Oscar said it without heat, a fact laid on the table like a receipt. "I don't want that. My mother says you're forgivable. I'm inclined to agree. But I won't lie to you about the arithmetic."

For a long moment the only sounds were the water and the distant bell and Reval's own breathing. Then he took the warrant from his coat, unfolded it, and looked at it properly for the first time — really looked, at the typed name *NYSTROM* and the blank line where Dahl's signature would go.

"You know what my wife used to say?" he asked. "She said I was a man who always signed the right thing for the wrong reason and the wrong thing for the right reason. She'd have liked this moment." He struck a match, and held the warrant to it, and watched the corners curl and blacken, and dropped the burning paper onto the wet stones where it flared once and died. "There. That's the last document I'll ever forge. Even if it's only forging my own cowardice."

Oscar extended his hand. Reval shook it. The boy's grip was calloused in the pattern of a craftsman — palm and fingertips, the marks of files and brass — and steady as a pendulum.

"We meet tomorrow," Oscar said. "Not at the workshop — they may be watched. The Vasa Museum steps, eight o'clock, when the tourists haven't arrived and the guards are still drinking coffee. Bring the original manifest register for October. I'll bring everything else."

"And if Berg comes looking for me before then?"

"Tell him you're close to an arrest," Oscar said. "It's even true. Just not the arrest he expects."

Reval stood, joints cracking, and gathered his ruined dignity around him like a thin coat. He was three steps into the fog when the boy's voice caught him.

"Herr Reval. One more thing."

He turned. Oscar Nystrom stood by the dying lantern, half-dissolved in gray, and for a moment he looked very young indeed.

"When this is finished — whatever happens to my family — could you tell Gustav that his father did the right thing? He shouldn't have to wait for a stranger like me to learn it."

Reval found, to his considerable embarrassment, that he could not immediately speak. He nodded instead, sharply, the way men do when feeling arrives at an inconvenient hour, and walked into the fog.

Behind him, muffled by the gray, he heard the boy's footsteps recede along the quay — quiet, unhurried, precise — and then stop. And then, faintly, the sound of Oscar Nystrom speaking to someone else in the dark, someone who had been waiting out of earshot the entire time: *"Linnea. It worked. But we have a problem — he'll want proof from inside the observatory, and there's only one person alive who can get it."*

Reval stood very still in the fog, a conspirator now in earnest, and wondered what in God's name he had agreed to — and, more troublingly, why his step felt lighter than it had in eleven years.
