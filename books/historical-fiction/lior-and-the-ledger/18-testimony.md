## 18. Testimony

I had rehearsed the speech eleven times.

Not on paper—paper could be seized, and after everything I had learned about paper in the past month, I trusted it the way one trusts a dog that has bitten you twice. I rehearsed it in my head, walking from Södermalm to Kungsholmen through a morning so cold the harbor smoke hung in the air like something carved. I rehearsed it while crossing Västerbron, counting the words to the rhythm of my boots: *My name is Oscar Nystrom. I am an apprentice clockmaker. I am here of my own free will, and I intend to leave of it too.*

That last part was a joke I told only myself, because if I did not joke, my hands would shake, and Inspector Dahl would see it, and a man like Dahl noticed everything the way I noticed escapements—not because he wanted to, but because his mind had been built that way and there was no help for it.

The evidence was wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, under my coat, where a man might carry a loaf or an infant or, in my case, three forged shipping manifests, a photograph of a dead forger's ledger, Linnea's copied pages, and Reval's signed confession of his own signature's misuse. It weighed perhaps four hundred grams. It weighed as much as a church bell.

* * *

Dahl received me in a room at the police headquarters that had clearly never hosted anything more dramatic than a dispute between two fishmongers. There was a desk, two chairs, a stove that ticked as it warmed—a stove with better timing than half the clocks in this city, I noted sourly—and a window overlooking a wall, which struck me as the architectural equivalent of Dahl himself: nothing wasted on ornament, everything designed to make a man feel observed without being able to observe back.

"Master Nystrom." He did not rise. He gestured at the chair opposite him with the courtesy of a man offering a seat in a dentist's parlor. "You requested this meeting. That is unusual. Most people request meetings with me only after I have requested them first."

"I'm told I'm unusual," I said, and sat, and unwrapped the oilcloth, and laid the documents on his desk in the order I had spent eleven rehearsals perfecting.

He looked at them for a long time. Long enough that the stove ticked forty-one times—I counted; counting is what I do when the alternative is screaming—and then he picked up the topmost manifest, held it to the window light, and said:

"These are good forgeries."

"They're bad ones," I said. "The ink is wrong. The Nystrom workshop mark has a serif on the N that my father cuts by hand, and whoever made these used a stamp. My father has never owned a stamp in his life. He thinks stamps are a form of vanity."

Something moved at the corner of Dahl's mouth. Not a smile. The memory of one, filed away for later. "And you can prove the difference?"

"I can prove it the way you'd want it proven." I set down my father's actual cutting knife—the one he used for the mark, borrowed from the bench without his knowledge, which meant I would be confessing to that theft as well, and I had budgeted for it in rehearsal seven. "Cut the letterforms side by side. Any engraver in the city will tell you in ten seconds. But that isn't why I'm here."

"No?"

"No." And here was the moment I had rehearsed hardest, the moment where the speech left the page and became simply mine. "Because the forgery is the smallest crime in this room. Someone inside the observatory—or the customs house, or both—is moving naval intelligence out of Stockholm under the cover of legitimate manifests, using my family's name as the drop point. They killed the forger who made those documents. They sabotaged the master clock to bring your investigators to our door. And they are going to do all of it again during the state visit, except next time they'll do it with a warrant bearing my father's name instead of a forged manifest."

I took a breath. In rehearsal, this was where my voice always wavered.

It didn't waver now. I have thought since about why, and I believe it is because for the first time in five years, every word I was saying was true, and spoken on purpose, and spoken aloud. Silence had been my native language since Riga. I was fluent in it. But a language is only a tool, and I had finally found something worth saying.

* * *

I talked for two hours.

I told him about the observatory clock and the letter wedged into its escapement—deliberately jammed, timed to fail, a message hidden in a mechanism that someone knew I alone would open. I told him about the wax seal, and Marta burning her hand rather than let me touch it, and I watched Dahl write *the mother* in his notebook and hated myself for it and kept talking anyway.

I told him about Volkov. All of it. Riga, 1905, the night my father Erik had been carried bleeding out of a printer's cellar by a Russian exile who owed him nothing, and the debt Erik had carried across the Baltic like a stone in his boot ever since. I told him the arrangement had died with peacetime—or Erik believed it had—and how someone had resurrected the old signals, the chalk marks, the drop points, forging my family into a network that no longer existed except as a name on paper.

"The debt is real," I said. "The crime is not ours. Those are two different things, Inspector, and I need you to hold them apart, because everyone else in this city will fold them together the moment it's convenient."

Dahl wrote that down too. Or something near it.

Then came the hard part. I had known it would come, and I had decided, somewhere around rehearsal nine, not to flinch from it.

"Aino Karjalainen stayed one night in our workshop during the snowstorm. She is a courier for Volkov. She is twenty years old, she was carrying documents she may not fully understand, and my mother sheltered her knowing it could hang us all." I laid Linnea's copied pages beside the manifests. "My sister obtained these from the customs house. I won't tell you how. Punish me instead—she did it for me, and I asked her."

"You're asking me to arrest you in her place."

"I'm asking you to weigh things correctly. It's what I do for a living. Weighing."

He set down his pen. For a moment he looked less like an inspector and more like a very tired man in a warm room, which I suspect is what he actually was underneath the coat of official varnish.

"You understand what you've done today," he said. "You've named yourself, your family, and a foreign agent in front of a police inspector four days before an imperial delegation arrives. Do you know what happens to apprentices who testify? The observatory will not keep you. The guild will hear of it. Half of Stockholm will know your face by Friday."

"Yes," I said.

"And you came anyway."

"The alternative was watching my family take the fall for a crime committed by someone who signs his work with our name. Inspector, I spent five years learning to be silent. I'm quite good at it. It nearly destroyed us anyway. So I've concluded silence is overrated as a survival strategy. It's excellent as a hiding place and useless as a home."

The stove ticked. Dahl looked at me for a long moment, and then he did something I had not seen him do: he closed his notebook.

"Nystrom," he said. "Do you know why I became a policeman? Because when I was your age, a man I trusted taught me that the world runs on secrets kept well. He was wrong. It runs on secrets told *to the right person*. It took me thirty years to learn the difference." He gathered the documents into a neat stack, squared their edges—there was a clockmaker's soul in that gesture, and I chose to take it as an omen. "Your testimony will be recorded formally. Under oath. You'll repeat everything you've said today, and more, and it will go into a file that may be read by men whose names you'll never know. Some of them may be the very men you're accusing."

"I know."

"And you still—"

"I counted the cost on the bridge this morning," I said. "All of it. Then I kept walking."

* * *

They took my formal statement that afternoon in a room with a stenographer whose fingers moved like a well-oiled train. I gave my name in full—Oscar Nystrom, apprentice of Hedlund & Sons, ward of Erik and Marta Nystrom, born in Riga—and I heard it spoken aloud in an official room for the first time, and it sounded strange and new, like a bell rung for the first time after casting.

I named the debt to Volkov. I named Aino. I named the chalk marks and the second letter and the night of shelter. When the stenographer read it back to me, hearing my own history in flat transcript, I found I felt lighter, not heavier. Confession, it turns out, is just testimony you give yourself first.

By evening, word had traveled—as Dahl promised it would, with the grim satisfaction of a man who enjoys being right about terrible things.

Hedlund & Sons sent a boy with a note. I stood on the doorstep and read it under the gas lamp while the boy shifted from foot to foot, embarrassed to be the messenger of it. Old Hedlund's handwriting, careful and cold:

*In light of circumstances, the firm must consider your indenture suspended pending inquiry. Your tools remain at the bench. Do not come for them until summoned.*

Suspended pending inquiry. Six words that unmade five years of patience. My journeyman examination was in spring. My whole future had been a row of gears, each one turning the next, and some clerk with a pen had put his thumb in the works.

Linnea read it over my shoulder. She was quiet a moment, which for Linnea is roughly the duration of a heartbeat.

"Well," she said. "Now you'll have time to finish your own clock. The one for the examination. Honestly, Oscar, the bench at Hedlund's was slowing you down. Old Hedlund measures time in decades. You measure it in seconds. It was never a good fit."

"That's the most generous interpretation of unemployment I've ever heard."

"I'm a generous person. Ask anyone. Don't ask Papa." She squeezed my arm, once, hard, the way she did instead of saying things. "Mama's making coffee. Come inside before you freeze. Heroes catch colds like everyone else."

Inside, the workshop smelled of brass and pine shavings and Marta's coffee, and Erik stood by the bench where my tools still lay, though I had been forbidden to fetch them—he had gone for them himself, I realized, walked into Hedlund's and collected them like a man collecting his son. He didn't say anything. He just set my loupe on the bench, precisely centered, and went back to his work, and the silence between us was the good kind, the kind we were building new.

* * *

I woke before dawn two days later to knocking—not the sharp knock of police, which I now knew by heart, but the soft, urgent knock of someone who does not wish to be heard.

Reval stood on the step, hatless in the falling snow, gray-faced, holding a leather folder against his chest the way I had held mine against mine outside Dahl's office. Behind him the harbor fog was rolling up the street, white and thick, erasing the world one house at a time.

"There's a ship," he said, without preamble, breath smoking. "Leaving tomorrow night with the delegation. And there's a man on the delegation's staff—a naval attaché—who has just learned that a customs informant gave testimony naming him." His voice cracked, steadied. "Nystrom, the file you gave Dahl. The one with your testimony. It was read by the wrong eyes before Dahl could seal it. Your words are already in the harbor, and the tide is going out."

Behind me, upstairs, I heard the floorboards shift—my father rising early, as he always did, to light the stove.

"He'll run," I said.

"He'll try. And when he fails—" Reval pulled his hat brim down against the snow, and in the lamplight I saw that the tired man's eyes had gone hard and bright. "When he fails, he'll burn everything behind him. Starting with the names in that file. Starting, I think, with yours."
