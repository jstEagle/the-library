## 20. A Debt Repaid

Erik Nystrom had not slept well in thirty years, and he had stopped apologizing for it sometime around his fortieth birthday. A man who had carried what he carried learned to make friends with the small hours. He rose before the fire remembered it was supposed to be warm, lit the stove in the workshop, and set the kettle on with the practiced economy of a person who had done the same thing eleven thousand times and intended to do it eleven thousand more.

But this morning, standing at the workbench with his hands wrapped around a cup that was mostly heat and habit, Erik found himself listening to the silence of the house the way one listens to a clock that has begun to run slow — not broken, exactly, but no longer to be trusted.

The scandal had broken ten days ago. The attaché had been escorted out of Stockholm with all the ceremony of a man being shown a door he'd already been walking toward. Inspector Dahl had come to the workshop once more, shaken everyone's hand as though concluding a business arrangement rather than an investigation, and left a copy of the official exoneration on the bench between the pinion gauges and a jar of Marta's lingonberry preserves. Linnea had framed it. Marta had threatened to frame the preserves jar too, out of solidarity.

And Oscar — Erik turned the thought over carefully, the way he turned a cracked mainspring, testing where it might still hold and where it would give — Oscar had spoken. In a courtroom, in front of inspectors and clerks and strangers, the boy who had arrived mute as a winter window had stood up and told the whole of it: the letter, the debt, Riga, everything he knew and half of what he guessed.

Everything except the part only Erik knew.

That was the debt Erik had been avoiding since the trial ended, and he had run out of clever places to hide from it. There were, he reflected, only so many hours in a workshop, and a man could only resharpen the same chisel so many times before even the chisel began giving him looks.

He heard the door latch, soft as snowfall.

Erik did not turn around at first. Some instincts you trust without examining them, the way you trust a balance wheel. He had known, in some chamber of himself that never fully slept, that this morning would come. He set down his cup.

"Volkov," he said.

* * *

Dmitri Volkov stood in the doorway of the workshop looking, Erik thought, absurdly like a man arriving at a church social. He wore a good coat brushed free of snow, and he carried under one arm a flat parcel wrapped in oilcloth, and he had the expression of someone who had rehearsed a speech on the walk over and was now fairly sure he would forget all of it.

"You look old, Nystrom," Volkov said, in Swedish that had kept its Russian vowels like a scar keeps its shape.

"You look alive," Erik said. "I'd call us even."

For a moment neither of them moved, and then they did the thing that men of their generation and their particular damage did instead of embracing: they shook hands, firmly, both gripping a beat too long, both pretending the extra beat was nothing at all.

"I came at dawn," Volkov said, glancing at the windows, where the sky was doing its January impression of a pewter teapot, "because I am a coward, and I hoped the household would still be asleep."

"My wife has been awake since five," Erik said. "She is always awake since five. She says it is when the house tells her its plans for the day."

"And what does it tell her?"

"That we're about to have guests whether we like it or not."

Volkov smiled — a real one, crooked and tired — and then his eyes went past Erik's shoulder, up the stairs, and Erik understood that the boy was already there. Of course he was. Oscar had inherited the household instinct for dawn; he'd probably been at the top of the stairs for ten minutes, listening, deciding whether the world was safe enough to descend into.

"Oscar," Volkov said quietly.

Erik stepped aside.

The boy came down slowly, in his shirtsleeves, hair uncombed in a way that would have earned him Marta's commentary within the hour. He stopped at the bottom step and looked at Dmitri Volkov the way a man looks at a photograph he has studied all his life and finally meets in the flesh — checking the likeness, finding it true, finding it insufficient anyway.

"You saved my father's life," Oscar said. It was not quite a question and not quite thanks. It was a boy setting a piece into a movement and waiting to hear if it ticked.

"In Riga," Volkov said. "Nineteen hundred and three." He looked at Erik. "Though I suspect your father has told you the version he tells himself, which is shorter and kinder than mine."

"The version I tell myself," Erik said, "is that we are standing in my workshop at six in the morning in December, and the stove is lit, and whatever story gets told next gets told sitting down with coffee. That is the only version I'm qualified to tell anymore."

Marta came down the stairs with Linnea behind her, both dressed, both composed, both radiating the particular alertness of women who had already counted the exits. Marta took one look at Volkov, said "You'll want breakfast," in the voice of a woman stating a law of physics, and disappeared into the kitchen. Linnea stayed on the stairs, arms folded, watching the Russian with open appraisal — the same look she gave suspicious shipping manifests.

They sat. The kettle found its voice. And Erik, holding his coffee he did not drink, finally paid the oldest bill in his ledger.

* * *

"Riga was a city of two clocks," Erik said. "The German one on the guildhall, and the Russian one underneath it, and they disagreed by eleven minutes, and everyone chose which time they lived by. I chose the German one because my master did. Your father—" he nodded at Oscar— "chose whichever clock got him home to your mother fastest."

Oscar sat very straight. Linnea had abandoned the pretense of the stairs and taken the chair beside him, and when his hand found the edge of the table, hers found the edge of his sleeve.

"I was twenty-six. An idiot with a file and opinions. I had gotten work repairing chronometers for ships out of the harbor, and one of the ships belonged to people who used it for things other than herring. I didn't ask questions — that was the trade's whole curriculum, don't ask — but I noticed. You notice things when you work with your hands. You notice that a crate built for machine parts shouldn't weigh what this one weighs, and that the manifest is written in two different inks, and that the captain prays in three languages depending on who's aboard."

"You hid papers in a chronometer case," Volkov said. It wasn't an accusation. It sounded almost fond.

"I repaired a chronometer case that happened to be hollow," Erik said. "What it contained afterward was between the case and God. Until the night it wasn't. Soldiers came to the yard — not soldiers, men dressed as soldiers, which in Riga was a distinction with paperwork but no practical difference — and they wanted the crate I'd sealed that afternoon. I told them I was a clockmaker, not a customs officer. They explained, with a truncheon, that those were the same profession that evening." His hand went, without his permission, to his ribs, where a scar lived that Marta had kissed exactly twice and asked about exactly zero times. "They walked me toward the harbor. There was ice on the water that year. Real ice, the kind that takes a body and files the report later."

He paused. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled, the house clearing its throat.

"And then," Erik said, "a man fell out of a doorway."

"A doorway?" Volkov raised an eyebrow. "I have spent twenty years telling people I leapt from a wall like a panther."

"You came out of a fishmonger's awning," Erik said, "covered in herring scales, hit the first man with a boat hook, put the second man's face into a bollard, and told me — in Swedish so bad it was nearly poetry — to run. So I ran. I got to the workshop, I got my coat, I got out of Riga on a boat whose captain owed me for a regulator, and I never learned your name until four years later, when a letter reached me through three hands asking if the man whose life I owed was the same Erik Nystrom now keeping a shop in Stockholm."

"He never asked how I found him," Volkov said to Oscar. "Your father has a talent for not asking things. It is why we survived each other."

"How *did* you find him?" Linnea asked. She had the family gift for the question that everyone else was politely swallowing.

"Because I was not a fishmonger," Volkov said. "I was what the fishmonger's awning was concealing, which is to say I worked for men who moved documents the way Erik moved clockwork — quietly, and with clean hands. The crate he sealed that afternoon held our papers. The men dressed as soldiers worked for other men who wanted them burned. When I pulled him off the ice, I did not know yet that he was one of ours. I learned it after, when I opened the crate he'd saved and found our network's whole correspondence packed in sawdust between a barometer and a music box." He spread his hands. "A clockmaker hides things better than most professionals. Things made to keep secrets — cases, casements, hollow pendulums — it is a whole philosophy of construction. I have thought about this for years. I believe your father cannot build a box that does not have somewhere inside it to hide something else."

"It's called a drawer," Erik said. "It's very common."

Everyone laughed, and the laugh broke something loose in the room, some pressure that had been sealed in since 1908, and Erik felt it go out of his shoulders like air out of a valve.

"But here is the part I owe you, Oscar," he said, and his voice changed, and the room changed with it. "Here is the part I buried, because burying it seemed safer, and I was wrong, and burying it nearly cost us everything this month. The debt to this man did not end in Riga. In nineteen-oh-eight, when your letter came — the letter about a boy named Nystrom, orphaned, alone — Volkov wrote to me separately. Not to ask me to take you in. To remind me that he had a right to ask me for anything, and that someday he might. And I said yes. I wrote back and said yes, whatever it is, yes — because a life is a life, and yours was worth any price I could imagine paying."

Oscar's face did something complicated. "You took me in because of a debt?"

"I took you in because Marta read your letter aloud at the kitchen table and cried, and Linnea was four years old and asked if you'd have your own bed, and because any fool could see what you were," Erik said. "But I will not pretend the debt wasn't in the room. It was in the room every day. It was why I taught you to listen more than speak. Not because I didn't want your voice — God knows I waited five years for it like a man waits for spring — but because I was afraid that someday the debt would come due, and I wanted you armed. Silence is armor, boy. I just forgot to mention that armor gets heavy, and that a man can suffocate inside it as easily as live behind it."

The room was very quiet. Oscar was looking at his hands, turning them over, and Erik recognized the gesture — the boy examining his own instruments, checking for cracks.

"The letter in the observatory clock," Oscar said finally. "The first one. It asked you to shelter a courier. One night."

"It did," Volkov said. "And I want it understood, by everyone at this table, that the request was genuine and the network that abused it was not mine. Someone forged my seal onto a scheme I had retired from. I came to Stockholm in October intending to find them before they found you. I was slower than your son was." He inclined his head toward Oscar with a formality that was almost courtly, and Linnea made a small sound of approval she tried to disguise as a cough.

"So the debt," Erik said, "which I have carried for fifteen years like a stone in a boot—"

"Is hereby discharged," Volkov said. "Formally, finally, and with interest paid in full — though I confess the interest ran the wrong direction. You sheltered my courier. Your daughter risked her future copying manifests. Your son testified in a courtroom against an imperial attaché, which is a sentence I have said aloud several times now and still do not entirely believe." He drew the oilcloth parcel across the table and unwrapped it.

Letters. A thick bundle of them, tied with string gone soft with age, edges foxed brown. Erik knew them on sight — his own handwriting, younger and angrier, and Volkov's cramped Cyrillic-mixed script, and the wax seals he'd last seen pressed shut in a Riga winter.

"Burning them seems dramatic," Volkov said. "I considered framing one, as your daughter framed the exoneration, but I am told the stove works perfectly and sentiment should be put to practical use."

Marta appeared in the doorway with a tray of bread and cheese and eggs, took in the bundle, and set the tray down with the calm of a woman who had burned one of these letters herself five years ago and singed her fingers on principle. She handed Volkov the tongs.

* * *

They fed the letters to the stove a few at a time, because Volkov insisted on reading a line or two of each first — "for inventory purposes" — and the readings turned into a kind of wake, or perhaps a wedding, Erik couldn't decide which. There was the letter from 1905 in which Erik had described, at length and with profanity, the pricing practices of Stockholm hardware merchants, and Linnea laughed so hard she had to leave the room. There was one from 1909 in which Volkov reported that the boy was settling in — secondhand news, passed along a chain of couriers, the whole underground network briefly employed as a family's anxious relatives — and Oscar went still, and Marta put her hand on the back of his neck, and no one said anything for a moment, and nothing needed saying.

There was the last one. Dated 1913, unsent — the twin of the letter Oscar had found in the clock. Volkov held it a long moment before feeding it to the flames.

"For the record," he said, "the true text asked for one night's shelter for a frightened girl, and promised that afterward I would release you from everything, and I meant it, and here we are. History will record that the forged version caused considerably more trouble."

"History," Marta said, "can record whatever it likes as long as it does it after breakfast."

When the last letter caught, Erik watched his own young handwriting curl and blacken and let go of itself, and felt the strange lightness of a man setting down luggage he had forgotten he was carrying. Fifteen years of weight, and it burned in under a minute. That was the trick of secrets, he thought. Enormous to carry; quick to destroy. Like ice on a harbor. Like everything, really.

Volkov stood, buttoned his good coat, and shook Erik's hand again — the long grip, the honest one.

"Your son has a name now," Volkov said. "His own. Use it loudly. Names spoken quietly get stolen."

Then he was gone into the blue morning, and the door latched behind him, and the workshop smelled of woodsmoke and old paper ash and, faintly, of Marta's eggs, which meant the world had decided to continue.

* * *

Erik found Oscar at the bench afterward, because of course he did; the boy went to the bench the way other people went to church. He was fitting a tiny brass wheel into a movement — the journeyman clock, nearly done — with the total absorption Erik recognized as his own native language.

"Oscar."

The boy looked up.

"There's one more thing," Erik said. "Not Riga. Something smaller, and harder, and I've owed it to you since 1908." He pulled the stool around and sat facing his son, knees nearly touching, the way you sit when you're about to say something you've only rehearsed while sanding wood. "The day you arrived, you stood in that doorway — that exact one — and you didn't speak, and Marta fed you, and I watched you watch us, cataloguing every exit. And I thought: this boy will either become a son or a lodger, and it depends entirely on whether anyone ever asks him what happened to him." He swallowed. "Nobody ever asked. We gave you rules instead of questions, and we called it kindness, and some days it was. But a family isn't a safe-deposit box, boy. It's supposed to rattle. You're supposed to hear it ticking in the night and know it's alive."

Oscar's throat moved. "You're talking now," he managed.

"I'm talking now," Erik agreed. "Late. Clockmakers are never early with the important things — ask any husband in Sweden." He put his hand on the bench, palm up, an offer rather than a demand. "So. Ask me. Anything. About Riga, about the debt, about why I flinched every time you hummed that tune your mother used to hum — yes, I noticed — about any of it. The shop is closed today. The stove is hot. I have nowhere in the world to be except here, answering."

For a long moment Oscar didn't move. Then he set down his tweezers with great care, the way he did when a piece was seated perfectly, and put his hand in his father's, and asked his first question — and it was not about Riga at all. It was about the tune. And Erik, laughing and crying in roughly equal measure and lying to absolutely no one about it, began to answer.

Outside, the snow started again, soft and unhurried, falling on a city that had eleven months earlier been ready to break the Nystroms and had, instead, been rearranged by them. Upstairs, Linnea was writing a letter. On the bench, the journeyman clock waited for its final regulation.

And in the east, over the rooftops, the sky was doing something pale and gold that Erik hadn't bothered to notice in years — dawn, arriving on schedule, keeping better time than any clock he'd ever built.
