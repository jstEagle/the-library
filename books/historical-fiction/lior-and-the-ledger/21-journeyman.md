## 21. Journeyman

The workshop had lost its shutters.

That was how you knew, walking down Katarina Street in the gray light of an October morning, that something had happened here. Every other shopfront on the lane wore its winter boards neat and painted, but the Nystrom workshop's shutters were gone—taken by the police in November, when Inspector Dahl's men catalogued everything from the lathes to the jar of Marta's cardamom buns—and never returned. Erik had written three letters asking for them. He'd received two replies and a form. So the workshop stood with its windows bare to the street, and anyone passing could look straight in at the benches, the lamps, the great wall of drawers with their brass labels worn to ghosts of letters.

"Good for business," Linnea said, when Mother fretted about it. "Half the city wants to see where the smugglers kept their tea."

Nobody laughed, exactly. But nobody frowned either, which was its own kind of weather in our house now.

The scandal had not ruined us. It had done something worse and better: it had made us known. Before, we were the Nystroms, clockmakers, competent and unremarkable, the kind of family a neighbor could live beside for twenty years without learning your mother's first name. Now we were *those* Nystroms—the ones in the newspapers, briefly, before the attaché's fall swallowed us whole and spat out something larger. Customers came anyway. Some came out of curiosity, craning at the bare windows like visitors to an aquarium. Some came because a cleared name was still a name, and a name that had survived an investigation seemed trustworthy in ways that polish never was. And some, I think, came quietly, without saying why, and left their watches to be cleaned as if leaving an offering.

We survived diminished. Father said it plainly one night over supper, in the voice he used for accounts: fewer orders, slower payment, the naval contracts gone entirely—they'd found another supplier within the week, a firm in Gothenburg with no history and no questions. But the rent was paid. The lamps were lit. Marta baked as though defiance were a recipe.

And in all of it, through all of it, I had a date circled on my calendar in pencil, then ink, then carved so deep into the wood of my bench I could feel it under my thumb: **the twelfth of December. Journeyman examination.**

* * *

You have to understand what the examination meant to someone like me.

An apprentice becomes a journeyman by making a piece—unaided, unsupervised, in a set number of hours—and submitting it to the guild examiners along with his papers. Most boys make what their masters make. The sons of cabinetmakers build small cabinets; the sons of watchmakers build watches. The piece is a demonstration that you can repeat your teacher, nothing more. It is a door that opens onto more of the same room.

I had no trade father to repeat. Erik Nystrom had taken me in when I was fourteen and mute with grief, and taught me everything he knew, but he always said—"always," meaning twice, which for Erik was a torrent—that I would have to make something that was mine. Not his. Not Hedlund's, whose master clock I had repaired and whose goodwill I had spent entirely on testimony. Mine.

For eleven months I had been building it in the evenings, after the shop work was done, at the bench by the bare window where the last of the daylight fell. A regulator clock. Not a showpiece—a working instrument, meant to keep time better than anything else in the room, honest about every gear. I had made the escapement myself, a Graham deadbeat, filing the pallets until my fingerprints nearly vanished into the steel. I had cut the wheels, thirty teeth each, and blued the hands over the flame the way Marta blued her sewing needles, watching the metal turn straw-yellow, peacock, cornflower, and snatching it back at the exact blue of a winter dusk.

The case I built of birch, because birch was cheap and because I liked that it looked like nothing special until you got close.

Linnea caught me sanding it one Sunday and stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, doing her arithmetic face.

"It's plain," she said.

"It's honest."

"It's plain," she repeated, "and it's going to pass, because examiners are men who distrust beauty the way priests distrust dancing." She came in and ran her finger along the hood. "But you'll put your name inside the case, won't you? Where only the repairman will ever find it?"

I hadn't thought of that. That is a lie; I had thought of it for months and told no one, because telling felt like spending something I couldn't replace. "O. Nystrom," I said. "Cut in the backboard."

She smiled—not her sharp smile, the other one, the rare one—and said, "Father will pretend he hasn't noticed for a year and then mention it to every customer he has."

* * *

The examination was held in the guild hall off Hötorget, a room that smelled of cold coffee and lamp oil, with a long table where three examiners sat behind their spectacles like crows on a fence. Twelve candidates. Eleven of them fidgeted. I set my clock on the table, opened the case door so the pendulum swung visible, and stepped back.

The senior examiner, a heavy man named Forsell with ink stains up both wrists, looked at the movement for a long time. Then he did the thing that examiners do: he reached in and stopped the pendulum with one finger.

My heart did not stop with it. I had practiced for this too. "It will restart itself," I said. "Weight on the crutch is compensated. Give it a push past center or wait—it recovers from a five-degree disturbance on the fourth swing."

He raised an eyebrow, released the pendulum, and we both watched. It staggered, gathered, steadied. On the fourth swing it might have been running undisturbed all evening.

"And the rate?" Forsell asked.

"Two seconds slow per day, drifting toward zero as the oil beds in. It was six when I finished the escapement in March." I had brought the rate book—eleven months of entries, temperature and time, in my smallest handwriting. He read it longer than he'd looked at the clock. Then he closed it and said, in the flat tone of a man refusing to be impressed in front of colleagues:

"The pallets are filed by hand. Whose files?"

"Mine, sir. Sharpened myself."

"Hm." He wrote something. Crows write things; you are never meant to know what. "Report to the hall on Friday."

Friday, they pinned a paper to the board with twelve names on it and mine third from the top, and below my name, for the first time anywhere in any ledger of the guild, the word **journeyman**. Erik shook my hand in front of everyone, which he had never once done, and his grip lasted half a second longer than a handshake and said everything his voice wouldn't.

Marta cried, of course, and fed me as though grief and joy required identical treatment: buns, herring, more buns. And Linnea—Linnea bought a bottle of punsch with her own wages and made a toast at the kitchen table so perfectly balanced between mockery and love that I can't reproduce it even now. Something about how the boy who couldn't speak had become the man who wouldn't stop, and how the guild had no idea what it had certified.

I slept that night with the certificate on the chair beside my bed, because I didn't trust the world yet to leave it where I couldn't see it.

* * *

Aino came back to us in the second week of December.

She arrived without warning, the way she'd arrived the first time—in snow, at the wrong hour, standing in the doorway with frost melting in her dark hair—but this time there was nothing hunted in her posture. This time she knocked. This time Marta opened the door and the two women looked at each other across the threshold, and whatever passed between them was older and quieter than words, and then Marta pulled her inside and the house folded around her like it had never learned any other shape.

She stayed four days. She helped Marta in the kitchen, sat with Father over the account books and found two errors in the coastal freight entries that made him grunt in a way I recognized, by then, as delight. She spoke Swedish with a Finnish accent and Russian when Volkov's name came up, and she laughed easily now, and ate heartily, and slept—I noticed this, I noticed everything about her, I had made a private science of it—without waking at small sounds.

On the fourth evening she found me at the bench, finishing the last adjustments on a customer's carriage clock. The workshop was warm; the bare windows were black; the lamps made little gold rooms in the dark.

"They leave tomorrow," she said. "Dmitri Andreyevich and I. There is a ship, and then a train, and then a place I am not going to tell you, because you are terrible at being lied to and I refuse to be responsible for your face."

"My face is fine."

"Your face," Aino said, "is a newspaper. Your whole family's faces are newspapers. It is why I am alive." She said it lightly, but underneath it was the truth: the household had broken its rule for her, one long snowbound night, and she had walked out of Stockholm because of it while others walked out in handcuffs or coffins. She didn't sentimentalize it. Neither did we. That was our shared language.

"What will you do?" I asked. "Out there."

"Work. Carry things. Read clocks." She glanced at the regulator on the shelf—my examination piece, home again, ticking its patient two-seconds-slow-and-mending tick. "Dmitri says a network is only as good as its timekeeping. Someone must keep the schedules true." A shrug. "I am good at being trusted."

"You're good at surviving."

"Those are the same skill wearing different coats." She was quiet a moment, watching the pendulum. Then: "Oscar. You know what he would give you, if you asked. A place, papers, a new name in a city where no one has heard of Nystrom. After what your family paid. He would consider it a debt, and he hates debts worse than he hates the Tsar."

I looked at my hands. They had changed in two years—broader, scarred at the knuckles, steady. Hands that could file a pallet true and hold a lie and hold a pen in a courtroom.

"I spent eighteen months learning to be invisible," I said. "And it nearly cost us everything, because invisible people can't defend themselves. They can only hide and hope." I turned the carriage clock's key a final quarter-turn. "I'm done hiding. No offense to your coats."

"No offense," she said gravely. "Taken none."

She left the next morning before full light. We all stood in the doorway to see her go—Erik and Marta and Linnea and I, four Nystroms in the cold, watching a young woman walk down Katarina Street with a single bag, unhurried, like someone going to market. At the corner she stopped. She came back. She had forgotten, she said, one thing.

She put it in my hand: a small brass wheel, perhaps the size of a coin, pinioned and polished, forty teeth, cut finer than anything our shop produced. I turned it over. On one flat face, scratched with a scriber in a hand far steadier than her Swedish, was a single word in Finnish: *kiitos.*

Thank you.

"It's from a ship's chronometer," she said. "One that kept perfect time through a very bad year." She looked up at me, and her eyes did the thing that made liars confess and informants change sides. "Keep it where you keep your name."

Then she went, and this time the corner let her, and the street was empty, and the snow began again as if it had been waiting for privacy.

* * *

Winter deepened. The workshop filled and emptied with its ordinary traffic of broken watches and anxious owners. Father took on a delivery contract with a shipping office that asked no questions and paid on time, which after everything seemed almost suspiciously virtuous. Marta planted bulbs in a window box, absurdly early, on the theory that the police had confiscated enough of ours. Linnea's sweetheart—the customs clerk, Arvid, brave in the way of quiet men—began taking Sunday supper with us, sitting where Aino had sat, looking terrified and happy in equal measure.

I hung the brass gear from Aino on a nail above my bench, next to the rate book and the guild certificate. Forty teeth. I counted them sometimes when a job went badly. Forty teeth, and every one of them cut by someone who had chosen, against all sense, to trust a house full of careful hands.

And on the twenty-ninth of December, a letter came.

It was not from Volkov. It bore the crest of the Stockholm Observatory, and inside was a note in a clerk's careful copperplate: the master clock, fully restored to service since spring, had been formally inspected; the director wished to express the observatory's continued satisfaction with the repairs executed by Herr O. Nystrom, journeyman; and further, the observatory would be honored if Herr Nystrom would attend on New Year's Eve to perform the ceremonial winding of the master clock, as the signal would mark not only the new year but the observatory's return to public confidence. There would be a gallery. There would be guests. There would, the clerk added, be a place in the gallery for the Herr Nystrom family.

I read it twice. Then I carried it home through the blue dusk and laid it on the supper table between the bread and the salt, the way important documents were laid down in our house—flat, face up, no commentary, letting the family do its silent reading around it.

Linnea read it first. Marta read it over her shoulder. Erik picked it up last, held it at arm's length the way he held everything, and read it through once, slowly, moving his lips.

"Well," he said at length. "They want the clock wound properly."

"That's all?" Linnea said.

"That's all it says." He set the letter down and looked at me across the table, and for a moment neither of us needed the other hundred words underneath. The clock I had saved. The clock that had started everything—the letter in the gears, the debt, the trial, the name I had spoken aloud in a courtroom while inspectors wrote it down forever. They wanted me to come back to it and wind it, and they wanted my family to stand in the gallery and watch, in full view of the whole city, as the new year began.

Marta reached over and squeezed my wrist, once, hard, which in her language was a paragraph.

"We'll need new clothes," she said. "All of us. Real ones."

"There's the tailor off Hornsgatan," Linnea said immediately, already planning, already dangerous. "Arvid knows him. If we go Thursday—"

And I sat there in the lamplight with my family's voices rising around me like warmth off a stove, holding the letter, thinking about a clock that had once kept a secret in its gears and now kept only time—honest, public, striking for the whole city to hear—and thinking that in two days I would climb the observatory stair with the people who taught me silence, and wind the mechanism, and let it run.

Outside, the snow kept falling on Katarina Street, softening the world, promising nothing, asking nothing.

Inside, the regulator on the shelf ticked on toward midnight, two seconds slow, mending.
