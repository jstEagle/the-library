## 22. The Master Clock, Wound

The invitation came by ordinary post, which Oscar took as a kindness. No official seal, no police envelope, no handwriting he would have to explain to Marta before she opened it. Just a plain card from the Royal Academy of Sciences, addressed to *Mr. Oscar Nystrom, Journeyman*, and inside, in the observatory director's careful hand:

*The master clock has run faithfully since your repair. We would be honored if you would wind it personally on New Year's Eve, when the city will set its time by it. Bring your family.*

He read it four times at the kitchen table while his tea went cold, and each time his eye snagged on the same word: *journeyman*. Eleven months ago that word had belonged to a future he could not quite imagine. Now it was printed after his name as though it had always been true.

"You'll wrinkle it," Linnea said, sliding the card out from under his fingers. She read it faster than he had — she read everything faster than he had — and then she looked up with an expression he had learned, over the past year, to distrust slightly. "You're going to say yes."

"I'm going to say yes."

"Good. Because if you said no I'd have gone in your place, and I'd have wound it too tight out of spite and blown the escapement into next Tuesday."

"That's not how escapements work."

"It's how *my* escapements work." She set the card down between them like a treaty. "Mama's already planning what we're wearing."

"She is not—"

Marta appeared in the doorway with a length of grey wool over her arm, wearing the particular smile of a woman who has been planning something for three weeks and has merely been waiting for permission to admit it.

---

The year had been long in the way that only years containing testimony can be long. Oscar still sometimes woke in the dark with the shape of Inspector Dahl's interview room behind his eyes — the bare bulb, the notebook, the moment when he had said his own name aloud, clearly, for the record: *Oscar Nystrom. Apprentice — journeyman now — of Hedlund & Sons, but born Nystrom, son of Anders Nystrom of Riga.* He had expected the words to cost him everything. They had cost him a great deal: the observatory position, certainly; several months of looking over his shoulder; one memorable week of newspaper coverage during which strangers had pointed at him on the tram and he had discovered that being visible was a full-time occupation.

But it had not cost him the family. That was the arithmetic he could never quite finish, the sum that refused to balance against all his old fears. He had spoken the whole truth — the letter in the clock case, the debt to Volkov, Aino shivering in the workshop through that snowstorm night, the forged manifests, the naval attaché laundering intelligence through the very institution whose time the city trusted — and when it was done, when Dahl had closed his notebook and the delegation had withdrawn in scandal and the warrant had been quietly burned, Erik had put his hand on Oscar's shoulder in the doorway of their own home and said, gruffly, in the voice of a man who had rehearsed the sentence for days:

"You did well, boy. Loudly, but well."

From Erik Nystrom, this was a eulogy, a knighthood, and an embrace all at once.

---

On the afternoon of the thirty-first, the family walked up the hill to the observatory together, because Marta declared that no son of hers would arrive at such an occasion alone, and because — though she would never say so — none of them wanted to be anywhere else that night but together.

The city below was already dressing itself for midnight. Lamplighters made their rounds along the boulevards, and here and there windows glowed with preparations Oscar could only guess at: tables being set, corks being eased, children being warned for the hundredth time not to touch the matches. Snow lay thin and clean on the rooftops, squeaking underfoot, and the cold had the bright, ringing quality of a bell freshly struck.

Erik walked beside him, hands clasped behind his back, saying nothing, which from Erik was conversation. Twice he glanced up at the observatory dome against the darkening sky, and twice he nodded to himself, as though confirming some private ledger entry.

"You've been up there often?" he asked at last.

"Every week since spring. It runs well. It wanted cleaning more than repairing, in the end — the jamming did it no favors, but the movement itself is sound. Good work underneath." Oscar paused. "Someone built it carefully, a long time ago, and everyone since has mostly just gotten in its way."

"Mm," said Erik. There was a ghost of a smile under his mustache. "There's a lesson in that."

"For clocks?"

"For most things."

Linnea, walking backward ahead of them so she could address the whole party at once — a habit that had once driven Marta to distraction and now merely drove her to resigned affection — was explaining to their mother, for the third time, exactly where they would stand.

"—the gallery wraps around the instrument room, Mama, and the director said specifically, *specifically*, that we are to be on the north side, because the north windows face the city and at midnight they fire the signal gun from Skeppsholmen and you can see the flash—"

"And you memorized this because?"

"Because someone in this family has to remember things out loud." Linnea's grin softened into something else, briefly — the look she got when she was thinking about manifests, or customs houses, or the evening she had copied documents at risk of her own good name because a brother who wasn't legally hers needed saving. "It's a useful skill. I recommend it."

"I recommend you marry that clerk of yours before he realizes what he's agreed to."

"He hasn't agreed to anything yet. I'm letting him think it was his idea. Another month ought to do it."

Marta sighed the sigh of mothers everywhere and adjusted her hat.

---

Inside, the observatory smelled the way it always had — cold stone, lamp oil, and beneath it all the faint dry sweetness of machine oil and old wood that Oscar associated with every important room of his life. The corridors were quiet; most of the staff had gone home early or were gathering in the instrument rooms below, and their footsteps echoed off walls built thick enough to keep out weather, war, and gossip alike.

The director met them at the stair, rubbing his hands together, radiating the nervous hospitality of a man hosting something larger than himself. He shook Erik's hand. He bowed over Marta's. He told Linnea that the Academy could use a person of her memory, and Linnea said, "I know," in a tone that made Oscar certain the Academy would indeed hear from her within the decade, whether it invited her or not.

And then they climbed the narrow stair to the clock room, and Oscar stood again before the master clock.

It stood taller than a man, cased in dark walnut, its brass pendulum gleaming, its dial white and sober as a page awaiting its sentence. Through the glass of the case he could see the movement — the great wheels, the escapement ticking its patient tick — and he felt the old familiar pull in his chest, the one that had brought him here first as a mute apprentice sent alone on an errand, and later as a suspect, and finally as the man who had taken it apart bolt by bolt and put it back together with his own hands.

A year ago tonight, he reflected, he had been someone who listened to gears because people were dangerous. The gears had never lied to him. They had only ever hidden things, and even then — he thought of a folded letter wedged deliberately into an escapement — even then, they had hidden things *on purpose*, waiting for someone patient enough to find them.

Perhaps that was the whole of his education, he thought. Perhaps everything he knew about truth he had learned from machinery: that concealment is a kind of speech; that whatever is jammed will eventually stop the works; that a mechanism, however complex, can be understood by anyone willing to sit with it long enough and count.

"You're doing the face," Linnea whispered.

"What face?"

"The one where you're having a philosophical experience with a piece of furniture."

"It's not furniture. It's the most honest citizen in Stockholm."

---

At half past eleven, the director showed him the winding crank and withdrew with the family to the gallery, leaving Oscar alone with the clock, as tradition apparently required — *the keeper winds it alone*, the old rule ran, *so that the year begins with one man's care and no one else's negligence.*

Oscar took off his coat. He rolled his sleeves the way Erik had taught him, precise folds above the elbow, and fitted the crank to the barrel.

The great weights hung low in their shafts, having given nearly all themselves to the dying year. He began to turn.

It was hard, steady work, the kind he loved best — work that answered effort with motion, that kept no secrets and required none. Ratchet click by ratchet click, the weights rose. He could feel the health of the mechanism through the crank, the way he had learned to feel it in a thousand smaller clocks: the slight resistance of a bearing wanting oil come spring, the clean bite of the pawl, somewhere far above the faint, untroubled heartbeat of the escapement. *Tick. Tick.* Forty years of ticks, he'd been told. Wars had begun and ended within earshot of this clock, treaties signed, kings crowned, ships launched, and through it all the escapement had counted, indifferent and faithful, asking nothing but winding and trusting its keeper.

His arms ached pleasantly by the end. He set the crank aside, wiped his hands, and stood a moment in the warm hush of the room, listening.

Then he went out to the gallery, where his family waited.

---

They arranged themselves at the north windows as Linnea had commanded. Below, Stockholm spread out in a spill of lamplight and snow — the ridges, the water black between the islands, the ferries' lanterns crawling, and beyond it all the dark country sleeping toward the new year. Somewhere down there, Anton Reval was spending the evening with his son; he and Oscar had exchanged letters through the autumn, stiff at first, then less so. Somewhere across the sea, Dmitri Volkov was raising whatever exiles raise in place of champagne. Somewhere in the harbor fog, a young woman named Aino carried papers for a network that was, this year, on the side of the light — and in Oscar's waistcoat pocket, wrapped in a scrap of cloth, rode a small brass gear she had left on his bench the morning she departed, cut to no wheel in any train he owned, useless and perfect, polished by her thumb until it shone.

He touched it once, through the cloth, and left it where it was.

"Ten o'clock dinner at home tomorrow," Marta was saying behind him. "Erik, tell me you haven't promised Herr Lindqvist you'd look at his grandmother's regulator on New Year's Day."

"I promised nothing. He asked. I may have failed to say no."

"Erik."

"It keeps my hands busy, Marta."

"Your hands have been busy for fifty years. Sit down once in a while. The world will survive an unwound regulator for one day."

"The world," said Erik, with immense dignity, "would notice."

Linnea laughed, and the sound went up into the dome and came back down warmer. And Oscar stood at the window with his hands folded, watching the city, feeling the clock tick at his back through the wall — through the floor, through the stone, through his own ribs — and understood that he was, at this moment, happier than he had ever been, and that part of why he could feel it was that he had stopped believing happiness had to be hidden to be kept.

That had been the old silence — the silence of 1908, of a grieving boy on a stranger's doorstep, of a household where love was expressed as concealment, where questions were dangerous and answers more so. That silence had protected them, in its way. It had also nearly buried them. He had spent a year learning the difference between a silence that hides and a silence that simply *holds* — the silence of a finished mechanism, all its parts known to one another, needing no words to run true.

The gallery filled gradually with staff and their families, murmuring, stamping cold feet. The director checked his pocket watch against the clock and announced the quarter hour. Down in the city, church bells began to stir.

"Nervous?" Erik asked quietly, coming to stand beside him.

"No."

"Liar. Your jaw does the thing your mother's does."

Oscar smiled. "A little. It feels like an examination."

"It isn't. You passed those already." Erik looked out at the lights below, and for a while said nothing, and the nothing was comfortable, worn smooth as a well-handled tool. Then: "Your father would have liked this. He liked machines that outlast their makers." A pause. "He would have been proud of you, Oscar. Not of the testimony — though that too. Of this." He nodded at the wall, at the ticking. "Of a man the city can set its watch by."

Oscar found he couldn't speak for a moment. He managed, finally, "Thank you," and Erik patted his shoulder twice, briskly, the way he tightened a case screw, and turned back to Marta as though nothing had happened, which was its own kind of tenderness, and always had been.

The director raised his hand. The murmurs died.

"Five minutes, gentlemen. The clock will strike the hour, and the gun will answer from Skeppsholmen, and the year will begin. Mr. Nystrom—" he inclined his head toward Oscar, formally, before the whole gallery— "the master clock is yours."

---

Midnight came the way Oscar had always believed it should: mechanically, inevitably, beautifully.

The great clock gathered itself, and struck — twelve deep bronze strokes rolling out over the city, each one falling into the streets like a stone into still water. On the twelfth stroke, far below, the signal gun flashed orange from the island, and a beat later the report arrived up the hill like a handshake. Bells broke out everywhere at once, a dozen steeples answering the master, and along the quays whistles shrieked and windows lit and voices rose, ten thousand of them, all suddenly saying the same thing to one another in the freezing dark.

Beside him, Linnea was cheering. Marta had both hands pressed to her mouth, laughing. Erik stood straight as a mast, and if his eyes were bright, no one was rude enough to mention it.

Oscar watched the city greet its new year and let the noise wash over him without needing any of it.

The clock ticked on behind the wall, unhurried amid all the celebration, already counting the first minutes of 1914. It didn't know what the year held. Neither did he. But he had taken this mechanism apart and found it sound, and he had reason to believe the same might be said of most things, given patience, given care, given hands willing to do the work in the cold.

He reached into his pocket and closed his fingers around Aino's little gear, warm now from his own warmth.

*Tick,* said the master clock, beginning the year's first minute. *Tick. Tick.*

Oscar listened — not because he had to, not because speaking was dangerous, but because it was beautiful — and smiled, and stayed.

*The End.*
