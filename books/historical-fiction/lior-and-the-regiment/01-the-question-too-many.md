## 1. The Question Too Many

The census taker arrived on a Tuesday, which Mara Hartwell would remember afterward as the first cruelty of it — that the day had begun so ordinary. Rain since dawn, the kind of thin autumn rain that made the cobblestones of Voss Street shine like spilled mercury and turned every errand into an argument with wet wool. Mara had spent the morning at the kitchen table of the house on Voss Street, mending the seam of a driving glove with waxed thread, and the house around them had done what it always did in the early hours: settled into its bones.

The Voss house was a tall, narrow thing, three stories of grey brick with white lintels that Edith insisted be repainted every spring whether they needed it or not. It smelled of lamp oil and lavender water and, underneath both, faintly of coal smoke that no amount of scrubbing ever fully banished. In twenty years Mara had learned the house the way a sailor learns a ship — every groan of the staircase, every window that swelled shut in damp weather, every place the floor dipped where a hundred years of feet had worn the boards concave. They knew which step to skip on the back stairs because it sang, and they knew that if you stood very still in the front hall at half past eight you could hear Miss Edith moving above you, the particular rhythm of her walking: brisk, purposeful, a woman rehearsing even her mornings.

Mara was thirty-eight years old by the papers they carried, though the papers were wrong about nearly everything else, so there was no particular reason to trust them about the age. The papers said Mara Hartwell, driver, born in the mill town of Harrow's Bend, resident of this district for six years. Six years. Before that, other districts, other names on other registers, each one chosen with the care other people gave to choosing curtains. A name was a coat. You wore it until it wore out, and then you found another, and after two decades Mara owned a whole wardrobe of them, folded away in memory like garments too dangerous to burn but too heavy to carry openly.

The knock came at ten minutes past nine.

Three raps, even and official. Not the hurried double-knock of a delivery boy, not the soft apologetic tap of a neighbor come to borrow sugar. Three raps said *the government is at your door*, and Mara's hands went still over the glove before their mind had finished deciding to be afraid.

"Miss Hartwell?" the taker called through the door, because of course he'd already asked the neighbor, and the neighbor had already told him. Mrs. Pell across the way knew everything about everyone on Voss Street except the things that mattered, and she gave away the things that mattered freely because she didn't know they were the ones that mattered. "Census enumeration. District Nine. I'll only take a quarter hour."

Mara set down the glove. They looked once toward the stairs, where Edith's rhythm had paused — she'd heard the knocking too, and was listening the way she listened to everything now, from behind a door, weighing whether it concerned the legend or merely the woman. Then Mara crossed the hall, checked their face in the dark oval of the hall mirror — *nothing*, they told the reflection, *show it nothing* — and opened the door.

The man on the step was younger than expected. Forty perhaps, with rain beaded on the brim of a felt hat and a leather document case under one arm, held with the careful dryness of a man who understood that paper was his whole religion. He had a long, patient face, the kind of face that made you want to fill its silences, and grey eyes that did not blink quite often enough.

"Mr. Fen," he said, producing a card. "Aldous Fen. Commission of Enumeration." The card was stiff cream stock, printed in small severe type, and Mara noted — filed away without meaning to — that the Commission's seal on it was newer than any seal Mara had seen used for a mere headcount. "I apologize for the weather and the hour."

"The hour is fine." Mara stepped back to let him in, because refusing entry to a census taker was itself a kind of answer, and answers were the one currency Mara had spent twenty years hoarding. "You'll want the table, I expect. The light's better."

*The light is better.* As if this were a portrait sitting. As if anything about this were casual.

Fen set his case on the kitchen table, unbuckled it with two precise movements, and withdrew a ledger bound in green board, a stoppered ink bottle, a pen, and blotting paper, arranging all four items in a row so neat it looked ceremonial. He did not unfold the ledger yet. That was the first odd thing. Most takers opened their books on the doorstep like peddlers eager to show their wares.

"You are—" he began, and then consulted a loose slip rather than his ledger, which was the second odd thing, because a proper enumerator had his households memorized by the second visit, and District Nine was not a first-visit district. "—Mara Hartwell. Occupation, driver and — ah — correspondence assistant?"

"Reader," Mara said. "I read letters aloud. Miss Voss's eyes aren't what they were."

"A valuable trade." Something moved behind the grey eyes — not a smile, but the place a smile would live on another man. "Literacy in service of others. The Commission finds such work notable. We're recording skills this year, not just names. Names are the least of it, really."

He said it lightly, but Mara heard the sentence land wrong, like a stair that isn't where your foot expects it. *Names are the least of it.* Every instinct Mara owned, all of them sharpened on years of checkpoints and registration desks and men with ledgers, stood up at once and paid attention.

*It's a census,* Mara told themself. *They count people every ten years. It's arithmetic, not memory.*

But the last time the counters had come, twenty years ago, the counting had been followed by lists, and the lists by warrants, and the warrants by the war that officially never happened — the civil conflict that the histories now called *the Unrest*, as though the country had been a restless child rather than a nation tearing its own throat. Twenty years since the guns stopped. Twenty years since the victors sat down to decide what the war had been about, and discovered that what it had been about was inconveniently full of women.

"Shall we begin?" Fen uncapped his ink.

They began. Name — Mara Hartwell, given without hesitation because hesitation was a smell and dogs and bureaucrats both caught it. Age. Birthplace, Harrow's Bend. Years resident in the district, six. Marital status — no. Dependents — none. All of it flowed out of Mara smooth as poured water, each answer worn so long it had gone soft at the edges, comfortable as an old glove. Fen wrote in a hand that was surprisingly fine, almost clerical, each figure formed with a draftsman's patience. The rain ticked against the kitchen window. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard shifted; Edith was still listening, or had gone back to her speech, and Mara could not tell which and did not look up to find out, because looking up would tell Fen something too.

Then Fen turned a page, ran his pen down a column, and asked the question.

"And where were you during the war years, Miss Hartwell? For the record of national service. Everyone's being entered — residence and disposition, '41 through '45. Where were you when the country was — unsettled?"

The kettle on the stove had been heating since before the knock, and it chose that moment to begin its low gathering whistle, thin at first, building. Mara rose to move it off the heat, and the movement bought four seconds, five, and in those seconds they built the answer they had built a hundred times before in a hundred doorways: *domestic service in the western counties, then Harrow's Bend again after my mother took ill.*

"Western counties mostly," Mara said, back turned, lifting the kettle. "Domestic service. Then home to Harrow's Bend when my mother fell ill. She died in '44. I stayed on after."

"I see." A pause, the length of a pen stroke. "Which parishes? The western counties are large."

"Small places." Mara set the kettle down and turned, and their voice came out level, pleasant, bored almost — twenty years of practice compressed into three syllables. "You wouldn't know them."

"I make a study of knowing places." Fen's pen hovered. He wasn't writing. That was worse than writing. "My apologies — I don't doubt you. Only the Commission has found gaps, here and there, in the wartime registers. Parishes that burned, clerks who fled. So for persons whose records are — incomplete — we ask a supplementary question." He looked up, and his eyes were mild as milk and just as opaque. "Where were you on the fourth of October, forty-two?"

The date went through Mara like a nail through cloth.

The fourth of October. The bridge. The morning the 3rd Regiment came down the coast road with fixed bayonets and orders to cross into the mill district, and three women stood on the span with their skirts whipping in the wind off the river, and the regiment — the whole armed weight of it — passed without a shot fired. The story the whole country knew now had one woman on that bridge. The story had a white dress and raised hands and a speech quoted in schoolbooks. The story had Edith Voss's name carved into a monument going up next month, and Mara had driven the stone-mason's cart to the quarry herself, had sat in the cold cab while men chiseled a legend into granite, and had said nothing, because saying nothing was the job, saying nothing was the wage, saying nothing was what kept a certain old desertion charge sleeping in some forgotten drawer.

And the fourth of October, forty-two, was also — smaller, quieter, known to almost no one living — the day a young army driver named something other than Hartwell had signed out a staff car for the last time and pointed it west instead of east, and become, in the eyes of the military clerk who would eventually notice, a deserter.

"No one remembers dates like that," Mara said. "Not after twenty years."

"Some do." Fen blotted nothing, because he had written nothing yet, and the absence of ink in that moment was louder than any scratch of pen. "Witnesses tend to. The Commission has learned that people hold their significant dates the way they hold scars. They can't always see them, but they know exactly where they are."

Outside, the rain thickened. Down in the street a cart went by, wheels hissing on wet stone. Upstairs, unmistakably now, Edith's footsteps had stopped altogether, and the silence above them had the quality of a held breath.

"Miss Hartwell." Fen closed his pen cap with a small click, and leaned back, and for a moment he looked less like a census taker than a physician delivering a diagnosis he'd suspected for some time. "You've answered nineteen questions in eleven minutes, and every answer was fluent. Fluency is good. But I'm required to note that question twenty took you — " he glanced at his watch, an affectation, a prop, Mara thought wildly, a little theater of precision — "forty seconds and a kettle."

"The kettle wanted moving."

"It did. And before that, your hands were still." He said it without accusation, almost gently, the way one remarks on weather. "I've interviewed four hundred and six persons in this district. Do you know how many of them went still at the wartime questions?"

"I couldn't say."

"One." He opened the ledger at last, and Mara watched his fine clerical hand begin to move, and could not read a word of it upside down, and hated how much that mattered. "Most people ramble at those questions, Miss Hartwell. They give me their whole lives, unasked. Uncles, harvests, a cousin shot at Kessel Ford they've told a thousand times. Rags and noise. You gave me four clean sentences and silence. Silence is rarer." The pen scratched on. "Silence is interesting."

"Silence is grief," Mara said quietly. "My mother died in that war's shadow. There are rooms in those years I don't open. Surely the Commission doesn't require a widow's daughter to inventory her griefs."

Something flickered across Fen's face — respect, possibly, or its bureaucratic cousin, professional disappointment at a wall well-built. "No," he said. "Of course not. Grief is exempted. The form allows for it." And he wrote, briefly, a single line, and Mara watched the nib travel and still could not read it.

He packed his case with the same ceremony he'd unpacked it, buckled it, and stood. At the hall door he put on his hat and turned the brim down against the weather he hadn't yet walked into, and paused with his hand on the latch.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "your answers will be entered as complete. Residence confirmed, disposition — domestic service, western counties. It will stand." A beat, the rain loud on the step beyond him. "Unless something should surface that requires the entry to be revisited. Entries are revisited sometimes. When documents surface."

"What sort of documents?"

"Oh — registers. Pay books. Old army paperwork, mostly." He said it the way a man mentions rain coming. "Paper survives, Miss Hartwell. People assume wars burn everything, but paper is stubborn. It waits in drawers. It waits for commissions." He tipped his hat an inch. "Thank you for the use of your light."

The door closed behind him, and Mara stood in the hall with the echo of the latch still in their ears and their heart going hard and slow, like a fist knocking from inside a wall.

Twenty years. Twenty years of doors just like that one, opening and closing on the same constructed woman, and never once — not at a checkpoint, not at a registration desk, not at the marriage registrar's counter where a helpful clerk had squinted too long at a birth parish — never once had anyone written something down *because of the silence*. Noise they could file. Noise was safe. But Fen had written a line after Mara's four clean sentences, and Mara would think about that line all day, and all week, would turn it over at night like a coin whose faces wouldn't match.

From the top of the stairs, Edith's voice came down, pitched carefully casual. "Was that the government, or a salesman?"

"Census," Mara called back, and climbed the steps, skipping the singing one out of habit, and found Edith standing in the doorway of her study with her speech pages in one hand, seventy years old and straight-backed as a flagpole, her famous face arranged into its public mildness — the face from the pamphlets, the face from the bridge.

"Well," Edith said. "Did they ask you many questions?"

"The usual."

Edith studied her for a moment too long. Twenty years of shared silence had made them fluent in each other's pauses, and this pause said: *you've gone pale, child.* What Edith said aloud was, "Good. I hate how they pry. As if a person's whole life fits in their columns." She retreated into the study, and the door clicked shut, and beneath it her footsteps resumed their rehearsal rhythm, brisk, purposeful, a woman walking toward October the fourth all over again.

Mara went back down to the kitchen. The mended glove lay on the table where they'd left it, needle still threaded, and beside it, unnoticed until now, dropped in the excitement of the packing-up, one small card of stiff cream stock.

**ALDOUS FEN. COMMISSION OF ENUMERATION, DISTRICT NINE.**

Mara turned it over. On the reverse, in that fine clerical hand, in pencil, faint as breath: a date. *4.X.42.* Nothing more. No accusation, no question mark. Just the date, preserved, patient, waiting in a drawer somewhere to become whatever the Commission needed it to become.

Mara stood holding it as the rain came harder against the glass, and understood, with the calm of a person watching ice spread across a river they must soon cross, that the twenty quiet years were over.

* * *

That night Mara could not sleep. The house ticked and settled around them; Edith's lamp burned late above, as it always did before a speech. Mara lay listening to the rain and doing the arithmetic they had sworn off doing: eleven weeks, roughly, until winter session — until, if the newspapers' hints meant anything, the new National Archive opened its doors and sealed its shelves, and everything the country remembered became permanent, official, and wrong.

In the drawer of the bedside table, under a folded map and a spare glove, lay the oldest thing Mara owned: not a photograph, not a keepsake, but a number — a regimental service number, memorized so long ago it lived in the body now, in the marrow, in the scar tissue of the left hand. A number attached to a name that no one had spoken aloud in twenty years.

Mara whispered it once, into the dark, just to feel the shape of it. Just to prove it still fit.

Then they got up, and lit the lamp, and took out a clean sheet of paper, and sat looking at it for a long time before writing a single word — because tomorrow they would drive Edith to the quarry unveiling rehearsals, and read her letters, and edit the dangerous passages by habit, and be invisible, flawless, fluent.

But tonight, in the lamplight, Mara Hartwell began — slowly, in a hand they hadn't used since the war — to make a list. Of parishes in the western counties they'd have to learn. Of lies they'd have to shore up. And at the bottom, after a hesitation that lasted the length of a held breath, of the one person alive besides Edith who had stood on the bridge, and who might still, after twenty years, be found.

The pen touched the page. The rain went on. Somewhere across the sleeping city, in a lodging house Mara had never seen, Aldous Fen was writing something down too.
