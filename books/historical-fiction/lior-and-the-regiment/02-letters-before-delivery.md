## 2. Letters Before Delivery

The morning letters came in a canvas sack that smelled of coal smoke and other people's lives, and Mara carried it up the back stairs the way she had carried it every weekday for six years—balanced against her hip, sorted in her hands before she reached the landing. Bills first, left side of the arm. Invitations, middle. The small heap of letters from strangers who had read about Edith Voss in the papers and believed themselves moved to write to her: those went on the right, and those were the ones that mattered.

Mara had made a science of the right-hand pile.

She let herself into the morning room, where the fire was already laid but not yet lit because Edith liked to light it herself, a small ceremony she performed with a brass poker and an expression of great seriousness, as though the flame were a motion before parliament and required her personal assent. The room was all soft greens and worn leather, books stacked in drifts against the wainscoting, a globe with the war's borders still inked on it because Edith refused to buy one of the new maps. *The old map is honest,* she said. *It shows where the scars are.*

Mara set the sack on the desk, drew the curtains halfway—the light in that room was meant to be borrowed, not taken—and began the work.

The trade was officially called *secretary*, though Edith introduced her as *my driver* at rallies and *my reader* among friends, and privately, when she'd had two glasses of the good sherry, as *the person who keeps me honest*, which was a joke between them with teeth in it. The truth was that Mara did all three things and none of them cleanly. She drove the motorcar down roads that still remembered artillery ruts under their new asphalt. She stood at the edge of platforms while Edith spoke, watching crowds for the faces that weren't cheering. And each morning she sat in this green room and read the correspondence aloud, because Edith's eyes had begun, three winters ago, to betray her—words swimming, lines doubling—and because Edith would sooner have surrendered the vote itself than admit to a crowd that the great Edith Voss needed her letters read to her by the woman who also tuned the motorcar's carburetor.

So they had built a ritual out of necessity, and like most rituals built that way, it had become load-bearing.

"From the top," Edith said now, coming in with her sleeves already rolled, which meant she intended to answer at least half of these before luncheon. She settled into her chair by the cold hearth, waiting for her fire like a queen awaiting a crown she intended to place on her own head.

Mara slit the first envelope with the bone knife kept in the desk drawer for exactly this purpose.

*"Dear Mrs. Voss,"* she read. *"I was at the Harrowgate rally on Saturday and I have not stopped thinking since of what you said about the bridge—"*

"Skip ahead," Edith said. "They always take a page getting to the bridge."

Mara skipped. The bridge. It was always the bridge. Twenty years on, the bridge at Callow's Ford had become less a place than an organ of the body politic—something the country claimed to feel with, cited when it ached, swore by when it wanted to seem brave. Every schoolchild could recite it: how on the fourth of Thaw-month, in the last winter of the war, a single woman had walked onto the span with nothing but a lantern and her voice, and had stood alone in the road while a full regiment came down upon her, and how the regiment—two hundred men with loaded rifles and orders to cross—had stopped. Had halted, boots ringing on the planks, and then turned around, and gone home. Because of her. Because of Edith Voss, alone.

Mara had heard the story so many times she could no longer hear it at all, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. It played underneath everything now. Under the rallies. Under the anniversary speeches. Under the letters from girls who wrote to say they wanted to be *like her someday*, alone and unafraid on some bridge of their own.

She read the letter through, editing as she went—not aloud, never audibly, but in the small silent arithmetic she performed without thinking anymore, the way a bank clerk counts change. A phrase here about *"the cowards in government"*—softened in the reading to *"the men in government,"* because the word coward, printed and quoted, gave the newspapers a stick to beat the movement with. A sentence there describing what the writer had seen soldiers do at Harrowgate—omitted entirely, because the writer named names and the writer's husband worked at the telegraph office and would lose his position if his wife's letter became public knowledge. A paragraph of grief about a son lost at the Ford—kept, every word of it, delivered whole and slow, because grief like that was the movement's truest coin and Edith needed to touch it with her own hands.

"You're doing the thing," Edith said, not looking up from the address book in her lap.

"What thing."

"The thing where you decide for me." But she said it mildly, almost fondly, the way one accuses a dog of stealing food one has oneself slipped it under the table. Six years ago, when Mara had first begun the silent edits, Edith had fought her on it—had demanded full text, verbatim, *every word, Hartwell, I'm not a child*. Then a letter had been stolen from her desk at a rally in Dunmore and printed in the opposition paper, and a family had been ruined over a sentence Edith hadn't even written, and after that Edith had said only, quietly, *use your judgment*, and had never mentioned it again. Judgment was a leash you handed someone and asked them to hold the other end themselves.

"I'll tell you if anything needs deciding," Mara said. "This next one doesn't. Invitation from the Women's Guild in Ashford. They want you for the anniversary."

"Of course they want me for the anniversary. Everyone wants me for the anniversary." Edith said it lightly, but her pen had stopped moving. "Twenty years. They're talking about a plaque this time. A bronze plaque on the bridge itself."

"They should put it on the far end," Mara said. "Where the regiment stopped."

Edith looked up then, and something passed across her face too quickly to name—a flicker, a shadow of a bird crossing a window—and was gone. "The near end is where I stood," she said. "The plaque goes where I stood."

The fire got lit. The ceremony was performed. And Mara read on.

* * *

There were forty-one letters that morning, which was a light day; after a speech it could run past eighty, and once, after the parliamentary defeat four years ago, past two hundred, arriving for weeks like ash after a fire. Mara had a system. Bills were totaled and set aside for the accountant. Threats—there were always threats, fewer than there used to be but never none—were read flatly and without inflection, per an agreement older than either of them cared to remember, and then locked in the second drawer with the others, because Edith insisted on keeping them (*evidence,* she said, *of how frightened they are*) and Mara insisted they be kept somewhere with a lock.

The strange letters were her favorites, though she'd never have said so. The ones written in pencil by children. The one, last spring, from a man who claimed to have been a private in the very regiment at the bridge and wrote three pages about the weather that day and nothing else, signing off *tell her the sky was the color of a scalded throat and we were glad to be stopped.* That one Mara had read twice, silently, before reading it aloud once, and had then put in her own pocket instead of the archive box, and had told herself she didn't know why.

By eleven o'clock the pile had thinned to the dregs—the circulars, the charity appeals, the crank mail—when Mara's thumb caught on an envelope unlike the others.

It was heavier stock, for one thing. Cream, not white. And the hand on the front was not a stranger's hand. It was small and exact, every letter formed with the same pressure, the kind of writing taught in clerical schools or acquired by men who had spent their lives making marks that other people would be judged by. She knew it because she had spent ten minutes, three days ago, watching it move across a census form in her own kitchen, and because she had spent twenty years learning to notice handwriting the way sailors notice weather. Handwriting was the one thing people couldn't help. You could change your name, your road, your face. Your hand betrayed you every time you picked up a pen.

*Mrs. E. Voss,* the envelope said. *Hollowfield House.*

And beneath the address, in the same precise script: *Office of War Records & Historical Commission.*

Mara stood quite still with the letter in her hand. Outside the window, October was doing its patient work on the garden—browning the hydrangeas, pulling the last leaves off the plum tree one by one, as though the season had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Somewhere down the lane the postman's bicycle bell rang, cheerful and receding.

"Hartwell." Edith had looked up again. "You've gone quiet. What is it?"

"A letter." Mara heard her own voice come out level, and marveled briefly at the machinery of it, the way it worked without her. "From a historian's office."

"Well." Edith held out her hand, palm up, in the gesture she used for everything—letters, microphones, verdicts. "Open it."

But Mara didn't open it. Not yet. She turned the envelope over once, twice, feeling its weight, and inside that weight she felt something else, something with edges. A census taker who writes things down when you don't answer. An office of war records asking for papers. Eleven days apart. Two small events, and events, Mara had learned, rarely traveled alone—they traveled in convoys, dressed as coincidence.

Because here was the thing about being invisible for twenty years, about practicing invisibility the way other people practiced piano, daily, until the fingers knew the keys blind: it made you expert in the opposite discipline. You learned to see. You learned that nobody watches the driver. And you learned—this was the bitter curriculum, the tuition paid in full—that the world's paperwork never forgets anyone, even when everyone does.

There was a name Mara had not written in twenty years. There was a form, filled out in a barracks office in the last winter of the war, that still existed somewhere in some drawer, bearing that name in a younger hand. She had told herself for two decades that the state had lost interest in her—that she had fallen through the cracks of the peace, one deserter among thousands, one name among the shuffled millions, too small a fish for the nets of the new government. She had told herself that so long and so steadily that it had begun to sound like fact.

And now a cream envelope, in a careful clerk's hand, addressed to the most famous woman in the country, from an *office*—not a historian, offices had historians the way churches had priests, many, interchangeable, working in shifts—an office of war records, requesting papers.

"Hartwell." Edith's palm was still out. "The letter."

Mara slit it with the bone knife. One clean stroke. Inside was a single sheet on Commission letterhead, the crest stamped in gray ink rather than colored, which struck her as somehow worse—gray ink was what governments used when they didn't want to spend money on something they might disavow.

*"Dear Mrs. Voss,"* she read aloud, and her voice stayed level, the machinery holding. *"In anticipation of the twentieth anniversary of the Armistice, this Commission has undertaken the preparation of an authoritative history of the home front during the late conflict. As your name features prominently in the historical record of that period, we respectfully request access to any papers, correspondence, diaries, or testimonies in your possession relating to your wartime activities, and in particular to the events at Callow's Ford bridge, fourth of Thaw-month. Such materials may be deposited with the Commission at your convenience, or examined in situ by an officer of this office. We are, madam, your obedient servants—"*

"Stop," Edith said.

Mara stopped. In six years Edith had interrupted her readings perhaps a dozen times, and never on this word, never with this tone—which was not annoyance but something quieter and more dangerous, the voice of a woman listening for a sound under a sound.

"*Examined in situ,*" Edith repeated slowly. "An officer of this office. Coming here." She set down her pen with exaggerated care, the way she did when she wanted her hands to look steady. "What officer? Did they give a name?"

Mara looked at the signature block. She had known it would be there. Some part of her had known it since she saw the handwriting on the envelope, since the moment in her kitchen three days ago when a mild gray man with ink-stained cuffs had asked his question too many and written something down anyway.

*"Yours faithfully,"* she read, *"A. Fen, Field Correspondent, Records Verification Division."*

The room was very quiet. The fire Edith had lit herself popped once in the grate and settled.

"Fen," Edith said. "Do you know him?"

"No," Mara said.

Which was true, and not true, and both halves of it were necessary. She did not know him. She knew his handwriting. She knew he took tea with two sugars and stirred counterclockwise, an affectation or a habit she couldn't decide which. She knew that when he asked where she had been during the war years, and she gave him the answer she had given landlords and registrars and gossips for twenty years—*domestic service, ma'am, various households, the war mostly passed me by*—he had smiled the way clerks smile, which is to say not at all, and had written something down anyway.

Something that was not what she had said.

"He wants your war papers," Mara said. "Or he wants to come and look at whatever you keep."

"And what do I keep?" Edith asked lightly, too lightly, reaching for her sherry though the hour was early even for her ceremonies. "Clippings. Speeches. Photographs of me looking determined. The nation's memory of me is kept in bronze and newsprint, Hartwell, not in my desk drawers."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you need." Edith drank, set down the glass, and Mara watched her become, visibly, the public Edith—the spine straightening, the chin lifting, the whole architecture of legend assembling itself around her like armor being buckled on by an experienced hand. "We will decline, of course. Politely. We will wish the Commission every success and inform them that my private correspondence is private. That has always been sufficient."

"For twenty years," Mara agreed.

"Yes." A pause, thin as wire. "Why wouldn't it be now?"

Mara looked at the envelope again. At the small, exact, merciless handwriting. At the words *Records Verification Division*, which had the ring of a thing invented recently by people who understood that the old words—archives, history, truth—had grown soft with use and needed replacing with words that sounded like machinery.

"Because verification," Mara said, "isn't collection. Collection is what you do when you want to remember. Verification is what you do when you think somebody's lying."

Edith looked at her for a long moment, and in that moment Mara saw something she had never seen in six years of mornings: Edith Voss, alone, without an audience, looking afraid. It lasted perhaps a second. Then the armor closed.

"Send the decline," Edith said. "My hand, not yours. And Hartwell—" she turned back to the fire, to the globe with its honest scarred borders, to anything but Mara's face— "bring the motorcar round at two. We've a rally in Millbrook and I intend to be magnificent."

Mara gathered the letters, the bills, the threats for the locked drawer. She left the Commission letter on the desk, squared neatly, because Edith would want to read it herself a dozen times through her failing eyes and pretend she wasn't memorizing it.

At the door she paused. It was a small pause, half a breath, the kind of pause invisible people are permitted.

But Edith noticed. Edith always noticed the pauses, even the ones no one else could see.

"Yes?"

"Nothing," Mara said. "The motorcar at two."

She went down the back stairs with the empty canvas sack folded under her arm, and in the cool of the stairwell, where no one could watch, she let herself think the thought she had been refusing since the kitchen three days ago:

*A. Fen. Field Correspondent. He asked me where I was during the war. He wrote something down anyway. And eleven days later, a letter arrives at this house, asking for the papers of the most famous woman in the country—from the same hand.*

Coincidences traveled in convoys. And somewhere behind that convoy, she suspected, came the question she had dodged for twenty years, patient as October, coming up the lane toward Hollowfield House with all the time in the world and every intention of using it.

The motorcar needed oiling before two. Mara went to see to it, and found that her hands, which had done six years of steady work, were not entirely steady now.
