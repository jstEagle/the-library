## 3. The Legend of Edith Voss

The study smelled of lamp oil and cold tea when Edith Voss rose at six to rehearse her death.

Not her own death—she was sixty-one and intended to outlast several more governments—but the death she had staged for herself twenty years ago on the Kestrel Bridge, the one the whole country would be asked to remember again in three weeks' time. She had learned long ago that a woman could die publicly and go on living privately, and that this arrangement suited everyone. The nation got its martyr. She got her mornings.

She stood at the window first, as always, watching the fog lift off the garden in gray ribbons. The greenhouse caught the early light and threw it back in panes. Somewhere below, the gravel crunched—Mara, already up, already sweeping the drive or checking the motor's tires or doing whatever quiet, indispensable thing Mara did at that hour that made the rest of the day possible. Edith had stopped trying to catalogue those labors years ago. It was enough that they happened, the way weather happened.

Then she turned to the lectern.

It was a plain thing, pine, bought secondhand from a defunct chapel, and she had delivered four hundred speeches from it if she had delivered one. It stood now beside her writing desk, angled toward the empty armchair where no audience sat, and on its lip lay the manuscript: eleven pages in her own hand, corrected so many times the margins had gone black with revision.

She adjusted her spectacles. She cleared her throat once, softly, out of habit rather than need—the house was asleep, and even if it weren't, Edith Voss did not clear her throat apologetically—and began.

"Twenty years ago," she said, "on a morning much like this one, I stood alone upon the Kestrel Bridge."

The words came out smooth as river stones. That was the trouble with them. They had been handled so often, turned over so many thousands of times between her teeth, that every rough edge had worn away, and what remained was beautiful and frictionless and bore no resemblance to anything that had ever actually occurred.

*Alone.* The word sat there in the middle of the sentence like a coin too large for its purse.

Edith paused. She took off her spectacles, breathed on the lenses, polished them with her handkerchief, and put them back on. This ritual took eleven seconds. She knew because she had timed it once, in another life, when she still believed that pausing before a lie might make it easier to carry.

"I stood alone," she repeated, "when the regiment came down the coast road."

And here was the remarkable thing—the thing she had never confessed to anyone, not in twenty years of confession-shaped conversations that never quite arrived at confession: as she said it, standing in her dressing gown at a pine lectern with fog still clinging to the glass, she almost saw it. A woman alone on a bridge. Gray coat, gray morning, a column of soldiers coming on like weather. Her own hands raised—not in surrender, never in surrender, but in that gesture she had practiced until it lived in her shoulders, palms open, arms wide, *stop.*

The image was hers. She had earned it through repetition. If a story is told ten times, it is a story; if it is told ten thousand, it becomes a window, and you look through it and see yourself standing on the far side of the glass.

Mostly she believed it.

That *mostly* was the hinge her whole life swung on now, though she had learned to walk past the hinge without touching it, the way one walks past a door in one's own house that has been locked so long it has become furniture.

* * *

By seven the light had strengthened and Edith had worked through the speech twice, and by eight Mara knocked—two soft raps, always two, never three—and entered with the tray.

"The post came early," Mara said. "Three letters worth reading, two worth burning, and one bill."

"You read them to me after breakfast."

"After breakfast." Mara set the tray down: toast, marmalade, the pot of strong black tea without which Edith was famously unlivable. Mara moved around the study the way water moves around stones—unhurried, finding every path, disturbing nothing. Twenty years of mornings had worn that ease into both of them. "You were speaking when I passed the door."

"Rehearsing."

"For the anniversary."

"For the anniversary." Edith buttered her toast with the crisp economy of a woman who had once been poor and never forgot it. "They've built a platform on the north approach. Granite. Did you know? A granite platform, on a wooden bridge, for a woman who stood on nothing but planks and nerve."

"They mean well."

"They mean granite," Edith said, and Mara's mouth did something at the corner—not quite a smile, not quite its opposite—and withdrew to the doorway, and then was gone, and Edith sat alone with her tea and her speech and the particular silence of a house holding something in its walls.

She read the pages again while she ate. *I stood alone.* She had written the phrase first in 1909, in a letter to a newspaper that had gotten the bridge story wrong in an interesting way—they'd had her facing down cavalry, which was absurd, there hadn't been cavalry within forty miles—and in correcting them she had discovered something about herself: that correction was a door, and behind the door was a room, and inside the room she could arrange the furniture however she liked. The newspaper printed her letter. Readers wrote back. By the following spring, *stood alone* was no longer a phrase but a fact, and facts, unlike memories, could be engraved.

There had been a moment—she could locate it almost precisely, a Tuesday, rain—when she had last held the true version in her mind all at once, complete, like a photograph taken in full sun. After that Tuesday she had begun setting it down piece by piece, the way one sets down a heavy load when no one is watching, telling herself she would pick it up again directly. She never had. The load was still lying by that road somewhere, and she had walked twenty years without it, and her shoulders had forgotten the weight, and that forgetting was the closest thing to peace she had ever purchased.

She did not think of Cass.

She thought of Cass immediately, of course—that was the trick of not thinking of someone, that they arrive the instant you forbid them—but she did not *think* of her. There was a difference, and Edith Voss had become a scholar of the difference.

Cass Okafor. The name surfaced and she pressed it down the way one presses a stitch into fabric, flat, hidden, functional.

She drank her tea. Outside, the fog had burned away entirely, and the garden lay green and innocent, and somewhere beyond the wall the twentieth century went on manufacturing new reasons why women should stand on bridges.

* * *

At nine she worked on the speech properly, aloud, pacing the length of the study with the pages in hand, because Edith believed a speech was not written but walked.

"Twenty years ago," she said to the bookshelves, to the globe with its outdated borders, to the framed photograph of the 1908 march—ten thousand umbrellas like a field of black flowers—"twenty years ago, I stood alone upon the Kestrel Bridge, and I told a column of soldiers that they would have to go through me to cross it."

Here she raised her arms. Palms open. The gesture lived in her shoulders now; it cost her nothing.

"And do you know what they did?"

The bookshelves did not answer. In the hall version, the pause would stretch three seconds—she had measured it against the tick of a hundred mantel clocks—and then, softly:

"They stopped."

She let it land. Even in the empty room it landed; that was the craft of it, the part of her work she was genuinely proud of, the part no critic had ever managed to name. Anyone could shout. What Edith Voss could do was make a room full of strangers hold its breath in unison, and then release it, and in that release pour whatever she wished.

"They stopped," she said again, quieter, walking. "Five hundred men with rifles on their shoulders, and they stopped, because one woman would not move."

In the true version—

But she did not visit the true version. She noted its address, the way one notes the address of a house one used to live in, and walked on.

In the true version there had been mud, for one thing. No one ever put mud in the legend. There had been mud to the ankles and the wind coming off the estuary hard enough to make speech useless, which was why—this detail survived in her private ledger of facts, folded small—there had been shouting at all, why the whole episode had been less a confrontation than a chaos, three voices against five hundred, none of them audible beyond ten feet.

And there had been the captain.

Ah, the captain. Captain Reave, that was the name, though the legend required no names but hers. Captain Reave, whose horse—no, he hadn't ridden, he'd marched at the column's head with his collar up—Captain Reave, who had looked at the women on the bridge and then looked away, deliberately, theatrically, at the sky, at the estuary, anywhere but at them, and had given the order to halt, and then the order to find a ford, and had led his five hundred men splashing around through the shallows half a mile south as though the bridge were haunted. Which, in a sense, it had been. Bribes haunted things. Money changed hands quietly and soldiers obeyed, and afterward everyone agreed that conscience had done the work, and conscience was cheaper to commemorate than cash.

Edith had not paid the bribe.

This fact existed. She kept it in the same private ledger, on the same page, beneath the mud and the wind: *the money was not mine, the plan was not mine, the courage—if we must use that word—was distributed.* And above these entries, in the margin, in ink darker than the rest, the sentence she had written only once and never crossed out because crossing it out would require reading it twice: *and yet I let them print it anyway, and I slept, and I woke, and I let them print it again.*

She set down the pages.

The study was very quiet. The fire had settled to a red murmur. On the desk, the anniversary correspondence waited in its tidy stack—invitations, programs, a request from a sculptor asking how tall she had been at the time (five foot four, but the statue would be eight feet, statues inflate), and near the bottom, face-down, the envelope Mara had mentioned, the one with the unfamiliar return address: *Office of the Historian, Commission on the War Record.*

A historian wanted her papers.

Edith looked at that envelope for a long moment. Then she picked up her speech instead, and squared its edges against the lectern, and told herself what she had told herself for twenty years, which was that the story belonged to the movement now, and the movement needed it whole, and a story that keeps schools open and shelters lit and the vote itself breathing is not a lie so much as a load-bearing wall—you did not tear out a load-bearing wall to inspect its honesty. You hung your life on it and called it architecture.

"I stood alone," she said to the fire, one final time, testing the grain of it.

The fire said nothing. Fires are honest that way.

* * *

At noon she dressed and went down, and found Mara polishing the motorcar's lamps with a cloth gone gray from use.

"We're due at the Whitmore school Thursday," Mara said without looking up. "And Mrs. Aldous wants you Saturday, but I told her Saturday was doubtful."

"Saturday is doubtful."

"It's always doubtful. It's becoming our best-known position."

Edith smiled despite herself. This was the other country she and Mara inhabited together, the borderland of jokes, and she loved it the way one loves any country one cannot officially claim. She watched Mara work—those quick capable hands, the sleeves rolled, the smudge of polish on one wrist—and felt the old warmth and, beneath the warmth, the older colder thing, the thing shaped exactly like a bridge at dawn.

"Mara."

"Yes, ma'am."

"When I speak on the anniversary. When I say the words." She hesitated, and the hesitation itself was rare enough that Mara looked up. "How does it sit with you? Hearing them. All these years."

Mara considered the headlamp, turning it a degree, chasing some flaw of light only Mara could see. "It sits fine," Mara said.

"Just fine?"

"Just fine, ma'am." A pause, precisely measured, and then, mild as milk: "Though you say *alone* twice in the opening. Once might hit harder."

Edith laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and Mara went back to the lamps, and the moment closed over like water, and neither of them remarked on what had just passed through it, because they had spent twenty years perfecting the art of not remarking, and they were both, in their different ways, masters.

But that night, unable to sleep, Edith went down to the study in her dressing gown with a single candle, and she did not take out the speech. She unlocked the lower drawer of her writing desk—the drawer she opened perhaps twice a year, at night, always at night—and looked at what was in it, and did not touch it, and locked it again, and stood in the dark listening to her own heart behave badly.

Because that afternoon's post had contained a second letter she had not mentioned to Mara. It had come in the same morning delivery, tucked among the invitations, addressed in a cramped laborer's hand she had not seen in nineteen years but recognized instantly, the way one recognizes a voice through a wall.

She had read it standing in the hall. She had read it twice. Then she had folded it into quarters, smaller than any letter needs folding, and carried it upstairs, and it was in the drawer now, beneath the false bottom, with the other papers.

*I am not asking for glory,* the letter had said. *Glory was never the trade I was in. I am asking to be remembered correctly, before the people who could confirm it are gone. There is to be an official history, Mrs. Voss. They are collecting papers. I have decided I would rather be in the record than in your shadow, and I am ashamed it took me nineteen years to say so.*

Signed: *C. Okafor.*

Edith stood in the dark of her own study, candle guttering, and understood—with the clarity that comes only at midnight, that treacherous hour when every load-bearing wall shows its cracks—that the story she had told ten thousand times had just been told back to her by someone who had been standing on the bridge too.

And that the person who had carried the letters to the drawer, who swept the drive, who said *once might hit harder* with eyes that saw everything—Mara had sorted that morning's post. Mara had handed her the invitations and kept the count of the bills.

Had Mara seen the second letter?

Edith blew out the candle.
