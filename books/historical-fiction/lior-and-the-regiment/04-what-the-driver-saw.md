## 4. What the Driver Saw

The car smelled of petrol and old leather and, beneath both, the ghost of Edith Voss's lavender water, which had soaked into the seat cushions over eleven years of drives until it seemed less like a scent than a fact of the upholstery. Mara sat behind the wheel in the garage with the engine off and their hands at ten and two on nothing, and let the memory come, because lately it came whether they invited it or not.

Twenty years. That was the arithmetic the census taker's pencil had scratched into being three days ago—*where were you*—and now every quiet hour turned into a ledger of its own, tallying what Mara had been doing all that time. Driving. Reading letters aloud. Standing just outside the frame of photographs, holding doors, holding silence. Twenty years of being the person history walked past on its way to somebody more useful.

Mara got out of the car and went inside through the kitchen door, because the memory wanted walking room.

* * *

The road to Harrow Bridge had been mud to the axles that October, and the Ford had complained about it the whole way, gears grinding, tires sucking free of ruts with a sound like a reluctant kiss. Mara was twenty-three then and went by another name, wore a soldier's greatcoat dyed black so it wouldn't be recognized, and drove for the Women's Relief Circuit the way other people drove for the army—with papers in the door pocket that would hang them if anyone read them closely.

Three women in the back seat that morning. Not one. Three.

Edith Voss sat by the window in her grey coat, the one she'd later describe in speeches as her *armor*, though Mara remembered it as simply damp, smelling of woodsmoke from the farmhouse where they'd slept. Beside her, Cass Okafor, broad-shouldered, twenty-six, laughing at something even then—Cass laughed at funerals, at artillery, at the particular joke of being Black in a war both sides claimed to be fighting for freedom. And wedged against the far door, Ruthie Marsh, small as a folded letter, who had not spoken above a whisper in weeks and who held in her lap a satchel that clinked when the road jolted.

Nobody in the histories ever mentioned the satchel. Mara had never once heard Edith mention the satchel.

"Driver," Cass had said, leaning forward, her gloved hand on the seatback, "you going to get us there or get us famous?"

"Famous is cheaper," Mara had said. "I'll get you there."

Edith hadn't laughed. She'd been rehearsing. Mara could see it in the movement of her lips in the wing mirror, silent, shaping sentences—the speech she still gave, in some form, forty years on. *I stood alone at the bridge.* Mara watched those silent lips work and felt the first faint unease, though they were twenty minutes from the bridge and the truth was still intact, still shared among four people and a bribed captain, and there was no reason yet to think it would curdle into anything else.

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn. The world smelled rinsed.

* * *

In the present, the kettle began its low climb toward whistling, and Mara took it off the heat before it could, out of habit—Edith liked quiet in the mornings, said noise before nine was theft from the day. The house was Edith's sister's house originally, left to Edith along with the money and the grievances, and it had the particular hush of a place where important things had happened, or were believed to have happened, which after enough years amounted to the same thing.

On the hall table lay the morning's post, and on top of it the envelope from the historian's office, and beneath the handwriting on that envelope Mara's own eye kept snagging, the way a thumb finds a splinter. She'd seen that hand before. She'd seen it three days ago, looping an answer onto a census form while asking where she'd been during the war.

She made tea she didn't drink and carried it to the window anyway, and the window looked east toward the river, and the river ran toward the coast, and somewhere along its course stood Harrow Bridge, rebuilt twice, plaqued once, and lied about daily.

* * *

They had reached the bridge at first light.

It was a narrow span, iron-bellied, thrown across the gorge where the river ran green and fast, and the regiment was expected by eight. Everyone knew it. The whole district knew it—that was the point. The Fourth Regiment, withdrawing from the failed eastern offensive, was to cross at Harrow and burn the mill stores on the far bank, and the mill stores fed eleven villages, and eleven villages held perhaps four thousand people who had already buried too much of the war to bury their children's winter too.

The plan, such as it was, had been Edith's in outline only: stand on the bridge, in the regiment's path, unarmed, and refuse to move. It was theater, and everyone understood it as theater, and everyone also understood that theater sometimes worked on tired men, and that sometimes it did not, and that the difference was usually measured in blood.

What the speeches never said—and what only four living people knew, three of whom had agreed, without ever saying so aloud, never to say it either—was that the theater had a stagehand.

Ruthie Marsh had gone out two nights before, alone, across the lines, carrying the satchel. Mara had driven her to within a mile of the regimental camp and waited in the dark with the engine off and the cold coming up through the floorboards, and had watched a small figure become smaller and then become nothing. Ruthie carried money—not relief-circuit money but money from a source Cass never named, gold sovereigns wrapped in flannel—and she carried a message written in a captain's cipher that Cass had copied from a dead man's coat at a field station where she volunteered as a laundress. The message offered Captain Ellery Vane something his government couldn't: a way to cross the bridge with his men alive, his stores unburned, his conscience furnished with a story he could live inside. In exchange, the regiment passed without a shot, and the mills stood.

Ruthie came back before dawn, soaked to the shoulder, one shoe lost in the marsh, and climbed into the car and said, in her whisper, "He counted it." That was all. Then she slept against the door like a child, and Mara drove, and Cass put her coat over the sleeping woman, and Edith rehearsed.

At the bridge, in the grey light, the four of them stood in the middle of the span—Edith front and center, naturally; she was brave, that should be granted, standing where the bayonets would arrive required a courage no bribe could substitute—but Cass stood at her right hand and Ruthie at her left, and Mara stood back by the car, close enough to hear everything, far enough to run if running became the job.

The regiment came at eight exactly. Mara heard them before seeing them: boots, a thousand pairs, arrhythmic on the iron deck. Then they rounded the bend in the road and filled it, grey coats, slung rifles, faces the age of Mara's own face then—young, exhausted, hungry in a way that had stopped registering as hunger.

Captain Vane rode at the head. He drew rein six yards from Edith, and the column halted behind him with a sound like a wave deciding not to break.

What followed took four minutes. Mara timed it afterward, obsessively, for years: four minutes. Edith spoke first—the speech, or the seed of it, her voice steady, telling him the mills fed children and that he would have to walk through her to reach them. Vane looked past her. He looked at Cass. He looked at Ruthie, and something passed between Ruthie and the captain that no one watching from a distance could have read—a nod, no more, the smallest transaction ever concluded on that bridge. Then he looked at Edith again and said, loudly, in the voice of a man making history rather than accepting delivery of it: "There will be no burning today."

The column crossed. A thousand boots went over the bridge around the four of them, and not one rifle was unslung, and the mills stood, and eleven villages ate that winter.

And Edith Voss stood in the middle of the span afterward, arms slightly raised, transfigured, and Cass took her hand and Ruthie took her other hand and for one photographless moment the three of them were the thing itself, the true thing, before any of them knew how badly the true thing would need hiding.

Mara leaned against the Ford and wept, silently, uselessly, twenty-three years old and certain the world had just seen a miracle.

The world had seen nothing. The world would be told a version. And the driver would drive it home.

* * *

"You're brooding."

Edith stood in the doorway of the morning room in her wool dressing gown, seventy-one years old and upright as a flagpole, watching Mara over the rims of her spectacles with the expression she used on correspondents who had misspelled her name. She was sharp this morning. Some mornings the fog of age took her gently and some mornings she cut through the house like weather, and today was weather.

"I'm drinking tea," Mara said.

"You're holding tea and brooding. I've known you eleven years, Mara. I can tell the difference between your silences the way conductors tell violins." She came into the room and lowered herself into her chair by the window with the controlled economy of a woman who refused, on principle, to be helped. Her eyes went to the post on the table, then to Mara. "That letter. From the historian's office. You haven't given it to me yet."

"It came this morning."

"You've had it since seven. It's half past nine." Edith held out her hand. "I don't employ a courier who sits on correspondence."

Mara fetched it. Watched Edith turn it over, read the return address—*Commission on the War Record, Historical Division*—and saw, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, the small pause in Edith's hands, the quarter-second where the envelope became heavier than paper.

"The Commission," Edith said lightly. "Twenty years late and they want my papers. Well. They can have my speeches. They're all printed." She set the letter down without opening it, which was unlike her; Edith opened letters the way duellists fired, immediately and at the heart.

"They may want more than speeches," Mara said. "It says records. Papers. Correspondence relating to the Harrow Bridge action."

"Harrow Bridge." Edith said the name the way she always did now, from the podium of herself, a place she owned outright. "I suppose anniversaries make historians ambitious. Twenty years—they'll want to hang a medal on me before I die and save themselves the trouble of casting it." She smiled, and the smile was genuine, which was the terrible part. She believed it. Mara watched her believe it, this woman she loved, watched the legend sit on her like skin, worn so long there was no longer any seam where it met the body.

"There were three of us on that bridge," Edith added, almost idly, gazing out the window at the river light. "Two companions. Good women. One has passed, I believe, and the other—" a small wave of the hand, a gesture that dismissed and mourned in a single motion, "—lost to us. People scatter, after wars. That's what wars are *for*, in a sense. Scattering."

Cass Okafor was alive. Mara had confirmed it eleven months ago, through a mill-district union newsletter and a name in a laundry workers' roll. But that was a different chapter, and Mara kept it shut.

"Yes," Mara said. "People scatter."

Edith looked at her then, a moment too long, the spectacles catching the light so her eyes went briefly opaque, two coins behind glass. Whatever moved behind them stayed there. "You've been odd since the census man came," she said. "Since he asked you his question too many."

"He didn't ask me anything improper."

"He asked where you were during the war. Mrs. Pellory told me—he asked her the same about her brother, and she nearly set the form on fire with indignation." Edith's mouth curved. "You went white as the porch rail. I was watching from the study."

Of course she had been watching. Edith Voss missed nothing that happened within sight of her legend; it was the things beyond its border she'd trained herself not to see.

"I went pale because the man had cold hands and wrote with a wet pen," Mara said. "It's nothing."

"Mm," said Edith, in the tone of a woman filing something away in a drawer of her own.

She rose, collected her spectacles case and the unopened letter, and paused at the door. "We're driving to Corleigh on Thursday. The unveiling. The committee wants me to say a few words at the memorial stone." Her voice was easy, but her knuckles were white on the doorframe, and Mara saw it, and saw that Mara was meant to see it, or was meant to think it wasn't there—one of the two, and after twenty years of reading Edith Voss, Mara could no longer tell which games were deliberate and which had simply grown in the dark.

"A few words," Mara said. "You'll give them the speech."

"They love the speech."

"The speech says you stood alone."

Edith's chin lifted a degree. For an instant—half a heartbeat, no more—the armor slipped, and underneath it was not guilt exactly, nor defiance, but something older and wearier, the look of a woman standing in a house whose foundations she had poured herself and could no longer inspect. "The speech says what happened," she said, "in the way that matters."

Then she was gone up the stairs, slow and straight, and the letter from the Commission rode in her hand like a summons she had decided, for the moment, to ignore.

* * *

That night Mara did not sleep. The house ticked and settled around them, and the river light came and went with the moon, and the memory played its full reel, all the parts, including the ones that hurt.

Including Ruthie counting out the sovereigns on the farmhouse table the night before, her whisper saying, "He'll take it. Men like him always take it. That's how you know the war's a lie—everybody in it can be bought, except the ones nobody bothers to buy."

Including Cass, laughing on the far bank after the column passed, laughing with tears running down her face, saying, "Four thousand suppers. Write *that* down, somebody."

Nobody had written it down. That was the whole of it, laid bare now under the census man's pencil: nobody had written it down, and the ones who might have were scattering or silenced or sleeping in a laundry row in the mill district with their names spelled wrong on the union rolls, and the official version stood on its plaque by the bridge the way Edith stood in photographs—alone, centered, lit.

One witness left. Mara turned the phrase over and found it fit worse than it used to. Ruthie lived, somewhere—Ruthie had to live, the soldiers' homes upriver were full of women the war had emptied out, and small quiet women who stopped speaking did not stop existing. Cass lived. Even the widow of Captain Ellery Vane might live, might hold letters, might hold the other end of the bribe in her keeping.

Not one witness, then. Perhaps three. Perhaps more, scattered like seeds across a country busy paving over them.

But seeds keep, Mara thought, watching the ceiling go grey toward morning. Seeds keep, if you get to them before the ground is sealed.

Down the hall, Edith's clock struck five. In eleven weeks, if the historian's letter was to be believed—and Mara intended to find out precisely what the Commission believed, and from whom, and how much it cost to ask—the ground would be sealed over all of it, permanently, in the victors' spelling.

Mara got up in the dark, dressed quietly, and took the Commission letterhead from the desk drawer where Edith's discarded drafts went to rest. There was an address on it. There was, Mara noted, copying it out in careful block capitals, a lodging-house address for the Commission's field inquiries office—and attached to it, in the corner, the name of the inquiry officer assigned to this district.

*A. Fen.*

The same hand that had looped *occupation? residence? where were you during the war years?* onto a census form was the hand that would decide what the record remembered.

Mara put on a coat. Some questions, they had learned on the road to Harrow Bridge, you could not wait to be answered honestly. Sometimes you had to drive to the question yourself.
