## 5. The Historian's Card

The lodging house on Corvane Street smelled of boiled cabbage and lamp oil, the way every cheap room in the city smelled after the war—when there were too many people and not enough of anything else. Mara climbed the stairs slowly, telling herself this was nothing. A courtesy call. A citizen's curiosity about a government form. People did it all the time.

Nobody did it at all, actually. In twenty years of censuses and registrations and permit renewals, no one had ever come looking for a census taker to ask him about his own questions. That was precisely why it would work, Mara told herself, and precisely why it wouldn't. Both things could be true. Most things could be true, if you held them carefully enough.

* * *

Mara had spent two days deciding how to dress for it. Too fine, and Fen would remember a lady had called. Too plain, and he'd remember someone trying not to be remembered. In the end: the grey coat, the plain hat, the gloves with the mended seam at the left wrist that Edith had offered twice to replace and Mara had refused twice, because a driver with perfect gloves was a driver with something to hide. Everything about Mara's life was calibrated like that now, had been for so long that the calibration was the self. She sometimes wondered what was underneath it, and then she stopped wondering, because wondering was a luxury of people whose names matched their faces.

Corvane Street ran behind the freight yards, where the census office kept its district annex—a brick building with a green door and a sign so sun-faded it read only CEN—S TA ER. Fen's lodgings were above a cobbler's shop three doors down. The landlady, a broad woman with flour on her apron, took Mara's coin without asking any questions, which was the most reassuring thing that had happened all week.

"Third floor," she said. "He keeps odd hours. Goes out with his little case before light sometimes."

"Does he?" said Mara, in the tone of a person making conversation.

"Goes to the river district, mostly. Down where they don't answer doors." The landlady shrugged. "Government work. I don't ask."

That was the trouble with this whole enterprise, Mara thought as she climbed. Nobody asked. That was how the country had healed itself—by agreeing, all at once and without discussion, to stop asking. Twenty years of not asking, stitched over the wound like a neat seam, and now a man with a leather case and a fountain pen was going door to door pulling at threads.

The door on the third floor had a card tacked to it. Not printed—a card, hand-lettered, in the same careful upright script Mara had seen on the envelope addressed to Edith Voss:

**ALDOUS FEN — ENUMERATOR & HISTORICAL RECORDS COMMISSION**

Historical Records Commission. Mara stood on the landing longer than was wise, long enough that somewhere below a door opened and closed and a child laughed at something. Enumerator was one thing. Enumerator was a clerk with a form. Historical Records Commission was something else entirely. It sounded like a place where paper went to become permanent.

She knocked.

* * *

Fen answered on the second knock, and up close he was smaller than he'd seemed in Edith's parlor—narrow through the shoulders, with the kind of face that would be thirty or fifty depending on the light. His rooms were spare almost to hostility: a bed made with military corners, a washstand, a table covered edge to edge in papers weighted down with teacups and a brass letter-opener. One window looked out on the freight yard. He'd pinned a map of the district to the wall beside it, marked in colored pencil.

"Miss Hartwell." No surprise in his voice at all, which was the first bad sign. "You found me. Most people don't bother."

"The landlady was helpful." Mara held up the paper bag she'd brought—rolls from the bakery on Tern Street, still warm, because you didn't call on a stranger empty-handed in this city unless you wanted him to remember you badly. "I hope it's not an intrusion. I had questions about the census, and the office sent me here, and—" She let the sentence die the way sentences died when they were only mostly true. It was a skill. She'd had practice.

"They sent you nowhere," Fen said mildly. "There is no forwarding service from the annex. But please, sit down. I have exactly two chairs and one of them is worse than standing."

He moved a stack of forms off the good chair himself. On top of the stack, face down, Mara caught a glimpse of a page headed **INCONSISTENCY REPORT — DISTRICT 9** before Fen laid another paper over it, unhurried, like a man covering bread dough while it rose.

"I'll be honest with you, Miss Hartwell," he said, settling into the bad chair himself. "I assumed you'd come eventually. You're the first one who has, actually. Out of forty-one households where the answers didn't sit right, you're the only one curious enough to knock on my door."

"Didn't sit right," Mara repeated. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that later, remembering it. "I'm not sure what a census answer has to do with sitting right."

"Nothing. Everything." Fen poured tea from a pot that had seen better decades and pushed a cup toward her. "A census is a strange instrument. You ask a man his trade and he tells you his pride. You ask a woman her age and she tells you her fear. You learn to read the gaps. What people don't say is usually the most accurate thing on the form." He smiled—not unkindly, which somehow made it worse. "Your household's form was very eloquent."

"I gave honest answers."

"You gave careful ones. There's a difference, and the difference is interesting." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "But you didn't come across town for rolls and reproach. Ask me what you came to ask."

Mara drank the tea. It was terrible, stewed and bitter, and she was grateful for the seconds it bought. Outside, a freight train shunted cars along the yard with a sound like slow thunder.

"All right," she said. "The card on your door. Historical Records Commission. The census takers I've known were census takers. They counted heads and moved on. What does history have to do with counting?"

"Ah." Something shifted in his face—not triumph, exactly. Relief, maybe. The relief of a man who has been carrying a speech around and finally finds someone willing to hear it. "That's the question, isn't it. Everyone thinks we're counting them. We're not. Or—we are, but the count isn't the point." He reached to the table and drew out a slim pamphlet, cream-colored, official-looking, and slid it across. The seal at the top was one Mara knew: the Commission's, stamped into every government building since the armistice, the crossed sword and quill. **THE NATIONAL RECORDS COMMISSION: A NOTICE TO CITIZENS.**

"Read the third paragraph," Fen said.

Mara read it. *In preparation for the twentieth anniversary of national reunification, the Commission has been authorized to compile the Authoritative History of the Civil Conflict, incorporating all surviving testimony, records, and documentation. Upon completion, the Archive of the Conflict will be formally sealed, and its contents constituted as the permanent and definitive record.*

"Sealed," Mara said.

"Eleven weeks from Thursday." Fen said it the way other men announced train departures. "The archive closes. Whatever is in it becomes the truth. Whatever isn't in it—" He spread his hands. "Was never true. That's not cynicism, Miss Hartwell. That's the statute. Section twelve. I've read it enough times to have opinions about it."

"And what goes in it?"

"What we collect. What survives collection." He leaned back. "Do you know what we're working from? Regimental muster books—half of them burned in the depot fire in the last year of the war, and half of the remainder rewritten afterward by officers tidying their own records. Hospital registers. Pension files. And testimony—such as it is. We send out field agents, I being one, to interview anyone who might hold a piece of it." He gestured at the map on the wall, the colored pencil marks clustering along the river districts. "Most of them don't want to talk. Can't blame them. The war taught this country that talking gets you killed, and twenty years of peace hasn't fully untaught it."

"And when the witnesses won't talk," Mara said slowly, "what fills the space?"

"Official record. Officers' accounts. Commemorative publications." Fen's mouth did something that was almost a smile and almost the opposite. "The victors wrote the first draft. The Commission writes the final one. And the final one is the only one that counts, legally speaking. Schools will teach from it. Monuments will cite it. Pensions will be granted or denied by it." He picked up his teacup. "It's remarkable, when you think about it. A whole country's memory, sealed shut like a tomb, and everyone agrees it's a monument."

Mara set down her cup so her hands would have something to do besides shake. Eleven weeks. The words sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She thought of Edith's study, the locked drawer she'd never been permitted to open, the boxes in the attic marked in Edith's handwriting: *war correspondence — personal.* She thought of women scattered upriver and down the coast, laundresses and widows and one woman who drove a car for a living under a name she'd taken off a dead cousin's baptismal certificate.

"Suppose," Mara said, and heard her own voice go careful again, careful the way it got near the wound, "suppose someone had testimony. Suppose they'd waited too long, and now the deadline—"

"Then they have eleven weeks," Fen said simply. "And it's harder than that. Testimony for the Authoritative History requires corroboration—two named witnesses, minimum, for any contested event. Named, mind. The Commission learned early that anonymous accounts breed disputes, so they ruled them out entirely." He shook his head. "Which means the people with the most reason to hide can't testify at all, and the people willing to put their names down are the ones whose names already appear somewhere. It's a sieve built to keep out the truth and let through whatever matches the pattern." He said it flatly, without heat, the way a man reports weather. Then he looked at her, and for a moment the mildness dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was sharp and attentive and not fooled by anything. "Is there such testimony, Miss Hartwell?"

The room went very quiet. The freight yard clanked. Somewhere below, the cobbler's hammer tapped its patient rhythm against a sole.

"Why would you think so?" Mara said.

"Because you crossed the city to ask me about a pamphlet." He folded his hands. "And because of the bridge."

The word landed in Mara's stomach like ice water.

"There's a commemorative volume in preparation," Fen went on, watching her. "Anniversary edition. The Voss account—the bridge, the regiment, the woman who stopped it alone—is the centerpiece. Every schoolchild will read it within five years. It's already in three textbooks." He tilted his head. "I enumerated a household last week where the daughter recited it like scripture. *Edith Voss stood alone.* Four words. Very efficient legend." He paused. "Legends are efficient, Miss Hartwell. Truths are heavy. Legends travel light. That's why they get everywhere first."

"You seem to have doubts," Mara managed.

"I have inconsistencies." He touched the stack of papers beside him, the one he'd covered, lightly, almost affectionately. "Forty-one households' worth. Little things. A pension file that says a hospital orderly was at Harrow Ford in a month the regiment wasn't. A woman listed as deceased who paid taxes in Millbrook until three years ago. A name that appears in one muster book and vanishes from every copy made after the armistice." His eyes stayed on Mara's face with the patience of a man who could wait all day. "Individually, nothing. Together, a shape. I write them down. That's my actual job, whatever the card says. I am a collector of shapes."

"And what do you do with the shapes?"

"So far? File them." He rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking down at the freight yard with his back to her. "My superiors prefer patterns that confirm the commemorative volumes. Shapes that contradict them get filed in a drawer and forgotten. The Commission is not in the business of disturbing monuments, Miss Hartwell. It's in the business of building them." He turned his head, just enough that she saw his profile against the grey window light. "Though drawers can be reopened. Before a seal goes on, anything can still go in. Afterward—" He let it hang.

Mara stood. Her legs felt distant, like borrowed legs. She thanked him for the tea in a voice she barely recognized as hers, and he walked her to the door, and just as she stepped onto the landing he said, quietly:

"The envelope I sent to Mrs. Voss. You recognized the hand. I noticed you noticing, that day in the parlor. You have a document examiner's eye, did you know that? Most drivers skim. You read." He smiled his almost-smile. "If you ever find yourself holding testimony that needs a shape explained—or a shape that needs a witness—my door is on the third floor, and I keep odd hours, and I answer to the archive, not to the men above it. There's a difference. It matters more than you'd think."

* * *

Mara did not remember crossing the city. That frightened her more than anything Fen had said—that twenty minutes of streets and tram bells could pass through her without leaving a mark, her body driving itself home on rails worn smooth by two decades of habit while her mind sat elsewhere, in a narrow room above a cobbler's shop, staring at a paragraph about sealing.

She parked the car in the mews behind the house and sat in it with her hands on the wheel.

Eleven weeks. Two named witnesses. Names, always names—the currency she had traded away at nineteen years old on a night she still dreamed about, trading her name for her life and calling it a fair price ever since. To testify she would have to buy it back. And the seller was the state, and the state was holding a desertion charge in one hand and a pen in the other, and Fen knew—he knew something, maybe everything; he'd said *the bridge* the way a surgeon says *there*, marking the spot—

And Edith. God, Edith. The anniversary was eleven weeks out too, the great speech, the crowds, the funding dinners, the schools named for the woman who stood alone. If the archive sealed with the legend inside it, Edith would be safe forever—monumental, untouchable, and alone in there, walled up alive in a story that had eaten the three women who'd actually done the thing.

If it didn't seal—if the record opened even a crack—everything Edith had built might come down with it. And the movement, the shelters, the vote petitions, the thousand small works funded by the legend's glow—all of it balanced on four words in a textbook.

Mara pressed her forehead against the cold of the steering wheel.

*The record is theirs,* Cass Okafor had said once, years ago, before she'd stopped saying anything at all. *Why hand them more of us?*

But it wasn't theirs. Not yet. For eleven more weeks it was nobody's, and a door stood open, and on the far side of it a narrow man with a collector's eye was filing shapes in a drawer and waiting to see which way the wind blew.

Mara straightened up. Started the engine. Drove around to the front of the house, where she could see the study window, and the lamp behind it, and the silhouette bent over papers at the desk.

She had gone to Corvane Street pretending curiosity. She drove home with none left to pretend. What she had instead was a date circled in her mind in red—eleven weeks—and a list assembling itself behind her eyes, unbidden, item by item: *Cass Okafor, mill district. Ruthie—Ruthie who? Ruthie where? The attic boxes. The drawer. The muster book with the missing name.*

Her name.

The house door opened before she'd even cut the engine, and Edith stood on the step with a letter in her hand, squinting against the evening, and called out, "Mara—come quickly, you'll want to see who's written," and the dread in Mara's chest sharpened, all at once and completely, into purpose.
