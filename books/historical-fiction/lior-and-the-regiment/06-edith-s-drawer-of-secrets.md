## 6. Edith's Drawer of Secrets

The letter came on a Thursday, which Edith Voss would remember afterward, because Thursdays were the days she allowed herself the good tea—the loose-leaf from the tin at the back of the pantry that she saved for occasions, though she could no longer have said what she was saving it for. She was in the study with her reading glasses low on her nose, working through the stack of correspondence Mara had left on her desk that morning, when she saw the handwriting and felt the room tilt slightly, the way a ship tilts.

She knew the hand before she knew the name. That was the terrible thing about handwriting—it arrived ahead of its meaning, like footsteps ahead of a guest. The letters were narrow and slanted, pressed hard into cheap paper, the ink gone brownish where it had been folded too long. A hand accustomed to writing against resistance. A hand that had learned its letters late and never quite forgiven them for it.

*Cass Okafor,* the signature read. *Mill District, Harrow Lane.*

Edith did not open it immediately. She sat very still in her chair with the letter flat on her knee, one hand resting on top of it as though it were a bird that might startle. Through the study window she could hear the gardener's shears ticking away at the hedge, patient and indifferent, and somewhere below, the sound of water running in the kitchen where Mrs. Pell was scalding jars. The ordinary sounds of an ordinary afternoon, twenty years deep into an extraordinary life—or so the newspapers said, so the pamphlets said, so the anniversary committee said, and Edith had repeated it all so many times now that the words had worn smooth as river stones and slid through her mind without catching on anything at all.

Except on Cass Okafor's handwriting. On that, everything caught.

She opened it at last with the bone-handled letter knife, because even now—especially now—she observed the forms. The paper inside smelled of soap-fat and lye, the smell of laundry work, and Edith closed her eyes briefly at the recognition of it, because she knew that smell from another lifetime, from a wash-house by the river where three women had once sat with their skirts hiked up and their feet in buckets, laughing about something Edith could no longer recall, only that they had laughed.

*Dear Madam Voss,* the letter began, and Edith flinched at the formality of it, the distance of it, *Madam* between them like a wall built of correct address.

*You will not know me, or you will say you do not. I was at the bridge. You will know what I mean or you will not, and if you do not, then burn this and forgive me the trouble.*

Edith's thumb whitened against the page.

*I have been twenty years a laundress and I am not ashamed of it, but I am fifty-three years old and my hands are going and I find I think more than I used to about what gets remembered. I read in the Gazette that there is to be a history written. An official one. They are asking for papers, testimonies, whatever people kept.*

*I kept nothing. That is the whole of it. There is nothing in my name anywhere—not spelled right, not spelled at all—and I have come to understand lately that this is a kind of dying. You can be alive and still be unwritten. I stood on that bridge same as anyone and now the story says I was never there.*

*I am not writing to make trouble for you. I want you to understand that first, because I know how it will look. I am writing because you are the only person who knows we were there and who has the standing to say it. One line in your speech. One sentence: that there were others. You would not even have to give our names, though I confess I would like mine given right. Okafor. People always put the f where the k should go.*

*I will not write again after this. I am not a beggar and I won't be made one twice over.*

*Respectfully,*
*Cass Okafor*
*Harrow Lane, Mill District*

Edith read it three times. On the third reading her hands had begun, very faintly, to shake—not with fear, she told herself, but with the cold; the study was always cold in the afternoons, the fire burned down to its red bones, the windows tall and leaking autumn through every seam.

*One line in your speech.*

How simple she made it sound. As though a sentence were a small thing, a coin dropped in a cup, costing nothing. As though Edith's speeches were not load-bearing structures, every word fitted against every other word, the whole edifice resting on what it did *not* say. Twenty years of schools funded and shelters opened and petitions gathered and women marched to the polls in the teeth of thrown stones—all of it standing on the story, and the story standing on the bridge, and the bridge—

The bridge standing on a lie.

No. Edith set the letter down and pressed her fingertips to her eyes until she saw gold sparks. Not a lie. She had learned to be exacting with herself about this, the way one learns to walk carefully on ice. Not a lie. A distillation. The historians themselves would have called it that—a necessary simplification, the individual sacrificed to the emblem, as all emblems require. When they carved figures into monuments they did not carve crowds. They carved one figure, arm raised, and let the crowd stand for it. That was not lying. That was how memory worked; that was how *nations* worked; that was how a movement survived its own scattered, unphotographed, misspelled dead.

But the voice in the letter did not sound like a crowd. It sounded like a woman named Cass who wanted her name spelled right.

* * *

She took the letter to the kitchen range herself, which was foolish, because Mrs. Pell was there, elbow-deep in jar-water, and Edith had to invent a reason—to burn an old draft of the anniversary speech, she said, a draft with errors in it—and Mrs. Pell nodded without interest, and Edith stood at the stove feeding the pages in one at a time, watching each corner catch and curl and go black at the edges like a leaf in frost.

The lye-smell rose off the burning paper and mixed with the smoke and for one vertiginous moment Edith was back at the wash-house by the river, steam on the windows, Cass Okafor's sleeves rolled to the elbow, her forearms strong as a stevedore's, saying something funny that Edith had lost forever, and Ruthie laughing with her hand over her mouth the way she always laughed, as though laughter were contraband—

Ruthie. When had she last thought of Ruthie? Years. Years and years. The thought arrived with a shame so physical it was almost nausea, a weight behind the breastbone, and Edith fed the last piece of the letter to the flames and watched it go.

*There is nothing in my name anywhere... I have come to understand lately that this is a kind of dying.*

"You could answer her," Edith said aloud to the empty kitchen, quietly, testing the sentence in her mouth the way one tests a rotten rung. "You could send money. You could send her a line. You could—" But the list collapsed under its own arithmetic. Money bought silence and silence was a debt that compounded. A line in a private letter was worse than nothing—it created evidence, corroboration, a thread that some Commission clerk could pull until the whole garment unraveled. And a public acknowledgment—one sentence, one name—was the door itself, the thing she had spent two decades keeping shut, because doors, once opened, admitted everything: the captain, the bribe, the ledger, the smaller part, the truth that Edith Voss had not stopped a regiment alone at all.

And the movement could not afford the truth. That was the phrase she used, the phrase she had polished until it shone, and it was even true, that was the devil of it—it was *true*, the way a well-made lie is true, built of true materials. The schools were real. The shelters were real. The women who had voted last spring for the first time were real, and they had walked to the polls along roads paved partly by the legend, by the anniversary dinners and the commemorative plates and the wealthy widows who wrote checks because they believed in the woman at the bridge. Take away the woman at the bridge and what stood? Committees. Arguments. A hundred worthy causes with no face, no bell, no story that a tired washerwoman could tell her daughter at night.

*Which truth can they afford?*

That was the sentence. That was the sentence she returned to, always, the way the tongue returns to a broken tooth. She had never said it aloud to anyone. It lived in her like a tenant who paid no rent.

Mrs. Pell glanced over. "More coal, ma'am?"

"No, thank you, Mrs. Pell." Her voice came out steady. Twenty years of practice would do that for a woman; she could speak steadily through nearly anything now. "I'm finished here."

* * *

That night, when the house had gone down to its creaks and settlements, Edith unlocked the bottom drawer of the writing desk in her bedroom—the drawer behind the drawer, the false front that opened on a shallow box no larger than a hymnal—and lifted out the things she kept there the way other women kept love letters.

A ribbon of black ash, folded in glassine. What remained of Cass's letter, because she could not bring herself to sweep it out with the stove ash, could not let it become anonymous among the day's burnt drafts. Some instinct older than prudence required that it be kept, that the destruction at least be witnessed, archived, mourned. She looked at it now under the lamp: fine grey flakes, a curl of paper edge on which one word survived unburned, legible in its little charred window—*bridge.* Even the fire had kept the record, in its way. Even the fire testified.

Beneath the ash, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the diary.

Her real diary. Not the commemorative volume the historical society had photographed last spring—that handsome fabrication, entries selected and softened, the bridge day rendered in language suitable for schoolchildren—but this: a cheap marbled notebook, spine cracked, water-stained from a wet October long ago, filled with the handwriting of a younger Edith Voss who had not yet known what her life would need to cost.

She did not open it. She almost never opened it anymore. She simply held it for a while, feeling its weight, the particular weight of a thing that knows it will outlast your explanations. She knew what was in it. She could recite the entry from memory, though she had not allowed herself to recite it in years:

*Cold today. Three of us went out and came back. The captain took the money and looked the other way and I keep thinking that this will be remembered as something finer than it was, and I don't know yet whether to mind.*

*I don't know yet whether to mind.*

The younger woman had not known. The older one had decided. That was the whole biography of Edith Voss in two sentences, and she set the diary back in the drawer with hands that were perfectly steady, perfectly steady, and locked it, and turned the key, and put the key where the key went.

Then she sat down at the vanity and looked at herself in the glass—a woman of sixty-one, silver at the temples now, famous, useful, alone in the particular way of people whose truest company is a locked drawer—and she practiced, silently, the opening of the anniversary speech. *Twenty years ago, I stood upon the bridge...*

It sounded beautiful. It always sounded beautiful. That was the trouble with it.

Below the window, at the gate, she heard the motor arrive—Mara bringing the motorcar around for the morning, early as ever, faithful as weather—and Edith thought, with a pang she neither examined nor permitted, of the driver who read her letters and edited them without being asked, who saw everything and wrote nothing down, who was the only other soul alive who knew the shape of the silence. Faithful as weather. And weather, she reminded herself, turning down the lamp, changes.

In the drawer behind the drawer, the ash of Cass Okafor's letter waited in its glassine sleeve, one burnt word facing upward, patient as a witness: *bridge.*
