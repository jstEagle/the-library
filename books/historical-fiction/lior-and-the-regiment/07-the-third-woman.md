## 7. The Third Woman

The mill district announced itself before Mara saw it — a smell of wet wool and lye and coal smoke that seeped through the motorcar's floorboards even with the windows shut, as though the place had soaked into the very air the way dye soaked into a laundress's knuckles. Mara parked the Voss roadster two streets from Okafor's Steam Laundry & Pressing, under a water tower streaked brown with rust, and sat for a while with both hands on the wheel.

It was Tuesday. Edith believed her driver was running an errand to the stationer's for cream paper and blue-black ink. There was cream paper in the basket on the seat beside her, bought that morning precisely so the lie would have a receipt attached to it. Mara had learned long ago that the best cover story was one with a purchase involved. Paperwork was how the world believed you.

The census taker's card lay in Mara's coat pocket, and had lain there for three days now, carried from lodging house to motorcar to kitchen table like a splinter working toward the heart. *Aldous Fen, Enumerator.* But enumerators did not follow up. Enumerators did not send letters on Commission stock requesting war papers of celebrated suffragists. Mara had turned the card over forty times looking for something more and found only the smudge of graphite where Fen had written, then half-erased, a word Mara could not make out. It might have been *bridge*. It might have been nothing.

But it had sent Mara here, to the mill district, to a name pulled out of memory like a thorn left in too long: Cass Okafor.

Twenty years ago the woman had been Cass Adeyemi then, a girl of maybe nineteen with forearms like rope and a laugh that carried across water. She had been one of the three. One of the women who stood where the legend said only Edith stood. And if the government was compiling its authoritative history — if Mr. Aldous Fen was writing things down and erasing other things, eleven weeks or ten weeks or however long remained — then the question was no longer whether Mara would speak. The question was whether Mara would be the only one left speaking.

Mara got out of the car.

* * *

The laundry occupied the ground floor of a brick building whose upper stories leaned toward each other across the alley like conspirators. Steam poured from vents along its foundation, so that the whole street seemed to be exhaling. Inside, through clouded windows, figures moved among the cauldrons and mangles — mostly women, sleeves rolled, hair bound up in scarves, their arms red to the elbow. The sound was tremendous: the slap of wet cloth, the hiss of presses, a girl singing somewhere off-key and unbothered by it.

Mara stood in the doorway a moment, letting eyes adjust to the steam-thick light, and looked for a woman who would now be past forty, who had been beautiful in the hard way of people who work with their bodies and have made peace with them.

She found her at the second cauldron, stirring sheets with a wooden paddle taller than she was. Older, yes. Heavier, her hands wrapped in strips of cloth against the heat. But there was no mistaking the set of her shoulders, or the way she lifted a whole sodden sheet out of the boiling water one-armed and snapped it flat like a whip.

"Cass," Mara said.

The paddle stopped mid-stroke.

Cass Okafor did not turn all the way around. She turned enough — a quarter, perhaps — and looked at Mara sideways, the way you look at a dog you're not sure is friendly. Her face did something complicated: a flicker of recognition, quickly buried under something flatter and harder.

"You've got the wrong woman," she said. "Name's not Cass."

"It's Okafor now. You married a stevedore named Emmanuel Okafor in the spring after the war. You kept the name when he went to the coastal yards." Mara kept her voice low, pitched under the noise of the room, though every word felt like setting a match to paper. "You were at Tarrow's Bridge on the fourteenth of October. You stood on the north side. I drove you there."

For a moment the laundry noise seemed to fall away entirely, the way sound does when your blood starts moving too fast. Then Cass thrust the paddle back into the cauldron and walked, without hurry, toward the back door. She did not look to see whether Mara followed. She knew Mara would follow. That, too, was a kind of recognition.

* * *

The yard behind the laundry was small and gray, hemmed in by walls, hung with lines of sheets that steamed faintly in the cold air. A cat — enormous, orange, missing an ear — regarded them from atop a barrel of washing soda. Cass took down a sheet from the nearest line, began folding it with quick, violent precision, and only then spoke.

"Who sent you?"

"No one."

"Nobody comes twenty miles to a laundry yard for nobody." She snapped a corner of the sheet straight. "Was it the Commission? The anniversary committee? One of Edith Voss's people wanting me to sign something?"

"I came on my own account." Mara moved closer, into the steam. "There's a man going door to door taking the census. Only he isn't taking the census. He's asking questions about the war years, and writing answers down, and some of what he writes goes to a Commission that's building an official history of the war. They mean to seal it. Eleven weeks, maybe less. After that, whatever they've written is the truth forever, and everything else is gossip."

Cass folded the sheet in half, then in half again, creasing each fold with the edge of her hand as if she were cutting something. "And you thought you'd come collect my piece of it."

"I thought—" Mara stopped. Started again, honestly this time. "I thought you'd want to be remembered right."

That got her a full look at last, and it was not a kind look. Cass's eyes were dark and dry and very steady.

"Remembered right," she repeated. "By whom?"

"By history. By the record."

"The record." Cass laughed once, without any humor in it at all, and hung the folded sheet over her arm. "Do you know what happened the last time somebody wrote me down?"

Mara didn't answer.

"After the war. When they were handing out commendations and pensions and pardons, depending on which side of the line you'd been standing on." Cass's voice stayed level, almost conversational, which somehow made it worse. "A clerk in a government office wrote down my name. Wrote down that I'd been present at Tarrow's Bridge during the incident with the Fourth Regiment. Wrote it into a register. Do you know what that register was for, driver? It wasn't for remembering. It was a list. A list gets used."

The cat shifted on the barrel. Somewhere beyond the wall, a factory whistle blew the quarter hour, and neither of them moved.

"They came in the spring," Cass went on. "Two men, polite as priests. My husband answered the door because I was nursing the baby and didn't hear it, and that's the only reason I'm standing here talking to you now, because Manny told them his wife wasn't home and had never been anywhere near any bridge, and men like that don't check behind a lie when the lie is convenient. But they wrote *his* name down instead. And eighteen months later he couldn't get work on the docks. Couldn't get work anywhere with a Union card. Nobody told him why. Nobody had to." She shook out another sheet from the basket at her feet and pegged it to the line, her movements precise, economical, furious in the way of a woman who has learned to put fury into labor because fury needs somewhere to go. "He died five years ago of the lung. Mill dust and dock rot. And every time I pass a government building, I think: somewhere in there is a page with our names on it, and that page is why my boy grew up poor."

"I'm sorry," Mara said, and meant it, and knew how thin it sounded.

"Sorry doesn't unpickle anything." Cass turned to face her fully. Up close, in the gray light, she looked tired in a way that sleep wouldn't touch. "So. Twenty years I keep my head down. Twenty years I wash other people's linens and I pay my rates and I vote when they let me and I don't vote when they find a reason I can't, and I raise my son to keep his mouth shut about where his mother was in October of the war year. And now a woman drives up in a fine motorcar — and it's a fine one, I know whose crest is on the door, everyone in this district knows Edith Voss's car — and tells me the record's about to close and wouldn't I like to be in it."

"Not in *their* record," Mara said. "In the true one. Testimony. Under protection. There are procedures—"

"There are always procedures." Cass stepped closer, and Mara caught the smell of lye and hot cotton and, beneath it, something floral and cheap — a soap she must buy for herself, one small luxury. "Procedures are how the list got made. Listen to me, whoever-you-are. The record is theirs. The paper is theirs, the ink is theirs, the building with the seals on the doors is theirs. Every time we've handed them the truth, they've weighed it and filed it and used whichever corner of it cut us deepest. Why would I hand them more of us?" Her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, just enough to show the seam under the iron. "Why would I give them my son's mother, spelled out plain, in a book they own?"

"Because if you don't," Mara said quietly, "the only version that survives will be the lie. Three women stood on that bridge. If the sealed archive says one, then your son grows up believing a pamphlet instead of knowing his mother. Isn't that worse?"

Something moved behind Cass's eyes — pain, or its cousin — and was shut away fast, like a drawer pushed closed.

"My son believes what I taught him to believe," she said. "That his mother worked hard and kept clean and asked nothing of anybody. That's not a shame. Don't you come into my yard and tell me my life is a shame because it isn't printed."

"That's not what I—"

"That's exactly what you're doing. All of you." She gestured with her chin at the motorcar visible through the gate, at the whole invisible architecture of money and reputation that had delivered Mara to this yard. "Edith Voss stands on stages and says *I* stopped the regiment. And maybe she needs to. Maybe the schools and the shelters and the vote grow out of that story like a plant out of manure — I'm not simple, I understand what the lie buys. But understand what it costs. It costs that it wasn't hers alone. It costs that there were three of us and the other two are supposed to be grateful to be forgotten." She picked up her basket. The interview, such as it was, was over; Mara could see it in the way Cass squared herself, the laundress reasserting itself over the witness. "Go home, driver. Tell your lady her secret's safe with me. It always has been."

"Cass—"

"*Mrs. Okafor.*" She was already at the door. Then she paused, one hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "You want to know the worst part? The part I think about at night?" Her voice dropped. "I remember everything. Every minute of that day. The fog coming off the water. The boots. Which one of us spoke first. I carry it all, whole, twenty years, and there is nowhere on this earth I'm allowed to put it down." She pushed the door open. "They taught us that being written down is how you get erased. You learn it young, or you don't survive."

The door closed behind her. The steam kept rising off the sheets. The orange cat watched Mara with one ear and an expression of profound feline indifference.

* * *

Mara did not leave at once. She stood in the yard a while longer, among the hanging linen, breathing the lye-smell, feeling the shape of what Cass had said settle onto her like the cold. Being written down is how you get erased. Mara had spent twenty years believing the opposite — had built a whole life on the doctrine that invisibility was safety, that the fewer pages bearing your name the freer you were. And here was a woman who had tried the same strategy from the other end, who had been visible once, on a single fatal page, and had paid for it with her husband's livelihood and her son's childhood and twenty years of silence, and who had arrived at the identical conclusion from the opposite direction: *disappear.*

Two roads, same destination. It occurred to Mara, standing in the steam, that this was the true genius of the victors' machinery — not cruelty, which anyone could resist, but design. They had built a world where truth-telling was punished and truth-hiding was punished, where every exit led through the same tollgate, until the survivors policed each other's silence out of pure love. Cass wasn't refusing out of fear for herself. She'd made her peace with fear decades ago. She was refusing out of love — for a dead husband, for a boy who believed his mother had simply worked hard all her life — and love was a lock no Commission key fit.

Mara thought of Edith in her study, rehearsing the bridge speech in front of the tall window, her voice taking on that bell-like resonance it found only for audiences. *I stood alone.* And Cass in her yard, holding twenty years of memory with nowhere to put it down. And somewhere — Mara's mind reached for the third shape and found, unsettlingly, that it had blurred — the third woman. Ruthie. Small Ruthie, who had cried easily and laughed easily and carried messages no grown man could have carried, because no soldier searches a slip of a girl selling chestnuts. Where was Ruthie now? Was she alive? Was she silent too?

The wind shifted and a sheet broke loose from the line and drifted down over the barrels like a surrender flag. Mara caught it, folded it badly — laundresses' folds were a trade, clearly — and draped it over the line again, and in doing so noticed what she hadn't noticed before: pinned to the inside of the yard door, half-hidden by coats, a scrap of printed matter. A handbill, cheap paper, the kind passed out at markets. Mara eased it free.

It was a commemorative program. The twentieth anniversary of the Peace of the Two Rivers. On its cover, a woodcut of a woman standing on a bridge, arm raised, cloak streaming — Edith, unmistakably Edith, rendered heroic and solitary. Beneath it, the printer's flourish: *THE WOMAN WHO STOPPED THE REGIMENT.* Someone had gone over the figure's face in pencil, darkening it, defacing it, or perhaps — Mara turned it to the light — perhaps trying to see it better.

On the reverse, in pencil again, in a hand Mara didn't recognize but could guess at, someone had written three names in a column. The first was struck through so hard the paper had torn. The second read *C. Okafor*, the letters pressed deep, angry. And the third—

The third was barely legible, written small and hurried, as if the writer had feared even the pencil betraying her: *Ruthie — soldiers' home, upriver, ask for the quiet one.*

Mara stood in the yard with the handbill trembling slightly in her hand, and understood that Cass had not refused her entirely. That somewhere between the spoken refusal and this pinned scrap, the laundress had decided — perhaps days ago, perhaps the moment she'd seen the Voss crest at her gate — that some part of her wanted to be found after all. That the silence had a crack in it, and the crack was shaped like Ruthie.

From inside the laundry, faint through the wall, came the sound of singing again — the off-key girl, still unbothered — and beneath it, lower, a second voice joining the chorus. Steady. Familiar. A woman singing while she worked, twenty years of memory held whole, and nowhere yet to put it down.

Mara pocketed the handbill next to Aldous Fen's card, where the two scraps of paper touched like opposite poles of the same magnet, and went back through the steam to the waiting car — because in two days she was due at the coast, at the wheel, with Edith beside her and a hundred miles of road in which two people who loved each other would circle the same buried event, each pretending not to know what the other knew.
