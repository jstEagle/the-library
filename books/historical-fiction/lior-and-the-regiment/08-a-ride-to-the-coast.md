## 8. A Ride to the Coast

**MARA**

The motorcar smelled of leather and rain that hadn't fallen yet. Mara ran a cloth along the dashboard out of habit more than need—she had already wiped it twice—and listened to Edith's heels on the gravel behind her, unhurried as a metronome. Two decades of mornings had taught Mara every sound of that house: which floorboard lied about Edith's weight, how long the kettle took when Edith was nervous (faster; she always forgot it), and now this new rhythm, the tap of shoes that meant an unveiling day, a speech day, a day when Edith Voss would be handed to the public like a coin fresh from the mint.

"You've checked the tires twice," Edith said, arriving at the passenger door with her hat pinned at the angle that meant *the legend is on duty*.

"Roads to the coast aren't roads. They're suggestions."

Edith laughed and let herself be helped in, though she needed no help, though neither of them mentioned that her hands had gone stiff in the cold months. That was the architecture of their life: a thousand small refusals to notice things, load-bearing walls of silence holding up something that looked, from the street, like a home.

Mara took the driver's seat and did not start the engine at once.

"The papers came?" Edith asked, too casually, looking straight ahead through the windscreen.

"Some."

"Anything from—" She stopped. Rerouted. "From anyone interesting?"

There it was again, the third time this week. The historian's letter sat in Mara's breast pocket like a stone warmed by her body. She could feel its corner pressing her ribs with every breath. *Dear Miss Voss, the Commission for the Authoritative History requests any papers pertaining to your service during the war years, particularly the events of the eleventh of October…* She had read it four times. She had not given it to Edith. That was the trade they'd made without ever speaking it aloud: Mara filtered the world before it reached her, and in exchange—

In exchange for what? Shelter. A name on a ration card. Twenty years of not being asked where she had been.

"The usual," Mara said. "Invitations. A man who wants money for a statue of someone else." She turned the key, and the engine coughed itself awake, and whatever else Edith might have said was swallowed by the road.

* * *

**EDITH**

The coast road unspooled like ribbon off a bolt, grey-green hills folding down toward a sea the color of hammered tin. Edith watched it go by and let herself enjoy the particular pleasure of being driven—the surrender of it, hands folded, destination handled by someone else. It occurred to her, not for the first time, that this was the only hour of her life in which she practiced what she preached. Everywhere else she steered.

"Mara," she said, over the engine noise, "do you remember the Harrowdale rally? Last spring?"

"I remember the mud."

"The mud is not the part I mean." Edith turned in her seat to study her driver's profile—that careful face, always half-turned away even when fully turned toward you. "A girl came up afterward. Seventeen, maybe. She said she'd walked eleven miles because of the bridge. Because of me." She heard how it sounded and pressed on anyway. "She said, *I didn't know one woman could do that.*"

"And what did you say?"

"That I didn't do it alone." Edith smoothed her skirt. "That there were others."

It was nearly true. There had been others. That was precisely the trouble.

Mara said nothing, but Edith felt the silence change shape, the way a room changes when a door opens somewhere in the house. She had built a career on sensing silences—in committee rooms, in newspaper offices, in her own kitchen—and this one had a draft coming through it.

"The Commission wrote to me," she said, deciding, abruptly, to spend some truth like a coin she couldn't spare. "Or rather, they will have written. I had word from a colleague that the historians are collecting. Papers. Testimony. Everything about the war, everything about—" she gestured vaguely at the hills, at twenty years, "—everything."

The car held its line around the curve. Mara's hands stayed easy on the wheel.

"Will you give them yours?" Mara asked.

"My what?"

"Your papers."

Edith looked out at the sea appearing and disappearing between headlands, silvering and dulling, silvering and dulling. "I don't keep papers," she said, and heard, as she said it, how much it sounded like a lie, and how much it sounded like a confession, and how those had come to be the same thing.

* * *

**MARA**

They stopped at noon in a village whose name Mara never learned, at an inn with a sign of a whale painted by someone who had clearly never seen one. Edith took tea inside. Mara stayed with the car, eating bread and cheese standing up, and allowed herself, finally, to take out the letter.

The envelope was cheap stock. The hand was small and upright, a clerk's hand, patient as stitching. *Mr. A. Fen, Office of Records.* She had known it the moment she'd seen it yesterday morning, and knowing it had done something to the week—bent it slightly, the way a frame bends when the house settles. The census taker. The man who had asked his one question too many and written down her deflection with that same patient hand.

*Where were you during the war years, madam?*

*I keep no papers.*

Two lies, hers and Edith's, rhyming across the same weeks. Mara folded the letter away and stood a moment looking north, inland, toward the city and the mill district where Cass Okafor fed cloth through rollers for twelve hours a day and refused, flatly and forever, to be remembered. *The record is theirs,* Cass had said. *Why hand them more of us?*

Because it's the only record there'll be, Mara had wanted to answer. Because in eleven weeks—or was it ten now?—the doors close and the ink dries and whoever holds the pen holds the past.

Edith came out with two apples wrapped in a napkin, having paid, Mara knew, too much for them. "For the road," she said, handing one over. "You drive better when you're chewing. You sulk slower."

"I don't sulk."

"You brood at forty miles an hour. It's very efficient sulking."

Mara laughed despite herself, and the laugh loosened something, and for a mile or two after they set off again it was simply the two of them and the road and the sea flashing white where the light struck it, and Mara thought—not for the first time, but with a new sharpness—that this was the thing she could not put in any testimony: that the person whose lie she might have to break was also the person who had taught her to drive, to read a crowd, to hold her spine straight in a room full of men. That love and loyalty were supposed to be the same animal, and lately they kept pulling in different directions like a team that had stopped agreeing on the road.

* * *

**EDITH**

The monument stood on the headland above the ferry landing: a limestone arch with the war's dates carved across the top and, beneath, in letters cut deep enough to survive weather and politics both, THE BRIDGE WAS HELD. Edith stood before it while a local alderman talked too long about sacrifice, and she performed the listening posture she had perfected—chin inclined, hands quiet—and let her mind move underneath the words like water under ice.

Three years ago, when the arch was proposed, she had insisted on the plural. *Women held the bridge*, she had wanted carved. The committee had balked—it sounded agitational, it sounded like the pamphlets—and she had accepted the singular pronoun in the end, told herself it was a battle chosen later, told herself so many things so fluently that the telling had become a kind of native language and the truth a tongue she was forgetting for want of use.

She caught sight of Mara at the edge of the small crowd, arms crossed against the wind, watching. Not the arch. Her. And Edith felt, in the pit of her stomach where conviction usually lived, the specific weight of being seen by the one person who knew.

Afterward, when the alderman had shaken every hand and the photographers had packed their tripod, Edith walked to the rail where the land gave up and became sea. Mara followed, as she always did, stopping a respectful three feet away—a distance measured and maintained for twenty years, close enough to catch her if she fell, far enough that no one would wonder why a lady's driver stood so near.

"It's a fine arch," Mara said.

"It's a lie in limestone."

Mara went still. The wind filled the pause with salt.

"That's not what I meant," Edith said quickly. "It's a simplification. Monuments are simplifications, that's their whole trade. No one carves *and then everyone went home exhausted*. They carve THE BRIDGE WAS HELD and let people make of it what they need." She gripped the rail. Below, the ferry came in, small and patient. "A girl walked eleven miles, Mara. What am I supposed to tell her? That history is made by committees and bribes and luck? That the eleventh of October was a shambles that happened to work?"

"You could tell her the truth," Mara said quietly. "It worked. That's the remarkable part. It worked even as a shambles."

Something in the phrasing snagged. *Even as a shambles.* Not *even though it was one*. As if Mara had been present at the shambling. As if she'd been—

But Mara had been everywhere, those years. Driving. Fetching. Standing in doorways. Of course she knew things. Knowing things was her profession, alongside the driving and the reading-aloud. Edith looked at her—at this woman she had sheltered and shaped and perhaps, if she were honest, kept—and felt the old tenderness and the old fear arrive together, twins, inseparable.

"What would you carve?" Edith asked. "On the stone. If it were yours."

Mara considered the sea for a long moment.

"*They held it together,*" she said. "Four words. Room for everybody."

* * *

**MARA**

They drove back in the long gold light, the sun ahead of them now, laying the road out in brass. Edith dozed against the window for twenty minutes—an honor Mara understood perfectly well, sleep requiring a trust that waking courtesy never quite confessed to—and woke as the hills closed in and the coast fell behind.

"There was a woman," Edith said, apropos of nothing, eyes still half-shut. "Years ago. She wrote to me."

Mara's hands tightened on the wheel by a degree. "Oh?"

"Asking to be remembered. Asking—" Edith's voice did something complicated and settled into flatness. "Asking for acknowledgment. For her part in things."

"What did you write back?"

The silence lasted a full minute. The wheels hummed. Somewhere behind them the sea kept doing what the sea does regardless of monuments or commissions or the hearts of aging women.

"I never answered," Edith said at last. "And I've regretted it in the way one regrets everything one tells oneself was necessary."

Mara stared at the road. In her mind, unbidden, the drawer in Edith's study flashed—she'd seen it open once, years ago, glimpsed a bundle of papers tied in ribbon, assumed correspondence, thought nothing of it. Thought nothing of it. That was the discipline of her whole life: thinking nothing of things until the thinking-nothing piled up into a wall between her and the daylight.

"She may write again," Mara said carefully. "People like that usually do."

"People like that," Edith repeated softly. "Yes." She straightened, and Mara watched the legend reassemble itself in the mirror—spine first, then chin, then the level public gaze—as the first roofs of the city appeared through the dusk haze. "Tomorrow there are letters to answer. The Commission among them, I expect. We should discuss what we send them."

*We*, Edith had said. As if there were a document between them already, unsigned, waiting.

"We will," Mara said, and drove them both home through the gathering dark, each carrying what she carried, the road narrowing behind them like a sentence closing.
