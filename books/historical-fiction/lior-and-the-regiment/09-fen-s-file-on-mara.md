## 9. Fen's File on Mara

The lodging house on Corben Street smelled of boiled mutton and lamp oil, and Aldous Fen had come to prefer it to his own rooms in the Commission quarter, where the corridors were polished and silent as tombs. Silence, he had learned, was not the same as order. Order was a thing you built yourself, page by page, and it could be built anywhere—even here, at a desk borrowed from a landlady who asked no questions because he tipped her not to.

He unlocked the document case with the small brass key he kept on a chain inside his waistcoat, against his heart, though he would have denied any sentimentality about it if pressed. The case held forty-one files. Forty-one lives, flattened and cross-indexed. He took out the thinnest of them—the one marked only with an accession number and a region code—and laid it flat beneath the lamp.

*HARTWELL, M.* The name had been written by a clerk's steady hand, but underneath it, in Fen's own smaller script, he had penciled the original designation: *see cross-ref.*

He turned to the cross-reference.

The paper was old enough now that its edges had gone the color of weak tea. A wartime registration form, filled out in a hand that slanted hard to the right, as if the writer were leaning away from their own words even then. Fen had studied that handwriting for hours—had, in fact, commissioned a comparison against the signature on Edith Voss's household employment register, obtained through channels that did not officially exist. The loops matched. The peculiar crossed *t*, the *a* that never quite closed. Twenty years, two names, one hand.

*Mara Hartwell,* the census said. But the registration form bore another name entirely, and beside it, in the column marked *STATUS AT DISCHARGE*, a single stamped word:

**DESERTER.**

Fen sat back and let the lamp hum. He had been a field agent for eleven years, and before that a junior archivist, and before that a boy in a government school being taught that the war had ended cleanly, that the record was a finished thing, a cathedral, and that his generation's sacred duty was maintenance—not renovation. He believed it still, mostly. Belief, like a filing system, required occasional acts of faith performed so routinely they stopped feeling like choices.

But this file troubled him, and he was honest enough with himself—at night, alone, when honesty cost nothing—to admit why.

It was not the desertion charge. Half the country carried some small falsification from those years; whole villages had agreed, collectively and without discussion, to misremember which sons had fled and which had died standing. The Commission tolerated a certain softness in the record the way a tailor tolerated seams: necessary, invisible, structural.

No. What troubled him was the census interview itself.

* * *

Three days ago, in the parlor of the Voss house, he had watched Mara Hartwell answer fourteen questions with the smooth fluency of long practice. Occupation: driver and secretary. Duration of employment: nineteen years. Place of birth: given correctly, down to the parish. It was the fifteenth question—the war-years question, standard since the last enumeration, the one designed to catch exactly the discrepancies the Commission existed to catch—that had changed the temperature of the room.

Fen had a talent for reading people, or believed he did, which amounted to nearly the same thing in his line of work. Most subjects, when they lied, overproduced. They elaborated. They gave him three details where one would do, and the surplus was always the tell.

Hartwell had done the opposite. *I was abroad,* they had said. Two words. No country named. No reason offered. And then—and this was the part Fen kept returning to, turning it over like a coin whose edge didn't match its face—they had looked at him. Directly. Not with the panic of a caught liar, but with something older and more settled. A look like a door closing on a room where a lamp had just been lit.

And then, unprompted, they had added: *You'll want to note my employer's full title. She prefers it read in full.* A deflection, executed flawlessly, offering him a larger and more interesting target than themselves.

It had been masterful. That was what bothered him. Incompetent lies were noise; they filed themselves. Competent lies were architecture, and architecture implied an architect, and architects had reasons.

So he had written it down anyway—*abroad, unverified, subject composed*—in the margin, in pencil, knowing that pencil was how the Commission said *we are watching* without saying it aloud. Pencil could be erased. That was its cruelty.

Now he drew the file toward him again and began, methodically, to build the report.

* * *

The Commission's forms had a poetry to them that outsiders never suspected. Section headings like *Observed Inconsistencies* and *Recommendations Re: Verification* read dry as bone, but Fen knew that every heading was a door, and behind each door stood a person, and behind each person stood a life that could be amended—his word, the Commission's word—for *clarity*. Clarity was the great verb of the age. The war had been muddy; peace would be legible. And legibility, applied consistently, looked very much like erasure to the people being made clear.

He wrote:

*Subject identified as Mara Hartwell, resident in household of E. Voss, occupation domestic/driving. Subject's responses during enumeration consistent excepting item 15 (war-years whereabouts). Cross-reference of subject's current signature against Registration 4471-C (attached) suggests prior identity under name [REDACTED PENDING VERIFICATION]. Recommend discreet verification prior to anniversary period. Note: subject occupies position of unusual access re: E. Voss correspondence and papers.*

He paused over that last sentence. *Unusual access.* It was true, and true in the way that mattered. A historian's office had already written to Voss requesting her war papers—Fen knew this because he knew everything that passed through the historian's office, having spent four months arranging to be useful to it. The request would be answered by whoever answered Voss's letters. By Hartwell.

Which meant Hartwell would see the request coming before Voss did. Would weigh it. Might shape the answer.

Or might destroy things first. People with secrets, given warning, behaved predictably: they burned, buried, fled, or fortified. Fen's job was simply to know which, and to have his documentation ready when they chose.

He blotted the page and set it in the drying rack, then pulled the registration form out again and read it straight through, as he did most nights, the way other men reread scripture or letters from the dead.

Name: struck through in the archive copy, but legible beneath the strike—a habit of the wartime clerks he'd found again and again. They were ordered to strike, and they struck, but they struck lightly, as if leaving a thread for someone to pull later. Fen liked to imagine those clerks. Quiet men and women at long tables, doing small insubordinations with ruling pens. Perhaps he was their descendant in spirit. Perhaps everyone who worked in records was.

Age at registration: twenty-two. Assigned unit: transport corps, third division. Notes in the margin of the form, in a different ink: *recommended for commendation, convoy work under fire, twice cited.*

Twice cited. And then, eight months later, the stamp.

That gap was the whole story, wasn't it? Somewhere between the second citation and the desertion stamp there was a human being who had driven wagons through shellfire well enough to be commended twice, and then had walked away from all of it. Fen had pulled the divisional records—informally, through a man who owed him—and found the discharge summary terse even by military standards. No date given for the alleged offense beyond a month. No pursuing party listed. No court-martial convened. The charge existed, and then the charge was left hanging, like a coat forgotten on a hook.

Charges left hanging were either clerical failures or deliberate instruments. Fen had learned to assume instrument.

Someone had wanted Hartwell gone from the army but not tried. Someone had wanted the threat alive but dormant. And someone—or perhaps the same someone—had then taken Hartwell in, given them a roof, a wage, a new name to hide behind, and nineteen years of proximity.

Edith Voss.

Fen turned to the thick file at the bottom of the case. VOSS, E. — CELEBRANT, ANNIVERSARY COMMISSION. Inside: clippings, speeches, the official biography in three editions with its discrepancies quietly harmonized between printings, and—his own addition—a timeline of the bridge event assembled from every public account Voss had ever given. He laid the timelines side by side. The story grew more detailed as the witnesses thinned. In the earliest telling, printed in a small weekly two months after the armistice, Voss had written: *I went with others to the bridge.* Others. Unnamed. Unnumbered.

By the tenth anniversary, the others had become a crowd. By the fifteenth, a crowd that faltered, leaving Voss alone at the rail. By the twentieth—this year's version, already typeset for the commemorative pamphlet—she had stood alone from the beginning, a single figure against a regiment, and the engraving showed it so: one woman, small and upright, and across the span of the bridge, a wall of bayonets turning aside.

It was a beautiful story. Fen could see why it survived. It had the shape of the stories nations need: one person, one act, one meaning. Complexity was for archives; simplicity was for monuments.

His job was the archive.

And yet—he tapped the registration form, that tea-colored page with its struck-through name—he could not shake the sense that he was holding not evidence of fraud but evidence of *cost*. Every document in the Hartwell file pointed the same direction: a person who had served honorably, been charged obscurely, hidden completely, and attached themselves for two decades to the woman whose legend their silence helped protect. That was not the profile of a grifter. Grifters traveled light. This was the profile of a hostage—or of a co-author.

Co-authors, Fen thought, were far more dangerous than frauds. A fraud could be exposed. A co-author knew where everything was written.

* * *

He worked until the landlady's clock struck eleven, assembling, cross-referencing, initialing. The report on Hartwell grew to six pages. He drafted a second document as well—a routine authorization request to pull the original commendation citations from regional storage, phrased to suggest administrative housekeeping rather than interest. If Hartwell had burned anything after receiving the historian's letter, the citations would be all that remained of the truthful version of their service. Paper was patient. It outlasted intention. That was the whole religion of his profession, and he was, whatever else, a believer.

At half past eleven he allowed himself the evening's single indulgence: he poured the last of the landlady's cooking sherry into a water glass and stood at the window looking down at Corben Street, where a lamplighter was making his slow way along, touching flame to wick, and the street came alight behind him pool by pool, like a sentence being read aloud.

He thought about the moment in the parlor when Hartwell had told him to note the employer's full title. *She prefers it read in full.* There had been something almost protective in it—not of Voss's vanity, but of something else. As if the title, spoken whole, were a kind of armor. As if Hartwell understood precisely what titles and names did to a person in the eyes of the state, better than anyone Fen had ever interviewed.

Of course they did, he reminded himself. They had shed one like a skin.

He rinsed the glass, dried it, and returned to the desk. One task remained, the task he both dreaded and relished, and he performed it as he performed all tasks: carefully, and in pencil.

On the report's final line, under *Recommended Action*, he considered the standard options. *Interview subject.* *Surveil correspondence.* *Refer to legal section.* His pencil hovered. Then, instead of choosing, he wrote a single line of his own, the kind of line that would raise eyebrows in the section office if anyone ever traced it to him:

*Subject may prove more valuable speaking than silenced. Assess before acting.*

He read it back. It was dangerously close to sympathy. He told himself it was strategy—a live informant inside the Voss household was worth ten seized diaries—but the sherry had loosened something in him, and he knew, in the private ledger he kept against himself, that it was also the observation of a man who had spent three days unable to stop thinking about a pair of eyes closing like a door.

He sanded the page, blotted it, and locked the file away. Then he took out a fresh sheet of the Commission's cream-laid stationery and began the letter that would go, tomorrow, by ordinary post, to the offices of the Historical Commission proper—requesting, in his capacity as a junior enumerations officer, copies of any correspondence recently sent to prominent celebrants regarding their war papers. A fishing letter. Harmless. Routine.

Except that he addressed it to a specific clerk, a nervous young man named Pell whom Fen had cultivated for a year, and the request contained a coded phrase—*expedite per standing circular*—that meant: *tell me exactly which letters went out, to whom, and when, and do not log this inquiry.*

By Thursday he would know whether Hartwell had yet seen the historian's letter. By Friday, whether anything had been burned. And by the following week—

He stopped. Set down the pen. Looked at the document case sitting shut and innocent on the corner of the desk, forty-one files sleeping inside it like passengers on a night train, each one bound somewhere they had not chosen to go.

There was one more thing, and he had been avoiding it all evening, the way he avoided all the items at the bottom of his lists. He took out the Hartwell file one final time and slid the registration form free of its protective sleeve. On the reverse, in the lower corner, the original clerk had noted the subject's next of kin.

The line was not blank.

It bore a name and an address upriver—a town Fen associated in his memory with nothing at all, which meant it was exactly the sort of place where people went to become unreachable. The name meant nothing to him yet. But next-of-kin lines were the roots of trees; follow them down and you found the water.

Fen copied the name onto a card, slipped the card into his breast pocket where it lay against the brass key, and blew out the lamp.

In the dark, the room resolved into gray shapes, and he sat for a moment longer listening to the rain begin against the window, thinking about doors and lamps and the particular quality of Mara Hartwell's composure. Composure like that was bought, and paid for over years, and Fen had never yet met a purchase he couldn't find the receipt for.

Tomorrow he would write to the town upriver.

Tonight, in pencil, at the very bottom of his personal memorandum book, he wrote the sentence he would build the next eleven weeks around:

*Find who they were, and the legend will introduce itself.*
