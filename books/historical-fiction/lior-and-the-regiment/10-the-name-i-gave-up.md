## 10. The Name I Gave Up

The kettle had gone cold on the stove again. Mara noticed it the way one notices an old scar—without surprise, without much feeling—and did not get up to warm it. The kitchen of the Voss house was quiet in the particular way it went quiet on Sunday evenings, when Edith retired early with her speeches and the servants had been given their half-day, and the only sound was the clock in the hall ticking off seconds that belonged to no one.

Mara sat with the census taker's card on the table in front of them, turning it over and over between two fingers. *Mr. A. Fen, Commission for the War Record.* Heavy stock. Official watermark held to the light. The kind of paper that outlasted people.

*Where were you during the war years?*

The question had followed them home from the market square like a stray dog. It sat at the table now. It would sit at the foot of their bed tonight. Twenty years of practice, twenty years of deflection—*I drove carts, sir, country work, nothing worth writing down*—and still Fen's pencil had moved across his little book, scratching down something Mara would never be permitted to read.

Because there was a thing written down already. Somewhere in some regimental office, in some provincial archive, in some clerk's careful copperplate, there was a page that said what Mara Hartwell had been called before there was a Mara Hartwell.

Mara closed their eyes, and the kitchen fell away, and it was the autumn of the last war year again, and their name was Maren.

* * *

Maren Hale had enlisted at seventeen, which was legal if you lied about your age by a year, and Maren had lied about everything else too—the cut of their hair, the bind of their chest, the low rasp they practiced into their voice in the privy behind their mother's house until it stopped hurting to hold. The 4th Provisional took anyone who could ride and read, and Maren could do both, so they became a dispatch rider, which meant they belonged to no company and every company, threading between the lines with orders sewn into their jacket lining.

It should have been a good war for hiding in. Wars are generous that way. Nobody looks closely at a courier caked in road mud, and the army asked fewer questions than any family ever had. For three years Maren Hale rode through a country burning at its edges and felt—for the first time in their life—anonymity as a kind of safety. Not happiness. Safety. There is a difference, and Maren learned it early.

Then came the village called Sennet Ford, and the order that ended the first life.

They remembered it afterward in pieces, the way broken things remember themselves. The order came sealed, carried up from division: the ford village was suspected of harboring irregulars. The garrison was to make an example. *Make an example* was a phrase that had a grammar all its own, and everyone who read it understood what it conjugated into. Maren delivered the order because that was the job, and stood in the yard while Captain Brevane read it, and watched the soldiers begin to move the villagers out of their houses into the square—the old men first, then the women, then a line of children blinking in the afternoon light like owls dragged from a dark place.

Maren had carried orders before. Orders were paper. Paper was not the same as watching a woman put her body between a soldier and her daughter and hearing the soldier laugh, not cruelly, just tiredly, the laugh of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

What Maren did next took four seconds and unmade twenty years. They walked to the wellhead where the horses were watered, took their own mare, and rode—not away, that would have come later—but straight into the middle of the square, shouting the first lie of their second life: that the order was countermanded, that irregulars had been taken at another crossing, that the village was to be released. They had a courier's voice and a courier's seals and a courier's terrible credibility, and it worked. It worked, and Brevane squinted at them, and for one long moment the whole square hung on whether a nineteen-year-old in a muddy coat could out-stubborn a captain with a signed order.

Then Brevane shrugged, and said, "Paperwork," in the tone men used for weather, and stood the troops down, and the village lived.

Maren rode out that night and kept riding. Because the countermand was a forgery, and forgeries are discovered, and discovered forgers are shot—or worse, undressed, examined, found out, named correctly for the first time in three years by men who would enjoy the finding-out.

The desertion charge came eleven days later, posted in garrison towns along the river: *MAREN HALE, DISPATCH RIDER, 4TH PROVISIONAL—ABSENT WITHOUT LEAVE IN THE PRESENCE OF THE ENEMY. FORGERY OF MILITARY CORRESPONDENCE. TO BE APPREHENDED.*

Two charges. One of them was even true.

* * *

The winter that followed was the winter of learning how large a country could feel when it was hunting you. Maren went north, then east, working under names that lasted a season each—a stable hand called Ren, a mill boy called Hal, once, absurdly, a choir soprano in a river town that had lost all its young men and didn't ask questions of anyone who could hold a note. The war ended in the spring. Everyone said it would get easier then. It got worse. Peace brought the commissions and the record offices and the pension boards, and peace wanted accounts settled, and a deserter-forger was exactly the kind of loose thread the new government liked to pull tight in public.

Maren might have run forever. Running is a trade like any other, and some grow skilled at it. But that second summer Maren caught the fever—the marsh fever, the slow kind that burns you down to a wick over six weeks—and collapsed in a ditch outside Harrowgate, which was where Edith Voss found them.

Not found. That was never the word Edith used. *I didn't find you,* she said later, in the greenhouse, among the orange trees she grew against all climate sense. *You were delivered. Everything important is.*

She was forty-one that year, already famous, already a name on placards, traveling the county on the lecture circuit with a hired carriage and a cause like a lit torch. Her driver had quit over wages. She saw a shape in the drainage ditch, and—this was the part Maren could never make ordinary, however many times the memory played—she stopped. She got down herself, skirts and all, in the mud, and put her hand on a fevered forehead, and said, "Well. You're a long way from anywhere," as though addressing a stray dog she intended to keep.

Edith nursed them in the back room of a printer's shop she owned through a cousin, three weeks of fever and rationed quinine and a woman reading aloud to them from pamphlets about the franchise while sweat soaked through the straw tick. On the fourth day of lucidity, Edith sat on the stool beside the cot and said, without preamble:

"You flinched when I said the Commission was offering amnesty for deserters who surrendered. So you're either a deserter or you're lying about something else entirely. I don't need to know which. Here's what I need you to know: the movement needs a driver who can read and write and keep his mouth shut, and I can give you a name that has no charges hanging on it. In exchange—" and here she had smiled, that famous smile, warm as a stove and about as easy to stand close to for long—"you'll give me your silence about whatever it is. Not confession. Silence. Those are different things, and I only deal in the second."

The name she offered was Mara Hartwell. Hartwell was her mother's maiden name, dead thirty years, unattached to anything living. *Mara,* she said, *because you look like a Mara. You have a Mara's face. You'll grow into it.*

And here was the thing Maren Hale had not anticipated, lying on that cot with the fever finally broken: how easy it was. How the new name slid on like a coat cut to measure. How the silence she demanded cost almost nothing, because silence was the one currency Maren had been rich in since Sennet Ford. They took the name. They took the seat beside her. They took, though neither of them said it in those words, the bargain.

For twenty years the bargain had held, and mostly it had felt like rescue.

Mostly.

* * *

Mara opened their eyes. The kitchen was darker now; the clock had ticked itself into full evening. The kettle still sat cold. Their fingers had stopped moving on Fen's card; it lay face-up on the wood, patient as a trap.

Here was the arithmetic Mara had avoided doing for two decades, and which the census taker's pencil had forced upon them anyway:

Edith had saved their life. This was true, and would always be true, and Mara loved her for it in the bone-deep, unsentimental way one loves air.

Edith had also, in the same act, closed a door and pocketed the key. Because a person with a forged countermand and a desertion charge is not a free person who owes a debt. A person with a forged countermand is a person who can never leave, never testify, never be *found*, never answer a plain question on a plain form without the whole architecture of their second life folding shut like a fan. Edith had known this. Edith, who built a career on the principle that a silenced woman is a governed woman, had purchased herself a driver whose entire existence depended on remaining silent—and had done it kindly, generously, with quinine and a warm cot and a mother's name bestowed like a gift.

That was the part that made the throat close, even now. That it hadn't been cruelty. Cruelty would have been simpler. It had been kindness with a hitch in it, protection with a leash braided so finely into the collar that it took twenty years to feel the weight.

*I gave you a name,* Edith would say, if Mara ever worked up the courage to say any of this aloud, and she would be right, and she would be wrong, both at once, the way most true things are.

Because the name had saved them. And the name was also the cell. And somewhere in the Commission's files—Fen had all but said so, standing in the market square with his polite eyes and his heavy-stock cards—there was a page with *Maren Hale* written on it, and a photograph maybe, or at least a description, *small build, reads and rides, voice low,* and a charge that had never been dismissed because peace, unlike Edith, does not forgive; it only archives.

Mara stood, finally, and lit the stove, and set the kettle on, because the hands needed work or they would shake. Through the window, the lane ran dark toward town, and past town, invisible but perfectly located, the Commission offices sat with their lamps burning late, filing the world into columns.

Eleven weeks, Fen had said at the lodging house. Eleven weeks until the archive sealed. He hadn't said it to Mara, not exactly—he'd said it to the room, to the tea, to no one—but Mara had ears trained by twenty years of listening for the sentence under the sentence.

If the archive sealed with Maren Hale inside it, sealed with whatever else it held—the bridge, the captain, the truth of that day that Mara alone still carried complete—then the erasure wouldn't be the victors' doing anymore. It would be everyone's doing. It would be Mara's doing. Twenty years of silence, compounded weekly, coming due.

The kettle began its low climb toward boiling. Mara took down two cups out of habit, then put one back. Edith was asleep upstairs behind her door with her anniversary speech, believing—I believe it *mostly*, she'd say, laughing, and Mara had laughed too, every time, for twenty years—

Mostly.

Mara poured one cup and drank it standing, looking at the dark window where their own reflection hung faint and doubled, Mara Hartwell overlaid on someone older, someone nineteen, someone with a name like a stone dropped down a well that had never finished falling.

Tomorrow, Mara thought, I will drive her to the unveiling, and I will make small talk, and I will watch her lie gently to the wind off the coast, and I will say nothing.

But tonight—tonight, Mara took Fen's card from the table and did not throw it in the stove. Tonight they slipped it instead into the lining of their jacket, where orders used to go, where the forged countermand had once ridden against the heart.

Some documents you carry because you must obey them. Some you carry because you intend to answer them.

The kettle clicked off. Outside, far off toward town, a train sounded its whistle at the crossing—one long note, held, like a voice remembering it had a name.
