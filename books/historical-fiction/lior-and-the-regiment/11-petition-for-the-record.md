## 11. Petition for the Record

The Commission offices occupied the old customs house on Meridian Street, a building that had once taxed every crate that came up the river and now taxed something less tangible: memory. Mara had driven past it a thousand times without looking twice—a gray granite façade, columns too grand for their purpose, windows so clean they seemed to apologize for the soot of the city around them. Edith had spoken there once, years ago, from the steps, when the Commission was new and still pretended to be neutral. Mara had waited by the car with the engine running, as she always did.

Now she climbed those same steps alone, in her good coat, the one with the mended cuff turned discreetly inward, carrying a folder that weighed almost nothing and everything.

She had rehearsed this on the drive over—her own hands steady on the wheel, her voice running through the words while the streets slid past. *I wish to submit testimony regarding events at Calloway Bridge, autumn of the war's final year.* Simple. Factual. The kind of sentence that could not be argued with, only received. She had said it aloud twice at a red light, startling herself with how ordinary it sounded, how small a thing the truth could be when you finally set it down on paper.

The doors were heavy. They always are, in buildings built to outlast people.

* * *

Inside, the customs house had been divided into the geography of bureaucracy: a hall for records, a hall for claims, a stair to the offices where, presumably, the actual rewriting happened behind doors that closed quietly. Mara followed the brass letters on the wall—*SUBMISSIONS*—down a corridor that smelled of floor wax and cold tea, into a room lined with clerks' desks like pews in a church where nobody sang.

A queue of perhaps eight people waited ahead of her. Mara studied them the way she studied everyone now, twenty years of habit making an archivist of her eye. An old man clutching a discharge paper soft as cloth from handling. A young widow with a child asleep against her shoulder, holding a marriage certificate in a frame's worth of protective cardboard. A woman about Mara's own age, dressed well, who kept looking at the door as if she might still change her mind.

Everyone in the line was trying to put themselves back into the record. That was the thing none of them said aloud. The war had ended, and the country had begun sorting its dead and living into categories, and these were the ones who didn't fit—who had been misfiled, or left out, or written down wrong—and they had come here, cap in hand, to ask the victors' clerks to please, kindly, remember them correctly.

Mara took her place at the end of the line and felt the folder press against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

* * *

The clerk at the window was a man of perhaps fifty with ink-stained cuffs and the patient, defeated air of someone who spent his days explaining rules he hadn't made. When Mara reached him at last—forty minutes of shuffling forward, of watching the widow leave without having been helped—he looked up with eyes that had already catalogued her coat, her accent, her category, whatever it was.

"Name."

"Mara Hartwell." It came out level. Twenty years of practice.

"Purpose of visit."

"I'd like to submit testimony. For the historical record. Concerning the events at Calloway Bridge during the final campaign of the war."

Something moved behind the clerk's eyes—not surprise exactly, but a small recalculation, the way a man shifts weight when a customer turns out to be more trouble than the queue suggested. He drew a printed form from a drawer, laid it on the counter, and turned it to face her.

"Testimony submissions require Form 44-B. Witness particulars, event particulars, supporting documentation if held." He tapped the paper with a blunt finger. "And corroboration."

"Corroboration?"

"The Commission doesn't accept unsupported accounts." He said it in the tone of a weather report. "Personal testimony is subject to error, exaggeration, self-interest. Every account must be corroborated by no fewer than two additional witnesses, named on the form, who will themselves be interviewed and must independently confirm the substance of your statement. Names, current addresses, willingness to appear before a hearing officer." He slid a pen across the counter. "Sign here to acknowledge the requirements."

Mara did not pick up the pen.

"I was there," she said. "I saw what happened. Isn't being present the whole point of testimony?"

"It would be," the clerk said, "if the Commission trusted anyone. It doesn't." There was, beneath the flatness, the faintest weariness—the sound of a man who had delivered this speech to the old man with the soft discharge paper, to the widow, to everyone, and knew precisely what it did to faces like hers. "Two named witnesses, madam. Who can corroborate that you were present at Calloway Bridge, and that your account of the events is accurate."

"And if they're dead?" Mara asked. "If they've scattered? If—" She stopped herself, because the next word was going to be *if they're hiding*, and hiding was her own trade, and she would not hand this man even the shape of it.

"Then the record stands as filed," the clerk said simply.

* * *

She carried the form to one of the side tables meant for filling things out and sat with it under the green-shaded lamp, reading every line the way she read Edith's dangerous letters—looking for what was between them.

*Witness One:* blank. *Address:* blank. *Relationship to submitter:* blank.

*Witness Two:* blank. *Address:* blank.

Below, in smaller type, the clause that did the real work: *The Commission reserves the right to reject testimony deemed insufficiently corroborated, inconsistent with established record, or contrary to the public interest.*

Contrary to the public interest. Mara read that phrase four times. It was beautifully made, that phrase—elastic, civil, deniable. It could stretch to cover anything the Commission decided it didn't want to hear, and it would never have to explain itself, because who could be against the public interest? Not the old man with the soft discharge paper. Not the sleeping child. Certainly not a driver in a mended coat.

That was the architecture of it, then. That was the wall. Anyone could walk in off the street and file a lie—that required nothing but paper and nerve—but the truth had to arrive escorted. The truth needed references. The truth needed two living, findable, willing people who would stand in an official room and say *yes, she was there, yes, it happened as she says,* knowing what standing in an official room could cost a person. Knowing what lists were kept of the people who appeared.

The lie traveled light. The truth came with baggage checks.

Mara thought of Cass Okafor's face in the mill district doorway, the steam rising off her shoulders, and the words she'd thrown like scalded water: *The record is theirs. Why hand them more of us?*

Cass would never sign. Cass had already told her, plainly, what signing meant.

And the other name—the third woman, the one whose absence had been a stone in Mara's chest for twenty years—Mara didn't even know if she was alive. Ruthie. Quiet Ruthie, who had gone upriver after the war the way water goes downhill, without decision, without trace. No address. No last sighting Mara could swear to. Perhaps a soldiers' home somewhere, if the war had left her mind whole enough for one; perhaps a grave under a misspelled name, which was its own kind of erasure.

Two witnesses. Named. Findable. Willing.

Mara looked down at the form and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, like cold water finding a crack, that the Commission had designed this requirement not to verify the past but to ration it. Witnesses died. Witnesses scattered. Witnesses hid, reasonably, from rooms like this one. Give it ten more years—give it five—and there would be no one left alive to corroborate anything except the official version, which needed no witnesses because it had typesetters.

They weren't sealing the archive against lies. Lies got in free. They were sealing it against the slow, inconvenient survival of memory.

* * *

She filled in what she could. Her own name—*Hartwell, Mara*—and paused over the line marked *name at time of event,* the little box that had been waiting for her all along, patient as a trap. She left it blank. Not today. Not yet. But her pen hovered over it a moment longer than a blank box deserved, and she noticed herself noticing, and filed the hesitation away the way Fen had filed his observations—in some ledger of her own she was afraid to open.

Under *event particulars* she wrote: *Calloway Bridge. Final autumn of the war. The regiment's crossing was stayed without bloodshed by the intervention of civilians.* Three words she chose with care—*civilians,* plural, *intervention*—each one a small act of smuggling, true enough to pass, shaped to keep the door open for what came after.

Then Witness One and Witness Two, and the pen went dry in her hand, figuratively speaking, though she sat there long enough that the clerk at the window called *next* twice and the woman who'd been watching the door finally left without submitting anything at all.

In the end Mara signed the acknowledgment—she would take the form home, unfinished; a petition half-made was at least a key cut from a lock—and carried it back to the window. The clerk stamped it received, date-stamped it, gave her a carbon copy pink as a wound.

"You'll want to secure your witnesses promptly," he said, and for just a moment the bureaucratic flatness cracked and something human showed through—pity, maybe, or the ghost of his own lost causes. "The hearing schedule closes at the end of the quarter. After that, nothing further enters the record. Nothing." He met her eyes briefly. "People assume there'll always be another chance. There isn't, this year."

"How long?"

"Eleven weeks. Then the archive seals."

Eleven weeks. Eleven weeks to find a woman who refused to be found, convince her to do the thing she'd sworn she'd never do, and locate another woman who might be anywhere between a soldiers' home upriver and a grave with the wrong name carved on it—or nowhere at all, which was the possibility Mara kept folded smallest and deepest.

"Thank you," she said, and meant it more bitterly than any thanks she'd ever given.

* * *

Outside, the afternoon had gone gold and cold, and Meridian Street was full of people going about lives that did not require them to prove they had existed. Mara stood on the steps of the customs house with the pink carbon copy in her hand and let herself feel it fully, for one unguarded minute, before the driver's composure closed back over her like water over a dropped stone.

Twenty years she had practiced invisibility, and she had been good at it—good enough that the census taker's question had been the first crack. But invisibility, she saw now, had never actually protected her. It had only deferred the bill. The record had her absent, and absence accrued interest, and today the Commission had quoted her the terms: two names, eleven weeks, or silence forever.

Edith's legend would survive the sealing effortlessly. Legends don't need witnesses; they *are* their own witnesses, self-corroborating, retold until the telling becomes the fact. It was only the inconvenient truths that required paperwork.

Mara descended the steps and walked toward the garage where the car waited, and her mind was already working the problem the way it worked routes—obstacles, alternatives, the fastest line through. Cass first. Cass again, despite everything, because refusal wasn't the same as impossibility, and because Mara had watched her long enough at the mill gate to see that the refusal cost Cass something too, some tax paid daily, some flinch when her son asked questions about heroes.

And then the other name. The third woman. Ruthie, quiet as snowfall, last seen going upriver.

Mara unlocked the car, sat behind the wheel, and did not start the engine. On the seat beside her, the pink carbon caught the light through the windshield, absurdly cheerful.

Two named witnesses. She had one name that would never sign, and one name that was nowhere.

Somewhere upriver, if the river had been kind, a woman who hadn't spoken in years was keeping somebody's routine like armor—and Mara was going to have to go and knock on it.
