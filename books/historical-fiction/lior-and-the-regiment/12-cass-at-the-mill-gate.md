## 12. Cass at the Mill Gate

The whistle went at six, and Cass Okafor came out of the mill the way she came out every evening: shoulders first, as if pushing through a door that did not want to open. The steam from the wash-house clung to her sleeves. Her hands were red to the wrist, cracked along the old split on her left thumb that never quite healed in winter, and she flexed them once, twice, out of habit rather than hope, before tucking them into her apron pockets.

She saw the driver's motorcar before she saw the driver.

It stood across the lane from the mill gate, parked with its nose toward the canal, too clean for this district, too clean by half. Paint the color of wet slate. Brass lamps polished until they threw back the gaslight in little coins. Nobody in the mill district owned a car like that. Nobody in the mill district would dare park one here overnight; by morning it would be on blocks.

And leaning against its fender, hat in hand—not wearing it, holding it, which was somehow worse, more patient, more deliberate—stood the person who had been waiting three evenings running.

Cass had counted. She told herself she hadn't, but she had. Tuesday: a figure by the gate, gone when she looked again directly. Wednesday: closer, near the pawnshop window, pretending to read the cards of wedding rings and concertinas. Tonight the figure had stopped pretending altogether and simply stood where any person leaving the mill would have to pass, the way you'd stand at a door you meant to knock on eventually but hadn't found the nerve.

Mara Hartwell. Edith Voss's driver. The letter-reader. Cass knew the name the way everyone in the movement's old circles knew names—half-remembered, half-distrusted, filed away in the drawer where she kept everything from those years, the drawer she had nailed shut in herself twenty years ago and told no one about, not even Tomas.

She could walk past. That was always the choice, and every evening she chose it. Past the car, past the fender, past the held hat, down the lane toward the tram stop, her boots finding the cobbles by feel because she refused to look down and give the waiting any satisfaction. And every evening the figure said nothing, asked nothing, only turned slightly, the way a compass needle turns, tracking her without pursuing.

Tonight, though, something was different. Tonight there was a smell of rain coming off the canal, and the gaslamps had just stuttered alight, and the shift-whistle's echo was still dying in the brickwork, and Cass was tired in a way that had nothing to do with twelve hours of other people's boiling linen. She was tired of being watched by someone who wouldn't speak. Silence, she could bear. Polite, patient silence was another thing entirely. It reminded her of forms. It reminded her of clerks who wrote your name down wrong and then made the wrong name true by repeating it.

So instead of turning down the lane, she crossed it.

"You'll ruin that coat, standing out here," Cass said. "Canal damp gets into wool and stays."

Mara straightened. Up close, in the lamplight, they looked older than their years and younger than their eyes—there was a particular agelessness to people who had spent two decades being careful, Cass thought. Carefulness preserved nothing; it only wore you smooth.

"I've been meaning to introduce myself properly," Mara said. "I should have done it days ago instead of lurking. I'm sorry for that. Mara Hartwell. I drive for Edith Voss."

"I know who you are." Cass pulled off her cap and shook out her hair, which came down gray-streaked and flattened on one side from the cap. Let them see the gray. Let them see what twenty years of mill steam did to a woman who used to be photographed for pamphlets. "Everyone who was anyone knows who drives Edith Voss. What I don't know is why you're spending your evenings outside a laundry gate in the Fenwick district when you could be home by a fire."

"Because I've been trying to find the courage to say why, and I keep failing."

That honesty caught Cass off guard. She had prepared defenses against flattery, against appeals to sisterhood, against the soft-voiced rhetoric the movement people used when they wanted something. She had not prepared for plainness.

"There's a bench," Cass said finally, nodding toward the canal path. "If we must talk, we'll sit while we do it. My feet have opinions and they win."

* * *

The bench was iron and green paint gone to rust at the armrests, set where the canal path met the footbridge over to the mill workers' terraces. Rats worked the reeds below; a barge lay moored and dark, its cargo of coal dusted over everything within thirty yards including, apparently, the air itself. Cass sat with her back straight and her hands folded on her apron, a posture she had learned in a different life, standing in front of different rooms, saying different things.

"So," she said. "You know who I am. You've known since before you started haunting my workplace. Say it plainly and let's be finished."

"Cass Okafor," Mara said. "Formerly of the Women's Emergency Transport Corps. Formerly—" They hesitated, and Cass watched them choose a word with visible care, laying each one down like a plank over deep water. "Formerly at the bridge. The Merrow Street bridge. The eleventh of Frostmonth, twenty years ago."

Cass had imagined this moment, insofar as she allowed herself to imagine anything from those years, and in her imagining she had always been calm. She had rehearsed the sentence: *I don't talk about the war.* Four words, delivered flat, ending conversations the way a shutter ends daylight. But hearing the date spoken aloud—the actual date, the day itself dragged up into the lamplight and named—did something the rehearsal had not accounted for. Her hands, folded so neatly, began to tremble, and she pressed them harder together as if she could arrest the shaking by force of will, and could not, and hated that Mara could see it.

"Don't," she said.

"I haven't come to make you testify. I haven't come with papers or petitions or anything official. I came because I didn't know how else to begin, and I thought if I waited long enough you might decide I wasn't going away, and then we could talk like people instead of like—"

"Like what? Like a woman and her haunting?" Cass laughed, short and hard, and the laugh surprised her with its bitterness. She had not heard her own voice take that tone in years. At the mill she was known for evenness. *Okafor never loses her temper,* the forewoman said, approvingly, the way you'd praise a boiler for holding pressure. "Twenty years, Mr.—Ms.—Hartwell. Twenty years, and now a motorcar outside my gate. Why now?"

"Because the government is writing the war's history right now. A Commission. They're compiling an authoritative account, and the archive seals in eleven weeks, and after that whatever they write becomes the truth forever. Everything else becomes rumor. Or nothing."

"And you want me to hand them my piece of it." Cass turned to look at the canal, because looking at Mara was suddenly unbearable. On the black water the gaslamps laid down long trembling columns of light, and a moorhen cut through them, leaving a wake that healed itself behind it as though nothing had passed. "I'll tell you what I told myself the last time someone official asked me questions. The record is theirs. It has been theirs since the day the shooting stopped. They wrote who won, they wrote who surrendered, they wrote who mattered, and when they got to women like us they wrote us small or they wrote us wrong or they skipped the page entirely. And now, after twenty years of letting us be nobody, they want our testimony. Why? So they can weigh it and file it and decide again what we were worth? Why hand them more of us?"

"Because if you don't," Mara said quietly, "the only version left will be theirs. And theirs is already written. I've seen the drafts."

"Theirs and Edith Voss's agree nicely, I expect."

The silence that followed had weight and edges. Cass felt Mara go still beside her, felt the stillness of someone who has been struck in a place they cannot show.

"That's cruel," Mara said at last.

"It's true, which is crueler." Cass stood up. She hadn't meant to stand; her legs decided on their own, some old instinct for ending interviews before they ended her. She walked two steps toward the footbridge and stopped, because her son's face had risen in her mind unbidden, and his face had a gravity all its own, stronger than anger, stronger than the bridge, stronger than everything.

"Tomas," she said, without turning around. "My boy. He's seventeen. He works the dye floor at Halloway's, and he's saving for night classes because he wants to be an engineer, and he—" She stopped. Started again. "He has the anniversary pamphlet pinned above his bed. The one with Edith Voss at the rail of the Merrow Street bridge, alone, arm raised, stopping a regiment with her voice. He showed it to me last Frostmonth. He said, *Ma, you lived through that time, weren't they magnificent?* And he looked at me with such pride in the whole business of it. In her. In the story. And I said yes. I said yes, they were magnificent."

Now she did turn. The lamplight caught the water on her lashes and made it shine, and she let it shine, because hiding it would cost more than showing it.

"Do you understand what you're asking? Not the Commission. You. You're asking me to tell my son that the picture over his bed is a lie. That the woman he worships stood on that bridge with three other women and let them be erased. That one of the erased women was his mother, who let him grow up on the lie because telling him the truth felt like handing him a stone to carry instead of the bread he'd already eaten. You're asking me to teach him that his country's history is a room full of locked drawers, and that his own family is one of the things locked inside them. Seventeen years old. He believes in something clean. You want to hand him something true, and truth at that scale is filthy—it's bribed captains and frightened women and a famous lady who took the whole story home with her like a coat that didn't belong to her and wore it until it fit."

Mara had risen too, and stood with the hat crushed forgotten in both hands. "And what happens to him," they asked, "when he finds out anyway? Because he will find out. Things sealed in archives leak. Things buried in people leak worse. Wouldn't you rather he heard it from you than from a newspaper?"

"He won't find out," Cass said. "That's the point of doing it properly. That's the discipline of it. You bury a thing deep enough and quiet enough, and the ground holds."

"Does it?" Mara's voice was gentle, and gentleness was worse than argument. "You've thought about that bridge every day for twenty years. I know it because I drove the car that morning. I know it because I watched you count the soldiers' rifles with your lips moving. Do you think the ground held for you? Or did it just hold still, while you carried the weight on top of it?"

Cass opened her mouth to answer and found there was no answer that was not a surrender. She closed it again. A train went by somewhere beyond the terraces, hauling its long complaint across the dark, and beneath the bench a rat moved through the reeds with the small industrious sound of a life that did not care about any of this.

"I can't," she said finally. "Not for your petition. Not for the record. Not for the memory of Ruthie, wherever she's got to, and not for the dead captain and not for Edith Voss and not for the movement, God forgive me, and not even for the truth, which I used to think was a thing you could serve like a master. I have one loyalty left that I trust, and it eats supper at my table and calls me Ma and thinks the world is mostly good. I will not spend him to buy back my name. There isn't enough of him to spend."

"No one's asking you to spend him."

"They're asking exactly that, whether they say it or not." She was walking now, backward at first, then forward, toward the footbridge, because staying was becoming impossible. "Every testimony has a reader. Every reader talks. Every talking reaches a schoolyard or a dye floor or a dinner table where my son is sitting. You can't amend a public record privately, Ms. Hartwell. That's not how records work. That's how wounds work."

At the middle of the footbridge she stopped, one hand on the cold iron rail, because there was one more thing and it would not stay down.

"When I came off that bridge," she said to the water, to the dark, to whoever was listening on either side of it, "I swore three oaths. Never to speak of it. Never to seek credit. Never to let my family carry what I carried. Two of them I've kept. And some nights I hate myself for keeping them, and most nights I don't, and I don't know which nights are the honest ones. That's what's left of the woman who stood there. Not courage. Arithmetic."

She crossed the rest of the bridge without hurrying and without looking back, which took everything she had, and let herself into the terrace house where the fire was banked and her son's boots sat by the door with his socks stuffed in them, waiting for morning, and the pamphlet hung above his bed with its four-color legend, its lone magnificent figure at the rail, its history approved by the Commission for schools.

* * *

She slept badly and woke before the knocker-up's tap came tapping down the terrace, and lay in the gray hour listening to Tomas breathe in the next room, and hated Mara Hartwell with a tired, admiring hatred, the kind you feel toward weather that has changed the season whether you consented or not.

At breakfast he pushed the marmalade toward her and said, "There's a rally Saturday. The anniversary. She's speaking—Edith Voss. They're printing special editions. Denny says maybe fifty thousand people." His eyes were bright with borrowed glory. "Fifty thousand, Ma. For one woman on a bridge."

"Fifty thousand," Cass agreed, and buttered her toast with a steady hand, and understood, with the terrible clarity of the half-awake, that she had won nothing by refusing. The question had gotten into the house. It sat at her table now, wearing her son's admiration like a coat, and it would not leave.

Because fifty thousand people would hear the legend on Saturday. Fifty thousand voices would repeat it into the next twenty years. And somewhere in that crowd, she knew without knowing how she knew, would stand a careful, patient figure with a hat in hand, watching, waiting, counting—as Cass herself had once counted rifles on a bridge—with lips moving, writing everything down.
