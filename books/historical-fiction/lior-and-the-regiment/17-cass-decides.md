## 17. Cass Decides

The pamphlet came home in Elias's coat pocket, folded into quarters the way boys fold things—carelessly, with a crease that cut straight through the middle of everything.

Cass found it on the kitchen table when she came in from the evening shift, her hands still stinging faintly from the bleach water, her hair still smelling of steam and lye from the drying room. The house was warm. Someone had banked the stove. And there sat her son, fourteen years old and all elbows now, hunched over the table under the lamp with his forefinger moving down a printed page.

"Ma." He didn't look up. "Ma, look at this."

She set down her satchel. Her shoulders ached in the way they always did by Thursday, the left one worse than the right because of the wringer, and she thought—not for the first time—that she would trade every ache she had for ten minutes of not being looked at like this. Like something was about to be asked of her.

"What is it?"

"It's the anniversary thing. The bridge thing. Mr. Halloway handed them out at school today—everyone got one. It's got the speech in it, the whole speech, and the picture of the bridge." He smoothed the paper flat against the wood with both hands, the way he'd done since he was small, pressing wrinkles out of things, making them right. "Look at the picture, Ma."

She looked.

The engraving was a good one, she had to give it that. The river rendered in fine crosshatched lines, the far span of the old bridge arching up into fog, and there at the near end, standing alone against the whole gray weight of the image, a single figure in a long dark coat. Even in ink, even at this size, you could tell it was Edith Voss. The posture did it—the chin lifted, the shoulders squared as if the wind were nothing, as if the regiment coming across the water were nothing. Twenty years of newspapers and lithographs and now school pamphlets, and every artist drew her the same way: alone, upright, unafraid.

Cass stood in her own kitchen with bleach still under her fingernails and felt the floor tilt, very slightly, the way it always tilted when she looked at that picture. Not falling. Just tilting. Just enough to remind her the ground was never quite where people said it was.

"There's writing on the back," Elias said. He turned it over. "It tells the story. Listen—it says, *On the fourteenth of October, twenty years ago, Mrs. Edith Voss stood alone upon the Kessel Street Bridge and stayed the advance of an entire regiment through the strength of her conviction alone.*" He read it the way he read everything now, fast and hungry, his voice cracking on the big words in a way he tried to hide. "Alone," he said again. "It says she was alone."

"Mm."

"And then there's a list. Of witnesses. People who saw it happen, it says. It's got names." His finger went down the column, and Cass watched him go down it too, and she knew—she knew before he reached it—what he would find there, because she had known for twenty years what was in that column and what wasn't. She had seen the lists. She had seen the plaques and the programs and the commemorative plates they sold at the fairs, and every time she had run her eyes down those names and found them empty of anything that belonged to her, and every time she had told herself the same thing she always told herself: *the record is theirs. Let them keep it wrong.*

Elias's finger stopped.

"Ma," he said. "There's a name here that looks like yours."

The stove ticked. The kettle on it had gone quiet some time ago, the water hot but unbothered, and Cass found herself staring at it instead of at her son's face.

"Does it."

"*Okafor, C.*" He sounded puzzled, pleased, confused, all three tangled together the way feelings get at fourteen. "That's—Ma, is that you? Were you there? Did you see it? Why does it say—" He turned the pamphlet over again, hunting, and Cass watched him hunt. "Because if you were there, why aren't you in the picture? It says she stood alone, but if you were a witness, you were there, so—"

"Elias."

He looked up at her then, and God help her, his face was open. That was the worst of it. Fourteen years old and his face was still wide open to her, trusting, expecting sense from the world because so far the world had mostly supplied it. He had her mouth and his dead father's stubbornness and no idea at all what he was holding in his hands.

"Why isn't your name in the story?" he asked. "If you saw it. If you were *there*."

*The record is theirs,* said the voice in her head, the one she'd built over twenty years out of brick and mortar, out of keeping her head down and her name out of print and her memories locked in a box behind her ribs that she opened only at night, only sometimes, only when the steam of the laundry made her dizzy enough to forget she'd sworn off remembering.

But her son was looking at her. And the record, whatever else it was, whoever else it belonged to—her son would read it. Her son already had read it. Her son was standing in her kitchen holding a lie in both hands and asking her to make it true or make it not.

"Sit down," Cass said. "I'll make tea."

"I don't want tea, I want—"

"Sit *down*, Elias."

He sat. She put the kettle on properly, though it hardly needed it, because her hands wanted work and the kettle was the nearest honest labor. She got down two cups even though she wouldn't drink hers. She let the silence stretch until it hurt him a little, because some lessons you can't hand a boy gently; you have to let him feel the edges first.

"The name in that list is mine," she said at last, her back to him, watching the kettle. "*Okafor, C.* Cass Okafor. That's me."

"You were there." Not a question anymore. Wondering, now. Awe starting to kindle somewhere in him, and she hated that most of all, because awe was the wrong fuel. Awe fed legends, and legends were the thing eating her alive.

"I was there."

"On the bridge. With—" He caught himself, recalculated, and she heard the moment the arithmetic finished, heard the wonder curdle into confusion. "With Mrs. Voss? But it says she was *alone*. It says—Ma, the whole thing says she was alone. There's three names in that witness list. Three. If there were witnesses, then other people were there, so how could she be—"

"How indeed," Cass said quietly.

She brought the tea to the table. She sat across from him, and she took the pamphlet out of his hands—gently—and looked at the picture for a long time. The woman in the dark coat. The fog. The regiment implied in crosshatch, a thousand men reduced to shading.

Twenty years ago, on the fourteenth of October, there had been three women on that bridge, and none of them had been alone, and the regiment had passed without a shot fired for reasons that had nothing to do with conviction and everything to do with a captain's price—and Cass Okafor had carried the message that made the whole arrangement possible, walking it through the picket lines in a laundry basket with her heart going like a drum, and Ruthie had stood at the rail with her hands white-knuckled on the iron doing the thing that actually stopped the column, and Edith Voss had arrived late, coat still buttoned wrong, and been talked afterward into standing for the picture.

One picture. One figure. One legend, poured like concrete over the truth of two other women, and setting hard for twenty years.

And now her boy sat across from her with the concrete in his hands, asking her to sign it.

"Here's what I need you to understand," Cass said. She kept her voice level. Laundry taught you that—level voice, steady hands, no matter what the machines were doing to you. "Everything I'm about to say, I want you to hear all the way to the end before you ask me anything. Can you do that?"

He nodded. He was good at that, at least. Had been since he was small.

"I was on that bridge. What the pamphlet says happened didn't happen the way it says. The woman in the picture was brave—I'll not take that from her, and don't you go telling your schoolfellows she wasn't—but she wasn't alone, and she wasn't the whole of it, and the story they printed leaves out the best and the worst parts, and I am one of the parts it leaves out." She turned the page over, to the witness list. Three names. Hers misspelled in spirit if not quite in letter—*Okafor, C.*, as if a first initial were a person, as if a letter could stand where a life should. "They put my name here because someone had to vouch for the story, and then they took me out of the picture because the story was better without me. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Elias was very quiet. Then, carefully, the way he handled anything he suspected might break: "Why would they do that?"

"Because one hero makes a better statue than three."

"But that's not fair."

"No," Cass said. "It isn't."

"So we should—we should write to somebody, shouldn't we? Tell them it's wrong. They'd fix it if they knew. Wouldn't they?" And there it was, the last of the open face, the fourteen-year-old faith that the world was a machine with a complaints department, and Cass felt something crack in her chest—not breaking, she'd have known breaking, this was more like ice shifting on a river in spring, groaning, moving after a long freeze.

"They know," she said.

The kettle ticked. Somewhere down the street a cart rattled past, and a dog answered it, and the ordinary sounds of the mill district went on around their kitchen like water around a stone.

"They know," Cass said again, softer. "They've known for twenty years, my love. That's the part nobody wants to hear. It isn't a mistake. Mistakes get fixed. This is a choice, and choices belong to people, and the people who made this one are not going to undo it because a laundress asks nicely." She pushed the pamphlet back across the table toward him. "So here is your answer, and I want you to carry it, because someday you'll be a man and men get asked these questions at dinner parties and in enlistment offices and everywhere else the world tries to hand you a story and call it history. Here's your answer: I was there. It happened the way I'll tell it, not the way they printed it. And whether anyone writes it down correctly is not up to me anymore—or it wasn't. Until tonight."

"Tonight," Elias repeated.

"Tonight." She looked at the witness list one more time. *Okafor, C.* Twenty years of being an initial. Twenty years of telling herself the record was theirs, that handing them more of her was just handing them more to burn, that a Black woman in the mill district who made noise about a war story would find her hours cut and her references soured and her boy's schoolmasters suddenly cold. All true. All still true. None of it new.

What was new was the boy across the table, folding the lie into quarters, creasing it through the middle of everything.

"Go on up to bed," she said.

"It's barely nine."

"Go on up anyway. I've got a letter to write."

* * *

After he was gone—after the third time she called up the stairs and the third time the footsteps pretended to obey—Cass cleared the table, wiped it down, and sat with a sheet of paper and her good pen, the steel-nibbed one she used for the rent book and nothing else.

The lamp hissed softly. Down at the corner the public house was letting out its nine-thirty crowd, and she could hear the laughter carrying, the loose happy noise of people whose wars were the kind that ended.

She wrote the date. She wrote *Mrs. Hartwell*—no, she crossed it out. Mara Hartwell didn't stand on ceremony, and besides, ceremony was a door and she meant to come through swinging. She wrote *Mara.*

*Mara. My boy brought home the anniversary pamphlet today. They're handing them out in the schools now. There's a picture of the bridge and the speech entire, and a list of witnesses on the back with my name in it—an initial, anyway, Okafor, C., like I'm a filing cabinet—and my son looked at that list and asked me why I'm not in the picture.*

*I didn't plan to tell him. You'll understand that. You've spent twenty years not telling people too. But he sat there with the thing in his hands and his face all open, and I thought, he is going to grow up believing a story that erases his own mother, and he will believe it politely, the way everyone believes it, and he will be proud of the wrong thing his whole life, and I will have to watch him do it.*

*I won't.*

*You came to the mill gate twice and I sent you away twice, and I'll tell you why once and then never again: because I thought the record was theirs. I thought anything I gave them they'd only use to hang me with. I thought a woman like me gets written down only to be written off. That was true for a long time. Maybe it's still true. But it turns out it doesn't matter whether it's true, because the record has come into my kitchen anyway. It came folded in quarters in my son's coat pocket. It found him without my leave. So the question isn't whether we hand them more of us. The question is whether our children inherit their lie or our truth, and I find I cannot bear the first any longer.*

*You asked me to testify. I'm telling you I will. On conditions, because I'm not a fool and neither are you: I'll speak to the Commission, on the record, in my own name, spelled correctly—all of it, O-C-A-F-O-R, let the clerks copy it out ten times till they learn it. I'll say what I carried and where I carried it and whose orders moved the captain, as much as I ever knew of that business. I'll stand next to you and whoever else you find and I'll not flinch. But I won't be an initial. I won't be a witness propping up her monument. If I go into that record, I go in as a woman who was there, whole, with her own account in her own words—and if they want the legend instead, then they can keep the legend and lose me entirely.*

*I'll say it right or not at all. Those are my terms. Bring them to your lady and bring them to your census man, and tell them Cass Okafor is done waiting to be remembered correctly.*

*Come Tuesday. After six. I'll have the kettle on, and this time you can come inside.*

*C.O.*

She read it through twice. On the second pass her eye caught the line about the captain and hovered there—the bribed man, the widow she'd heard whisperings of, the whole rotten ledger of it—and her pen twitched toward caution, toward the old instinct, *say less, give them less, keep yourself small.* She pressed the nib down harder instead and let the sentence stand.

Then she folded the letter, sealed it, and addressed it in her best hand, the one the mission school had given her and no commission had ever taken away: *Mrs. M. Hartwell, care of Mrs. E. Voss, Harrowgate House.*

Outside, the public house had gone quiet. The lamp hissed. Upstairs, at last, her son's breathing had slowed into sleep, and Cass sat alone at the clean table with the empty envelope and thought about Tuesday, and about a bridge, and about a girl she had been at twenty-three, walking through a regiment's picket lines with a laundry basket and a heartbeat like a war drum, who had never once believed she would live to say any of it out loud.

The girl had been wrong about dying young. Perhaps she'd been wrong about everything else too.

Cass banked the stove, blew out the lamp, and went up the stairs in the dark—past her son's door, where she paused, hand on the frame, listening to him breathe—and thought: *Tuesday. And then, God willing, the record.*

Down in the kitchen, on the table, the commemorative pamphlet lay where she'd left it, folded in quarters, its crease cutting through the lone woman in the dark coat, through the bridge, through the fog—through the whole printed lie, straight down the middle of everything.

She left it there. Some things deserved to sit out overnight, in plain view, waiting to be dealt with in the morning.
