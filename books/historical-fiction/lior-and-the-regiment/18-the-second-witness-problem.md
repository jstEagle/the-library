## 18. The Second Witness Problem

The clerk behind the Commission's counter had a voice like wet gravel and the patience of a man who had spent thirty years saying no.

"Petition received," he said, stamping Mara's papers without reading them—or reading them so many times before that reading had worn the meaning away. "You'll be notified."

"When?"

"When you're notified." He slid the papers beneath the counter into a drawer that looked, to Mara, like the mouth of some patient animal. "Next."

Mara did not move. They had learned, over twenty years of driving Edith Voss through hostile towns and hostile weather, that the person who refused to move was often the only one who got anything done. "I'd like to know what happens next. To the petition. What it needs."

The clerk looked up then, and something in their face must have interested him, because he set down his stamp. "Testimony requires corroboration," he said. "Two named witnesses, independent of the petitioner, who can attest to the events described under their own full legal names. In writing. Sworn." He said it the way other men recited scripture—flatly, from memory, without belief. "That's the rule."

"And if the events happened to people who don't have legal names anymore?" Mara asked. "People who changed them. People who were never written down in the first place?"

The clerk's eyes flicked up, then away. "Then I suppose you'd best find two who do," he said, and called, "Next," again, louder this time, as if volume could close a conversation the way a door closes a room.

Outside, the morning was doing its ordinary work—the fish carts, the gulls arguing over the harbor refuse, a boy sweeping the steps of the Commission building as though truth were a thing you could push along with a broom. Mara stood on those steps with the receipt crumpled in their fist and felt the shape of the problem settle over them like a coat cut too small.

Two named witnesses. Cass Okafor was one—if Cass would say it right or not at all, which meant, possibly, not at all. And the third—

Mara closed their eyes and went back, the way you go back to a house you lived in long ago, expecting the furniture to be where you left it and finding instead that someone has moved everything an inch to the left.

Three women on the bridge. That was what the diary entry had said, in Edith's own looping hand, dated the day the regiment came north: *We stood together—Cass, Ruthie, and myself—and the captain turned his column.* Three. Mara had carried all three of them that day in the old Ford, rattling over the mill road with the engine knocking, Cass in front with her hands folded tight in her lap, Ruthie in back holding a basket of laundry as though clean linen might be proof against bullets. Edith had sat beside Mara and said almost nothing the whole ride out. It was the silence Mara remembered most—not the bridge, not even the regiment, but the silence of a woman rehearsing herself.

Ruthie. Ruthie Vance, that had been, in the days when names mattered differently. Small, quick, with a laugh like a struck match. She had been the one who went through the lines afterward—though Mara hadn't known that until the diary, hadn't known how much of the story walked out on Ruthie's narrow feet while the legend gathered itself around Edith like a coat of paint around a fence post.

And Ruthie, if the pension rolls were to be believed—and Mara believed nothing else these days, so they may as well believe this—had been committed to the soldiers' home at Harrow Bend, forty miles upriver, nine years ago.

*Unaccounted for,* the roll said. A phrase that sounded administrative and meant, underneath, *we stopped looking.*

* * *

Mara drove out to the mill district that afternoon with the Ford smelling of its usual cargo—Edith's lavender water, axle grease, and the ghost of every speech she'd ever given from the back seat. The mills were letting out shift-change when Mara arrived, and the street ran brown and loud with women, hundreds of them streaming out of the gates with lint in their hair and their arms swinging loose, and somewhere in that river of faces was Cass Okafor, walking home the way she'd walked home for twenty years, carrying the memory of a bridge the way other women carried marketing baskets.

Mara didn't approach her. Not yet. There was a thing Mara had learned about asking people for their testimony, which was that you got more by standing still than by reaching. So Mara leaned against the Ford and waited, watching the gate, and let the crowd thin the way evening thins, gradually and then all at once, until the street held only stragglers and the smell of cooling machinery and one woman in a gray shawl who slowed when she saw the car, because she knew it, and knew whose hands were usually on its wheel.

"You've been to the Commission," Cass said. She said it flatly, not as a question. She had ink on her fingers—she did the ledgers for the laundry now, Mara had learned, kept the books for three floors of washwomen who trusted her arithmetic more than they trusted the owners'. "I heard. Old Prewitt told everyone at the gate there was somebody petitioning about the war record. Somebody asking questions." Her jaw worked. "You couldn't leave it be."

"I couldn't," Mara agreed. "They're going to seal the archive. Eleven weeks, and then whatever isn't written down is gone forever. And what is written down is wrong."

Cass stood very still. Around them the street emptied; a tram clattered past; somewhere a whistle blew the end of the second shift and nobody came out, because everybody already had. "My son believes the legend," Cass said at last. "He cuts the picture of Mrs. Voss out of the paper. He has it pinned above his bed. You want me to tell him his mother stood on that bridge and it wasn't enough—that she had to stand there and watch another woman take the credit, year after year, speech after speech, and smile about it?" Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about Cass; she never rose. She descended, like water finding the low places. "You know what that does to a boy. It doesn't make him proud of me. It makes him ashamed of her, and ashamed of me for letting it happen, and ashamed of himself for ever having hung the picture up."

"Cass—"

"He thinks the world worked once," she said. "He thinks somebody stood up and it counted. Don't take that from him just because it's true."

"It's true anyway," Mara said quietly. "Whether you tell him or not. Whether any of us tells anyone. The truth is already true, Cass. All we get to decide is whether we're in it or outside it."

Cass looked at them for a long moment. Then she pulled the gray shawl tighter around her shoulders, though the evening was warm, and said, "If I do this—I haven't said I will, mind, but *if*—it'll need more than me. Two named witnesses, Prewitt says. Everyone at the gate says. The Commission wants corroboration, like the war was a debt and they need a cosigner." Her mouth twisted. "So it's not just my name you need. It's hers."

"Ruthie," Mara said.

"Ruthie," Cass said, and for the first time since the mill gate, since the bridge, since the whole twenty years, something moved behind her face that was not anger and not fear but something older and sadder, something that had been waiting a long time for permission. "Harrow Bend. The soldiers' home. I wrote to her once, years back. Never got an answer. I told myself she'd died, or been moved, or—" She stopped. "I told myself stories. Same as Edith does. Same as everybody. It's easier than driving forty miles to find out."

"Will you drive them?" Mara asked. "Forty miles. With me."

Cass was quiet for so long that the gaslamps came on down the whole length of the mill street, one after another, little orange suns rising over the puddles. "I'll think on it," she said finally, which from Cass, Mara suspected, was nearly yes. "But Mara—" and it was the first time she had used their name aloud in twenty years, and hearing it landed in Mara's chest like a stone dropped down a well, taking a long time to hit bottom—"if we go upriver and find that home and ask after Ruthie Vance, they're going to write us down. Names, dates, purpose of visit. Everything we've spent twenty years keeping out of books, we'll be handing to a clerk with a ledger. You understand that? We'll be making ourselves findable."

"I understand it," Mara said. "I've been foundable for a month now. It hasn't killed me yet."

"No," Cass said. "It's only started."

* * *

Edith took the news of the journey worse than Mara expected, which was to say she took it silently, which from Edith was always the worst register.

Mara told her at dinner, over the cold chicken and the candle that Edith insisted on burning even in summer because electric light made her feel like a specimen. "I'm going upriver tomorrow," Mara said. "To Harrow Bend. There's a woman there who was on the bridge."

Edith's knife paused above her plate. "A woman," she repeated.

"Ruthie Vance."

The knife came down. Cut. Lifted. Edith chewed with the deliberate composure of a woman who had addressed ten thousand people and could therefore certainly address one. "I don't recall a Ruthie Vance," she said.

"You wrote her name in your diary," Mara said. "June the fourteenth, twenty years ago. You wrote all our names."

The silence that followed had texture to it, weight, temperature. Edith set down her fork and folded her hands and looked at Mara across the candles with an expression Mara had seen her wear at rallies when hecklers got loud—not anger, exactly, but the swift cold assessment of a commander deciding whether a position could be held.

"So you've read it," Edith said. "My private book."

"I read the page about the bridge. I'm sorry. I shouldn't have. But I did, and now I know what actually happened, and so do you, and the difference between us is that I'm willing to drive toward it."

"Toward it." Edith laughed, a short dry sound with no humor in it. "You speak of it like a town on a map. Toward it. As if the past were a place you could visit and come home from unchanged." She pushed back her chair and stood, and in the candlelight her shadow went up the wall behind her, enormous, wavering, the shadow of someone much larger than the woman casting it. "Do you know what happens to the shelters if the anniversary fund collapses? Do you know how many girls are learning to read this winter because women give money to the Edith Voss story? You want to go digging up Ruthie Vance and Cass Okafor and—whoever you were before you were Mara Hartwell—and lay it all out on a table for men like Aldous Fen to sort through. And when you're done sorting, the story will be smaller. That's what truth does, Mara. It makes things smaller. It shrinks a movement down to the size of a fact."

"Or it grows a fact up to the size of a movement," Mara said. "It depends which direction you're looking from."

For a moment—one unguarded flicker, there and gone like a match in wind—something in Edith's face looked almost grateful. Almost relieved. As if she had been waiting twenty years for someone to argue with her honestly and had forgotten, until this instant, how much she'd missed it.

"Go to Harrow Bend, then," she said at last, quietly, sitting back down. "Drive your forty miles. But Mara—" she picked up her fork, resumed her dinner, became again the composed and legendary woman, except that her hand was not quite steady— "when you find her, if you find her, remember that whatever she remembers, she remembers it about all of us. Including the parts I'd rather she didn't."

"That's rather the point," Mara said.

"Yes," Edith said, to her chicken, to her candle, to no one. "I imagine it is."

* * *

Mara packed the Ford that night by lamplight: spare tire, water tin, the road atlas with Harrow Bend marked in pencil from an earlier life, and—wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the kit, where no customs man or Commission clerk would think to look—the diary, the widow's ledger page, and the carbon copy of the petition receipt. Evidence traveled badly, Mara had learned. It liked to be damp, lost, confiscated. You had to carry it the way you carried a live coal: carefully, constantly, knowing that the moment you put it down was the moment it went out.

Cass met them at dawn at the corner of Foundry Street, wearing her good coat and carrying a paper sack of food and an expression that dared Mara to comment on either. She climbed into the passenger seat without a word, arranged her skirt, folded her hands in her lap exactly as she had on the day of the bridge, twenty years ago, and only then did Mara realize she was doing it deliberately—a rehearsal, a returning, a woman putting her own history back on like a coat.

"Forty miles," Cass said, as the engine caught and coughed and caught again. "How long does it take?"

"Two hours, if the river road's open. Longer if they've got checkpoints."

"They will have checkpoints," Cass said. "Fen's lot photograph everyone coming and going now. Prewitt heard it from a cousin who drives supply wagons." She looked straight ahead through the windshield at the pale strip of dawn over the mill roofs. "Two women who once stood on a bridge, sneaking upriver toward a soldiers' home, hoping to find a third woman who hasn't spoken in nine years, and convince her to sign her name to something the government would very much prefer stayed unwritten." She exhaled through her nose, almost a laugh. "My son would call it an adventure."

"What would you call it?"

Cass considered. The mills fell away behind them; the road bent toward the river; the morning opened up ahead, wide and uncertain and running out.

"I'd call it Tuesday," she said.

Mara shifted into second gear, and the Ford rolled north, and somewhere ahead of them—forty miles, two hours, one checkpoint, one clerk with a camera, one silent ward in a soldiers' home by the water—Ruthie Vance was waiting without knowing she was waiting, and the archive doors were closing at the speed of bureaucracy, which is to say slowly, and then all at once.
