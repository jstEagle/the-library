## 19. Fen Closes In

The Voss house stood at the top of Corben Street like a sentence with its chin lifted.

Aldous Fen had spent the morning walking past it twice—once on the far side of the road, once under the plane trees where the shade broke his outline into pieces—and both times he had made himself look at it the way a clerk looks at a ledger page: without appetite, without fear. It was a skill he had cultivated over eleven years of Commission work, and it was not courage, whatever his superior's letters implied. Courage was for men who wanted something. Fen only wanted the record to close properly, seam to seam, nothing left gaping. A history with holes in it was worse than no history at all. That was the whole of his creed, written out in his own hand in the front of his field manual, beneath the printed instructions on interviewing technique and the columns for inconsistency reports.

He climbed the steps at four minutes past two, because arriving early suggested eagerness and arriving late suggested contempt, and Edith Voss was owed neither.

The door opened before he could knock. Not by a servant—there were no servants now, only the driver, and Fen knew precisely where the driver was this afternoon because Fen had paid a boy at the livery stable three shillings to report the comings and goings of a certain grey motorcar. The car had gone out at half past one with Miss Hartwell alone at the wheel, bound, according to the boy, for the mill district. The mill district. Fen filed that away in the drawer of his mind where he kept Cass Okafor's address, and Ruthie Lark's last known location, and the photograph of a woman entering the soldiers' home at Harrowgate whose face he had not yet matched to a name but would, given another week, match to two.

So the door was opened by Mrs. Pell, the housekeeper, a broad woman with flour on her wrists, who looked at his card as though it might be contagious.

"Miss Voss doesn't see callers on Thursdays."

"I'm not a caller," Fen said pleasantly. "I'm an instrument of His Majesty's Commission on the War Record. She'll see me."

He had learned long ago that there was no point softening such sentences. Softening them made people argue. Stating them plainly made people obey, and then resent him quietly afterward, which was fine; resentment was a renewable resource and cost him nothing.

Mrs. Pell went in. Fen stood on the step and let the afternoon arrange itself around him. Somewhere below the hill, the trams clanged along their wires. Somewhere beyond that, the river carried its barges toward the sea, and beyond the sea, presumably, the world continued in its indifference. He thought, briefly and without sentiment, about the smell of the archive in the Commission basement—the cold paper smell, the smell of things being finished—and how he had once loved it, or believed he had.

Then Mrs. Pell returned, wearing the expression of a woman who has lost a bet with herself, and showed him through.

* * *

Edith Voss received him in the study, standing, which he noted and admired. Women of her age and stature usually sat behind their desks to make visitors feel like schoolboys. She remained on her feet beside the window with her hands folded, silver-haired and upright as a fence post, and she let him cross the carpet to her rather than coming to meet him halfway—a small territorial maneuver, executed without visible effort, and Fen tipped his hat to it internally.

"Mr. Fen." Her voice was the voice from the pamphlets and the newsreels, low and level, a voice built for halls. "The census man."

"The census man," he agreed. "Among other capacities."

"You've come a long way from counting chimneys."

"Chimneys don't lie," said Fen. "People do. It's why I prefer them professionally."

Something moved behind her eyes—not anger, he judged, but appraisal. She was measuring him, deciding what he was worth and what he could be made to do. He had seen the same calculation in generals and grocers alike. He took the chair she indicated, set his leather case on his knees, and did not open it. Let her wonder about the case. Wonder was a lever; you got more out of a room when the room was slightly afraid of your luggage.

"You'll forgive my directness," he said. "I have a great deal of work and less time than I'd like, so I'll tell you what I've come to tell you, and then I'll ask what I've come to ask, and then I'll go."

"That would make you unique among officials."

"I try." He unbuckled the case anyway, slowly, and drew out a single sheet of paper, which he laid on the desk between them, blank side up. "In eleven weeks, the Commission seals the official war record. After that date, no new testimony, no amendments, no corrections. Whatever the record says on that day, it says forever. My task—insofar as I can explain it without invoking phrases that put people to sleep—is to ensure that what goes into the seal is *true*. Or," he added, because precision mattered even here, especially here, "as true as the Commission finds convenient. You'll notice I said both things. I meant both things."

"How refreshing," said Edith Voss dryly. "An honest liar."

"Honesty is cheap when you're nobody, ma'am. It's the somebodies who have to buy it." He turned the sheet over. On it, in his own neat hand, was a list of dates. "I'm going to recite these to you, and you're going to listen, and I'd take it as a courtesy if you didn't interrupt, because interruptions are data too."

She raised one eyebrow, which was, he suspected, the most dangerous thing she could do while remaining seated.

"October the ninth, war year six: a column of the Second Regiment crosses Vane Bridge without firing a shot, in open defiance of orders to clear the crossing. October the tenth: Captain Aldery, commanding, files a report citing 'civilian presence of sufficient moral force to render action unwise.' October the eleventh: Captain Aldery requests transfer. December of the same year: Captain Aldery resigns his commission with unusual speed and unusual money. And in every published account since—" he tapped the dates with one finger, gently, as one taps a sleeping animal— "the woman who stopped that regiment was you. Alone. At the bridge. With nothing but conviction."

"And is that not the account?" Edith said. "It's the account I've given twenty years running."

"It is," said Fen. "It's a very good account. It has a shape. One woman, one bridge, one regiment. History adores a shape. But accounts with shapes are usually built, and built things have builders, and builders leave offcuts." He paused, and let the silence do a little work. "There were three women on that bridge, Miss Voss. Not one."

For the first time since he'd entered the room, Edith Voss was still in the way furniture is still—entirely, deliberately, with everything locked down. Fen had interviewed enough frightened people to know the difference between stillness and stillness. Most people, when struck, flinch or laugh or reach for words. The truly practiced ones went wooden. He gave her five full seconds of it before he continued, because five seconds was long enough for the wood to start creaking.

"Cassandra Okafor, of the mill district. Ruthie Lark, late of Harrowgate. Both alive, as of my last inquiries. And both absent, conspicuously, from every speech, every pamphlet, every anniversary observance for two decades." He folded his hands atop the case. "Now. Here is what I've actually come to say, and I say it as a courtesy, because whatever else I am, I am courteous."

He leaned forward slightly.

"I know about the diary."

The word landed in the room like a coin dropped in a church.

Fen watched her face with the particular attention he brought to all his interviews—not the vulgar attention of a man hunting guilt, but the finer attention of a man reading weather. And the weather told him something interesting. Because Edith Voss did not glance at the desk drawers. She did not glance at the writing bureau against the wall, or the cabinet by the window, or anywhere at all. Her eyes came to rest instead on the middle distance, on the window and the garden beyond it, and they stayed there with the fixed quality of a person doing rapid and terrible arithmetic.

She did not know where the diary was safe. She was calculating whether she knew where it was at all.

"There is no diary," she said.

"No," Fen agreed. "There isn't. There's no ledger either, no letter from Okafor requesting remembrance, and no entry dated the ninth of October describing, in your own hand, the arrangement made with Captain Aldery and the parts played by each woman present. None of those things exist. I merely find it instructive, Miss Voss, how many things don't exist in this house. A house with this many absences in it begins to feel crowded."

He rose. There was no reason to prolong it; the interview had accomplished what interviews accomplish, which is not information—he already had the information—but position. She would spend the evening thinking about him. She would spend the night wondering what he had, whom he'd spoken to, how much the ground under her had shifted. Surveillance worked not by watching people but by letting them feel watched, until they began watching themselves, and then they made mistakes, and mistakes were the raw material of the record.

At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and delivered the last of it almost kindly.

"One more thing, and then I'll trouble you no further. Your driver keeps remarkable hours. Out to the mills yesterday. Out again today, alone, in a motorcar that costs more per year to run than Okafor earns at her laundry tubs in ten." He let that settle. "Loyalty is a lovely thing, Miss Voss. I've read about it extensively. But loyalty under pressure tends to choose a side, and I should very much like to know, before the sealing—which side yours chooses."

He heard it then. Behind him, faint, the sound of the front door opening downstairs, the sound of a key in the lock, footsteps in the hall—footsteps he recognized by their rhythm, having listened to them across three months of reports. The driver was home.

Fen did not turn around. He simply smiled at the window, at the garden, at the long light coming gold across the lawn, and said, softly enough that only she could hear:

"Ah. Speak of the devil, and the census arrives."

And he walked out past Mara Hartwell in the hallway—past the stiff shoulders and the carefully blank face and the eyes that flicked to his case and back with a hatred so controlled it was nearly polite—nodded good afternoon to Mrs. Pell, and descended the steps of Corben Street into the falling light, leaving behind him a silence with a crack running clean through it.

Behind him, above, a door closed upstairs. Then another. Then nothing at all—which was, Fen reflected as he turned toward the tram stop, making a note in the margin of his manual as he walked, exactly the sound a house makes when the people inside it have just discovered they cannot speak freely anymore.

He wrote: *Subject aware of diary's exposure risk. Driver returned 16:40. Observe whether confrontation occurs. If Hartwell runs—*

He paused, pencil hovering, and looked back once at the tall windows of the Voss house, where a lamp had not yet been lit and the rooms stood grey and waiting.

*If Hartwell runs,* he wrote at last, *she'll run upriver.*

He closed the manual and went to catch his tram, and behind him the house on Corben Street held its breath, all its clocks ticking toward a reckoning none of its inhabitants had yet agreed upon.
