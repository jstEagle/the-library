## 20. The Upriver Journey

The mill district gave way to hedgerows before dawn, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge they were on the coast road proper, the Voss motorcar humming along the gravel like a contented animal that didn't know it was carrying contraband. Mara drove with the window cracked despite the cold. She had learned years ago that a closed window made her sleepy, and sleepiness, these days, was a luxury she could not afford.

Cass sat beside her with her arms crossed and her good coat buttoned to the throat, staring out at the fields as if she expected them to lie to her.

"You're doing it again," Cass said.

"Doing what."

"That thing where you drive like the road is a question you're afraid of." Cass turned from the window. "You've checked the mirror four times since the last mile marker. There's nobody behind us."

"There's always somebody behind us. That's rather the point of the last few weeks."

Cass snorted, but it was not quite humor. She reached into her bag and produced a paper-wrapped bundle of bread and hard cheese, tearing it down the middle without asking whether Mara wanted any. It was a laundress's habit, Mara thought—portioning, dividing, making do—and she took her half gratefully.

They ate in silence for a while. The river appeared and disappeared between the trees on their left, wide and brown and patient, going about its business as though no commission of men in gray suits had ever decided what it should have witnessed.

"Do you remember," Cass said at last, "the last time we went up this valley together?"

Mara did. She remembered everything, which was both her trade and her affliction.

"I remember you complained the whole way about the mud on your boots."

"My one good pair." Cass looked at the road. "And I remember Ruthie singing. Badly. On purpose, I think, because Edith kept wincing and Ruthie liked making Edith wince. She thought it proved Edith was human."

"She was right."

"She was right," Cass agreed softly, "and then twenty years passed, and now she can't prove anything at all."

The checkpoint came up faster than Mara wanted it to—the first one, anyway. She'd known there would be three between here and the soldiers' home at Harrow Bend; the Commission had grown fond of checkpoints the way a nervous man grows fond of locks. A striped pole barred the road, and behind it stood a hut of fresh unpainted lumber, two soldiers in field-gray, and a clerk's desk set out under an awning as though the war itself were still being conducted here, twenty miles upriver from anywhere that mattered.

Mara slowed the car. Her hands were steady on the wheel. She noticed this and was grateful for it; her hands had learned steadiness the hard way, during years when unsteady hands got people questioned.

"Papers," said the nearer soldier.

She handed over her driving permit and the letter of passage Edith had drafted—a bland, official-sounding note requesting transport of a domestic employee to the commemorative unveiling circuit, signed in that famous looping hand that opened doors precisely because everyone knew whose hand it was. The soldier read it twice, glanced at Cass's work papers, and waved them toward the desk.

The clerk was a thin young man with ink-stained fingers and a camera on a wooden tripod beside his chair, the lens cap off, the bellows extended like the head of a curious animal.

"Just the driver first, please," he said pleasantly. "Sit in the chair. Look at the lens. Don't smile—it blurs the plate."

Mara sat. The camera smelled of chemicals and dust. Somewhere inside it, she knew, was a glass negative that would carry her face into a government drawer, filed and cross-referenced, waiting for some future morning when someone would lay it beside another photograph and find the match. She had spent twenty years avoiding exactly this. Now she sat in the chair and looked into the black glass eye and thought: *remember me correctly, if you're going to keep me.*

"Hold still." A flash of powder-light, a whiff of smoke. "Name?"

"Mara Hartwell."

"Occupation?"

"Driver and correspondence secretary to Mrs. Edith Voss."

He wrote it all down in a ledger with ruled columns—name, occupation, origin, destination, purpose—and Mara watched him write *Hartwell* in a hand so neat it might have been printed. Twenty years ago, a different name had lived in that space. She had buried it so thoroughly that sometimes she almost believed it had never existed, except that somewhere in Fen's files it was surely sitting in its grave, patient as a seed.

"And the passenger?"

"Cass Okafor. Laundress. Traveling as my assistant."

The clerk photographed Cass too, and asked her name twice, and wrote it twice, and Mara saw Cass's jaw tighten at the second asking—not fear, exactly. Something older than fear. The particular anger of a woman who had been written down wrong once already and knew what the pen could do.

"Destination?"

"The garrison town at Harrow Bend," Mara said, which was true, more or less. The soldiers' home was three miles past it, up the farm lane. "Then on to the unveiling at Corren Point Thursday week."

The clerk stamped the passage letter, initialed the ledger, and smiled with professional warmth. "Mind the second crossing. The ferry's been temperamental since the frost."

*Thank you,* Mara said, and drove through, and did not let her breath out until the hut was a speck in the mirror.

"He photographed us," Cass said.

"Yes."

"Why? What does a census need with photographs?"

Mara kept her eyes on the road. "It isn't a census anymore. You know that. Fen told me himself—he works for the Historical Commission. They're compiling the authoritative record. Everyone who enters a checkpoint goes into a file, and every file gets compared against every other file until the inconsistencies shake loose."

"Inconsistencies." Cass said the word like she was tasting spoiled milk. "That's what we are now. Inconsistencies."

"That's what I am. You've managed to stay consistent. You told them you were a laundress in the mill district for the whole war, and that's close enough to true that no ledger will catch the difference." Mara flexed her fingers on the wheel. "I'm the one who changed her name. I'm the one with a desertion charge folded up inside her like a letter nobody's delivered yet."

Cass was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its edge. "Ruthie too. Ruthie's got nothing but consistency left. That home has her registered as a widow of the regimental auxiliary, no given history, no next of kin listed. She let them erase her because erasing was easier than explaining." She looked down at her half-eaten bread. "I used to think that was cowardice. Before my son started asking questions, I thought it was cowardice. Now I know it's just what survival looks like from the inside."

The road climbed away from the river and into pine country, and the light went green and dim beneath the branches. They passed a cart loaded with turnips, a boy walking a dog, a shrine at a bend where someone had left wilting flowers for the war dead—small offerings to a story everyone agreed upon because agreeing was cheaper than remembering.

At midday they stopped at the second crossing, where the ferry was indeed temperamental, and waited forty minutes on the near bank while the ferryman argued with his engine. Cass walked the bank. Mara stayed with the car and let herself think about Edith, which she had been trying not to do all morning.

Edith had kissed her cheek at the gate that morning—had held her shoulder a moment longer than usual, and said only, "Bring her back gently. Whatever's left of her." As if Ruthie were a wounded bird. As if the thing being carried home from Harrow Bend weren't the end of everything Edith had built.

But there had been something else in Edith's face. Underneath the worry. Mara had turned it over on the road and finally named it: relief. The relief of a woman who has been carrying a heavy secret alone and has just felt someone else put a hand under the weight. Edith had not said *stop*. Edith had said *gently*, which was a different word entirely, and Mara was still deciding what to make of that.

The ferry groaned across. On the far bank, the third checkpoint waited, and this one was different.

* * *

They saw it before they reached it: a larger hut, a taller pole, and a queue of perhaps a dozen travelers—farmers with carts, a doctor's motorcar, two women with market baskets—all shuffling toward the same awning, under which the same model of tripod camera stood in the same posture of patient attention.

"They've expanded," Mara murmured. "Fen said the Commission was thorough. He undersold it."

"Look at the line," Cass said. "Half these people are going to market. They'll be in a government drawer by supper, smiling—or not smiling, apparently, it blurs the plate—and none of them will ever know why."

There was no way around. The lane forked a quarter mile back, but the east fork ran to a ford that was flooded with spring melt, and the west fork simply rejoined this road below the checkpoint, funneling every traveler through the same narrow throat. Whoever designed it understood people the way a weir understands water.

Mara pulled the car into the queue and killed the engine.

"We could turn around," Cass said quietly.

"No."

"I didn't say we should. I said we could." Cass studied her. "I want to hear you say why we won't. Not for me. For you. Because when that clerk asks me why a laundress is traveling to a soldiers' home, I'm going to need to borrow whatever it is you're carrying."

Mara looked down the line of hats and baskets and tired faces. Ahead, a soldier was waving the doctor's car forward, and the flash-powder went off with its little cough of smoke, adding one more face to the archive.

"Because I spent twenty years being careful," Mara said. "Careful is how I survived, and I don't regret it—I'd be dead or transported otherwise, and Edith would have lost the movement and I'd have lost her, and none of the rest of it would matter. But careful has a price, and I paid it in installments, and I only just realized the account is empty." She turned to Cass. "Two women stopped that regiment. Three, counting Ruthie. And the record says one woman did it alone, and the record is about to be sealed shut with the lie inside it, lacquered and official, forever. If I'm careful today, then careful wins. And I find—" her voice caught, and she steadied it, "—I find that I mind."

Cass nodded slowly. Then she did something unexpected: she reached over and straightened Mara's collar, briskly, the way she might have smoothed a sheet.

"All right," she said. "Then here's what we carry. We are going to visit an old friend of the family who did laundry work near Harrow Bend during the war—true enough, she mended for the garrison hospital, I'll swear to it—and we heard she was poorly, and Mrs. Voss, who has a famously soft heart, sent her driver to check on her. Every word of that will survive scrutiny, because it's all nearly true, and nearly true is the best armor anyone's ever forged." She met Mara's eyes. "Nearly true got us through the war. Let's see if it gets us through a checkpoint."

They moved forward in the queue, car by car, face by face. When their turn came, the clerk at this post was older than the first one—a gray-mustached man with the unhurried manner of someone who had processed ten thousand faces and remembered none of them. He took the passage letter, read Edith's signature, and his eyebrows rose a fraction.

"You're from the Voss household."

"I'm Mrs. Voss's driver."

"She's the bridge woman." He said it the way other men said *the general*—with the flat reverence of the officially instructed. "My daughter recites her speech at school. 'One woman stood where the guns must pass.'" He stamped the letter. "An honor, in a way. Photographs all the same. Regulations."

*One woman stood.* Mara sat in the chair and looked into the lens and let the sentence ring in her skull like a struck nail. One woman. The words were everywhere now—in schoolrooms, in pamphlets, in the mouths of clerks at checkpoints. They had hardened into something load-bearing; pull one out and people feared the whole structure would come down. Perhaps it would. Perhaps that was the wager they were all making now, the four of them and the cold tea and the ledger page hidden in the lining of Mara's suitcase.

The flash popped. The clerk waved them through with the same pleasant indifference he'd give a turnip cart.

Cass didn't speak until the checkpoint was out of sight. "He loves her," she said. "That man loves her. His daughter recites the lie at school and it makes her happy, and he's proud of it, and he photographed us anyway, and none of it—none of it—makes the lie less of a lie." She laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what I thought, standing on that bridge? I thought: someday they'll write this down, and it'll be hard, but it'll be *ours*. Somebody will get it right eventually, because that's what records are for." She shook her head. "Twenty years old and stupid."

"You weren't stupid. You were hopeful. There's a difference, and it costs more."

* * *

The garrison town at Harrow Bend came up in late afternoon, all shuttered armories and widows' weeds hanging in shop windows, and they passed through without stopping. Three miles beyond, the farm lane branched off toward the river flats, and at the end of it, behind a stand of poplars, stood the soldiers' home: a long brick building with a chapel spine and rows of small windows, each window box holding the same geraniums, planted by the same instruction, blooming in obedient red.

A sign at the gate read HAVEN HOME FOR WARTIME AUXILIARIES AND DEPENDENTS — VISITORS BY APPOINTMENT OF THE COMMISSION.

*Of the Commission.* Mara read it twice. Even charity had been absorbed into the apparatus. Even mercy had a filing system.

She parked the car in the shade of the poplars and sat for a moment with her hands in her lap. Through the nearest ground-floor window she could see a common room: high-backed chairs arranged in a circle, a fire laid but unlit, and in one of the chairs, small and straight-backed and utterly still, a woman with white braided hair looking out the window at the poplars—at the car—at them.

Even at this distance, even after twenty years, Mara felt the recognition go through the glass like a current.

Ruthie had seen them. Ruthie had gone still in the particular way she used to go still before speaking, gathering herself the way other people gathered breath.

Cass's hand found Mara's arm. "She remembers us."

"She remembers everything," Mara said. "That was always her trouble."

They got out of the car. The gravel crunched under their feet, loud as testimony. At the front door, brass plaque freshly polished, a smaller notice had been pinned with four neat tacks, and Mara stopped to read it, and felt the day tilt beneath her:

BY ORDER OF THE HISTORICAL COMMISSION, ALL VISITORS TO THIS INSTITUTION WILL BE INTERVIEWED REGARDING THEIR WARTIME ASSOCIATIONS. RESIDENTS ARE REMINDED THAT UNAUTHORIZED DISCUSSION OF PAST EVENTS IS A REPORTABLE OFFENSE.

Below it, someone—some resident with a steady hand and nothing left to lose—had written in pencil, faint but legible: *Reported to whom?*

Mara looked at Cass. Cass looked at Mara. And somewhere behind the door, footsteps were already coming down the hall—quick, light, unmistakable footsteps belonging to a woman who had not spoken a full sentence in six years, coming to answer a door she wasn't supposed to open.

"Miss Hartwell," Cass whispered, gripping her sleeve. "If they've forbidden her to talk—"

The lock turned.
