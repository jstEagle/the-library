## 24. The Bribe's Other Side

The widow lived at the end of a lane that the Commission's maps had stopped bothering to name.

Aldous Fen had walked it twice before he trusted himself to walk it a third time. The first visit he had told himself was reconnaissance; the second, that he had merely lost his way. By the third he had run out of euphemisms and admitted what the file in his satchel already knew: that he was going to ask a grieving woman to trade one kind of truth for another, and that he did not yet know whether he was doing it for the Commission or against it.

The lane ran behind the tannery hill, where the town's respectability ended and its labor began. Fences leaned into each other like tired men. A dog of no particular allegiance trotted alongside him for forty yards, decided he was not worth the trouble, and turned back. At the end of the lane stood a cottage with whitewash gone gray at the corners, a garden gone to seed except for one stubborn row of cabbages, and laundry on the line—men's shirts, all of them too big for any man who might still live there, washed and hung and rewashed as if the hanging itself were the point.

He knocked. He heard, from inside, the small domestic symphony of a kettle, a chair pushed back, footsteps that did not hurry.

The woman who opened the door was perhaps sixty, built like the doorframe she held—narrow, upright, load-bearing. Her hair was iron-colored and pinned without vanity. She looked at Fen the way people in this district looked at anyone wearing a good coat: with an arithmetic running visibly behind her eyes, calculating what he wanted and what it would cost him to get it.

"You're the census man," she said.

"I was," Fen said. "Mrs.—"

"Bray. You'll have my husband's name off your papers or you'll not have it in your mouth either."

"Mrs. Bray. My name is Aldous Fen. May I come in? I'd rather say what I've come to say sitting down. It takes longer than standing up."

She considered him for a moment the way she might consider a bill of uncertain provenance. Then she stepped aside.

* * *

The cottage interior was clean in the way of rooms cleaned by someone with nothing left to clean them for. One chair by the fire had a cushion worn into the shape of a man's shoulders—a shape that would never again be filled, but which Mrs. Bray had evidently declined to reverse, the way one declines to move a grave.

Fen took the straight-backed chair. She took the worn one, which surprised him slightly; he had expected her to surrender it out of some instinct of hospitality. But it was her chair, and she was a woman who knew precisely what was hers.

"You'll be wanting the ledger," she said.

Fen kept his face still, which took effort. "What ledger?"

"Don't insult us both, Mr. Fen." She poured tea without asking whether he wanted it—two cups, though, which he noted: she had expected him, and hospitality was habit, not warmth. "You're the fourth person to come sniffing down that lane since the anniversary notices went up. Two were newspaper boys playing historian. One was a woman from the Commission proper, all ink-stained fingers and a voice like a school bell. And now you." She set his cup down with a click that ended a sentence. "You're the only one who didn't pretend he wasn't."

"The others wanted the story," Fen said. "I want the document."

"And there's the difference, is there?" Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Sit there and drink your tea while I decide how much of a fool you are."

She rose, went to the dresser against the far wall—the good dresser, walnut, the kind of piece families keep through bankruptcies—and did not open it. Instead she laid her hand flat on top of it, the way one lays a hand on the shoulder of a horse that might bolt.

"Elias Bray," she said. "Captain, Fourth Regiment, seconded to the river garrison in the last year of the war. Dead eleven years now, of his heart, in his sleep, which was more than he deserved and less than I'd have chosen for him, if you follow me." She looked at Fen over her shoulder. "You'll know the rest. Everyone wants the rest. The woman on the bridge. The regiment that turned around. The miracle they put on the commemorative plates they sell at the fairs."

"I know the official account," Fen said carefully.

"The official account has my Elias turning his column because his conscience woke up." She said it evenly, without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "Conscience. Elias couldn't find his conscience in a drawer with a lamp. What turned that regiment was three hundred crowns in banknotes and a promise about land grants after demobilization, written in a hand so fine it could only belong to someone who'd never had to scrub anything. The money came in a biscuit tin. I packed that biscuit tin myself when we moved quarters. There were ginger nuts in it the week before. Afterward there were banknotes, and Elias sat up all night counting them and drinking, and in the morning he gave the order, and twenty years later half the country drinks toasts to his conscience."

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside the dog barked once at nothing and thought better of it.

"The ledger," Fen said quietly.

"His pay book, properly speaking. The pages they don't print in the regulations. Elias kept accounts the way misers keep accounts—everything written down, everything owed, everything paid. Including that." Her hand stayed on the dresser. "Date, amount, source, the promise appended in his own words. He wrote it all down because writing it down made it a transaction instead of a treason. That was how his mind worked. A man can forgive himself a transaction."

"And you've kept it for eleven years."

"I've kept *him* for eleven years," she said. "The book comes with him. They're not separable, Mr. Fen, whatever your Commission thinks about documents."

Fen set down his cup. This was the moment he had rehearsed on the walk up the lane, and like all rehearsed moments it now refused to cooperate with the script.

"Then let me tell you why I've come," he said, "and you can tell me what it costs."

* * *

He told her plainly, because she was plainly a woman who smelled varnish at fifty paces. The Commission's authoritative history. The deadline. The sealing of the archive. The petition filed by one Mara Hartwell—here the widow's eyes flickered, the first involuntary movement Fen had seen in her—and the requirement of two named witnesses. Cass Okafor, located. Ruthie Vane, unaccounted for. And beneath all of it, the question the amended record would turn on: why the Fourth Regiment had turned around at the bridge, and whose name belonged to that turning.

"You want the page," Mrs. Bray said when he finished. "The entry. To put in your archive."

"I want the record to be true," Fen said, and heard, even as he said it, how the sentence had changed weight in his mouth over these past weeks. Once it had been a job description. Now it sounded almost like a belief.

"True." She tasted the word and spat it out. "My husband bought a miracle with a biscuit tin and a lie about land grants. That's the truth you're shopping for. Shall I tell you what happens when you buy it?"

"Tell me."

"They take the page. They print it, maybe, in the appendix nobody reads. And every schoolchild who learns the bridge learns the name Bray—not the martyr they've been drinking to, but the bought man. The coward who sold the crossing. Do you know what that does to a street like this one?" She gestured at the window, the lane, the tannery smell drifting faintly even here. "These people drank to Captain Bray for twenty years. Put flowers on his stone. Told their sons about him. And then your corrected record arrives and tells them they were fools, and their grief was spent on a fraud. They won't blame the historians, Mr. Fen. Historians are paper. They'll blame me. The widow who handed over the proof. The woman who sold her own dead."

"That's why you haven't come forward yourself," Fen said. It was not a question.

"That's why four of you have come down my lane and none of you have left with anything." She sat back down. For the first time her hands were not entirely steady, and she looked at them with something like annoyance, as at tools failing on schedule. "So now we arrive at it. What do you offer?"

Fen had prepared offers. Three of them, drafted in his neat field-agent's hand, each more generous than the last, each authorized by no one but himself. He had intended to begin with the smallest and bargain upward, the way one bargains.

What he heard himself say was none of them.

"Your terms," he said. "You hold the document. Name your price."

That startled her. Good, he thought distantly; let us both be off our scripts.

"My price," she repeated. She was quiet a long moment. The kettle, forgotten, ticked as it cooled. "Twenty years ago I watched my husband sell the one thing he had left—his name meant something to those men, whatever else it meant. And for twenty years I've lived inside the purchase. Every toast, every flower, every commemorative plate with the woman on the bridge—I've drunk from that cup too, Mr. Fen. Don't imagine I haven't. It's warm in here. It's very warm, being married to a legend, even a false one. Especially a false one. Nobody looks too hard at a legend's widow." She folded her hands. "Here is my price. You may have the page. You may print it, seal it, read it aloud in whatever marble room you people do your remembering. But the entry names the payment and the promise—it does not name the payer of the other side. Not Elias's name. Strike it. Redact it. Call him 'the officer commanding.' Let the record say a captain was bribed, and let the bribed captain stay a shadow. Because the truth you want—the whole truth, every name nailed down—is not justice. It's just a bigger inventory. And some things in an inventory should be allowed to stay in the dark, or the dark stops being mercy and starts being just another room with the lights on."

Fen sat with that. He thought of his inconsistency reports, stacked in his lodging like sedimentary rock—each one a small excavation of somebody's buried life. He thought of Mara Hartwell's original registration, and the name abandoned under it, and what he intended—or no longer intended—to do with it. He thought: *everyone I meet is bargaining with truth. The widow wants a redaction. Hartwell wants witnesses. Voss wants her myth. Even I—*

Even he wanted, he realized, for the record to absolve him of having served it badly for so long.

"A redaction," he said slowly, "in an official history. You understand that's the very thing I've spent weeks fighting. Erasure dressed as procedure."

"There's a difference," Mrs. Bray said, "between erasing a man and letting a dead man keep his coat on. Your Commission erases people who were never allowed to exist. I'm asking you to leave one alone who'd give anything not to exist the way you'd make him." She leaned forward. "You think truth is a thing you dig up and it's pure because it was underground. It isn't. Truth comes up dirty like anything else, and somebody has to decide which stains to wash and which ones are the point. That's not corruption, Mr. Fen. That's just the work."

"The Commission will want a full name. Corroboration standards. They'll—" He stopped. He heard himself arguing for rigor and understood, with a lurch, that rigor and cruelty were sharing a coat again.

"The Commission," Mrs. Bray said, "seals its doors in nine days, if the newspaper boy who came round was telling it right. So here is how this goes, and then you'll drink your tea and go. You bring me the amendment language before you file it. In writing. The entry reads: payment received, sum stated, source stated as 'an intermediary of the reform committee,' officer unnamed. If the page says that, you leave with it and I never see you again. If it doesn't, I burn it, and the bridge stays a miracle, and you can spend your nine days explaining to your superiors how the authoritative history's cornerstone is a ginger-nut tin that no longer exists."

Fen looked at his tea. He thought about the word *intermediary*. He thought: Edith Voss's committee raised funds that year; funds moved in ways committees did not minute; the fine handwriting on the land-grant promise could belong to anyone who'd never had to scrub anything, and the list of such hands in this affair was short enough to be counted on one finger by anyone being honest. He thought: she knows more than the ledger. She has always known more than the ledger. She is not protecting Elias. She is protecting the shape of a story she can live inside, and she has simply chosen a different shape than Edith Voss chose.

Everyone bargaining with truth. Everyone keeping a drawer.

"Intermediary of the reform committee," he said. "Unnamed officer. Payment and date in full. I'll draft it tonight."

"And the corroboration standard? Your two named witnesses?"

"The ledger corroborates them," Fen said. "Not the other way round. That's the point of documents—they testify when people can't, or won't, or daren't." He paused. "There's a woman named Ruthie Vane in a soldiers' home upriver. The third witness. If she speaks, the record closes complete. If she doesn't—" He found he could not finish the sentence, and Mrs. Bray, watching him fail, nodded as if he had finished it perfectly.

"If she doesn't," the widow said, "then two women and a dead man's arithmetic will have to be enough. It usually is. Arithmetic always shows up; it's the living who go missing." She finally opened the dresser drawer—only wide enough to show him a corner of oilcloth, a glimpse of a pay book's cracked spine, nothing more—and closed it again. "You'll have it when the language is agreed. Not before. Don't look wounded, Mr. Fen; you'd have done the same in my chair."

"Yes," Fen said. "I would."

He drank his tea. It was strong and oversteeped and exactly right for a house where hospitality was a form kept out of habit. She asked him nothing about himself, which he found he was grateful for; he was not sure what he would say if anyone asked him, anymore, what side he was on.

At the door, with the gray light of the lane in front of him and the worn chair-shape of a dead captain glowing faintly at his back, Mrs. Bray said one thing more.

"The Hartwell woman. The one who filed the petition." Her voice had changed—lower, careful. "When you see her next, tell her the tin had ginger nuts in it the week before. She'll know what it means."

Fen stopped on the threshold. "Why would she know that?"

Mrs. Bray looked at him for a long moment, and behind her eyes the arithmetic ran again—but this time, he thought, it was not calculating what he wanted. It was calculating how much to give away, and to whom, and whether the ledger was the only inheritance in the house.

"Because," she said, "on the morning after the payment, there was a driver waiting at the far end of the bridge with the engine running. And whoever sent that driver sent the tin. Tell her the ginger nuts are remembered kindly. And then, Mr. Fen—you'd best hurry home and write your amendment. Because if the Hartwell woman reaches the widow's price before you do, I'll be selling to her instead, and her terms will cost your Commission rather more than a redaction."

Fen walked down the lane with the tannery smell rising and the dog declining to accompany him, and by the time he reached the corner he was already composing the amendment in his head—*payment received, sum stated, source: an intermediary of the reform committee*—and trying not to hear, underneath the officialese, the sound of another drawer opening somewhere in the city, and another price being named.
