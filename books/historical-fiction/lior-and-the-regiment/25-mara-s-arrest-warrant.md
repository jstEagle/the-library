## 25. Mara's Arrest Warrant

The warrant arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside an envelope that smelled of pipe smoke and cheap glue, and Mara knew what it was before the paper cleared the envelope because Mr. Aldous Fen had done her the courtesy of using good stationery.

They were in the kitchen of the Voss house when it came, the hour between breakfast and Edith's first appointment, when the light came slantwise through the east window and made the copper pots glow like something holy. Marta the cook was at market. The house held only the two of them and the ticking of the hall clock, and Mara stood at the counter with a paring knife and three apples, doing the small domestic arithmetic that had become the shape of their days: two apples for Edith's lunch, one for the driver's pocket, peeled thin because Edith liked them peeled thin.

The knock came at the side door—the servants' entrance, as though whoever knocked already understood the hierarchy of the house and wished to be precise about it. Mara set down the knife. Through the frosted pane of the door they saw a figure in a gray coat, hat brim low, and for one absurd moment they thought: *census again*, thought: *another decade's questions*, before understanding settled into their chest with the cold weight of a stone dropped down a well.

Not the census. The Commission.

The man at the door was young, younger than Fen by fifteen years, with the particular blankness of someone who has been given a job to do and has decided not to have feelings about it. He did not offer his name until Mara asked twice. "Corporal Hesse," he said finally. "Office of Records Integrity." He held out the envelope with both hands, formally, like a boy presenting a school prize, and there was something almost apologetic in the gesture—as if he hoped Mara would understand that none of this was his idea, which of course it wasn't, which of course never is.

"You're required to acknowledge receipt," Hesse said. "There's a line at the bottom."

Mara read the line. It asked for a signature and the time of receipt, and beneath both, in smaller print, it asked for *relationship to addressee*, and Mara understood that even now, even here, the paperwork wanted to know what to call them—driver, ward, associate, *person of interest*—and could not decide, and so left the decision, like everything else in this affair, to the person being catalogued.

Mara signed. Wrote *employee*. Watched Hesse walk down the gravel path with his collar up against the wind, and did not close the door until he had turned the corner by the hedge, because some animal part of them wanted to watch his back the whole way, the way you watch a dog you suspect of biting.

Then they opened the letter standing in their own kitchen, apples forgotten, knife still wet on the board.

It was not dramatic. That was the first thing Mara noticed, and perhaps the cruelest. No wax seal, no boldfaced threats. Just a typed page under a letterhead Mara had seen once before, on Fen's desk, the eagle-and-scales of the Historical Commission rendered in ink so pale it looked embarrassed to be there.

*RE: Hartwell, M. — Registration No. 4471-C (War Years). Notice of Reinstatement of Inquiry.*

*Pursuant to ongoing review of inconsistencies identified during the National Census of [year], the Commission hereby reopens Case File 4471-C, concerning registration irregularities and alleged unauthorized absence from assigned regimental post during the period of active hostilities. The original charge of desertion, suspended under Provision 9 of the Demobilization Act, is restored to active status effective immediately. The subject will present themselves for formal interview at the Office of Records Integrity within forty-eight (48) hours of receipt of this notice, or arrangements will be made for collection.*

*Collection.* They had put it so gently. As though Mara were a parcel that had gone astray, a library book overdue twenty years, something to be collected rather than seized.

Mara read it three times. On the third reading their hands were steady enough to notice the detail that mattered most, the detail buried in the bureaucratic sediment like a coin in a riverbed: the case file number. 4471-C. Not a new number. The old one. Fen's number—the number Fen had recited to them across his lodging-house table weeks ago, that night in his narrow room with its single window and its stacks of inconsistency reports, when he had shown them their own wartime registration and watched their face while they read the name they had buried.

*Corren,* the registration said. *Aldric Corren.* A name that belonged to a person who had died somewhere between the bridge and the coast road, a name Mara had shed like a coat soaked through, and here it was again, resurrected by men who preferred legends to people, stamped and filed and *restored to active status*.

The apples browned on the board. Mara peeled one anyway, because the hands needed occupation or they would shake, and ate it standing up, tasting nothing, and thought: *forty-eight hours.*

* * *

Edith was in the study when Mara brought the letter to her, because Mara brought it to her—of course they did, that was the whole architecture of their life, every document crossing the threshold of that house passed through Mara's hands first, and had passed through them edited, softened, sometimes silently destroyed. For twenty years Mara had been the filter between Edith Voss and the world's paper. Now they laid the Commission's letter on the blotter beside her unfinished speech drafts, unfiltered, unedited, and watched her read it.

Edith read quickly; she always had. But halfway down the second paragraph her eyes stopped moving, and Mara saw her go back, and saw her lips form the words *desertion* and *collection* without voicing them, and then she sat very still in her chair the way she did when she was deciding how large a thing was going to be.

"They can't do this," she said at last. "Provision 9 was a general amnesty. Every soldier who walked away in the last winter of the war—there were thousands. Tens of thousands. They were all pardoned in the reconciliation."

"Yours was," Mara said. "If you'd ever registered one."

The silence that followed was not angry. It was worse than angry. It was the silence of a door opening onto a room both of them had agreed, wordlessly, over two decades, never to enter—and finding it now flooded with daylight, every piece of furniture visible.

"Mara." Edith took off her reading glasses, which she did only when she wanted to look at someone without the mediation of glass, without any surface between herself and them. "You told me once, the first winter you worked for me, that your war papers were in order. You said it the way people say things they need me to believe."

"I needed you to believe it because I needed the position." Mara kept their voice level. "And you needed a driver who didn't ask why a celebrated pacifist kept a locked drawer in her desk. We were honest with each other in exactly the ways that suited us."

"That's not fair."

"No," Mara agreed. "Very little of this is fair. That appears to be the design."

Edith rose and went to the window, hands clasped behind her back in her public posture, the posture from the photographs, and Mara realized with a small cold shock that she did it unconsciously, that even alone in her own study with no audience but a driver she was always slightly on stage, that the legend had grown through her like roots through a wall until separating the woman from it might bring the whole structure down.

"Fen did this," Edith said. "I sat in my own parlor and answered his questions about flower shows and he sat there knowing—" She stopped. Turned. "Did you know he had your file? Before I interviewed him? Did you sit in that car afterward and say nothing?"

"I found out after. And yes. I said nothing." Mara met her eyes. "Because I thought he wanted the truth, Edith. Because for a few weeks I believed the Commission actually wanted witnesses instead of decorations. That was my error. Apparently it's also my crime."

"It's not a crime to have been—"

"A deserter?" The word came out flat and strange, a coin from another country. Twenty years Mara had not said it aloud. "Yes. It is, actually. That's rather the point of the charge. I left my post in the last winter. I walked away from a regiment that was feeding boys of seventeen into a line that existed only to be fed into. I don't repent it. But I ran from the consequences, changed my name, and let a woman with a famous house hire me under a fiction, and now the fiction has come home to roost wearing an official seal." They picked up the letter, refolded it along its crease. "Forty-eight hours. If I present myself for interview, they'll take my statement, and the statement will be whatever their superiors want it to be, because Fen told me himself—he's not compiling history anymore, he's compiling corrections. The bridge stays the way the pamphlets tell it. Three women becomes one woman, and the one woman is you, and everyone who remembers otherwise is an inconsistency to be resolved."

"And if you don't go?"

"Then they collect me. From wherever I am." Mara allowed themselves one bitter half-smile. "Though I imagine they'd prefer not to arrest the personal driver of Edith Voss on her own front steps. Bad optics. Even for men who've stopped caring about anything but optics."

Edith crossed the room then, and for a moment Mara thought—hoped, shamefully, after all these years—that she would take their hand, or say something unguarded, something from before the legend, from the early winters when the two of them had been merely a woman with a cause and a stranger with a secret sharing a kitchen table at midnight. Instead she said, quietly, "We'll fight this. There are lawyers. There are people who owe me—"

"There are people who owe the *legend*," Mara said. "That's the trouble. You taught me that yourself, in the greenhouse." They had argued there weeks ago—truth against necessity, the myth that funded schools and shelters and the vote itself weighed against the three women at the bridge—and the argument had never ended, only paused, the way storms pause. "Don't spend your capital on me. Spend it where it buys something. I have forty-eight hours and a choice, and I'd like to make it myself."

"What choice? You have no choice. Either you submit to their interview or—"

"The choice," Mara said, "is what I do with the hours." And they picked up the car keys from the hook by the study door, and Edith understood, and her face did something complicated that Mara chose not to read.

"You're going to Cass," Edith said.

"I'm going to find Ruthie," Mara said. "Cass already knows where she stands. It's Ruthie who's been quiet as snow for twenty years, and snow melts, Edith. Eventually. If someone stands near it long enough." They paused at the door. "Upriver. Soldiers' home past Harrow Bend. Fen mentioned it in passing once, thinking I wasn't listening. Men like him always think no one listens."

"Mara." Edith's voice caught them at the threshold. "Whatever you decide to do with those hours—do you understand what it costs? If they take you, they won't give you back quickly. And if you testify, they'll bury you under the charge and call it procedure, and I will stand at podiums for the rest of my life knowing what I traded."

"I know what it costs." Mara turned the keys over in their palm. "I've known what visibility costs since I was nineteen years old watching a man in a gray uniform write my name in a ledger. The question was never the price. The question is whether the thing you buy with it is worth handing to anyone after you." They looked back at her once. "Get the diary ready. Whichever drawer it lives in. If this goes where I think it goes, we're going to need everything true that either of us owns."

And they left her standing among her speech drafts, in the slanting light, a woman holding a letter that had just made her myth-maker's hands tremble.

* * *

The garage was cold and smelled of oil and leather, the way it had for twenty years, and Mara found the ritual of preparation steadying: checking the water, the tires, the fuel gauge, wiping the windscreen clear of the morning's damp. The motorcar was the one space in the world that had always been theirs entirely—not Edith's house, not the movement's platforms, but a machine they maintained with their own hands and drove with their own judgment, mile after mile, invisible behind the wheel the way they had learned to be invisible everywhere.

Except they weren't invisible anymore. That was the whole lesson of the last months, arriving all at once now, the way costs always arrive—not gradually, not in installments, but in a single lump sum presented by a polite corporal at the servants' door. For twenty years invisibility had protected them: the desertion charge couldn't find a man named Aldric Corren if Aldric Corren had dissolved into a driver called Mara Hartwell. But visibility had a price tag too, and the Commission had finally read the meter.

Mara sat in the driver's seat with the engine off and let themselves feel it—really feel it—for perhaps two minutes, which was all they permitted. Fear, yes. Beneath it, unexpectedly, something like relief. The double life had weighed more than they'd admitted, all these years: the constant small translations, *Hartwell* for *Corren*, the practiced shrug when war came up at dinner tables, the careful forgetting. Whatever happened in the next forty-eight hours, the hiding was over. The hiding had cost more than arrest could.

They thought of Cass Okafor at the mill gate, saying: *The record is theirs. Why hand them more of us?* And of Ruthie somewhere upriver, silent as snowfall. And of Fen, whom Mara did not hate—had never quite managed to hate him, because underneath the gray functionary there was a man who kept files on heroism and called them inconsistencies, and a man like that was a door left ajar, and doors left ajar were how Mara had survived everything.

Two days. Forty-eight hours minus the hour already spent peeling apples and signing receipts.

Mara started the engine. It caught on the second try, coughed, settled into its steady workmanlike rumble, and they backed out of the garage into a morning that looked entirely ordinary—hedges dripping, a milk cart two streets over, gulls arguing above the roofline—and drove toward the mill district to pick up a laundress who had every reason to refuse them, and beyond her, upriver, toward a soldiers' home full of men who had stayed at their posts and women who had been erased from theirs.

Behind them the house grew small in the mirror. Somewhere in its study a diary waited in a drawer beside a pinch of ash. Ahead, checkpoints, a clerk with a camera, a clock running.

Mara drove faster than they usually did, and did not care who saw.
