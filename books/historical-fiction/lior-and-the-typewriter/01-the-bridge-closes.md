## 1. The Bridge Closes

My father used to say that a town is a sentence, and the bridge is the only punctuation it can afford. Everything before the bridge is a clause; everything after it, a rumor. He said this while cutting type, of course, because my father never said anything worth saying unless his hands were busy. Idle hands, in our trade, go straight to philosophy.

I was thinking about this on the last market day before winter, because the market square had gone quiet in a way that made my teeth ache. Not empty—never empty—but quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone important has just stopped speaking.

The stall-keepers still shouted their prices. The fishmonger's wife still argued with the cooper about a barrel she swore leaked. But underneath all of it ran a new sound, or rather an old sound wearing new clothes: people talking with their heads bent together, voices pitched just under the threshold where words become legible. A whole town whispering in the same key. You could set that in type, if you had enough sorts. You'd need a font of nothing but italics.

I sat at the bench by the south window, graver in hand, cutting a lowercase *e* into a blank of type-metal, and I let the whispers come to me the way they always did. That is the unadvertised benefit of being the woman at the print shop: nobody tells you anything directly, but everybody assumes you already know everything. A printer's daughter is a confessor who takes no vows and grants no absolution. People leaned over my counter all morning to buy paper and left having deposited things—names, dates, the shape of a column of smoke seen from the far bank three nights ago.

"The revolution," Herr Brandt said, buying a ream of foolscap he did not need, "has reached the other side of the river."

"Has it," I said, and cut my *e*.

"They burned the tollhouse at Kessel. My cousin saw it. Or his wife saw it. Or someone's wife." He lowered his voice to the register men use when they believe they are being discreet. "They say the garrison will close the bridge."

"The bridge closes every winter."

"Not like this." He paid in coins he counted twice, which told me more than his cousin's wife ever could. "Not weeks early. Not with soldiers on both ends."

I thanked him for his custom and went back to my *e*, which was coming out slightly crooked, the way things do when your hand knows something your face refuses to admit.

Here is what I have learned about dread: it dresses itself in routine, because routine is the only coat it owns that doesn't attract attention. All day I did exactly what I do every day. I cut type for the almanac pages due next month. I sorted the case of fourteen-point bodoni that some apprentice—I suspected Lior, years ago, and suspected him still across four years and a river—had left in cheerful disorder. I inked the proofing press and pulled two proofs of a funeral notice for old Frau Haller, may she rest, though between us her notice had been composed so many times it read less like grief than like typography.

And through all of it, the whispering continued. The revolution reached the far bank. The bridge would close. The garrison had doubled its watch. A man from the customs house had been seen carrying papers with red seals, and red seals meant either taxes or decrees, and lately there was little difference.

By evening the market stalls had folded themselves away, and the town settled into its winter posture: shutters half-closed, chimneys smoking, everyone pretending they weren't watching the road that led down to the water.

* * *

I lit the lamp in the composing room around seven, because darkness in a print shop feels less like rest than like neglect. The shop is my father's in every way that matters and mine in every way that counts—the deed says one thing, the ledgers another, and the smell of lead and ink says a third thing entirely, which is the truest of the three. Officially, Master Ehrlich owns the business and I am the widow-of-the-trade who keeps the bench. Unofficially, Master Ehrlich owns the debts and I own the hands, and we have both agreed not to examine the arrangement too closely.

That night I wasn't composing anything official. I was cutting a special sort—a private alphabet, really—that I keep in a case at the back of the room, behind the job cases, behind the stack of spoiled calendars no one has wanted since the year of the bad harvest. It isn't forgery, exactly. It's more like... preparedness. There are punches in my father's cabinet that were never catalogued, faces that exist in no specimen book, ligatures designed for names that appear in no parish register. My father was a man who believed that every alphabet should have a spare key hidden under the mat, and he built his accordingly.

I was fitting a *g* into the new face when I heard the boots.

You learn to hear boots in a garrison town the way sailors learn weather. These were regulation boots, four of them, unhurried, and they stopped—not passed, but stopped—in front of the shop.

I put down the graver. I did not blow out the lamp, because a lamp going out at the sight of soldiers announces more than a lamp burning ever could. Instead I walked to the front room at the pace of a woman who has nothing better to do than look out her window, which is even true, in a way.

Two soldiers stood at my door. One held a lantern; the other held a board, a hammer, and a folded paper sealed in red wax. Red seals. Taxes or decrees, and I'd have bet my father's entire cabinet it was the latter.

The one with the hammer was young—young the way boys get sent to garrison towns, all jaw and uncertainty—and he fumbled with the nail while his companion read the door as if checking the address against orders. Then the paper went up, four strokes of the hammer, each one landing in my chest like a proof pulled too hard.

*By order of the District Command,* it began, in type I recognized immediately, because I had set it myself not six months past: the garrison's standing order form, the one they kept locked in their own office, the one whose spare copy lived in a drawer forty feet from where I stood. *Effective upon publication, the bridge over the River [blank] shall be closed to all civilian traffic until further notice. Crossing without written authority constitutes treason against His Imperial Majesty and will be punished accordingly.*

Until further notice. Weeks early. With the word *treason* set in the same size as *bridge*, as if they were neighbors.

The soldiers walked off toward the tavern, their boots receding in the particular rhythm of men whose work is done and whose supper is not. I watched them go. Then I watched the paper. It hung slightly crooked, nailed by a boy whose hands hadn't learned the trade yet, and I thought—with a calm that frightened me more than any panic could have—that I could have set that decree better. Tighter leading. A proper em-dash before the final clause. If you're going to close a river, close it beautifully.

Then the calm cracked, just at the edge, the way ice cracks at the edge first, quietly, where nobody's looking.

Because here is the thing I had been not-thinking since Herr Brandt's coins clicked on my counter: the far bank was where Lior had gone. Four years ago, when the trouble started and the pamphlets got dangerous and my father died with his punches clean and his affairs in the order of a man who knew, Lior crossed that bridge with a printer's bag over his shoulder and a warrant with his name on it waiting somewhere behind him. And before he went—years before, back when we were only an apprentice and a bench-girl with ink to our elbows—we had made each other a promise beside a pot of cooling type-metal, the kind of promise children make because they cannot yet imagine the price of keeping them.

*If you ever need hiding,* I had said, *this shop keeps you.*

I had meant it then. I had even, in the cowardly way of people who mean things comfortably, assumed I would never be asked.

I looked at the decree on my door. I looked upriver, where the bridge lamps were already changing—one by one, the far bank going dark as the garrison doused what it controlled. Somewhere beyond that darkness, on the wrong side of a closing river, a man I had promised things to was either safe, or running, or lying in a ditch with his blood soaking into snow that no one would find until spring.

The lamp flickered in the composing room behind me. My *g* waited unfinished in the stick. And the shop, my father's shop, with its hidden punches and its uncatalogued alphabets and its floorboards I suddenly found myself measuring with my eyes—my shop sat very still, the way a house does when it is listening.

Winter had come early this year. And something told me, in the voice my father used for the things he was surest of, that I would not be spending it alone.
