## 2. A Promise Made in Peacetime

The trouble with promises, I have since learned, is that they are cut in metal and not in paper. Paper burns. Paper can be soaked in the river until the ink gives up and floats away like a drowned moth. But a promise made over molten type-metal—poured at exactly the temperature my father insisted upon, no hotter, no cooler—is cast. It holds its shape. You can melt it down again if you're ruthless enough, but you will never quite get the same letters back.

I was thinking about this on the third night after the bridge closed, while I trimmed the burrs off a new set of eights with a graver older than I am, when someone knocked three times on the shop door. Then twice. Then once more.

That was our knock. Ours.

My hands did something interesting then: they went perfectly still, the way hands do when the body understands a thing before the mind consents to it. The candle guttered. Somewhere across the square, a patrol laughed at some joke soldiers find funny, which is to say a joke about other people's misfortune. And outside my door, in the dark, someone who should have been forty miles away or dead was waiting to be let in.

I want to tell you that I hesitated out of caution. That would be tidy. The truth is I hesitated because I already knew what opening that door would cost me, and some cowardly part of me wanted one more minute of being a woman who had merely made a promise instead of a woman who had to keep it.

I opened the door.

* * *

Six years ago, in the summer of '42, the shop smelled of hot metal and linseed oil, and Lior Vasseur was seventeen years old and insufferable about it.

"Your father's punches are better than anything in Vellacourt," he said, holding an m up to the lamplight as though it were a reliquary. "Do you know how rare that is? A provincial cutter who cuts like this?"

"He'd say it's because he's patient."

"He'd say it's because everyone else is lazy." Lior grinned. He was an apprentice at Duval & Sons two streets over, sent to us that afternoon to commission a jobbing font, and he had spent most of the visit doing what he always did, which was asking questions no one had hired him to ask. Why did my father cut his serifs the way he did. Where the type-metal came from. Whether Marguerite Almeida intended to take up the bench herself or whether she was content to sweep around it.

"I intend to take up the bench," I told him, "if anyone asks."

"Has anyone asked?"

"No one has thought to."

"They will," he said, with the absolute certainty of a boy who had never yet been wrong about people, only about his own luck. "You'll be the best cutter on the river, and everyone will pretend they always knew."

We sat by the cooling crucible that evening, after the work was done, because the metal takes hours to set and there is nothing to do in that time but talk. The shop windows were open. Someone was playing a fiddle badly two houses down, and the river smelled like summer, which is to say like mud and possibility. Lior turned a cooled piece of scrap type over in his fingers—a crooked b, a casting failure—and said, apropos of nothing:

"If it ever goes bad—the politics, I mean. If it ever actually comes." He was quiet for a moment, which was unlike him. "If I ever need hiding, your shop would keep me. Wouldn't it?"

I remember being irritated, mostly. Sixteen years old, and he was asking me to sign a contract with no terms, no date, no exit clause. "What kind of question is that?"

"The only kind worth asking in peacetime," he said. "In wartime nobody has to ask. They just run somewhere, and either the door opens or it doesn't." He held out the crooked b. "Cast it into your promise. That's how printers do it, isn't it? What's set stays set."

So I took the crooked b and I pressed it into the soft edge of the cooling metal cake at the bottom of the crucible, where no one would ever look, and I said, "This shop keeps you," and he laughed and called me sentimental, and we went back to arguing about kerning.

A crooked b. Of all the letters. My father found it years later, puzzled over it, and I told him it was a flaw I liked, and he nodded and said flaws you like are called character, and that was the last real conversation we had before his heart quit on him mid-winter with a graver still warm in his hand.

The b is still there. Under the crucible stand, in the corner of the founding bench. I checked it the night the bridge closed. Some habits are prayers.

* * *

Now, back to the door.

He nearly fell through it. That is the honest accounting: I opened the door and Lior Vasseur fell against me smelling of woodsmoke, river water, and blood, and I got an arm under him and hauled him inside and shut the world back out behind us.

They had worked him over—I could see that much by candlelight. His left eye was swollen to a slit, there was a gash above his ear that had been bound with a strip of shirt torn off somebody's shirt, and his breathing had the wet rattle of a man whose ribs had lost an argument. But it was the fever that frightened me more than the wounds. Wounds are local. Fever travels.

"Marguerite." He said my name like a man testing whether a bridge would hold.

"You absolute fool," I whispered. "There were notices. Your face is on them. Half the garrison could draw you from memory."

"That's why I came at midnight." He tried to smile; it cost him. "You said—the shop—"

"I know what I said."

He sagged against the doorframe. "I didn't know where else—" and then his legs simply resigned their post, and I caught him, barely, both of us going down together onto the cold flagstones of the composing room with all the grace of two sacks of flour.

* * *

Here is a fact about print shops that saved his life that night: they are built like strongboxes. My father kept the valuable stock—finished matrices, the good paper, the coin box—under the floor of the composing room, beneath a section of boards he'd laid loose over a shallow brick-lined vault. He showed me once, years ago, with the offhand air of a man showing me where the candles were kept. "If anyone ever comes with bad intentions," he said, "you'll be surprised how few of them think to look down."

It took me twenty minutes to get Lior into that vault. Twenty minutes of dragging him across boards that groaned like a congregation, of stopping every time the patrol passed, of pressing my hand over his mouth when the fever made him moan. He weighed nothing and everything. By the time I had him settled on the old sacking with a blanket over him and a shuttered lantern within reach, my arms were shaking and my dress was filthy and I had never in my life been more afraid.

"Marguerite." He caught my wrist as I stood to replace the boards. His good eye found mine in the dark. "If they come—if it costs you—don't pay it. Promise me that part too."

"No," I said. "One promise per lifetime. That's the rate."

And because I am my father's daughter, and because sentiment must always be disguised as commerce in this family, I added: "Stay quiet. Stay still. The fever breaks in two days or it doesn't, and either way you don't make a sound unless I knock our knock. Three, two, one. Do you understand me?"

"Three, two, one," he repeated, and closed his eye.

I laid the boards back over him. From above, the floor looked like any floor. From below, if you knew where to look, there was a knothole the size of a coin through which a little light fell, and through which, I suppose, a little hope did too.

Then I banked the fire, put my father's gravers back in their roll, sat down at the bench, and picked up the crooked b's twin from the day's casting failures.

Outside, the snow had started again. Somewhere beyond the river, the revolution was sharpening itself. And beneath my feet, breathing as quietly as a man can breathe with cracked ribs, lay the promise I had made in peacetime—set, cast, holding its shape.

I had perhaps four days before Herr Brandt next inspected the accounts. Four days before the garrison's requisition order for the press arrived, as orders do. Four days to learn, properly, the trade my father had left me:

the cutting of faces that were never meant to be found.
