## 3. What the Type Cases Hold

My father used to say that a punch was the most honest tool a man could own. It cuts one letter and only that letter, he'd say. It cannot flatter. It cannot lie. It can only be what it is, over and over, until the metal wears smooth.

He never met a garrison officer.

I had been cutting travel papers for nine days when Captain Vasseur walked into my shop with snow on his boots and requisition orders in his gloved hand. Nine days of forging passes for a man who did not officially exist, using the very punches my father had sworn could not lie. I like to think Papa would have understood. He also said rules were for people with nothing better to do than follow them.

"Madame Almeida." Vasseur pronounced it correctly, which I found more alarming than if he'd mangled it. "The garrison requires your press."

"For what purpose, Captain?"

He set the order on my father's old workbench, smoothing it flat with one hand as though it were a gift he was unwrapping for me. "Wanted notices. The agitators on the far bank have names now, and names require ink." He smiled. He had the kind of smile that had been issued to him along with his uniform. "You'll be serving your town, Madame. Your bridge. Your river."

"My river," I repeated.

"The Emperor's river," he corrected pleasantly. "But we needn't quarrel about ownership this close to Christmas."

* * *

From beneath the floor of the composing room, where the heat from the stove seeped down through the boards like a small mercy, Lior heard everything.

I know because he told me afterward, in the dark, in the voice of a man describing his own funeral arrangements. He heard Vasseur's boots cross the room. He heard the officer run one finger along the type case—the very case whose false bottom concealed the hatch to Lior's hiding place—and remark that the shop kept its tools in fine condition. He heard me laugh. Apparently I laughed. A small, dry, professional laugh, the laugh of a woman discussing type with a customer.

"I thought you'd rehearsed it," Lior said. "That laugh. Like a printer pulling a proof—clean, no smudges."

"It was rehearsed. In my head. For four seconds."

"Well. It worked."

It had worked, but barely, and we both knew what the finger on the case meant: the hunt had moved into our house. Every notice Vasseur ordered printed would pass through my hands, and among those faces would be—

"You should see it," I told him that night, holding a candle low over the hatch so the light wouldn't show at the windows. "Your face. They've done you in an engraving. You look very stern. Very revolutionary."

"What does it say?"

"*Lior Anselm. Printer's apprentice. Enemy of public order.*" I read it twice, letting the words sit on my tongue like coins of bad metal. "They got your age wrong."

"They always do."

"And they spelled your name with an *f*."

For the first time in nine days, he laughed—a real one, brief and cracked, the laugh of the boy who had once fallen into the cooling trough of type-metal because he was showing off his grip strength to me. "An *f*. My mother will be furious."

"Your mother is three hundred miles away and thinks you're dead."

"Yes," he said quietly. "Well. So does everyone else. That's rather the point."

* * *

The trouble with hiding a man in a print shop is that a print shop has no shadows worth the name. Everything in it exists to be seen clearly. Ink is blackness made obedient; paper is whiteness made useful. There is nowhere in such a room for anything ambiguous, and Lior was becoming increasingly ambiguous—a fever that came and went, a cough he buried in his sleeve, a man-shaped silence in a building full of honest noise.

And then there was Herr Brandt.

The owner. Or rather, the owner since my father died—the man whose name was on the deed while my hands did the work, an arrangement that had suited us both for six years because Brandt wanted the profits and I wanted the bench, and neither of us asked questions about the pamphlets that went out the back door for whichever faction paid. Brandt was not a coward. Cowards are at least consistent. He was something worse: a careful man in an era that punished carelessness and rewarded nothing else.

He began noticing things the way he noticed everything—sideways, through ledgers.

The ink inventory ran short. The paper stock, thinning. The stove burning hotter than a shop alone required. And once—I saw him see it—a second cup beside the washbasin upstairs, still damp.

"Marguerite." He stood in the doorway of the composing room with his ledger under his arm like a breviary. "We are printing wanted notices for the garrison."

"We are printing what pays."

"One of these men—" He laid the proof sheet on the bench. Lior's face looked up at us both, stern and misspelled. "One of these men apprenticed here. Under your father. I remember his hands."

"People's faces change."

"Not their hands." Brandt tapped the engraving, right where the artist had drawn Lior's fingers curled around a printing frame—an apprentice's habit, burned into muscle memory, reproduced by some far-off engraver working from a description. "Not their hands, Marguerite. And yours have been shaking for a week."

I looked at him. He looked at me. Between us lay six years of mutually profitable silence, and I could feel it stretching, creaking, deciding whether to hold.

"Say what you've come to say, Herr Brandt."

He closed his ledger. That was the terrible part—he didn't open it, didn't threaten, didn't name a price. "I've come to say nothing," he said. "Nothing is what I'm very good at saying. But you should know that Captain Vasseur counts the copies of every notice we print against the paper we report using. He is a man who counts things." He turned to go, then paused. "Whatever is under the composing-room floor, Marguerite—it is using up my paper faster than arithmetic allows."

* * *

Lior took the news better than I did. He had a soldier's relationship with bad information by then: receive it, fold it small, put it somewhere it doesn't weigh.

"So Brandt knows."

"Brandt suspects. There's a difference."

"In my experience the difference is about four days." He sat on the edge of the hatch, legs dangling into his own tomb, looking up at me with the candle between us. "You should turn me in."

"Don't be stupid."

"I'm serious. You burn the papers, tell them I broke in, that you found me, that you feared for the shop. Vasseur would believe it. Women fear things; it makes them credible to men like him." He said it without bitterness, the way you'd describe weather. "You keep the shop. You keep the bench. You inherit what you were supposed to inherit."

I knelt at the edge of the hatch and looked down at him—at the hollows the fever had carved, at the bandage on his shoulder I'd changed that morning, brown at the edges despite the boiling of the linen. Six summers ago he'd stood at this same spot above this same floor, showing off, showing off, always showing off, and I'd promised him the shop would keep him if ever he needed keeping. I had been sixteen and the promise had cost nothing, the way promises made in peacetime cost nothing until peacetime ends.

"I didn't promise your safety to save the shop," I said. "I promised the shop to keep you safe. It's a different sentence, Lior. Same words, different order."

"That's not how sentences work."

"That's exactly how sentences work. Ask any typesetter." I blew out the candle before he could argue, because arguing with Lior was a luxury and luxuries were running short. "Two weeks. Maybe less. The ice is going gray at the edges—I can see it from the window. When the thaw comes, the patrols move to the banks and the crossings empty. We move on the last night of ice or not at all."

"And until then?"

"Until then, I print wanted notices for the man hunting you, and I make sure every single copy comes off my press slightly blurred." I smiled in the dark, where he couldn't see it and didn't need to. "Vasseur wants the town to recognize your face. I intend to make sure nobody can."

* * *

For six days it almost worked. Blurred eyes, a soft jaw, the engraving dissolving into rumor—men stared at the notices nailed to the bridge gate and saw a stranger, any stranger, no one in particular. Vasseur came twice to complain of quality; twice I blamed the damp, the cold, the paper, the age of my father's press. He could not argue with the press. Men like Vasseur believe machines over people; it is their one virtue and it served me.

On the seventh day, Brandt did not come to the shop.

On the eighth day, a boy came instead, out of breath, with a message that Brandt had gone to the garrison that morning carrying his ledger, and had not yet come out.

And on the ninth day—three days before the last night of ice, two days before the thaw would begin eating the river—Captain Vasseur returned to the shop with four soldiers and a warrant, and stood in my composing room tapping one boot against the floorboards, listening.

Listening for the echo of a hollow space.
