## 4. The Last Night of Ice

There is a particular sound a man makes when he decides to arrest you. It is not a shout. It is a clearing of the throat — small, polite, almost apologetic — as though the whole machinery of the garrison were merely a formality and you a misdirected letter.

I heard it from the composing room floor, where I knelt with my hand pressed flat over the trapdoor, over Lior's fever-warm silence beneath it, while Captain Vasseur stood in the doorway of my father's shop with two soldiers behind him and the requisitioned press at his back still warm from printing his own wanted notices.

"Mademoiselle Almeida," he said. "We've had a report."

"Of what, Captain? The weather?" I kept my voice level, though my knees were beginning to ache against the floorboards. "The ice has been groaning all week. Very unsettling."

"A report," he repeated, with the patience of a man who enjoyed repeating things, "of documents. Forged travel papers. Printed on this press." He let his gaze wander across the shop — the cases, the stone, my father's bench with its punches laid out like surgical instruments — the way a man wanders through a house he already owns. "You understand that harboring an agitator carries penalties beyond confiscation. There are penalties for the person herself."

*The person herself.* As if I were a typographical error to be corrected.

"I cut type, Captain. I don't forge anything. My father taught me that a punch either cuts true or it doesn't. There's no lying in metal."

"No," Vasseur said softly. "But there is in ink."

And then Herr Brandt stepped out from behind the press.

I should have known. Of course I should have known. The owner who had grown suspicious by inches all winter — who counted the sheets, who weighed the ink pots, who asked why the travel papers for the Marcher family bore serifs so like my father's own hand — had gone, in the end, not to the revolutionaries nor to me, but to whichever side would pay his conscience's price. He would not meet my eye. That was the only mercy in it.

"He is below the floor," Brandt said to no one in particular, to the room, to his own reflection in the ink stone. "The composing room. Under the cases."

Vasseur smiled at me then, and it was almost kind, which was worse than cruelty. Kindness from a man holding a warrant means he believes you have nowhere left to go.

"Well then," I said.

Here is the thing about inheritance that nobody tells you: it is not the bench, or the punches, or the press, or even the name over the door. It is the moment your father described to you when you were fourteen and burning your thumb on cooling type-metal — the moment when everything you have made becomes evidence, and the only question left is whether you will be its witness or its accomplice.

My father chose accomplice every time. He printed pamphlets for both sides and called it *balance*. He hid men in the cellar during the last bad year and called it *inventory*. He told me: *Marguerite, paper burns faster than people forgive. Remember which one you'd rather keep.*

I remembered.

The lamp sat on the cutting bench where it always sat, oil still half-full because I am frugal, because frugality is how shops survive winters, because frugality is how shops die. Vasseur took one step toward the trapdoor. Brandt looked away entirely, finally, decisively — a man closing a ledger.

I picked up the lamp and threw it into the paper stock.

* * *

Fire, I will tell you, is faster than soldiers. It is faster than forgiveness too, whatever my father said. The pamphlet stock went up like a breath, and the oil in the rag bin followed, and within ten seconds the shop that had held three generations of Almeidas was a bright orange argument that no captain cared to win. Vasseur swore and grabbed for my arm; I gave him my sleeve instead — wool tears, I recommend it — and went out the back door through the smoke with the heat pressing against my shoulder blades like a hand shoving me toward the river.

Lior was already up. He'd heard the fire before I reached the hatch — of course he had; he'd been lying under those floorboards for eleven days listening to the building breathe. He met me in the alley, gray-faced, one arm pressed against his ribs where the bullet had grazed him in November, and he looked at the flames coming through the windows of the print shop and then at me, and his face did something complicated.

"The papers," he said. "The proofs. Everything—"

"Burns," I said. "That was rather the point."

"The punches—your father's punches—"

"Are brass and steel. They don't burn. They'll dig them out of the ash and find nothing but tools." I pulled his good arm over my shoulders. "Walk. Sentiment later."

We went down through the tannery lane, past the smokehouse where old Ferenc slept through the end of the world as reliably as he slept through everything else, down the frozen goat path to the riverbank. Behind us the town was waking — bells, shouts, bucket lines forming with the doomed optimism of bucket lines everywhere. The bridge stood black against the orange sky, closed since December, its gates chained, its guard post empty now because every soldier in it had run to watch a print shop die.

The ice stretched out before us, white-gray under the stars, and it was talking.

I want to be precise about that, because everyone says the ice *cracks*, and that suggests something sudden, dramatic, a single gunshot sound. This was not that. The ice had spent three days thawing by day and refreezing by night, and it spoke continuously — long groans that traveled under our feet like the building settling, short sharp reports off to the east, a low grinding moan far out near the channel where the current ran strongest. It sounded less like ice and more like a very large animal turning over in its sleep, deciding whether to wake.

"We could wait," Lior said. His breath came in white rags. "For full freeze. One more cold night—"

"There are no more cold nights. And there's no more shop to hide in, in case you missed the part where I burned it down." I tested the edge with my heel. Solid enough to argue with. "Besides, they'll figure out within the hour that neither of us is in the ash. Vasseur counts everything. He'll count us missing."

"You burned down your father's shop," he said quietly. Not a question. A man taking inventory of a debt he couldn't repay.

"My father's shop burned down," I corrected. "Tragic. Terrible draughts in that building. Practically invited it." But my voice caught on the last word, and Lior — feverish, hunted, half-dead — squeezed my shoulder with the hand I wasn't carrying, and we stood a moment at the edge of the river while the ice groaned its long opinion, and neither of us said anything true.

Then we stepped out onto it.

* * *

I will not pretend the crossing was brave or beautiful. We went on foot, apart, spread out the way you spread weight on rotten floorboards, walking wide of the channel's dark seam. Twice the ice spoke directly beneath me — a crack racing past my boot like a sentence being written very fast — and twice I kept walking, because stopping felt more fatal than going. Once, near the middle, Lior went down to his knee through a skin of water over black ice, and I hauled him up by the collar of his coat, and he laughed — actually laughed, a wild, feverish, wonderful sound — and shouted at the sky, at the soldiers, at the burning town:

"She keeps her promises! Ask anyone!"

"Hush," I hissed, hauling him on. "You'll crack the ice and the revolution both."

The far bank came up slowly, then all at once — mud and willow scrub and the first stink of the thaw, which is the smell of everything ending and beginning. When my boots hit soil I nearly fell, my legs refusing to believe solid ground, and I turned back once to look.

The town burned prettily against the last of the dark. The bridge stood untouched, absurdly intact, and I understood with a strange clarity that by tomorrow it would open — the thaw ending the closure, the soldiers crossing south, the war flowing both directions over my father's river. Let them come. Let them sift the ash of the Almeida print shop and find melted lead, warped presses, the ghost of ink. Let them find my father's punches, if brass survives fire — I like to think it does — and know that somewhere across the water, hands that learned on those punches were already cutting new ones.

"What do we do now?" Lior asked. He was shivering, feverish, free. "Everything you had is on that side of the river."

I looked down at my hands. Ink in the creases. Burn blisters. Soot under the nails. Not a printer's hands anymore, exactly. Something else.

"Now," I said, "we learn to write by hand."

Behind us, with a sound like a door closing, the ice let go.
