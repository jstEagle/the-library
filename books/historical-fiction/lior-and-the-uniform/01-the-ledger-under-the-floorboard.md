## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

The river froze in the second week of December, and by Christmas it had become a road.

Sable Ishikawa could see it from the ward window: a broad white plain where men walked and sleighs ran and, on clear evenings, the lamps of the garrison across the water burned like a string of low stars. The ice made enemies of neighbors. It let them visit each other. It also, she had learned, let armies count each other's chimneys from the far bank, which was why the hospital's north-facing shutters stayed closed after dark, and why the orderlies spoke of the frozen river the way farmers speak of weather—constantly, superstitiously, with an eye always on the sky.

She had been at St. Aldric's River Hospital for fourteen months. Long enough to know which floorboards sang under the night matron's boots, long enough to know that the boiler coughed twice before it held, long enough to stop flinching at the sound of a man crying out in his sleep. Fourteen months of lice powder and carbolic, of bandages boiled dry and stiff as bark, of writing letters for boys who dictated slowly because they were choosing between the truth and something their mothers could survive.

History, Sable had decided somewhere in her first month, happened elsewhere. It happened to other people, in other rooms, at distances measured in telegraph wires. Here there was only proximity: the near work of keeping bodies alive one more day, and then another, until the war either finished with them or didn't. She had come to find a strange comfort in that smallness. A nurse did not win anything. A nurse kept things—breathing, pulse, the thin warm line of a life—from being lost. That was all. That was enough.

She told herself this every evening, and every evening it was almost true.

* * *

At eleven o'clock, when the last round was done and the ward had settled into its chorus of breathing and rattling and the occasional wet cough, Sable took off her apron, folded it over the rail at the foot of Bed Nine, and knelt down beside the iron cot in the corner she thought of as hers—not assigned to her, nothing here was assigned to her, but hers the way a habit becomes yours.

Under the floorboard there was a gap the width of two fingers, left by a carpenter in some earlier decade who had cut around a knot of bad wood. Inside the gap, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, lay a ledger. Not a hospital ledger. Those lived behind the matron's lock, ruled and numbered and stamped. This one was a school exercise book she had bought in the autumn of 1915 from the stationer on Cordwainer Lane, fourpence, blue cover going soft at the corners now from handling. On its first page, in her smallest and most careful hand, was written:

*Pvt. Thomas Ellery, 2nd Battalion. Fever, taken 14 October. Not entered.*

And beneath it, the date, and beneath that, the words the intake officer had used when Sable asked—asked once, carefully, in the tone of a woman confirming a clerical matter—why the boy wasn't on the list: *"Died before admission."*

He hadn't. He had died forty minutes after admission, in Bed Six, holding her wrist with a grip that surprised her, saying a name that wasn't his mother's. But the paperwork said otherwise, and the paperwork was what the army would keep, and so Sable kept the other thing.

There were eleven names in the book now. Eleven entries in fourteen months, which sounded like very little until you considered that each one represented a moment when the official record and the truth had parted company, quietly, without ceremony, the way ice parts from a piling. Some omissions were small—a misspelled surname, a regiment transposed—and those she recorded too, because a man whose name is wrong in the record is a man whose mother will write to the War Office and receive back a letter saying no such soldier exists, and she will spend the rest of her life half-believing she dreamed him.

Some omissions were not small.

Sable did not think of herself as brave. She thought of herself as accurate. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole architecture of her nights: bravery required confrontation, witnesses, risk in the open air. Accuracy required only a pencil, a knee pressed to cold floorboards, and the discipline to write *what happened* instead of *what was reported*, and then to close the book and stand up and be, again, exactly what everyone believed her to be—a quiet foreign-born nurse with good posture and no opinions, useful and unremarkable, the kind of woman whose name people forgot even while she was sewing their father's arm back together.

Invisibility, she had found, was not a condition. It was a skill. And like any skill it improved with practice.

* * *

Tonight the ward held thirty-one men. Twenty-nine would live; the boy in Bed Twelve, Marlowe, seventeen and shot through the lung, was the arithmetic nobody said aloud but everybody carried. The stove ticked. Snow came down past the window in fat unhurried flakes, and beyond the glass the river lay white and silent under a lid of cloud, holding its breath the way it had since December.

Sable checked Marlowe's pulse—it fluttered like a moth against her fingers, quick and shallow—and adjusted his blanket, and moved on.

Bed Fifteen: Corporal Dane, sleeping, the amputation stump freshly dressed, his face slack with the particular peace of a man whose worst day is finally over. Bed Seventeen: the two frostbite cases from the pontoon company, hands wrapped in oiled silk, moaning softly in tandem like a pair of badly tuned instruments. Bed Twenty-Two: empty, stripped, the mattress rolled. Bed Twenty-Two had been occupied yesterday by a gunner named Pell who had come in with his abdomen opened by shell fragment and had lasted nine hours, most of them conscious, all of them asking for water he was not permitted. This morning his bed had been stripped and his name had gone into the discharge column of the day-book in Captain Rennick's tidy hand: *transferred.* Transferred where, the paper did not say. The paper did not have to say. The dead are the army's most obedient soldiers; they never contradict a report.

Sable stood a moment at the empty bed. Then she went to her corner, knelt, lifted the board, and unwrapped the oilcloth.

The pencil stub was short enough now that she had to hold it near the end, her fingers cramped into a writer's claw. She wrote: *Gnr. Alfred Pell, Royal Artillery. Wounds, abdomen. Admitted 3 January, died 4 January, approx. 0400. Entered as "transferred."* She added the date and the ward and, because she had begun doing this in November, a line of description—the kind of detail no form ever asked for, the kind that makes a name into a person: *Liked chess. Beat Orderly Fenn twice. Asked for water.*

She looked at the last three words a long time. Then she left them. The ledger was not for comforting anyone. It was for the record, and the record should be ashamed of itself, and shame requires specifics.

She blew on the page until the graphite dried, wrapped the book, slid it home, and pressed the floorboard down with her palm until it sat flush. From the corridor it would look like nothing. From the corridor everything in this hospital looked like nothing; that was the genius of the place, if you wanted to call it genius. Rows of identical cots, identical blankets, men reduced to beds and beds reduced to numbers, and numbers flowing daily into ledgers that flowed monthly into reports that flowed, eventually, into the great official memory of the war, where they would be sorted, summed, printed, bound, and believed.

That was the part that had gotten under her skin, in the beginning, and had never gotten out: *believed.* Someday, Sable understood, someone would open those bound volumes and read them as history. They would read that Private Ellery never existed, that Gunner Pell was transferred, that the convoy of October arrived with full strength intact. And they would believe it, because the handwriting was official and the binding was government issue, and because the only witness who knew otherwise was a woman kneeling on a cold floor at midnight with a pencil stub, whom no one would ever think to ask.

History happens elsewhere, she reminded herself, replacing the board. To other people. You are a nurse. Keep the pulse. Keep the breathing. Let the century take care of itself.

It was a good philosophy. It had held for fourteen months.

* * *

On her way back down the ward she paused at the window, as she always did—one minute, no more, a luxury she permitted herself the way a miser permits himself one coin touched nightly but never spent.

The snow had thinned. Through the breaking cloud the far bank showed itself: the dark hump of the garrison hill, the palisade fence a gray stitch across it, and along the shore a scatter of lights that were not quite the same color as the town's lights behind her—yellower, fewer, arranged differently. Internment camp, the orderlies called it, when they called it anything at all. Enemy aliens, several hundred of them, merchants and sailors and a dentist and God knew who else, swept up in the first panicked months of the war and parked on the far bank like furniture in storage. Sometimes on still mornings smoke from their cookhouses drifted straight across the ice, and the smell of it—strange spices, unfamiliar bread—reached the ward windows and made the wounded men complain about the food with renewed vigor.

Sable watched the far lights and felt nothing in particular. That was the trick of it, she had learned. Feel nothing in particular, about anything in particular, and the days would pass over you like weather.

Her brother Jun had been dead for two years. She had stopped feeling that in particular a long time ago as well—or rather, she had filed it, the way she filed everything, in the deep drawer where nurses keep the things they cannot afford to carry on rounds. *Killed in the early fighting,* the telegram had said, in the language telegrams use, which is the language of erasure: regret to inform, killed in action, expression of sympathy, signature of a stranger. No body recovered. No grave located. Just the word *killed*, doing its work, closing the account.

She had been twenty-two. She had cried for one night, alone, into a towel so the other probationers wouldn't hear, and in the morning she had gotten up and gone on duty and discovered that the world's cruelest gift is that it continues. Somewhere in that first gray week she had made herself a promise that felt, even then, like a betrayal: she would not go looking. She would not write letters to bureaus, would not haunt the casualty lists, would not spend her life pressing her ear to the ground of the war hoping to hear one specific silence. Jun was gone. The record said so. And though she trusted the record less than most people—less than anyone, perhaps, given the contents of the floorboard—there were limits to what a person could audit. You could not check the whole world. You could only keep your own small books true.

So she kept them true. Eleven names. Twelve, counting Pell. A candle's worth of light under a floorboard, in a hospital at the edge of a frozen river, in a war that had already eaten millions of names and showed no sign of indigestion.

Marlowe coughed in Bed Twelve, a deep wet sound, and Sable turned from the window and went to him, and held the cup while he drank, and told him he was doing well, which was the kind of lie nurses are allowed.

* * *

She was asleep within the hour, in the narrow room she shared with Nurse Okafor, and she slept the flat dreamless sleep of the truly tired, and if the river groaned in the night—that long shifting complaint the ice makes when the temperature drops and the water beneath contracts and the whole frozen mile grinds against its own edges—she did not hear it. The river had been talking to itself all winter. Everyone had stopped listening.

Morning came gray and businesslike. The kettle went on. The dressings were changed. Marlowe's fever spiked at ten and broke at noon, and Sister Hale said *hmm* in the tone that meant maybe, and Sable wrote his temperature on the chart and did not allow herself to hope, because hoping was a particular feeling and she had given up particular feelings.

At two o'clock the telephone in the matron's office rang—the field telephone, the one with the crank and the voice like a bee in a jar—and Sable, passing the door with an armful of soiled linen, heard Matron Grell answer it, and heard the words that made her stop walking.

Convoy. Forty stretcher cases, direct from the railhead. Arriving by sled before dark. Prepare the overflow ward.

Forty. The largest convoy since October. Sable stood in the corridor with the linen against her chest and listened to Grell's voice giving orders—beds, hot water, the surgeon to be fetched from his dinner—and felt the familiar machinery of the hospital begin to turn around her, everyone moving, everyone purposeful, the building waking like a body called to labor.

She should have felt dread. Convoys meant blood and exhaustion and the particular horror of triage, deciding with a glance who could be saved and who could only be made comfortable. Instead she felt the thing she always felt, the flat readiness, the nurse's trance: *work is coming, therefore I will work.*

She put away the linen. She counted clean sheets. She set out kidney dishes and safety pins and the little wooden labels that went on each bed frame, blank for now, waiting for names.

She did not yet know that among the forty men coming across the snow that afternoon, bleeding and shivering and guarded, would be one who was not on any list at all—who would arrive carrying, folded inside a bloodstained coat, a letter in handwriting she would know anywhere in the world, in any century, in any life.

The sleds came at dusk, bells jingling, runners hissing on the packed road. Sable stood at the doors with the others, breath steaming, and watched the lanterns swing closer through the blue snow-light, and had no idea that the floor was about to drop out of her ordered world, or that the first crack had already opened—quiet, invisible, running under the ice of everything she believed—

Like a river, remembering spring.
