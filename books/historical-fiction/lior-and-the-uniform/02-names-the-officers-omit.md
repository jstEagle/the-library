## 2. Names the Officers Omit

The convoy brought forty-one men, and the intake list would remember thirty-eight.

Sable understood this the way she understood the river froze from the banks inward—by observation, by repetition, by the slow accumulation of things no one asked her to know. She had been at St. Aldric's River Hospital for fourteen months, long enough to see three convoys come down the ice road with their bells and their lanterns and their cargo of young men folded into blankets like letters no one wanted to open. Long enough to learn the arithmetic.

Forty-one became thirty-eight. Sometimes thirty-eight became thirty-six. The numbers never grew. Wounded men did not multiply on paper; they only diminished, quietly, in the margins of an officer's neat hand, until the ledger of the war showed a loss so much smaller than the truth that even grief could be made to seem efficient.

She had not started her own ledger out of principle. That was the lie she told herself some nights, when she wanted to believe she was better than she was. She had started it out of boredom, or something adjacent to boredom—a restlessness that lived in her hands. History happened elsewhere. It happened in the capital, in the war offices, in dispatches read aloud over breakfast in towns whose names she barely knew. Here at the river's edge there was only the work: the wounds, the fevers, the endless washing of hands, the lamps trimmed low after ten. She was a nurse, which was to say a woman who stood at the doorway between living and dying and held the door for whichever side needed it, and was thanked for neither.

But the names. The names bothered her.

Not morally—not at first. She was too tired for morals in those first months, too busy learning the particular grammar of this hospital: Matron Grell's footsteps, the squeak of the third floorboard outside the pharmacy, the way Dr. Hale said *hmm* when a wound was worse than it looked. What bothered her about the missing names was simpler and more shameful. It offended her the way a miscounted change offends a shopkeeper. Something had been added wrong. Someone had carried a number in his head all day, through the mud and the noise and the cold, a number that meant *I am alive, I made it back*, and then some officer with a clean cuff had subtracted him before the ink dried.

So she began to write them down. Under the loose floorboard beside her cot, in a notebook bought for two coppers from the stationer who also sold ribbon and rat poison. Just names, ranks, regiments where she could get them, dates. A private accounting. She did not tell herself it mattered. She told herself it was a habit, like counting stitches, like checking a pulse twice.

Habits are how cowards pray.

* * *

The sleds arrived at dusk, as they always did, and by lamplight the intake began.

Colonel Ashe did not do the striking himself, not usually. He had a captain for that—Captain Renn, a narrow man with a narrow mustache and a voice like paper being folded. Renn sat at the trestle table in the receiving ward with the master list before him and a pencil sharpened to cruelty, and the stretcher-bearers came to him one by one and gave their counts, and Renn wrote, and sometimes he crossed out.

Tonight Sable worked triage at the far end of the ward, sorting the arriving men into rows: the ones who might be saved tonight, the ones who might be saved tomorrow, the ones for whom the hospital was merely a well-lit place to stop. It was work she was good at because it required no feeling, only judgment, and judgment she had in abundance. Feeling she kept folded away like her second uniform, pressed and unworn.

The boy came in on the fourth sled.

She registered him first as a weight problem—the bearers staggered coming up the ramp, which meant either equipment or a heavy man—and then as a blood problem, because the blanket was soaked dark along its whole left side and blood freezes slower than you'd think, and there was a smell of iron and snow that triage nurses learn to hate. Then she pulled back the blanket and saw that the weight problem was that he weighed almost nothing at all, that the heaviness was the blanket itself and the frozen field dressing wrapped around his middle, and that the face under the cap was fifteen years old if it was sixteen.

"Name," she said, because that was the ritual, and rituals hold the world together.

"Hollis," said one of the bearers, reading off a tag. "Private Hollis, T—Regiment. Took shell fragments at the crossing action. We dressed him at the aid post but—" He didn't finish. Bearers never finished those sentences. They handed them to you instead, along with the blanket.

Sable put her fingers to the boy's throat. There was a pulse. It was fast and thin, a bird's pulse, a pulse making arguments it was going to lose. His abdomen was rigid as a drumhead and his skin was grey and waxy, and she had seen this presentation enough times to know what the next six hours looked like, and they did not look like anything.

"Row three," she called. "Hot bottles. Get me Hale."

She wrote his name on her triage slip—HOLLIS, PVT., T-REG.—and pinned it to his blanket, and moved on, because row four had a man screaming about his leg and row five had a man who was silent in the way that silence is worst.

That was when she heard the pencil.

It was a small sound. That was the thing she would think about later, in the nights when she lay awake listening to the river groan—that the erasure of a human being made such a small sound. A graphite whisper, a flick of Renn's wrist, done almost in rhythm, the way a man crosses items off a shopping list.

She looked up the ward. Captain Renn had finished with the bearers and was now reviewing the slips himself, matching them against the master list, and as he went he struck. Not many. Three, in the space of two minutes. Three little lines drawn through three little names, each one followed by a small notation in the margin that she couldn't read from that distance but knew already, because she had invented it herself in her head months ago: *not received.*

The first name she never learned. The man was already in surgery, his slip carried up with him, and whatever Renn's line did to him happened entirely on paper while the actual flesh of him was being opened under ether two floors above. Perhaps he lived. She chose, that night, to believe he lived, and she logged him anyway, because belief was not evidence and she was keeping evidence.

The second was a sergeant with a crushed foot who would live to complain about the weather for fifty years, and whom someone upstairs evidently decided was one wounded man too many for the monthly count. She got his name from an orderly: Sergeant Oduya. She wrote it down that same night, spelling it twice to be sure.

The third was Hollis.

* * *

She found out at half past eleven, during the night rounds she had volunteered for because volunteering for the bad hours was another way of being invisible—no one suspects the woman who takes the ugly shift; they pity her, which is safer.

Hale had opened the boy up and closed him again within twenty minutes. "Peritonitis," he said, not unkindly, washing his hands. "It was in him before he ever reached us. Hours old." He said it the way he said everything clinical, as though distance were disinfectant. Then, because he was not entirely stone: "He's conscious. You might sit with him."

So she sat.

The boy was in row three under a single lamp, and the ward around him breathed and murmured and coughed, and he lay very straight the way dying boys often do, as though posture could argue with physics. He was trying not to cry. She could see the effort of it in his jaw, the way a swimmer's shoulders shake holding still against a current.

"Can I get you anything?" she asked, knowing the answer.

"My mum," he said, and then, mortified at himself, "No, miss. Sorry. Tea, if there is any."

There was no tea. There was never tea at that hour. She gave him water warmed with a spoonful of sugar from her own ration, and he drank it in small sips like a man pacing himself across a desert, and she thought: *this is the arithmetic.* This is what gets subtracted. Not a number on a list. This. The sugar spoon. The trying-not-to-cry. The mum.

"What's your first name, Private?"

"Tobias," he said. "But everyone calls me Toby."

"How old are you, Toby?"

"Nineteen," he said, with the flawless confidence of the young lying to authority, and something in her chest moved—an inch, no more, a crack no wider than a hair—but she felt it, felt it run through the plastered wall of her neutrality like frost finding a windowpane.

"I was nineteen once," she said. "It's overrated."

He laughed. It cost him; she watched it cost him; she wished immediately and violently that she could take the laugh back and give him the breath instead. But he squeezed her wrist with a hand that was mostly tendons and cold, and said, "You're all right, miss," as if *she* were the one needing comfort, as if the whole apparatus of the world hadn't failed him twice already—once in the field, once at the trestle table.

Because she had checked. While he was in surgery, she had walked past Renn's table, slow, unhurried, a nurse with somewhere to be, and had looked down at the master list with the mild blank expression women are trained to wear, and had seen the line through HOLLIS, P., and the marginal note in Renn's tidy hand, and had understood.

*Not received.* He had been received. She was sitting with him. She could feel his pulse under her fingers, quick and losing, and the paper said he had never arrived.

"Why," she had asked Renn—not then, but in her head, all evening, the question turning over and over like a stone in a stream—*why?* And she knew the answers, because she was not stupid and neither, really, was Renn. Because the convoy had exceeded its expected losses. Because a colonel somewhere had promised numbers. Because a boy of sixteen lied about his age to serve and died of it, and a dead sixteen-year-old who officially enlisted at nineteen is a scandal, whereas a boy who was never received is nothing at all. Paper has that power. Paper is the only country where the war can still be won.

At one in the morning, Tobias Hollis stopped arguing with physics.

She was holding his wrist when it happened. The pulse didn't fade so much as decide, the way a bird decides, mid-flight, to land. His breathing spaced itself out like footsteps getting further away, and then there were no more footsteps, and the ward went on breathing and murmuring around them, and she sat for a moment with a dead boy's hand in hers and felt the anger arrive.

It did not feel the way she had imagined anger should feel. It was not hot. It was cold and extremely precise, and it located itself somewhere behind her sternum like a splinter of ice, and it said, in a voice very much like her own: *They will strike his name tomorrow morning. He will have died nowhere. No one will ever be told where to grieve him.*

His mother would receive a letter eventually—or she wouldn't. That was the true obscenity, Sable thought. Not the death; the war had plenty of death, death was the war's entire industry. The obscenity was the possibility of the mother who waited forever, who could not mourn because mourning required a fact, and the fact had been erased by a pencil.

She closed the boy's eyes. She straightened his blanket. She did what nurses do, the liturgy of the small dignities, and then she walked back down the ward, calm-faced, lamp in hand, past the sleeping men, past the orderly dozing at the desk, down the stairs, along the corridor to her cell of a room, where she knelt and lifted the loose floorboard and took out the notebook.

* * *

She wrote by candlelight, kneeling on the cold boards, coat still on.

*Tobias Hollis, Pvt., T-Regiment. Died 11 February 1916, approx. 1:10 a.m., St. Aldric's River Hospital, peritonitis following shell wounds. Age on his tag, nineteen. Age in his face, sixteen. Struck from intake list by Capt. Renn, same date. Received. He was received.*

Her hand shook on the last word, and she let it. She had spent fourteen months telling herself this book was a habit, a tally, an eccentricity of a tired woman's hands. Lying to yourself works fine until the night it doesn't. Tonight the ledger had changed in her grip—it had become what it perhaps had always been becoming—testimony. A court record for a court that did not exist yet. Evidence against a crime so ordinary no one had thought to outlaw it: the crime of subtraction.

She added the other two. The unnamed man from surgery. Sergeant Oduya, crushed foot, alive as far as she knew, erased regardless. Three lines in her book answering three lines in Renn's. Let him keep his pencil, she thought. She had a floorboard.

And beneath the entries, without quite deciding to, she wrote one more line, small, almost hidden:

*Someone should know these men existed. If not the army, then me. If not now, then later.*

She blew out the candle. In the dark she could hear the river, faint and constant, working at the underside of the ice, patient as bureaucracy. Fourteen months she had stood at this hospital's windows and watched that river and felt nothing but the cold coming off it—history happened elsewhere, she had believed that, she had built her whole quiet life on that belief, and tonight the belief had cracked the way ice cracks, invisibly, from underneath, running outward from a single point of pressure.

A dead boy's laugh. A sugar spoon. *Everyone calls me Toby.*

She lay down and did not sleep. Somewhere across the river there were other lists, other ledgers, other people being subtracted—and she did not know yet that one of them was writing her brother's handwriting, that the ice she listened to hid more than water. All she knew, in the hour before dawn, was that she was angry, and that the anger was not going to cool.

It was going to keep accounts.
