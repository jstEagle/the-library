## 3. A Letter Written in Jun's Hand

The prisoner arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing, because in the winter of 1916 every day arrived like every other day, gray and salt-stained and smelling of carbolic and river ice. But I would remember it as a Tuesday for the rest of my life, because that is what memory does with the worst days—it gives them a name so you can say them out loud later.

I was changing the dressing on a man from the 4th Regiment whose foot had gone bad below the ankle. He was sleeping through it, thanks to the laudanum, and I had learned to work quickly in the dark of the long ward, moving bed to bed by feel and by the small light I permitted myself. The windows were furred with frost. Beyond them the river lay flat and white under the last of the afternoon, and beyond the river there was another country where our war was also their war, and beyond that, everywhere else in the world where history was happening to other people.

That was my belief then: that history was elsewhere. That it happened in capitals and trenches, in ministries and mud, to men whose names were printed in columns, and that those of us at the river's edge lived in the margin where nothing important could reach us. I had built my whole life inside that belief the way a bird builds inside its own song. I record the names, I thought. I keep the truth safe in a floorboard. And the world grinds on elsewhere, enormous and indifferent, and I remain invisible, and invisibility is a kind of safety.

The door at the end of the ward opened and let in a blade of cold air and two shapes: a guard in a greatcoat too heavy for him, and between his hands a man who walked only because the guard's grip decided he would.

"Prisoner," the guard said, as if announcing weather. "Shrapnel. Left side. Colonel Ashe wants him fit for transfer within the fortnight."

"We don't take prisoners," I said. "There's a ward at the garrison."

"The garrison is full." He said it the way men said everything that winter—shrugging, blaming, passing the burden along like a bucket in a fire line. "He's your problem now, nurse. Voss. Emil Voss. Foreign national, interned across the river. He stepped on something that used to be a fence post while they had him clearing debris at the dockyards. Don't let him die before we can move him. Paperwork's a nightmare if they die here."

They laid him on the cot nearest the stove—the one reserved for men expected to be difficult—and left. The ward exhaled. Somewhere down the row a man moaned in his sleep about horses.

Emil Voss was perhaps thirty, hollow-cheeked, with the gray pallor of a man who has been fed on soup and suspicion for two years. His coat was wrong for him—too big, army issue stamped with someone else's number—and beneath it his left side was soaked through and stiffening. He looked at me with an expression I had seen a hundred times on a hundred faces: not fear exactly, but the careful blankness of a man deciding how much of himself it is safe to show.

Then he did something no wounded man had ever done to me.

While I cut away the sodden cloth, while I cleaned and probed and packed the wound while he bit down on the strap I gave him without a single sound—he pressed a folded piece of paper into my hand.

It happened fast, in the moment when my fingers were deep in the wound and both of his hands were free and the guard was a hundred yards away signing a book at the desk. His palm closed over mine around the paper, once, deliberately, and then his hand fell back to the blanket and he was just a patient again, sweating, staring at the ceiling.

I finished the dressing. I washed my instruments. I wrote *Voss, Emil — shrapnel, left flank — stable* in the intake log in my neat, upright hand, and all the while the folded paper burned against my wrist where I had slipped it up under my cuff, small as a moth.

I told myself I would read it at midnight, in my room, under the floorboard, where all true things went. I told myself it was probably contraband—a letter to a sweetheart, a list of names, a scrap of camp gossip worth bread. Nurses found things on prisoners all the time. There were procedures. I knew the procedures better than anyone; I had spent two years learning precisely which procedures existed so that I could stand beside them without being seen.

But my hands were shaking, and I am not a woman whose hands shake. I had dressed wounds through artillery audible across the river. I had held dying men's heads while they called for mothers who were already dead. My hands do not shake. So when I caught them trembling over the washbasin, I understood, in the way one understands a thing before one permits oneself to know it, that whatever was folded against my wrist had already reached past my hands into somewhere deeper.

* * *

Midnight came the way it always came, with the boiler ticking down and the frost climbing the glass and Matron Grell's footsteps crossing the corridor above me at her exact, metronomic hour. I waited until they faded. I lit my candle—the stub I kept for the ledger—and knelt, and lifted the board, and set the ledger beside it, open, ready for whatever new names the world required of me.

Then I unfolded the paper.

I have tried, since, to describe to people what happened next, and I have never managed it, because the words available are all wrong. *Shock* is a word for cold water. *Surprise* is a word for parties. What happened was that the floor dropped—not metaphorically, though I gripped the boards as though it had—and everything I had built my life on went down with it, quietly, like a stone through ice.

The handwriting was Jun's.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. It was his—his cramped, forward-leaning script with the crossed t's like little flags, the way he made his J with a loop at the bottom because he had learned to write from a sign-painter and never unlearned it, the small ink blot where his pen always hesitated after my name. I knew that hand the way I knew my own reflection. I had watched it crawl across school copybooks, across letters home from his first year away, across the last letter that arrived before the letter that said *we regret to inform you.*

We regret to inform you. Two years ago. They sent his effects in a box: a watch that didn't run, a hymnbook, a photograph of the two of us on the riverbank with the summer behind us. I had buried an empty box with my mother's hands gripping my shoulder, and I had not cried at the graveside because someone had to stay level, and I had chosen myself, and I had paid for that choice every night since at the floorboard, writing other families' sons into my ledger because no one else would write them anywhere.

And now his handwriting sat in my lap by candlelight in a hospital cellar, alive on the page.

The letter was short. It was dated in October. It read:

*Sable —*

*If this reaches you, then Emil got out, which means Emil is braver than either of us ever guessed, and it means you are reading this by some light you shouldn't have, which will make you angry, and I am glad. Be angry. Anger keeps you upright.*

*I was not at the place where they said I died. You know how their lists are—I know you know, it's why I'm writing this way, why I'm trusting a stranger with this. The regiment lost us in the retreat and I was taken, and then they moved us, and the paperwork died somewhere between the two places and I did not. I am across the river. I am thinner and my singing has not improved. I am interned as an enemy alien, which is absurd, since the only country I have ever betrayed is yours, briefly, by staying dead.*

*Don't come. That's the first thing. Don't come. The ice is not kind and neither are the patrols.*

*But answer me. One line. Let me know the promise still stands—that neither of us gets buried without the other knowing. Because they buried you for me, Sable. They gave me a date and a plot number and I have carried it two years, and I need to know that somewhere on your side of the water someone refuses to be buried properly.*

*Your brother,*

*Jun*

*I enclose nothing. If Emil is searched, this must be only paper. Burn this after reading. Forgive me for asking.*

I did not burn it.

I sat on the cold boards of my room until the candle guttered, and I read it four times, and each time the same sentence rose out of the page like a hand breaking water: *they buried you for me.* All those nights at the ledger, recording the names the officers struck, keeping history honest from the margins—and all the while the largest omission of all had been sitting in my own life, filed and closed, a box in the ground with a watch that didn't run. Jun was not a name omitted from a report. Jun was omitted from the world. And he had known, somehow, that I was the sort of person who would notice. He had bet my life on it—had handed a folded page to a stranger with shrapnel in his side and said, *find the nurse with the ledger,* or something like it; I did not know yet how the message had traveled, only that it had found me, that across a frozen river and two years of deliberate death, my brother had aimed a letter at the one person in the empire most likely to smuggle it.

Because of course he had. I almost laughed. I almost wept. He had always said I was the stubborn one, the one who kept score, the one who at nine years old had demanded the umpire overturn a call in her favor because being right mattered more than winning.

The candle died. I did not relight it. In the dark I could hear the river—not the silence a frozen river should make, but a low working sound, ice shifting against ice, miles of it grinding in its sleep. On the far bank, somewhere in the dark, in a camp I had never been permitted to imagine, my brother was thin and alive and asking for one line.

One line. As if a line could hold what I had. As if I could write *the promise stands* and fold it into a bottle and trust the winter.

I put the letter under the floorboard with the ledger, and I understood even as I did it that I was putting them together because they belonged together—because the ledger and the letter were the same document, really, both of them lists of the officially dead who refused to cooperate, both of them testimony waiting for a court that might never sit.

Then I lay down and did not sleep, and somewhere in the black hours I stopped being the woman who believed history happened elsewhere.

History had crossed the river on the ice. History was lying in the ward near the stove with shrapnel in his left side, asleep, breathing shallowly, guarded by a boy in a borrowed greatcoat. History needed his wound dressed twice daily for a fortnight, until Colonel Ashe pronounced him fit for transfer—which meant that for fourteen days, give or take, the whole living proof of my brother's existence would lie within reach of my hands.

Fourteen days. Then a train, a harder camp, a deeper erasure.

I rose before the bell. I washed my face in water with ice skinning the basin. In the glass a woman looked back at me whom I recognized and did not: same neat collar, same controlled eyes—but behind them something had shifted its weight, some load that had rested balanced for two years and was now tipping, slowly, toward motion.

At seven I began rounds. At the cot near the stove I paused, checked the dressing, noted the pulse, and wrote nothing down that anyone would care about. Emil Voss watched me with that careful blankness, waiting to see whether his paper had landed or been destroyed, whether I was a nurse or merely a uniform.

I leaned close, as any nurse leans to hear a weak man's chest, and said, very quietly:

"You'll tell me everything. Tonight."

His breath caught—the first crack in the blankness—and beneath it I saw the thing I would come to know well in the weeks ahead: grief and hope tangled so tightly they were indistinguishable, the look of a man carrying someone else's letter through a war.

"Yes," he whispered. "Nurse. Yes."
