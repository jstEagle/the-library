## 4. What Emil Carried

The letter had been in his coat for eleven days, and Emil Voss had learned to sleep with it against his chest the way other men slept with a knife.

He had never been a brave man. He wanted that understood, at least by himself, in the honest ledger of his own head. He had not volunteered for anything in his life except a swimming club in Bremen, dissolved in 1911 for lack of interest. He had come to this country as a clerk for a shipping firm, a man whose most dangerous act had been mislabeling crates of tinned herring. And when the war came and the flags changed and the neighbors' faces changed with them, he had not run, had not resisted, had not done anything at all except be arrested, which required no courage whatsoever, only an address.

They had put him across the river, behind wire, in a camp that did not appear on maps because officially there was nothing there to appear. Enemy aliens. A phrase that made him sound like something that had crawled out of the water. He was twenty-nine years old and he had been reduced to a category.

And then, in the second winter, in the typhus ward where they sent the men nobody expected to keep, he had heard someone singing. Badly. Enthusiastically. A song in a language Emil half-knew, murdered with total confidence, and he had opened his eyes because a man dying of typhus deserved to know what was killing him, and it turned out to be joy.

* * *

The man in the next cot was thin as a fence rail and grinning like he'd won something.

"You," the man said, in accented but serviceable German, "sing worse than I do. That is a comfort. I was beginning to think I was alone in the world."

"I wasn't singing. I was groaning."

"You were singing. Off-key, with conviction. There is a word for that where I come from." He leaned over, and Emil saw his eyes properly for the first time—dark, quick, alive in a face that had been starved of everything else. "It is called Tuesday."

His name was Jun Ishikawa. He had been a sailor, then a dockworker, then a category, same as Emil. He had been in the camp fourteen months. He had typhus, or the tail end of it, and the doctors had written him off, which Jun announced cheerfully was the second time in his life a piece of paper had been wrong about him.

"The first?" Emil asked.

"My birth certificate. Says I was born quiet."

Emil laughed, and laughing hurt, and Jun said, "There. That's the medicine. They keep it locked in the wrong cabinet."

* * *

You could not understand the camp unless you understood the paper.

Everything was paper. The wire was paper in the sense that a signature had put it there and a signature could move it. The food was paper—rations calculated on forms, and when the forms were wrong, the bread was thinner. The men themselves were paper: names, nationalities, categories, and if your paper said one thing and your body said another, the paper won. Emil had watched a man die of a paper error. His name had been transposed with another prisoner's, and the medicine went to the wrong cot, and by the time anyone admitted the mistake, the right man was the wrong man, and the wrong man was no man at all.

Jun understood paper the way a sailor understands weather. He watched it. He read the camp the way he'd read the sky off a deck, and he taught Emil to do the same.

"See the clerk with the green visor? He copies the sick list twice. Once for the commandant, once for the relief commission. The relief copy is the honest one. He's lazy, not cruel. There's a difference, and the difference is a man's life about once a month."

"And the commandant's copy?"

"Is literature," Jun said. "Very fine literature. Everyone in it is either well or dead. Nothing in between. In between is too much trouble to write down."

Emil thought he was joking. He learned, over the months, that Jun joked the way other men prayed—constantly, and about the things that frightened them most.

* * *

The friendship was fragile because everything in the camp was fragile, including the men. They argued the way brothers argue, over nothing, over everything—over whether Bremen or Yokohama had worse winters (Jun won; he had lived through both), over whether hope was a virtue or a symptom. Jun held that hope was a discipline, like rowing. You did it whether you felt like it or not, or you drifted.

"You talk like a man who expects to get out," Emil said once. "Nobody here expects to get out."

"Expecting is for weather. This is work." Jun was mending a shirt with thread pulled from its own hem, the camp's only reliable textile. "I have a sister. Sable. She's a nurse, on the other side of the river, at the hospital by the coal yards. She thinks I'm dead."

Emil set down whatever he was holding. In the camp you learned to set things down carefully, because your hands shook.

"The notice came two years ago," Jun went on, in the flat voice he used for the few things he did not joke about. "Killed in the first autumn. Some engagement with a name like a sneeze. There was a body, or there was a report of a body, or there was a form that wanted a body, and my name went on it." He pulled the thread tight. "I wrote to the consulate. I wrote to the Red Cross. I wrote to the hospital, care of anyone. Paper goes in; paper does not come out. You know how it is."

Emil did know how it was.

"So she is on the other side of that river," Jun said, "believing her brother is at the bottom of a field in the first autumn of the war, and every day I am not dead is a lie she is living inside." He looked up, and for once there was nothing quick in his eyes, only a terrible patience. "I have thought about this every day for two years, Emil. Every single day. You think about your wire. I think about my sister believing a piece of paper."

* * *

The promise had been made, Jun said, on a summer afternoon that belonged to another world. He told it the way he told everything, with his hands.

They were children on the river—before it was a border with a hospital on one side and a camp on the other, when it was just water with fish in it and a rope swing some older boys had hung from an elm. Jun had fallen in, on purpose, to show off, and had come up sputtering, and their game that day had been burial. Children's burial: you lay in the grass, eyes shut, hands folded, and the other one delivered a eulogy. Sable's eulogies were famous on their stretch of river. She took them seriously. She listed everything—what you'd been good at, what you'd broken, who would miss you and precisely why.

"And I got bored," Jun said, "lying there, so I opened one eye, and she was furious. Not because I moved. Because—" and here Jun's voice did something Emil had never heard it do, "—because she said, *you have to tell me. If you die, you have to find a way to tell me. I don't care how. I don't care what it costs. I am not going to spend my whole life at a grave that's a mistake.*"

He was quiet a moment. In the camp, quiet was expensive; it let the wire hum and the wind get in.

"So that's the promise. Neither of us gets buried without the other knowing. It's a stupid promise. It's the kind of thing children swear and adults smile at." He tied off the thread and bit it. "I have kept my half for two years by staying alive, which is the lazy way to do it. If I ever get the chance to keep it properly, I'll take it. And if you ever get out of this place, Emil—if you ever cross that river, if you ever get within a hundred miles of that hospital—"

"I'm a category," Emil said. "Categories don't cross rivers."

"Categories cross rivers all the time. They carry them across on stretchers." Jun's grin came back, but it was the grimmest version of it. "Listen to me. If you cross—if you ever cross—find the nurse with the ledger."

"The what?"

"She keeps a ledger. I don't know what's in it. I know she has one, because she was a child who counted things, and the war has not improved her. She'll be the one nobody notices. The one the officers talk over. Find her, and give her what I give you, and don't read it on the way." He pressed his thumb and forefinger together, a small precise gesture, sealing something. "Swear it."

"I don't swear things. Swearing is how men get killed."

"Everything is how men get killed. Swear it anyway."

And Emil, who had never volunteered for anything in his life, heard himself say, "I swear it," and felt the words go into him like a splinter—small, deep, and working its way somewhere he couldn't reach.

* * *

The chance, when it came, came the way chances came in wartime: as an injury.

Emil was taken from the camp to the riverfront hospital in the third winter—shrapnel, absurdly, from an artillery accident on the far bank, a war he wasn't even fighting reaching across the water to collect him. He was escorted, guarded, catalogued. A category on a stretcher, exactly as Jun had said.

But before they marched him to the boat, Jun had come to the fence, and the guard—paid, the way everything was paid—had let them stand three feet apart in the falling snow for four minutes. Emil knew it was four minutes because Jun counted them aloud under his breath, and at three minutes he started talking faster.

Inside his coat, folded into a square the size of a playing card, Jun had put a letter. Written over three nights by candle stub, in handwriting that slanted left and pressed hard enough to dent the page, because Jun believed faint writing was cowardice even on paper.

"Eleven days," Jun had said, gripping Emil's wrist through the wire. "It's been in your coat eleven days. Every day you don't deliver it, she believes a lie. I'm not blaming you. I'm telling you what the letter weighs. It gets heavier. That's how letters work."

"I'll deliver it."

"I know." And then, because he was Jun, because he could not help himself, because joy was his discipline: "Tell her I still sing badly. Tell her it's Tuesday."

The guard had coughed, and the four minutes were over, and Emil had walked to the boat with the letter against his chest and the splinter working deeper, and on the water he had looked back at the camp—smoke, wire, a figure at the fence not waving, because Jun did not believe in goodbyes, only in next times—and had understood, with the clarity of the very frightened, that he was now carrying the only true document in either country.

* * *

The hospital smelled of carbolic and coal smoke, and it was full of the war's paperwork walking around in uniforms, and Emil lay in a cot in a ward for prisoners and waited for a nurse who would not be noticed.

He saw her on the second day, or thought he did: a small woman moving along the far row of beds, efficient, quiet, the kind of face officers' eyes slid off like rain off glass. She did not look at him. Nobody looked at him. He was a category in a cot.

But on the third evening, when the ward was dim and the orderlies were changing shift, she came to his bedside with a tray, and her hands were steady in the way of someone who had decided long ago exactly what her hands were for, and Emil Voss—who had never been brave, who had only ever sworn one oath and meant it—slid the folded square from inside his coat and pressed it into her palm.

Her fingers closed around it. And when she looked down at the paper, at the handwriting slanting left across the fold, the color left her face so completely that Emil thought for one appalling moment he had killed her with four minutes at a fence and a dead man's letter.

Then she looked up at him, and her voice, when it came, was almost nothing at all:

"Where is he?"

And Emil opened his mouth to answer, and found that everything he knew—the camp, the wire, the smoke, the figure at the fence, the four minutes counted aloud—had to pass through a single sentence, and that the sentence was the heaviest thing he had ever carried.
