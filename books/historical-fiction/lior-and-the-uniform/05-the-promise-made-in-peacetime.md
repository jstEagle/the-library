## 5. The Promise Made in Peacetime

The letter stayed under my pillow for three nights.

I told myself it was caution. A nurse learns to be careful with paper—records burn, orders vanish, a signature in the wrong place can end a career. But that was a lie I let myself keep the way you keep a fever blanket after the fever breaks: not because it serves, but because letting go of it means admitting what happened. Jun was alive. My brother, dead two years on official paper, mourned at a distance of one hundred miles and a war's worth of silence, was alive across a frozen river with a bad lung and an enemy's name.

I did not open the letter again until the third night, when the ward had gone quiet and Matron Grell's lamp had passed my door for the last time. Then I sat up with my back against the cold wall and read it by the light I'd learned to hide from everyone, including myself.

*Little sister.* He still called me that, though I had outgrown the word by a head and a decade. *I won't waste your time or mine telling you I'm sorry. You never believed the telegrams anyway—you said once that paper lies and people only believe paper when they want to. You were right. I'm here. That's the whole of it.*

*I'm here.*

Two words. The entire architecture of my ordered world came down around them, and I sat in the rubble feeling something I hadn't permitted myself since the telegram arrived: not grief, not relief, but a fierce, childish anger. How dare he be alive. How dare he make me carry his death for two years like a stone I'd finally learned to balance, and then hand me this.

And beneath the anger, steady as a pulse: *the promise.*

You cannot understand what I have to do unless you understand the promise. So I will tell it properly, the way it deserves, because promises made by children are the only ones we keep without negotiating terms.

* * *

The summer I turned eleven—we called it the summer of the green boat, though the boat was more grey than green by then—the river ran warm and slow past the willows below our grandmother's house, and Jun was fourteen and certain of everything.

He had built the boat himself from fence boards and tar, and it leaked, and we loved it the way you love anything that shouldn't work but does. We'd push off from the mud bank in the late afternoon, when the light went long and gold and the water looked solid enough to walk on, and drift down to the bend where the current slowed and the fish rose in rings. Jun would lie back with his hands behind his head and narrate our voyages. We were explorers. We were smugglers. We were the last survivors of a drowned kingdom, poling through the ruins. He never ran out of kingdoms.

That particular afternoon he was quiet instead, which should have warned me. Jun quiet was Jun loading a catapult.

"Sable," he said, testing the word like a coin between his teeth. "Do you know what happens to drowned people?"

"You get wet," I said. I was eleven and invincible and did not yet know that I would spend my adulthood wringing water out of other people's lives.

"They disappear," he said. "Papa told me about the ferry accident. Before we were born. A whole boat of them, and some families never found anyone. Not a body, not a boot. Nothing." He sat up, and the boat rocked, and he put one hand flat on the boards as if steadying the whole world. "Their own mothers didn't know where they were. Can you imagine? Dying and your mother doesn't even know where."

"Then don't drown," I said.

"That's not the point." He was looking at the water, which was doing what water does—holding the sky, hiding its depths, keeping its own counsel. Even then the river kept secrets. Even then I should have known what I was looking at. "The point is, nobody should just *vanish*. Not from their family. Whatever happens—war, flood, getting lost, getting taken—you tell someone. You make sure they know. Dead or alive, lost or found, you *know*."

"So if I drown," I said slowly, working it out, "you have to find out where I am."

"If you drown, if you're buried, if you're taken somewhere and can't come home—if anything happens to either of us, ever, the other one finds out. Not from a stranger. Not from a piece of paper." He spat over the side, elaborately, at the concept of paper. "From us. However far apart we are. However long it takes. You cross whatever's in the way."

"It's a stupid promise," I said. "Nothing's going to happen to us."

"Swear it anyway."

I swore it anyway. Children always do; it costs nothing on a warm afternoon with the current carrying you and your whole life still ahead of you like an unwritten page. We pressed thumbs—I remember his thumb was sticky with tar—and he said the words formally, the way he did everything, turning even a child's vow into a small ceremony: *neither of us is buried without the other knowing.* Buried. He chose the word deliberately, because we were children who had just learned that bodies could go missing, and the promise was about bodies, about graves, about the terrible arithmetic of not knowing.

Then he ruined the solemnity by standing up in the leaking boat to announce our arrival in a new kingdom, and we both went into the river, and Grandmother fished us out with a switch and a lecture, and the promise sank into the ordinary sediment of a childhood the way all such things do—unremarked, unremembered, until the day you need it.

* * *

I need it now.

Here is what I know, lying awake in the third watch with the letter folded against my sternum like a second heartbeat. Officially, Jun Ishikawa died in the autumn campaigns of 1914. There was a telegram. There was a form. Somewhere in a filing room a clerk entered his name in a ledger of the dead, and the ledger closed over him like water.

Unofficially, he is interned as an enemy alien in a camp on the far bank—a foreign national caught on the wrong side of a border when the war began, too inconvenient to release and too alive to shoot. He has been there two years. He gave a wounded stranger a letter and a plea: *find the nurse with the ledger,* as if he knew—as if he somehow knew—that I had become a keeper of names. Perhaps brothers simply know. Perhaps he assumed that any sister of his, in any war, would end up writing things down that other people wanted erased.

The promise says: however far apart, however long it takes, you cross whatever's in the way.

Between us there is a river. In winter, a river becomes a road—or a trap. Men have crossed it. Men have died in it. The ice is a country with its own laws, written in white and black and that particular grey-green that means the cold hasn't reached the bottom of the truth. I am a nurse. I know bodies. I know what cold water does to a human being in four minutes, and I know that the river between here and the camp is patrolled on both banks by men with rifles and lamps and forms of their own to fill out.

Officially, crossing is treason. Officially, Jun is dead, so there is no one to cross to. The bureaucracy has solved the problem elegantly: you cannot commit treason to reach a man who does not exist.

But I have spent two years watching officers strike names from lists, and I have learned the great secret of paperwork, the one they never teach you in training: paper only holds if no one contests it. A death is a fact the moment someone signs it—but facts are made by hands, and hands can be moved. Jun's death is ink. His life is a man with a bad lung on the far bank, waiting to learn whether his sister keeps her promises.

I got up that night and stood at the ward window with the letter hidden in my sleeve, and I looked at the river.

It lay under the moon like a held breath. White, mostly—white with ridges where the ice had buckled and refrozen, white with drifted snow, white with the kind of blankness that makes men believe nothing moves and nothing can. But I have eyes that were trained on wounds, trained to see the change in a face before the monitors catch it, the flush that means fever, the stillness that means the end. And even from the window, even in moonlight, I could see that the whiteness was not uniform. There were patches along the eastern channel that lay darker, smoother, glassy—ice too thin to hold its own snow, let alone a woman with a letter.

Somewhere under that blank page, the river was still moving. Rivers always are. That is their whole nature—they refuse to stop, they only pretend, and every winter the pretense gets better and every spring it fails.

Jun used to say the same thing about me. *You never learned to lose gracefully,* Emil had told me, repeating it like scripture, and I had almost laughed because it was true and because hearing my brother's voice through a stranger's mouth was the closest thing to resurrection I would permit myself. He was right. I never learned. When the other children beat me at cards I would demand rematch after rematch until midnight, until they surrendered just to go to bed. When Grandmother said girls didn't need sums, I did the market accounts in secret for a year until she caught me and, instead of scolding, handed me the pencil permanently. I do not lose gracefully. I lose loudly, or I do not lose.

So this is what I promised him, on a green boat, in another country called peacetime: neither of us is buried without the other knowing. He knows I'm alive—he sent a letter through a stranger's wound to prove he believed it. Now it is my turn, and the debt runs the other direction, and I have never once in my life left a debt unpaid.

They will call it madness. A nurse, alone, at night, onto the ice, toward an enemy camp, for a man the government has already declared dead. If they catch me, I lose everything—my post, my license, possibly my freedom, certainly my invisibility, which has been my armor for two years. If the ice catches me first, I lose more than that, and Jun will learn of it the worst way, the way the ferry families learned: nothing found, nothing known, a sister vanished into the same river that took our childhood.

But the alternative is worse. The alternative is spring. The thaw comes, the ice rots and breaks, the river opens into its old impassable self, and the transfer lists move, and camps shuffle their forgotten men deeper into the interior, and one day the trail goes cold—not because anyone lied, but because everyone filed. Bureaucracy doesn't need malice to bury a man. It needs only time and weather and everyone minding their own business. I have watched it happen to forty-one names in my ledger. I will not watch it happen to the forty-second, not when the forty-second is Jun.

* * *

On the fourth night I took the letter from under my pillow and did not put it back.

I sat at my little table with my candle shielded, and I opened the ledger—the real one, the one under the floorboard, the one with forty-one names in my careful hand—and I turned to a fresh page. At the top, where I record date and ward and source, I wrote nothing. Some entries are not records. Some entries are orders.

Then I wrote, in the shorthand only I can read, three lines:

*J. ISHIKAWA — ALIVE — FAR BANK.*
*Promise stands.*
*Ice holds maybe six weeks. Maybe four.*

Four to six weeks. That was my whole inheritance: a window of frozen water and a brother's handwriting. Everything else—guards, patrols, permits, the colonel with his striking pen, the matron with her sharp eyes—all of it was weather, and weather changes.

I closed the ledger, slid it back beneath the floorboard, and pressed the board down with my palm until it seated silent. Then I blew out the candle and stood a long moment in the dark, listening to the hospital breathe around me—the moans, the coughs, the clock in the corridor counting out the hours of other people's suffering—and beyond it all, faint but unmistakable, a sound I had never noticed before and would never stop noticing now.

The river, groaning. Shifting its weight in the dark. Talking in its sleep.

I went to the window and looked out at the ice, and for the first time I did not see a barrier. I saw a schedule. A surface with habits, with weak places and strong ones, with hours when the lamps swept east and hours when they didn't. I had spent two years learning how institutions erase men. It was time, I thought, to learn how rivers behave—because between me and my brother lay a mile of frozen water, and I intended to read it like a chart, learn it like a face, and cross it before it woke.

In the morning, I would begin watching.
