## 6. Enemy Across the Ice

The first thing I learned was that the river had a color for every hour of the day.

At dawn it was pewter, flat as a slate wiped clean, the frost on its surface so fine it looked like breath held too long. By midmorning the sun found the eastern bank and the ice went white-blind, throwing light up into the ward until Matron Grell ordered the blinds half-drawn and complained about coal and glare in the same sentence. At noon it turned a hard, honest blue-white, the color of porcelain, of teeth, of things that could break without warning. And at dusk—the hour I came to love and dread in equal measure—it went violet and then grey and then black, black as spilled ink, black as the ledger's pages, and I would stand at my window with my forehead nearly touching the cold glass and watch it become something that no longer looked like water at all.

Officially, I was studying nothing.

I had told myself that on the first morning. Nurses look out windows; it is what nurses do between tasks, a small theft of sky permitted by the uniform. No one questions a woman at a window. That was the whole architecture of my life—no one questions a woman at a window—and for two years it had kept me safe. The window was where history did not happen. History happened to men in trenches and to officers with pens, and I stood behind glass and recorded its omissions.

But Jun was not dead.

I turned the sentence over every morning the way one turns a stone in the pocket, feeling its edges until they wear smooth. *Jun is not dead.* Two years I had carried his death like a coat I could not take off, heavy but familiar, and now Emil Voss—a man with shrapnel in his shoulder and my brother's handwriting folded against his chest—had handed me the news that the coat never fit. Jun had drowned at Virey, the telegram said. Jun had been among the unrecovered, the report said. What the reports did not say, because reports never say such things, was that some men who drown walk out of the river on the far side of someone's paperwork and keep breathing.

He was across the river. A mile of ice away. Interned. Alive.

And officially, reaching him was treason.

* * *

I began keeping a second record.

Not in the ledger—the ledger lived under the floorboard beneath my cot, and it held only names, the omitted dead, the men the officers struck from the intake lists because their deaths were inconvenient arithmetic. The ledger was testimony. This new thing was something else: a working document, a surveyor's notes disguised as nothing at all. I wrote it in pencil on the backs of dispensary labels, small enough to fold into a palm, and each night I read it until I knew it and fed it to the stove.

*East lamp: sweeps the near bank at 11 and 1. Between, forty minutes dark.*

That was the first night's entry, and it had taken me four hours of pretending to check on a sleeping ward to learn it. The guard post on our side of the river was a squat wooden hut with a stovepipe, and the man inside—I learned his shape before his face—came out on the hour, walked twenty paces east, twenty west, swept the bank with a lantern whose beam died halfway across the ice, and went back in. He was cold and he was bored and he was human, and all three of those facts were in my favor.

*Far bank: two posts, higher ground. Their lamps reach the middle third.*

The middle third, then, was mine. The middle third was where no light reached from either shore, a mile-wide country of darkness that belonged to no flag. Men had crossed there—I knew this the way one knows anything in wartime, through the mouths of orderlies who talked over card games. Smugglers, deserters, a woman two winters back who went over for reasons no one ever learned because she froze before anyone could ask her. They spoke of her the way people speak of weather. It did not occur to any of them that I might be listening with both hands.

On the third night I began learning the ice itself.

This is harder to explain, because it required me to do nothing more suspicious than stand at a window and listen, and yet it felt like the most criminal act of my life. The river talks. Anyone who has wintered beside it knows this—it groans, it ticks, it cracks like distant gunfire—but most people hear only noise. I needed grammar. So I listened, night after night, cataloguing the sounds the way I catalogued the lamps.

There were the sharp cracks, high and ringing, like a piano string snapping—the sound of new ice forming, of the river adding armor. Harmless. There were the deep booms, rolling down the channel like thunder trapped under glass—the ice settling, shifting its great weight, adjusting. Startling, but harmless. And then there was the third sound, rarer, the one I made myself stay awake to catch: a low, wet, grinding moan that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, the sound the ice made over the places where the current ran fast beneath and the freezing had gone thin. I heard it once from the direction of the old ferry landing and marked it in pencil: *black patch here. Do not trust.*

Black patches. I had read about them in a hydrographic manual I borrowed from Dr. Halloran's shelf and returned before he noticed it gone. Ice is not one thing; it is many, laid down in layers by different nights of different cold, and where the layers meet—where pressure ridges heave up or currents gnaw from below—the surface can look solid and be a lie. White ice can hold a horse. Grey ice, the manual said, might hold you. Black ice—ice through which you can see the water, dark and moving—will let you through as soon as look at you.

From the window, at dusk, when the light lay low along the surface instead of glaring down into it, I could begin to see them: places where the river's skin went thin and dark as a pupil. I mapped them. I sat with paper on my knee and drew the river as best I could from memory and sight—the bend, the island with its dead willows, the ferry pilings standing out of the ice like pulled teeth—and I inked in the weak places as I identified them, night by night, an anatomy of the enemy.

Because that is what the river was now. Not a barrier. An enemy—or possibly an accomplice; I had not decided which, and suspected it depended entirely on how well I learned its moods.

* * *

"You'll ruin your eyes, Nurse Ishikawa."

Matron Grell stood in the doorway of the side corridor, arms folded, her cap pinned with the severe precision of a woman who had never once allowed the world to tilt her. I turned from the window with the easy slowness I had practiced for two years—the slowness of a tired woman, nothing more.

"The wards are quiet," I said. "I was watching the river freeze."

"Watching the river." She came forward a few steps. "Most people find it dull."

"It isn't dull. It makes noises at night. Like something walking around under the floor."

Something moved behind her eyes—not warmth, exactly, but recognition. She looked past me at the window, at the black expanse with its scattered lamplight, and for a moment she wore the expression of a woman hearing a sound from a room she'd thought empty.

"Yes," she said. "It does that." Then, briskly, as if the moment had been a draft to be shut out: "Ward Four wants changing at eleven. See to it."

She left. I stood very still and counted my heartbeats until they slowed. I had been careless—standing at the same window at the same hours develops a pattern, and patterns are what institutions notice. Grell noticed everything; that was her function, her talent, perhaps her affliction. I resolved to vary my hours, to give myself legitimate errands along the east corridor, to become again what I had always been: furniture that happened to breathe.

But her voice stayed with me. *Like something walking around under the floor.* She had heard it too. Everyone who wintered here heard it. The river was full of buried movement, of things alive beneath surfaces that pretended to be still.

I knew something about that.

* * *

On the fifth night, I risked the bank.

Not the ice—God, not the ice, not yet, not in the dark, not alone and unpracticed. Only the bank itself: the strip of frozen mud and snow between the hospital's back wall and the waterline, where the laundry hung in winter and the coal was delivered and no one went after nine except the guard on his rounds. I wrapped my coat over my uniform, took the smallest candle-end from the dispensary, and stepped out into air so cold it felt less like temperature than like intent.

The cold was the first lesson and it was merciless. I had imagined crossing the river as a walk—difficult, dangerous, but fundamentally a walk of perhaps thirty minutes. Standing on the actual bank, with the wind coming down the valley funneling straight along the ice toward me, I understood within ninety seconds that the river would not merely resist me. It would try to kill me honestly, openly, with the full cooperation of the sky. Thirty minutes in this wind, soaked to the knees if I went through even once, would end me as surely as a bullet. Emil's wound had been luck. My brother's survival had been luck. Luck does not cross rivers twice.

I crouched at the waterline and put my bare hand flat on the ice.

It hummed. Not metaphorically—there was a vibration in it, faint and ceaseless, the pulse of the current underneath, transmitted up through three feet of frozen silence. I pressed harder, the cold biting into my palm, and let my fingers learn it. Strong ice, I thought, would feel dense, quiet, certain. Weak ice would carry a different tremor, a hollowness, a suggestion of space beneath.

I had no idea if that was true. But I had read that trappers and mail-runners judged ice by sound—struck it with a stick, listened, moved on—and so on the sixth night I brought a stick. A broom handle, cut short, wrapped in cloth to deaden nothing but suspicion. I walked ten paces onto the ice from the shelter of the coal shed—ten paces, no more, with the guard hut's stovepipe smoking placidly fifty yards east and the wind covering whatever small sounds I made—and I tapped.

The ice answered. Near the shore it rang solid, a dull confident knock. Ten paces out it changed—thinner, sharper, with a faint rattle underneath, like a cup with a crack. I retreated, marked the spot mentally, and went in to write it down and burn it.

Night after night. Ten paces became twenty, became the distance to the first pressure ridge, a heaved-up wall of shattered slabs where the ice had fought itself sometime in December and lost to both sides. The ridges, I learned, were landmarks—they were also cover. From the far bank, a crouching figure behind a pressure ridge would vanish into the broken skyline of it. The river had built me a road with walls.

And every night I timed the lamps. The east lamp at eleven and one, forty minutes dark between. The far posts, their rhythms slightly offset, their beams dying at the middle third. There was a window—an actual window of time, roughly half past midnight to ten minutes past one, when the entire river belonged to the darkness. Forty minutes to cross a mile of treacherous ice in killing cold.

It would have to be enough. It was either enough or it was nothing.

* * *

On the ninth night, I saw him.

A figure on the far bank, at the internment camp's wire, walking the perimeter with the slow shuffle of a man taking exercise because exercise was compulsory. The distance was too great for faces, but the posture was wrong for a guard and right for a prisoner—shoulders dropped, hands in pockets, head turning toward the river as he walked. Toward our side. As though the river were a window and he, too, spent his evenings looking through glass.

I did not know if it was Jun. It almost certainly was not—there were hundreds interned over there, and hope is a liar that puts familiar shapes in every crowd. But I stood at the window with my breath fogging the pane and my heart going like a bird against a pane as well, and I let myself believe it for exactly one minute. One minute of believing: *that is my brother, he is alive, he is looking at my side of the river, he knows I am somewhere on it.*

Then I took out my pencil and wrote, on the back of a morphine label:

*Prisoners exercised at wire, dusk. Camp wall visible from ridge line. Wire has gates on north face—counted three.*

Fear transmuted into method. That was the discipline I set for myself, the rule above all rules: every night the fear would come, and every night I would answer it with an entry. Fear said *you will die out there*; method answered *the middle third is dark for forty minutes.* Fear said *you will go through the ice*; method answered *tap with the stick, mark the black patches, keep to the ridges.* Fear said *they will hang you for this*; method answered *then don't be seen.*

I burned the label each morning and memorized it each night, and somewhere in those repetitions the fear stopped being a wall and became a fuel. I had spent two years being invisible, recording other people's deaths in secret, telling myself that history happened elsewhere. Now history had swum the river and knocked on my ward door in the body of a wounded foreigner, and it was asking me—my brother was asking me—to stop watching through glass.

* * *

The complication arrived on the eleventh day, wearing Colonel Ashe's greatcoat.

I heard the motorcar before I saw it—the roads were bad and the engine complained all the way down from the ridge—and by the time it crunched into the yard I had arranged my face into the pleasant blankness that officers mistake for competence. Ashe came through the ward on inspection with two adjutants trailing him, touching nothing, seeing everything, and stopped at the foot of Ward Three where Emil Voss lay healing under canvas and regulation.

"How is the prisoner?" he asked the ward nurse, not me, which was its own message.

"Mending well, sir."

"Good. He'll be fit for transfer soon enough. These camps—" a small gesture at the window, at the river, at everything across it, "—are best administered promptly. Sentiment breeds complications."

His eyes passed over me in passing, the way a man checks a lock he has no reason to doubt, and moved on. But I stood at my station with my hands folded and understood that a clock had started running that had nothing to do with ice. Emil would be transferred—east, deeper inland, to some camp whose name would arrive on paperwork I would never see. When he went, the last living link to Jun's voice, Jun's laugh, Jun's terrible insistence that his sister had never once in her life lost gracefully—all of it would leave on a train, and the river would freeze and thaw and freeze again over a brother I had found and failed to reach.

So the method sharpened. I added a column to my mental chart: not just the lamps and the patches and the ridges, but the days. How many crossings' worth of darkness remained before the transfer? Before the thaw came early, as the old lamplighter swore it would this year? Before the ice I was learning to read dissolved into the same grey water that had supposedly taken my brother two years ago?

That evening I stood at the window in the blue dusk, and the river lay below me, black and patient and talking in its sleep.

"I'm coming," I told it quietly, because no one else could hear, and because saying it aloud made it a fact rather than a wish.

Behind me, in the doorway, a floorboard shifted.
