## 7. The Matron Suspects

The candle told on me.

I should have known it would. A hospital runs on small accounting—the grams of carbolic, the rolls of bandage, the inches of candle burned—and Head Matron Grell ran this hospital the way a quartermaster runs an arsenal. Every ward had its ration. Every ration had its record. And every night for six nights I had carried my candle down the corridor past the last bed, past the linen closet with its locked door, to the corner of Ward C where the floorboard lifted, and I had knelt there in the dark writing names into my ledger while the wax dripped faster than any honest round could account for.

Six nights of extra light. Six nights of wax that had to go somewhere.

I found her waiting for me on Thursday, or she found me—it was hard afterward to say which, and I think the ambiguity was deliberate. It was half past eleven. The wards were dark except for the pilot lamps, and the river outside the windows lay white and silent under a sky like unpolished tin. I had just come back up from Ward C with the ledger flat against my stomach, bound beneath my apron where no one ever looked, and there she stood at the end of the corridor, a gray silhouette against the gray window, holding a candlestick of her own.

"Nurse Ishikawa," she said. "You're late."

"The men in Ward C are restless, Matron. The new amputee especially. He sleeps better if someone checks twice."

It was a good answer. It was true, as it happened—Private Okafor did sleep better when checked twice—but Grell's face did not change, and I understood that the truth of the answer was irrelevant to whatever arithmetic she was doing behind her eyes.

"Your candles," she said, "are burning short."

"Mine?"

"Third week running. Ward B draws four candles a week and burns through five." She turned the candlestick in her hand, slowly, so the flame swung and threw our shadows long across the floorboards. "The stores ledger doesn't lie. People do. Candles only melt."

"I'll make them last," I said.

"Yes," said Grell. "See that you do."

But she didn't move. She stood there in the corridor with her candle and her terrible stillness, and I stood opposite her with a dead man's handwriting folded against my belly, and neither of us spoke, and the river groaned once out in the dark—a long settling sound, like a house shifting on its foundations—and I watched her head tilt slightly toward it, listening, before she looked back at me.

"You've been here two years, Ishikawa."

"Two years in April."

"And in two years you have never once been late. Never sick. Never asked for leave. Never given me a reason to remember your name." She said it without accusation, almost without interest, the way one recites the specifications of a well-made instrument. "In my experience, nurses who are never anything are either saints or liars, and I have met very few saints who could carry a full ward without weeping."

"And which am I?"

"That is what I intend to find out."

* * *

She summoned me properly the next afternoon—not to her office, which would have been official, but to the linen room, which was something else entirely. The linen room was the one place in the hospital with no beds and no witnesses, a narrow chamber stacked with sheets smelling of starch and cold, and Grell stood in the middle of it folding pillowcases with the crisp violence of a woman who found peace only in exact work.

"Fold," she said, and pushed a stack of them at me.

I folded. For a while there was nothing in the room but the sound of cloth and the tick of the pipe along the wall.

"You wrote to the War Office in November," she said.

My hands did not stop, though everything below my ribs went very quiet. "I did."

"Asking after your brother."

"Asking after his grave. There's a difference." I creased a pillowcase down its middle, sharp enough to cut. "They sent me his death notice in 1914. They have not, in fourteen months, been able to send me a location. I wrote to ask where they had buried him. I thought that was a reasonable question."

"It is a reasonable question." She set a fold with the edge of her hand. "I asked it myself, thirty-one years ago."

I looked up then. In two years I had learned perhaps six facts about Head Matron Grell: that she came from the northern coast, that her hands were ruined from some early life of labor she never mentioned, that she drank her tea bitter and her discipline absolute, that she feared God in the way one fears weather, and that no one, in living memory, had heard her speak of herself.

She kept folding. Her eyes stayed on the linen.

"My brother's name was Grelle," she said. "With an e on the end. Our father spelled it that way, and his father before him, and the parish register has it that way going back past anyone's caring. When he enlisted, the clerk at the depot heard it spoken and wrote what he heard. *Grell.* No e. And my brother marched off to war as a man whose name did not belong to him, and when he died—at some river I will not dignify by remembering—he died under that wrong name, and the letter came to our house addressed to a family we were not, and for three weeks we did not open it because we assumed it concerned strangers." She laid the folded pillowcase on the pile with exaggerated gentleness, as if laying a wreath. "By the time we opened it, he had been in the ground a month, buried by men who never knew what to call him."

The pipe ticked. Somewhere above us a trolley squeaked along the ward floor.

"So when you tell me," Grell went on, "that you write letters asking after your brother's grave, I do not hear a nurse making a nuisance of herself. I hear a sister trying to keep hold of a name." At last she raised her eyes to mine, and they were pale and level and utterly without mercy. "What I want to know, Nurse Ishikawa, is why a sister keeping hold of a name needs to burn extra candles in the middle of the night."

* * *

There are moments when lying is easy, and moments when it is impossible, and the difference between them is rarely the quality of the lie. It is the weight of the thing being hidden. My ledger weighed nothing—paper, ink, a few ounces of binding—and yet standing in the linen room with Grell's eyes on me, it pressed against my ribs like a stone.

"I read," I said.

"Read."

"At night. After rounds. It helps me sleep."

"What do you read?"

"Whatever the doctors discard. Journals. Anatomy plates. Last month it was a treatise on gas gangrene." I let a little dryness into my voice, because fear, I had learned, disguised itself best wearing boredom. "I promise you, Matron, the scandal of my evenings is medical literature."

Grell studied me for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she almost smiled—a movement around the eyes rather than the mouth, brief as a fish surfacing.

"Gas gangrene," she repeated. "Well. There are worse vices in a hospital." She took the finished stack of pillowcases out of my arms and squared them against her chest. "I will tell you what I told the last nurse I caught burning irregular candles, twenty years ago in another hospital in another war. I do not care what my nurses do in the dark, provided it does not cost a patient, provided it does not cost the hospital, and provided—" she paused, and the almost-smile was gone, and what remained underneath was older and harder than her face— "*provided it does not put them in front of a tribunal.* Because tribunals, Nurse Ishikawa, do not care what you were reading. They care about paper. Paper with dates on it. Paper that shows where you were and when. A girl who keeps records of her own secret movements is building the evidence of her own hanging, and she is usually too clever to see it until the rope is ordered."

My hands, folded at my waist, did not move. I have never been more grateful for two years of practicing stillness over dying men.

"Is that a warning, Matron?"

"It is an observation about candles." She moved toward the door, then stopped with her hand on it, her back to me. "One more thing. The convoy expected tomorrow—Colonel Ashe is coming with it. He inspects intake personally now. He has developed," and here her voice acquired a fine, acid edge, "*views* about the accuracy of our lists."

"I've seen his views."

"Yes," said Grell. "I imagine you have. You strike me as a woman who watches lists very carefully." She opened the door. Cold corridor air came in, smelling of carbolic and snow. "Watch them all you like. But watch yourself harder. The river is starting to talk at night—I can hear it from my room. Ice that talks is ice that thinks, and ice that thinks is thinking about spring." She stepped through the doorway, then added, without turning around, in a voice pitched so low it might have been meant for no one:

"They misspelled him, you know. On the memorial they put up last year in my home town. *Grell,* no e. Thirty years, and they carved the wrong name in stone. His widow never corrected it. Said it wasn't worth the trouble." A pause. "I have never decided whether she was wise or whether she was the most cowardly woman on the coast."

And she was gone, her footsteps fading down the corridor, even and unhurried, leaving me alone among the starched linen with my heart going like a trapped bird.

* * *

That night I did not go down to Ward C. I sat on my cot instead with the lamp turned low and made myself think like Grell, because she had shown me, without meaning to—or perhaps meaning to exactly; I could still not decide which—that I had been careless in ways I hadn't imagined. The candles. The letters to the War Office, copies of which presumably sat in some file with my name spelled correctly on it. The late rounds, noted now by the one person in the hospital who noticed everything and forgot nothing.

And tomorrow, Ashe.

I took out the letter—Jun's letter, Emil's letter, the letter that had turned my orderly world into a held breath—and read it again by inches of lamplight, though I knew every stroke of it now the way I knew my own signature. Across the river. Alive. Ill, but alive, and waiting, and believing that his sister was the kind of woman who kept promises.

He was right. That was the terrible, wonderful, ruinous fact of it. He was right about me, and Grell was right about me too: I was building the evidence of my own hanging, one entry at a time, and I could not stop, because the entries were the only honest history being written in this whole building.

I slid the letter back beneath the loose brick in my wall—safer than the floorboard now; Grell had taught me that much—and I blew out the lamp, and I listened to the river talking to itself in the dark, low and grinding and patient, like a witness rehearsing testimony.

Tomorrow Ashe would stand at the intake desk striking names. Tomorrow Grell would watch me across the ward with those pale arithmetic eyes. And somewhere between them, I would have to become a person who could lie to both and keep faith with neither's rules.

Outside, the ice boomed—a single deep report, like a door closing far underground.

Spring was coming. Whatever I meant to do, I would have to do it soon.
