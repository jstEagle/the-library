## 8. Treating the Messenger

The wound in Emil Voss's shoulder was a lesson in geography. Shrapnel did not care for borders; it had entered on one side of a muscle and exited through another, tearing a channel that I packed with gauze twice a day and watched close by degrees. Infection was the enemy that didn't consult anyone's registry of aliens.

He lay in the corner bed of Ward C, the one nearest the window, because Matron Grell had put him there before I could ask why, and had said only: "Light is good for healing," in a voice that gave away nothing. Perhaps she knew what he carried. Perhaps she only knew what winter does to men far from home.

I changed his dressing at seven each morning and again at dusk, when the ward lamps were lit and the orderlies were busy with supper trays. Those were the rules of my access to him, and I understood even then that they were temporary. Wounds heal or they kill; either way, the nurse loses her patient. A man who recovers becomes someone else's problem—a transfer order, a train, a file stamped and closed. I had seen it happen a hundred times without ever once thinking of it as a clock ticking. Now every clean edge of granulating tissue looked to me like sand running out.

"You're staring at my shoulder as if it insulted you," Emil said. His English was accented, careful, the English of a man who had learned it from books and then been forced to use it for survival.

"I'm assessing it."

"You're assessing it like a woman reading a timetable."

I smoothed the last strip of tape and did not answer. He was right, and we both knew he was right, and there was a strange comfort in being seen so precisely by a stranger.

* * *

Emil Voss had learned to measure time in other people's routines. In the camp across the river, the days had been marked by the bread cart, the latrine detail, the guard who whistled off-key. Here, in this hospital where he was technically a prisoner and practically furniture, time was marked by the nurse.

She came at seven and at dusk. She never wasted a motion. She spoke to him the way she spoke to everyone—briefly, professionally, her eyes doing all the real work—and he might have believed she felt nothing at all if he hadn't seen her face the night she took the letter.

He had thought about that letter for two years. Carrying it had become less like carrying paper and more like carrying a bone—something inside him, structural, that hurt when the weather changed. Jun Ishikawa had pressed it into his hand across a fence wire in a camp where hope went to die slowly, of boredom mostly, and had made him repeat the instructions three times: *the riverfront hospital, a nurse named Sable, she keeps a ledger, find the ledger.*

"And how will I know her?" Emil had asked.

Jun had smiled—the smile that Emil suspected was the family inheritance, the one that held everything back and gave away nothing. "You'll know. She'll look like me but angrier."

She did look like him. That had been Emil's first shock, seeing her bend over another man's bandages: the same narrow jaw, the same dark level brows, the same way of holding still while the world moved around her. The anger was hers alone, though—or not anger exactly. Something banked and buried, like a fire laid in a stove and left unlit for years.

Now, on the eighth morning, she finished the dressing and instead of leaving, stood there with her hands folded, and asked the question she had clearly been assembling behind her teeth for eight days.

"Tell me how he looked. When you saw him last."

* * *

I regretted the question before it finished leaving my mouth. Asking was surrendering; asking meant admitting that the letter had done what it was written to do, that my brother was alive, that two years of grief had been spent on a man who was eating bad bread on the wrong side of a frozen river. But Emil set down the tin cup he'd been holding and folded his hands, and I understood that he had been waiting for the question the way a courier waits to deliver.

"He was thin," Emil said. "But everyone was thin. It wasn't remarkable." He paused, choosing words like a man crossing ice. "He had a cough in the first winter. Bad one. Then it went away."

"Went away, or got quiet?"

He considered this honestly, which I both hated and respected. "Got quiet. He worked in the kitchen after that. Kitchen men eat better." A small shrug that cost him something—I saw it pull the wound. "And he sang."

"Sang." I stared at him. "Jun doesn't sing."

"Jun sings terribly," Emil said, and something moved through his tired face that took me a moment to recognize as affection. "Every Saturday night. Off-key. Loud. The guards hated it. One threatened to confiscate his voice."

"That sounds like a threat he'd take seriously."

"Everyone did. And then next Saturday, louder." Emil's mouth curved. "There was a Latvian farmer who wept when Jun sang. We assumed from the quality. Later he told us it was because his own brother used to sing the same song, equally badly, before the war took him somewhere no one sang anymore."

I turned toward the window so he wouldn't see what my face was doing. Outside, the river lay white and enormous, scarred with pressure ridges, indifferent. Somewhere on the far side of it my brother was singing badly on Saturday nights, alive, alive, alive.

"He talked about you," Emil said quietly. "Not often. But when he drank the coffee they issued—it was terrible coffee—he would say, 'My sister makes coffee like an apology and drinks it like a victory.' Is that true?"

"It's true that he has no respect."

"He said you never learned to lose gracefully. Chess, cards, races down the riverbank. He said—" Emil hesitated, and I braced myself, because I could hear the shape of the sentence coming, the way you hear thunder before it arrives. "He said if anything happened to him, you would want to know. Not be told. *Know.* There's a difference, he said. Being told is what governments do."

* * *

That evening, Sable brought him coffee.

Real coffee—not the hospital's boiled chicory sadness, but actual beans she must have traded something for, because nothing in that place appeared without a cost. Emil held the cup and looked at her over the steam and understood that this was gratitude in her language, and also an apology, and also perhaps a bribe for what she was about to say, which was:

"The matron mentioned a transfer. Discharge assessment within the fortnight. They'll send you back across the river."

"Yes," Emil said. "That is the arrangement. I am wounded property. They repair me, they return me." He tried to make it light and failed. "Two weeks. Maybe less."

The fire in the stove popped. Down the ward, a man moaned in his sleep about mud, endlessly, patiently, the way men did.

"Then listen carefully," Sable said, sitting on the edge of the chair beside the bed, upright, official, as though delivering a lecture. "When you go back—you tell him nothing about me. Not my name, not my post, not the ledger. If the guards search you and find a second letter, he dies for it and I hang for it, and the promise dies between us, which is the only thing I'm not willing to trade. Do you understand?"

"You want me to carry silence."

"I want you to carry silence. Tell him the coffee story. Sing at him. He'll understand."

Emil looked into the cup for a long moment. "You know what he said about you? The last thing, the day I left. The wire had a gap near the woodpile, and the guard was drunk, and Jun said—" Emil closed his eyes, retrieving it exactly, because some sentences you keep whole. "He said, 'My sister will come. Not because I asked. Because she never learned to lose gracefully.'"

The ward was quiet except for the sleeping man's mud. Sable sat very still, and Emil watched the sentence land on her like weather.

"He shouldn't have told you that," she said finally.

"Why?"

"Because now I have to prove him right." She stood, straightened her apron with two crisp tugs, and became the nurse again, sealed and efficient. "Drink your coffee. It cost me two pairs of stockings."

* * *

After she left, Emil lay awake listening to the hospital breathe.

He thought about the camp across the river, the woodpile, the gap in the wire that Jun had pointed out to him months ago, casually, the way a man points out an exit he doesn't intend to use himself. He thought about the letter against his chest for two years, through searches and fevers and one long terrible week of dysentery, and how it had weighed nothing and everything.

He thought about what he had not yet told her.

It was not deception, he told himself. It was sequencing. A wound like his needed time; a message like the one he still carried needed the right hour, and the right hour was not yet here. But lying awake in the dark, with the river groaning faintly outside like something turning over in its sleep, Emil admitted the truth he had been stepping around since the day he arrived: there was more in Jun's letter than greeting. There was a second page he had never shown anyone. And the second page was why Jun had chosen him, out of four hundred men, to cross the ice.

Down the ward, the man dreaming of mud fell silent at last.

In nine days, maybe ten, they would put Emil Voss on a barge and ship him back across the river, and whatever Sable Ishikawa intended to do, she would have to do it soon.
