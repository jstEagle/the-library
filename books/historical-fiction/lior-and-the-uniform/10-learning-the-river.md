## 10. Learning the River

The lamplighter's name was Piotr, and he drank his tea with enough sugar in it to qualify as dessert.

I learned this on a Tuesday, in the third week of my watching, when I finally stopped studying the river from the ward window and started studying the man who walked its bank every dusk with a brass pole and a ladder and a face that had seen everything and judged none of it.

He lit the lamps along the embankment road — eleven of them, counting the one at the boathouse whose glass was cracked so it threw a crooked star of light instead of a circle. He finished his rounds by half past six, and then he sat on the low wall behind the coal shed with a tin cup and watched the ice do what ice does, which is nothing, magnificently, for hours.

I did not approach him all at once. That would have been clumsy, and clumsiness was the one luxury I could no longer afford. Instead I made myself a fixture of his evenings the way I had once made myself a fixture of the wards: present, unremarkable, useful. I began carrying out two cups of tea instead of one. I never spoke first. I answered what was asked of me, which after four nights amounted to *yes, the cold is worse this year*, and *no, I don't suppose the hospital will get more coal*, and once, memorably, an entire conversation conducted in silence while we both watched a fox cross the ice with the exaggerated care of a creature who understood the medium better than any of us.

On the fifth night he said, "You look at it like you're pricing it."

"Pricing what?"

"The river." He nodded at it with his cup. "Most people look at ice and see weather. You look like you're counting."

I considered three replies — denial, deflection, a small laugh — and discarded all of them, because Piotr had the particular patience of men who work alone in the dark, and lies would only bore him.

"I need to know where the patrols walk," I said. "And when."

He drank his tea. A long time passed, long enough that I thought he simply hadn't heard me, or had decided not to have heard me, which among people like us amounts to the same courtesy.

"Thursdays they come early," he said finally. "Before full dark, because the officer in charge has a wife in town and Thursdays are her evening. The rest of the week they cross the footbridge around nine, walk the near bank as far as the boathouse, back along the wall, done by ten unless something spooks them." Another sip. "Nothing ever spooks them. It's the coldest beat in the garrison and everyone hates it, so they hurry. Hurrying men don't look down."

"And the far side?"

"Far side isn't ours." He set down the cup. "Far side is theirs, and their patrols are their business, but from here — " he pointed with his chin at a bend upstream where the banks narrowed — "you can't see either shore's lamp from the middle. Dark water, dark ice, dark sky. If a person were foolish enough to be out there, that's where she'd want to be."

I looked at him. He looked at the river.

"I didn't ask about anyone going out there," I said.

"No," he agreed. "You asked about patrols." He stood, brushed the coal dust from his coat, and took his cup back inside, and that was the whole of the transaction — except that the next evening there were two coins under my saucer, which meant he'd been expecting payment and was declining it, and I understood that I had just acquired something rarer than information. I had acquired a man who had decided not to know what I was doing, which in wartime is the closest thing to an alliance that doesn't require signatures.

I paid him anyway, later, in ways that looked like nothing: a scarf from the stores marked damaged, extra sugar smuggled out in a twist of paper, a night when I treated the cough he pretended he didn't have. Debts between careful people are settled in currency that leaves no receipts.

* * *

After that I walked the bank.

Not all at once — never all at once. I went the way nurses go everywhere, at odd hours, wrapped in a coat over my uniform, carrying the air of a woman sent on some errand too tedious to explain. Invisibility had been my condition for two years, my native climate; now I discovered it could be equipment. Nobody stopped a nurse. Nurses were furniture that moved. A guard at the footbridge glanced up as I passed on the third night, registered the uniform, the bucket I'd brought as a prop, and looked away before the glance finished forming. I might as well have been the lamplighter himself, part of the architecture of the dark.

So I walked, and I learned the river the way you learn a patient: first the obvious complaints, then, night by night, the things it hides.

The ice near the shore, I learned, lied. It looked thickest where it met the land — white, ridged, confident — because snow piled against the bank and froze into what the fishermen called false edge, a shelf of slush and hope that would hold a cat and nothing heavier. The true ice announced itself by color. Where it lay blue-green and clean of snow, wind-scoured, it was thick enough to carry a wagon; the river kept its strength bare-faced. Where it wore a smooth white blanket, it might be sound underneath, or it might be hiding a current-fed hollow, and there was no telling without asking, and asking cost a leg.

The black patches were the ones that frightened me into respect. Thin places showed as dull lead-colored scars in the moonlight, wet-looking even in hard frost, where the current ran fast beneath and the ice stayed the thickness of a plate. There was one below the outfall pipe where the hospital drained its warm water into the river, and I stood over it one night listening to it tick softly to itself like a clock, and I thought: *that is the sound of the river keeping a door open.* I marked it in my mind the way I marked everything now — precisely, obsessively, the ledger habit turned outward onto the world.

The pressure ridges were different. Those were the river arguing with itself, great plates shoved up against each other in jagged walls a man could hide behind if he were foolish enough to be out there needing to hide. They formed along old lines — the channel's memory made visible — and they held, mostly, because compressed ice is honest ice. But at their bases, where the plates had buckled upward, the surrounding surface cracked into spiderwebs of fracture lines that glowed faintly green under a lantern, and a careless step on a web was how strong swimmers drowned in eight inches of water, pinned under a roof.

I mapped it all in the only way available to me, which was repetition. Eleven lamps. Footbridge at nine. Patrol to the boathouse, back along the wall, done by ten. False edge at the north slip. Blue ice off the point where the wind kept it swept. Black scar below the outfall. Ridge line running from the willow stump toward the far bank's pilings, then a gap of open-looking white I crossed only twice, both times flat on my stomach, arms spread, weight distributed, heart hammering a rhythm I could feel in my teeth.

Twice. I let myself go onto the actual ice exactly twice in those weeks, and both times I turned back at the midpoint, because the second lesson the river taught me was patience, and I intended to pass the examination.

* * *

It was on one of those two crossings — the first, in fact, a trial run I had told myself was merely reconnaissance — that the river spoke to me properly for the first time.

I had gone perhaps forty yards from the north slip, past the false edge, out onto the wind-swept blue, moving in the flat crouch the fishermen used, when the whole world under me groaned.

There is no preparing for the sound. It comes up through your bones before it reaches your ears, a deep tectonic complaint, the sound of a thing the size of a cathedral shifting its weight. Every instinct in the body screams *run*, and running is death, so you stand in the middle of the noise and you let it happen and you learn — this is the lesson, the real one — that the groaning is not the ice breaking. The groaning is the ice being alive. It contracts in the cold of the night, expands in the day's weak sun, settles, stresses, sings. A silent river is the dangerous one. A talking river is one still holding itself together.

I stood on the singing ice with my breath smoking and my hands shaking, and I laughed. Actually laughed, one short ugly bark of it, alone in the middle of a frozen river at midnight, because somewhere in the terror was a thought that would not leave me alone:

*Jun is on the other side of this sound.*

Every night since the letter, I had built the crossing larger in my imagination — longer, colder, more impossible — until it had become less a distance than a wall, a border between my life and his, patrolled by geography itself. But standing on it, hearing it complain like any overworked thing, feeling it hold me the way a tired nurse holds a patient — firm, grudging, sufficient — I understood that the river was not a wall. It was a corridor with conditions. And I had spent three years learning conditions: pulse rates, dosages, the exact pressure of a bandage, the precise moment to act and the iron discipline to wait for it.

I knelt and put my palm flat on the ice. Cold bit through my glove instantly, hungrily. Beneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat through a mattress, I felt the water moving — the current, patient, permanent, doing its work whether the war noticed or not.

"You'll hold," I told it quietly. "Hold until I'm ready."

It groaned again, long and low, which I chose to take as agreement.

* * *

Back on solid ground I did what I always did with knowledge: I wrote it down. Not in the ledger — the ledger lived under the floorboard with the names, sacred and separate — but in a small notebook of treatment notes, in the shorthand we used for medications, so that to any eye but mine it read like clinical drudgery. *Patrol: 21:00–22:00, Thu earlier. Route: bridge—boathouse—wall.* *N. slip: false edge 4m.* *Point: blue, sound.* *Outfall: black, avoid.* *Ridge: willow—pilings, base fractures, green glow.* It looked like the observations of a woman with a peculiar interest in hydrotherapy.

Grell found me writing it, once. Late, in the duty room, candle burned down to a puckered stub — the extra wax she had already remarked upon. She stood in the doorway a moment, reading nothing, seeing everything.

"The river," she said.

"The river," I agreed, and turned the page as if the gesture proved something.

She came further into the room and stood looking out the dark window at the pale ribbon below. For a long moment she said nothing, and I braced for the question — *why* — the question I could not answer without confessing, and which she, whatever else she was, had not yet earned.

What she said instead was: "In '70, the year of my brother, the thaw came in February. Early, like this one feels." Her breath fogged the glass. "Men who waited too long found their plans underwater."

"Then I should hope," I said carefully, "to be neither early nor late."

"One is never both," Grell said. "That is the whole art of anything." And she took the candle stub from beside my hand, replaced it without comment with a fresh one from her apron pocket, and left, and I sat with my notebook and my new candle and the understanding that the matron had just told me the ice was failing sooner than I thought — told me in the language of dead brothers and Februarys, told me obliquely, deniably, the way careful people give each other gifts.

* * *

The last night of my mapping, the weather turned.

A south wind arrived in the afternoon, soft and wrong, smelling of mud and distant water, and by dusk the temperature had climbed enough that my breath barely smoked on the embankment. The river noticed immediately. All along its length the ice began to talk — not the nightly groans but a higher, busier chatter, cracking and ticking and sighing, a city waking up. At the outfall, the black scar had widened visibly, a wet eye opening in the river's face. The false edge at the north slip sagged where it met the land, gone the consistency of porridge.

Piotr stood at his wall with his tea and did not drink it.

"Too warm too soon," he said. "Ice needs a slow spring or none at all. This kind — " he shook his head — "this kind rots from inside. Looks fine. Sounds fine. Then a man steps where he stepped yesterday and yesterday isn't there anymore."

"How long?" I asked. I kept my voice level, professional, a nurse asking about a prognosis.

He looked at me then, fully, for the first time in five weeks, and I saw that he had known the answer to my original question long before I asked it, had perhaps known it the first night I carried out two cups of tea, and had simply waited for me to be ready for the rest.

"Three weeks," he said. "Maybe two, if this wind holds. After that, no one crosses, and no one should." He tipped the cold tea out onto the stones and stood. "Whatever you're pricing, nurse — buy it soon."

He walked away up the embankment with his ladder on his shoulder, lighting his lamps one by one against the coming dark, and I stood at the rail with my notebook in my pocket and my heart going like a triage drum, and I did the arithmetic I had been refusing to do for days.

Two weeks. Perhaps less. Against that: Emil's wound knitting well, discharge-and-transfer looming, Colonel Ashe's inspection scheduled, and Jun across the water growing no younger, no better, no more alive on any paper I could touch. The river had given me a corridor with conditions, and the conditions were closing like a fist.

I opened the notebook by the light of the third lamp and added a final entry beneath the patrol routes and the ridge lines, in plain language, no shorthand, because some things deserved to be written honestly:

*Time remaining: short. Go soon.*

Below the embankment, out in the dark, the river chattered and ticked and sang, counting down in a language I had finally learned to hear — and somewhere across all that failing silver, a man with my brother's handwriting on his letters was waiting for a knock that had not yet come.
