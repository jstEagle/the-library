## 11. What the Ledger Costs

The trouble began with ink.

Sable noticed it on a Tuesday, in the way she noticed everything—sideways, without appearing to notice at all. Colonel Ashe's adjutant had come through the ward office the previous evening asking for the intake registers from the third convoy, and he had taken them away under his arm like a man carrying something that might bite. Now they were back on the shelf, but the ribbon that marked the February pages had been retied. Sable tied her ribbons in a particular way, a flat knot with the ends even, a habit from her mother, and the knot before her now was a bow.

Someone had opened the book and closed it again. Someone who did not think about knots.

She kept her hands moving—restocking gauze, squaring the medicine trays—and let the knowledge settle into her the way cold settled into stone. Slowly. All the way through.

* * *

Ashe came at nine o'clock the next morning, which was early for him and therefore deliberate. He arrived without ceremony, no escort beyond the adjutant, his greatcoat still buttoned against the river damp, and stood in the center of the ward office as though measuring it for a coffin.

"Sister Ishikawa." He did not use her name so much as deploy it. "You keep the night intake records."

"I transcribe them, sir. The officers write them."

"And you transcribe them faithfully."

The question was shaped like a compliment. She had learned, over two years of war, to hear the hooks inside polite sentences. "I write what is given to me, Colonel."

"Mm." He turned to the shelf, drew out the February register, and laid it open on the desk with two fingers, the way a man handles evidence. "Then perhaps you can explain this."

Sable looked. She made herself look slowly, with the mild puzzlement of a woman being shown a page she had never seen before, though she knew every line of it by heart—including the three names she had added in her own small hand, in the margin, in pencil, where pencil could be smudged or denied: *Keller, Anton. Brandt, the younger one. Osei.*

Except the margins were clean. All three names gone, erased so thoroughly the paper had been rubbed thin, and beside each blank space, in fresh official ink, a notation: *transcription error — corrected.*

"Transcription error," Ashe repeated, watching her face the way a fisherman watches still water. "Three errors. On one page. In your hand."

"The officers' lists were difficult that night, sir. There was shelling until midnight and the lantern light was poor." She heard her own voice deliver the lie and marveled distantly at how steady it sounded, how reasonable. "If I misread names, I regret it."

"You regret it." He let the words hang. Then, mildly, terribly: "Do you know what happens to a hospital's records when they are unreliable, Sister? They become useless. A dead man whose death is not recorded may be written alive somewhere else. A living man whose name is struck may be written dead. Families receive the wrong grief. The state pays the wrong pensions." He closed the register gently, almost tenderly. "I do not tolerate wrong grief, Sister Ishikawa. It breeds questions. Questions breed worse."

"Yes, sir."

He studied her a moment longer—a long, level look that seemed to weigh her against some private standard—and then inclined his head and left, the adjutant trailing. At the door he paused.

"I will be speaking with the rest of the staff. Orderlies, stretcher-bearers, the night porter. Anyone with access to the registers." He said it to the room rather than to her. "Discrepancies have a way of finding their authors."

* * *

They found him within two days.

His name was Tomas Reiner, an orderly of about fifty with a bad knee and a good laugh, the kind of man who carried dying soldiers as gently as sleeping children and told them terrible jokes while he did it because he believed, sincerely, that laughter loosened the grip of fear. He had worked the riverfront hospital since before the war. He had taught Sable, her first week, how to lift a man off a stretcher without dropping his dignity along with his weight.

She watched from the dispensary doorway as Ashe questioned him in the ward office, the door left open—open deliberately, she understood later; an interrogation with an audience is not an interrogation but a lesson.

"Tomas Reiner," Ashe read from a paper. "Night orderly, February fourteenth. You handled the intake registers that evening."

"Somebody had to carry 'em to the stove, sir. Cold in that office."

"And did you look at them?"

"Can't read much past my own name, sir. Never saw the need."

"No?" Ashe's voice stayed pleasant. "Then perhaps you can explain why a boy named Anton Keller appears in the ward treatment log—fever chart, dressings, morphine doses, all initialed—but appears nowhere in the intake register. A patient treated by this hospital who was never admitted to it. Ghosts don't take morphine, orderly."

Sable's hands went still around the mortar and pestle. She had known this risk from the beginning—the ledger lived in two places, her floorboard and the ward logs, and the ward logs could not lie. Treatment required initials. Initials required a body. She had been careful everywhere except where care was impossible.

Reiner was quiet for a moment. Through the doorway Sable could see him standing with his cap in his hands, turning it, turning it.

"Well," he said at last. "That'd be my doing, sir."

"Your doing."

"The boy come in half-dead, sir. Shell fragment, fever like a furnace. The officer writing the list that night—he'd had a bottle in him, if you want it plain, and he wrote down maybe half of what come through. I seen the boy missed off the list after we'd already got him upstairs and started on him." Reiner shrugged, a big slow shrug that moved his whole tired frame. "I meant to tell somebody in the morning. Then the boy died anyway, round dawn, and I thought—what's the use stirring it? Paper's paper. Boy's past caring what we write."

"So you altered the register."

"I rubbed out what needed rubbing, sir. Figured it was tidier."

Sable felt the sentence go through her like a splinter driven under the nail. *I rubbed out what needed rubbing.* He was lying—fluently, calmly, with the practiced ease of a man who had spent fifty years covering for other people's mistakes—and every word of it was a wall built between her and the tribunal, and she could not stop him, could not even look at him, because looking would be confession.

"You destroyed official records," Ashe said. "On your own initiative. To tidy things."

"To keep 'em honest, sir, if you want the truth of it. List said the boy never existed. But he existed. I carried him." Reiner put his cap back on, straightened it. "Seemed to me the record ought to say so."

There was a silence in which Sable forgot to breathe.

Then Ashe, softly: "Ten days' confinement. Docked pay. If it happens again, orderly, you will face a board, and boards are not sentimental." A pause. "Get out of my sight."

* * *

She caught up with him that evening in the linen room, where he sat on a stack of folded sheets eating bread and cheese with the untroubled appetite of a man who had made his peace decades ago.

"Tomas."

"Sister." He didn't look surprised. That was the worst part—he wasn't surprised at all.

"You can't—" Her voice came out wrong, too low, too fast. She stopped, started again. "Ten days in a cell. For me."

"For a name," he said comfortably. "Cheap at the price."

"It isn't yours to pay."

"Whose, then?" He looked at her then, and there was nothing comfortable in his eyes at all; they were old and clear and sorry for her. "Girl, I've been here thirty years. I've scrubbed blood out of these floors since before you could walk. You think I don't know what goes on in this place? You think I haven't seen the lists come up short, year after year, and nobody saying a word?" He took another bite of bread, chewed, swallowed. "Somebody finally writes the names down, and you think I'm going to let them hang it on you?"

Her throat closed. She had prepared, in the last two days, for many things—for suspicion, for interrogation, for the cell herself. She had not prepared for kindness, and it undid her more thoroughly than any accusation could have.

"They'll watch you now," she managed.

"They've watched me since 1901. I'm very boring." He brushed crumbs from his knee. "Ten days is a bed, three meals, and no work. My knee will thank me." He glanced at her sidelong. "Whatever it is you're keeping under that floorboard—I'm not asking, mind—but whatever it is, it better be worth a man's ten days."

"It is," she said, before she could decide whether to say anything at all.

"Then stop looking like a funeral and bring me a decent book when you visit. Something with pirates in it. And Sister—" he waited until she met his eyes— "be more careful with your knots."

* * *

That night she knelt on the cold boards of her room and lifted the ledger out from its hiding place and held it and could not open it.

This was the arithmetic she had never done, the sum she had avoided for two years: that every name she saved cost something from someone. That honesty, kept secretly, was not free—that it was paid for, always, by whoever happened to be standing nearest when the bill came due. She had imagined herself invisible, a ghost with a pencil, taking nothing and giving nothing, history's honest clerk working in the margins. But Tomas Reiner was sitting in a cell because she had been careless with a knot and arrogant enough to believe her carelessness would land nowhere.

And it would happen again. She knew it the way one knows weather coming. Ashe had found one discrepancy; he would find others, because there were others, because the ledger was full of them, twenty months of them, and each name was a thread and Ashe was pulling.

She thought of Jun then—not with the sweet ache of memory but with a new and colder clarity. This was what he had run toward, she realized. Not glory; he had never been a fool. He had run toward the understanding that hiding costs more than standing, eventually, and that *eventually* always arrives. Two years dead on paper, and she had mourned a fiction while the truth sat across a frozen river, and the only reason she knew it was that a stranger had bled his way across a border carrying a letter, and letters did not survive erasure unless someone was willing to bleed for them.

She opened the ledger. The lamp flame bent in a draft from the window, and the river beyond the glass made its low grinding sound, ice shifting against ice, the sound of something enormous deciding whether to hold or break.

She dipped her pen. Below the last name she added a line—not a soldier's name this time, but a date, and beneath it, in her smallest hand:

*Tomas Reiner. Ten days. Paid in my place.*

If the ledger was testimony, then let it testify to everything. Let it record the cost as faithfully as the loss.

She was blotting the page when she heard the step outside her door—soft, hesitant, twice-stopped—and then a knock, and Emil Voss's voice, low and urgent through the wood: "Sister Ishikawa. Please. I have to show you something. Before they move me—it has to be tonight."
