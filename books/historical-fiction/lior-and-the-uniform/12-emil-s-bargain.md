## 12. Emil's Bargain

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time she opened the door, and I understood, watching her face in that thin light, that she had been waiting for me the way a drowning man waits for a rope he refuses to name.

"Quickly," she said. "The corridor."

I had rehearsed this. All afternoon, while the orderlies changed dressings and the ward smelled of carbolic and wet wool, I had turned the words over like stones in a pocket, choosing which to keep and which to throw away. But standing in her doorway with the paper pressed flat against my ribs where I'd hidden it from the search at intake, all my rehearsals fell apart.

"They're moving me," I said instead. "Not in ten days. Sooner. The orderly who brought my supper—he didn't mean to tell me, but he counted on his fingers and then went pale when he realized what he'd said. Three days. Maybe four."

Her face did what it always did—closed, smoothed, became a wall you could lean against but not see through. Two years of nursing a war had taught her that trick, and I hated how well she wore it.

"You should sit," she said.

"I should show you." My hand was shaking. I noticed it the way you notice weather, from a distance, as if it belonged to someone else. "Please, Sister Ishikawa. Please."

* * *

She let me in only as far as the chair by her window, the one that faced the river, and I understood why before she even drew the curtain aside: from here you could watch the whole frozen width of it, black under the moon where the ice was thick, gray where it thinned toward the channel, and beyond it—a smudge of rooftops, a chimney smoking, a fence line marked by posts too small to see except that I knew they were there—the camp.

"That's where he is," she said. Not a question. She had known since the night I pressed the letter into her hand; she had simply not allowed herself to say it aloud until now.

"He's in the second hut from the water. The infirmary hut is the third." I unfolded the paper on my knee. I had drawn it three times in my head and twice in secret, once in charcoal on the back of a ration notice, and this last version I had copied out in the hospital's stolen pencil during the long useless hours when my shoulder wound kept me flat and useless and thinking. "I'm not an artist. But every line is true."

She took it carefully, the way she took everything—two hands, elbows tucked, a nurse's economy—and I watched her eyes move across it and saw the moment the lines stopped being lines and became a place where her brother lived.

"The gate is here, on the road side. There are two sentries by day, one by night, and the night one walks his post in eleven minutes—I counted for a week because there was nothing else to count. The wire has a gap behind the wood store where the flood last spring tore it loose and they mended it lazily. The commandant is a man called Brenner, who is not cruel, which in my experience is worse than cruelty because cruelty at least keeps a man alert." I heard my own voice going faster and made myself stop. "Sister Ishikawa. Sable."

It was the first time I had used her Christian name. It hung in the room between us, and she did not correct me, which I chose to take as permission.

"There's something else. And I've been trying all day to find a way to say it that wouldn't—" I stopped again. My rehearsals, dead on the floor. "Jun is ill."

* * *

She did not gasp or cry out. What she did was worse to witness: she went very still, the way water goes still just before it freezes, and I knew I was watching two years of grief being asked to un-grieve itself and refusing.

"What kind of ill?" she said. Her voice was level. Her hands, holding the map, were not quite.

"Chest. At first we thought it was the damp—it's always damp there, the huts sit on the floodplain and the river breathes through the floorboards. He coughed all autumn. By December he was coughing blood." I made myself say it plainly, because Jun had made me promise plainly, had gripped my wrist with those long piano-player's fingers of his and said, *Emil, if you ever get out of here, you will not soften it. She'll know softening when she hears it. She's my sister.* "He laughed about it. He said the war couldn't kill him properly so it settled for making him cough. He sang while he coughed. Terribly. You know how he sings."

"I know how he sings," she whispered.

"In November he collapsed in the ration line. They put him in the infirmary hut, which sounds like mercy but is really just the coldest hut, furthest from the stove. The doctor comes twice a month from the garrison town and he has no medicine worth the name and half his supplies go to the guards' quarters. Jun is not dying today. But he is dying the way ice melts—not all at once, not dramatically. From underneath."

The candle guttered. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed, and we both froze like children, and when the silence held she spoke again.

"How long?"

"I don't know. Neither does he. That's the truth of it. He knows it could be spring and he knows it could be six weeks, and he has decided—" I heard my voice crack and let it, because hiding it would have been its own lie— "he has decided that what matters is that you know. That was the whole letter, wasn't it? Not *come save me*. Just: *I am alive, and I remember the promise.*"

She looked at the window. Beyond the glass the river lay under the moon like a blade laid flat, and I watched her measure it—not with a soldier's eye, because she was not a soldier, but with something harder, something that had been forged in wards full of boys the officers' lists forgot. She measured it the way a person measures a thing they have already decided to do and only need to find the method for.

"The thaw," she said. "When does your camp expect the relief exchange?"

"Spring. April, if the paperwork moves. Later if it doesn't. Paperwork never moves in wartime; paperwork is the war's slowest soldier." I leaned forward, and my shoulder screamed, and I ignored it. "But he may not have April. And I won't have three days after that. Sable—listen to me. When they transfer me it will be east, to the hard camp, the one where men stop writing letters because there's nothing left to write them on. Whatever happens next happens without me. I need to give you everything now, tonight, while I still have hands to give it with."

* * *

She set the map on her desk and weighted its corners with a spool of thread, a spoon, a book, and stood over it like a general, and I confess that some small vain part of me was proud—proud of my ugly drawing, proud that it mattered, proud that Jun's faith in me had produced a thing worthy of his sister.

"If I cross," she said slowly, "the Red Cross office in the garrison town processes relief workers on Tuesdays. A woman named Frau Haller runs the volunteer registry—Grell mentioned her once, years ago, before the war ate everyone's friendships. If I can get papers—"

"Grell." I said the name carefully. "The matron. Can she be trusted?"

"I don't know yet." Something moved across her face, quick as a fish under ice. "That's the honest answer, Emil. I don't trust anyone, and I've discovered recently that this is both my armor and my cage."

"Then don't trust me either," I said. "Trust the map. Check every line against whatever intelligence your officers already hold—they'll have aerial photographs or prisoner sketches, someone in this building knows the layout of every camp on the river. If my map is wrong in a single detail, throw it away. But it isn't wrong. I walked that ground for fourteen months. I know it the way you know the ward at night—with your feet, in the dark."

She looked up at me then, and in the candlelight I finally saw what Jun had tried to describe in his letters, in his terrible roundabout way, between complaints about the food and jokes about the commandant's mustache. He had written: *My sister is the quietest person I know, and also the most stubborn, and when she decides a thing the decision has already been made somewhere deeper than argument can reach.*

He was right. I was looking at it.

"Why?" she asked suddenly. "Why did he choose you? Out of four hundred men in that camp, why give his letter to a foreign national with a bad shoulder?"

And there it was—the question I had been carrying since the wire, the question I'd hoped she wouldn't ask because the answer embarrassed me with its smallness.

"Because I cried," I said. "The first week, after the transport, after they took our coats, I sat against the wire and cried like a boy, and everyone looked away because looking at grief costs something. Except him. He sat down next to me in the mud and said—in my language, badly, he'd learned three phrases from a merchant in his university town—*not alone, friend*. And then he taught me a card game and lost at it deliberately for a month until I stopped shaking." I smiled and found my eyes wet and did not care. "That's all. That's the whole heroism of it. He sat down. Four hundred men and one of them sat down."

She came around the desk and stood close, and for a moment I thought she might take my hand, and I don't know what I would have done if she had. Instead she touched the map, lightly, over the third hut from the water.

"He used to sit with strangers at the river when we were children," she said. "Fishermen, travelers, anyone alone on the bank. Our mother scolded him for talking to strangers. He said strangers were just friends whose names he hadn't learned yet." Her voice caught on the last word and steadied itself with visible effort. "Thank you for sitting with him, Emil. Thank you for carrying this."

"Don't thank me yet." I tapped the map's edge, where I'd written the dates of the guard rotations in my cramped hand. "Thank me when you're back across the ice. Because here is my bargain, Sister Ishikawa, and I'll not pretend it's anything gentler than a bargain."

* * *

I stood, and my wound pulled, and I stood anyway, because this part deserved my whole height.

"When they transfer me, I go east and I likely don't come back—men come back from the eastern camps, some of them, but their letters don't. So hear my terms. You cross the river. You reach him. You tell him—" and here my voice failed me entirely for a moment, and I pressed on through the failure because he deserved the words exactly— "you tell him that Emil Voss says he was the finest man on either bank of that river, and that the card game was never lost deliberately after the first month, and that I expect him alive in April to lose at it again. You tell him his sister kept the promise. And then you get yourself back across that ice alive, because if you drown in that river doing this, I will have carried his letter across a war zone for nothing, and I am a vain man, Sable Ishikawa, and I refuse to have done anything for nothing."

The silence afterward was enormous.

"That's your bargain?" she said at last. "You want nothing for yourself?"

"I want my friend alive. Everything else is decoration."

She studied me for a long moment, and I had the strange sensation of being read—not like a suspect, not like a patient, but like a page in some ledger of hers I would never see. Then she nodded, once, the smallest nod in the world.

"All right," she said. "Then here's mine. I'll need three things before tomorrow night: the exact hour of your transfer, the name of the officer signing the order, and—" she glanced at the window, at the river, at the blade of it lying under the moon— "a way past the patrol on the near bank, which changes its schedule Thursday, which means I have less time than you do."

"How do you know the schedule changes Thursday?"

"Because I bribed the lamplighter," she said, with the first ghost of a smile I had seen on her, "and lamplighters know everything."

I stared at her. Then I laughed—a short, disbelieving bark that I strangled instantly, mindful of the corridor—and thought: *Jun, you wretch, you never told me she was like this. You told me she was quiet. You let me believe I was carrying a letter to someone fragile.*

"And Emil." She folded the map along its creases and slid it inside her sleeve, and it vanished the way her brother's handwriting had vanished into my coat months ago—one secret passing to another keeper. "If the ice takes me, the map goes with me. No one else carries it. No one else risks it. Swear you'll accept that."

"I swear nothing of the kind—"

"Swear it," she said, and beneath the quiet there was iron, all the way down, and I understood at last that I had never been the messenger at all. I had been the message. Everything else—the map, the schedule, the desperate arithmetic of days—was hers.

From the far side of the river, faint as memory, a bell rang the hour, and we both turned to the window as if the sound had summoned it: the ice lay white and silent and treacherous, and somewhere on its far shore a man with a pianist's hands and ruined lungs was waiting for news that was already two years late.

"Three days," I said quietly. "Maybe four."

"Then I cross tomorrow night," said Sable Ishikawa, and began, calmly, to trim the candle.
