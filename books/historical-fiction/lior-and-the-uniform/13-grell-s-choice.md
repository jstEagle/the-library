## 13. Grell's Choice

Sable stood outside the matron's office for the length of two bells before she knocked.

She had rehearsed this. Not the words—words were easy, words were her trade now, names and dates inked in a ledger under a floorboard—but the posture of it. How to stand so that a woman like Grell would read her correctly. Not as a supplicant. Not as an enemy. As something rarer: a person telling one true thing out loud.

The office smelled of carbolic and cold tea. Grell sat behind a desk too large for the room, mending a splint with the quick economy of someone who had done it ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more. She didn't look up when Sable entered.

"You're off rotation."

"I know."

"Then you're either sick or stupid, and you don't look sick." She tied off the bandage, set it down, and finally raised her eyes. They were gray and unhurried, the eyes of a woman who had watched men lie from their deathbeds and knew every variation. "Well?"

Sable closed the door.

* * *

There was no way to say it that wasn't dangerous, so she said it plainly, the way she'd learned to say everything that mattered.

"The prisoner Voss is carrying something for me. From across the river."

Grell did not move. That was the first mercy—not to flinch, not to reach for the bell that would summon an orderly, not to let her face do any of the work that faces do. She simply sat, hands flat on the desk, and let the silence stretch until Sable understood it was hers to fill.

"My brother," Sable said. "Jun Ishikawa. He was listed dead in the autumn of '14. Two years ago." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "He isn't dead. He's interned at Halden Point. Voss was there with him. Jun gave him a letter and told him—if he ever crossed—to find me."

"And why would your brother think a letter could reach you through a wounded foreign national?" Grell asked. Not cruelly. The question of an auditor.

"Because I keep records he doesn't know about," Sable said. "Names. Soldiers the officers strike from the intake lists. Men who die without ever having officially arrived." She heard herself and was startled by how simple it sounded, how obvious, as if she had been confessing to keeping a garden. "Jun always said I couldn't leave a wrong thing alone. Even as a boy. He said I'd get myself drowned correcting the river's spelling."

Something moved at the corner of Grell's mouth. It might have been amusement. It might have been its funeral.

"You're telling me this because—"

"Because Voss is being transferred in five days. To a camp where letters don't come out and people don't either. Because my brother is ill—I have reason to believe he's ill—and because once Voss goes, there is no thread left between me and him except the ice." She stopped. Made herself finish. "I'm going to cross. Before the thaw. And I'm telling you because if I don't come back, someone should know why, and know it was mine, and not hang it on some orderly the way Colonel Ashe hangs things on orderlies."

She had not meant to say that last part aloud. But it was true, and it had been sitting in her chest since the day Toma Brenner was marched out with his wrists bound and his face turned away from her, wearing a crime that was hers down to its ink.

Grell stood. For a moment Sable thought—absurdly—that the woman was going to strike her, the way officers struck lists. Instead Grell crossed to the window and looked out at the river, which lay white and ridged under a sky the color of old gauze.

"Do you know what I did before this war?" she said.

"No, Matron."

"Twenty-two years at St. Aldric's in the capital. Surgical. You learn things at St. Aldric's. You learn that a hospital is a machine for forgetting, and that the ones who run it best are the ones who forget fastest." She turned. In the window light her face looked carved, all planes and weather. "You learn to keep your hands clean by never asking whose blood is on them."

"Then you understand why I can't stop."

"I understand nothing of the kind." Grell came back around the desk but did not sit. She loomed, and she knew she loomed, and she used it. "What I understand is that you have just handed me enough to end three lives. Yours. Voss's. Mine, if I'm judged to have known. A tribunal doesn't care about your brother or your promises or your little book under the floorboard—it cares about discipline, and discipline is the only medicine this army dispenses reliably."

Sable's heart went cold and steady. This was the answer she had expected. She nodded, once, and reached for the door.

"Sable."

It was the first time Grell had used her name. She stopped.

"I lost a brother too," Grell said, to the desk, to the tea, to no one. "Frederick. At Mons, in the first month. Except it wasn't Frederick—it was Fredrick, with the second R, the way our mother spelled it, and some clerk corrected him into a stranger. My mother spent eleven months writing to bureaus about a man who technically had never existed. By the time they confirmed the grave, she had stopped signing her own name. She'd begun signing his."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a ward bell rang twice, and neither of them moved toward it.

"So don't mistake me," Grell said, and now she did look up, and her eyes were dry and terrible and older than the war. "I am not helping you. If Ashe asks me, I will tell him I found nothing and suspect less. If you are caught on that river, I will be the first to say you acted alone, against orders, against sense. Do you hear me? There will be no help from this office. None."

"I hear you, Matron."

"Good." Grell picked up her splint again, bent over it, dismissed her. Then, as Sable's hand touched the latch: "Pharmacy inventory is tomorrow. Tonight the lock has been sticking. Report it in the morning if you like. Or don't."

Sable walked back down the corridor with her pulse loud in her ears, not daring to believe it, not daring to disbelieve it. Half a confession, half a permission. It was more than she'd hoped for and less than she needed, and it was the only thing on offer in the whole frozen world.

That night, passing the pharmacy on her final round, she tried the door.

It swung open without a sound.

Inside, on the third shelf, exactly where a careful hand might place it for a nurse with tired eyes and a long night ahead—a small brown vial of laudanum, unlabeled, unrecorded, waiting.

Sable took it. And in the dark, holding it, she understood that Grell had given her not help but absolution in advance—the only kind a coward's courage allows—and that tomorrow morning the lock would stick, and the vial would never have existed, and neither of them would ever speak of it again.

* * *

Head Matron Grell finished the splint, then started another, though there was no need.

She had lied twice in that conversation, and both lies had been for Sable's benefit. The first: that she would say nothing if asked. She would say more than nothing; she would construct, brick by patient brick, a wall of dull administrative fact—rotations, wax counts, the ordinary fog of a hospital—behind which a clever girl might hide. She had built such walls before. She had built them for doctors who drank and sisters who stole morphine for their own pain, and she had never once been thanked, because gratitude leaves fingerprints.

The second lie was smaller and heavier: that she suspected nothing.

She suspected everything. Had suspected since November, when the candle stubs in the east corridor began burning shorter than the ward logs allowed. Since she'd found a scrap of paper in the ash bin with a name half-burned on it—*Keller, P.*—in handwriting too fine for any officer's list. Since the girl had started standing at the river window during off-hours with the stillness of someone memorizing rather than mourning.

Grell had spent forty years learning to read hospitals the way sailors read water. She knew what moved underneath.

And she knew, too, what it cost to do nothing. That was the lesson of Frederick, of the corrected name, of her mother's slow erasure. The world did not lose people to bullets alone. It lost them to clerks, to convenience, to the great soft machinery of *it cannot be helped*. Every institution she had ever served ran on the fuel of things that cannot be helped.

She set down the splint and opened her desk drawer, past the inventories, past the duty rosters, to the bottom, where a photograph lay face-down: two children on a riverbank, squinting into summer, a girl and her brother, taken a lifetime before she wore gray wool and carried keys to everything and ownership of nothing.

She had not helped Frederick. No one had asked her to. There had been no one to ask.

There was a knock. An orderly's voice: "Matron, the transfer papers came early—Voss moves at dawn, not Friday. Colonel's orders. And the ice—" the boy hesitated, embarrassed by weather— "the ice is talking tonight. Loud. Fishermen won't go near it."

Dawn. Not Friday.

Grell closed the drawer gently, the way one closes a coffin, and rose to meet whatever was coming down the corridor. On her desk, the candle burned short and even, and she thought of a girl counting wax, and a river beginning to break its word, and the small brown vial already gone from the shelf where she had placed it hours ago—taken, thank God, taken at last.
