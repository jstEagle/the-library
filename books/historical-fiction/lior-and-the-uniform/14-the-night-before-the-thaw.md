## 14. The Night Before the Thaw

The river began to speak on the third of February.

Sable heard it first as a sound she could not place—a long, low groan beneath the wind, rising through the hospital's stone foundations like something turning over in its sleep. She was counting gauze rolls at the supply window when it came again, deeper this time, and the glass in the frame shivered against its putty.

The orderly beside her, a boy named Pell with a bandaged hand, glanced up. "Ice is talking," he said, and went back to his crates.

*Ice is talking.* As if it were nothing. As if the whole frozen spine of the world hadn't just shifted an inch and announced that spring—early, treacherous, weeks ahead of itself—was coming to take the crossing away from her.

She finished her count. Her hands were steady. She had trained them to be steady through two years of triage and amputation lists and officers who spoke of men as *expenditures*, and they did not fail her now. But underneath the steadiness her heart had begun to run, quick and light, like a animal testing the edges of a cage.

*Not yet,* she told the river silently. *Hold. Hold until I'm ready.*

The river, being a river, made no promises.

* * *

Colonel Ashe arrived at four o'clock, unannounced, which meant he had been planning the visit since morning.

She saw him come up the drive through the frosted window of Ward C, his grey motorcar throwing slush, his adjutant half a step behind him carrying the leather folder that never left his hands. Ashe walked into hospitals the way he walked into everything—like a man entering property already deeded to him. He paused at the door to knock the snow from his boots, precisely twice on each boot, and Sable felt the old cold move down her back, the cold that had lived there since the day she watched him strike three names from an intake list with a pencil stroke so casual it might have been brushing away a fly.

One of those names had belonged to a boy of nineteen.

He found her at the nurses' station, naturally. He always found her; she had long ago stopped believing it was chance.

"Sister Ishikawa." He consulted the folder without opening it, as though the contents were written somewhere behind his eyes. "The Voss transfer has been moved forward."

Her pen kept moving on the chart in front of her. She had learned that trick early—the hands go on working while the mind screams—and she used it now. "Moved forward, Colonel?"

"Dawn tomorrow. The train comes through at six-forty." A pause, thin as paper. "There have been irregularities in the intake records. I prefer to move him before further questions arise."

"Of course," she said. "I'll prepare his discharge papers tonight."

"You do that." He closed the folder. Then, and this was the thing about Ashe, the thing she both hated and understood: he looked at her directly, and for one unguarded second there was something almost like regret in his face, quickly filed away. "You've been diligent, Sister. More diligent than some. I'd hate to see diligence wasted."

"I don't waste anything, Colonel," she said. "It's a habit of poverty."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile. He turned and went down the ward, boots ringing on the boards, pausing here and there to look at charts he would not read, and Sable stood at the station with her pen still moving across a line she had already written three times.

*Dawn.* Not ten days. Dawn.

And the ice was already talking.

* * *

She went to Emil's room at seven, after the evening medications, carrying fresh dressings she did not strictly need.

He was sitting up in the narrow bed, propped against the pillow, and he knew the moment she came in. She saw it happen in his face—the way hope and dread arrived together, twins, indistinguishable. He had grown thinner in the weeks since his wound began to close; the shrapnel had taken flesh along with muscle, and the hospital food sat poorly with him. But his eyes were Jun's eyes' opposite: dark where Jun's were pale, watchful where Jun's had always been open, and yet they held the same stubborn light at the bottom, the light of a man who had decided what he was willing to lose.

"They moved me," he said. It wasn't a question. Someone had told him, or he had read it in the way the guards changed their rotation, the way the corridor hummed.

"Dawn," Sable said. "Six-forty train."

Emil looked at the window, where the last grey light was dying over the river. For a long moment he said nothing. Then: "You can't cross yet. The ice isn't—"

"The ice is failing either way." She set the dressings on the chair and began unwinding his bandage with brisk, unnecessary efficiency, because her hands needed work or they would shake. "In ten days there will be no crossing at all. In ten days you'll be at Halden or wherever they send you, and I'll be no closer to him than I am now. Tonight is the only night that exists."

"And if you go through?" His voice was low, urgent. "The river doesn't care about your brother, Sister. I've seen what it does. Men fell through at the camp in December. They fished two out alive. One."

"Then I'll be the other one."

"That's not—" He stopped. Pressed his fist briefly against his mouth. When he lowered it, he said, quietly, "Jun made me promise I wouldn't let you do anything foolish. That was the entire content of his letter, practically. *Find the nurse with the ledger, and don't let her be foolish.*"

"Did you promise?"

"I promised I'd try."

"Then you tried." She smoothed the last of the clean linen over the healing wound, and her fingers lingered there a moment longer than medicine required—over the scar tissue, warm and human, proof that a man could carry shrapnel and a letter and a stranger's hope across a war and still lie breathing under her hands. "Emil. Look at me."

He looked at her.

"In the morning they will put you on a train, and I cannot stop that. But tonight I can carry his letter across the ice, and tomorrow night I can stand in front of him, and neither of us will have to wonder anymore. Do you understand? Wondering is the disease. Everything else is treatment."

For a moment his composure cracked—all of it, the camp-honed patience, the prisoner's flatness—and she saw the young man underneath, twenty-three perhaps, homesick and frightened and carrying another man's brother like a stone in his chest. "He sings terribly," Emil said. "Did you know that? Off-key. Loudly. The whole barracks begged him to stop and he only got worse. He said—" Emil's breath caught. "He said his sister was the same, and that it ran in the family, and that anyone who couldn't bear it deserved whatever war gave them."

Sable laughed. It came out of her sideways, wet and startled, and she pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth. "That sounds like him."

"He talked about you every day. Every single day. The others stopped listening after a week. I never did." He reached under his pillow and brought out the map—folded small, soft at the creases from months of handling, drawn in pencil on the back of a ration notice. The internment camp rendered in careful lines: the palisade, the guard towers, the infirmary marked with a small cross, the gap in the northeast fence where the woodpile stood. "Memorize it. Then burn it."

"I don't burn things," Sable said. "I keep them."

"Then hide it where you hide everything else." He held her gaze. "Under the floorboard. He guessed, you know. About the ledger. He said, *my sister keeps accounts; she always has; find the account of him.*"

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly, the way it does on a ship. She took the map. Their fingers touched on it, hers steady, his not.

"Thank you," she said, which was far too small a thing, and they both knew it, and neither tried to make it bigger because there wasn't time.

* * *

At eleven she went down to the pharmacy for lint and carbolic, and found the door unlocked.

This was new. Grell kept that door locked the way other people kept their tempers—close and constant. Sable stood in the dark corridor a moment, listening to the river groan below, then stepped inside and lit no lamp. On the shelf, exactly where a hurried nurse might overlook it and a knowing one would not, stood a small brown vial of laudanum, unlabeled, and beside it a twist of paper holding six tablets of morphine, and beneath both, folded once, a slip of paper in Grell's angular hand:

*The east stair is unwatched after midnight. The lamplighter's gate sticks; lift, don't push. If asked, you were here for supplies and I signed for them.*

No signature. No blessing. No absolution. Just the words, and the vial, and the unlocked door, and the vast silence of a woman who had lost a brother to a war that misspelled his name and had decided, tonight, which side of that loss she stood on.

Sable stood in the dark pharmacy and let herself feel it—one full breath of gratitude so fierce it was nearly grief—and then she took the laudanum, left the morphine, and went upstairs to pack.

* * *

She dressed in layers, in the dark, by touch. Wool stockings, two pairs. The thick skirt over riding breeches she had bought with three months of saved cigarette rations from an orderly whose conscience was negotiable. Her oldest boots, the ones with the hobnails worn round. Over everything, the heavy coat, and inside the coat, sewn flat against her chest with thread pulled from her own hem, the letter—Jun's handwriting, Jun's living hand, dry and safe against her heartbeat.

The map she had memorized and then hidden, as promised, under the floorboard, beneath the ledger, beneath two years of names the officers omitted. The ledger stayed. It would keep. It had learned how.

Last, she wrote a note and did not send it, because there was no one to send it to and nothing it could say except what she was already doing. Instead she sat on the edge of her cot for one minute—she counted it, sixty seconds, the discipline of a lifetime—and let herself be afraid. Properly afraid. Terror with the lid off, the whole animal weight of it, cold sweat and hammering heart and the image, unbidden, of black water closing over wool and hobnails.

Then she closed the lid, stood, and blew out her candle.

Clarity came with the darkness, the way it always did. This was the strange mercy she had discovered in the past weeks: fear and clarity arrived together, twins, like hope and dread, like her and Jun. Fear said *you may die tonight*. Clarity said *yes, and if you do, you will die moving toward him, which is more than the war has offered anyone in this building for two years.*

She picked up the laudanum—for the fever she might find at the far end, for Jun, if he needed it—and slipped out into the corridor.

* * *

The east stair was unwatched, as Grell had said. The kitchen was dark except for the banked coals breathing red in the range. At the lamplighter's gate the latch stuck, exactly as promised, and lifted, and gave.

Outside, the cold hit her like a wall with intent. The sky was clear and brutal with stars, the kind of winter night that kills exposed men in an hour, and the wind came off the river carrying the smell of open water somewhere downstream—that wet, mineral, ancient smell that means the season is turning whether or not anyone consents.

She crossed the bank road in the shadow of the coal shed. The patrol lamp was visible far upstream, a yellow bead swinging slowly along the eastern embankment, moving away from her on its circuit. She had timed it for six nights. Eleven minutes to cross the open ground, four minutes' grace at the turn.

The river lay below her, white-grey in the starlight, and it was groaning.

Not the distant grumble of three days ago. This was louder, nearer, a language of stress and separation—long moans running under the ice from bank to bank, sharp cracks like rifle fire, and beneath it all a deep irregular knocking, as of something enormous shifting its weight. Here and there the surface showed dark patches where the snow had sunk into slush, and along the old pressure ridge down the center, the plates had reared up and split, leaving seams of bare black water steaming faintly in the cold.

Every instinct she owned said *no*. Every calculation she had made for six nights said *now or never*.

Sable Ishikawa stood at the top of the bank with the letter over her heart and the promise of a summer afternoon thirty years gone in her ears—*neither of us gets buried without the other knowing, Sable, swear it, swear it properly*—and she swore it again, silently, to the river, to the stars, to the boy with the terrible singing voice who had been dead on paper for two years and alive in her chest for one impossible month.

Then she went down onto the ice.

The first hundred yards were firm, packed snow over solid blue, and she moved fast, bent low, boots crunching. At the slush line the sound changed under her—hollow, drum-like—and she slowed, spreading her weight, stepping where the ice looked darkest and therefore thickest, avoiding the white patches that meant air pockets and weakness. The groaning rose around her until it seemed to come from inside her own ribs. Once, directly beneath her feet, something boomed like a struck door, and she froze, arms out, breath smoking, counting.

Ten seconds. Twenty. The ice held.

Halfway across, near the pressure ridge, she heard the lamp bell upstream—the patrol turning early—and dropped flat behind a heaved plate of ice as the yellow light swept the river's skin, close enough that she could see dust frozen in the beam. A man's voice, muffled, bored, complaining about the cold. The light passed on. She waited until her heartbeat dropped from her throat, and moved.

She was forty yards from the far bank when the ice spoke her name.

The crack came from ahead, sudden and total, a gunshot splitting the night, and the surface tilted under her like a deck. She threw herself sideways toward the thicker shelf near the ridge and the ice behind her opened—a seam racing past where she'd stood, exhaling a breath of river-smell—and then the plate under her right foot gave way with a sound like a sigh, and she was in.

The water took her to the waist. Cold beyond cold, beyond thought, a white fire that clamped her chest and stole her breath whole. Her hands found the ice edge and it crumbled; found it again farther on and held—good blue ice, a foot thick, humming with the river's voice. She hauled, screaming silently, lungs locked, wool dragging at her like drowning hands, and got one knee over the lip, then her chest, then rolled free onto the solid shelf and lay gasping in the starlight, steaming, her lower body soaked and already stiffening.

*Get up.* The clarity again, cold and clean as the water. *Get up or die here, and dying here helps no one.*

She got up.

The last forty yards she barely remembered—only movement, and the burning of her legs, and the letter, and one hand pressed flat against her coat over her heart, checking, checking, the paper's small stiff rectangle still there, still dry, still Jun's. The bank rose before her, muddy snow and bare willows, and she clawed up it on hands and knees and knelt at the top, shaking so hard her teeth chattered, and looked back.

The river lay behind her, groaning and glittering, the broken seam already skimming with new black ice. Somewhere across it, dim under the stars, the hospital's windows held their faint candle-glow. Grell was awake up there; she was sure of it. Watching the river. Knowing.

Sable got to her feet. Her wet clothes froze in the wind within minutes, armor of ice, and she welcomed it—it made her solid, made her walk. Ahead, past the willows, according to the map burned into her memory, the woodpile stood against the northeast fence, and beyond it the internment camp slept, full of forgotten men, one of whom sang terribly and wrote letters in a hand she would know blindfolded and had been dead for two years.

She pressed her hand once more over her heart. Dry. Safe.

Then Elodie Fairweather's nurse—no. Then Sable Ishikawa, sister, keeper of names, promise-keeper, walked out of the starlight and toward the fence, leaving the river to its breaking behind her.
