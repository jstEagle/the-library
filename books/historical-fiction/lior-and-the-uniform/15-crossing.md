## 15. Crossing

She went out through the laundry door because its hinge was the only one she had not oiled.

That was Grell's doing—Sable understood it now. A week ago the matron had stood beside her in the dispensary and said nothing at all while she unlocked the cabinet, and this morning Sable had found the pharmacy bolt drawn back an inch and a vial of laudanum sitting on the shelf like a sentence without a verb. Not help. Not permission. Just a door left open behind her, so that whatever happened on the ice would happen to a woman who had chosen it.

The laundry door opened onto the coal yard, and the coal yard onto the lane, and the lane down to the bank where the willows grew black and bare as stitches in a wound. No moon. She had waited for no moon the way other women waited for full ones, counting the almanac's thin crescents, and tonight the sky gave her what she had asked: darkness with a few hard stars in it, enough light to find a pressure ridge by, not enough to be found by.

In her coat, over her heart, wrapped in oilcloth and then again in flannel, the letter rode against her ribs. Under her left arm, folded to the size of a prayer book, Emil's map—the camp fence drawn from memory, the guard hut marked with an X, the woodpile where a man could stand unseen if he was small and patient. Jun had always been both.

The river spoke before she reached it.

Everyone in the town knew that sound by now, the way you know a sick relative breathing in the next room—a long groan under the wind, rising and falling, the ice flexing against its own weight. For three months the river had been silent, a road of white lying between two countries like a held breath. Now, with February failing and something warm moving up from the south, it had begun to talk in its sleep.

*Hurry,* the sound said. *Hurry.*

Sable stood at the top of the bank and pulled her cap low and did the thing she had done every night for eleven nights, which was to stop being a person. It was easier than it should have been. Two years of writing dead men's names in a ledger hidden under a floorboard had taught her that a self could be set aside like a coat—that the nurse who moved through the wards at three in the morning, invisible, unremarked, taking nothing and leaving nothing, was not really Sable Ishikawa at all but a shape the dark made when it needed to move. She put that shape on now. She stepped out of herself and onto the ice.

The first hundred yards were the old channel shallows, and here the ice was white and thick and sang a high thin note under her boots, the sound of a wineglass touched by a wet finger. She counted her steps as she walked. Counting kept the mind in the body and the body in the present, and the present was the only country she could afford to live in tonight. Forty steps. Sixty. The white ice gave way to grey, then to the black-and-white marbling she had mapped by lamplight from the hospital window—old freeze over new freeze, the seam between them strong as rope or weak as paper, depending on things no one alive could tell her.

At eighty steps the ice made a sound she had not heard from shore: a crack that began somewhere behind her and ran forward faster than sound, a zipper opening the whole width of the river, and she froze with her arms out the way Emil had told her to freeze—weight spread, feet apart—and waited while the crack passed under her and went on into the dark toward the far bank, and the ice held, and she breathed, and walked.

She thought of Jun. That was dangerous, and she did it anyway.

Not the Jun of the photograph in her room—young, uniformed, smiling the way people smiled for cameras in 1914, believing the camera was the most permanent thing in their future—but the Jun of the summer afternoon on this same riverbank, twelve years old and furious about losing a race to a girl two years younger, who had sat down in the mud and refused to speak until she promised him the promise. *Swear it. If I die and you don't know, that's the worst thing there is. Worse than dying. Swear.* And she had sworn, laughing, because he was twelve and alive and covered in mud, and the word *die* was a toy they were throwing back and forth, harmless, weightless.

The word had weight now. She carried it across the ice along with everything else.

Two hundred steps. The grey ice ended and the true crossing began—the deep channel, where the water ran fast even under its lid, where the ice was black as a slate and only an inch of frozen skin thick in the places the lamplighter had warned her about. He had taken her coin with shaking hands and said, *Miss, I lamp the bank road, I don't lamp the river, whatever you're doing, God keep you off my conscience,* and she had thanked him and never learned his name, which felt suddenly, horribly like the officers striking names from lists. Everyone who helped her became anonymous. She was making a country of them.

She dropped to a crouch and went forward on all fours, spreading her weight across the black ice, listening. This was the part she had rehearsed a hundred times in her head and never once in her body: the listening, the reading of the surface, the way a blind woman reads a page. Black ice told the truth if you listened. Solid ice was quiet. Thin ice ticked.

Twenty feet into the channel, the ice ticked.

She backed up on her palms, slow as syrup, feeling the cold come up through her skirt and sleeves, and worked her way left along the edge of the bad patch, following the tick's silence the way she'd follow a pulse to find where it stopped. Her breath came out in flags of steam. Somewhere upstream a ridge had risen in the night—a wall of broken plates shoved up by the pressure of the current—and she could hear the wind playing over it, a low flute note, marking the spot where the river's bones stuck through its skin. She would have to cross the ridge. There was no way around it; the map in her memory said so, and the map in her memory had been drawn by a man who had crossed this river once, at night, half-starved, and lived.

She reached the ridge at the channel's center.

Up close it was worse than it sounded: a rampart of shattered slabs tilted at every angle, some standing chest-high on end, their edges green-black and glassy, welded together with newer ice that looked solid and wasn't. She climbed it the way you climb anything treacherous—slowly, testing each hold, keeping three points of contact, telling herself the story of the next five feet and nothing beyond it. Next five feet. Next five feet. On the crest of the ridge the wind found her fully for the first time and took her breath away bodily, snatching it out of her mouth, and for a moment she hung there astride the broken spine of the river with the whole black width of it below her on either side and the stars wheeling overhead, indifferent, ancient, and she understood with total clarity that no one had ever done this, that she was the first, that history—which had always happened elsewhere, to other people, in rooms with maps on the walls—was happening right here, to her, on her hands and knees, and its name was a promise.

Then the patrol lamp swept the bank behind her, and she stopped being history and started being prey.

It came from the east side—the home side, the side she'd left—a yellow eye swinging along the bank road, and beneath it a voice, muffled by wind, calling to another voice. Sable flattened herself against the ridge's far face, into its shadow, and watched the light travel. It moved slowly, methodically, pausing at the willows where she had come down. Pausing at the coal yard gate. The guards were checking her trail backward, or checking nothing at all, doing their rounds the way men do rounds on the last cold hour of a night shift—half asleep, resentful, seeing nothing. The light swept out onto the ice, caught the ridge's near face, slid along it—

And stopped.

Sable pressed herself flat. The ice under her cheek was cold enough to burn. Through the gap between two slabs she could see the lamp's glow, diffused, hovering, and hear the two voices resolve into words.

"—something out there. On the ridge."

"That's the ridge settling. River's talking all night."

"It stopped when we stopped."

"Everything stops when you point a light at it, Karl. That's what light does."

A long pause. The lamp held. Sable counted her own heartbeats because there was nothing else to count, and found that she was not afraid—not the way she'd expected to be afraid, all trembling and prayer. She was afraid the way a surgeon is afraid, precisely, locally, with her attention narrowed to a single point: the letter over her heart. Whatever happened to her arms, her legs, her foolish trespassing body, the letter had to stay dry. That was the whole of the mission, reduced to its final form. Everything else was weather.

The light swung away. The voices moved west along the bank, arguing softly about whether the river talked or didn't, and the dark came back like water closing over a stone, and Sable lay on the ridge of a frozen river in two countries' no-man's-land and let herself shake for exactly ten seconds, timed against her heartbeat, because ten seconds was all the fear got. Then she climbed down the far side.

The descent broke her.

Not all at once. The first slab gave way under her right boot with a sound like a rifle shot, and she threw her weight left, and the second slab held just long enough for her to reach it, and the third—she was almost down, almost to the flat black sheet beyond the ridge, victory in her fingertips—cracked through the middle like a dropped plate and dropped her into the river.

Cold is a color. That was her first thought, absurdly, as the water closed over her: that the cold had a color, a white so complete it erased everything else, sight and sound and name and self. The river took her to the shoulders before her boots found the bottom—it was shallow here, waist-deep, the channel's edge, thank God, thank God—and she stood up into air that hit the wet wool of her coat like a hammer and turned it instantly toward iron.

Later, she would remember three things from the next ninety seconds, and only three.

One: the letter. Her hand flew to her chest before her mouth finished gasping, and through the soaked layers she felt the oilcloth packet, intact, dry, riding her heartbeat like a second heart. Dry. *Dry.*

Two: her legs refusing. The cold did not hurt so much as cancel; her thighs simply declined her orders, and for a moment—a long moment, three heartbeats, four—she stood waist-deep in a freezing river and understood how people died here, not dramatically but administratively, the body quietly closing departments one by one until nothing remained open but the heart, and then not even that.

Three: Jun's voice, twelve years old, mud-splattered, furious: *Worse than dying, Sable. Swear it.* And her own voice answering across twelve years and a river: *I swore.*

She moved.

She did not decide to move; the promise decided, and she went along with it, hauling herself hand over hand up the near-vertical shelf of ice at the ridge's base, boots finding cracks, fingers going numb past feeling and then past numbness into a kind of burning clarity. She crested the far side of the ridge and rolled away from it onto the flat black ice and lay there a moment, steaming, her wet clothes already stiffening, the wind stripping the heat out of her faster than her blood could make it.

*Get up.* Nobody said it. She said it. Or Jun did, or the ledger did, or the forty names in the ledger under the floorboard did, the boy with the belly wound and the farmer from the convoy and all the others the officers had struck from the record, all of whom were dead and none of whom had ever gotten a choice like this one. *Get up. You are carrying their proof too. Get up.*

She got up.

The rest of the crossing she barely remembered afterward—this was the mercy of the cold, that it filed experience away unexamined. She walked the black ice in a crouch, then the grey, then the singing white of the shallows, counting steps, one hundred and forty of them, her clothes frozen into armor that cracked at the elbows and knees with each stride like breaking crockery. Twice more the river zipped its long crack beneath her, and both times she froze, spread her weight, waited, walked. Once she heard, distantly, the train on the far bank shunting freight in the dark, and the sound was so ordinary, so human, that she nearly laughed and might not have been able to stop.

The far bank rose out of the dark: a wall of willows and then, above them, the pale scar of the bank road, and somewhere beyond it, invisible, the wire and the huts and the watchtower of the internment camp, holding four hundred forgotten men including one who had been declared dead in the spring of 1915 by a clerk with good handwriting and a bad heart.

Sable crawled the last twenty yards on her belly through the willow scrub, mostly because her body demanded the contact with earth, and knelt at the base of the bank with the river roaring its groans behind her and the stars beginning to pale at the eastern edge.

She got to her feet. Her wet clothes froze in the wind within minutes, armor of ice, and she welcomed it—it made her solid, made her walk. Ahead, past the willows, according to the map burned into her memory, the woodpile stood against the northeast fence, and beyond it the internment camp slept, full of forgotten men, one of whom sang terribly and wrote letters in a hand she would know blindfolded and had been dead for two years.

She pressed her hand once more over her heart. Dry. Safe.

Then Elodie Fairweather's nurse—no. Then Sable Ishikawa, sister, keeper of names, promise-keeper, walked out of the starlight and toward the fence, leaving the river to its breaking behind her.

* * *

The woodpile smelled of birch bark and mouse droppings, and it hid her completely, which she tested by circling it twice in the grey pre-dawn until she was satisfied that from the tower—she could see the tower now, a black silhouette against the paling sky, maybe four hundred meters off, its lamp burning steady and stupid—she was a shadow among shadows.

Emil's map said the sentry walked the northeast stretch twice an hour, at the half hour and the hour, and paused at the corner post for a smoke. Sable crouched in the woodpile's lee and waited, teeth chattering, watching the frost form on her own sleeves in feathers and ferns, beautiful and lethal, the river's signature written all over her.

The sentry came at the half hour exactly. Young, collar up, rifle slung, walking with the rolling gait of a man whose feet were colder than his temper. At the corner post he stopped, produced a cigarette, cupped his hands around the match. Ninety seconds. Emil had said ninety seconds; Emil had been right about everything else, the fence height, the loose plank behind the infirmary latrines, the dog that barked at strangers but was chained on the south side after midnight.

Sable crossed the open ground in the time it takes to read a telegram.

The loose plank was exactly where the map said, and it lifted exactly as silently, and she slipped through into the camp like a needle through cloth, and replaced the plank, and stood in the narrow stinking alley between two huts with her heart going like a drum corps and the sky turning from black to iron to the faintest bruised suggestion of pink at the rim of the world.

Dawn. Dawn meant the camp woke. Dawn meant orderlies and kettles and men stumbling out to latrines, and dawn also meant the sentries' relief, fresh eyes, and somewhere across the river a hospital waking to find one nurse missing from her bed, and a colonel named Ashe who missed nothing.

She had perhaps an hour. Perhaps less.

The infirmary hut was third from the latrines, according to the map, and she found it by smell before she found it by sight—carbolic and boiled cabbage and the particular sweet rot underneath both that no army in any country had ever successfully disguised. A chalkboard by the door listed the sick in a clerk's careful hand, and Sable scanned the names with her heart in her throat, and there, fourth from the bottom, spelled correctly, blessedly, irreversibly recorded:

*Ishikawa, J.—fever, pulmonary. Cot 11.*

He was alive. The letters on the board were alive, official, undeniable—he existed in ink on the enemy's own chalkboard, and Sable put her frozen hand against the doorframe and laughed once, a small broken sound, and pressed her sleeve against her eyes because they had begun, inconveniently, to thaw.

Cot 11. She pushed the door open.

The hut was dim, blue with early light through frost-furred windows, rows of cots, rows of sleeping and sleepless men. She walked the aisle counting, seven, eight, nine, ten—and stopped.

The man in cot 11 was thinner than her brother. Grayer. His hair had gone from black to salt at the temples and his cheeks had the hollowed, translucent look she saw every day in the ward and hated every day. His breathing had the fine dry rattle she knew better than her own name.

But his hands, folded on his chest above the blanket, were Jun's hands exactly—the long fingers, the scar on the left thumb from the bicycle chain, the summer he was thirteen—and when she stopped beside the cot, those hands twitched, and his eyes opened, gray and fever-bright, and fixed on her face with the blankness of the very ill.

Then they changed.

"Sable," Jun whispered, in the voice of a man who has been talking to the dead for two years and has just been contradicted. "You can't be—Sable, they'll shoot you. They'll shoot you for this."

"I know," she said, and knelt beside the cot, and took his scarred hand in her frozen one, and the promise, made flesh, made warm, closed its circuit at last. "I crossed anyway."
