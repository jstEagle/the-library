## 16. The Camp of the Forgotten

The camp had no walls worth the name.

Sable saw that at once, standing in the queue of relief workers outside the wire, and the sight of it frightened her more than a fortress would have. A fortress you could climb or cut. This was nothing but posts and three strands of rusted wire across a white field, as if the men inside had been imprisoned not by iron but by exhaustion—as if the war had simply decided they weren't worth better materials. The guard towers were wooden platforms with tar-paper roofs. The barracks were long, low sheds sunk to their sills in snowdrift, their windows fogged from within by the breath of too many men.

She adjusted the armband on her sleeve. Red Cross cotton over her nurse's grey. It had cost her everything she'd carried across the ice: two of Grell's laudanum vials traded through the lamplighter's cousin for the loan of the band and a stamped transit chit belonging to a woman named Frau Heller, who had influenza and would not miss one inspection. It had cost her the last of her money and nearly the last of her nerve. The river behind her was already weeping at its edges, black seams opening along the southern bank like wounds.

*You came anyway*, she told herself, and could not decide whether it was comfort or accusation.

"Name?" The gate clerk was a corporal with a red nose and the manner of a man who had stopped reading names years ago.

"Heller," Sable said. "Relief service, section nine."

He glanced at the chit, at the armband, at her face—longer than he should have—and she prepared herself to be turned away, arrested, marched back across a river that might no longer hold her weight. Then his eyes slid past her shoulder to the cart of blankets arriving behind her, and whatever suspicion he'd entertained drowned under the arithmetic of paperwork versus work.

"Ward C is cholera watch. Ward B is general. Don't go into A without an escort." He waved her through. "And don't promise anyone anything. They'll ask."

They did ask. From the moment she passed the wire they asked—a man missing three fingers tugging her sleeve to ask if she carried letters; a boy who couldn't have been twenty asking if the exchange lists had come; an old man asking, in four languages, whether anyone outside remembered this place existed. She gave them what she had: the truth shaped as mercy. *I'm new here. I don't know yet. I will find out.* Each answer bought her ten steps deeper into the camp, and each one felt like a small theft.

The infirmary was Ward B, at the far end of the compound, distinguishable from the barracks only by the smell—carbolic and wet wool and the sweetish undertone beneath it that every nurse learned early and never forgot. She paused at the threshold with her hand on the doorframe and made herself breathe once, twice, the way she breathed before a difficult round. All winter she had been certain of one thing: that Jun was alive. Certainty had gotten her across a freezing river. But certainty and seeing are different countries, and she stood now on the border between them.

She went in.

The ward held forty beds, most of them occupied, most of the occupants asleep or pretending to be. Men coughed in the dark like distant machinery. An orderly dozed in a chair by the stove, chin on chest. Sable moved down the aisle reading faces the way she read intake lists—quickly, professionally, refusing to let hope make her slow—and she was halfway down the ward before she understood why none of them had stopped her heart.

Because none of them looked like him. And then one did.

He was in the fourth bed from the window, and he was so thin that for a moment her mind refused the arithmetic of it. Two years. He had been broad once, a boy who swam the river in August and ate enough for two and laughed with his whole ribcage. What lay in the bed now was a scaffolding of him, a man drawn in charcoal and half-erased. His hair had gone long and lank. His cheekbones stood out like roof beams in a burned house. There was a grayness under his skin that she knew clinically—it meant the body was spending its own substance to keep the heart going—and his breathing had the shallow, careful quality of a man conserving even that.

But his hands, resting on the blanket, were folded in the particular way he'd always folded them, right thumb over left, and Sable felt two years collapse like the ice behind her.

"Jun," she said. Just that. Her voice came out smaller than she intended, swallowed by the ward's dark.

His eyes opened. For a moment they were unfocused, ward-eyes, eyes that had learned not to bother focusing on voices in the night. Then they found her face, and she watched recognition arrive in stages—the way dawn comes up, first gray, then color—and she watched something worse follow it. Not joy. Not yet. First there was fear, naked and immediate, the fear of a man who has survived two years by wanting nothing, suddenly handed the thing he wanted most.

"No," Jun whispered. "No, no—you're not—" He pushed himself up on his elbows and winced, and she moved without deciding to move, crossing the aisle and pressing him back down with both hands on his shoulders, feeling how little there was of him, feeling him tremble under her palms like a struck wire.

"I'm here," she said. "I'm real. I crossed the river last night. I've been walking since before dawn."

"You crossed—" His voice cracked apart on the second word. He caught her wrists, gripped them hard enough to hurt, as if testing whether she'd dissolve. "Sable. *Sable.* You're supposed to be in your hospital being careful and invisible and—"

"And you're supposed to be dead," she said. "We're both disappointing people tonight."

A sound came out of him that was half laugh and half sob and entirely the sound of their childhood, and for a moment—one whole, suspended moment—he pulled her down and pressed his forehead against hers, and they stayed like that, brother and sister, foreheads touching the way they used to when one of them was feverish, breathing the same air in the same rhythm, and the ward's darkness closed around them like water closing over a stone.

Then he shoved her back.

Not hard—he hadn't the strength to shove anything—but with unmistakable force, his hands flat against her shoulders, pushing her upright, and his face had changed completely. The tenderness was still there underneath, but rage had risen over it like floodwater.

"You crossed the *river*," he said, keeping his voice low with an effort that shook him. "In February. On rotting ice. Alone."

"Yes."

"At night."

"It was the only time the patrols—"

"I know when the patrols run, I've counted them for two years, that isn't the *point*—" He stopped, jaw working. Somewhere down the ward a man moaned in his sleep and neither of them flinched; they had both grown up around suffering, in different classrooms. "Do you know what happens to me if you're caught? Do you understand what they do to internees who receive visitors? I am not a soldier, Sable. I have no rights to lose. I am inventory. And inventory that attracts trouble gets moved—or gets lost."

"I know what you are to them," she said quietly. "I've spent two years writing down what they do to men like you. Why do you think I came?"

"Because you've never learned to lose!" The words burst out of him louder than he meant, and the sleeping orderly stirred, and Jun dropped his voice to a furious whisper. "Since we were children. The swimming race. The chess games. Papa's chessboard, do you remember? You flipped the table rather than concede. You cannot bear to lose a single thing—not a game, not an argument, not a *brother*—and so you have thrown your entire life onto a frozen river because some promise we made when I was twelve—"

"When you were twelve and I was fourteen, and it wasn't some promise. It was *the* promise." Her own voice was shaking now, and she hated it, and she kept going anyway. "'Neither of us gets buried without the other knowing.' You said it. Under the willow. You made me swear on Mama's ring."

"That was peacetime! That was before any of this! Children make promises about death because death is a story to them—"

"And what is it now?" She leaned close. "Tell me what it is now, Jun, because I've watched men die for two years and written their names in a book under the floorboards so that somebody, someday, would know they existed, and if that makes me a child still playing at promises then I don't want to be anything else."

The ward was very quiet around them. The stove ticked. Snow ticked against the window glass. Jun stared up at her, and slowly the fury drained out of his face, and what replaced it was so raw that she had to look away—so she looked instead at his hands, at the way they'd gone slack on the blanket, at the blue nails and the tremor in the fingers that had once beaten everyone in the village at knucklebones.

"You wrote our name," he said at last, barely audible. "In your book."

"You were the third entry. After the boy from the artillery convoy and before a sergeant whose name the colonel didn't like the look of." She swallowed. "I wrote 'Ishikawa, Jun—declared dead, seen alive by reliable witness.' I never doubted the witness. Emil Voss carries your handwriting like a man carrying a lit coal. He wouldn't give it up even under guard. Even shot."

"He took shrapnel getting to the wire to post my letter," Jun said. His voice had gone thick. "Three months ago. I told him not to. I told him the letter would sit in a censor's drawer until the war ended and we'd all be old men. He went anyway. That fool went anyway." A breath. "Is he alive?"

"Wounded, mending, furious about it. He talks in his sleep. Mostly about chess." She managed something that was almost a smile. "He says you cheat."

"I absolutely cheat. He lets me, out of pity." And then Jun laughed—a small, cracked, astonished sound—and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes, and his shoulders began to shake, and Sable sat down on the edge of the narrow cot and gathered him against her the way she used to when he was small and the thunder was bad, his head against her collarbone, her hand moving over the ruin of his hair.

He wept the way soldiers and nurses weep: silently, efficiently, as though even grief had to be rationed. She held on and counted his breaths and thought, with a clarity that felt almost cruel: *he weighs less than the ledger.*

When it was over he pulled back and wiped his face with the heel of his hand, embarrassed, already rebuilding himself.

"They'll notice you," he said. "A new relief worker who spends forty minutes at one bed."

"I'm checking his lungs," she said. "I've done rounds in worse wards than this. Listen to me—there's no time, so I'm only going to say it once, and then you're going to be angry again and I'm going to survive that too." She kept her voice level, clinical, the voice she used for delivering bad prognoses. "Emil gave me a map. Your hand, again—every hut, every tower, the gap in the northeast wire where the drainage ditch runs. I have a contact in the relief service through Head Matron Grell—don't ask, it's complicated, she's either our ally or the best actress I've ever met. There are spring exchange lists. Sick internees go first. Jun—do you hear me? *Sick goes first.* For once in your life your weakness is a currency."

Jun was silent for a long moment. Outside, somewhere across the compound, a bell rang the hour, and men stirred in their bunks, and the ordinary machinery of the forgotten went on turning.

"You've planned my escape from inside a prison you broke into last night," he said finally.

"I've planned the beginning of it. The rest needs you. Which brings me to the part you're really going to hate." She took his hand—careful of the IV bruise, careful of everything—and held it between both of hers. "You have to get sick."

He stared at her. "I *am* sick. Have you looked at me?"

"You're sick the way these men are sick—slowly, uselessly, invisibly. The exchange lists take men who can't stand. Not men who are merely thin." She heard her own voice go flat and cold and hated that too, hated that two years of triage had taught her to speak of her brother as a case file. "The infirmary records. The camp doctor's assessments. If you're listed as failing—if you can't walk to the wire by April—you go on the list. If you're merely malnourished and improving, you stay on the labor detail until the war ends or you die, whichever comes first."

"You want me to perform dying."

"I want you to *be* dying convincingly enough that a doctor with four hundred patients writes one line of truth." Her grip tightened. "It's the same thing I do every night, Jun. I write down what actually happened. I'm just asking you to help me get the facts right for once."

For a moment something flickered across his face that she couldn't read—something old, something from summers that belonged to another country—and then he exhaled, long and slow, and looked past her toward the window, toward the river he could not see but could surely feel, the way prisoners feel weather.

"There's a problem," he said quietly. "There's always a problem. Camp Doctor Reiner is honest. Genuinely honest—half Swiss, half stubborn, the only man here who hasn't learned to lie on forms. If I perform, he'll see it, and if he sees it, he stops trusting his own assessments, and then nobody goes on the list." He turned back to her, and his eyes, hollowed and fever-bright, were suddenly the sharpest thing in the ward. "So before you build any more plans on top of that one, little sister, you need to know something else. Something I couldn't put in a letter."

The cold walked up her spine. "What?"

"Reiner keeps a private list of his own. Men he believes were transferred to camps that have no record of receiving them." Jun's hand closed around hers, hard, desperate. "Sable—three of those names are men who were on your riverbank hospital's intake rolls last autumn. Men who were well enough to travel. Men who left your ward on Colonel Ashe's transfer order—and arrived nowhere."

* * *

Outside the infirmary window, far off, the river made a sound like a great door groaning open, and Sable felt every plan she had built begin, very quietly, to crack.
