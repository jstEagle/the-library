## 17. The Argument on the Far Bank

**Sable**

They gave her a stool in the infirmary corner and pretended it was hospitality.

The camp infirmary was a converted granary: high rafters, a smell of grain dust and carbolic, forty cots arranged in rows so close the orderlies had to walk sideways between them. Most of the men were asleep or pretending to be. Somewhere near the stove a kettle ticked as it cooled. Sable sat with her hands in her lap like a visiting dignitary, still wearing the borrowed Red Cross armband that had gotten her through the gate, and looked at her brother.

He was thinner than the letter had warned. The blanket came up to his chest and beneath it he seemed assembled from angles—collarbone, sternum, the ridge of each rib visible through the gray shirt they'd issued him. His hair had gone long and was pushed back behind his ears in the way he'd done since he was twelve, when he'd decided it made him look like a philosopher. It made him look like a boy playing at being old.

"You crossed the river," Jun said.

"I crossed the river."

"In February."

"Late February," she said, because precision was the only armor left in her kit. "There's a difference."

"There is no difference, Sable." His voice stayed level, which she knew was worse than shouting. Jun shouted when he was angry; he went quiet when he was frightened. She had learned this before she learned to read. "The ice was rotten. I could hear it from the window of my barracks. It sounds like someone dragging furniture across a floor all night. You walked out onto a floor that's being dragged apart, to reach a man who is—" he gestured down at himself, at the cot, at the granary with its dying men and its grain-dust light "—this."

"This," she agreed. "And you're alive. So the arithmetic works."

"The arithmetic doesn't work. That's not how arithmetic works." He closed his eyes briefly, and she saw him doing it—the thing he did, counting something invisible behind his eyelids, some private column of figures he'd kept since the war took everything he'd been promised. When he opened them again they were wet, and he was furious about it, and furious about being furious. "You were supposed to be safe. That was the whole point. That was why I didn't write for two years. If they thought I was dead, then you were nobody's sister, nobody's leverage, nobody's anything. You were free."

"I was never free," Sable said. "I was invisible. There's a difference, and I've had two years to learn it."

The kettle ticked. An old man three cots over coughed in his sleep, a deep wet sound, and stopped, and started again.

Jun sat up slowly, wincing, and she moved before thinking—one step, hand out—and he waved her off with an expression so exactly their mother's that she nearly laughed.

"Tell me about the crossing," he said.

"No."

"Sable."

"You'll only use it to argue with me."

"I'm going to argue with you regardless. It's the only exercise I get." But there it was, the ghost of the old humor, surfacing through the fear like a fish rising in dark water, and she let herself feel one full second of relief before she pressed it back down. Relief was premature. Nothing was settled. He hadn't even asked yet what she'd brought.

She sat back down on the stool. Outside the frost-furred window, the river ran gray-white under a sky the color of dishwater, and she thought: *I have to cross that twice more in your imagination before I cross it once in fact, brother, so save your strength.*

"Emil Voss is alive," she said instead. "Your friend. He took shrapnel at the wire trying to reach the mail cart. He carried your letter inside his coat for eleven days, through a field hospital and a prison transport and a ward where half the men died of the fever. He wouldn't give it to anyone but me. He said—" and here her voice did betray her, just slightly, just enough that Jun heard it and went very still "—he said you told him to find the nurse with the ledger. You knew. Even across the river, even dead on paper, you knew I'd be keeping accounts somewhere."

Jun looked at her for a long moment. The fury in his face was losing a war with something else.

"How is he?" he asked.

"Healing. They'll transfer him within days unless something changes. A harder camp, north." She reached into her coat—not the letter, not yet, but the map, folded to the size of a hymnal—and laid it on the blanket between them. "He gave me this. And he told me you're ill. Don't insult both of us by denying it."

Jun looked at the map the way a man looks at a confession. Then he picked it up, unfolded it with careful fingers, and studied the camp he'd memorized well enough to draw from memory: the barracks blocks, the wire, the latrines, the infirmary marked with a small x, and at the bottom edge, in letters smaller than the rest, a note she couldn't read upside down.

"What does it say?" she asked.

"It says the east section of the fence is watchful but slow," Jun said quietly. "It says that in spring, if the exchange lists come through, names move. And it says—" his thumb moved along the last line, and his jaw tightened "—it says *tell her the ice will hold if she goes at night and prays to nothing in particular.*"

"That sounds like you."

"It is me. Past tense me. Past-tense me was an optimist."

* * *

**Jun**

His sister had become a stranger who was somehow entirely familiar, and Jun could not stop looking at her.

Two years. Two years since the mobilization notice, since the platform, since she'd stood with her chin up and her hands flat against her skirt and refused to cry until the train pulled out, and even then he'd seen her through the glass, one hand raised, the other already reaching into her bag—for what? Her notebook. Of course. She'd probably written his name down before the platform was out of sight, filing him among the living while everyone else filed him among the lost.

Now here she sat in a stolen armband in a camp where men died of boredom as often as fever, having walked across a frozen river in the last honest week of winter, and she was looking at him the way she used to look at unsolvable sums: as an offense to be corrected.

"You're angry at me," he said.

"I'm furious at you."

"Good. Keep it. Anger generates heat, and you'll need heat for the return crossing." He said it to wound her attention away from his hands, which had begun, very slightly, to shake. The illness did that. The doctor here—a Belgian, decent, powerless—called it exhaustion and malnutrition and wrote *treatment: food, rest* on a card that changed nothing, because food and rest were the two commodities the camp did not contain. "But understand something before you spend it. I am not coming back with you."

Sable's head came up. "I didn't ask you to walk across tonight."

"You brought a plan. I can see it behind your eyes. You've always carried plans the way other people carry coins—you can hear them rattling. So let me say it plainly: whatever the plan is, cut me out of it. You got what you came for. You know I'm alive. The promise is honored. Go home, put the ledger away, and live a life that doesn't include this river."

"The promise is not a receipt," she said. "It doesn't get stamped and filed. It's a promise. Neither of us buried without the other knowing—that was the wording, Jun. Do you remember the wording? Because I do. I remember every word, because I was nine and you were twelve and you insisted on formal language because you'd just discovered formal language. *Neither shall be buried without the other knowing.* Knowing, Jun. Not notified by a form letter two years late. Knowing means sitting here. Knowing means this conversation. The promise isn't honored until we both know everything, and I don't know everything, because you haven't told me what's wrong with you, and you're about to tell me you won't try to leave, and I refuse to accept either silence."

An orderly passed with a tin basin and gave them a look—visitors were meant to whisper, to sit, to leave. Sable smiled at him with such bland authority that he looked away first. Jun felt the old vertigo of watching his little sister operate: she had always been able to make the world blink first.

"There are four hundred men in this camp," he said, lowering his voice. "If you extract one, you extract the one whose name is already struck from every list. No one will look for a dead man. That's the argument you've built, isn't it? I can see it. Dead men travel light."

"It's a true argument."

"It's a selfish argument dressed as a true one. Listen to me." He leaned forward, and the movement cost him, and he hated that she saw the cost. "In spring there are exchange lists. Sick men go first—that's the one mercy in the whole apparatus, they ship the dying home to die elsewhere and call it diplomacy. My name isn't on any list. But Emil's might be, or might have been, before his transfer. And Grell—your matron, your ambiguous ally, don't look surprised, Emil described her—Grell has a contact in the relief service?"

"A name," Sable admitted. "A channel. Papers are possible. Slowly."

"Then the plan is papers and lists, not rivers. You go back, you feed that channel, and in April or May a document arrives with my name spelled correctly for the first time in two years, and I walk out of here upright, through a gate, in daylight. That is the plan. Not you, on black ice, at night, carrying me."

"And if the list never comes?"

"Then I wait for the next one."

"And if the next one never comes? You've waited two years, Jun. I've read the reports. I know what 'administrative review' means. It means a file in a drawer in an office where a colonel strikes names because striking names is easier than admitting the war is badly run. You taught me that. You said history is written by whoever holds the pen, so we should learn to hold pens. Well." She spread her hands. "I'm holding mine. Four hundred men, you said. Fine. Then we don't extract one—we make the lists honest, and every man here becomes harder to erase. Starting with you."

Jun stared at her.

Somewhere in the granary rafters, sparrows argued about the stove. The old man coughed. The kettle had gone silent, fully cold, and still the room felt warm in one specific place, the way a house feels warm around a lit window seen from the street.

"You rehearsed that," he said weakly.

"On the river. All night. I had nothing else to think about except not falling through."

"You fell through."

"Waist-deep. Once. It's handled." She said it the way she'd said *late February*, daring him to make it larger than it was, and he understood suddenly that this was what two years alone had built in her: not hardness exactly, but compression. Everything pressed tighter, hotter, denser. His soft, stubborn, ledger-keeping sister had been compressed into something that could walk across a river, and he was terrified of her, and proud of her, and grieving for her, all at once, in the same breath.

* * *

**Sable**

They fought for another hour. She counted later—it was a habit, everything went into the count—but during it, time simply moved the way water moves, taking what it took.

He argued the risk. She answered with numbers. He argued the bureaucracy. She answered with names—actual names, from her ledger, men erased and recoverable, proof that erasure was a choice someone had made and choices could be unmade. He argued that she'd lose her post, her safety, her invisibility. And there she paused, because that one landed, that one had teeth.

"I already lost it," she said finally. "Or I will, the moment I'm missed at the hospital. Matron Grell knows something. Colonel Ashe knows something. My face is now a known quantity on two banks of this river. Invisibility doesn't come back once spent, Jun. So I'm spending it on purpose. On you. That's the whole of my economics now."

"You could spend it on yourself."

"I tried that for two years. It bought me a floorboard and a candle stub and the privilege of watching boys die off the record." She heard her own voice sharpen and made herself lower it. "I'm not saying it was nothing. It was something. It kept me sane. But it was never a life. It was an antechamber. You want me to walk back into the antechamber and shut the door and wait for lists. I'm telling you the door doesn't shut anymore. So we build the plan together or we build nothing, but we build it awake."

Jun was quiet for a long time. Outside, the light was failing; the frost on the window had thickened until the river beyond was just a pale suggestion, a blank page.

"The Belgian," he said at last. "Dr. Renard. He controls the sick-lists that go to the relief commission. If your Grell's contact can match names to documents—if the paperwork arrives with the right spellings—Renard will sign. He signs anything that moves a patient toward home; it's the only rebellion available to him. But it takes weeks. Six, eight. And I have to stay on the sick-list the whole time, which means staying ill enough to qualify, which—" he smiled without humor "—is the easiest prescription I've ever filled."

"Six to eight weeks."

"The thaw comes in five."

"So I cross back," Sable said, "and I work the channel from my side, and when the thaw makes the river impassable, it also makes it unguarded, because no patrol stands on water that isn't there. Boats come after the thaw. Relief barges, supply runs. If the papers move and the lists move, then in six weeks I'm not crossing ice at all. I'm standing on a dock with a manifest."

Jun looked at her the way he'd looked at her chess board when she was fourteen and had beaten him for the first time—with the dawning, aggrieved recognition that the person across from him had been better than he'd allowed himself to see.

"When did you learn strategy?" he asked.

"I learned bookkeeping," she said. "Same thing, with higher stakes."

He laughed then—really laughed, brief and cracked and rusty, the terrible laugh Emil must have told her about, the one that was mostly nose—and several sleeping men stirred, and neither of them cared.

* * *

**Jun**

They shook on it, formally, because she insisted, because formal language had been hers since she was nine too—he'd forgotten that, how they'd caught the habit from each other like measles.

Her hand was cold and chapped and stronger than it should have been, and he held it one second longer than the terms of the bargain required.

"Conditions," she said. "One: you eat. Whatever Renard can get you, you eat it, all of it. Two: you stay on the sick-list honestly—no heroics, no work details, no east fence. Three: you write to me. Through the Red Cross channel once it's open. Real letters, real dates. If I don't hear from you for twenty days I assume the worst and start improvising, and you've seen my improvisation, so consider whether you want to be responsible for it."

"Those are outrageous conditions."

"They're standard terms. Sign or the deal collapses."

"Four hundred men," he murmured. "You said every man becomes harder to erase. Did you mean it, or was it rhetoric?"

"I never use rhetoric on family. It feels like cheating."

"Then here's my counter-condition." He pulled the map back toward himself and turned it over. The reverse was blank except for the small note at the bottom. He smoothed it flat against his knee. "When your channel opens, the first documents shouldn't carry only my name. Get Renard's full sick-list to Grell's contact—all of them, every man Renard has signed for. Let the relief commission check them against the exchange rolls. Some are already listed and don't know it. Some are misspelled. Some are struck." He tapped the paper. "You keep a ledger of the omitted. Fine. This is mine. Same book, far bank. We finish it together or not at all."

Sable looked at him, and he watched her decide—watched the calculation move through her face like weather, fast and complete—and then she nodded once, and took the map, and folded it away inside her coat beside the letter she still hadn't given him.

"The letter," he said.

She hesitated.

"Sable. The letter."

"It's Emil's," she said. "He carried it. I think—I think he needs you to have received it, and me to have delivered it, and those are different things, and I won't rob either of you. Read it tonight. I'll collect your answer in the morning." She stood, straightening the borrowed armband with brisk fingers, and for a moment the infirmary's grainy lamplight caught her face and he saw their mother there so clearly his throat closed. "Sleep, Jun. Eat. Stay boring. I'll be back before the orderlies change shift."

"You're leaving the camp tonight?"

"I have a barn two fields east and a patrol schedule in my pocket. Boring is your department now."

At the end of the cot row she paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the window and the fading river behind it.

"For what it's worth," she said, not quite facing him, "the two years weren't wasted. I know things now. Names. Systems. Where the bodies are buried and who buried them." A beat. "Metaphorically. Mostly."

"Sable."

"Hm?"

"I'm glad the floor dropped out of your world. I'm sorry it was the way it happened. But I'm glad."

She stood very still. When she spoke, her voice was perfectly level, which meant it cost her everything, and he loved her for the expense.

"Noted for the record," she said. "Both parties informed. Promise current."

Then she was gone, sideways down the row of cots, past the cold kettle, out into the blue dark, and Jun lay back on the narrow bed and took out the letter with Emil's eleven-day road still folded into its creases, and began, at last, to read.

Outside, faint under the wind, the river dragged its furniture across the floor all night long.
