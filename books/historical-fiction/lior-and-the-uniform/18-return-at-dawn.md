## 18. Return at Dawn

The river tells you when it's dying. Not all at once—it doesn't have the manners for that. It groans first, a long tectonic complaint from somewhere beneath your feet, and then it sighs, and then it cracks like a whip, sharp and close enough to flinch at, and you understand that the sound is not the ice breaking but the ice *deciding*.

Sable Ishikawa stood on the south bank at four in the morning with her boots wet to the knee and the letter dry against her heart, and listened to the river decide.

She had crossed once already tonight. She would cross again before the sky finished deciding what color to be. The thought arrived without drama, the way most unbearable thoughts do—flat, factual, already true. Somewhere behind her, up the slope through the black trees, the internment camp's watchtower lamp swung its slow arc. Somewhere ahead, across two hundred yards of failing ice, the hospital sat dark except for one window on the third floor, where she had left a candle burning every night for six years because Jun used to say you could find your way home by it.

He was alive. He was thin as a winter branch and furious at her and alive, and his hand had gripped hers hard enough to leave marks she intended to keep.

"Go," he'd said. "Before it goes light."

"You don't get to order me around anymore."

"I never got to order you around. That was always the problem." He'd almost smiled. Then the cough had taken him, doubled him at the waist, and she had held his shoulders while an orderly pretended not to look, and when he straightened he said, quietly, "Sable. Go."

So she went.

* * *

The ice near the far bank had been solid. She'd tested it on the way over with her lamplighter's iron rod, tapping the black patches, listening for the hollow drum of danger. On the way over there had been a moon, low and mean, and she had read the surface like a page: white where it was thick, gray where it thinned, black where it was already water wearing a skin of memory.

On the way over there had been no wind.

There was wind now. A warm one, absurdly warm for February, pushing up from the southwest with the smell of mud in it, and every gust pressed against her like a hand urging her back toward the camp, back toward Jun, back toward anything but the middle of the river.

She stepped off the bank and onto the ice and it spoke immediately—a crack running sideways out from under her boot, quick as a sentence cut short. She froze. Counted. Ten seconds. Twenty. The river muttered somewhere below, a deep wet sound, water moving under ice that no longer had any reason to hold together.

Go slow. Weight flat, not pointed. Rod first, always rod first. She had learned this from a lamplighter named Piotr who took her bribe in tobacco and gave her, free of charge, one piece of advice: *The river doesn't kill you, miss. The hurry does.*

She moved. One step. Tap. Listen. Another step.

The hospital came closer in increments, its dark bulk assembling itself out of the pre-dawn murk—the coal chute, the laundry line frozen stiff, the third-floor window with its candle still burning. Someone had kept it lit. Grell, probably. Or no one; maybe she'd simply never gone up to snuff it. Either way it burned, and Sable fixed her eyes on it the way you fix them on a lighthouse, because looking down was not survivable and looking back was worse.

Halfway across, the river groaned again—that long tectonic note—and then something enormous shifted beneath her, a pressure ridge flexing along a seam she couldn't see, and the ice tilted. Just slightly. Just enough.

She dropped to her knees.

It wasn't courage. It was arithmetic: a kneeling body spreads its weight; a standing body concentrates it into two small betrayals. She crawled. The rod in one fist, the letter pressed flat inside her coat with the other forearm, knees finding the rhythm of it—push, slide, listen, push, slide—while the wind shoved at her back and the eastern horizon began, traitorously, to think about gray.

Twenty yards from the north bank, the ice opened.

No warning. No groan, no crack, no courtesy. Simply a seam yawning beside her left hand, black water welling up through it, and the plate she knelt on rocking like a door coming off its hinge. Her leg went in to the thigh before she understood what was happening—cold beyond cold, a cold that was less temperature than opinion, the river's whole indifferent opinion of her—and her hand shot out and the rod caught the far lip of the seam and held.

For three seconds she hung between water and ice, and in those three seconds she thought, with a clarity that felt almost humorous: *Jun will kill me if I drown. He'll come back as a ghost specifically to be insufferable about it.*

She laughed. Actually laughed, a small mad bark of breath, and hauled.

Her knee found solid plate. She dragged the leg out—it came with a sound like a cork leaving a bottle, and the water in her boot turned instantly to a vise of cold—and crawled the last twenty yards on hands and knees with the river chewing at the ice behind her the whole way, plates grinding, seams opening, the whole surface coming apart in slow motion like a document being torn lengthwise.

Then gravel. Then bank. Then her hands in frozen mud and grass stubble, and the sky above the trees going the color of dishwater, and her breath tearing out of her in great white rags.

She lay there for ten seconds. She allowed herself exactly ten.

Then she got up and walked toward the candle in the window.

* * *

The walk from the riverbank to the hospital's rear entrance took eleven minutes. She knew because she counted everything now—steps, seconds, lamps, guards—as if numbers were a rope thrown across a chasm. Eleven minutes of squelching boots, of her soaked trouser leg stiffening, of her fingers refusing, at first, to work the latch on the coal-chute door. She'd left it unlatched yesterday. Of course she had. She'd planned everything else; why not that.

Inside, the boiler room breathed its damp iron warmth. She stripped the wet leg bare, toweled it with rags that smelled of coke and coal smoke, wrapped it in a spare blanket from the linen cart, and stood there shivering while feeling came back into her foot in needles. Above her, the hospital began to wake—doors, footsteps, the clatter of the early kettle. In forty minutes, rounds. In forty minutes, Sister Adele would expect Nurse Ishikawa on the ward with her ledger of official names, neat as scripture.

In forty minutes she would be either invisible again or she wouldn't.

The letter was dry. That was the only thing that mattered, and she checked it anyway, twice, her fingers clumsy inside her coat. Dry. Folded. Carried across a dying river in the dark, through water, past patrols, and dry. Somewhere in the infirmary across the river, Jun had a copy of the map and the promise, and Grell's contact's name written in her own hand, and a plan. He had argued with her about every clause of it and signed off on none of them and kissed her forehead at the fence gap like they were children again, and then he'd said, "Come back the same way," and she'd said "Obviously," and both of them had known that *the same way* was a country that might not exist by morning.

The thaw had come early. The thaw was here.

She climbed the stairs.

* * *

She made it to the second floor before she saw him.

He was standing at the landing window—not looking out, not looking at anything—Colonel Ashe in his greatcoat with his cap under his arm like a man attending a funeral, and the sight of him stopped her feet on the stairs the way a wall stops water. For one absurd moment her mind offered her alternatives: turn back, claim she'd been walking the grounds, invent a patient, invent anything. But her trouser leg was wrapped in a blanket and her boots were ruined and there was river mud to the knee, and lies require raw material, and she had none.

Besides. She had known. She had known since the night she memorized the patrol schedule, since the candle wax, since Grell's careful non-questions. A trap you build yourself is still a trap. You just get to know the dimensions of it.

She climbed the last steps.

"Miss Ishikawa." His voice was quiet. That was somehow worse than shouting. "You're up early."

"I could say the same, Colonel."

"I don't sleep well in February." He looked at her then, and his eyes did the thing they always did—the thing she'd watched him do to intake lists, the scanning, the subtracting. "You're wet."

"The riverbank is muddy this time of year."

"In February."

"The thaw has come early. You may have noticed the noise."

Something moved behind his face—not quite a smile, not quite a wince. He stepped aside from the window, and she saw that he hadn't come alone. Two military policemen stood at the head of the corridor, very still, very bored-looking in the way men are bored when they've been told to look bored. And leaning against the wall outside the pharmacy, arms folded, face carved out of oak, stood Head Matron Grell.

Grell wouldn't meet her eyes. Or—no. Grell met them, deliberately, for one long second, and in that second Sable read an entire unspoken sentence, and the sentence was: *I left the pharmacy unlocked. I left the laudanum. This part I did not sign up for.*

Or maybe the sentence was: *Run.* But there was nowhere to run that wasn't the river, and the river was busy dying.

"Ashe," Sable said. "Say it plainly. You've been waiting for me."

"I've been waiting," Ashe said, "since Tuesday." He turned and walked, unhurried, toward the ward doors, and she understood she was meant to follow, and followed. "Tuesday a lamplighter on the south road mentioned a woman asking after patrol schedules. Thursday an orderly noted candle wax where no candle should be. Last night a sentry on the far tower reported a figure on the ice at half past midnight, moving north." He stopped at the ward doors, hand on the handle. "I could have stopped you at the bank, you know. There were men in the trees."

"But then you wouldn't know where I went."

"And I would have had to invent it." He pushed the doors open onto the dim ward, the rows of sleeping men, the smell of carbolic and sleep. "I don't invent, Miss Ishikawa. That's rather the entire point of me."

* * *

They stood in the ward while the men slept. It seemed obscene, discussing treason among the breathing.

"You crossed to the internment camp," Ashe said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

"You saw someone there."

"Yes."

"Was it worth the tribunal?"

And here was the moment—she felt it arrive like weather—where a clever person would have begun negotiating. Where she might have said *what tribunal*, might have said *prove it*, might have remembered that officially her brother was dead and officially she was a nurse with muddy boots and officially none of it touched her. She had spent six years being nobody. She was very good at it. Nobody survives tribunals by not existing.

But she was so tired. And her leg ached where the river had bitten her. And somewhere down the ward a boy—seventeen, eighteen, the age Jun had been at the start of all this—murmured a name in his sleep that wasn't his own, calling for someone who was probably already a line struck through in a colonel's ledger.

"You keep asking me about worth," she said. "As if that's the question."

"It's the only question."

"No. The only question is whether it happened. Whether it's true. Worth is just the word people use when they want the truth to be smaller than it is." She pulled the blanket from her trouser leg, letting him see the wet wool, the mud, the evidence. "Yes, I crossed. Yes, my brother is alive, and your paperwork killed him two years ago, and I'm the one who corrected it. Arrest me or don't. But don't stand there asking whether it was worth it, Colonel, because you already know the answer, and it's the reason you don't sleep in February."

The silence stretched. Down the corridor, one of the military policemen shifted his weight. Grell had drifted closer without seeming to move, the way she did everything.

Ashe looked at her for a long time. And then he did something she had not predicted in any of the hundred versions of this morning she had rehearsed on the ice.

He reached into his coat and produced a notebook—small, black, government-issue—and held it, but did not open it.

"There are discrepancies," he said quietly, "in your intake records going back six years. Names received that appear in no report I ever filed. Dozens of them." His thumb moved along the notebook's edge, back and forth, a man touching a scar. "Someone has been keeping a list."

The ward was very quiet. The sleeping men breathed. Outside the frost-furred windows, the sky was going fully gray now, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the river groaned—the long, tectonic, dying-animal sound of the ice beginning to go.

"Someone has been keeping a list," Ashe said again, softer. "I want to know what's in it. And Miss Ishikawa—I want to know it before the river finishes breaking, because after that, neither of us will be able to pretend this winter ever held."

Sable thought of the ledger under the floorboard. Six years of names. Every one a man the war had tried to make disappear, kept alive in pencil by a nurse everyone had learned not to see.

She lifted her chin.

"Read your own records first, Colonel," she said. "Then we'll talk about mine."

Ashe closed his eyes, just briefly, like a man stepping off a ledge—and somewhere below them, the first plate of the river let go with a boom that rattled the windows in their frames.
