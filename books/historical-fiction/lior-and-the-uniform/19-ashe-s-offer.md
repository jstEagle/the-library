## 19. Ashe's Offer

The interrogation room was not a room for interrogations. It had been the linen closet until the war made everything else, and the shelves still smelled of starch and cold iron. Someone had put a table in it, and two chairs, and a lamp with a green shade that turned Colonel Ashe's face the color of old banknotes.

Sable stood because he hadn't asked her to sit. She was still in her crossing clothes—she'd had no time to change, only to strip off the frozen outer layer and pull on whatever hung in her locker—and beneath the clean uniform she could feel the river still living in her skin, a cold that had gotten into the bone and intended to stay.

"You went out at approximately eleven o'clock last night," Ashe said. He did not look up from the paper in front of him. "You returned at approximately four. Your boots were wet through. You signed nothing out of the dispensary, but the pharmacy door was found unlocked this morning, and a vial of laudanum is unaccounted for."

"Was it?"

He looked up then. His eyes were pale, patient, the eyes of a man who had spent two years striking names from lists and had never once needed to raise his voice to do it.

"Nurse Ishikawa. I have read your file. Three years of service. Not one reprimand. Not one late round until this winter." He set the paper down and squared its edges with the table's edge, precisely, as if the paper might feel it. "I am going to ask you where you went, and I would prefer—I genuinely would prefer—that you tell me something I can write down."

The lamplight buzzed. Somewhere beyond the wall, the ward carried on without her: the rattle of a bedpan trolley, a man coughing in the long wet rhythm she knew by heart.

"I walked," she said. "I couldn't sleep. The river helps."

"The river." He said it without contempt, which was worse. "Across the river there is an internment compound holding four hundred enemy aliens. Two nights ago a prisoner named Emil Voss was treated in my hospital for shrapnel wounds sustained under circumstances his escort has been unable to explain satisfactorily. This morning Voss is scheduled for transfer at dawn instead of in ten days, because certain people above me have decided he is more interesting than his paperwork suggests." A pause. "And this morning, Nurse Ishikawa, you came back across my ice."

*My ice.* She kept her face the way she kept her ledger: flat, orderly, legible only to herself.

"If you've already decided what I did," she said, "why ask?"

"Because decisions are cheap." He rose, crossed to the narrow window—there was no view from it, only the brick throat of the lightwell—and stood with his hands clasped behind him like a man at a funeral he approved of. "I have made perhaps six hundred decisions in this post, and every one of them is written down somewhere in my own hand. Do you know what that means? It means when this war ends, and men come looking for someone to blame—and they will come, they always come—there will be a paper trail leading directly to my desk. Every name I struck. Every convoy I reported as lighter than it was. Every family notified of a death that occurred three days later than the record shows, or not at all."

She said nothing. Her heart was behaving badly, hammering against the cold in her chest, but her face held. Three years of practice held.

"Do you know why the names come off the lists?" he asked, still facing the window.

"Because dead men who don't exist can't embarrass anyone."

"No." He turned. For a moment—only a moment—the banknote color dropped away from his face and she saw what was underneath, which was not malice. It was something smaller and sadder than that. "Because a convoy that loses thirty men is a tragedy requiring investigation. A convoy that loses twenty-seven is a statistic requiring a signature. Because a boy of nineteen bleeding out on the dock is a question some general will eventually have to answer for, and I am not important enough to be the answer, so I make sure the question never gets asked." His voice stayed level all the way through, and that levelness was the most damning thing about it. "I don't erase them out of cruelty, Nurse. I erase them because erasure is safe, and I have learned—slowly, at considerable cost—that safety is the only thing I'm any good at anymore."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way the ward tilted when she stood up too fast after a night shift. She had built him into a monster over two years, name by name by name in her ledger, and monsters were simple. Monsters could be hated cleanly. What stood in front of her now was a tired middle-aged man who had chosen cowardice the way other men chose a religion—early, sincerely, and without ever admitting it was a choice.

"That doesn't bring them back," she said. "The names."

"No. Nothing brings them back. That is rather the point." He sat down again, and this time he gestured at the second chair, and she refused it, and he didn't insist. "Here is what happens next. There are two doors out of this room."

* * *

Ashe had rehearsed it. Sable could hear the rehearsal in him—the small pauses where he'd decided a pause should go, the way he laid the two folders on the table side by side like a card trick he'd practiced in a mirror. She almost pitied him for it. Almost.

"Door one." He opened the left folder. Inside was a single sheet, blank except for a heading. "You sign a statement confirming that on the night of the twelfth you took unauthorized leave of the premises, that you were distressed—overwork, grief, the death of your brother, which I understand affected you deeply—and that you wandered the riverbank. In exchange, the matter goes no further. No tribunal. No discharge on your record. A note in your file that no one will ever read." He tapped the blank page once. "And Emil Voss crosses at dawn to a camp where his file says exactly what it said yesterday: foreign national, minor wound, no significance. And the man whose handwriting started all of this remains, officially, deceased. Which, I remind you, is the condition that has kept him alive. Dead men are not transferred east. Dead men are not counted. Dead men are *left alone*, Nurse. Erasure, as you call it, is also armor. Ask yourself whom it has been protecting."

Her hands were steady. She looked at them and willed them steady and they obeyed, and she thought: *Jun's hands do that too. We learned it from Mother.*

"And door two."

He opened the right folder. It was thicker. Tribunal summons. A form requesting her service record. A list of charges typed in the cramped bureaucratic hand that seemed to be the true native language of the war: *unauthorized communication with the enemy; conveyance of documents across a military frontier; conspiracy.*

"In door two," Ashe said quietly, "you confess. Fully. The letter, the ledger—I know about the ledger, Nurse, don't look surprised, I've known since autumn, I simply lacked proof and proof requires effort I preferred not to spend—you confess, and you face a tribunal, and so does Voss, and so does every person who helped you, whether knowingly or not. Your matron. Your orderlies. The lamplighter, if we find him. And the man across the river stops being safely dead and becomes a live case, a documented case, an interned enemy alien whose sister committed treason to reach him. Do you imagine that improves his prospects?" He closed both folders. "Door one, everyone stays invisible and therefore intact. Door two, everyone becomes visible and therefore condemned. Those are the choices. I didn't design them. I'm only the clerk."

For a long moment she let the silence sit between them, because she had learned from watching him that silence was a weapon, and she wanted, just once, to hold it the way he held it.

Then she said: "You're wrong about one thing."

"Which is?"

"You said you didn't design them. But you did. Six hundred decisions, all in your own hand—you told me so yourself. Every erased name was a choice, and every choice narrowed what was possible afterward, until tonight the only doors left are the two cowardly ones." She leaned forward, and the river-cold in her bones made her voice come out lower than she meant it, almost gentle. "That's what cowardice does, Colonel. It never looks like anything in the moment. It looks like prudence. It looks like a signature. And then one day you're sitting in a linen closet offering a nurse a bargain in which the best available outcome is that history stays a lie."

His jaw tightened. Not anger—recognition. She watched him recognize himself and flinch from it, and she understood then, fully, what she had spent two years failing to understand: that Ashe was not the opposite of her. He was what she would become if she ever once wrote a lie in her ledger and called it mercy.

"The names were real people," she said. "Private Aldous Wren. Corporal Teodor Havel. The boy—Milo Brandt, nineteen, drowned on the dock while his intake form was still wet. I have them all. Every one you struck. And here is the thing you've never understood, because you've never had to keep anything true: those names aren't evidence against you. They're evidence *for* you. The day you stop erasing and start recording, you stop being the clerk of a lie. But you won't. Because door one is right there, and it always will be, and it will always be easier."

Ashe looked at her for a long time. Outside, faint through the walls, the river made a sound—a long groaning crack, like a giant shifting in its sleep, like the world's largest door beginning to open.

"The transfer moves at dawn," he said at last, and his voice had changed, gone quieter, almost tired. "Voss leaves at first light. If there is anything you intend to do about any of this, Nurse Ishikawa, I would advise you to do it before then." He gathered the folders, squared their edges again out of habit, and paused with his hand on the left one—the blank confession, the safe door. "For what it's worth. My brother was killed at Tannenberg. The army spelled his name wrong on the notification. Grell, my mother's name—it was Grelle, with two l's, and no one ever corrected it, and my mother died with a memorial plaque on her wall bearing a stranger's name." He met her eyes. "So I know exactly what a misspelled name costs. That is how I know that forgetting is cheaper. Good evening, Nurse."

He left the folders. Both of them. On the table, side by side, like a man laying out instruments before an operation he cannot bear to perform himself.

Sable stood alone in the linen-smelling room with the green lamp buzzing and the river groaning under the ice, and she understood that she had just been given her orders—not by Ashe, but by everything he represented, everything the war was, every blank line where a name should have been.

She did not touch either folder.

She went to find Grell, and to tell her that tomorrow, at rounds, the names would be read aloud.
