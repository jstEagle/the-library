## 20. The Reading of the Names

I did not sleep. I sat on my narrow bed with the ledger open on my knees and read the names until they stopped being ink and became faces again—the boy from the third convoy whose mother's address filled half a page because he had dictated it twice to be sure; the sergeant with the burned hands who had asked me, in the last hour, whether it was raining outside; the three men Colonel Ashe had struck from the intake list in October while I stood close enough to smell his cologne.

Forty-one names. Forty-two if you counted Jun, and I did, though his was written differently—written not as an omission but as a resurrection.

At four o'clock I dressed in clean uniform, pinned my cap straight, and put the ledger inside my coat, against my heart, where Emil had carried his letter across the same river. It seemed the correct place for it. Things that matter travel closest to the blood.

The hospital at that hour was its truest self: lamps turned low, the corridors smelling of carbolic and cold iron, the ward breathing in unison like something enormous and wounded asleep. I walked through it the way I had walked through it for two years, invisible, and understood that by evening I would not be invisible anymore. There was no version of what I intended that left me hidden. That was the point. That had always been the point.

Grell was in her office when I passed it. The door was open a hand's width, which was unusual, and she was standing at the window looking out at the river, which was rotten now, black-veined, groaning in the night like an animal in pain. She did not turn around.

"Rounds are at seven," she said.

"Yes, Matron."

"You look terrible, Nurse Ishikawa."

"Yes, Matron."

A long silence. The river groaned again, a deep tearing sound, and somewhere down the bank the ice shifted and settled with a boom that traveled up through the foundations.

"I have been a matron for nineteen years," Grell said, still facing the window. "Do you know what that means? It means I have learned to see everything and do almost nothing about any of it." She turned then, and her face in the lamplight was older than it ever let itself be during the day. "Whatever you are going to do—and I am not asking you to tell me—you should know that I once watched a man erase my brother from a regimental list because he had spelled Kessler with one *s* and could not admit the error. My mother died believing he died a deserter. He died at Verdun with a rifle in his hands and a misspelled name."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that would not endanger her, and I was done endangering people who did not choose it.

"The pharmacy is locked again," she added. "As it should be." And she closed the door.

* * *

Colonel Ashe arrived at six-forty, earlier than scheduled, which told me he had been awake too, and that whatever decision he had made about me he wanted it finished before breakfast. He came into the ward with two staff officers behind him and his gloves tucked under one arm, and he found me at the central desk checking the night charts as though this were any morning in any winter of this endless war.

"Nurse Ishikawa." He set his cap on the desk, precisely, brim parallel to the edge. "You will be pleased to know I have given your situation considerable thought overnight."

"That's kind of you, Colonel."

"It is not kindness. It is administration." He drew a folded paper from his breast pocket but did not open it. "Here is what will happen. You will surrender the document you removed from these premises. In exchange, no charges will be filed. Your brother remains deceased—a clerical fact, regrettable, but one which protects him, since a dead man cannot be court-martialed for treasonous correspondence. You will be reassigned inland, quietly. Everyone keeps their dignity. This is the generous reading of events, and I advise you to take it, because the other reading ends in a tribunal with your name on the docket beside his."

Behind him, at the far end of the ward, the orderlies had gone still over their basins. Down the rows of beds, men who had been feigning sleep were no longer feigning anything; they were listening. Word traveled in hospitals faster than infection.

"And if I decline?" I said.

"Then I am compelled to act on evidence that will destroy you both. I have no personal appetite for it, Ishikawa. Understand that. I strike names because lists must balance, because a hospital that reports every death becomes a hospital that gets audited, and an audited hospital treats fewer soldiers. Sentiment is a luxury of people who don't run things." He opened the paper at last and laid it flat between us: the order of investigation, already drafted, already signed. "You have until seven o'clock. Which is—" he consulted his watch— "four minutes."

I looked at the order. Then at him. Then at the clock on the wall above the dispensary, whose second hand was moving the way all clocks move, indifferent and unstoppable, and I felt the strange calm that had been building in me all night finally settle into place like a bone going back into joint.

"Colonel," I said, "you may want to sit down."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Seven o'clock is rounds." I took off my coat, folded it over the back of my chair, and lifted the ledger out from where it had lain against my heart. "And rounds are conducted aloud."

* * *

What follows, I set down exactly as it happened, because I have learned that memory softens things and I refuse to soften this.

I began at the first bed on the left, as I always did. Private Aldous Renner, shrapnel, right thigh, improving, slept well. Ordinary. Ashe stood at my shoulder with his officers flanking him and the folded order in his hand, waiting for his four minutes to expire.

At the second bed I paused. Then I turned—not to the chart, but to the ledger in my hands—and I opened it.

"On the ninth of October," I said, in the clear voice I used for dictation, "a convoy arrived at twenty-three hundred hours carrying thirty-one men. The official intake list records twenty-eight." I looked up at Ashe. "Three names were struck before the list was filed. They were struck by your own hand, Colonel. I watched you do it."

The ward went very quiet. Not silent—the ventilators wheezed, a man coughed—but quiet the way a forest goes quiet.

"The first name," I read, "is Corporal Daniel Okafor, aged twenty-two. Died of wounds at oh-four-forty on the tenth of October. His mother lives at 14 Rope Walk, and she received no letter, because officially her son never arrived here."

Ashe moved toward me. I stepped back, out of reach, and kept reading.

"The second name is Private Tomas Wrenn, aged nineteen. He survived the night. He was transferred south on the twelfth, and I cannot tell you what became of him after that, because after that he ceased to exist in any record I have access to. But he existed. I held his hand while the morphine went in."

"Nurse Ishikawa." Ashe's voice was low and very controlled, the voice of a man holding a door shut against a flood. "Close the book."

"The third name," I said, louder, turning so that my voice carried down the whole row of beds, past the listening orderlies, past Matron Grell, who had appeared at the ward door with a tray of instruments she did not need and was not going anywhere, "is Private Elias Brandt, aged seventeen. He lied about his age to enlist. He died at dawn on the eleventh of October, asking whether it was raining. It was not raining. It has not rained in this city since November. But I told him yes, because it was the last kind thing anyone could do for him, and because he deserved to have at least one person in this world attend to the truth of what he needed rather than the truth of the paperwork."

I closed the ledger. Held it against my chest.

"Forty-one names," I said. "Two years of them. Men who died here without ever being here. Men whose mothers wait by windows for letters that will never come because some colonel decided their deaths were inconvenient arithmetic." I looked at Ashe, and I found that I was not angry anymore—that the anger had burned down through the night into something colder and more durable. "You offered me a choice between the book and my brother. I am giving you a different choice."

"There is no different choice," Ashe said. "You have confessed in front of witnesses. Do you understand what you've done?"

"I've done nothing yet that can be punished," I said, "unless you prove it true. That is the beauty of it, Colonel. Every name in this book is either accurate—in which case striking it was falsification of military records, which is a tribunal offense, and I have forty-one counts—or it is inaccurate, in which case I am a hysterical nurse who has embarrassed herself at morning rounds, and you may laugh at me and tear the pages out. Choose."

For a moment—one long, ringing moment—I believed he might actually lunge for the ledger. His hand came up. His jaw worked. Behind him, one of his staff officers, a young major with a bandaged arm, was looking at the floor with an expression I recognized: the expression of a man doing arithmetic of his own, and finding the sums no longer balanced.

It was Grell who ended it. She set her instrument tray down on the nearest bedside table with a clatter like a gunshot.

"Colonel Ashe," she said, "I have served under eleven commanding officers in three wars' worth of years, and I am telling you as Head Matron of this facility, on the record, that every intake discrepancy this nurse describes matches discrepancies I logged myself and was ordered to destroy. Ordered, sir. By your office." She folded her hands. "You may arrest us both. I would recommend the larger room. There are rather a lot of witnesses."

From the beds, a sound I had never heard in this ward: applause. Faint, ragged—men clapping with burned hands, clapping with splinted arms, a slow dry rustle like rain beginning. The boy in bed nine, Wrenn's replacement in the corner cot, started it, and it spread.

Ashe looked around the ward—at the orderlies standing straight as fence posts, at Grell immovable as a monument, at the major studying the floor, at the men applauding him with their ruined hands—and I watched something happen in his face that I suspect had not happened in it for many years. Not shame. Shame requires imagination. Something before shame: the sudden, vertiginous knowledge of being seen.

"This is insubordination," he said quietly.

"Yes," I agreed. "So is erasing a dead boy. One of us chose the better insubordination."

He gathered his cap from the desk. He picked up his folded order, looked at it, and did not tear it, because he was not a man capable of grand gestures even in defeat—he folded it smaller instead, and put it away, and I understood that it was not destroyed, only postponed, and that the war between us had merely changed fronts.

"You will be relieved of duty pending review," he said. "Effective now. Hand over your keys."

I gave him the ring of ward keys, all except one, which I had already returned to its hook in the pharmacy where Grell had left it unlocked for me weeks ago and which had never been mine to keep.

At the ward door he stopped. He did not turn fully around—only his head, in profile, gray at the temples, jaw tight.

"The river is breaking up," he said. "They'll have the exchange lists posted within the fortnight, once the ferries run. If a name appears on one that shouldn't exist"—his eyes flicked, just once, toward the ledger—"it will be because someone with authority arranged it. Not because a nurse crossed ice." And then he was gone, boots ringing down the corridor, his officers hurrying after him, and the applause rose again behind him, louder this time, following him out like weather.

* * *

Afterward, Grell found me sitting on the floor of the linen room, because my legs had finally informed me that the night had cost them something.

She lowered herself onto a crate of folded sheets with a grunt, which was the most undignified thing I had ever seen her do, and handed me a cup of tea without comment. We drank. Somewhere below us the river boomed again, longer this time, a sound like a door opening.

"He gave you nothing," she said at last.

"He gave me time," I said. "The exchange lists. A fortnight." My hands were shaking now that it was permitted, rattling the cup against its saucer. "Matron—if Jun's name is on those lists—"

"Then someone must be on the platform to meet him." She blew on her tea. "And someone must have packed that young man's things, because I intend to dismiss you formally by week's end, and a dismissed nurse travels light."

I stared at her. "You're dismissing me."

"I'm dismissing you *loudly*, girl, with paperwork and witnesses, so that when they look for someone to punish they find a closed case instead of an open wound." Her mouth twitched, which on Grell's face constituted open weeping. "Nineteen years, I told you. I have learned to see everything. I am too old to learn to do nothing about all of it."

Outside the little window, the first gray light came up over the river, and the ice sang—long, rising, cracking notes, the sound of a season dying and another being born. I sat on the linen room floor with tea I couldn't drink and shook like the last leaf of winter, and I thought: *the lists post within a fortnight. The ferries run when the thaw allows. Hold on, Jun. Cross the water. Come home.*

Then the orderly came running down the corridor, breathless, calling my name—and behind him, from the direction of the river road, a bell began to ring.
