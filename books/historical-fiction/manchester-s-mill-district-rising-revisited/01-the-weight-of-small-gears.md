## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The mantel clock had stopped at eleven minutes past four, and Mrs. Ellery swore it had been that hour for six days running, as though the clock itself had decided to grieve and would not be argued out of it.

Bram Zhou did not argue with clocks. They listened to them.

The shop was narrow enough that Bram could stand at the bench in the center and touch both walls with outstretched fingers if they tried, and the ceiling sloped low over the back corner where the lathe sat, so that anyone taller than Bram's five feet four inches had to bow when they came to watch the work. Above the chandler's on Mill Row, the whole room smelled of tallow drifting up through the floorboards and, beneath it, the cleaner bitterness of oil of clove and metal filings — the smell Bram had breathed since before they could reach the bench without a stool. It was, they supposed, what home smelled like. They had never had another to compare it against.

Mrs. Ellery stood by the door with her hands folded into her apron, watching Bram work the way people watched surgeons or priests: with hope and dread in equal measure, and no understanding whatsoever of what was being done on her behalf.

"It were my husband's," she said, for perhaps the fourth time. "He wound it every Sunday of his life. Forty-one years."

"Then it knows him better than most parsons would," Bram said, not looking up. Their loupe was set tight against their right eye; through it, the escape wheel swelled into a landscape, its fifteen teeth each worn at the tip like old men's boots. "The mainspring's let go. Not broken — tired. I can temper it back."

"Can you? Truly?"

"I can." Bram said it plainly because plainness cost less than reassurance. Reassurance was a currency, and the Zhous were short of every currency there was.

What Bram did not say — what they never said aloud, though the arithmetic ran beneath everything like groundwater — was *for free*. Mrs. Ellery had come in with her husband's coat over her arm, meaning to sell it, and had asked instead, in a voice she'd tried to make casual, whether clockwork could be mended on credit. Aldous had gone very still behind the counter, the way he did whenever money was mentioned within earshot, and Bram had caught his eye and answered before he could. *Leave it with me, Mrs. Ellery. We'll see what we see.*

Which meant free. Which meant the rent coin that might have come from her would not come from her, and the ledger in Bram's head — the one they kept without wanting to, the one that measured every object in this room by what it could be sold for — gained another entry in the column marked *given away*.

They lifted the movement from the case with both hands, cradling it like something newborn and ancient at once. Brass gears, steel springs, the great flat plates drilled and pinned by some journeyman's hands forty years dead. Bram set it on the leather cushion and bent close.

There was a language in these things. Most people heard only ticking, if they heard anything at all, but Bram had learned young that a clock spoke constantly about its own health. This one had a hitch in its third wheel — a stutter every ninth tooth, faint as a swallowed cough — and the pallet stones were glazed smooth where they should have gripped. Somewhere in its long life someone had oiled it with lamp oil instead of clock oil, a sin Bram had forgiven in a hundred customers' clocks and would forgive in this one too. People did what they could with what they had. That was the whole of Manchester in a sentence.

"You'll want it by Sunday?" Bram asked.

"If it's no trouble."

"No trouble." A small lie, worn smooth from use.

Behind them, at the counter, coins clicked against wood.

Bram knew that sound the way sailors knew weather. Click, slide, pause. Click, click, pause. Aldous counting, and recounting, because the first count always came up wrong and the second count was only permitted to confirm the first. Bram did not turn around. Turning around would mean seeing Uncle Aldous's face, and seeing his face would mean acknowledging the number, and the number was the same number it had been yesterday and the day before: not enough. It was always not enough. The number had a shape Bram could have drawn from memory — a shallow dish, always a shilling or three shy of the rim, and the rim was Mr. Prosser the landlord, who collected on Fridays and whose boots Bram could hear crossing the yard below even now, though it was only Tuesday.

*The lathe,* said the voice in Bram's head, unbidden, doing its terrible inventory. *Forty pounds new, thirty secondhand, twenty-five if you found a fool. The glass case by the window: twelve. The stock of watch glasses: two shillings the dozen. Grandfather's tools —*

No. Not those. The inventory voice went quiet whenever it reached the tool roll, the way a hand goes quiet near a flame. Some things were not for sale, and Bram kept them off the list by an act of will that cost more than any coin.

Grandfather Wen had carried that roll from Canton to Liverpool and Liverpool to Manchester, and inside it lived files finer than hairs, tweezers, a broach set, and a screwdriver ground down so small its blade fit screws no bigger than a grain of barley. He had died at this bench. Bram had been nine. What they remembered most clearly was not grief but the weight of the roll in their own two hands afterward, too heavy to lift properly, and Mother saying, *It's yours now, little wren. Mind it holds you as much as you hold it.*

Mother. Two years gone, and still the thought of her arrived whole, unannounced, like weather.

Bram bent lower over the escapement and let the work do what work did: close the world down to the width of a gear tooth. There was mercy in that. The hands knew what to do even when the heart did not. They drew the glaze off the pallet stones with fine emery, breathing slow, and the hitch in the third wheel turned out to be a burr raised by some long-ago clumsy winding, and they stoned it flush, and when they reassembled the train and gave the barrel its first careful turns, the escapement took up its tick-tock like a man taking up a familiar burden — willingly, wearily, faithfully.

"There," Bram murmured. To the clock, really. They talked to clocks. Marisol teased them for it mercilessly, and Bram defended it on principle: everything made by human hands deserved to be spoken to kindly, since human hands had made it and human hands would someday unmake it.

* * *

Aldous counted the rent money a third time while Bram ate standing up at the counter — bread, dripping, tea stewed to the color of the canal. The shop closed at seven in winter, which was to say the shutters went up when the light failed, and after that the two of them existed in the amber circle of one oil lamp among the shapes of clocks, a dozen of them hanging silent on the walls like sleeping birds.

"Fourteen and six," Aldous said. "Prosser wants seventeen."

"He wants nineteen," Bram said. "He told me nineteen last week when you weren't here. He tells you seventeen because he thinks you'll believe it's a kindness."

Aldous's jaw worked. He was fifty-one and looked older, a big man gone soft at the edges, with his brother Wen's heavy bones and none of Wen's stillness. He'd been a warehouseman for twenty years before the shop fell to him, and he still handled money like cargo — counting it, weighing it, stacking it — as though it might be loaded somewhere and shipped off to profit.

"I'll speak to him," Aldous said.

"You spoke to him Thursday. He laughed at you."

"He didn't laugh."

"He did, Uncle. I heard it through the floor." Bram set down the bread. They hadn't meant to say it that way, but there it was, lying between them on the counter. "I'll take the regulator to Tibb's on Deansgate. He'll give eight pound for it, maybe nine if I clean the dial first—"

"You'll do no such thing."

"It's sitting there doing nothing. It's been doing nothing for a year."

"That regulator was your grandfather's commission piece. He built it for the Corn Exchange waiting room. It has his name on the dial."

"It has his name on the dial and dust on the pendulum, and neither pays rent." Bram heard their own voice go flat and hard and hated it a little, and kept going anyway, because the alternative was to swallow it, and swallowing it meant the list in their head grew longer and darker until one morning they woke up measuring Marisol's coat by what it would fetch. "I'm not saying sell the tools. I'm saying sell the thing that isn't alive anymore."

Aldous looked at them across the lamplight for a long moment. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register he used for hard truths delivered gently, which was worse than shouting.

"Your mother sold her wedding ring to keep this shop open the winter you were fourteen. She never told you that. She told me to never tell you, and I've kept it two years past her grave, and I'm telling you now because you're talking about selling your grandfather's name off a clock face like it's spare brass." He pushed the coins together into a stack, squared it, squared it again. "We are not selling the shop's soul to pay a landlord who'll raise it again by spring. We find another way."

"What way?" Bram asked. "Name it. I'll do it tonight."

And Aldous, who had an answer for everything, who could talk a chandler out of a bad debt and a bailiff out of a doorway, looked at the stack of coins worth fourteen and six against a debt of nineteen, and said nothing at all.

The silence filled the shop. In it, Bram became aware of all the clocks at once — the widow's mantel clock ticking steadily on the bench where they'd left it, the wall regulators with their pendulums still, the carriage watches in their drawer, all of it dead weight and dead time, and themselves at the center of it, twenty years old, with hands good enough to mend anything mechanical in the district and no mechanism on earth for mending this.

Marisol came up the outside stair at half past six, letting in a gust of November and the smell of raw cotton. She was sixteen and worked the sorting floor at Ashworth's mill, and she brought the mill home with her in her hair, her collar, her lungs — Bram could hear it in the way she cleared her throat at the door, twice, quietly, thinking no one noticed.

Bram noticed. Bram noticed everything. It was the curse of the trade.

"Prosser's boys chalked the door again," Marisol said, shrugging off her shawl. "Big X. Mam always said a chalked door means they mean business."

"A chalked door means Prosser's a coward who won't knock," Bram said, and got the ghost of a grin from her, and was glad of it, and filed away alongside it the fact that she'd coughed again on the stairs, that her knuckles were cracked raw from the cotton oil, that she'd eaten her bread in four bites like someone who'd learned hunger early and expected it back.

She dropped onto the stool by the fire — the one luxury, a grate barely bigger than a hat — and held her hands out to it. "Anything happen today?"

"A widow's clock," Bram said. "Free."

"Bram."

"Don't start."

"She's got the pension, hasn't she? Ellery's widow?"

"Pensions don't cover clockwork."

"And what covers us?" Marisol said it without heat, which somehow made it heavier. She was like that, Marisol — she could put a knife into a conversation and leave it lying there flat, not twisted, just present, so everyone had to step around it. "Tibb's wants an apprentice, doesn't he? Proper wage. You could—"

"I'm not leaving the shop."

"The shop's leaving us, Bram. Can't you feel it? It's like standing on ice that's already talking."

Bram could feel it. That was precisely the trouble. Every object in the room had a price tag hanging off it in their mind's eye, and the sum of all the tags was less than the debt, and the difference between those two numbers was the sound the ice made when it talked.

Aldous cleared his throat. "There's work coming," he said, with the forced brightness of a man reading weather in a clear sky. "Mark me. Something will turn."

Something always turned, Aldous believed. Bram loved him for believing it and did not share the belief, because Bram had spent their whole life watching mechanisms, and mechanisms taught you one law above all others: a system either runs or it runs down, and wishing does not wind the spring.

* * *

They worked late. Mrs. Ellery's clock needed its case cleaned and its dial touched — the numerals had gone brown with age and tobacco smoke — and Bram sat alone after the others went to bed, at the bench, under the lamp, doing the small patient work that made the hours pass without asking anything of them.

This was the hour Bram liked best and trusted least. Hands busy, house quiet, the tick of the mended clock keeping time beside them like a companion. In this hour the mind wandered where it shouldn't. To Mother, mostly. Two years, and Bram still caught themselves turning to tell her something — a clever repair, a joke of Marisol's — and finding the air where she should be. She had died in February, quick and unfair, a fever that took her in nine days, and she had died with her affairs in order, which was like her, and with certain matters closed, which Bram had never questioned until lately. Locked boxes. A locked box, actually, one, in the chest under her bed, which Aldous had burned the papers from unopened, saying there was nothing in it but debts, and Bram had believed him, because why wouldn't they believe him?

The clock struck — Bram had set it going at dusk — a small domestic chime, ten notes, honest to the minute. Bram checked it against the observatory bell far off across the rooftops, the great boom that ruled the district's time, and found the little clock true to within a breath.

Everything in its right hour, Bram thought. Everything except us.

They were cleaning the last of the polish off the case glass when feet sounded on the outside stair — quick, official feet, boots with heels, not the soft tread of neighbors. At twenty minutes past midnight, on a Tuesday, nobody climbed to a failing clockmaker's shop above a chandler's except trouble or fortune, and in Bram's experience the two wore the same boots.

Aldous appeared in the doorway of the back room already half-dressed, having heard the same thing, his face gray in the lamplight. Below, in the yard, a dog began to bark.

Then came the knock: three blows, precise as a hammer on a gong.

Bram set down the cloth. On the bench, Mrs. Ellery's clock ticked on, indifferent, keeping perfect time in a house that had run out of it.
