## 2. Summons at the Observatory

The knock came again, three blows, patient as a creditor.

Bram went down the stairs with the lamp held high, past the chandler's storeroom where the smell of tallow and salt rope seeped through the floorboards, into the yard where the dog had worked itself to a frenzy against its chain. The night was the wet, close kind that Manchester did so well—soot-laden air pressing down on the mill-row like a lid—and in the yellow circle of lamplight stood a boy of perhaps fifteen, dressed in livery too fine for any street south of Piccadilly. Navy wool, brass buttons polished to a shine, and beneath his arm a leather case stamped with a crest Bram did not recognize but knew immediately to be expensive.

"You're the clockmaker?" the boy asked, and then, taking in the apron, the oil-stained fingers, the lamp trembling only slightly, corrected himself: "The apprentice. I'm meant to ask for Bram Zhou."

"I'm both, most nights." Bram kept their voice level. "It's past ten."

"He said you'd say that." The boy produced an envelope from the case—a heavy envelope, cream-colored, sealed—and held it out as though it might bite him. "I'm to wait for an answer."

*He*, Bram noted. Not *they*. Someone had given this boy instructions precise enough to anticipate refusal. They broke the seal with a thumbnail, angling the lamp so the light fell full on the page.

The paper was thick enough to have opinions of its own. The handwriting was copperplate, clerk-trained, each letter standing at attention:

*To the keeper of the establishment known as Zhou & Son, Clockmakers, Angel Meadow—*

*Aldous is not a son,* Bram thought, and then, absurdly, *and neither am I, whatever the name implies.*

*—You are respectfully requested to attend upon the Observatory at Heaton Park to undertake the examination and repair of the regulator clock, which has fallen into disrepair such that its striking work can no longer be relied upon. Payment shall be treble the customary rate, plus expenses of conveyance. Present this summons at the north door at your earliest convenience tomorrow. Ask for Mr. Petch.*

Bram read it twice. Treble the rate. For a regulator clock—the great astronomical regulators were temperamental creatures, jeweled pivots and mercury pendulums, but even so, no repair of ordinary difficulty commanded such terms. Either the clock was dying in some spectacular fashion, or whoever wrote letters on paper like this wanted something other than a repair done quickly.

"Who gave you this?" Bram asked.

"A gentleman." The boy shifted his weight. "At the observatory. I'm not told names, miss." A pause; he squinted. "Sir."

"Bram will do fine," they said, and watched relief flood the boy's face, because in 1847 a person who could be neither safely addressed was best addressed by nothing at all. "Tell your gentleman the shop opens at seven. I'll come before eight."

The boy touched his cap, hesitated. "He said—I'm to say it's urgent-like. But gentle-urgent. He made me say it back twice."

"Gentle-urgent," Bram repeated. The dog had finally exhausted itself and sat panting at the end of its chain, regarding them both with professional interest. "That's a new one. Go on home, before someone mistakes that coat for money."

They watched him out of sight past the chandler's wall, then stood a moment longer in the yard, the envelope in hand, feeling the particular weight of it—not paper-weight but consequence-weight, the way a summons differs from a letter the way a hook differs from a finger.

Above them, Aldous's window scraped open.

"Is it the bailiffs?"

"No."

"The parish? Because if it's the parish about the rates—"

"It's the observatory." Bram held up the envelope. "Heaton Park. They want the master clock repaired. Treble payment."

A silence, long enough that Bram counted four ticks from the shop above—Mrs. Ellery's mantel clock, still on the bench, still perfect, still unsold. Then the window shut, and footsteps crossed the ceiling toward the stairs, and by the time Bram climbed back up, Aldous was at the bench with the letter held close to the lamp, reading it the way he read everything: twice, slowly, lips moving, hunting for the trap between the lines.

"Treble," he said.

"So it says."

"No one pays treble for a regulator." He set the letter down and rubbed his thumb along the crest embossed on the wax fragments, though the wax would tell him nothing. His hands were good hands, Aldous's—broad, careful, stained at the knuckle with fifty years of oil and brass—but lately they shook when they thought no one was watching. "Your father repaired the Ellery mantels for forty years and never saw more than double, and that was for a church."

"Maybe it's a church clock."

"At an observatory." Aldous laughed, short and without humor. "Bram. Listen to me now." He pulled out the stool, sat heavily, and for a moment looked older than the shop, older than the trade, older than the century. "I've been poor my whole life, and I've been near rich people maybe six times, and every time it cost me more than it paid. Rich doors don't open onto kindness. Rich doors open onto rich problems. You go through them thinking you're carrying tools, and you come out carrying something else entirely, and there's no setting it down after."

"What else would I be carrying?"

"That's just it. You don't know till you're out the far side." He tapped the letter once, decisively, as if sealing an argument. "Send word we're honored but engaged."

"We are engaged. To starvation." Bram took the letter back and folded it along its original creases. On the shelf behind Aldous, the inventory of their decline arranged itself without invitation: the good lathe, worth perhaps two pounds if anyone in Manchester wanted a secondhand lathe, which no one did; the box of watch jewels, worth less than its velvet lining; the silver-cased lever watch Father had worn at his funeral and which Mother had refused to sell through two years of worsening winter, and which Bram had priced, silently, guiltily, at three pounds ten, over and over, like counting rosary beads. Rent fell due in nine days. The coin box held eleven shillings and the coins had begun to develop a familiar ring, the ring of coins counted too often.

"We take it," Bram said.

Aldous looked at them for a long moment. Whatever he saw in their face made him look away first, which was rare, and he busied himself straightening tools that were already straight. "Take Marisol with you, then. No—" He answered himself before Bram could speak. "No, she shouldn't walk that far. She's been gray all week."

"She says the dust at the mill is bad this season."

"Dust." Aldous said the word the way some men said *fever*, quietly, hoping to starve it of breath. "Dust. Aye." He turned back to the bench and picked up Mrs. Ellery's clock, holding it as though it might confess something. "Go, then. Tomorrow. And Bram—" He didn't finish. He set the clock down instead and wound it, though it needed no winding, because winding was what Aldous did instead of praying.

* * *

Morning came up the color of dishwater, and Bram went out into it with the tool bag over one shoulder and the family's hunger in their chest, walking north through streets that grew progressively cleaner the further they got from the mills, as though the city itself were embarrassed by its own industry and swept it southward to the poor.

Ancoats gave way to Collyhurst; Collyhurst to the green edge of Heaton Park, where the trees stood in the damp morning like a congregation waiting for something to begin. The observatory rose at the top of the hill—a squat stone drum crowned with its dome, built by men who believed the heavens ran on schedule and had never spent a week in Angel Meadow. Bram stopped at the gate and let themselves feel, for exactly thirty seconds, the thing they would later refuse to admit feeling: hope. Clean, cautious, arithmetic hope. Treble rate. Expenses of conveyance. Nine days to rent. If the mechanism wanted real work—a broken escapement, worn pallets—it could mean a fortnight's employment at wages that would keep the bailiff from the door until spring.

Then they climbed the hill, presented the summons at the north door as instructed, and met Mr. Petch.

Petch was not what the letter had prepared them for. Bram had imagined a clerk, or an astronomer—ink-stained, abstracted, a man whose eyes lived in telescopes. What opened the door was a small, round, sweating man in shirtsleeves, with the red knuckles of someone who washed his hands often and thoroughly and recently, and eyes that performed the trick of meeting yours while never actually resting on anything about you. He looked at the summons, not at Bram. He looked at the tool bag, not at Bram. When he finally did look, it was briefly, like a man checking a pocket watch.

"You'll be the clockmaker's apprentice."

"I'll be the one who fixes your clock."

Something flickered across Petch's face—amusement, or its respectable cousin. "This way. Mind the step."

Inside, the observatory smelled of stone and lamp-oil and something faintly medicinal, like a room that had recently been scrubbed of something. They passed through a corridor lined with instrument cases under dust sheets, each sheeted shape standing like furniture in a dead man's house, and up a spiral stair into the clock chamber.

The master clock occupied its own room, as befitted it: a tall case of black walnut, glazed, standing against the east wall with the gravity of an altar. Through the glass Bram could see the pendulum swinging, slow and solemn, and hear the tick—deep, deliberate, the heartbeat of a building that told the whole district what time it was. Every clock in Manchester's public buildings took its correction from this one. Bram knew that the way they knew their own pulse.

"There she is," Petch said, and there was something in his voice Bram couldn't place at first, and then could: relief, but the wrong kind. Not a man relieved that help had arrived. A man relieved that responsibility was about to change hands.

Bram set down the bag, unlocked the case, and put their ear to the movement before touching anything, as Father had taught them before they were tall enough to reach the bench. Listen first. The clock tells you what ails it, if you speak its language.

What it said troubled them.

The going train was sound—better than sound, immaculate, oiled within the last month. But the strike train had a hesitation in it, a fractional drag between the release and the lift, the sort of fault that wouldn't show on any test shorter than a day and would never, ever be found by a man who merely listened at the case door. Bram drew out the count wheel and held it to the light.

Someone had been here. Recently. The retaining click showed fresh tool marks, bright metal in the shadow of old patina, and one of the locking pins had been eased a hair's width out of true—enough to delay the hammer, enough to make the hour fall slightly late. Not broken. *Adjusted.* A clockmaker's sabotage, invisible to laymen, deniable by experts.

"When was she last serviced?" Bram asked, not turning around.

Silence. Long enough to be an answer.

"Mr. Petch. When was she last serviced?"

"Before my time," Petch said. "I keep the records. The records say... the records are incomplete."

Bram turned. Petch was standing by the door, and he had positioned himself, Bram noticed, between Bram and the stairs, whether by habit or design. His eyes went to the count wheel in Bram's hand and away again, quick as a debtor's.

"Your letter said the striking work can't be relied on," Bram said. "That's true. It's also true it's been made unreliable. Deliberately, and by someone who understood what they were doing." They set the wheel down carefully, aligning it precisely where it had rested, and watched Petch watch them do it. "Who commissioned this repair? Your letter wasn't signed beyond a name to ask for. Yours. Who sent you to send for me?"

"The committee."

"Which committee?"

"The observatory committee." Petch produced a handkerchief and blotted his forehead, though the chamber was cold. "Mr. Zhou. Miss. Bram." He stumbled on the name and recovered badly. "I'll be honest with you, since you'll find out anyway. I didn't choose you. I was given your shop's name and told to write the letter, and told the terms, and told you'd come despite them being strange, because strange terms buy obedience faster than fair ones." He attempted a smile; it died of exposure. "There's a gentleman who takes an interest in this clock. He'll want to know you found the pin."

"And if I tell him?"

Petch looked at Bram then, fully, for the first time—and what was in his face was not menace, which Bram could have handled, but fear, which spread the way spilled ink spreads, finding every seam. "I'd think very carefully," he said quietly, "about what a young person with a failing shop and nine days' rent owing can afford to know."

Bram hadn't told him about the rent.

They stood in the cold chamber while the pendulum swung its slow arc between them, keeping a time that was no longer trustworthy, and Bram heard themselves say, in a voice that came out steadier than they had any right to expect:

"I'll need the key to the case. And I'll want the service ledger. And I'll start today."

Petch nodded, already backing toward the stair. "The ledger's missing pages," he said, at the door. "I'd be doing you no kindness to pretend otherwise. There's a letter in the case base—left from before, tucked behind the weight shell. I'm told it's yours to find or not find, as you see fit. That's all I was instructed to say." He swallowed. "Gentle-urgent. That's how he put it to me too."

The door closed behind him. Bram stood alone with the great clock, listening to it tick its altered, lying time into the morning, and thought of Aldous at the bench last night, winding a clock that needed no winding, and of the words *rich doors open onto rich problems*, and of the bright tool marks on a pin that should have been dark with years.

Then they knelt, and reached behind the weight shell, and their fingers closed on paper.

An envelope, sealed with wax, addressed in a hand Bram knew better than their own—

Their mother's handwriting.

Two years dead, and writing to them from inside a clock.
