## 4. Sister of the Sorting Floor

The lint came down like weather.

Marisol Zhou had learned to read it the way other girls read clouds. There was the fine white drift that hung in the high rafters all day and never settled, and there was the heavier fall that gathered in the troughs between the sorting tables by the second bell, thick enough to scoop, and there was the grey breath of it that rode every exhalation out into the yard at dusk so that the whole shift walked home haloed, ghosted, each one trailing a faint visible soul of cotton. She was seventeen and she had breathed Manchester's lint since she was nine, and she thought of it now as a thing with intentions. It wanted to be inside her. It got inside everyone eventually; the older women coughed it up in grey ropes and spat into the sawdust buckets without breaking rhythm, and Marisol copied them exactly, because copying was how you survived a sorting floor.

"You're slow today," said Hannah Oakes, two tables down, not unkindly. Hannah was forty and had hands like tanned leather and could pick boll-weevil damage out of an American bale faster than any machine the owners dreamed of buying.

"I'm not slow."

"You are. You missed a stained lock on the Patel bale and Mrs. Clegg saw you miss it." Hannah's fingers never stopped moving, flicking, flicking, sorting the raw cotton into grades while her eyes stayed on Marisol. "Third time this fortnight."

"Then she can dock me." Marisol pulled the next bale toward her and slit the ties. "She'll dock me whether I'm fast or slow, so I'd rather be slow."

Hannah snorted. Around them the floor roared its ordinary roar — the opening of bales, the shriek of the willow machine beyond the partition, forty women's voices raised over both — but beneath the roar ran the other sound, the one that had arrived three days ago and would not leave: whispering. It moved along the tables like fire along a fuse. *Ancoats.* Always Ancoats. A weaver's child dead on George Street. Two more families shut up in their rooms. The doctor from the dispensary seen carrying his bag at a run near the canal. And under all of it, the word nobody said above a breath: *cholera.*

Marisol sorted. Her hands knew the work — coarse from fine, clean from stained, Egyptian from Surat — and her mind went where it liked, which lately was nowhere good.

Her brother Bram would tell her to stop listening. Bram, who mended clocks upstairs over the chandler's on Mill Row, who believed that everything wrong with the world was a thing that could be taken apart, understood, and put back together truer than before. Bram had hands like theirs — like their mother's — quick and exact and always slightly ink-stained. Bram would say: *rumor is a loose gear, Mari. It rattles because nothing is broken yet.*

But Bram wasn't here, on the floor, when Mrs. Clegg came down the aisle at the midday break with a slate in her hand and her mouth set in the particular line it took when the office had sent down instructions.

"Names," Mrs. Clegg said. "I've to take names. Any of you with a cough that won't clear. Any of you been sick this week. The overseers want a list."

The floor went quiet in a way the floor never went quiet. Even the willow seemed to hold its breath behind its partition.

"What list?" said old Bridget Flannery, who had worked the floor since before Marisol was born and had earned the right to ask questions in a flat voice that expected answers.

"A list for the doctors." Mrs. Clegg wouldn't look at anyone. She looked at her slate. "There's sickness in Ancoats, as you'll have heard. The Board of Health wants to know who's poorly in every mill. It's for your own good, they say."

"For our own good." Bridget turned the phrase over like a counterfeit coin. "And what happens to the names on the list?"

"They're for the doctors, I told you—"

"And after the doctors? Do the ones on the list keep their places? Or do they get sent home to be locked up with the rest, and their places given to girls off the boats from Ireland who'll work for bread?"

Mrs. Clegg's silence answered it. Everyone heard it answer. Somewhere near the windows a girl began to cry quietly, without changing the motion of her hands, which was the Manchester way of crying, learned early, practiced daily.

Marisol felt something cold walk up her spine. Because she had a cough. It had started small, a tickle in the throat she blamed on the dust, and dust was a fair blame, everyone had the dust-cough, half the floor hacked like consumptive choirs from first bell to last. But hers had changed in the last two days. It had gone deeper. It had begun to arrive in fits that bent her double and left her tasting copper, and last night —

Last night was why she stood now with a folded square of cloth pressed against her ribs, inside her apron, where no one could see it.

"No names," Bridget said loudly, into the quiet. "No one gives their name. We cough together or not at all."

"We'll all lose our places—" someone began.

"We'll lose them anyway, once they start listing us. A listed girl is a gone girl. You know it and they know it." Bridget looked down the long aisles of bent heads. "Who here wants their name wrote down?"

No one did. The silence said so. Even the crying girl stopped crying, because crying marked you too.

Mrs. Clegg wrote nothing. She left the way she'd come, slate empty, and the roar of the floor rose again around the shape of the thing nobody would name.

* * *

The rest of the shift passed in the particular concentration of a woman pretending very hard to be well.

Marisol had gotten good at pretending. She'd learned it young, watching their mother do it — watching Ellen Zhou stand in the shop doorway with her back straight and her voice even, telling customers the clockmaker would see them shortly, while behind her the rent money dwindled and the illness that would kill her went about its business in her chest without once being spoken of aloud. Their mother had died the way she'd lived: declining to be an inconvenience. Marisol used to think that was strength. Now, with her own folded cloth growing warm against her ribs, she suspected it was something else, something she had inherited along with the dark eyes and the stubbornness, and she hated the inheritance and clutched it anyway.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd done a hundred times since morning, doing it again now as her fingers flew through the Surat bale: if she gave her name, she left the floor today or tomorrow. If she left the floor, she lost her wage. If she lost her wage, the shop lost the six shillings a week that kept the rent paid while Bram's repairs trickled in thin as winter light. Uncle Aldous counted coins like a man counting his own heartbeats; she'd watched him do it last night through the open door, lips moving, and the total had made him close his eyes. If the rent failed, they were on the street, and the street in a quarantine month was not a metaphor. People spoke of the fever-houses the way sailors spoke of reefs. You didn't come back from them to tell the shape of them.

And if she gave her name and it *was* the cholera — she made herself finish the thought, made herself look at it straight — then being locked in a fever-house meant dying among strangers, in a ward that smelled of chloride of lime, with a stranger holding her hand if any hand was held at all. Whereas if she stayed home and it was the cholera, at least she'd die — 

*If,* she corrected herself fiercely. *It isn't. It's dust. It's only ever been dust.*

She coughed then, a short hard fit she smothered against her shoulder, and when she took the cloth from her apron pocket at the end bell, in the privy stall where the light came through a gap in the boards like a blade, she unfolded it and held it up and let herself see what she'd been refusing to see since dawn.

Blood. Not much. A flecking, rust-brown, dried now, like old iron stains on the kerbstones after rain. From the lungs, or from somewhere the lungs were connected to. She knew enough — everyone on the floor knew enough, they were women, they mended each other and birthed each other and buried each other — she knew that blood from deep in the chest was never dust.

She folded the cloth small again, small as a coin, and put it back against her ribs.

"Not me," she said aloud, to the stall, to the gap of light, to whatever ledger the city was already writing her name into. "Not yet. You don't get me yet."

* * *

She walked home the long way, which was also the honest way, past the canal where the evening barges sat low in the water with their loads of outbound goods, past the mill gates where the night shift funnelled in pale as moths. Rumors walked beside her the whole distance, wearing different voices. A boy selling watercress swore the cordon posts were already being built at the district corners — he'd seen the timber stacked by the toll house, he'd *seen* it. Two dyers' lads said no, it was the church bells that would tell you, the bells would change their pattern when the cordons were coming, that was the arrangement between the parishes and the magistrates, everyone knew it. Everyone knew everything and nothing was certain except the lint in her lungs and the six shillings a week and the folded cloth riding warm against her side like a second, smaller heart.

At the top of the chandler's stairs she paused, as she always paused, hand on the rail, listening. This was habit from childhood: you listened before you entered, to learn which version of the family you were walking into. Tonight she heard Bram's voice, low, reading figures aloud — Bram did that when working, recited sums and escapements and the names of gear trains like a litany — and beneath it the scratch of a pen, and beneath *that* a silence from the back room where Aldous kept the strongbox, a silence with weight to it, the silence of a man sitting very still among bad numbers.

She climbed the last steps and opened the door on lamplight and the smell of oil and metal, the smell of home, and Bram looked up with ink on their fingers and that expression — the new one, the one that had lived on Bram's face for two days now, ever since the summons to the observatory — an expression like someone carrying a full cup through a crowded room.

"You're late," Bram said. "Clegg dock you?"

"She tried to make a list instead." Marisol hung her shawl. Her throat tightened around the words and she pushed them out evenly, evenly, the way her mother would have. "Sick names. For the doctors. Bridget Flannery killed it stone dead."

Bram's face did something complicated. "A list. Of the sick."

"That's what I said." She sat at the table where a cold supper waited and made herself eat, though the swallowing hurt and the food tasted of nothing but tin. Across the room Bram watched her a moment too long, and Marisol met the look and gave back nothing, gave back the flat competent face of a sorting girl at the end of shift, because she was good at pretending, she was the best in the family at it, she'd had the most practice lately.

"Cough's worse," Bram said. Not a question. Bram noticed everything; it was the clockmaker in them.

"It's the dust. It's everyone's cough."

"Mari."

"It's the *dust*." Sharper than she meant. She softened it with the oldest weapon in their sibling arsenal: "You should hear the stories going round the floor. They say the bells will change pattern when the cordon's coming. Nine days' warning, that's the tale. Nine days and then nobody in or out of the district."

"Nine days." Bram turned that over, looking at the middle distance, at something Marisol couldn't see. Whatever it was, it made their jaw set. "And what happens in nine days, does the tale say?"

"The tale says the rich get out first. It says the mill owners are already shipping daughters south — whole boatloads of girls, contracts signed quick and dirty, cheap labor for cousins' mills in Derbyshire before the ports shut." She laughed, and the laugh cost her a cough she hid in her fist, and when she brought the fist away she kept it closed. "So the moral is, marry a warehouse clerk, Mari, and be saved."

Bram didn't laugh. Bram was looking at her fist.

For one moment — a moment stretched thin as wire — Marisol thought Bram would cross the room and open her hand and find the truth folded small as a coin inside it. She braced for it. Part of her, some exhausted honest part far down below the pretense, *wanted* it, wanted to unfold the cloth on the table and say *look, look what I'm carrying, tell me it's dust, you fix things, fix this.*

Then the knock came at the door — heavy, official, twice — and Bram rose to answer it, and the moment passed the way moments did in the Zhou family, unopened, folded small, put away warm against the ribs.

It was a neighbor's child with a note, and Bram read it by the door and went very still, and then very busy, pulling on a coat, saying something about a clock that couldn't wait until morning, and then the stair door banged and Bram was gone into the dark, and Marisol sat alone with the cold supper and the lamplight and her brother's unfinished cup of tea going cool.

She waited until she heard Aldous snoring in the back room. Then she took out the cloth and looked at the blood again, by better light, and told herself the story she'd decided on, the one she'd rehearsed all the way home: *it's dust, it's the dust, it has always been the dust, and in nine days none of it will matter because the bells haven't changed yet, and while the bells still ring the same old pattern I am a healthy girl with a wage, and I will be a healthy girl right up until the moment I am not.*

Outside, far off across the roofs of Ancoats, a church bell rang the hour — the same old pattern, steady, faithful, lying to her beautifully.

Marisol folded the cloth away and blew out the lamp.
