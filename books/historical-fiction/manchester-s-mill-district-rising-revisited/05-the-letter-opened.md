## 5. The Letter, Opened

Midnight did not fall over Ancoats so much as settle on it, the way lint settles on black wool — slowly, invisibly, until everything is grey. Bram Zhou sat alone at the shop bench with a single candle burning, and even that felt like an extravagance they could not account for. Below them, through the floorboards, the chandler's shop breathed its tallow-and-turpentine smell up into the room, and beyond it the mill-row slept in the particular silence of a district too tired to dream.

The letter lay on the bench where Bram had set it three days ago and not touched since.

It had traveled from the observatory case to their coat pocket to the drawer beneath the bench — the one with the false bottom where mother used to keep the good watch glasses — and back out again each night, held, weighed, set down. Three nights of this. The seal was unbroken: dark green wax, stamped with a device Bram could not read by candlelight, something curled and formal, a crest or a cipher or simply a man's vanity pressed into soft wax and left to harden into authority.

Their mother's handwriting addressed it. *Bram* — only that, no surname, as if she had written it to a stranger who shared her roof.

But their mother had been dead two years. Bram had stood at the graveside at Weaste in the rain and watched the sexton's men do their work badly, the way poor men's graves were always done badly, and there was no version of the world in which Eliza Zhou had sat at a desk somewhere afterward and written her child's name in ink that had not yet faded.

Someone had kept her hand. Someone had learned it well enough to counterfeit it, or — worse, in a way Bram could not yet articulate — someone had found letters she had actually written and cut the address from one and pasted it onto another, stitching together a dead woman's voice out of scraps. Either possibility made Bram's hands feel strange, too large for their tools, clumsy with a grief that had been handled by strangers.

They picked the letter up. Put it down. Picked it up again.

"You're being ridiculous," they said aloud, and the sound of their own voice in the empty shop startled them — low and rough, neither the voice they'd been given nor quite the one they'd built. They said it again anyway. "You repair clocks. You open cases. That is all this is."

But it wasn't all this was, and Bram knew it, and the knowing sat in their chest like a mainspring wound too tight.

* * *

They thought about their mother the way you think about a clock you have never been allowed to open: by its outward behavior. Eliza Zhou had been precise. She had risen before the mills woke and gone to bed after the last shift walked home, and in between she had moved through the world with the economy of a woman counting every second against some debt only she could see. She had taught Bram to file a pivot true when they were nine years old, holding their small hand around the graver with her own scarred one wrapped over it. *Steady,* she would say. *The metal doesn't care how you feel. It cares what you do.*

She had never spoken of 1826. Not once, not ever, not in twenty-odd years of living above a chandler's shop in the mill district where half the older hands still limped from the looms' demands and the other half remembered the year the weavers came out and were broken for it. Other families talked about it the way families talk about a hanging — in lowered voices, at wakes, after the third cup of gin. Nettie Hargreaves down the row had a son who'd been transported, and she said his name every Sunday like a prayer with teeth in it. But the Zhous said nothing. When the strike came up in the shop — a customer, a memory, a song sung drunk outside the window — their mother would go very still, the way she went still when a mechanism jammed, and then she would resume moving, and nothing more would be said.

Bram had always taken the silence for sorrow. There was plenty of sorrow to account for it. A father lost to the fever pits before Bram could remember his face. A sister born sickly and buried sicklier. Rent, and hunger, and the slow grinding humiliation of being Chinese in Manchester, called Chink at the market and charged twice for coal. Sorrow was the household currency; Bram had assumed their mother was merely rich in it.

Now, holding the letter, they understood that silence can also be a payment. That sometimes a family goes quiet not because it has nothing to say but because it is buying something with the quiet.

They broke the seal.

* * *

The wax parted cleanly, which told them the letter had been sealed recently — old wax crumbles, and this gave under the thumbnail with the fresh, stubborn give of new-made stuff. Inside were two sheets, folded twice. The first was in their mother's hand, or something so close to it that Bram's eyes watered trying to find the forgery in it. The loops of the *Z*. The way she crossed her *t*s with a single impatient stroke, left to right, always left to right, as if time itself might catch her mid-word if she dawdled.

*Bram,* it began, and then, immediately, *if you are reading this I am dead twice over, once in fact and once in reputation, and the second death is the one this letter concerns.*

Bram read it standing up. Then sitting. Then standing again, because sitting made the words swim.

*I was seventeen in the summer of 1826. You will know what happened that summer because everyone in this district knows it, except that nobody knows it whole. Forty thousand weavers came out. They starved politely for four months and then less politely, and the masters broke them with imported hands and hunger and the magistrates' men. And the masters could not have done it so cheaply without information. Every street had its ears. Every committee meeting had its report, sold for a few shillings to a clerk who sold it upward.*

*I was one of those ears.*

Bram stopped. Set the page face-down on the bench, carefully, as though it might bite. The candle guttered in a draft from the ill-fitting window and steadied. Somewhere below, a cat knocked something over in the chandler's shop and fled into the night, and the ordinary small violence of it seemed obscene.

Their mother. Their mother, who had wrapped Bram's hand in hers and said *steady*, who had skimped on her own bread so Marisol could have treacle on hers, whose funeral had drawn forty people from the row because everyone agreed she was the best of women —

*I informed on the Strike Committee of George Street. Nine times. I gave names, meeting places, and once — God forgive me, and He has not — the route of a food convoy the weavers had scraped together from Lancashire farms. It was intercepted. Two hundred people who had walked thirty miles carrying oatmeal for starving neighbors watched it seized at the toll bar and dumped into the canal.*

*I did it for six shillings a report. I did it because my father was dying of the lung and the medicine cost six shillings and my brother was eleven and someone had to buy the laudanum that let him die quietly instead of screaming. I tell you this not to excuse it. There is no excuse. I tell it to you because you deserve the whole shape of the thing, not the part that flatters me.*

Bram's legs gave out and they sat down hard on the stool, and the stool creaked, and they gripped the edge of the bench and breathed the way their mother had taught them to breathe when a spring fought back: slow, counted, mechanical. Four counts in. Four counts held. Four counts out.

The metal doesn't care how you feel. It cares what you do.

But this was not metal. This was the past, which cared enormously how you felt, and fed on it, and gave nothing back.

* * *

The second sheet was not in their mother's hand.

It was in a clerk's hand — round, neat, self-satisfied, the writing of a man who had never once smudged a page because he had never once hurried. The ink was better than anything in the Zhou house. The paper had a watermark that caught the candlelight: a fleur-de-lis, fine rag stock, the kind of stationery that arrived with invoices from banks.

The letter was dated — Bram checked twice — the twelfth of June, 1847. Eleven days ago.

*To the surviving family of Eliza Zhou, deceased:*

*Your late mother entered into certain arrangements of long standing, the particulars of which are known to you or soon shall be. Her obligations have passed by custom to her heirs. The sum customarily rendered — five pounds quarterly, remitted to this office under cover of clock repairs — has fallen into arrears since her death, an indulgence extended out of respect for grief that cannot be indefinitely prolonged.*

*In consideration of continued silence regarding the matters described in the enclosed testimony, the undersigned is pleased to offer the following: one (1) berth aboard the barge* Perseverance, *departing the Bridgewater basin on the eve of the cordon, destination Liverpool and passage onward. The berth is transferable within your family. It is not refundable and not repeatable.*

*Should the arrangement lapse, should inquiry be made, should any document bearing the Zhou name come before the magistrate's office in connection with the events of 1826, the enclosed testimony will be lodged with the authorities, and the consequences will fall upon all who bear the name. I say this with regret and with certainty.*

*Yours in the hope of continued mutual discretion,*

There was no signature. Instead, the same device that had sealed the envelope was stamped at the foot in green-black ink: a serpent coiled around a rod, or perhaps around a quill — Bram leaned close, tilting the page — no. Around a *key*. A serpent eating its own tail, threaded through with a key, and beneath it, worked into the design so finely you'd take it for decoration unless you were looking: the initials **S.C.**

Silas Cray. Bram didn't know the name yet, not properly — but they knew the initials the way you know a maker's mark, and they knew the office such stationery implied, and their stomach performed a slow cold revolution.

They read the second sheet again. Five pounds quarterly. Under cover of clock repairs.

Under cover of clock repairs.

Bram looked up at the shop around them — at the wall of clocks their mother had maintained, at the ledger on its shelf with twenty years of entries in two hands, theirs and hers. *Repair, mantel, Mrs. Ogden, gratis. Repair, regulator, chapel, 4s. Repair —* and here and there, scattered through the columns like grit in a bearing, entries Bram had never questioned: *Repair, private commission, paid.* No name. No description of the work. Small sums. Regular as a heartbeat.

Five pounds a quarter. For twenty years. Their mother hadn't been paying blackmail out of the shop's thin profits — she'd been routing it *through* the shop, laundering shame through honest gears, and Bram had sat at this very bench beside her a thousand evenings and never seen the grit in the train.

* * *

The vertigo came then, properly, the way it does at sea or on high places: not a feeling in the body so much as the body's discovery that the ground was never solid. Bram put both palms flat on the bench and let the wood hold them.

Everything reorganized itself around the letter. Their mother's precision — not thrift, but penance. Her silence about '26 — not sorrow, but sentence. The way she had flinched, actually flinched, the single time young Bram had come home humming the weavers' march and asked why nobody in the family sang it. *We don't sing other people's songs,* she had said, and her face had been stone, and Bram had thought: *she is proud, she is distant, she is difficult.* They had spent two years grieving a difficult, proud, precise woman.

They had been grieving an informer. Or grieving a girl of seventeen who bought laudanum with blood money while her father drowned in his own lungs. Both. The horror was that both were true in the same hand, the same steady left-to-right crossings of the *t*, and there was no way to sort the love from the crime except the way you'd sort wheels from a smashed movement — patiently, piece by piece, knowing some pieces would never run again.

And underneath all of it, coiling through it like the serpent on the seal: *five pounds quarterly.* The barge. One berth. *Transferable within your family.*

One berth. Three of them left alive — Bram, Marisol, Uncle Aldous. The cholera was coming up the canal from Liverpool; the bells would change their pattern soon and everyone in the district knew what the new pattern meant. And a man whose initials were S.C. was offering the Zhous exactly one door out of the burning building, priced at continued silence about a massacre committed with their mother's mouth.

He had waited two years after her death to press. Why now? Because the quarantine gave him leverage no season had ever given him before. Because fear was the one commodity that appreciated in Ancoats that summer.

Bram laughed — one syllable, ugly, swallowed immediately. The man had chosen his moment like a craftsman. Like a clockmaker. Strike when the works are most afraid; the escapement releases whether you will it or not.

* * *

The candle burned down toward its puddle. Bram became aware, distantly, that they had been sitting motionless for a long time, long enough that the stiffness in their neck had passed into numbness, and that Marisol's cot behind the curtain was empty — she was on nights at the sorting floor this week, wouldn't be home till the small hours smelling of cotton dust and other people's lint. Aldous was at his lodgings across the row, sleeping off the rent money he'd counted four times and still come up short.

Three lives. One berth. And a debt in weavers' names that none of them had incurred and all of them, apparently, inherited.

Bram did the thing they always did when the world refused to make sense: they reached for the work. There was the widow Ogden's mantel clock on the corner of the bench, its escape wheel off, waiting for Bram's file. They took it up. Held it. The brass was warm from the day, familiar, honest — it wanted only what it was made to want, to tick, to divide time evenly, to tell the truth at whatever speed it was asked.

Their mother's hands had touched this same kind of brass. Their mother's hands had written nine reports and taken nine times six shillings and watched oatmeal poured into the canal. The same hands. That was the thing Bram could not get past, the bearing that would not seat: there were no other hands. There was never a separate person who did the evil and a separate person who taught them to file a pivot true. It was one pair of hands, and Bram had those hands' habits in their own fingers, the same grip, the same impatience with a badly cut wheel, and no letter, however damning, could amputate that inheritance.

*Steady,* she said. *The metal doesn't care how you feel.*

"The metal doesn't care," Bram whispered. "But somebody does."

Somebody with a clerk's hand and a watermark and a key coiled in a serpent. Somebody who had been inside the observatory's master clock case — Bram's skin crawled as they remembered the tampering they'd found there, the deliberate wrongness of it — somebody who used clocks the way their mother had used silence: as cover, as conveyance, as a place to hide the true working from the face.

Bram folded the letter along its original creases. Did not burn it. Burning was what a guilty family did, and besides — it was evidence now, of something, of several somethings, though Bram couldn't yet have said of what. They slid it into the drawer with the false bottom where mother used to keep the good watch glasses, and their fingers paused on the wood.

"I'm sorry," they told the drawer, meaning her, meaning the weavers, meaning themselves, meaning nothing and everything. "I'm going to open everything."

Then they heard the stair.

Footsteps on the outside stairs at — Bram turned to the regulator on the wall — quarter past one in the morning. Heavy steps, careful steps, a man's steps taking the worn treads at the angle that avoided the loose board on the seventh stair. Only one person in the world climbed these stairs that way, avoiding noise the way other men avoided work.

Uncle Aldous.

He never came at this hour. He had his own lodging, his own lock, his own pride, such as it was. In fifteen years he had come up these stairs after midnight exactly twice: once when their mother died, and once the night the bailiffs threatened the shop.

The door opened and Aldous stood in it, candleless, backlit by the street lamp's oil-glow, and Bram saw — before he said a word, before his face arranged itself into its usual tired bluffness — saw him look past Bram directly at the bench. At the space where the letter had lain. At the torn halves of a green wax seal that Bram realized, with a drop of the stomach like a weight slipping a chain, were still sitting in plain view on the wood.

Aldous went white in the lamplight. White, and then a kind of grey, and his hand found the doorframe.

"So he's written to you, then," Aldous said quietly. "God help us. So he's written to you."
