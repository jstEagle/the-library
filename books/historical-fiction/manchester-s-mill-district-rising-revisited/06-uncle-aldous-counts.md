## 6. Uncle Aldous Counts

Aldous Zhou had learned to count in the dark.

Not literally—his eyes were still good enough for coin-work by candlelight, though the candle cost him a penny a night and he begrudged every farthing of it—but figuratively, in the way that men who have lost things learn. He counted the coins on the shop's back table twice, then a third time, because the third count was not for arithmetic but for comfort. Eleven shillings and sixpence. Rent was due in four days and stood at one pound four. The gap between those two numbers was a country he knew well; he had been mapping its borders for three years now, ever since his brother-in-law died and left him a clockmaker's shop that made clocks no one could afford to buy and a family that could not afford to eat without it.

He counted the widow's mantel clock too, in his fashion—the brass of it, the weight of the case, what a scrap dealer would give for the movement if it came to that. It would come to that eventually. Everything came to *that* eventually. Aldous had spent forty years learning the value of objects because objects were honest. A gear did not lie about its size. A spring did not promise to hold longer than its temper allowed. Coins clinked with the same dull sincerity whether they came from an honest wage or from somewhere darker.

It was the somewhere-darker money he counted most carefully. Not because he loved it less, but because it burned his fingers in a way no one else could see.

He heard Bram come up the outside stair—heard the particular rhythm of it, the pause at the fourth step where the tread had gone soft, the hand finding the rail. Bram moved through the world like someone checking their own work, always, even climbing stairs. Their mother had done the same. The thought arrived and Aldous set it down again quickly, the way a man sets down a coal that has rolled too near his boot.

The door opened. Cold mill-row air came in with them, smelling of canal water and coal smoke and the faint sweetish rot that had begun to hang over Ancoats these last weeks—a smell everyone pretended not to notice, the way you pretend not to notice a tooth going bad.

"You're counting again," Bram said. Not unkindly. That was the worst part, how kindly they said it.

"I'm auditing," Aldous said, which was a joke, which was armor. "There's a difference. Auditing implies there might be good news."

Bram hung their coat on the peg and didn't laugh. They'd stopped laughing at his jokes sometime in the past year, or perhaps they laughed less often, or perhaps Aldous simply noticed the absence more. He turned back to the table, swept the coins into their leather purse, drew the strings tight. Eleven and six against twenty-four. He had done this sum so many times it had worn a groove in him, like a pivot turning in the same bearing until it cut its own channel.

"Marisol's asleep?" he asked.

"Coughing in her sleep, but asleep." Bram sat across from him, elbows on the table, and studied him with those level grey-brown eyes that saw everything and forgave selectively. "The observatory keeper sent a boy round again today. They want me back Thursday. Something about the strike train needing adjustment after all."

"Did you take the work?"

"I took the work."

"Good." He meant it. Triple rate. He had told Bram to go the first time despite every instinct screaming otherwise—*rich doors open onto rich problems*, he'd said, and Bram had gone anyway, carrying the tool roll and the family's hunger, and had come back quiet in a way Aldous recognized because he himself had been quiet that way once, long ago, coming home from somewhere he shouldn't have been.

But tonight there was something else under the quiet. Tonight Bram kept touching their waistcoat pocket. Small motion, twice, three times while they talked about escapements and strike trains. The gesture of a person confirming a thing is still where they put it.

Aldous poured tea that was mostly hot water and gave Bram the stronger cup without comment. Outside, the mill row settled into its nighttime noises—the chandler below stacking crates, a dog somewhere, the great looms long since stilled but their silence somehow louder than their working. In nine days, maybe fewer, the bells would change pattern and the cordon would begin, and none of the noises would matter anymore because the district would be sealed like a jar.

He thought: *eleven and six*. He thought: *one berth*. He thought: *I am an old man keeping two young people alive on arithmetic, and the arithmetic is failing.*

"Bram." He said it before he could stop himself. "If anything happened—if the cordon comes early, if—" He stopped. Started again. "You know I'd see you right. Both of you. However I had to manage it."

Bram looked at him for a long moment, and Aldous felt the look go through him like a probe through a movement, testing for the loose part, the worn tooth. "I know," they said finally. And then, softer: "Uncle. If I asked you something, you'd tell me true?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped weight.

"Why wouldn't I?" said Aldous Zhou, liar, and drank his weak tea, and smiled, and the smile held, because it had held through worse than this. It had held in 1826. It had held at his sister's deathbed. It had held every month for eleven years when he walked down to the culvert behind the tannery and left an envelope in the hollow brick, and walked home with his hands in his pockets like any man out for air.

"Nothing," Bram said. "Forget it."

They went to bed. Aldous sat alone with the guttering candle and the ledger open before him, columns of figures that refused to reconcile, and after a while he took the key from around his neck and unlocked the drawer beneath the floorboard where the rent coins slept beside other things.

The letter was where it had lived for six days now, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the drawer, beneath a false bottom that had once hidden his brother-in-law's best jeweling tools from thieves and now hid this from Bram. Aldous unwrapped it with hands that shook only a little—he noted the shake with professional disapproval, as though his hands were apprentices who ought to know better—and looked, for the hundredth time, at the wax seal.

A wheel within a wheel. Pressed deep, cleanly struck, the kind of seal bought by a man who wanted his marks legible. Aldous had seen it first on a Tuesday in the autumn of 1836, in an envelope tucked into the shop's cashbox alongside eleven shillings he had not put there. *You are owed nothing and can be made to owe everything*, the letter had said, in a clerk's beautiful copperplate. *Small payments, regularly, will suffice. Silence is cheaper than scandal. Ask your sister what she paid.*

He had asked her. That was the mistake he had never stopped making, asking her, standing in this very room while she sat mending Marisol's stocking with hands steadier than his, and she had told him—not everything, never everything, but enough. The strike. The weavers' committee meeting at the Crown and Anchor, the names read out, the wages lists passed to the mill owners' men. Her husband's debts, the child already coughing blood, the magistrate's clerk who had come to her not with a threat but with *help*, help offered in that voice that made kindness and ruin sound identical. She had informed on Nettie Hargreaves and a dozen others to buy laudanum and a doctor, and the child had died anyway, and the money had kept coming because men like that do not release what they hold.

"He'll bleed us forever," Aldous had said.

"He bled me before you ever counted a coin here," she had answered. "And he'll bleed Bram after, unless we pay. That is the whole of it, Aldous. There is no clean side of this ledger. There is only choosing which page stays shut."

She had died owing him that truth and he had inherited it the way you inherit a debt, silently, involuntarily, with interest accruing. For six years he had paid. Fourpence some months, a shilling in the hard ones, whatever the hollow brick demanded. He had missed exactly one payment, in the winter Marisol had the fever, and the next envelope had contained not a threat but something worse: a copy of his sister's old confession to the magistrate, in her own hand, with a note suggesting that documents so easily copied might find their way to a newspaper, or to the memorial committee that was then raising funds to carve the strikers' names in stone. He had borrowed against the shop's lease to make the payment double. He had never missed another.

So when Bram had come home three nights ago with a strange stillness on them and gone straight to bed without supper, Aldous had known. He had known the way a clockmaker knows a train has jammed—not from seeing the fault but from hearing the tick go wrong. And yesterday morning, while Bram ran an errand to the chandler's, he had searched their coat pockets, which was theft, which was protection, which was both, and had found the second letter.

His sister's handwriting. Addressed to Bram. Sealed with the wheel within a wheel.

Cray had kept her letters. Of course he had. Cray kept everything; that was his profession and his pleasure both, a magistrate's clerk with a warehouse office and a charity patronage and an archive of other people's sins filed as neatly as invoices. He had taken a dead woman's words and mailed them to her child, and Aldous understood the message perfectly: *the debt passes down. Tell the boy—or girl, or whatever the eldest Zhou is calling themselves these days—or don't, but understand that the account is now in their name too.*

Aldous had put the letter back in Bram's coat before they returned. Let them find it themselves, or let Cray force it into the open; either way, the reading of it was a wound Aldous could not prevent, only fail to worsen. But his own copy—his copy of the confession, the original his sister had written out for him years ago in case anything should happen to her—his copy he could destroy.

He carried it to the grate now. The fire was nearly out; he fed it the paper a corner at a time, watching the flame take the ink, the words curling brown then black then gone. *I, Esther Zhou, do declare that on the ninth of October, 1826...* Gone. *...did convey unto Mr. Hargreaves's employer...* Gone. The heat touched his face and he let it, let it sting, because shame deserved a temperature.

Truth would kill them faster than cholera. That was the whole of his creed now, the whole of his love. Cholera killed the body, yes, and the cordon would kill livelihoods, and the fever carts would come—but those deaths were quick and public and mourned. Truth was slower. Truth would take Marisol's name off every respectable list in Manchester. It would take Bram's prospects—which were thin, granted, but real, an apprentice with a gift for fine work whose name appeared in no blacklist—and grind them to nothing. It would make the Zhous the family that sold the weavers, and districts remember; districts keep ledgers of their own, kept longer than any magistrate's. He had watched what truth did to Nettie Hargreaves, who had lost her loom and her husband and thirty years to bitterness. He would not watch it happen to the children. He would pay, and lie, and carry the filth alone, and die with it, and let them hate him if they ever learned, because hatred was survivable and ruin was not.

That was what he told himself. He was aware—it had come to him lately, in the small hours, with terrible clockwork clarity—that this was also convenient. That protecting them and protecting himself had long since become the same operation, and that a man who counts coins in the dark gets very good at not counting certain other things.

The last corner of paper flared and died. He raked the ash fine with the poker, mixed it into the cold cinders, and sat back down at the table.

The knock, when it came, was soft. One knock. Then the outer door, which he had left unlatched as always on paying nights, and footsteps crossing the shop below—light, unhurried, the steps of a man who owned the ground he walked on and knew it. Aldous rose, took the purse from the shelf where the quarter's skim lay ready, and went down into the dark shop, past the silent clocks ticking their separate hours, to meet Silas Cray for what he told himself, with a calm he did not feel, was merely another month's business.

Cray stood among the clock faces with his hat in his hand, looking almost gentle in the moonlight from the window, a stout respectable man with the face of someone's favorite uncle.

"Aldous," he said warmly. "I'm afraid this month I've come about rather more than the usual sum."

* * *

Above, behind the thin partition wall, Bram lay awake and listened to the murmur of two voices in the shop below—one rising, one low and smooth as oil poured into water—and could not make out a single word, only the shape of the conversation, which was the shape of a confession, or a transaction, or a prayer. After a long while the shop door opened and closed, and the stair creaked under their uncle's returning weight, and the house was silent again except for Marisol's coughing through the wall and, everywhere, underneath everything, the ticking of forty-odd clocks keeping forty-odd versions of the same hour.

In the morning, when Bram asked why the light had been burning past two, Aldous said, "Couldn't sleep. Counted the stock."

And Bram looked at him with those steady eyes and said, "Uncle. Who came to the shop last night?"
