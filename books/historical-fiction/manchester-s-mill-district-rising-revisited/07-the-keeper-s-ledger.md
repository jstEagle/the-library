## 7. The Keeper's Ledger

Bram did not sleep.

They told themselves it was the letter—its weight in the drawer beneath their tools, behind the false back of the case that had held Grandmother Zhou's opium tincture once and nothing since—but that was a lie, and Bram was growing tired of lies they hadn't chosen to tell. The truth was simpler and worse: somewhere between midnight and the grey hour when the mill-row cocks began their idiot arguments, Bram had understood that they could not sit still with a nameless thing in a drawer. The letter had handwriting. The letter had wax. Wax meant a seal, and seals meant men, and men could be found.

So at half past six, while Aldous slept off his coins and Marisol's cough rattled through the thin partition like something trying to get out, Bram dressed by feel, packed the tool roll, and let themselves down the stair with the particular silence of someone who has been leaving rooms quietly all their life.

The morning was the colour of unpolished pewter. Fog off the canal had climbed into the streets overnight and settled there, thick enough that the gas lamps still burned at seven o'clock, each one a smeared yellow coin hanging in nothing. Bram walked through it toward the observatory with the fog beading on their eyelashes and their breath making small ghosts that the mist swallowed whole. A milk cart passed, its driver a shape without features. Somewhere ahead, unseen, a child cried—not in distress, exactly, but in the flat persistent way of children who have learned that crying changes nothing—and then stopped as if a hand had covered its mouth.

*Rich doors open onto rich problems.* Uncle Aldous had said it twice, which for Aldous constituted a speech. But Aldous had not said what kind of problem, because Aldous didn't know, and Bram intended to find out before anyone richer or poorer found it first.

* * *

The observatory stood on its knoll above the district like a gentleman who had wandered into the wrong neighbourhood and decided to pretend he'd meant to come. Godlee Observatory, the plaque said—gifted to the town by a cotton man whose name was now a byword for philanthropy and, if you listened in certain public houses, for wages that starved slower than other men's. Its dome was green with verdigris. Its windows caught what little light the morning offered and gave none of it back.

Bram had repaired clocks in grander rooms than this—the mayor's parlour, a chapel in Cheetham whose vicar believed time should run beautifully even if his congregation arrived at whatever hour suited them—but never one where the room itself seemed to be listening.

The keeper met them in the vestibule. Mr. Prewitt: sixty-odd, dry as a pressed flower, with the watery courtesy of a man employed to say no on behalf of his betters.

"You've come about the clock." It wasn't a question, and there was something in how quickly he said it—as though he'd been standing at the door waiting for the knock, dreading it. "I'll show you up. You'll want to mind your head on the stair."

"I mind my head on stairs for a living," Bram said, and got no smile for it.

The master clock lived in a room beneath the dome, a long low chamber lined with instruments under dust sheets like furniture in a house of mourning. The clock itself dominated the wall: a regulator of serious pedigree, walnut case taller than a man, brass-faced, with a deadbeat escapement visible through its glazed trunk door. It was, Bram thought, a beautiful animal. And it was limping.

Prewitt hovered. "It loses time," he said. "Irregularly."

"Clocks don't lose time irregularly unless someone's made them do it, or something's worn unevenly. Which is it?"

A pause, one beat too long. "You'd know better than I. I'll leave you to it." And he went, too fast, his boots fading down the stone stair, and Bram stood alone in the instrument room with the smell of cold brass and lamp oil and the growing certainty that they had walked into the middle of something rather than merely been summoned to its edge.

They opened the case door. They looked at the movement. And they saw, immediately, what they had only suspected yesterday: the count wheel had been filed. Not broken—filed. Deliberately, precisely, by someone who understood escapements well enough to alter the interval between strikes without stopping the train entirely. A few thousandths of an inch of metal, gone. The clock would still keep hours; it would simply tell them slightly wrong, in a way no ordinary ear would catch but any document compared against another document might betray.

Someone had taught this clock to lie.

Bram sat back on their heels in the cold room and felt the thought settle over them like the fog outside, total and chill. Yesterday they had pocketed a letter in their dead mother's hand without reading it, telling themselves it was shock. Today there was no shock left to hide behind. There was only the filing marks on the count wheel, fine as scratches in a lover's note, and the knowledge that whoever made them had done it with patience, with skill, and with intent.

* * *

The ledger was in the keeper's office on the floor below. Bram knew this because Prewitt had mentioned it yesterday—"you'll sign the work book, of course, we keep everything official here"—with the small pride of a man who kept things official the way other men kept dogs. Official things had names in them. Names were what Bram needed.

But Prewitt was downstairs, and Prewitt watched.

Bram worked the clock for two hours first. This was not entirely theatre; the clock needed working on, and besides, Bram had learned long ago that the best disguise in any rich man's house was competence. Let them see you absorbed in your trade and they forgot you were also a person who could open drawers. They cleaned the pivots, trued a bent suspension spring, adjusted the crutch. They let the strike train run and counted the intervals against their own pulse, twice, three times, until they had the error exactly: four seconds early, every third hour. Not enough to notice. Exactly enough to matter, if what mattered was paper.

At eleven, Prewitt brought tea. He set it down on a stool beside Bram without asking whether they wanted it, and lingered, and looked at the movement with an expression Bram catalogued carefully: not ignorance, but fear wearing ignorance as a coat.

"You'll have it right soon?" he asked.

"Soon as I fit a new count wheel. I'll need to source one—there's none in the shop that matches. Might take me a day or two to make it from stock."

"A day or two." He said it as though weighing the words for hidden cost. "His worship will want it sooner, I expect."

"His worship?" Bram kept their eyes on the escapement. "I understood the commission came through the observatory trust."

Another pause. That same beat too long, the beat that was becoming a signature. "The trust. Yes. Well. The tea's going cold."

He left. Bram drank the tea—it was good tea, which told its own story about money—and began assembling the distraction in their mind the way they'd assemble a going barrel, one part at a time, checking each against the last.

* * *

The trick, Bram knew, was never to sneak. Sneaking invited watching. The trick was to be so plainly, boringly legitimate that watching felt foolish.

They descended to the office at noon carrying the old count wheel wrapped in rag, and found Prewitt at his desk with the ledger open before him, entering figures in a copperplate hand. The office was small and brown, smelling of sealing wax and damp stone. On the wall hung a framed engraving of the comet of 1843, and beneath it, a locked cabinet with a brass plate: *TRUST CORRESPONDENCE.*

"Mr. Prewitt," Bram said, "I'll sign the work book for today's labour, and I've a question on the commission particulars. The invoice needs a proper name for the record—my uncle does the accounts, and he's strict about who pays for what. Trust funds being trust funds."

It was beautiful, really, how well greed worked as a lever. Prewitt's face performed a small civil war—suspicion versus the terror of appearing irregular before a tradesman who might talk. Tradesmen talked. Everyone knew tradesmen talked.

"The commission is regular," he said stiffly. "You'll be paid regular."

"I don't doubt it. But *by whom?*" Bram smiled the smile they used on customers who haggled over widow-work. "My uncle's had trouble before. A gentleman commissions a repair, the gentleman dies, and suddenly nobody owes anybody. He's made me swear on his own Bible: full name, full address, or the work doesn't leave the bench."

And there it was: the ledger, lying open, and Prewitt's hand moving almost despite himself, turning it a few degrees so Bram could see the current page. Pride, Bram thought. Even fear keeps a little pride, and pride wants witnesses.

"Silas Cray," Prewitt said, tapping the entry with a dry forefinger, as though the name were proof of respectability itself. "Esquire. Clerk to His Worship Magistrate Fenwick. Commissioner of the Orphans' Relief Fund, if you please. A man whose money is as good as the Bank of England's."

Bram looked at the page and did not look like someone memorizing it.

But memory is a trade like any other, and Bram had been apprenticed to detail since the age of nine, when their mother first put a loupe in their hand and said, *the whole mechanism is in the parts nobody looks at.* So they took the page the way they took a movement apart: methodically, without hurry, fixing each element in place.

The date of commission, in Prewitt's copperplate: the ninth of October last. The nature of the work: *regulator, overhaul and adjustment of striking, per instructions of Mr. Cray, personal.* The word *personal* was underlined—twice, the second underline heavier, as though Prewitt had wanted to warn himself and had failed. The fee, entered and marked *paid in advance, cash,* in an amount that would have fed the Zhou household for four months. And the address: Cray's warehouse office on Withy Grove, above the counting house of some concern with a respectable-sounding name Bram fixed in memory along with the rest—*Hulme & Vause, factors.*

Four seconds early, every third hour, commissioned personally, paid in advance, by the magistrate's own clerk.

Bram signed the work book. *B. Zhou, clockmaker, Mill Row.* Their hand did not shake. They noted this about themselves with a distant, clinical unease—that the shaking had happened yesterday, in the first hour, and had apparently spent itself, and what remained was something steadier and considerably less comfortable. Grief, they were discovering, was a luxury available only when you didn't yet know whom to grieve against.

"Your master clock has been tampered with," Bram said, folding their rag around the old count wheel. They watched Prewitt's face go through three colours in the space of a breath. "Not by me. Before me. Whoever did it knew mechanisms. If I were you, Mr. Prewitt, I'd wonder who instructed him—and why a man pays cash in advance for a clock that strikes wrongly, and calls it *personal.*"

"I'm sure I don't know what you mean."

"No," Bram agreed pleasantly. "I expect you're sure of a great many things."

They gathered their tools. At the door, they paused, because there was one more thing, and it was the thing that turned resolve into something harder and more dangerous.

"The letter in the case," Bram said, not turning round. "When the clock was opened for cleaning—who else was present?"

Silence. Long enough to be an answer.

"Only yourself, then," Bram said softly. "Or only yourself and whoever wrote it. Either way—you know what it said. And you know I have it now. Sleep well, Mr. Prewitt."

They heard the man's breath catch behind them, a small mechanical failure, like a pallet dropping out of timing. Bram did not turn. They went out into the fog, down the knoll, into the streets, and the fog closed over the observatory behind them as though the building had never existed—which, Bram thought, was very nearly the ambition of everyone inside it.

* * *

They walked home the long way, along the canal towpath, because the towpath was empty and empty places let thinking happen at full volume.

The name turned itself over in Bram's mind with the slow inevitability of a flywheel coming up to speed. *Silas Cray.* They tried the name against the wax seal on the letter still hidden in their mother's old drawer—a crest, a bird over water, stamped deep—and found it fit the shape of the thing without touching its meaning. A clerk to a magistrate. A commissioner of orphans' relief. A man with a fund for saving children, paying cash in advance for a clock altered to strike falsely.

And underneath all of it, patient as sediment: *mother's handwriting.* Dead two years, and her hand on paper sealed with a stranger's crest, hidden inside a machine that lied about time. Whatever their mother had done—and Bram was beginning to understand that she had done something, something with weight and cost and a price still accruing interest—it ran through Silas Cray like a thread through cloth. Pull it, and the whole garment might come apart. Or pull it, and Bram might find the thread ran through the Zhous' own coat as well.

The canal slid past, black and patient, carrying its barges of coal and raw cotton and, these days, whispered rumours of berths and departures and cordons to come. A barge went by loaded with mill machinery bound for somewhere greener, and the hands on it stared at Bram staring at them, two kinds of people measuring each other across dirty water.

By the time Bram reached the chandler's stair, the fog had begun to lift and the light had sharpened into something nearly honest. Upstairs, the shop smelled of oil and brass shavings, and Aldous was awake, counting the rent coins again—he always counted them a final time, as though the coins might have bred or died overnight—and Marisol had already left for the sorting floor, her cough audible in the street below for a full minute after she'd passed out of sight.

Bram unlocked the false drawer. The letter lay where they had left it, seal intact, their mother's hand facing upward through the paper as though she were reaching out of the grave to hand them something—or to take something back.

Tonight, Bram promised it. Tonight they would break the seal and read every word. But first—

First they sat down at the bench, took out a sheet of cheap paper, and wrote from memory, in their smallest, most precise hand, everything the ledger page had shown them: the date, the fee, the underlined word *personal*, the name, the title, the address on Withy Grove. When they finished, they read it back against their memory and found it exact. Then they folded it small and sewed it into the lining of their waistcoat, next to the heart, where a person keeps the things they are not yet ready to become.

Because a name was not yet proof, and a filed count wheel was not yet confession, and a letter unread was not yet a weapon. But Bram had spent their life learning how mechanisms worked, and they knew this much for certain: every machine, however grand, however gilded, however protected by magistrates and trusts and clean collars—every machine had a point where one small, precise pressure, correctly applied, brought the whole train of wheels to a stop.

Silas Cray of Withy Grove had made the mistake of tampering with a clock and letting a clockmaker through the door.

Bram blew out the lamp, put the letter in their pocket instead of the drawer—they would not be separated from it again—and went upstairs to wait for dark, and for the truth, and for whichever of them arrived first.
