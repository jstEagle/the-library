## 8. Quarantine Bells

The bells changed on a Tuesday, and Marisol knew before the overseer said a word.

She had grown up inside the district's sound the way other children grew up inside a house. St. Saviour's rang the quarters; St. Philip's answered a half-beat late, its third bell cracked since before she was born, so that every hour came to the sorting floor slightly wounded. You could set your hunger by it, your aching feet, the exact moment when Mr. Ogden's patience with the slow sorters would run out. The bells were the district's pulse, and everyone in Ancoats carried that pulse in their bodies without ever thinking about it.

So when, at half past six in the morning, St. Saviour's rang nine strokes and then—paused—and then rang nine again, Marisol's hands went still over the cotton, and every woman on the floor went still with her.

Nine and nine. Nine days. The old pattern from the fever summer of '37, which her mother had described once, in a voice kept deliberately flat, the way you'd describe a debt. Nine strokes for nine days, morning and evening, until the cordons went up at the roads and no one came in and no one went out.

"Pick it up," Ogden said, though his own voice had gone thin. "Cotton doesn't sort itself because you're all standing about listening to church."

They picked it up. But the silence afterward was a different silence than the one before the bells, and Marisol worked inside it like a swimmer working against a current she could not see. Around her the great room breathed lint. It hung in the shafts of light from the high windows, turning slowly, gold-colored, beautiful if you refused to think about what it did to lungs. She had refused to think about it for three years. That was the price of the wage, and everyone paid it, and nobody spoke of the payment.

Her chest tightened. She swallowed the cough whole, let it break apart somewhere behind her breastbone, and kept her eyes on the bales.

* * *

At the midday break she took her bread and cheese to the yard, where the wall broke the wind, and found Hetty Lomas already there, hunched into her shawl, tearing her crust into pieces too small to eat.

"You heard," Hetty said. Not a question.

"Everyone heard."

"My cousin's girl is in service over in Hulme. She wrote that they're saying it's in Ancoats proper now. Cholera. Not just talk of it." Hetty's fingers kept working at the crust. "Saying there's been deaths off Canal Street."

Canal Street was eleven minutes' walk from the sorting floor. Marisol knew because she walked it twice a day, past the gin shop and the rag store and the house where the Zhou family had lived before her father died and the world narrowed down to a shop above a chandler's and an uncle who counted coins like rosary beads.

"There's always deaths off Canal Street," Marisol said. "That's what Canal Street is for."

"This is different and you know it's different."

Marisol did know it was different. She had known it was different for six days, actually, though not because of any bell or rumor. She knew it because of the cloth hidden in the bottom of her work basket, wrapped in an apron she'd ruined, folded small as a sin: white linen freckled with rust-brown spots no larger than poppy seeds. She had told herself the first time it was nothing. A cracked lip bitten in her sleep. The second time, a nosebleed that had run backward somehow, down her throat, in the night. By the fourth time she had stopped inventing explanations and started counting instead—how many hours until payday, how many days until the cordon, whether a doctor could be seen without the visit being written into the parish book, because the sick got written down and the written-down got locked in, and locked-in was a word that meant the fever sheds behind the workhouse, where her mother had once dragged her past at speed, saying *look straight ahead, love, look straight ahead.*

"I heard something else," Hetty said, and lowered her voice even though the nearest listener was forty feet away and deaf in one ear. "About the millowners. Mr. Ashworth's lot. They've been taking girls off the books this week. Quiet-like. Telling them there's work at the new mill in Derbyshire, better light, better wages, and putting them on a carrier's wagon before dawn so nobody sees them go."

"That's kind of him," Marisol said.

"It's twenty girls in four days, and all of them under sixteen, and none of their mothers knew till after." Hetty looked at her directly. "He's moving them out ahead of the cordon. Cheap hands are cheap hands, but dead hands earn nothing, and he knows what's coming same as the bells know it. He's shipping out daughters first because daughters don't fight."

Marisol thought of the wagons. Thought of girls she knew—Nan Whitlow, who sang; the Pilling twins, who were fourteen and still grew their hair in plaits because their mother couldn't bear to cut it—being loaded before dawn into a cart like cotton bales with faces, bound for mills where no one would ask after them, where letters home would be discouraged, where *better wages* would turn out to mean whatever a man in a clean collar decided it meant when the wagon was already rolling.

"They'll be safer," she said, because the alternative to saying it was screaming, and screaming got you dismissed.

"Aye," said Hetty. "Safer. Like sheep are safer in the far field."

The bell for the afternoon shift rang—the ordinary bell, the ordinary pattern, and Marisol flinched anyway—and they went back in.

* * *

The afternoon passed in the particular agony that terror takes when it must wear the clothing of routine. This was the skill the floor demanded, Marisol thought, more than quick fingers, more than good eyes: the ability to be dying inside and keep your counts accurate. Every woman here possessed it. Mrs. Brierley, whose husband had been crushed in the carding room two winters back, who sorted with red-rimmed eyes and never once dropped below quota. Old Sukey, who had buried four children and smiled at the new girls like spring itself. Terror compressed into rhythm—that was the whole trade of the poor. You did not get to fall apart. Falling apart was a luxury item, priced beyond them.

Marisol's cough had learned manners by now. She had trained it the way you'd train a dog: never in front of the overlookers, never more than twice an hour, always into the crook of her elbow, always followed immediately by a stretch of exaggerated vigor, some show of industry, so that anyone glancing her way saw not sickness but effort. She had gotten frighteningly good at it. She could feel a cough assembling itself three seconds before it arrived, deep in the tubes of her chest like weather coming off the moors, and she would time it—wait for Ogden to turn toward the window, wait for the loom-adjacent din to swell—and release it quietly into her sleeve, and press her palm flat over the spot beneath her ribs that had begun, lately, to ache in a way that felt structural. Foundational. As though something in her had been built wrong at the base.

Once, only once, the cough escaped her properly—a long, tearing fit that bent her double over the bale—and she surfaced from it to find the nearest three women looking at her, and the silence around her station suddenly wide enough to drive a hearse through.

"Dust," she said, hoarse. "Bit of dust."

"Aye," said Mrs. Brierley, after a moment. "Dust."

And they all turned back to their work, every one of them, and Marisol understood that they knew—knew exactly what dust meant, what they were all swallowing shift by shift, year by year—and that their mercy consisted of agreeing to the lie together. There was a strange comfort in it. There was also the knowledge that the same courtesy would extend to her all the way to the fever shed door, and no further.

When the shift ended she lingered at the basket longer than usual, arranging, rearranging, until the room had emptied. Then she unwrapped the apron at the bottom and looked at today's addition. More spots than yesterday. Slightly larger. In the gray light through the high windows they looked like a map of somewhere she didn't want to go.

She wrapped it tighter and put it back, and told herself the thing she had been telling herself for six days: *not yet.* Whatever it was, it was not yet bad enough to count. When it was bad enough to count, she would decide what to do, and the decision would be hers, made clear-eyed, not made by panic in a doorway with an overseer watching. She was sixteen years old and she had survived her father's death and her mother's death and Uncle Aldous's arithmetic, and she was not going to be written into any parish book by a cough she hadn't authorized.

Outside, the evening came down over Ancoats in its usual colors—coal smoke and lamplight and the smell of the canal—and the bells began.

Nine strokes. The pause, long as a held breath. Nine strokes again.

She stood in the street with her shawl pulled up over her mouth and counted along, because someone had to, because counting was a way of refusing to flinch. Around her the district did the same in a hundred doorways: women pausing with water jugs in their arms, men stopping halfway through lighting pipes, children being pulled indoors by wrists. Nobody spoke. The second nine finished, and the silence that followed was the loudest sound Manchester had made all week.

Eight days now.

* * *

Home was two rooms above the chandler's shop, reached by stairs that smelled of tallow and lamp oil, and Bram was not yet back from wherever Bram went these days—which lately meant anywhere but home, hours vanishing into errands Marisol wasn't given the shape of. Uncle Aldous sat at the bench with the shop ledger open and a candle burning down beside it, doing sums that never came out in the family's favor.

"You're late," he said, without looking up.

"The bells delayed me."

That got his head up. He looked at her for a moment too long, and she watched him do his own arithmetic—the cough she'd been hiding badly for a week, the pallor she couldn't powder over, the weight she'd lost that showed at her collarbones—and watched him decide, visibly, deliberately, not to say any of it. He was getting better at that, her uncle. Choosing not to know things. It was practically a profession with him now.

"Nine days," he said instead. "Then the roads close. I've been making lists."

"Lists of what?"

"What we can carry. What we can sell. What—" He stopped. Turned back to the ledger. "It doesn't matter yet. Eat something. There's bread."

There was bread, and dripping, and weak tea, and Marisol ate standing at the window because sitting down had begun to make the ache under her ribs settle in more thoroughly, like a lodger deciding to stay. Below in the street, the district moved through its evening rituals, and she found herself cataloguing it the way you catalogue a face you might not see again—the gin shop's light, the rag man's cry going thin up the hill, a girl of maybe thirteen hurrying past with a bundle tied in a checked cloth, walking fast, looking back twice.

Off the books, Marisol thought. Before dawn tomorrow, probably. One of Ashworth's twenty. Her mother would have stood at this same window and watched, and said nothing either, because what was there to say—*run after her?* Run her down and bring her back to the cordon, to the fever sheds, to the parish book?

Daughters first. Because daughters don't fight.

Marisol put her hand flat against the cold glass and realized, with a clarity that felt almost like the fever starting, that she was tired—not body-tired, though God knew her body had opinions—but tired of being managed. Aldous managing her by not asking. Bram managing her by not telling. The overseers managing her by the simple mechanism of a wage she couldn't afford to lose. Everyone in her life had arranged themselves around her as though she were another piece of fragile stock, to be packed carefully and shipped when convenient.

Well. She had spent three years learning to hide things—coughs, cloth, fear—and hiding, she understood now, was just fighting conducted in secret. And she was done conducting everything in secret.

"Bram's back late again," Aldous said behind her. "Third night running. Says it's the observatory job."

"Is it?"

"I don't ask. Asking costs more than not knowing, in my experience."

Marisol turned from the window. Her uncle's candle had burned to a stub; the ledger lay open to a page of figures crossed out and rewritten, crossed out and rewritten, a man arguing with arithmetic he was losing. She loved him. That was the worst part, the part that made everything else unforgivable and necessary both at once—she loved this frightened, coin-counting, truth-avoiding man, and she could see with terrible ease how a person could love a family straight into a lie.

"The bells rang nine and nine," she said. "In eight days the cordons go up. And this week, twenty girls have gone out of Ashworth's in covered wagons before sunrise, and nobody's written a word about it anywhere, because the people it's happening to are the people nobody writes words about." She heard her own voice come out level and cold and recognized, distantly, her mother's voice. "Somebody ought to write words about it."

Aldous looked up sharply. "Mari. Whatever you're thinking—don't. People who write words about things in this town end up in the canal, or worse, in courtrooms. We keep our heads down. Eight days, and we survive it the way we survive everything. Small, and quiet, and together."

Together, Marisol thought, was doing a great deal of work in that sentence. Together according to whose list. Together in what order of priority. She had seen the way her uncle's eyes moved among them at supper lately, quick and guilty, like a man pricing furniture he hoped no one noticed him appraising.

"Small and quiet," she agreed, and smiled at him, because smiling was also a thing you learned on the sorting floor, and she was very good at her trade.

She climbed to the room she shared with Bram, and by the light of a borrowed match she took out the little tin box she kept beneath a loose floorboard—her own archive, small and quiet—and added to it a fresh page torn from the back of her prayer book. On it, in pencil, in handwriting neat as a sorter's tally, she wrote what she knew: *Ashworth. Twenty girls. Wagons before dawn, Tuesday through Saturday. Nan Whitlow among them. Destination given as Derbyshire — unverified.*

Below that, after a moment, she added: *Someone should ask who signs for the wagons.*

Because manifests existed. Even for human cargo—especially for human cargo, when the cargo was being moved to dodge a quarantine, there had to be paper, names, signatures, a clerk somewhere with ink on his fingers and a copy of every departure filed away in a warehouse office. Paper could be hidden. Paper could be sold. Paper, Marisol thought, pressing her palm hard against the ache beneath her ribs until it quieted, could be *read aloud*, in the right room, at the right hour, in front of the right people.

She folded the page into the tin, pushed the floorboard home, and lay down in the dark to listen to her own breathing do its damaged work.

Eight days. Somewhere across the landing, she heard Bram come in at last—soft-footed, later than midnight, carrying something heavy in the way they moved, a weight that wasn't tools. Marisol closed her eyes and did not ask. Not tonight.

But soon, she promised the dark. Soon she would stop being the one everybody managed.
