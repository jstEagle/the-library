## 9. A Debt in Weavers' Names

Nettie Hargreaves lived at the top of a house that had once been three houses, on Angel Street, where the brickwork sweated soot and the stairwell smelled of tallow and old cabbage. Bram climbed the four flights with the letter folded against their chest, inside their waistcoat, over the heart, as though paper could be held prisoner by proximity. Twice they stopped to breathe. Once they nearly turned back.

The letter had been open all night on the bench beside the widow's mantel clock, its seal broken, its contents read eleven times. By dawn Bram knew it by heart, which was worse than knowing it at all, because a thing memorized stops being astonishing and starts being architecture—load-bearing, permanent, part of the structure of one's life. *The Zhou family knows what silence is worth. They have known since 1826.* The hand was not their mother's after all; that had been the first cruelty of the night, discovering the address on the envelope was forged, a mimicry good enough to wound but wrong in the way of a copied key that turns with difficulty. The true hand was cramped, clerkish, precise. A man who wrote for his living. A man like Silas Cray's ledger name, though Bram had not yet let themselves think that thought through to its end.

They had come instead to Angel Street, because there was one person alive who had stood where the letter claimed the Zhous had stood, and grief, however violated, however pocketed and carried about like stolen goods, still wanted witnesses.

* * *

Nettie opened the door before Bram knocked twice. She was small the way old machinery is small—all the non-essential parts worn away, only the working components left—and her eyes were the pale grey of unpolished steel. She looked at Bram a long moment.

"You're Mei's eldest," she said. Not a question.

"You knew my mother?"

"I knew everyone." She stepped back from the doorway. "Come up, then, if you've come down to it. Nobody climbs four flights for gossip."

The room at the top was clean and narrow and full of light from a single tall window that looked out over a forest of chimneys. Loom shuttles hung on the wall like relics in a saint's chapel—six of them, each polished dark by decades of hands. Nettie sat Bram at a table scrubbed white with lye and set down tea without asking whether it was wanted.

"Your mother sent you," Nettie said.

"No." Bram's voice came out smaller than intended. "A letter did."

Something moved behind the old woman's eyes—not surprise; recognition. "Sit straight," she said. "And drink your tea while it's hot. What I have to tell you goes better with something warm in you."

Bram drank. The tea was bitter and strong and tasted faintly of smoke, and it anchored them in the chair, in the room, in the terrible ordinary morning.

"I'll say the names first," Nettie said, "because if I say them last you'll remember nothing else. And because you deserve them plain, the way we got them plain, in '26, when half this district starved on nine shillings a week and the masters cut it again and called it charity." She folded her hands on the table. Her knuckles were enormous under the skin, knuckles built by thirty years at the loom. "The Zhous informed. Your grandfather Aldous the elder—he kept the chandler's books, he saw who came and went, who collected strike pay, who met whom behind the dye works. He wrote it down and sold it to the masters' committee through a man named Whitfield. Three families fed him names. The Zhous were the steadiest. Steadiest and cheapest."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way the floor of a mill does when the great engines below begin to turn. Bram gripped the table's edge.

"The strike broke in March," Nettie went on, level as a plumb line. "Committee arrested—seven of them, my Thomas among them. Blacklisted after. Thomas never worked a loom again. He died in '31 of what the doctors called inflammation and what we called the blacklist." She said it without heat, the way one recites scripture learned young. "There were others who informed. The Okonkwos left the city after. The Pritchetts stayed and prospered and are now respectable, which is the same as leaving. But the Zhous—" she looked at Bram, and her gaze was not cruel, which was somehow worse, "—the Zhous were the ones everyone trusted. That was the injury of it. Trust is the whole coin of a strike, child. Without trust, a strike is just hunger with a flag."

"My grandfather," Bram managed. "Not my mother."

"Mei was fourteen in 1826." Nettie poured herself more tea. "She didn't inform. She inherited the debt. That's how these things work—the sin doesn't die with the sinner, it sits in the till like bad coinage and gets passed hand to hand until someone has the stomach to melt it down or the cowardice to spend it." She paused. "Mei spent it. In 1838. When your brother was dying."

Bram's breath stopped. There had been a brother. A name never spoken in the shop above the chandler's, a small absence shaped like a cradle, a room that became a storeroom that became nothing mentioned at all.

"Jun," Bram whispered.

"Jun." Nettie nodded. "Consumption, or the fever—they called it five things in five months. Your mother took him to three surgeons and the infirmary turned them away twice, beds full, and the third time there was a medicine—laudanum syrup, quinine, God knows—a course of treatment that cost more than a year of a clockmaker's wages. And there was a man who came to the shop and offered to settle an old account. All Mei had to do was confirm what her father's book had recorded. Sign her inheritance. One signature, one vial, one month bought for the boy." Nettie's voice did not soften; it simply stated. "She signed. Jun lived six weeks longer than the surgeon promised. Whether the medicine bought those weeks or the boy fought for them himself, no one can say. But she signed."

* * *

Bram did not remember leaving the table. They found themselves at the window, looking down at the chimneys, at the district laid out below like a machine seen from above—every street a drive-belt, every rooftop a cog, every life geared into every other life, grinding forward. Their mother's face floated somewhere in all that machinery: Mei Zhou, who had taught Bram to file a pallet arbor true, who had said *a clock forgives nothing and forgets nothing*, who had died two years ago with her hands folded and her accounts balanced and her secrets, apparently, sold onward like everything else the family owned.

"She never told us," Bram said to the glass. "Twenty years. She never—"

"Would telling have saved you anything?" Nettie asked. "You'd have grown up under it either way. Children always carry the debts. Knowing the amount early doesn't lighten it. It only ruins the years you might have been happy not counting."

"That's not mercy. That's theft."

"It's both." The old woman came and stood beside them at the window, small and iron-grey. "Most things done out of love in this city are both. That's the trick of Manchester—it never lets you choose clean. Your mother betrayed men whose children went hungry so her own child could have six more weeks. You want me to tell you which side of that scale weighs more, and I can't, because I watched Thomas cough blood for four years and I would have informed on the Archbishop of Canterbury for one more spring with him. Love doesn't make people good, Bram Zhou. It makes them exact."

The word landed like a hammer on an anvil. *Exact.* Bram thought of the master clock's case, of the tampered escapement, of the sealed letter placed precisely where a repairer of clocks could not fail to find it. Someone had been exact too. Someone understood that the surest way to destroy a family was not to accuse it but to invoice it.

"The man who wrote to me," Bram said slowly. "He knew about 1838. He knew about Jun. That's not in any book my grandfather kept."

Nettie was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, careful, the voice of someone stepping onto thin ice having already tested it with one foot. "There was a copyist at the masters' committee in those days. Young then. Kept the minutes, kept the ledgers, kept everything, because keeping things was how a clever poor man made himself rich. He'd know what your grandfather wrote. He'd know what your mother signed. And he'd know—" she turned and looked at Bram fully, and Bram saw that the pale eyes were afraid, and that the fear was older than this morning, "—he'd know exactly what a family will pay to keep a thing buried."

"His name."

"You don't need it from me. You've already got it. It came to you sealed in wax, didn't it?" She crossed to the mantel, where six loom shuttles gleamed, and touched one gently, like touching a grave. "I'm eighty-one years old, Bram. I buried a husband and two sons and a strike. I've kept silent fifty years because silence was all that was left to trade. But silence rots same as anything else, and lately I can smell it going off in myself." She faced Bram. "So here's what I'll give you, and mind, it isn't forgiveness and it isn't absolution, because I haven't got either in stock. Your mother was a betrayer and your mother was a saint and both statements are true and neither one is the whole of her. If some gentleman in a clean collar thinks he can price that truth and sell it back to you in installments—"

She stopped. Down in the street, a bell had begun to ring—not the observatory bell, not yet, but the parish church, ringing the quarter hour with its usual cracked insistence. Nine days. Everyone in the district could now count to nine.

"If he comes for you," Nettie finished quietly, "you come back here. Because I think, Bram Zhou, that before this cordon closes, somebody's going to have to say the names out loud. All of them. And I am the last one living who remembers every name there is—including yours."

Bram stood, legs unsteady, the letter a square of fire against their chest. At the door they paused.

"Did my mother ever regret it? The signature?"

Nettie looked out at the chimneys a long moment before answering.

"She came here once. Year before she died. Sat where you're sitting and asked me the same question you just asked, word for word." The old woman's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "I told her what I told you: love makes people exact. And she said—" Nettie's voice caught, just slightly, the first crack in all that iron, "—she said, *then I leave it to my eldest to be more exact than I was.*"

Bram walked down the four flights with the letter burning against their heart and their mother's last instruction ringing in their skull like a struck bell. Whatever came next—whoever waited behind that clerkish handwriting and that respectable name—they would need tools for it. Precision. Patience. An artisan's revenge measured to the hundredth of a second.

They stepped out onto Angel Street and turned toward the observatory, where a keeper was waiting who had lied to them twice, and a ledger page that would not lie at all.
