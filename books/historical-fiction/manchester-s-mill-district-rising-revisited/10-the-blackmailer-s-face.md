## 10. The Blackmailer's Face

The name in the ledger had been written twice, once by the keeper and once countersigned, and Bram had carried it out of the observatory the way a pickpocket carries a stolen watch—pressed close, warm with fear of being caught holding it.

*Silas Cray.*

Bram said it to themselves on the walk home that night, testing its weight against their teeth. The name was not unfamiliar; that was the trouble. It was a name that appeared in places where good news lived. Painted small and gold on the subscription board outside the Ragged School. Printed in the *Manchester Guardian* among the lists of subscribers to the fever fund. Spoken by the vicar at St. Philip's with the particular reverence men reserve for those who give without being asked twice.

A benefactor. A clerk of the magistrate's court. A man whose hands, if the ledger was to be believed, had been inside their mother's clock case, and before that, perhaps, inside her life.

Uncle Aldous was asleep when Bram came in, or pretending to be, his back a wall of deliberate breathing in the room they shared above the chandler's shop. Marisol was still on the sorting floor—night shift, since the rumors started, because the owners paid a halfpenny more for the hours nobody wanted. Bram sat alone at the workbench with a single candle and did not open the letter again. They had read it once, at midnight, three nights past, and once was enough to know its architecture: the accusation of 1826, the demand of silence, the promise of one berth on a barge that would leave before the cordon closed. What Bram needed now was not more words but a face to hang them on.

So two mornings later, Bram put on their least-worn coat—the grey one, brushed, the cuffs turned so no one saw where the lining had gone shiny—and walked down into the respectable end of the district as though they belonged there.

* * *

Cray & Associates occupied the first floor of a warehouse on Whitworth Street that had been built new within the decade, its brick still the colour of dried apricots, its windows tall and clean enough that the light came through unbroken. Below, the ground floor held bonded cotton; above, according to the brass plate beside the door, were offices. A boy in buttons took Bram's card—Bram had made one themselves, on the shop's spare stock, *Z. ZHOU, CLOCKMAKER, MILL ROW*—and disappeared up the stairs with the unhurried gait of someone who has never been shouted at in his life.

Bram waited in the entryway beneath a print of the observatory, its dome rendered nobler than it was, and listened to the building breathe. Warehouses breathed differently from workshops. A workshop creaked and ticked and smelled of oil and metal shavings; this place exhaled slow and even, like something well fed.

The boy returned. "Mr. Cray will see you."

The office at the top of the stairs was everything the shop was not. Wide, carpeted, warmed by a grate that burned coal without economy. Books stood in matched sets along one wall, spines too unworn ever to have been opened. On the desk, arranged with an exactness that made Bram's craftsman's eye ache with recognition—a fellow hand at work, though on different materials—lay papers squared to the desk's edge, a blotter without a single stain, a letter knife of silver.

And behind the desk rose Silas Cray himself.

He was perhaps fifty-five, thickset without being fat, dressed in black broadcloth of a cut that managed to be both modest and expensive—the costume of a man who wished you to notice his charity rather than his tailoring. His hair was iron-grey and combed with care. His face was broad, ruddy, given entirely to warmth: the eyes creased for smiling, the mouth built for sympathy, the whole arrangement of him radiating the assurance of a man who has never once doubted that he is good.

"Master Zhou." He pronounced the name correctly, which unsettled Bram more than mispronunciation would have done. "I confess I've been hoping you'd call. Sit, sit—you'll take tea? Miss Pardoe," he called through the inner door, "tea for our guest, and the good cups."

"I haven't come about tea, sir."

"No," Cray agreed pleasantly, settling back. "You've come about a clock."

The word dropped between them like a gear finding its mesh. Bram kept their face still. They had rehearsed a dozen openings on the walk over—an inquiry about the commission, a fiction about the escapement, anything that would let them approach the matter sideways—but Cray had removed the sideways altogether, and Bram understood that this too was technique. He would let nothing be approached except directly, on his own floor, under his own lamp.

"The observatory clock," Bram said. "Your name is in the repair ledger. You commissioned the work."

"I did. Public-spirited thing to do, I'd have thought. The old mechanism was failing; the whole district sets its watches by that bell. Bakers, weavers, millowners, the courts—" he gestured lightly at the air, as though the district hung there on strings he controlled—"all of us keeping time together. A city is only as honest as its clocks, Master Zhou. That's a sentiment I'd expect a man of your trade to share."

"And yet the mechanism had been altered."

Something moved behind Cray's eyes—not alarm, nothing so crude. More like a man adjusting his collar in a mirror: a small, private correction.

"Altered," he repeated, tasting the word.

"Deliberately. The strike train's been modified so the bell falls out of true time by a variable interval—seconds some days, better than a minute on others, depending on temperature and how long she runs between windings. Whoever did it knew mechanisms very well. Better than the observatory keeper does." Bram set both feet flat on the floor. "It's elegant work, sir. I'll grant it that. It took me three days to find it, and I was looking."

Silence, then, of a particular quality. Miss Pardoe came in with the tea—good cups, as promised, porcelain with gilt rims—and Cray thanked her by name and asked after her mother's cough, and the exchange was so genuinely kind that Bram felt the trap of it close around the whole room. This was how he worked, Bram realized. Not with menace. With attentions.

When the girl had gone, Cray poured for them both, and spoke as though the conversation had merely paused for refreshment.

"You're your mother's child," he said. "Everyone said so. Mei Zhou, who could look at a broken thing and see exactly what had been done to it." He passed the cup across. His fingers were clean, the nails trimmed, a wedding band worn smooth. "She had that gift too. Seeing what was done. And knowing when to leave it alone."

Bram did not touch the cup. "My mother is dead, Mr. Cray."

"Two years this spring. Influenza, I understand, though the winter took so many." He sighed, and the sigh sounded like sorrow because every part of his face was built to sound like whatever it pleased. "She was a remarkable woman. Came from nothing, built a trade, raised you two alone after your father went into the river. The district likes a story like that. I like a story like that. I've told it myself, at subscription dinners—*the little clockmaker on Mill Row*, self-made, respectable." He smiled, and the smile reached all the way to his eyes, and that was the most frightening thing Bram had ever seen. "Respectability is a fabric, Master Zhou. Like any fabric, it can be woven, and it can be unpicked. Thread by thread. I hold a great many threads in this district. Some of them are yours."

"Then say what you mean," Bram said, and heard their own voice come out level, which surprised them. "Say it plainly. I mend clocks for a living; plainness is my trade."

Cray laughed—a real laugh, delighted, terrible. "Oh, she'd have liked that. Very well. Plainly."

He folded his hands on the blotter, and for a moment the benevolence drained out of his face like water from a lock, leaving something older underneath—not rage, nothing hot, but a kind of patient arithmetic. The face of a man doing sums in which people were figures.

"In 1826, certain information came into certain hands regarding the conduct of certain weavers on the canal-side mills. Names of strike committee men. Where they met, what they carried, which masters they'd leaned upon. The consequence was arrests, transportations, a broken strike eight months sooner than it otherwise might have run. Masters resumed at reduced rates. Order was preserved. Everyone agrees, now, that order was preserved, and everyone profits from order—including, may I note, the family of the apprentice clockmaker sitting in my office wearing his best coat."

"My mother was sixteen years old in 1826," Bram said quietly.

"She was, and she wasn't born until '29 besides—it was her father's hand, or so the paper says, and her inheritance from it bought the shop, and the shop fed you. Sins travel downstream, young man. Or—" the faintest tilt of the head, "—daughter. Forgive me; the district isn't sure what to call you, and neither, I think, are you, and I find I don't much care. You're a Zhou. That's sufficient."

Bram's hands had gone cold in their lap. They kept them still. *He knows,* they thought. *He has always known everything. Knowing is his trade, the way mending is mine.* "What do you want?"

"Continuation," Cray said simply. "The same thing I've wanted these twenty years. Silence. Your uncle has been admirably sensible about it—small sums, quarterly, never a fuss. But your uncle is a tired man, and tired men make errors, and errors require management. So I'm consolidating my accounts." He opened a drawer, withdrew a paper, and slid it across the blotter without letting go of it. "One signature. One confession—your uncle's, freely written, acknowledging the old business and absolving me of any part in arranging the berths that may or may not become available before the cordon. In exchange: discretion continues, the letter in the clock case becomes waste paper, and certain doors remain open that would otherwise—" he smiled again, warmly, helpfully— "close."

"And if we don't sign?"

Cray released the paper and leaned back, and the benevolence flowed back into his face like tide into a harbour, complete, seamless, as though it had never left.

"Why, then nothing at all happens today," he said. "That's the beauty of my line of work, Master Zhou—I don't do anything. Things simply happen. A clerk misreads a date. An inspector finds dust in a workshop's weights. A sick girl on a sorting floor is reported to the sanitary committee, and sick girls get counted, and counted girls get locked in when the cordon comes." He picked up his own teacup and drank, delicately. "I'm told the sorting floors are full of rumours. I'm also told your sister keeps working the night shift with a cloth she doesn't show anyone. Terrible thing, cholera. Terrible thing, consumption. So easy to mistake one for the other on a paper form. And forms, Master Zhou—" he touched the immaculate blotter, "—are my especial talent."

The room was very warm and very quiet. Somewhere below, a dray rattled past on Whitworth Street, ordinary, indifferent. Bram looked at Silas Cray—charity patron, subscriber to fever funds, beloved of vicars—and understood at last what they were looking at. Not a monster. Monsters could be fought; monsters announced themselves. This was worse: a good man's face stretched over a machine, smiling, and the machine ran on kindness and threats ground so fine together that no blade could separate them. He did not even hate them. That was the horror of it. You cannot hate a man back who has never hated you; you can only refuse him.

"I'll tell you what I told your mother, the last time we spoke," Cray said, setting down his cup. "I told her: *Mei, I am the best friend this family has. Every other man in this district who knows what I know would use it cruelly. I use it gently.* She believed me, in the end. She paid, and she stayed silent, and she died respectable." He rose, signalling the audience closed, and extended his hand across the desk. "Think about the signature. There's no hurry—well. There is always a hurry in Manchester, isn't there? The bells keep counting. But you'll find, I think, that you and I want very similar things. Safety. Quiet. Time."

Bram stood. Did not take the hand. Cray absorbed the refusal without a flicker, folding it away as neatly as he folded everything.

"One question," Bram said at the door. "The alteration to the clock. If the bell strikes untrue, and the courts set their sessions by that bell, and your papers carry dates and hours—then every document stamped against the observatory bell answers to you. Doesn't it, Mr. Cray?"

For the first time, Silas Cray's smile acquired a genuine edge, thin as a graver's blade.

"Elegant work," he said. "I did tell you your mother's gift ran in the family." He nodded toward the door. "Good day, Master Zhou. Mind the stairs—they're steep, and I should hate for anything to happen to you on the way down."

* * *

Bram walked back across the district without remembering any of the streets. The sky had gone the colour of wet slate, and the first spots of rain struck the pavement as the mills belched their noon smoke into it, and everywhere around them the city ticked on—carts and shuttles and hammers and hearts—all of it keeping time to a bell that lied.

They thought of their mother at sixteen-and-unborn, of Nettie Hargreaves' cracked voice naming names, of Aldous's quarterly sums paid in shame, of Marisol hiding a blood-flecked rag in the ash bucket. They thought of the escapement waiting under the observatory dome, tampered, obedient, the conspirator's heartbeat, and of their own hands, which knew mechanisms better than any man alive in this district.

Cray had offered them safety, quiet, and time.

By the time Bram reached Mill Row, rain running off their chin, they had decided which of the three they meant to take—and it was none of them.
