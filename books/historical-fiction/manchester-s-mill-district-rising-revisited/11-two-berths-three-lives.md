## 11. Two Berths, Three Lives

The note came folded inside a hymnal, which Aldous thought was a fine joke for a man like Silas Cray—God's own binding around a devil's errand—and he stood in the shop doorway turning it over twice before his nephew's voice from the bench made him pocket it unopened.

"Aldous, hold this gear train still, will you? The third wheel keeps skipping."

"Your hands are younger than mine," Aldous said. "Hold your own gears."

But he came anyway, because that was what Aldous Zhou did: he came, and he held things steady while other people did the work that mattered. He had been holding things steady since his sister-in-law died—since before that, if he was honest, since the day Wei Zhou had put her infant daughter in his arms and gone back to her accounts and her secrets and her slow, meticulous ruin. Bram's long fingers coaxed the escapement into rhythm, and the widow's mantel clock—another free job, another kindness the shop could not afford—began to tick with the patient regularity of something that did not know it was dying.

Aldous went up the stairs to the room behind the kitchen where he slept, or lay awake, which amounted to the same thing these nights. He locked the door. He took out the hymnal.

The paper inside was heavy stock, cream-colored, the kind that cost more per sheet than Marisol earned in an hour at the sorting floor. He already knew what it would say. He knew it the way you know the weight of a coin you have counted ten thousand times.

*Mr. Zhou,* it said, in a clerk's beautiful copperplate. *Circumstances have improved. Two berths are now available on the barge *Lady Amaranth*, departing the Bridgewater basin on cordon eve, nine o'clock. In consideration of this generosity, I ask only for your signature upon the enclosed document, which I am confident you will find both accurate and forgiving.*

*Yrs. etc.,*

*S.C.*

The enclosed document was exactly as forgiving as Cray's letters always were, which was to say not at all. Aldous read it standing, then sitting, then lying flat on the narrow bed with the paper held above his face because his arms had begun to shake. It was a confession, though it wore the clothing of one: a statement, sworn and signed, attesting that in the year 1826, one Silas Cray, then a junior clerk of no particular authority, had been coerced by unnamed parties—including, the document implied with a delicacy that was its own obscenity, certain members of the Manchester trading community—into recording testimony against striking weavers, testimony which Mr. Cray had always regretted and had spent his life attempting to amend through charitable works.

It absolved him. That was the whole architecture of it. Every clause bent toward the same quiet conclusion: *Silas Cray did what he could; others did worse.* And the signature line waited at the bottom like an open mouth.

Aldous set the paper down on his chest and looked at the ceiling, where the plaster had cracked in a pattern like a river delta, and he thought about arithmetic.

He was good at arithmetic. He had never been good at anything else—not at clocks, like Bram, who could listen to a movement and name the fault in it the way a mother names her child's cry in a crowded street; not at endurance, like Marisol, who stood twelve hours over the sorting tables and came home coughing and smiled about it; not at courage, whatever courage was, since he suspected it was mostly a thing other people had. But arithmetic—arithmetic he could do.

Two berths. Three lives.

He counted them the way he counted the rent coins every Friday night, sliding each one across the scarred table: *this one for the baker, this one for the coal, this one for the chandler below, and God help us all if the quarter-day falls early.* Three lives, two berths, and the counting went like this:

Marisol must go. That was not even a question; it was a law older than any confession. The girl was coughing blood into rags she hid under her mattress like a girl hiding love letters, and Aldous had found them, and had said nothing, because saying something would make it true. She must go. If the cholera came through Ancoats the way it had come through the other districts—if the cordon closed and the fever walked the streets picking doors—then Marisol, with her dust-scoured lungs, would be the first to answer that knock. Marisol must go.

And himself. Well. Himself was the difficult column.

He lay there doing the sums he had been refusing to do for six days, ever since the first letter, ever since the single berth had sat on the table between them like a loaded pistol everyone pretended was a clock. Because the honest sum—the sum that a better man would arrive at without flinching—was this: Bram should go.

Bram was twenty-three and had their whole life ahead of them, and was brilliant, and was—there was no gentler way to say it, and Aldous had learned long ago that gentleness was often just cowardice wearing a shawl—Bram could pass anywhere. That was the phrase that lived in his head, ugly and practical, when he woke at three in the morning and let himself think it. *Can pass anywhere.* A person who could stand in a magistrate's office and be taken for a clerk; who could walk into a warehouse office and be mistaken for a buyer's assistant; whose face, whose careful neutral manner, whose way of holding still and watching, opened doors that slammed on Aldous's broad red hands and Marisol's mill-girl stoop. Put Bram Zhou on a barge to Liverpool with a clean shirt and thirty seconds of nerve, and they would walk off at the far end into a life. Put Aldous Zhou on that barge and you had shipped a fifty-eight-year-old clockmaker's brother with bad lungs of his own and a face that every bailiff in Lancashire could pick out of a crowd.

One berth: Bram. That was the sum a better man would do.

But Aldous had never claimed to be a better man. He had only ever claimed to be the one who stayed, and staying had a way of curdling into keeping, and keeping into taking, and here he was at the bottom of the slope wondering how he'd got there so fast.

Because there was the other sum. The one he hated. The one that ran: *if I stay, who tends Marisol at the far end? Who signs the papers, who finds the lodging, who stands between her and the men who prey on girls alone in strange cities? Bram can pass anywhere—but Bram is also the one who reads blackmail letters and goes to observatories and asks questions, and asking questions in this district, this month, is a way of volunteering for the cholera cart.* And there was a third term in that equation, the one that burned: *if I give the berth to Bram, and Bram goes, then I have sent away the only person in this family who might yet dig us out of what Cray has buried us in.*

No. That wasn't honest either. He turned the coin over. *If I give the berth to Bram, I go into the cordon with Marisol, and if the fever takes me, Marisol is alone in a quarantined city with no one but a sibling who cannot be in two places at once. If I take the berth with Marisol, we both live, and Bram—*

*Bram can pass anywhere.*

The phrase again. He said it to himself the way sinners say the creed: not because it comforted, but because it was the thing they had agreed to believe.

He got up. He dressed. He went down to the kitchen and boiled water he didn't drink, and when Bram came down at midnight with ink on their fingers and asked why he was still up, he said, "Ledgers," and showed them a book of actual figures, which was the most truthful lie available to him.

* * *

He signed nothing for four days. He told himself this was prudence. Prudence had a long and distinguished career as the pseudonym of cowardice, and Aldous had employed the alias so often that sometimes he forgot which man answered to which name.

In those four days the bells changed. Everyone in Ancoats heard it before anyone understood it: the church bells, which had rung their ordinary pattern for decades, shifted on the Tuesday to the doubled peal—the quarantine signal, the one old women crossed themselves at, the one children had rhymes about that mothers boxed their ears for repeating. Nine days, the doubled peal meant. Nine days until the cordons closed and the district became a country of its own, walled in sickness, provisioned by charity and rumor.

Aldous watched the district change shape around the sound. Mill owners began moving daughters north in closed carriages, quietly, the way men move money ahead of a bank failure. The chandler below the shop sold his stock off at panic prices and bought, in the same week, passage for his family on a steamer he would not name. Men who had not spoken to Aldous in years suddenly wanted to talk about clocks, weather, anything, their eyes flicking to the door as though the cordon were a creditor who might arrive early.

And on the fourth day, Silas Cray came to the shop.

Not in person—men like Cray did not come to places like this in person, not anymore, not with a reputation as the district's benefactor to protect. He came as a smell of good wool and bay rum, as a carriage that stopped too long at the corner, as a young man in a clerk's coat who entered, bowed precisely enough to be insolent, and laid a leather folio on the counter among the pendulums and the dust.

"Mr. Zhou. Mr. Cray's compliments." The young man's voice was smooth as oiled brass. "He wondered whether you'd had leisure to consider his proposal. He understands the doubled peal has made gentlemen thoughtful."

"I've read it," Aldous said.

"Then perhaps I may observe—" the clerk glanced toward the back room, where Bram's file whispered against steel, and lowered his voice to a confidence "—that Mr. Cray considers the second berth a mark of particular respect. Not many families receive such consideration. Not many families *require* it." He smiled. "The document wants only a signature. Ink is provided."

"I don't sign papers I haven't weighed."

"Of course." The clerk's smile deepened by a degree, a man enjoying the theater of a foregone conclusion. "Mr. Cray said you'd say that. He said, *tell Aldous he was always the careful one.*" The clerk gathered his folio, paused at the door. "Nine days, Mr. Zhou. The barge doesn't wait for the thoughtful. Only for the decided."

The bell above the door gave its small cheap jangle, and the shop was itself again, except that it wasn't, except that the air tasted of ink and bay rum for an hour after.

Bram emerged from the back room wiping their hands. "Who was that?"

"Customer. Wrong shop."

Bram looked at him the way they looked at a clock that kept stopping—a long, level look, patient and terrible, the look of someone who would find the fault eventually because finding faults was the whole of what they did. Then they went back to work, and Aldous stood among his dead wife's husband's tools—he had never managed to think of them as anything but Wei's, though they had belonged to Wei's father, though ownership in this family had always been a fog—and understood that his time had run out.

* * *

He signed it on the sixth day, at night, alone, with the good pen and the good ink that Wei had bought for writing letters to suppliers who no longer existed.

He told himself stories while the nib filled. This was the skill he had perfected over forty years of staying: the telling of stories in which every terrible act was a form of protection. He signed, and the story said: *I do this so the tickets cannot be revoked. I sign the paper and take the tickets and once we are on the water the paper means nothing, words on a page in a city we have left behind.* He signed, and the story said: *Cray is finished anyway; the boy—the apprentice, Bram—is pulling threads somewhere, I've seen the ledger page copied in Bram's hand, I've seen the look on their face; by spring Cray will be undone and my signature will be ash in a fire someone else lit.* He signed, and the story said, in its smallest voice, the one he listened to least and obeyed most: *Bram will understand. Bram is the clever one. The clever ones always understand.*

He told himself the story in which this was temporary: he would take the tickets, hide them, and find some third way before cordon eve, some arrangement in which nobody was left, in which arithmetic failed and mercy won. He almost believed it. Belief, at one in the morning, with wax softening under the candle, was easy. He sealed his acceptance, hid the two tickets—in the false bottom of Wei's old tool chest, beneath the tray of pinions where no one would look, because no one touched Wei's tools but him—and blew out the candle, and lay in the dark listening to Bram's lathe ticking faintly through the floor like a heart that hadn't noticed anything was wrong.

*Bram can pass anywhere.*

He said it once more, into the dark, and for the first time it sounded less like a fact than like a sentence being passed.

* * *

On the seventh day the fever came for Marisol openly, and everything Aldous had arranged revealed itself, as all arrangements do, as a house of cards built on a table someone else was leaning on.

She came home at half past six, gray-faced, burning, and lied about it badly—"the heat of the floor, the steam-press room, I'm fine, Uncle"—and then fainted across the threshold, and Bram caught her, and Aldous stood in the doorway of his own kitchen and watched the two of them in the lamplight, the sick girl and the steady-handed one, and felt the full cost of his sums arrive at once, itemized.

Because Bram lifted her, and Bram's face over Marisol's shoulder was the face Aldous had feared for six days: not angry, not yet, but *working*—adding up the hidden rags, the changed bell pattern, the clerk with the leather folio, the uncle who had been lying about ledgers at midnight. Adding it all. And Bram was the one person alive who never stopped until the mechanism explained itself.

That night, after the doctor's boy had come and gone with laudanum and grim instructions, Aldous climbed to his room and took out the two tickets and looked at them under the candle: *Lady Amaranth*, cordon eve, nine o'clock, two berths, the names blank, awaiting whoever held them. Two names for three lives. The arithmetic sat on the table between his hands, patient as debt.

Downstairs, faintly, he heard Bram's footsteps stop outside Marisol's door. Heard the low murmur of voices. Heard silence open like a trapdoor.

He put the tickets back in the false bottom, and his hands were shaking now, properly shaking, and he thought: *two days. Give me two days to find the third way.*

From the room below, muffled through the floorboards, came the small precise sounds of Bram working at the bench past midnight—file, wheel, file—and Aldous lay staring at the cracked ceiling and understood, with the clarity that comes only too late, that whatever his nephew was building down there, it was not a clock.
