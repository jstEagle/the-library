## 12. Marisol's Bargain

Marisol Zhou learned about the berths the way she learned most things worth knowing: by keeping her ears open and her mouth shut.

The sorting floor was a cathedral of noise—carding engines below, the endless rain of cotton through the willow machines, girls' voices pitched to carry over both—and in that noise, secrets moved like moths. She had worked at Hartley & Sons' mill since she was nine years old, four years of standing at the sorting tables pulling sticks and stones from raw cotton until her fingers went numb past feeling, and in four years she had never once been surprised by anything she overheard between the bales. The overseers forgot that a small, quiet girl with dust in her braids could hear everything. They spoke of wages as though wages were weather, inevitable and no one's fault. They spoke of the cholera in Ancoats the way they spoke of wages. And on Thursday, bent over the tally desk near the loading bay, Mr. Ogden the second overseer said to his clerk, in the flat voice men used for arithmetic:

"Cray's taken two berths off the *Perseverance*. Paid for them himself, which is more than he usually does. That leaves the one."

"One what?" the clerk asked, not looking up.

"The last berth before cordon. Cray says it's spoken for. Family arrangement." Ogden's pen scratched. "Zhou. Some clockmaker connection. He didn't say more and I didn't ask."

Marisol's hands kept moving. Stick, stone, husk, out. The cotton did not care that her heart had stopped; the cotton never cared about anything. But somewhere beneath her ribs, where the cough had been living for three weeks now, something turned over and went cold.

*Two berths.* Their family was three people. Two berths, three lives, and Aldous had always been better at sums than at love.

She thought about it through the rest of the shift, through the walk home along the canal where the water lay green and sullen under a sky the color of unwashed linen, through the smell of the chandler's shop below their rooms—tar and tallow and the sweetish rot of rope ends. She thought about it while she ladled the thin barley soup into three bowls and set them on the table where Bram sat hunched over the widow's mantel clock, tweezers in hand, so absorbed in some gear-train problem that the world could have ended without their noticing.

Aldous came up the stairs late, smelling of the public house, and there was something in the way he set down his hat—carefully, squared to the table edge—that told Marisol he had been drinking to make himself brave rather than to make himself merry.

"You've heard, then," she said.

Bram looked up. "Heard what?"

Aldous's face did the thing it always did when cornered: it went smooth, a shutter drawn over a window. "Heard nothing. There's nothing to hear."

"There are two tickets," Marisol said, "on the *Perseverance*. Bought by Silas Cray. And one berth left on the whole blessed boat, and you're deciding right now which of us gets it, only you haven't had the courage to say it out loud because saying it makes it real."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed down on the soup bowls, on the clock parts scattered across the oilcloth, on the single candle they allowed themselves after dark because candles cost.

"Sit down, Mari," Aldous said gently, and that gentleness was worse than shouting. "Let me explain."

"I know how sums work, Uncle."

"It's not—" He stopped. Started again. "It's not what you think. It's not favoritism. Bram can pass anywhere. You know that. In any town, any city, anyone looks at your brother and sees a respectable apprentice, a craftsman, no mark on them. Bram can stay here through the cordon and come out the other side clean. But you—" His voice caught, and Marisol watched him decide whether to lie and then, exhaustedly, decide not to. "You've been coughing for three weeks. Don't look at me like that; I'm not blind, I raised you. If the fever takes you inside the cordon, there's no doctor, no physic, nothing but the pest-house and God's mercy. Outside, there are hospitals in Stockport. Real ones. I mean to put you on that boat if I have to carry you."

"And yourself?"

"I'll manage."

"No," Bram said quietly.

Everyone looked at them. Bram had gone back to the mantel clock—or appeared to—but now they set down the tweezers with great precision, aligned them parallel to the movement, and folded their hands.

"You'll manage," Bram repeated. "You'll manage the way Mother managed. The way she managed in '26, when someone needed informing on and the Zhous were for sale." They said it flatly, without heat, and somehow that flatness filled the whole room. "I opened the letter, Uncle. The one in the clock case. I know what we are."

Aldous sat down as though his knees had been withdrawn like a loan.

* * *

Later—much later, after Aldous had retreated to his pallet behind the partition with a candle stub and a face like a man rehearsing his own funeral—Marisol found Bram at the window, watching the district breathe its smoke into the dark.

"You knew," she said. "Before tonight. About the letter."

"A week." Bram didn't turn. "I should have told you sooner."

"Yes, you should have." She came to stand beside them. Below, the canal lay black and still, and beyond it the mill district spread itself out in a sprawl of roofs and chimneys, forty thousand lives stacked like cotton bales, waiting for the bells to change their pattern and the cordons to close. Nine days now. Maybe fewer. "Tell me everything. All of it. I'm done being protected."

So Bram told her—the letter in the mother's handwriting, the seal that matched the ledger at the observatory, Silas Cray with his clean collar and his charity patronage and his voice that made kindness and threat sound like the same word spoken twice. They told her about Nettie Hargreaves, about 1826, about the medicine bought with blood money for a dying child. They told her about the escapement, the tampering, the timestamps, though Marisol understood perhaps half of the mechanism. What she understood entirely was the shape of it: a man who sold children's salvation and called it charity, who had built a machine out of a clock and a strike and a dead woman's shame, and who meant to leave the district on a fine boat with his crimes signed away and sealed.

"And the plan," Marisol said, when Bram finished, "is that Aldous signs his confession, and you stay here marked as an informer's child, alone, inside a quarantine, with a price on our name and Cray free in the world to collect on it whenever he likes."

"That's not—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. Her hand, she noticed distantly, was trembling, and she couldn't tell anymore whether it was anger or the fever. Probably both. Everything was both, these days. "Don't tell me it's noble. Noble is what people call it when they've decided someone else gets to be sacrificed. I won't do it, Bram. I won't take that berth."

"You're sick."

"I'm sick," she agreed, and the admission came out of her like a splinter coming out of flesh, painful and relieving all at once, and she hated how good it felt to finally say it aloud in front of someone who wouldn't pretend she hadn't. "I've been sick since the third week of September. There's blood sometimes. Not much. Not yet. And if you think I will sit in a hospital in Stockport drinking beef tea while my brother stays in this district wearing our mother's sin like a brand, then you don't know me at all."

Bram looked at her then, really looked, and Marisol saw the fear in their face—not fear for themselves, never that, but fear for her, fear doing what fear does to clever people, making the world small until the only thing visible is the person you love and the shortest path to keeping them alive.

"Mari—"

"No. Listen to me. For once in this family, listen to me." She gripped the windowsill, and the wood was cold under her palms, cold enough to think against. "You keep saying the clock is the conspiracy's heartbeat. Fine. Then let's stop its heart. But a bell striking early proves nothing by itself—you said so yourself; Cray will call it a malfunction, a repairman's error, and whose name is on the repair order? Yours. He'll hang it on you before the magistrate finishes his tea. We need paper. Proof with ink on it. Something that shows what Cray has actually been doing with those berths."

Bram frowned. "The manifests?"

"The shipping manifests." The words were coming faster now, and Marisol let them come; she'd been holding them for days, turning them over in the dark of the dormitory loft where the girls slept, and they burned to be said. "Every boat leaving before cordon has to file one—cargo, crew, passengers, and who paid for each berth. The owners file them with the shipping office, and the copies route through the mill owners' association. Mr. Ogden handles the paperwork for Hartley's, and Ogden is lazy, and lazy men leave papers where a girl who fetches their tea can read them." She heard her own voice go low and fierce, a stranger's voice. "Cray isn't just buying berths for his friends, Bram. I've seen the names. Lady Whitcombe's daughters. A cotton factor from Liverpool and his wife. Half the berths on the *Perseverance* and two other boats—berths that were subscribed publicly, charitably, *for the orphan children of the mills*—sold at ten times the subscription price to families with full purses. The orphans are still here. The orphans are going into the cordon with us. And every one of those sales is written down, in Ogden's own hand, with dates and amounts."

For a long moment Bram said nothing. The candle guttered, threw their shadow huge against the wall, shrank it again.

"You could lose your place," they said at last. "If Ogden catches you copying—"

"If the cordon comes, there is no place. There's the pest-house or the grave, and Cray's signature on neither of them because he'll be safely upriver counting his profits." Marisol pushed off the sill and faced her brother squarely. "Here is my bargain, and I want you to hear all of it. I get the manifest. All of it—every berth, every name, every payment. Nettie speaks, and she speaks the whole truth, ours included; I'll stand beside her while she does it, and I'll name our mother with my own mouth if it comes to that, because a debt hidden is a debt that collects forever. And when Cray presents his pretty forged papers to the magistrate, we put the manifest in front of him at the same hour your bell strikes true. Let him explain why his timestamps and his subscriptions don't agree. Let him explain it to Nettie's face."

"And if it fails?" Bram's voice was very quiet. "If it fails, Mari, there's no boat, no bargain, no mercy. He'll see to it that none of us leaves this district. He may see to worse."

"Then we fail together, in daylight, with our names clean." She reached out and took their hand—Bram's hand, stained faintly with clock oil, steady as a pendulum even now, and she loved them for the steadiness and hated them a little for it too. "That's the trade. That's the only trade I'll sign. No berth with my name on it that costs you yours. I'd rather die honest in Ancoats than live saved by a lie with our family's name on the bottom of it."

Something moved behind Bram's eyes—she would remember this, afterward, would remember it through the fever and the long bad nights: not defeat, and not agreement exactly, but calculation, the same look they wore over a broken escapement, measuring what could be mended and what must be remade.

"Three days," Bram said finally. "Give me three days. There's a thing I have to do to the clock first—set the escapement so it strikes early on cordon eve, just a minute, just enough—and I need to be sure of the hour before you risk Ogden's desk. Can you hold that long?"

"I've held longer," Marisol said. And then, because honesty was apparently contagious tonight, she added: "I don't know that I can. The cough is getting worse. If I fall before the manifest is copied—"

"You won't fall."

"You can't promise that."

"No," Bram admitted. "But I can promise you'll be tended. Whatever happens. Whoever stays and whoever goes." They squeezed her hand once, hard, and released it. "Go to bed, Mari. Sleep if you can."

Sleep did not come. It rarely did now. Marisol lay on her pallet listening to the district cough around her—a hundred thousand lungs in the dark, some of them rattling already—and pressed her palm flat against her own chest and felt the heat banked there, patient as a fire in a closed stove.

In the morning she would go to work. She would sort cotton and fetch tea and smile her small quiet smile at Ogden, and when the moment came she would open the shipping drawer and begin to copy, line by line, name by name, the ledger of a respectable man's crimes.

Her hands shook as she undid her braids. She watched them shake, clinically, the way she watched everything now, and thought: *courage outrunning fear, but only just.*

Down in the street, far off toward the observatory hill, a single bell rang the half-hour—one minute, she might have sworn, one minute too soon.

Or perhaps that was only the fever, keeping its own time.

Either way, the clock was running.
