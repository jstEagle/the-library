## 14. Nettie's Condition

The walk to Ancoats took Bram past three houses with black crepe on the doors and a fourth where a woman stood in the doorway scrubbing the step with lye as though cleanliness were a prayer God had promised to answer. Cholera had crossed the Irwell, the milk-boy said. It was in Angel Meadow. It was in the cellar dwellings off Great Ancoats Street. It was always somewhere else until it wasn't.

Bram carried the letter.

They had carried it for eleven days now, folded against their chest inside the waistcoat pocket that still smelled faintly of clock oil, and they had learned what it cost to hold a thing without looking at it—the way one learns to ignore a tooth gone bad, or a wheel out of true that you can hear but not yet name. Their mother's handwriting had been on the outside of it. *For whoever opens this after me,* she had written once, years ago, in another hand entirely, on a recipe for cough syrup Bram still used. Two hands. Two women, almost. The mother who made syrup and the mother who signed her name to something that got weavers blacklisted.

Nettie Hargreaves lived at the top of a court off Bengal Street, in a room that had once been two rooms before some landlord's arithmetic divided them. She was eighty-one and spun flax by the window because her eyes could no longer follow thread but her fingers remembered everything, and when Bram climbed the stairs—past a family of six sharing a single hearth, past a girl no older than Marisol coughing into a shawl—she heard the knock and called out, "If you're from the relief committee I've had my coal and if you're from the parish I've had my sermon and if you're either of them again I'll set the dog on you."

"You haven't got a dog, Mrs. Hargreaves."

There was a pause, and then the door opened onto a small woman built like a knot in oak, her face a topography of wrinkles so deep they seemed carved rather than grown. Her eyes went over Bram's face the way Bram's own eyes went over a movement: reading the mechanism underneath.

"You'll be Mary Zhou's eldest," she said. "You've her chin. Come in before the whole court sees a Zhou at my door and starts its gossip."

* * *

The room smelled of flax dust and camphor and, beneath both, the sweetish ghost of pipe smoke though Nettie hadn't smoked in twenty years, she said, since her lungs voted against it. A spinning wheel occupied the window. A photograph—no, a daguerreotype, silver going grey at its edges—stood on the mantel: a young woman in a shawl, unsmiling, arms folded like a barricade.

"Your grandmother," Nettie said, following Bram's look. "Before the strike. She'd have hated knowing she'd end up framed." She lowered herself into her chair with the care of someone whose body had become a negotiation. "Sit. You didn't come across town to admire pictures."

Bram sat on the edge of the bed, which was also the sofa, which was also most of the room. They took out the letter and held it a moment, feeling its weight, then laid it on the table between them.

"I need you to tell me about 1826," Bram said. "About the strike. About who informed."

Nettie looked at the letter for a long time. Her hands, knotted around the distaff, did not stop moving; Bram suspected they never did.

"And why would I do that," she said, not as a question but as a weighing. "I've kept quiet forty years. Kept quiet through funerals and evictions and one hanging I don't talk about. Quiet is the only coat I own that's never worn thin. Why should I take it off now?"

"Because someone is using those names to bleed my family," Bram said. "Because he's selling berths off a quarantine barge meant for orphans and buying silence with the same coin. Because in nine days the cordon closes and people will be locked in with cholera while he sits behind his clean collar counting what he's made." Bram's voice cracked on the last of it and they hated that, hated the youth in it. "Because I thought you might be tired too."

"Tired." Nettie laughed, a dry sound like a shuttle passing through warp. "Aye, I'm tired. Tired isn't reason enough to hand a man like that a reason to come up these stairs."

"He won't know. I'd keep your name out of—"

"No." The word came flat and final as a weight dropping into a case. "That's your first mistake and I'll stop you there, because you're young and you think truth is a tool you can use part of at a time, like a spanner. Truth isn't a tool. It's a fire. You light half of it, you'll burn down exactly what you meant to save."

She set down the distaff. Bram realized, watching her hands go still, that she had stopped working for the first time since they'd entered—that whatever she said next she considered important enough to pay for with stillness.

"Ask me properly," Nettie said. "Ask me what you actually came to ask, and don't dress it up."

Bram's throat worked. "Will you testify? Publicly. That the informer reports against the weavers' committee were forged—or timed, or falsified—I believe Cray has been altering documents for twenty years using the observatory bell, and the old reports are among them. If the timestamps are wrong, the reports are wrong. If the reports are wrong, the names in them mean nothing."

"And the names in them," Nettie said, "include yours."

It was not a question. Bram said nothing, which was answer enough.

Nettie leaned back. Outside, the sounds of the court rose through the glass: a knife-grinder's wheel, a child crying with the flat persistence of hunger, someone beating a rug in slow exhausted strokes. The daguerreotype woman watched from her mantel with her folded arms.

"I knew your mother," Nettie said at last. "Not well. Well enough. She came to me in '44, when she was carrying you—" she nodded at Bram, "—or maybe it was the little one, the years blur. She came and she sat where you're sitting and she asked me the same question you just asked. Whether I'd ever speak. And I told her then what I'll tell you now."

She drew a breath that seemed to come up from her boots.

"In '26 I watched men starve their families on principle for seven months. I watched Samuel Fletcher die of fever in January because he'd been blacklisted in October and there wasn't bread. I watched his widow carry his boots to the pawnshop and come back with twopence and no expression at all. And I learned the names. All of them. Who wrote to the masters, who signed the affidavits, who pointed fingers across a picket line. Forty years I've carried those names the way you carry a stone in the boot—you stop noticing it hurts, you only notice when you take the boot off." Her eyes fixed on Bram, pale and terrible and clear. "And I'll tell you the worst of it, lad, since you've the chin to hear it. Your mother's name was on that list. Not as a victim."

The clock oil smell in Bram's own pocket suddenly felt like a brand. "I know," Bram said quietly. "I found her letter. In the master clock's case. It says what she did. It says she was paid."

"She was paid in medicine." Nettie's voice did not soften; that was the horror of it, that it did not soften at all. "Her first child—your brother, the one nobody in your house says aloud—was dying of the lung fever. The infirmary wanted five pounds up front and she hadn't five pence. A man came to her with an offer and a vial and she took both, and the child lived nine more days anyway, and she spent thirty years paying for those nine days. So don't sit there thinking I'm going to hand you a villainess to hate instead of a woman to grieve. Grief's harder work. Do it anyway."

Bram found they were gripping the edge of the bed frame hard enough to whiten their knuckles. The room tilted slightly and righted itself. Somewhere below, the coughing girl was being given something warm.

"So you knew," Bram managed. "All these years, you knew, and you never—"

"What? Shouted it across the district?" Nettie shook her head slowly. "What good, and to whom? The strike's dead men stay dead whether I speak or not. But now—" she lifted one knotted finger, "—now a man is selling escape tickets with those dead men's names as his currency. Now the lie is earning interest. That's different. A lie that costs nothing rots quiet. A lie that pays gets defended."

"Then you'll speak."

"I'll speak on a condition." Nettie picked up the distaff again—not working, just holding it, an anchor. "Full truth or none. You want me before the magistrate, or the newspapers, or a crowd on the steps of the Exchange, saying Silas Cray forged his papers? Then I say all of it. Every name. Including Mary Zhou. Including the two men from the Rochdale road whose families still live respectable on money they've never asked where from. Including, if you make me, the part I'm most ashamed of—which is that I saw your mother take the vial that day, and I said nothing, because I understood why, and understanding is its own kind of complicity and I've carried mine forty years same as she carried hers." She let that sit between them like cold iron. "No partial exoneration. Not for anyone. Not even for the dead. Especially not for the dead—they've had easy treatment long enough."

"My sister is seventeen," Bram said, and heard their own voice go low and dangerous. "She works the sorting floor fourteen hours a day and she's started coughing blood into a rag she hides under her mattress. If the Zhous' name goes into the record as informers, she carries it into whatever comes next. Into the quarantine house, possibly. Possibly into the ground. You're asking me to price her life against—"

"I'm asking you to tell the truth," Nettie said, "which is what everyone asks the person who happens to be holding it. And here's the arithmetic nobody wants: Marisol carries a name, or Samuel Fletcher stays forgotten twice—once by death, once by a bargain struck over his boots. Those are your choices. I know they're cruel ones. Cruelty doesn't come from me; I'm only the one refusing to lie about where it lives."

Bram stood. The room was too small suddenly, the walls too close, their mother's letter on the table between them radiating cold like a stone in winter.

"You'd trade my family's name for justice done forty years late."

"I'd trade *everybody's* name," Nettie said. "Yours, mine, the magistrates', the masters'. That's what makes it justice instead of revenge. Revenge picks its targets. Justice takes the whole bill." She looked up at Bram, and for one moment the carved old face shifted and Bram saw the girl she must have been, standing in a picket line in the rain of 1826, learning the same lesson. "You came here wanting a witness. What you got is a mirror. Take your time hating it. I'll be here. I'm always here."

* * *

Bram walked back through Ancoats without any memory of the streets between, which frightened them more than anything Nettie had said. At the corner of Bengal Street a handbill was pasted to the wall, fresh paste still gleaming:

**NOTICE — CORDON OF THE MILL DISTRICT COMMENCES SATURDAY NEXT, BY ORDER OF THE COMMISSIONERS OF HEALTH. PERSONS DESIROUS OF LEAVING BEFORE THAT HOUR SHOULD APPLY WITHOUT DELAY TO THE OFFICES OF S. CRAY, ESQ., CHARITY BERTHS GRANTED TO DESERVING CASES.**

*Deserving cases.* The words swam. Bram thought of Marisol's hidden rag, of Aldous counting coins in the shop with his lips moving, of their mother's two hands—one making cough syrup, one signing away strangers' livelihoods—and of the letter burning against their ribs.

They stopped under a gas lamp not yet lit and read the notice twice more. Saturday next. Nine days had become eight, then seven; the numbers were eating themselves.

And beneath the notice, smaller, almost an afterthought, a second bill:

**OBSERVATORY MASTER CLOCK UNDERGOING FINAL REGULATION. PUBLIC TIME THENCEFORWARD BY ITS STRIKING ALONE. ALL DOCUMENTS DATED BY OBSERVATORY BELL.**

Bram stood very still.

*All documents dated by observatory bell.*

Cray had built his whole edifice on that bell—every berth ticket, every affidavit, every forged report dated by the hour the great clock spoke. Which meant the bell was not just the conspiracy's heartbeat.

It was its signature.

And Bram held the screwdriver that governed its tongue.

They turned toward the observatory, walking faster now, the letter against their chest and Nettie's condition ringing in their ears like a struck bell that would not dampen: *full truth, every name, no partial exoneration.* Fine. Let the whole bill come due.

But first, Bram thought, climbing the observatory hill as the afternoon light went copper over the smoking town, first they would make certain that when the bell rang on cordon eve—

—it rang the *true* hour.
