## 15. Aldous Signs

The rain came down on Angel Meadow like a verdict, and Aldous Zhou stood under the broken awning of the shuttered chandlery and watched two of Cray's men come toward him across the cobbles with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told no.

He knew them by sight, as everyone in the district knew them by sight. The tall one was called Prosser and had hands like shovels; the short one was named Fenwick and smiled too readily, a man who kept his teeth for business. They did not run. Men like Cray never needed to run. The district itself did their walking for them, parting and closing behind them like water around stones.

Aldous's first thought — God forgive him, his first thought — was of the shop's ledger, and whether he had left it open on the bench where Bram might read it. His second thought was of the letter burning in his grate three nights past, how the wax seal had curled and gone the colour of an old wound before the paper took. He had watched it burn and told himself that was the end of it, that fire was a kind of forgetting.

Fire was not a kind of forgetting. Fire was only the beginning of what a man would do to keep from remembering.

"Mr. Zhou." Prosser stopped beneath the awning's edge, out of the rain with the ease of a man invited everywhere. "Mr. Cray would value a word."

"At this hour?" It came out thinner than Aldous wanted. "The shop closes at six."

"The shop." Fenwick smiled. "Yes. How is the shop?"

It was not a question about the shop. Everyone in Angel Meadow knew the state of the Zhou shop the way they knew the weather — the rent three weeks late, the widow's mantel clock mended for nothing because the woman had cried and Bram could not abide crying, the shelves thinning month by month like a man wasting of something the doctors couldn't name. Fenwick knew all of it. That was the whole point of him.

"I'll come," Aldous said.

* * *

They walked him not to the warehouse but to a private room above the Grapes on Withy Grove, where the noise of the taproom rose through the floorboards like the breathing of some vast sleeping animal. A lamp burned low. Cray sat at a small table laid with paper, ink, and — this detail Aldous would carry to his grave, the smallness of it, the domesticity — a plate of seed cake, untouched.

"Aldous." Cray rose and offered his hand, warm and dry. "You look worn. Sit. Take the chair by the fire, you're soaked through."

The fire was real and generous. That was the cruelty of Silas Cray: he understood comfort the way other men understood weapons. He poured whisky without asking, pushed the glass across until its base touched Aldous's knuckles, and waited until the first swallow was down before he said anything of substance.

"You received a letter," Cray said. "Some weeks ago now. In your sister-in-law's hand."

Aldous set the glass down very carefully, because his hand had begun to shake and he did not want the sound of it. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Cray agreed pleasantly. "Of course you don't. You know nothing. You've known nothing for eleven years, isn't that right? Since the winter of 'thirty-six, when you left the first envelope — five pounds, wasn't it? — under the loose brick by the mill-yard gate. You've been knowing nothing ever since, at regular intervals, always in small sums, always at night." He folded his hands. "You are, I think, the most knowledgeable ignorant man in Manchester."

The fire cracked. Somewhere below, a woman laughed, high and brief, and was swallowed by the taproom's murmur.

"You have me confused with another," Aldous said.

"I have you exactly right." Cray opened the drawer of the little table and drew out a document — two documents, in fact, laid side by side. One Aldous recognized at once, though he had seen it only once before, years ago, held briefly in lamplight and returned: the confession, the old one, the one naming the Zhous among those who had sold the names of striking weavers to the masters in 1826. His mother-in-law's name was on it. And beneath her name, in a younger hand than he liked to imagine, the name of the woman he had loved for thirty years — Bram's mother, Marigold, who had signed it at nineteen with medicine money in her pocket and a dying child waiting at home, or so Nettie Hargreaves would tell anyone who asked, if anyone ever asked.

The second document was new. Clean paper, fair copy, and at its foot a line left blank for a signature.

"What is it?" Aldous asked, though he already knew. He had known since the moment the two men appeared under the awning. Knowing and admitting were different countries, and he had spent his whole life paying passage between them.

"It is a statement," Cray said, "attesting that the allegations regarding my involvement in the affairs of 1826 are false — malicious fabrications spread by persons unknown — and that any letters suggesting otherwise are forgeries designed to extort an honest servant of this district." He turned it so the lamplight fell across the blank line. "You will sign it as head of the Zhou family. Your signature will settle the matter permanently. No more envelopes. No more bricks. No more letters in dead women's handwriting turning up in clock cases to torment your nephew."

"Bram found the letter," Aldous said. It was not a question either, but Cray answered it anyway, with a small nod, untroubled, as though they were discussing a delivery of oil.

"And that," Cray said, "is unfortunate. But manageable. Provided the family speaks with one voice. Provided the family says: this is forgery, this is extortion, we know nothing." He smiled then, the smile he wore in the newspapers, the one beside accounts of blankets distributed to the orphanages and subscriptions raised for the infirmary. "Silence has been expensive, Aldous. This makes it free."

"And if I don't sign?"

Cray sighed, genuinely regretful, and Aldous hated him most for that — for the care he took with his own regret, polishing it, displaying it.

"Then the magistrate receives the original confession. All of it. Every name. And when the cordon comes — nine days now, you'll have heard the bells change — the Zhou name will be on every list worth having. Informers' kin fare poorly in a locked district, Aldous. You know what people do with traitors when there's nowhere for the anger to go. There is no barge for the disgraced. There is no berth for the son of a strike-breaker." He paused, delicately. "And there is the small matter of the child."

The word went into Aldous like a blade slipped between ribs, so precisely placed that for a moment he could make no sound at all.

Marisol. On the sorting floor, coughing into her apron. He had seen the cloth she hid — he had found one in the wash copper and said nothing, because saying it aloud would make it true. Blood-flecked. The fever was in Ancoats; it was in New Cross; it was coming up the streets like water finding its level, and when the bells finished their changed pattern, the cordons would close and the sick would be counted, and the counted would be locked in with the dying.

"There is a berth," Cray said gently. "One berth, as the letter promised. But I am prepared to be generous — two berths. Two places on the *Lady Amaranth*, leaving Thursday night, before the cordon. For members of your household. Passage paid, papers clean, names unmarked." He tapped the unsigned confession. "This is the price of them."

Two berths. Three lives. Aldous sat with the arithmetic as a man sits with a debt he cannot pay, turning the figures over, looking for error, finding none.

"My nephew—" he began.

"Can pass anywhere," Cray said. It was almost kind. "That young person has a face and manner that opens doors — you've said so yourself, I believe, to the chandler, over cards. A skilled artisan, nonconforming, yes, but useful. Useful people survive quarantines. They are kept on. Whereas a girl with blood in her apron, in a sealed district, with an informer's name over her head—" He let it hang. He was expert at letting things hang.

Aldous looked at the fire. He thought: *Bram will understand.* He thought it the way a drowning man thinks of shore.

* * *

He signed.

The pen was good quality, the ink flowed black and even, and his hand — his careful craftsman's hand, the hand that had cut wheels for a thousand clocks — wrote *Aldous Zhou* in the blank space with a steadiness he did not feel and could not afterwards explain. Perhaps it was the whisky. Perhaps it was the fire. Perhaps it was simply that the body will perform its own betrayals smoothly when the mind has rehearsed them long enough.

Cray blotted the page, examined the signature, and nodded once, satisfied, the nod of a man accepting delivery of goods inspected.

"Thursday night," he said. "Nine o'clock, the lock at Dale Street. Give this token to the lock-keeper." He slid across a brass counter-mark stamped with the observatory's star. "Your tickets." Two slips of heavy paper followed, printed fine, berths seven and eight aboard the *Lady Amaranth*. "For yourself and the girl. I'd advise you not to speak of it. I'd advise you especially not to speak of it to your nephew."

"Why especially?" Aldous asked, and hated the question even as he asked it, because he knew the answer and wanted to hear it said aloud, wanted someone else to carry half of it.

Cray considered him with something that in a better man might have been pity.

"Because the young ones believe truth is a weapon that fires in one direction," Cray said. "Yours has just fired in yours. Let them keep their illusions a while longer. Kindness, Mr. Zhou, is mostly a matter of timing."

Then he stood, shook Aldous's hand again — the same warm dry grip, unchanged, which seemed obscene — and showed him out with the courtesies of a host whose guest had stayed perhaps a quarter hour too long.

* * *

The rain had thinned to a drifting mist by the time Aldous crossed the district home, and he walked slowly, taking the long way along the canal because he could not yet bear the shape of the shop windows against the dark. The water lay black and still, holding the gaslight in broken ribbons. A barge was moored downstream, tarred and silent, its cargo sheeted against the wet. In nine days, boats like it would carry out what could be carried and the rest would be shut in behind barricades and patrols and the great painted notices: QUARANTINE. BY ORDER.

He took out the two tickets and held them under a lamp bracket. Berth seven. Berth eight. His own name he would write on the manifest himself; Marisol's would follow. Two names, two spaces, and the third member of his household accounted for nowhere, protected by nothing except a lie with a signature on it and a talent for passing through doors.

*Bram will forgive me.*

He made himself think it fully, examine it honestly, the way he would examine a movement before closing a case. Bram, who had taken in the widow's mantel clock and mended it without charge and would not hear of charging. Bram, who had come home from the observatory white-faced with a letter they would not show him, and who had been carrying something heavy and alone ever since — Aldous had seen it in the set of their shoulders, in the way they now checked the shop door twice at night. Bram, who believed — who had always believed, God help them both, because Marigold had raised them to believe it — that a thing done rightly was a thing done finally.

Bram, who would learn that their uncle had signed away the family's one chance at a clean name, and had done it to save the wrong two lives, by the wrong arithmetic, and had pocketed the tickets like a man pocketing stolen bread.

*They will understand,* Aldous amended. *When the cordon comes. When the fever reaches the row. When they stand safe on the deck at Wigan or Liverpool and hear what happened here. Then they will understand that I chose the living.*

But even as he thought it, he saw the flaw in it, clear as a cracked pivot under the loupe: Bram would not be standing on any deck. Bram would be here, inside the cordon, wearing the name he had just signed back onto them — informer's kin, marked twice, once by history and once by him. He was not saving Bram from anything. He was spending Bram like coin.

And the worst of it, the unforgivable tender heart of it, was that some cold practical chamber in him had already weighed that too, and found it acceptable. Because Bram was strong. Because Bram had always been strong, had carried the shop's griefs and their mother's silences and the widow's clocks without complaint, and strength, in Aldous's arithmetic, was a thing that could be drawn upon. Strength was credit. He had been spending Bram's credit for years — the unpaid hours, the meals Bram went without so Marisol wouldn't, the coins counted and recounted on the bench while Bram worked — and Bram had never once called the account due.

So they would not call it due now. That was what he told himself, walking the last stretch of the row with the tickets folded against his chest like a second heartbeat. Bram would be angry — angry was survivable, angry blew through and passed like weather. But Bram would stay. Bram would tend the shop, and tend the district, and pass through the doors their face opened, and live, because Bram was the kind of person who lived. Whereas Marisol —

Marisol, who came home each evening smelling of cotton dust and hiding red-flecked rags in the ash bucket, who was sixteen and light as a sparrow and laughing at him across the supper table not four hours ago with that quick fierce laugh of hers, and whom he could not, would not, watch drown in a locked city for a sin committed before she was born.

He let himself in quietly. The shop was dark below; a candle still burned upstairs, behind Bram's closed door, and beneath the door he could see the shadow of them bent over the workbench, awake at midnight, working at something with the patience he envied and feared.

Aldous climbed the stairs, put the tickets in the tin box behind the loose brick in his bedroom chimney — the same trick, he realized with a lurch, that Cray had used for twenty years, the district teaching its tricks to everyone alike — and lay down in his clothes.

Sleep came slowly and left early. And in the grey before dawn he heard Bram's door open, heard the careful descent, the shop bell's small suppressed click, and knew that his nephew had gone out before first light — gone toward the observatory, or the canal, or whatever thread of the web they had been silently pulling these many days — and that the hour was coming, faster than the bells, when everything he had signed and hidden would walk back through his own front door and ask him to look at it.

He lay listening to the district wake, and told himself, one last time, in the voice he used for counting coins that would not cover the rent:

*Bram will forgive me.*

Outside, far off toward the town centre, the observatory clock began to strike the hour — and struck it, Aldous noticed, a shade too soon.
