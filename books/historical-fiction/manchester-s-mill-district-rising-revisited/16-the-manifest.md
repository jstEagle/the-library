## 16. The Manifest

The fever came for her the way the fog came off the canal—not all at once, but in layers, each one warmer than the last until she could no longer tell where the heat of the sorting floor ended and the heat inside her own skull began.

Marisol Zhou had learned, over three years at Hargreaves & Whitlow's sorting room, to read cotton by touch alone in the dark half of the morning when the gas lamps burned low and the windows were still black with soot. Her fingers knew a Egyptian staple from an American bale before her eyes did; they knew damp fiber from dry, short waste from good, and they knew—better than any overseer's glance—the precise moment a foreman was watching and the moment his attention had drifted to the girl at the second table who laughed too easily.

So it was her fingers that told her first, on the ninth day of the changed bells, that something was wrong. They were clumsy. Slow. The cotton felt like wool through them, like straw, like nothing at all, and twice she sorted a stained length into the good pile and had to fish it back out while Dora Ellery watched her with that particular sideways stillness women on the floor used instead of speech.

"You're burning," Dora said at the water break, not looking at her, addressing the tin cup in her own hands.

"I'm warm. It's the room."

"It's not the room." Dora's voice stayed flat, pitched beneath the clatter of the carding engines beyond the partition. "You've been grey since Tuesday. You cough like my mother did, God rest her, and you wipe your mouth with that rag you think nobody's seen."

Marisol said nothing. The rag in question was folded into her sleeve cuff, small and brown-stained, washed each night in cold water that didn't take the color fully out and never would.

"Cholera's in Ancoats," Dora went on. "Two streets from here. They've got the carts going already, you've heard them."

"I've heard the bells. Nine days, then cordons." Marisol turned back to her table. "The sick get locked in, Dora. The marked get locked in. I'm neither."

"You're both, if you don't sit down."

But sitting down meant being counted. Counted meant the ledger, and the ledger meant the quarantine house on Blossom Street with its barred windows and its reputation, which was that people went in and the carts went out and the two events bore only a passing acquaintance to one another. Marisol had seen a girl from the spinning room taken there in March, after the fever that wasn't cholera but might have been, and the girl had come back in eleven days thin as a bobbin and silent as one, and had never again spoken above a whisper in the yard.

She kept working. That was the whole art of it now: keep working, keep moving, keep your shoulders squared and your hands quick so that no eye lingered long enough to notice the shine of sweat on your brow or the tremble that had entered your fingers like a new apprentice who wouldn't learn. She worked through the dinner hour she should have eaten. She worked through the moment when the room tilted gently sideways and she gripped the table edge and pretended to be reaching for the lower basket.

And through all of it, underneath everything, folded into the lining of her bodice where it pressed against ribs that ached with every breath, was the paper.

She had found it four days ago, or rather she had earned it, though earning wasn't quite the word either. The mill owners' shipping clerk—a soft man named Prosser who smelled of ink and onions—had been called away during the afternoon delivery, leaving the counting-house ledger open on his desk while Marisol waited with a returned bolt that had been mis-sorted. She had not gone looking for anything. She wanted that recorded somewhere, in whatever court existed for girls on sorting floors: she had not gone looking. But the manifest lay open, and the top sheet was headed *Cordons—Passage Allocations*, and her eyes, which read as well as Bram's ever had despite leaving school at eleven, moved across it the way her fingers moved across cotton.

*Berths reserved under Orphans' Relief Fund (per S. Cray, Esq., trustee): 14.*

*Berths allocated: 14 — passengers: Ashworth (family of 4), Greaves (family of 3), Whitmore (2), Cray (1), Entwistle (5)...*

There were no orphans among those names. She knew some of them. Mrs. Ashworth wore silk to chapel. The Entwistles owned two warehouses and a horse that was better fed than any child in Ancoats.

Fourteen berths bought with charity money for children with no parents, sold instead—at what price she could only guess—to families who could pay for what dying strangers had been promised free.

She had stood there perhaps ten seconds. Ten seconds in which the fever in her blood seemed to go very quiet, the way a clock goes quiet just before it strikes. Then she had copied the page. Not all of it—her hands weren't steady enough and time wasn't generous enough—but the heading, the allocation line, five names with their numbers, and at the bottom, in the corner where clerks signed their accountability away, the initials *S.C.* beside the trustee's mark she'd have known anywhere by now, because Bram had drawn it for her once in the dust of the shop bench and made her memorize it: a coiled serpent around a bell.

Then Prosser had come back smelling of onions, and she had handed him her mis-sorted bolt, and walked home through streets that swayed like deck planks, and climbed the stairs to the rooms above the chandler's, and said nothing to anyone, because saying it aloud would make it real, and real things demanded action, and she was not certain her body could survive action.

That had been four days ago. The paper had grown heavier every night against her ribs, as though it were absorbing her fever, as though it fed on her.

Tonight, on the ninth evening of the changed bells—with eight days left and the cordon lists going up on the church doors tomorrow—she took it out.

* * *

Bram was at the observatory again. Third night this week. Uncle Aldous sat below at the shop bench pretending to clean a movement he had cleaned twice already, because pretending to work was easier than speaking, and the space between him and Marisol had developed a silence with walls to it, a silence she could feel pressing on her skin like a second fever.

She dressed carefully. This was its own ordeal: buttons required negotiation, laces required strategy, and by the time she stood before the cracked mirror in her good shawl her shift beneath was soaked through and her legs shook like the widow's mantel clock Bram was always repairing. In the mirror her face was flushed high on the cheeks, hollowed beneath, and her eyes were too bright, the way candles burn brightest just before the tallow gives.

Downstairs, Aldous looked up. His face did the thing it always did now—flickering between suspicion and fear and that terrible tenderness he thought he hid.

"You're going out."

"I'm going to the shop on Turner Lane. Thread." A lie so smooth it startled her. "We're out of black."

"At this hour?"

"The cordon comes, Uncle. Nothing will be ordinary hours again." She let that sit, and watched it work on him, watched guilt move behind his eyes like a fish under ice. He'd been strange for days—stranger than usual—pocketing things, burning papers in the grate after midnight when he thought she slept, wearing the look of a man carrying water in both hands across a frozen pond.

"Take the lamp," he said finally, which was surrender, and she took it, and the weight of it nearly buckled her arm, and she smiled at him anyway because the smile cost nothing and bought her the door.

The walk to Bram's workbench at the back of the chandler's loft—where Bram had taken to working late since the observatory summons, since the letter, since whatever it was that had made their hands unsteady for the first time in their life—was six streets. It took her forty minutes. She stopped twice, once against a wall whose brick was cold enough to make her gasp, once in the lee of the mission hall where a preacher was nailing up a notice about the cordon boundaries and she stood listening to the hammer blows count down something in her chest that wasn't the fever.

Eight days. And fourteen berths sold that belonged to orphans. And somewhere in Silas Cray's ledgers, the arithmetic of who lived and who was locked in, written in a respectable hand with a serpent-and-bell signature, and everyone in the district calling him benefactor.

Her mother had died with a name like that attached to hers, according to a letter Bram wouldn't discuss. Informer. Marisol had turned the word over for nights now, testing its edges. She had been nine when their mother died and had remembered only lavender water and hymns and hands that braided hair with great patience. Bram remembered more and said less. But whatever their mother had done, she had done it in a world built by men like Cray—men who sold salvation by the berth and called the pricing charity—and Marisol found, rising through the fever like a bubble through dark syrup, a fury so clean it frightened her. Not at her mother. At the arithmetic itself. At a city where a magistrate's clerk could buy silence with a boat ticket and call the transaction mercy.

She climbed the chandler's stairs on her hands at the last flight. There was no dignity left to preserve. There was only the paper.

* * *

Bram heard her before she reached the landing—that was the clockmaker in them, ears trained to hear a mechanism failing before it failed, and what they heard was a gait wrong in three places, breath wrong in four, and a hand on the banister that paused one beat too long at every step.

They opened the door before Marisol knocked.

Their sister stood in the corridor lit by her borrowed lamp, and Bram's whole understanding of the world, which had been quietly reorganizing itself around grief and blackmail and forged timestamps for two weeks, stopped and reorganized again around a single fact: Marisol was ill. Not tired-ill. Not dust-cough-ill. Ill the way the women in the quarantine house were ill, ill the way the word cholera was ill, and she had walked six streets at night to bring—

"What is it?" Bram pulled her inside. Marisol's skin, when Bram's fingers found her wrist, was a furnace. "You're burning. How long?"

"Since Tuesday." Marisol tried to laugh and it became a cough, deep and wet, and Bram guided her onto the stool by the bench and held her there with both hands on her shoulders. "It's nothing. It's the cotton. Everyone has the cotton cough—"

"Don't." Bram's voice came out harder than they intended. "Don't do the arithmetic, Mari. Not tonight. I can't—" They stopped. Steadied. "What did you carry here? Because you didn't climb six streets for thread."

For a moment Marisol simply breathed, head bowed, and Bram watched her gather herself the way she used to gather herself before standing up in class to answer the question none of the boys would attempt.

Then she drew the paper from her bodice.

It was warm from her skin and creased into softness along folds it had worn for days, and Bram unfolded it on the bench beneath the lamp and read it, and read it again, and the workshop went very far away.

*Cordons—Passage Allocations. Berths reserved under Orphans' Relief Fund: 14. Allocated: 14.*

Names they knew. Families with carriages. And the trustee's mark at the bottom, coiled serpent, bell.

"He's selling them," Bram said. Their voice sounded strange to themselves, flat and distant, like sound carried across water. "The orphan berths. The ones the Relief Fund paid for, the ones that were supposed to be for—" For children with no parents, for the ones with no uncle to sign confessions and no sister to walk through fever-dark streets, for exactly the category of person the district had agreed, in its occasional fits of conscience, must be saved first. "He's selling them to the Ashworths."

"To whoever pays." Marisol's voice was hoarse but level, and there was that in it which Bram had heard only twice before—once when she was twelve and had stood between a mill girl and an overseer's strap without flinching. "I watched the counting-house four days before I could copy it clean. There'll be more pages, Bram. There'll be a price list somewhere, I'd stake my life on it, except—" the laugh again, broken, "except apparently I'm staking other things."

Bram looked at their sister properly then. The flush, the hollows, the bright burning eyes, the soaked collar. The rag still tucked in her cuff, brown-stained, that Bram had noticed days ago and chosen, in the family tradition, not to see.

"How long have you been hiding blood in that cloth?"

"Bram."

"*How long.*"

"A week." Marisol lifted her chin. "Maybe eight days. It doesn't matter—"

"It matters because in—" Bram did the calculation they had been refusing to do, the cordon arithmetic, the bell-count, "in seven days the district seals, and if you're marked, they take you to Blossom Street, and we both know what the mortality is in Blossom Street, and you have been walking past that knowledge for a week with a document sewn into your shirt like it was a love token—"

"It IS a weapon!" Marisol pushed herself upright, swaying, gripping the bench, and even shaking with fever her voice filled the little room like the master clock filling the observatory tower. "That's why I brought it. Listen to me, Bram, listen—Nettie won't testify unless the whole truth comes out, fine, the whole truth, our mother included, I've made my peace with that, Mama paid for medicine with other people's ruin and we'll say it out loud, all of it. But Nettie's testimony hangs Cray for the old crime, the strike informers, and old crimes are old, the magistrate will call it stale air. THIS—" her finger came down on the manifest, trembling but precise, striking the names one by one, "THIS is now. This is next week. Fourteen children locked behind the cordon so the Whitmores can summer safely in Liverpool. You want to destroy him? You want the magistrate to cross-check something? Cross-check THIS. Match these names against the Relief Fund subscriptions. Follow the money into his accounts. He forged timestamps—I know about the timestamps, don't look surprised, you talk in your sleep when you're puzzling at mechanisms—but a timestamp proves he lies. THIS proves what he lies FOR."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved to trim it.

And Bram, staring at the page, felt the monstrous shape of the week ahead assemble itself in full for the first time, all its parts visible at once, the way a movement laid open on blue paper shows every wheel and lever and spring. Two berths—Aldous had tickets hidden somewhere, Bram had known that much since the burning papers, had smelled the wax and said nothing—and a sister with fire in her blood, and seven days, and a barge, and a choice that had seemed, a month ago, like the worst question the world could ask.

Which one gets the berth. That had been the question. Aldous or Marisol, protection or future, the betrayer or the betrayed.

It was not the question anymore. It had never been the question. The question had always been larger and uglier: how many berths existed, who had bought them, and whether the Zhous would spend their one claim on escape—or on the fourteen children whose names had been sold out from under them, on Nettie Hargreaves and her dead comrades, on a truth that would lock every door in Manchester against anyone named Zhou for a generation.

Sacrifice, Marisol offered it so freely, holding out her fever like a coin. Take my strength, take my risk, take me. And Bram understood suddenly that they could not accept the offering, not one piece of it, not her courage spent at this exchange rate.

"Mari." They took her hands. They were shaking, hers; theirs, surprisingly, were not. "You're going to bed. Right now. You're going to drink everything I put in front of you, and tomorrow I'm sending for Dr. Latham whether you consent or not, and you're going to hate me, and I'm going to survive it. And this paper—" they touched the manifest, "this goes where it needs to go. Not through your hands. Never again through your hands. Do you hear me? Whatever happens with the berths, with Uncle, with Cray—you've done your part. Let me do mine."

Marisol looked at them for a long moment. Then, terribly, she smiled—the old crooked smile, the one that had gotten her through every beating and every hunger winter—and said, "You sound like Mama."

And then her knees gave.

Bram caught her before her head struck the bench, caught all ninety pounds of her burning like a struck match, lowered her to the floor with the manifest crumpled between them, and screamed down the stairwell for their uncle with a voice they did not recognize as their own.

* * *

Aldous came up the stairs two at a time for the first time in years, and what he found in the lamplight was Bram on the floor with Marisol's head in their lap and the fever standing in her cheeks like twin lamps, and beside them on the boards, fallen open, a page of figures that he recognized with a lurch older than shame—the trustee's mark, the coiled serpent he had paid in secret for eleven years, and beneath it, names he dined with, names whose wives wore his charity ribbon at Easter.

He knelt. He put his hand on Marisol's brow and it was like touching a kettle.

"Get Dr. Latham," Bram said, without looking up. "Now. And Uncle—" their voice dropped into something he had never heard from them, quiet as a escapement, final as a striking bell, "when you come back, we are going to talk about what else that seal has bought. All of it. Tonight."

Aldous rose. At the door he looked back once—at the girl on the floor, at the paper on the boards, at the clock on the mantel ticking toward midnight with perfect, merciless honesty—and went down into the dark to fetch the doctor, knowing as he went that the hour of arithmetic was over, and the hour of reckoning had struck.
