## 17. Which One Gets the Berth

The two tickets lay on the bench between the gear trays and the oil pot, and Bram could not stop looking at them.

They were small things—pasteboard, printed in a clerk's careful hand, each stamped with the magpie seal of the Canal & Navigation Company. *Berth One. Berth Two. Barge *Perseverance*, departing Ancoats Basin at six of the clock, Thursday morning.* Six of the clock. Nine hours from now, if the shop's regulator was honest, and Bram's clocks were always honest. It was the only honesty left to them.

Marisol slept on the cot in the back room, her breath coming in a thin, wet whistle that Bram heard through the partition wall as clearly as if she were lying against their shoulder. The fever had broken once in the night and come back before dawn like a tide that refused to be reasoned with. Her hands had been hot as a kettle when Bram had sponged them. Her lips were cracked. She had smiled anyway—smiled, and said, *Don't you dare look at me like I'm dying,* which was the most Marisol sentence ever spoken by a girl who might be.

And Aldous. Aldous had not come home.

He had signed Cray's confession—Bram knew it now, knew it the way one knows a clock is running slow, not from any single proof but from the accumulated drag of small wrongnesses: the way Aldous would not meet their eyes at supper, the way he had taken to wearing his good coat indoors, the way his counting had gone silent. For thirty years Aldous Zhou had counted coins aloud every night, a litany, a rosary of halfpence and shillings. Three nights ago the counting had stopped. That silence told Bram everything the tickets themselves confirmed.

Because the tickets were in Aldous's coat pocket, and Bram had found them there while looking for laudanum, and had stood in the dark of the shop holding them for so long that the mantel clock ticked off four hundred seconds without their noticing.

*Two berths.* Not one. Cray's generosity, purchased with a signature on a lie. And in the margin of the ticket stubs, in Aldous's cramped hand, three initials: **A.Z.** and **M.Z.** and then, scratched out so hard the pasteboard had nearly torn through, **B.**

Bram ran their thumb over the scratch.

Their uncle had meant it kindly. That was the terrible thing. Bram could see the whole arithmetic of it laid out like parts on a bench: Marisol sick, needing clean air; himself young and strong and clever enough—"can pass anywhere," Aldous had said to someone, once, thinking no one heard; and Aldous staying behind because an old man was an old man, and because guilt is its own anchor, and because some part of him perhaps believed he deserved whatever came. He had dressed the choice up as sacrifice. He had made it sound noble even inside his own skull. But the shape underneath was still arithmetic, and arithmetic was how the Zhous had always lied to themselves. Mother had done sums too: one name given to the mill owners, weighed against medicine for a dying child. The scales balanced perfectly. The scales balanced perfectly every single time, and the district had filled up with graves.

Bram set the tickets down and picked up a pair of pliers, then put them down again, because there was nothing on the bench that needed pliers, and they were only holding something so their hands would have an answer ready when their mind asked the question.

*Which one.*

Say it plainly, the way Cray would say it, the way the magistrate's clerks said it, the way the mill owners said it when they shipped daughters out ahead of the cordon: two berths, three lives, choose. Say it plainly and the answer should be easy. Marisol. Of course Marisol. She was seventeen, she was burning with fever, she was the best of all of them—the one who copied manifests while shaking, the one who refused to be counted among the sick because the counted got locked in, the one who had said *I won't leave if it means you stay marked.* Marisol was the obvious choice, and the obviousness of her frightened Bram more than anything else in this whole filthy season, because obvious choices were what Cray traded in. Cray built whole warehouses of respectability out of obvious choices. *Someone must go. Someone must stay. It cannot be helped.* Every cordon, every strike broken, every child smuggled or abandoned began with someone saying *it cannot be helped* in a reasonable voice.

And Aldous? Let Aldous stay? Let the man who had paid a blackmailer in secret for years, who had burned his own copy of the letter rather than tell them, who had planned to carry Marisol away and leave Bram standing alone on the towpath with a forged confession bearing the family name—let him take the fever's chances instead? Bram tried to hold that thought and found it curdled into something they did not want to be. Because Aldous had also taught them to file a tooth true. Had also gone hungry, visibly, deliberately, so the rent coin stayed whole. Had also wept at their mother's grave in a way he thought no one saw, both hands pressed flat to the earth as if taking its pulse.

Betrayal and love in the same hand. Nettie Hargreaves had said that of their mother. It was true of all of them. It was possibly true of Silas Cray, which was the thought that kept Bram awake—that evil wore a clean collar, and maybe under the collar there was a boy once, in Ancoats, doing sums.

No. That way lay paralysis, and there was no time for paralysis. The barge left at six.

Bram stood, took the lamp off the bench, and went through the partition.

* * *

Marisol was awake.

She was lying very still, the way she had learned to lie still on the sorting floor when the overlooker passed, and her eyes tracked Bram across the little room with a ferocity that fever could not dim.

"You found them," she said. Her voice was gravel and steam. "I heard you going through his coat."

"You should be asleep."

"I've been asleep for two days." She tried to push herself up on her elbows, failed, and glared at the failure as though it were a person she intended to ruin. "Give me a minute. I'll sit up."

Bram knelt and put a hand behind her shoulders and helped her, and felt the heat of her through the nightshirt and hated the heat, hated it with a specificity that had no words—hated the dust in her lungs from the sorting floor, hated the manifest she'd carried through the rain, hated the world that had asked a seventeen-year-old girl to buy truth with her own blood-flecked handkerchiefs.

"Two tickets," Marisol said, when she was propped against the wall. "So he took Cray's bargain after all."

"He thinks he's protecting us."

"He thinks he's protecting *you*. He's taking me because I'm cargo that can't argue." She coughed, turned her head, spat into the rag Bram held ready. There was pink in it. Neither of them remarked on the pink; they had an agreement about the pink, unspoken and absolute. When she could speak again she said, "You're going to give me your berth. I can see it settling on you like frost. Don't."

"Marisol—"

"Don't." Her hand shot out and closed around Bram's wrist with a grip that had no business being that strong. "Listen to me. I've had two days of lying here doing nothing but think, which is more thinking than I've done in my life and I never want to repeat it. If I get on that barge and you stay here, I will spend every mile of that canal knowing what my name costs. You'll be the informer's child. The papers will say Zhou, and everyone will read *Zhou* and think of the strike, of Nettie's people, of the men who went back to work with their caps in their hands. And I'll be safe somewhere with clean air, eating bread bought with that name, and I will choke on it. Do you understand me? I would rather die here honest than live there on credit."

"That's fever talking."

"It's *Zhou* talking," she said, and grinned, cracked lips and all, and Bram laughed despite everything—a short, broken sound—and then neither of them spoke for a moment, and through the window the district breathed its smoky breath, and somewhere far off the observatory clock ticked toward five bells of the afternoon watch, keeping time for a city that had stopped trusting time.

"I'm not choosing," Bram said at last.

Marisol frowned. "You have to."

"No." And here it was, the thing that had been assembling itself in Bram all night, gear by gear, the way a movement assembles in the pans of a lathe—not designed all at once but arriving, click, click, click, until suddenly it was a mechanism and it wanted only to run. "That's what he wants. That's what Cray has always wanted. Not any particular cruelty—just the choosing. Two berths, three lives, and whoever holds the tickets does his arithmetic for him. We do the cutting, and our hands are bloody, and his are clean, and next year he sells two more berths and lets someone else choose. I'm not doing his sums, Riss. I'm going to forge a third option."

"With what?" She gestured at the room—at the failing shop, the empty till, the city sealing itself around them like a fist closing. "You've got nine hours and two pasteboard tickets."

Bram looked down at the tickets, still lying where they'd left them on the cot's edge. Pasteboard. Printed ink. A magpie stamp.

"I've got a clock," they said slowly, "that strikes the hour for the whole district. I've got a magistrate who cross-checks delivery times against the observatory bell—I watched him do it Tuesday, with his own almanac open. I've got a manifest proving Cray sold orphan berths to rich families. And I've got—" their hand closed over Marisol's hot fingers "—a sister who knows exactly which warehouse office keeps the duplicate berth allocations, because she copied the shipping ledger that names it."

Understanding moved across Marisol's face like weather.

"You're going to break his books."

"I'm going to make the books and the bell disagree," Bram said. "Publicly. On cordon eve, in front of the magistrate, in front of witnesses. A man who sells quarantine berths forfeits them—that's statute, it's written right on the company's own notice board at the basin, I read it twice. If Cray falls tonight, his allocations don't sail with him. They revert. The magistrate releases them." Bram heard their own voice go quiet and strange, the voice they used over a movement that had finally revealed its fault. "Three lives, two tickets. So I'll make more tickets exist. That's the third option. Not choosing—whoever chose, Cray wins. Breaking the scale itself."

Marisol stared at them. Then, horribly, wonderfully, she began to laugh—a real laugh that turned into coughing that turned into laughter again, wheezing and triumphant, until Bram had to push her gently flat and hold the rag over her mouth.

"You're mad," she managed. "You're completely mad. He'll have men at the observatory."

"He'll have men everywhere. That's why the bell has to do the accusing, not me." Bram stood, already moving, already seeing the escapement in their mind's eye, the pallet stones, the precise alteration—one minute early, no more, a discrepancy small enough to hide and large enough to prove. An artisan's revenge. Patient. Mechanical. "Which means I need to go now, and it means you need to do something for me."

"Anything."

"Stay alive until six o'clock tomorrow. That's your whole task. Don't be brave, don't be clever, don't get up and follow me. Lie there and breathe and hate me if you like. Just keep breathing." Bram picked up the tickets and hesitated, then tucked them back into Aldous's coat pocket, exactly where they'd been found. If this failed, those two tickets were still the only mercy in reach, and Bram was not so pure-hearted as to burn mercy on principle. If this worked, they wouldn't be needed at all.

"And Aldous?" Marisol asked, from the pillow. Her eyes were closed now, fever pulling her back under, but her voice held. "When he comes home?"

Bram paused at the partition, the lamp low, the shop beyond full of ticking.

"When he comes home," Bram said, "he'll find out what his son is made of. And then we'll see whether love and betrayal can share a hand without one of them letting go."

Behind them, Marisol murmured something that might have been *go*, might have been *God*, and slid into a sleep that was too much like the other thing, and Bram took the tools they needed—a file, a jeweler's screwdriver, a scrap of brass—and let themselves down the stairs into the blue evening, toward the basin, toward the observatory hill, toward the hour that was going to learn how to tell the truth.

The master clock waited above the rooftops, patient as guilt, keeping perfect false time.

By morning, Bram meant to make it honest—or die winding it.
