## 18. Setting the Escapement

The observatory at night was a different animal than the observatory by day.

By day it belonged to the keeper, to his ledger and his cough and his careful evasions; by night it belonged to the clock itself—a presence that filled the round stone room the way water fills a well. Bram climbed the last of the spiral stairs with a shuttered lantern in one hand and a leather roll of tools against their ribs, and stood for a moment in the dark, listening.

*Tick.* A breath. *Tick.*

Most people heard a clock as one sound. Bram had never been able to. The master clock was an orchestra pretending to be a single instrument: the great driving weight descending through its channel with a whisper like wind in flax; the escapement's pallets kissing the teeth of the escape wheel—*tick*, then a smaller tick beneath it, the remontoire easing the strain on the train so the seconds would not stretch or shrink between winds; somewhere above, the strike train's fly fan turning in its slow idle, waiting for permission. Eleven separate voices, if you counted honestly. Twelve, counting the pendulum, which did not speak but breathed.

Bram had counted them twice already this week, once in daylight with the keeper hovering, once at dusk when they'd claimed a final "adjustment" and stayed past the hour when honest workmen went home. Tonight was the third time. The third listening was not for learning. It was for farewell—or as near to farewell as an artisan could come while the instrument still lived.

They set the lantern on the low ledge, shuttered to a coin of light no wider than a sixpence, and knelt before the open case.

Marisol slept at home with a wet cloth on her forehead and Aldous sitting up beside her, pretending to read a pamphlet about drainage. The manifest lay folded inside Bram's shirt, against the skin, where the ink had begun to ghost onto them from sweat and warmth: three pages in Marisol's cramped, feverish hand, proving what Cray had sold and to whom, dated and initialed by the shipping clerk who'd been too frightened to say no to a dying girl's stare. Tomorrow night—the cordon's eve—Cray would carry his forged papers to the magistrate's chambers and present his tidy account of berth allocations, every delivery timestamped by the only authority the district trusted without thinking.

The observatory bell.

That was the beauty of it, Bram thought, and the obscenity. Cray had not needed to forge timestamps himself. He had simply arranged, years ago, for the district's very definition of the hour to lean a minute in his favor—and a minute is nothing. A minute is the space between a knock and a door opening. No magistrate cross-checking documents would ever think to doubt the bell. You might as well doubt your own heartbeat.

But Bram had spent eleven days inside this mechanism, and they knew its heartbeat better than Cray ever could.

* * *

They began with the hands, because the hands were the lie everyone saw and therefore the first thing to make honest again.

The great minute hand had been fitted with a collet that rode a hair loose on its arbor—Bram had found it on the second day, a deliberate slackness that let the hand lag behind the true position of the train, so that even if someone checked the dial against another timepiece, the dial would show the old, corrupted minute. Cray's clock-falsifier had been clever. He had understood that you cannot simply stop a public clock and move it; the whole city would feel the stutter. Instead you introduce error the way a forger introduces age into paper—slowly, invisibly, in places no eye goes.

Bram unhooked the counterweight, eased the minute hand free of its arbor, and refitted the collet with a shim cut that afternoon from a scrap of hard brass, filed to half the thickness of a fingernail's edge and polished until it shone like a fish scale. Their mother had taught them this: any repair visible is a repair unfinished. *A joint should confess nothing,* she used to say, holding a mantel clock's mended case to the window light. *Let it look like it grew there.*

The thought of her still caught, sometimes, like a burr in wool. It would catch less, Bram suspected, after tomorrow—not because the grief would lift but because it would finally have a floor to stand on. Nettie Hargreaves had given them that floor: *the whole truth, or none of me.* And the whole truth meant the Zhous' name spoken aloud in the same breath as Cray's, their mother's desperation set beside her crime and neither excused nor hidden. Bram had agreed. They had agreed with their stomach clenched and their jaw aching, and they had not slept well since, and they did not regret it.

You could not set an escapement true while telling yourself a comfortable lie about some other wheel. That was the whole craft, really. That was maybe the whole life.

* * *

The escapement proper lived behind the great wheel, in the dark of the movement where only a hand as small and steady as Bram's could go.

They worked by touch more than sight, the lantern coin turned away so its glare wouldn't lie to them. The anchor escapement's pallets were steel-faced, jeweled at the points of contact—an extravagance for a public clock, and further proof that someone with money had commissioned this instrument to be tampered with rather than replaced. A new clock would have drawn questions. An *improved* clock drew praise.

Here was Cray's genius, laid bare under Bram's fingertips: the lifting face of the entry pallet had been re-cut, ground down by perhaps the width of two hairs, so that each swing of the pendulum released the escape wheel a fraction late. A fraction per tooth. Sixty fractions per minute, compounding into a full stolen minute every hour, absorbed and hidden because the pendulum—honest, swinging thing—was regulated to match. The clock ran perfectly by its own corrupted standard. It was a forgery all the way down, self-consistent, beautiful in its way.

To correct it outright—to file the pallet back to its true angle—would take a day's work and leave tool marks, and the keeper would notice within the week, and word would climb the ladder to Cray before the ink dried on the magistrate's disappointment.

So Bram was not going to correct it. Not yet. Not tonight.

Tonight they were going to do something far more delicate. They were going to let the clock keep lying—until one appointed minute tomorrow night, when it would tell the truth all at once, loudly, over the whole sleeping district.

They took out the pallet bridge. Two blued screws, warm from the movement's warmth, into the palm of a gloved hand. Beneath it, the pallet arm sat in its slot, and Bram had prepared for this the way they prepared everything: with a small paper envelope, folded and labeled in pencil, containing a wedge of hardened leather—yes, leather; their mother had used it for damping chime hammers, and it had the virtue of swelling slightly with humidity, which Manchester provided generously. The wedge, tanned thin and cut to a taper, would go under the pallet arm at the appointed point, lifting the entry pallet just enough that the escape wheel's tooth would release *early*—not a fraction late but nearly a full minute early, accumulated across sixty swings of the pendulum in the final hour before ten o'clock.

No. Not quite. Bram checked the arithmetic again, because arithmetic was the one friend who never betrayed you. The strike train released off the minute wheel at the top of the hour. If the going train began running fast during the tenth hour, the minute wheel would reach its trip position early, and the bell would begin striking before ten by however much the escapement had been hastened. One minute early required stealing fifty-nine seconds across three thousand six hundred seconds—one part in sixty-one. The wedge's taper had been calculated against the pallet's geometry three times, on paper, with the shop's good dividers, while Marisol dozed and Aldous pretended not to watch.

It would not be exact to the second. It did not need to be. Cray's forged delivery note placed the manifest in the magistrate's possession at ten o'clock precisely—"by the observatory bell," the note said, smugly, invoking its own alibi. The magistrate, prompted quietly by Nettie's grandchildren's employer—a man who owed Nettie a debt older than the strike—would have in his pocket the shipping office's own recorded time of the same delivery, taken from a watch that kept Greenwich time. A gap of even forty seconds, witnessed, documented, undeniable, would unravel him. A gap of exactly one minute, struck publicly by the bell itself at the moment Cray stood swearing he had delivered at ten sharp, would be a confession with a bronze tongue.

And here was the part Bram loved, in a cold and secret place they were not proud of: the discrepancy would point backward, too. Because once the bell was proven to have lied at ten o'clock, every timestamp Cray had ever certified against it became suspect—including the ones from 1826. The informer reports. The weavers' names. The whole architecture of his respectability rested on a clock, and Bram held its pulse.

They seated the wedge with a pair of tweezers, breathing through their mouth the way their mother had taught them—you breathe through your mouth when your hands are working, because the chest must be still—and drew the pallet bridge back down over it. The screws turned home with the soft authority of well-cut threads.

Then the release mechanism, which was the heart of the whole design and the part Bram had lost sleep over.

A permanent alteration would be discovered. So the wedge needed to be *temporary*—in place for the tenth hour tomorrow, gone by morning, and the evidence of its having been there indistinguishable from ordinary wear. Bram had solved it with thread and wax. A length of fine silk, waxed, ran from the wedge's tail end up through a drilled passage they had made two nights ago in a corner of the frame where no inspector's eye would fall, and out through a hairline gap in the case's rear board, coiled now behind a loose panel that Bram alone knew opened. At the appointed moment—no. Not Bram's hand. Bram would need to be elsewhere, visibly elsewhere, when the bell spoke. The thread's far end would be tied inside the belfry stair to the latch of the ventilator door, which the keeper opened every night at half past nine for air, without fail, without looking, his ritual as fixed as the clock's own. The door's swing, gentle as a sigh, would draw the thread, draw the wedge, and restore the pallet to its corrupt geometry—so that by the time anyone investigated, the escapement would be exactly as Cray's man had left it.

The bell would have told the truth once, at the only moment it mattered, and then returned to its long captivity. Like a witness who testifies and goes home.

Bram threaded the silk with fingers that shook only a little, and tied the knot they had practiced forty times on the shop bench—a slipped hitch that would hold under tension and pay out clean, leaving no mark on the wedge, no fray on the thread.

* * *

They sat back on their heels afterward and let the silence of the tower settle over them, and found that their eyes had gone hot.

Not from the work. The work was good. The work was the best work of their life and they knew it, the way you know a joint has closed true without testing it. But kneeling there in the coin of lantern-light, surrounded by gears that had been made to lie by a man who fed orphans at Christmas, Bram felt the full weight of what a small thing a minute is, and how much it can hold. A minute had decided whether the barge's gangplank came up. A minute had decided, in 1826, whether a mill gate locked before or after a constable arrived with names in his pocket. Their mother's name. Somewhere in a clerk's ledger, the Zhous' betrayal probably occupied a line item no longer than the wedge now resting under the pallet arm.

*We were priced in minutes,* Bram thought. *All of us. The whole district.*

The pendulum swung its patient arc, indifferent, faithful to whatever geometry it was given. That was the terrible honesty of mechanisms: they could not choose. Give them a lie and they would keep it forever, faithfully, until a human hand intervened. Everything depended on the hand.

Bram closed the case, hooked the hasps, and wiped every surface they had touched with a rag dampened in spirits, taking up the dust of their own working so the case would show nothing but the keeper's own neglect. They checked the thread's run one last time with a fingertip, tracing it blind through its passage, feeling it lie quiet as a spider's line.

Then, because they were their mother's child and their mother's child checked everything, they took out the shop's good watch—the dead father's watch, silver-cased, keeping time against the stars themselves these past eleven days—and held it beside the dial. Ten minutes to midnight. The master clock's hands showed nine minutes and fourteen seconds to midnight, lagging, lying, faithful to its corruption.

Tomorrow night at this same hour, the difference would be reversed. Tomorrow night the great bell over Ancoats would cry out one full minute before the world expected it, and Silas Cray—magistrate's clerk, charity patron, beloved benefactor, wearer of clean collars—would hear his own conspiracy tolling over his head like a judgment.

Bram shuttered the lantern, shouldered the tool roll, and paused at the head of the stairs with one hand flat against the cold stone, the way you'd steady a horse you were about to send into traffic.

"Hold true till then," they whispered—absurd, talking to a machine—and went down into the dark, out past the keeper's sleeping lamp, into streets that smelled of river and coal smoke and the coming quarantine.

At home, a candle still burned in the front window. Marisol's fever had climbed through the evening; Aldous had sent the neighbor boy running for the surgeon at nine, and the boy had come back alone to say the surgeon would come in the morning, *if* the roads were still open, *if* the cordon hadn't started early, and Bram stood in the doorway reading all of that in the candle's behavior—the way it guttered, the way Aldous's shadow crossed it twice and stopped—and understood with perfect clarity that tomorrow night had just become the only night there was.

Inside, Marisol was coughing in a register Bram had never heard from her, deep and wet and wrong.

The wedge was set. The thread was run. Now everything else—Nettie, the magistrate, the manifest, the bell—had to happen on time.

For the first time in their life, Bram Zhou prayed to no one in particular that the clock would keep its promise.
