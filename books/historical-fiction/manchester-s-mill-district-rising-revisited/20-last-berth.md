## 20. Last Berth

The tickets were heavier than paper had any right to be.

Bram held the three of them in one palm as they stood in the magistrate's anteroom on Cordon Eve, watching the light through the high window turn them from cream to gold. Thick card. Printed borders. A red stamp on each that read *Passage Guaranteed — Rochdale Canal — Departure at Dawn*. Somewhere beyond the district's rooftops a barge lay loaded with water and bread and the promise of clean air, and these three small rectangles were the only keys to it left in all of Ancoats.

Three days ago there had been dozens. Cray's ledger — the true one, pried out of his warehouse office by men who did not ask Bram's permission first — had recorded forty-one berths sold to families whose names appeared nowhere on any orphan list. Forty-one children's places, bought and paid for while the actual orphans waited behind cordons with their names inked in a different book, a book no one intended to open.

Now the tickets sat in Bram's hand like small hot coins.

"You needn't decide this instant," the magistrate's clerk said. She was a tired woman with ink-stained cuffs and the particular patience of someone who had spent three days listening to grown men lie about paperwork. "But the barge will not wait past dawn. And after dawn, Mr. Zhou—" she paused, corrected herself with a small bow of the head, "—*Mx.* Zhou — after dawn these are only cardstock."

Bram nodded. The title still caught in people's throats; it always had. Their mother had taught them early that a name was a thing you carried like a tool — useful, sometimes heavy, occasionally dangerous if swung carelessly. Bram carried theirs the way they carried their smallest screwdriver: close, familiar, unremarkable to anyone who didn't know its worth.

"I'll decide before I leave this room," Bram said.

Outside, the bells were wrong.

That was the thought that kept surfacing, absurdly, as Bram stood there turning the tickets over. All day the district had been ringing itself hoarse — not the quarantine pattern, which everyone knew now, nine strokes then silence, but something ragged and continuous, church after church, as though the whole city were trying to say a word too large for any single bell. Cray's arrest had gone through the streets faster than cholera rumor. *They've taken Silas Cray. They've taken the charity man. The magistrate himself came to the warehouse with soldiers.*

Bram had watched it happen from the observatory steps, hands still black with clock oil, and felt nothing so simple as triumph. What they had felt was closer to what they felt when an old mechanism finally turned over after years seized: relief, yes, and underneath it the grief of everything the seizure had cost while it lasted.

Nettie Hargreaves was waiting for Bram when they came out of the magistrate's office into the grey churn of Angel Meadow. The old weaver sat on the low wall where she always sat, shawl pulled tight despite the warmth, her grandchildren clustered around her like iron filings around a magnet — two boys and a girl, the eldest perhaps ten, all of them with her same stubborn chin.

"They're saying you broke him," Nettie said, without preamble. Her voice was rougher than usual; she had shouted herself hoarse yesterday outside the warehouse, Bram had heard it even through closed windows. "The clerk's boy says the bell did it. Says the observatory struck the quarter-hour four minutes before the churches, and the magistrate stood up mid-sentence and asked Cray to explain his timestamps." She looked up, and her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "Four minutes. All them years of lies, undone by four minutes."

"By the truth arriving on time," Bram said. "Or slightly before it."

Nettie snorted. Then, quieter: "You let them name me. In the square. You could've let the Zhous take the whole weight and you didn't."

"No," Bram agreed. "You wouldn't have permitted it."

"No," she said, and something passed between them that was not forgiveness exactly — Bram suspected neither of them would ever fully forgive, and had come to understand that this was not required — but was perhaps recognition. Two people who had both refused the smaller, kinder lie.

Her granddaughter tugged her sleeve and whispered, and Nettie waved the child off impatiently, but Bram saw how her eyes followed the girl. Saw how the whole huddle of them leaned against her like a wall leaning on a foundation stone.

"How many of you are there?" Bram heard themselves ask.

Nettie's jaw tightened. "What's that to you?"

"The barge leaves at dawn. There are places."

"We've no money for—"

"There's no charge." Bram opened their hand. The three tickets lay in the afternoon light, and Nettie stared at them the way a starving woman stares at bread — wanting, disbelieving, afraid of the trap she couldn't yet see.

"Whose are those?" she said slowly. "Whose berth was that meant to be?"

And there it was. The question under every question since the letter. Bram had spent weeks imagining this moment, rehearsing answers in the dark above the chandler's shop while Marisol coughed and Aldous counted coins that would never cover rent. *My mother's. My uncle's. My sister's. Mine.* Every answer had a different flavor of guilt, and Bram had tasted them all.

"It doesn't matter whose it was meant to be," Bram said. "It matters where it goes now."

* * *

They walked home through streets that had gone strange with hope. People stood in doorways talking instead of hurrying past. Someone had chalked *CRAY IS CAUGHT* across the mill gate in letters a yard tall, and beneath it someone else had added, in a smaller hand, *and God is not asleep*, and beneath *that* some wit had written *but he does keep irregular hours*. Bram found themselves laughing — a cracked, unexpected sound that startled a passing coal cart horse.

The shop smelled of oil and cold tea. Aldous was at the bench, doing nothing, which for Aldous was the loudest confession there was. He'd signed Cray's false confession five days ago; he'd pocketed two berth tickets and told himself stories about necessity; and then the arrest had come and burned every story he owned. Now he sat with his hands flat on the wood like a schoolboy caught without his slate.

"Bram." His voice came out sanded down. "I've been trying to think what to say to you since yesterday and I've got nothing. Nothing that isn't filth."

"Don't say anything yet." Bram set the remaining ticket — they had given one already? No. Not yet. They had promised, but the giving hadn't happened; Bram checked their pocket again out of habit, all three still there — set the tickets on the bench between them. "I need to tell you what I'm doing with these. So you hear it from me and not from the street."

Aldous looked at the tickets and his face did something complicated — hunger and shame fighting openly, neither winning. "Marisol can't travel. Doctor said it plain this morning, the fever's broken but her lungs—" He stopped. Started again. "She shouldn't go. And I'm not leaving her, and you—" His voice failed on the second *you*.

"I'm not going," Bram said. It came out steady, which surprised them, because inside they had only decided it in the last hour, standing over Nettie's grandchildren, feeling the decision settle into place like a pallet into its bearings. "I'm staying. Through the cordon. Someone has to keep the shop, and someone has to tend her, and—" they almost said *someone has to watch the clock*, because the observatory keeper had already asked, half-ashamed, whether Bram might stay on through the quarantine months to maintain the master mechanism now that its honesty mattered to the whole district's courts and calendars — "and I want to. That's the part you won't believe. I'm choosing it."

Aldous stared. "You'd give away your own—"

"I'd give away anyone's. That's rather the point." Bram picked up the tickets. "Listen. Nettie Hargreaves has three grandchildren and one room and a name that's been mud since 1826 because she was the only weaver in Ancoats willing to speak plainly. She gets two tickets — the girl and the younger boy. The eldest stays with her; he's fourteen and he'll pass as a laborer when the cordons loosen, and he refuses to leave her anyway. She fought me on taking even two. She made me promise the third went to someone worse off than her, and I intend to honor that."

"And the third?"

"There's a woman on the sorting floor — Marisol knows her — Widow Okonkwo, came up from the docks with twin girls, six years old, no papers, no kin, no name on any list because lists require that a person once existed somewhere official. Marisol's been slipping the girls bread for a month." Bram's throat tightened. "Marisol told me about her yesterday, between coughs, and asked — she *asked*, Aldous, my sister who hasn't asked anyone for anything since our mother died — she asked if there might be any way at all."

Aldous put his head in his hands. For a long moment the only sound was the shop's one honest clock ticking on the mantel — the widow's clock, the one Bram had repaired for free in the first chapter of all this, still keeping perfect time as if it had known something.

"I planned to take you," Aldous said finally, to his palms. "That night. The two berths. I told myself you could pass anywhere, that you'd land soft, that you were the one of us the world was built to tolerate. Do you know what that is, Bram? That's arithmetic wearing love's coat. I dressed cruelty up as strategy and called it protection, and if Cray hadn't fallen first I'd have done it and hated myself properly only afterward, in America, where self-hatred is comfortable."

"I know," Bram said.

"You know?"

"Uncle. I've been reading ledgers since I was eight. I read yours long ago." Bram touched the tickets. "I'm not giving these away because I'm good. I'm giving them away because I finally understand what our mother understood too late — that a thing hoarded rots, and a thing passed on lives. She hoarded the truth of what she'd done and it poisoned everything she touched. Cray hoarded berths and bought children's places like furniture. The only inheritance worth carrying out of this family is the choice itself. The moment where you hold the thing everyone wants and decide who breathes next year and who doesn't. Mother never let me make that choice — she made it all for us, silently, and died with it. You tried to make it for me too. So." Bram slid two tickets toward Aldous. "Take these to Nettie. Tell her they come from the family that informed on her, and that she should spit on the envelope if it makes the gift sit easier in her stomach. Tell her the debt isn't paid — debts like that don't get paid, only acknowledged. But tell her it's acknowledged."

Aldous took the tickets with both hands, the way one takes communion or a live coal. When he spoke his voice was wrecked and lighter than it had been in twenty years. "And the third?"

"I'll take it to Widow Okonkwo myself. Tonight. Before the cordon bells start."

* * *

The walk to the widow's room near the canal basin was the longest short distance of Bram's life.

The district was sealing itself even as they moved through it. Soldiers at the corner of Great Ancoats Street checking passes. Families loading handcarts, though there was nowhere within the lines to load them *to*. The smell of lime wash everywhere, whitewash going up on cellar doors as the sanitary men marked houses. And over it all the bells, still wrong, still saying their enormous unfinished word.

Widow Okonkwo opened her door before Bram knocked — a tall woman with grey threading her braids and the watchful stillness of someone who had survived things that taught her to read footsteps. Behind her, in the single candlelit room, two small girls slept curled together in one bed like commas in a sentence not yet finished.

"You're the clockmaker," she said quietly. "Mari's brother."

"Sister and brother," Bram said. "Both, depending on the day and the weather."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — the ghost of a smile, rationed like everything else in the room. "She talks of you. Between the coughing. She pretends she doesn't, but she does." Her eyes dropped to Bram's hand, to the ticket visible between their fingers, and the watchfulness sharpened into something wary and terrible. "If you've come to sell me passage, I have sixpence and a ring that was my mother's."

"I've come to give it to you." Bram held it out. "No charge. No condition. It's yours and the girls'. The barge leaves at dawn from the basin — the master there has your names already. Marisol sent them ahead."

The widow did not move. "Nothing in this city is free. I learned that in my first week off the boat."

"This isn't the city giving it. It's me." Bram heard their own voice steadiness itself around the words. "My family owes this district more than one ticket can cover. This isn't payment. It's a beginning of acknowledging the account exists."

For a long moment the widow studied Bram's face, searching it the way Bram searched a movement for falseness — looking for the escapement, the trick, the place where the mechanism lied. Whatever she found, or didn't find, she reached out and took the ticket with a hand that trembled only once.

"Why?" she said. "Truly. Why give away your own berth? Everyone runs. Running is the whole of wisdom right now."

Bram looked past her, at the sleeping twins, at the candle flame bending in the draft from the ill-fitted window, and found the answer arrived whole, as if it had been waiting in them for years the way a letter waits sealed in a clock case.

"Because someone stayed for my mother once, when running would have been wiser, and I am alive because of it. Because my sister gave me her courage when she could barely stand, and I'd dishonor it by spending it on escape. And because—" the words came slower now, harder-won, "—because I repaired the clock that exposed Cray, and I learned what it taught me. A clock doesn't keep time for itself. It keeps it for everyone in the room. That's its whole purpose, and it can't do it halfway. I think maybe people are built the same."

The widow pressed the ticket to her chest. Then she did something Bram did not expect: she stepped aside and gestured them in, and woke the twins gently, and made them say thank-you properly, solemnly, two small sleep-heavy voices in the candlelight. And when Bram rose to go she caught their hand in both of hers — strong hands, dockworker's hands — and said, "When the cordon lifts, you'll come to dinner. That is not a request. The dead are owed remembrance and the living are owed bread, and you'll have both at my table."

* * *

Dawn came grey and windless over the canal basin.

Bram stood on the towpath with Aldous and watched the barge take on its passengers. Nettie's grandchildren went up the plank first, the girl clutching a bundle of her grandmother's weaving samples because Nettie had insisted the craft travel even if she couldn't. Then the Okonkwo twins, wide-eyed, waving until their mother gently turned them forward. The widows and the orphans and the ones whose names had finally been read aloud from the right book — Bram watched them go and felt the strangest sensation, like setting a mechanism running after repair: the tension released, the wheels engaged, the thing moving off under its own power toward a future Bram would never see the inside of.

Nettie herself stood beside them on the bank, arms crossed, refusing tears. "I told you I'd only speak the full truth," she said. "You got it. Ugly middle included."

"The ugly middle is the only part that was ever real."

"Hm." She bumped her shoulder against Bram's, briefly, roughly, the weaver's equivalent of an embrace. "Keep that clock of yours honest, Zhou. This town's had enough lying hours to last a century."

The barge pulled away into the mist. Aldous put his arm around Bram's shoulders — awkward, unpracticed, the gesture of a man relearning a language — and together they stood until the horn sounded twice and faded and the water closed flat and silver over the wake.

Then, from the direction of the town, the first cordon bell began to toll.

Nine strokes. Silence. Nine strokes.

The gates were closing. The long months were starting. Bram turned back toward the district, toward the shop, toward the barred window of the quarantine house where Marisol waited with her fever and her ferocity and the manifest copied in a hand that shook — and found, beneath the fear, a strange and stubborn steadiness, like a well-set escapement.

Whatever came through those gates, they would measure it honestly, minute by minute, and they would not look away.

The bell tolled on, and Bram walked toward it.
