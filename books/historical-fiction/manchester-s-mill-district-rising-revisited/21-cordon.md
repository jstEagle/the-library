## 21. Cordon

The quarantine house had been a warehouse once, before the cordon—somebody's fortune in baled cotton stacked to the rafters, somebody's ledger of profits tallied on the ground floor where the desks still stood with their drawers half open, as if the owners had fled mid-arithmetic. Now the cotton bales were gone and the iron bedframes had come instead, row upon row of them down the long dim floor, and Marisol lay in the third row from the window and counted them the way she used to count hanks of raw cotton: by tens, by fifties, because counting kept a mind from wandering into places it could not come back from.

Forty-one beds. Thirty-eight occupied. Three empty, and she knew what the empties meant, because every morning the orderlies came with a canvas litter and took someone out through the far door, and no one ever came back through it.

She was not going to think about the far door.

*You are not counted among the sick until they say you are,* she told herself, except that was a lie now, wasn't it. She was counted. Her name was written in the admission book in a clerk's careful hand—*Marisol Zhou, aged sixteen, sorter, Ancoats Mill No. 3*—and her clothes were boiled and her hair was cropped short to keep the lice and fever from having anywhere to live, and when she pressed her palm flat against her own sternum she could feel the heat coming off her chest like a loaf drawn too soon from the oven. The blood-flecked cloth she had hidden for three weeks in the sorting floor's privy wall—the cloth she had sworn to herself was nothing, dust, a scratch—had turned out to be exactly what it looked like.

The fever did strange things with time. It folded hours like cloth, so that the morning bell and the evening bell seemed to ring within minutes of each other, and the orderly who brought the gruel might be the same orderly or a different one or the same one wearing a different face. Sometimes she woke and could not remember whether she had dreamed Bram's face at the window or whether Bram had actually been there, standing in the yard below with their satchel of tools slung across their back like a soldier's pack, hands raised toward the barred glass.

Today, though. Today the light through the window had the particular slant of late afternoon, amber and low, and she could hear the district beyond the wall: carts, a dog, the distant grinding complaint of a mill that was still running, because cotton did not stop for cholera. And under all of it, faint but unmistakable, the tick of some clock keeping honest time. She thought of the observatory on its hill. She thought of Bram up there with the master clock's heart open in their hands.

Someone had told her—Nettie Hargreaves herself, shouting it through the fence on visiting day before the matron shooed her off—that Cray was finished. That the bell had struck early and lied him to ruin in front of the magistrate and half the respectable city. That his name would be struck from the charity boards and spoken in the papers with the word *fraud* nailed to it like a bill of sale. Marisol had held that news against her fever like a coal held in bare hands, warming herself with it hour after hour. Whatever else happened behind the far door, she had helped do that. The manifest she had copied with shaking, burning fingers—her handwriting gone crooked as a drunkard's by the last page—had mattered. She had mattered.

But news was thin soup, and it ran out fast, and the fever always came back for her in the dark.

* * *

The dark came at six o'clock, when the lamplighter's pole tapped along the street outside and the ward grew dim except for one oil lamp per row. In the dark the fever spoke. Not words, exactly—it was never quite words—but shapes and pressures, a voice made of heat that told her things in her mother's cadence, in Aldous's mutter, in her own voice from years ago. It told her the beds were boats. It told her the far door opened onto water. Once it told her, very clearly and kindly, that she had already died three days ago and nobody had wanted to be the one to tell her, which was why everyone was being so gentle, and she had lain rigid until dawn refusing to believe it, because if there was one thing the Zhous were good at it was knowing when a story was forged.

On the fifth night—it might have been the fifth; the tally marks she scratched inside the bed frame said five since the cordon sealed—she heard Bram's voice.

Not the fever's version of it. The real one. Low, a little rough from disuse, carrying that particular flatness Bram got when they were frightened and refusing to show it, the same voice they'd used over their bench when a mainspring snapped near their face. Marisol turned her head on the pillow, and there they were: outside the window, standing in the frozen mud of the yard, breath smoking in the cold, one gloved hand raised against the glass.

The night orderly—a heavy woman named Mrs. Okonkwo who had lost none of her Liverpool bluntness to the epidemic—looked from Bram to Marisol and back again. "That one," she said. "Every night this week. I told them the rules. They said they'd wait till the rules got tired." Something in Mrs. Okonkwo's face suggested that the rules were, in fact, getting tired, and that she had decided not to notice certain small deliveries left at the base of the wall: oranges, a jar of beef tea, a twist of paper containing willow bark and quinine that Marisol was not supposed to have and swallowed anyway.

"Five minutes," Mrs. Okonkwo said now, to no one in particular, and walked away down the row with the lamp, leaving the ward in blue darkness and Marisol alone with the window and her brother.

Marisol got up. It took longer than it should have—the floor tilted, the beds swam—and she had to grip two bedframes on the way, her nightgown hanging off her like a sail with the wind gone out of it. But she made it. She put her hand flat against the cold pane, opposite Bram's glove, and the glass between them was so thin and so vast all at once, a quarter inch of everything that separated the counted sick from the uncounted well.

"You look terrible," Bram said through the glass, muffled.

"You should see the other side of this window," Marisol rasped. Her voice was a stranger's. "I'm winning."

Bram's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling—one corner lifting, the rest of the face refusing to commit. They pulled their glove off and set their bare palm against the pane. Their fingers were long and stained faintly with oil, the way they always were, the way they had been since they were nine years old and took apart the mantel clock and put it back together better than it had ever run. Through the glass, Marisol could feel almost nothing—just the ghost of warmth, the suggestion of pressure. It was enough. It was more than she'd had all week.

"I brought something," Bram said. "It's not medicine. The matron confiscated the actual medicine, so this is—" They stopped. Started again. Marisol watched them struggle the way she'd watched them struggle over the letter from the clock case, over Nettie's terms, over the tickets: Bram fighting language itself, trying to build a sentence that would hold the weight of what needed saying without collapsing.

"It's names," Bram finally said. "I've been coming every night to read them to you. You've been asleep most nights. Mrs. Okonkwo says sometimes people hear things even when they're asleep, so I keep reading anyway." They reached into their coat and drew out a folded paper, creased soft as cloth from much handling. "They're restoring the memorial. The 1826 one. All the weavers' names are going back on it—all of them, including the ones Cray's reports erased, including—" a breath, held and released— "including Mother's name. On the other side of it. Both sides. Nettie insisted. No partial truth carved in stone."

Marisol closed her eyes. Behind them the fever shifted, restless, but she held onto the sound of Bram's voice like a rail in fog.

"Read them," she said. "All of them. I want to count."

* * *

So Bram read.

They stood in the freezing yard with their coat buttoned wrong and their breath pluming, and they read the names of dead weavers through a barred window to a dying—or not dying, she refused the far door, she *refused* it—girl, and the names went up like sparks and hung in the dark above the quarantine house.

"Hannah Ogden. Samuel Ogden, her son, aged fourteen. Thomas Whitlow. Esther Whitlow. Joseph Nadin—" Bram stumbled on that one, and Marisol knew why: Nadin had been the constable who rode against the crowd, whose name had been spat in the district for twenty years, and yet here he was on the restored stone too, because the truth did not sort itself into comfortable piles. "Reuben Farrow. Dinah Farrow and child. William Crawshaw. Martha Crawshaw—"

"Keep going," Marisol whispered, her palm still on the glass.

"Alice Barlow. James Barlow. Peterloo names first, then the strike names, then—" Bram's voice caught, steadied. "Then the ones who informed. Judith Zhou. Written plain, no title, no shame added and none subtracted. Just her name, where the truth can see it."

Outside, somewhere past the wall, the observatory bell began to toll the hour. Marisol counted the strokes out of habit, the sorter's habit, everything in tens—and heard, beneath the bronze, a rhythm that ran true. Even. Honest. A clock with no lie in its escapement, striking exactly when it meant to, neither early nor late, because her sibling had rebuilt its heart and given it nothing but accuracy to speak.

"That's your work," she said, when the last stroke faded. "I know your work. It sounds like you."

Bram pressed their forehead briefly against the glass. Up close Marisol could see how worn they were—hollows under the eyes, a cut healing on their knuckle, the particular exhaustion of someone who has been holding a whole family's weight on a spine not built for it and has decided, stubbornly, to go on holding. "It runs true now," Bram said. "That's all I ever wanted anything to do. To run true."

"The fever talks to me," Marisol said. She hadn't meant to say it. It came out the way blood comes from a nose—sudden, unstoppable. "At night. It tells me things. It uses Mother's voice." Her throat closed. "And I hate that I miss it. Even the false version. Even the fever's forgery of her."

Bram was quiet a moment. When they spoke, their breath fogged the pane between them, and for an instant their faces overlapped, theirs and hers, ghost on glass. "Mother's name is on the memorial," Bram said. "Both sides of it. That's more true than any voice in your head. When the cordon lifts—if it lifts—you and I will walk up to Ancoats churchyard and read it together, standing up, in the cold, and it'll be real in a way nothing in this room is real. That's the appointment. You have to keep it. You don't get to leave me standing alone in a churchyard talking to a rock."

"That's the worst invitation I've ever received."

"It's the only one I've got."

Marisol laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing bent her double, and through it she heard Bram's hand hit the glass and Mrs. Okonkwo's boots coming back down the row, and she waved them both off—one hand for each of them—because she was not done. She straightened. She put her hand back on the pane.

"Bram. Listen. If the far door—"

"No."

"If it does—"

"*No.*" The word cracked against the glass. Then, quieter, wrecked: "Don't. Don't make me stand out here and listen to you write my eulogy through a window. I spent two years not saying goodbye to Mother because she went in the night and nobody woke me. I'm not practicing for another one. You want to fight the fever? Fine. Fight it angry. Fight it bored. Fight it the way you sorted cotton—like it owes you money. But you're not doing it with me standing here agreeing to lose."

Marisol looked at them—at this sibling who had stayed behind so she could leave, who had then torn up the arithmetic entirely and given away every berth including their own, who had beaten a magistrate's clerk with nothing but a clock's honesty and a refusal to choose between love and truth—and felt the fever recede a little, the way a tide hesitates, not defeated, just reconsidering.

"All right," she said. "Angry and bored. Like it owes me money."

"And eat the oranges."

"There were oranges?"

"There were four oranges over the course of the week. Mrs. Okonkwo ate one. She says it's her fee."

Behind them, down the row, a man in bed nineteen began to moan, and an oil lamp guttered, and the cold deepened the way February cold does—not colder exactly, just more certain of itself. Bram gathered their coat around them and stepped back from the window, and Marisol felt the loss of their palm's ghost-warmth immediately, a small bereavement.

"Tomorrow night," Bram said. "More names. There's a hundred and forty. We're at sixty-two."

"Sixty-two," Marisol repeated, and filed it where the fever couldn't reach: in the counting place, the sorting place, the deep clean drawer of her mind where numbers stayed true no matter what burned around them.

She watched Bram cross the yard, small in the lamplight, and pause at the gate, and look back. She raised her hand. They raised theirs. Then the gate, and the lane, and the dark, and Marisol walked back down the row on legs that shook but carried her, and lay down in bed thirty-four of forty-one, and slept without dreams until nearly dawn—which was the first night since the cordon that the fever had failed to find her, and she chose to believe, in the stubborn arithmetic of her heart, that it was because there was no room left in her head. It was full of names.

* * *

Morning brought the doctor on his rounds, a tired man with ink-stained cuffs who listened to her chest with a stethoscope older than she was, and frowned, and frowned again, and wrote something in his book.

"Interesting," he said.

"Don't say interesting," Marisol croaked. "Doctors say interesting about corpses."

He almost smiled. "Your fever's broken twice in the night and climbed again toward morning. That's not the usual pattern." He tapped her chart. "Mostly it climbs and stays climbed, or it breaks and stays broken. Yours is arguing with itself." He looked at her over the spectacles. "Whatever you're doing in here, keep doing it. There's a woman in Manchester General who swears she survived typhus on spite and beef tea, and I used to think she was joking."

"Oranges," said Mrs. Okonkwo, passing with her linen cart. "And visitors who won't take no for an answer."

After the doctor moved on, Marisol lay looking at the ceiling, where the light of the new day crawled slow across the beams, and did her sums. Fever broken twice. Strength in her legs this morning—unsteady, but present, hers. Sixty-two names read, seventy-eight to go. Somewhere outside the wall a cart rattled past, and she caught the smell of bread from the relief kitchen, real bread, and her stomach growled for the first time in days with ordinary, vulgar, living hunger.

Three empty beds this morning, and one of them had been nineteen, where the moaning man had been. But hers was still occupied. She was still in it. And tonight, when the lamplighter's pole came tapping, Bram would be at the window with their folded paper and their wrong-buttoned coat, reading the dead back onto the earth one name at a time, and she intended—with everything in her, with the fury and the boredom and the greed of the living—to be sitting up to hear it.

She scratched a sixth tally mark inside the bed frame. Then, after a moment, she added a small letter beside it: *B*, for the names, for the bell, for the boy—or not a boy, Bram had never been a boy or a girl, just Bram, just theirs—who stood in frozen mud every night because love was the only thing small enough to fit through the bars.

Seventy-eight names to go. She meant to hear every one.

Down the row, near the far door, an orderly paused at bed twelve and picked up a chart, and Marisol's eyes followed the white apron the length of the ward—and stopped, because the orderly was not heading for the far door at all. She was coming down Marisol's row, chart in hand, with the doctor's ink-stained cuffs just visible behind her, and she was smiling.
