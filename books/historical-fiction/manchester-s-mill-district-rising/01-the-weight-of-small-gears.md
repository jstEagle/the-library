## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The workshop smelled of oil and iron and the ghost of last night's coal fire, which Master Fenwick had banked rather than quenched, as he did every night in winter, because a cold lathe took an hour to trust its hands again. Marguerite Eastwick arrived before the light did — that was the rule, unspoken but absolute — and let themselves in with the key they were permitted to carry but never to copy, and stood a moment in the doorway while their eyes adjusted to the dark.

Not true dark. Never quite. The window over the workbench faced east across Ancoats, and even before dawn the mill district gave off a glow of its own: gas flares at the gates, furnace-light through tall windows, the orange smear of a town that had forgotten how to sleep. It came through the glass like something drowned, pale and wavering, and it was enough to see by. It had to be. Candles cost money, and Fenwick counted every farthing twice.

Marguerite hung their coat on the peg behind the door, tied on the leather apron that had been theirs for three years now and still bore, faintly, the shape of the shoulders it had been cut down from — a dead man's apron, though nobody said so, and Marguerite had learned early not to ask whose shoulders those had been. They lit the lamp over the bench nearest the window because that bench was theirs, had been since their second year, and set out the day's first task where it waited under a square of oiled cloth: the overseer's watch.

It belonged to a man named Pratt — Mr. Josiah Pratt, overseer of the spinning floor at Harrow Mills, third bell to fifth bell, six days of seven — and it had come into the workshop two days ago wrapped in a handkerchief that smelled of machine grease and peppermint. *Runs slow,* the note had said, in a clerk's careful copperplate. *Loses time. Wants putting right before quarter day.* And beneath that, after a space, as though the writer had debated whether to include it: *It must be exact. You understand. Exact.*

Fenwick had read the note, folded it once, and put it in the drawer where such notes went, and said nothing, and Marguerite had asked nothing, because that was the arrangement upon which everything else — the cot in the back room, the wages of nine shillings a week, the name *apprentice* spoken without contempt — quietly rested.

The watch lay open on its cushion like a small sleeping animal, gold case gone dull at the edges where a thumb had polished it ten thousand times. Marguerite picked up the eyepiece, screwed it to their brow, and bent close.

The movement was good. That was the first thing. A lever escapement, English-made, the kind of work that cost more than a weaver earned in half a year, and beneath the balance wheel someone's initials had been engraved and then, deliberately, partly effaced — scraped to a blur, as if the maker had wished to be unremembered. Marguerite noted this the way they noted everything: silently, thoroughly, and without comment. The hairspring was sound. The pivots were clean. But the escape wheel's teeth carried wear in one sector only, a bright crescent of metal worn away unevenly, and when Marguerite turned the train slowly between finger and thumb they felt it — a hesitation, tiny as a held breath, each time the fourth wheel passed the same point of its revolution.

Something had caught in there once. Something small. A fleck of grit, perhaps, or a sliver of a broken tooth from some other mechanism, fallen in through a gap in the case and lodged where it could do its quiet damage day after day, minute after minute, four seconds lost here, seven there, until a man who measured other men's lives by this watch found himself arriving everywhere slightly too late and could not say why.

Marguerite understood watches better than they understood people. This was not modesty; it was arithmetic. A watch told you what was wrong with it if you knew how to listen. It had no interest in deceiving you. Its silences meant only one thing — *look closer* — whereas the silences of men and women in the mill district meant a dozen things, most of them dangerous, and the whole of Marguerite's education, formal and otherwise, had been in learning to tell them apart.

They were still listening to the watch when the bell above the door rang and the cold came in with it.

* * *

Mr. Pratt was a large man made larger by his clothes — a coat of good broadcloth, a waistcoat with a pattern too bold for a working district, boots that had never seen a loom floor. He filled the little shop the way weather fills a room, pushing the air aside, and he did not take off his hat. Fenwick emerged from the back within moments; he always seemed to know when a customer crossed the threshold, as though the bell rang somewhere inside him too.

"Master Fenwick." Pratt's voice was pleasant enough. Voices usually were. "My watch."

"Being attended to." Fenwick's voice was flat as a filed plate. "Sit, if you like."

Pratt sat, or perched — on the single customer's chair by the counter, knees high, hat still on. Marguerite kept their head down over the bench. This was the second rule, learned alongside the first: *don't look up.* Customers came to a clockmaker to have time handled, and there was something about being watched while it was done that unsettled them, as though the apprentice might see what the watch had witnessed — every late arrival, every early departure, every moment of a man's day laid out in ticks. Or perhaps it was simpler than that. Perhaps they simply did not like being observed by someone whose trade was noticing.

The shop was very quiet. Outside, the district went about its noise — carts, the mill whistle, a woman calling a child home through the sleet-grey morning — but inside there was only the tick of the regulator clock on the wall, Fenwick's own masterwork, and the small sounds of Marguerite's tools: the whisper of the pegwood, the click of tweezers meeting brass.

They worked the grit free with a camel-hair brush and a drop of the finest oil, and then they cleaned the sector of worn teeth as best as wear could be answered, which is to say imperfectly, honestly — the watch would keep better time than it had, but the teeth would never be new again, and Margueride would tell the master so, and the master would tell Pratt so, and the price would be adjusted accordingly. There was a cleanliness in this transaction that Marguerite clung to. Work priced according to its truth. Not everything in Manchester worked that way.

"Quarter day coming," Pratt said to the room, or possibly to Fenwick.

"It comes every quarter," Fenwick said.

"Aye, well. Some quarters matter more." A pause, filled with the regulator's ticking. "Audits. Commissioners poking about, wanting everything squared. Hours checked against wages, wages against hours. As if a man can't be trusted to count his own floor."

"Can he?" Fenwick asked. It was not a joke. Nothing Fenwick said was ever quite a joke, which was why he said so little.

Pratt's laugh came out wrong, one note too high. "He can if his instruments are honest." Then, quickly, as if to cover the sentence before it finished landing: "That's why I'm here, isn't it. Wanting mine put right."

Marguerite, bent over the watch, saw none of this. Heard all of it. And it was at this point — they would remember this precisely afterward, the way one remembers the exact position of a part before disassembly — it was at this point that Fenwick, without looking up from the ledger in which he recorded nothing of importance, said:

"How long has it been losing time?"

And Mr. Pratt flinched.

It was a small thing. A tightening of the shoulders, a half-turn of the head, the sort of motion a man makes when a door slams behind him. Anyone watching would have called it nothing. But Marguerite was not anyone; Marguerite was fluent in silence, and the silence that followed the word *losing* — just a beat, just the space of a single tick — spoke more plainly than any confession. The word *hours* had not even been used yet. Only *time.* And already the man across the counter had gone still as a stopped clock.

*Don't ask.* The first rule. It settled over Marguerite like the apron they wore, familiar and constraining. They did not ask. They did not lift their head. They pegged the fourth wheel, oiled the pallets, and listened to the two men talk about the weather, which was foul, and the river, which would freeze soon, and the bridge, which would close when it did — and all the while the flinch sat in their memory like a fleck of grit in a train of wheels, small, misplaced, certain to cause trouble eventually, and impossible to ignore entirely.

* * *

At noon Fenwick sent them for bread, which was also permission to stand in the street for six minutes and breathe air that did not smell of oil. The snow had begun — fine, dry, indifferent snow, the kind that fell all winter in Ancoats and never quite managed to be beautiful, settling on soot and turning grey before it landed. Marguerite pulled their collar up and joined the current of bodies moving along the canal towpath, heads down, hands in, everyone fluent in the same silence.

They passed the mill gates. They always passed the mill gates; there was no way through the district that didn't. The five-storey bulk of Harrow Mills rose against the white sky like something geological, windows in ranks, and from inside came the sound that never stopped — the great loom-thunder, felt in the teeth more than heard. Workers streamed in through the gate for the afternoon bell, and Marguerite slowed without meaning to, watching their faces.

They were young, most of them, and tired in a way that had stopped being temporary. A girl with cotton dust whitening her eyebrows. Two boys arguing cheerfully about football, or whatever the mills played in winter. A woman carrying her dinner in a tin, eating as she walked because the bell would not wait. None of them looked up either. That was the thing Marguerite had understood only gradually, in three years of living here: it wasn't only apprentices who kept their heads down. The whole district moved with its chin tucked, its eyes on the cobbles, its words kept behind its teeth. Silence wasn't a survival skill here. It was the local language, and everyone spoke it, and Marguerite was merely more fluent than most.

*Don't ask, don't repeat, don't look up.*

Three rules. Fenwick had never stated them outright, not once, in three years. He had taught them the way he taught everything — by demonstration, by omission, by the weight of what he declined to say. Don't ask: the day a journeyman named Aldous had asked a shipping clerk why the bales weighed light on the manifest and heavy on the dock, and had packed his tools that same evening and left for Liverpool, and no one had mentioned him again, ever, as though he had been a watch taken in for repair and never collected. Don't repeat: the way Fenwick burned his notes each Saturday, every scrap, in the little iron stove, and watched the flames until they died. Don't look up: the posture of the entire district, walking past the mill gates, walking past the overseers, walking past the things that happened on the upper floors that were discussed nowhere, ever, in any tense.

Marguerite had resented the rules once, in the first year, when resentment was still affordable. Now they wore them like the apron — a dead man's shape, cut down to fit, warm in places and stiff in others. Because the rules worked. That was the terrible thing. The rules had kept Marguerite alive and fed and learning a craft they loved with a depth that frightened them sometimes, and every person they had ever known who broke a rule was gone, and being alive and fed and skilled was not nothing. It was, in fact, nearly everything. It was everything except the part of yourself that noticed a man flinch at the word *hours* and wanted, badly, to ask him why.

They bought the bread — a stale loaf, discounted, which was what the discount baker sold to those who paid cash and asked nothing — and stood eating the heel on the towpath, watching the canal. Ice laced the edges now, thin as watch-glass. In a week, maybe two, it would thicken, and then the council would close the iron bridge for the season, the way it did every year, because ice floes took out bridges and bridges cost money, and the mill district would be sealed off from the rest of Manchester like a mechanism in a closed case. No crossing. No posting letters beyond the district post, which was read. No leaving until thaw.

Marguerite thought about this the way they thought about everything: practically, and then, underneath the practicality, with a low unease they had no tools to examine. Sealed in. Sealed in with the mills and the rules and the flinching overseers, until spring.

* * *

The afternoon passed in the ordinary miracle of the work. This was the truth Marguerite could never explain to anyone who didn't share it: that repairing a watch was not drudgery but devotion, that there was a holiness in bringing a broken thing back to truth. By lamplight they trued the escape wheel's teeth with a fine file, checking each pass against the loupe, breathing through their mouth because breath fogged the steel. The watch began, slowly, to recover. The hesitation in the train eased, then vanished. When Marguerite set the balance swinging and held the movement to their ear, it spoke evenly again — that steady, unbothered voice, *I am counting, I am counting, I am counting* — and something in Marguerite's chest loosened in answer.

If only people were so easily put right. If only you could open them up, find the grit, remove it, oil what was worn, and have them keep honest time again.

Fenwick appeared beside the bench near dusk, silent as ever, and held out his hand. Marguerite surrendered the watch into it and watched the old man test it — winding, setting, holding it to his own ear, turning it crown-down and back, the whole catechism — and then nod once, which from Fenwick was applause.

"Exact?" Marguerite asked.

"Within a few seconds a week. Tell him the teeth are worn. Tell him it will want seeing again inside two years. Write it down." Fenwick paused, and Marguerite braced, because pauses from Fenwick were load-bearing structures. "You heard what he said. About audits."

"I heard."

"And?"

Marguerite kept their eyes on the bench. "Nothing, master. A man worried about his watch."

Fenwick regarded them for a long moment. His face was a mechanism too, older and rustier, and mostly it showed only what it was built to show. But just then something moved behind it — not approval exactly, and not disappointment. Recognition, perhaps. One fluent speaker acknowledging another.

"Worry," Fenwick said, "is a word men use when they mean fear and don't want to pay for it." He set the watch down, closed its case with a click that sounded final. "Clean your bench. We're done."

* * *

That night, in the back room, on the narrow cot under the eaves, Marguerite lay awake and listened to the district settle around them: the last cart-wheels, a dog, the distant shift-change bell of the night mill, wind worrying the loose slate above the window. Their hands ached pleasantly from the day's work. Their mind would not stop.

*Hours checked against wages, wages against hours.* Pratt had said it lightly, and flinched before he'd finished saying it, and Marguerite turned the memory over and over like a suspect part, looking for the flaw. Why would an overseer flinch at the auditing of hours? Unless his hours and his wages did not agree. Unless somewhere between the clock that marked the time and the book that counted the pay, something was eating the difference — quietly, daily, in amounts too small for any single worker to notice and large enough, multiplied across hundreds of workers across years, to build somebody a coat of good broadcloth.

Marguerite sat up in the dark.

This was how trouble started. Not with questions asked aloud — nobody in the mill district asked aloud — but with questions asked at all, even silently, even alone, even in the privacy of one's own skull at midnight. Thoughts had weight. Thoughts accumulated, like wear on a tooth, invisible until the mechanism failed. Aldous had thought too loudly once. Marguerite resolved, lying back down and pulling the blanket to their chin, to think more quietly. To file the flinch away in the drawer where such observations went, next to the notes Fenwick saved and never reread, and leave it there to gather dust, and be glad of the warmth of the cot and the nine shillings a week and the craft they loved.

They almost managed it. They were nearly asleep when the thought surfaced anyway, smooth and cold as the first ice on the canal:

*Quarter day is coming. And the commissioners will be checking hours against wages. And I am the apprentice of the only clockmaker in the district — and any day now, someone may walk through that door and ask us to service the clock that all of it hangs on.*

They did not know, then, that the summons was already written. They did not know it was coming for them, on the morrow, in a liveried coat, wearing the Harrow crest. They only knew that the watch on the bench downstairs was running true again, and that they themselves were not, and that outside the window the snow went on falling, patient as arithmetic, covering everything alike.
