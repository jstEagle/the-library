## 2. A Summons in Winter Light

The knock came at half past nine by the workshop clock, which was to say at some other time entirely, for Master Fenwick's regulator ran six minutes fast in winter and Marguerite had long ago stopped correcting it. A workshop keeps its own weather. You learn to live inside it the way a fish lives inside water — not because the water is kind, but because it is what holds you up.

Marguerite was at the bench with a mill overseer's pocket watch open under the loupe, the balance wheel lifted out and trembling on its jewel like a small frightened heart. The watch belonged to a Mr. Tredlow, who had brought it in three days ago with a cracked mainspring and a manner of looking at everything in the shop except the shop itself. He had stood by the door the whole time, hat in hand, saying it was his wife's father's watch and that time had got away from him lately, and Marguerite had said nothing, because the rules of survival in the mill district were three and simple and older than Marguerite's apprenticeship: don't ask, don't repeat, don't look up.

The knock came again. Fenwick looked up from his own bench, where he was boring a pivot with the patience of a man who had made peace with every slow thing in the world.

"See who that is," he said. "And mind the cold comes in with them."

Marguerite set down the tweezers, laid the balance wheel on chamois leather, and went to the door. Through the frosted pane the winter light stood grey and thin, the light of a Manchester January that never quite committed to being morning. Beyond the glass, Ancoats was performing its usual transformation of coal smoke into weather, the chimneys of the mills along the canal standing in their rows like organ pipes playing one sustained, tuneless note.

The man on the step wore livery. That was the first wrong thing. Livery did not come to Fenwick's workshop; Fenwick's customers came in their own coats, wiping their boots twice, counting their coins in their pockets before they crossed the threshold. This man's coat was dark green with brass buttons polished to a shine that said someone else did the polishing, and his hat was beaver, and under one arm he carried a leather case with a silver clasp.

"Master Fenwick?" he asked, though he was looking at Marguerite, at the oil-stained apron, at the age that would not yet pass for master of anything.

"Apprentice," Marguerite said. "Master Fenwick is within."

"Then I'll deliver to the master." He held out the case anyway, and Marguerite took it because refusing it would have required a reason, and reasons were a currency Marguerite did not spend lightly. The case was heavier than it looked. Something inside shifted with a sound like a small door closing.

Fenwick had come to the doorway behind them, wiping his hands on a rag that had been wiping hands for thirty years. He read the livery the way he read a movement in a train of gears — instantly, completely, and without any change in his face.

"Harrow Mills," the messenger said, and the name went through the little shop like a draught under a door. Harrow Mills owned half the skyline Marguerite could see from the workshop window: the great six-storey piles along the canal, the counting house with its Greek columns, and above them all, on the rise at the district's edge, the observatory with its dome, from which the master clock sent out the time that every mill, every warehouse, every wage clerk in the district obeyed. When the observatory struck, the mills started. When it struck again, the mills stopped. Between the two strikings lived several hundred people's wages, and everyone knew it, and no one said it, because it was simply how the world was geared.

"The observatory clock," the messenger went on, in the tone of a man reciting something composed by someone more important. "It has been losing time. The quarter audit falls on the last day of the month, and the master clock must keep correct time for the inspection. Mr. Harrow requires the work of a certified craftsman. Mr. Harrow requires it done inside the compound."

"Inside," Fenwick said.

"The clock is not to be moved, and the mechanism is not to leave the observatory. The work will be done under supervision. The fee—" he named a figure, and Marguerite heard it the way one hears a bell rung in another room, a sound that belonged to a different and larger life. It was more than Fenwick took in a good month. It was possibly more than Fenwick took in two.

Fenwick did not answer at once. He turned the leather case over in his hands, and Marguerite watched him and understood, with the particular dread of an apprentice who has learned to read a room before reading a letter, that the answer had already been made and that the messenger's visit was a formality arranged to give the acceptance the appearance of choice.

"The apprentice will do the work," Fenwick said. "My hands are not what they were for climbing clock towers. The apprentice is quick, and careful, and knows when not to talk."

Marguerite felt those last words settle on their shoulders like a hand.

"I was told to ask for you by name," the messenger said, and now he did look directly at Marguerite, and there was something in the look that was not about clocks at all — an assessment, a weighing, the look of a man checking a coin against a known weight. "The Eastwick apprentice. You come recommended."

"Recommended by whom?" Marguerite asked, and heard the rule break in their own mouth — *don't ask* — and could not stop it, because the question had been standing in their chest for three days now, ever since Tredlow the overseer had flinched at the word *hours* and left without his change.

The messenger smiled the way a locked door smiles. "By the mill," he said. "The mill recommends whom it recommends. The cart will come Thursday morning. Bring your own tools; the compound provides nothing that leaves it." He touched his hat to Fenwick, inclined his head to Marguerite in a way that was almost respect and almost warning, and went down the steps into the grey.

Fenwick closed the door and stood with his hand flat against it a moment, as though the cold were a thing that might follow a man in.

* * *

They did not speak of it while the light lasted. That was Fenwick's way and the workshop's law: work first, words after, and some words never. Marguerite went back to the bench and to Tredlow's watch, and the balance wheel waited where it had been laid, patient as everything in the shop except the people. Marguerite fitted it, pinned it, let the mainspring run its slow breath through the train, and watched the second hand begin its circuit — and thought, not for the first time, that a watch was the most honest thing a person could hold. It could not flatter. It could not lie. It could only run true or run false, and either way it told you so.

If only the world were geared the same way.

Tredlow came at four for his watch, stamping snow off his boots, and paid without counting, which was itself a kind of information — a man who doesn't count his money has more of it than he admits. At the door he paused, watch in his waistcoat pocket now, and said, to the middle distance rather than to either of them:

"They'll have it running right for the audit, then. The master clock." A pause, the length of a held breath. "It's important it runs right. For the audit."

"It always runs right," Marguerite said, "once it's mended."

Tredlow's eyes came to them then, briefly, and Marguerite saw in them the same flinch they had seen three days ago — a man hearing a word he had been told not to hear. "Aye," he said. "Once it's mended." And he was gone into the snow.

Fenwick waited until the door had latched. Then he banked the fire, which was his way of calling a meeting, and sat on the stool by the bench, and looked at Marguerite for a long time with eyes the colour of old brass.

"You'll go Thursday," he said. "You'll do the work. You'll be paid the fee, and the fee is fair, and we'll not starve this winter, which is more than most shops in this district can say."

"Master—"

"You'll not ask questions inside those walls. You'll not repeat anything you see or hear there, to me or to anyone. You'll mend what you're sent to mend, and if the clock runs true when you've done, then it runs true, and if it runs as it ran before, then it runs as the mill wants it to run, and that is the mill's arithmetic, not ours." He said it evenly, the way he said everything, a man reading the terms of a contract he had not written. "Do you understand me?"

Marguerite understood him. That was the terrible part — that they understood him completely, and that the understanding sat in the chest like a swallowed stone. Fenwick was not a wicked man. Fenwick had taken in an orphan with oil-stained cuffs and taught them a trade, and had never once raised his voice or his hand, and had taught them which silences keep people safe as carefully as he had taught them how to true a wheel. He was offering Marguerite the only protection he had: the protection of knowing nothing.

"The fee," Marguerite said. "It's too high for a repair."

"The fee is what it is."

"Master Fenwick. A clock doesn't lose time for no reason. Something is worn, or broken, or—" *or filed*, said a voice in Marguerite's head that they did not permit to finish the sentence. "Or interfered with. And if it's interfered with, and I mend it, and the mill wants it unmended—"

"Then you'll mend what you're told to mend." Fenwick's voice did not rise. It never rose. But something in it closed like a case being shut. "Marguerite. I have buried two apprentices in this district, and I don't mean in churchyards. One asked questions in a counting house. One was seen looking at papers. You know the stories. You were here when—"

"I was here," Marguerite said quietly, "when Daniel was taken for questioning and didn't come back."

The fire ticked. Somewhere down the lane a cart went by, iron tyres grinding the frozen ruts.

"So you'll go," Fenwick said, "and you'll be silent, and you'll come home, and you'll take the fee, and we will both forget the fee was ever strange. That is the whole of my instruction. The rest—" he looked at the bench, at the small brass heart of Tredlow's watch still ticking under its glass, "—the rest is between you and whatever you find up that tower."

* * *

Thursday came with snow.

Marguerite rose in the dark and packed the tool chest by candlelight, and the packing was a kind of prayer, each tool wrapped and set in its bed of wool: the files in their graduated sizes, the burnisher, the staking set, the loupe, the oilstone, the small brass pliers with the worn jaws that had been Fenwick's master's before they were Fenwick's. Tools were the only inheritance a craftsman could trust. A tool did not disown you. A tool asked nothing but that your hands stay steady.

The cart was at the door before the light was. The driver did not speak except to say *compound* and *sit*, and Marguerite sat, the chest on their knees, and watched Ancoats go by in the falling snow — the canal black and slow between its ice shelves, the mill windows already lit in their hundreds, the weavers' children on the corners with their breath standing round them like small ghosts. The district that had raised them, passing by window by window, and Marguerite did not know, watching it recede, that they were looking at it for the last time as someone who could leave it whenever they chose.

At the river, the iron bridge stood in the snowfall with its lamps still burning against the grey morning, and the cart slowed, and the driver said, "Last week it'll cross, likely. Ice is coming early this year." He said it the way men say weather, meaning nothing by it, and the words went into Marguerite and stayed there, cold and patient, a splinter working its way toward something vital.

Beyond the bridge, the compound walls rose — brick, high, capped with stone — and the gate stood open for them as though it had been standing open all night, waiting.

The messenger from Monday was there to meet them, snow gathering on his beaver hat, and he smiled his locked-door smile and said, "You're expected," and Marguerite carried the chest through the gate and heard, behind them, the sound of it beginning to close.

Not all the way. Not yet. But closing.

And in the tower above, faint through the snow, the master clock struck the quarter — four notes, unhurried, certain, telling the whole district what time it was.

Marguerite counted them, out of habit, out of craft.

They were a little slow.
