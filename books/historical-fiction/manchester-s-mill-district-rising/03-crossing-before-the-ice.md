## 3. Crossing Before the Ice

The messenger had said to come at first light, and so Marguerite came in the dark before it, when the mill district was still more smoke than city and the gas lamps burned in their cages like patient prisoners. The tool chest rode on their shoulder the way it always did — forty pounds of iron and brass and inheritance, the leather strap worn to a groove that fit no one else's collarbone now but theirs. They had packed it by candlelight: files wrapped in flannel, the good loupe nested in wool, Fenwick's spare burnisher because he had pressed it into their hand at the door without a word, only a look that said *bring it back*, which meant, in the grammar of Master Fenwick, *bring yourself back*.

The river ran black beneath the frost fog, slow and muscular, carrying on its surface the first skins of ice — thin grey petals that spun and gathered and broke apart against the piers of the iron bridge. Marguerite stopped at the foot of the span and looked up at it the way they looked at any mechanism before trusting it: rivets in their thousands, each one a small promise; the lattice of girders climbing into the fog like the ribs of some drowned cathedral raised just high enough to breathe. It had been built eleven years ago by men who argued about tensile strength the way theologians argued about grace, and it sang, very faintly, under the weight of the carts already crossing — a hum Marguerite felt in their teeth before they heard it.

They had crossed this bridge perhaps two hundred times in their life. They had never once crossed it carrying everything they owned of consequence.

The snow began as they stepped onto the planking.

Not falling so much as arriving — flakes materializing out of the fog without origin or direction, settling on the iron rails, on the shoulders of the men ahead, on the chest of Marguerite's coat where they melted into dark spots like ink deciding what to become. The air smelled of coal smoke and river weed and, underneath, the sweetish sourness of raw cotton, which was the smell of money in this district and therefore the smell of everything.

Ahead, the last barges of the season were being unloaded at the Harrow wharf. Marguerite could see them even through the fog's gauze: great bales of cotton swung from ship to quay in slings, each one the size of a sleeping ox, each one caught and carried by lines of men who moved with the weary precision of people who had done this since before they had names for their own exhaustion. The bales went up the ramp and into the warehouse mouth, and the warehouse swallowed them, and the men came down empty-handed and turned around and went back for more.

Marguerite counted without meaning to. It was a habit of the trade — counting escapements, counting teeth, counting the beats between one tick and the next. Fourteen men to a line. A bale every ninety seconds, give or take. At that rate the hold would be empty by noon, and then the barges would go, and then the ice would come, and the river would close like a door.

*Before the quarter audits,* the messenger had said, standing in Fenwick's workshop with his livery too clean for the room, his boots leaving prints on sawdust that Fenwick had swept that morning and would sweep again after. *Mr. Cobbett requires the master clock in working order before the commissioners' inspection. The observatory clock. You understand its importance.*

Fenwick had understood something else. Marguerite had watched him understand it — watched the old man's face do nothing at all while his eyes went somewhere private and came back with a decision already made. *My apprentice will attend to it,* Fenwick had said, and his hand on Marguerite's shoulder had been light and heavy at once, the way a hand is when it is both giving an opportunity and closing a trapdoor behind you.

*You understand its importance.* Everyone in the district understood the importance of the observatory clock, though few thought about it the way Marguerite was beginning to. It sat above the mill compound in its stone tower like a god with one eye, and from that eye the time flowed downward into every workshop, every counting house, every wage ledger in Ancoats. When the observatory clock struck seven, seven thousand people became late or punctual in the same instant. Its word was not checked. Its word was not questioned. A clock that keeps the time for everyone else is never asked to prove its own honesty — that was the first thing Fenwick had taught them about public clocks, back when Marguerite was twelve and still believed teaching was a form of kindness rather than a passing-down of burdens.

Halfway across the span, the wind found them.

It came up the river valley with the flat cold authority of winter itself, funneling between the girders, plucking at the chest straps, driving the snow sideways until the world was all white motion and the far bank a rumor. Marguerite shifted the tool chest to the other shoulder and kept walking, head down, watching the planks pass beneath their boots — iron-sheathed wood, worn smooth in two pale channels by decades of feet going the same direction. Toward the mills in the morning. Away from them at night. Nobody walked this bridge slowly. Nobody stood on it to admire the view. It was not that kind of bridge. It was a throat, and twice a day the district passed through it.

A cart overtook them, wheels ringing on the iron, the driver hunched under sacking with a boy beside him who stared at Marguerite's chest — at the chest of tools, at the strap, at the small figure carrying it — with the frank curiosity of the young before they learn that looking costs. Then the cart was past and the snow closed behind it.

Near the far pier, where the span met the wharf road, the loading line had backed up. One of the slings had fouled — Marguerite could see the bale swinging a half-foot off true, twisting on its hook while a foreman shouted and the winchman froze his lever. The men below waited with their hands up, ready to catch and guide four hundred pounds of compressed cotton that would break every bone in a careless body. For a long moment nobody moved except the bale, turning, turning, patient as a pendulum finding its amplitude.

Then a woman's voice cut through the wind — sharp, young, certain — calling the winchman by name, telling him *ease her, ease her, let the swing die on its own, don't fight it.* And the winchman eased her, and the swing died, and the bale came true, and the men below took its weight and walked it home into the warehouse, and the whole machine of the wharf resumed its rhythm as though nothing had happened, which was how machines preferred things.

The woman who had shouted stood apart from the line now, pulling work gloves off finger by finger. She was younger than Marguerite had expected — nineteen, twenty — with cotton dust ghosting her dark hair white at the temples and a red shawl knotted tight against the cold. She was looking at the bales going in with an expression Marguerite recognized from mirrors: the look of someone counting something that could not be counted out loud.

She noticed Marguerite noticing her. Her chin came up.

"You're the clockmaker's one." Not a question. News traveled faster than weather in the district. "Fenwick's. They said you'd be crossing this morning."

"I'm Marguerite Eastwick. I keep for Master Fenwick."

"Keep." She tried the word like a coin bitten for gold. "Bess Carrick. I weave on the north floor — third shift, when there's a third shift, which is whenever Mr. Cobbett decides hours are elastic." Something flickered in her face at her own words, gone as fast as it came, and Marguerite felt the old reflex rise — *don't ask, don't repeat* — and pressed it down, and hated pressing it down, and pressed it down anyway.

"You know the clock's stopped?" Bess said.

"It runs slow," Marguerite said carefully. "That's what I'm told. Four minutes a day."

"Four minutes a day." Bess looked out at the river, at the ice petals spinning past the piers, and her voice went flat and strange. "Do you know what four minutes a day comes to, over a fortnight? Over a year? Because we do. Every soul on those floors does the arithmetic whether they want to or not. We're not educated, most of us, but we can count minutes better than priests count sins." She pulled the glove back onto her hand, hard, as if she were angry at it. "The bell rings and we walk to our looms, and the bell rings and we walk away, and the ledger man writes down what the tower says, and what the tower says is short. Always short. Never long. Funny, that. A clock that only ever loses."

The wind gusted. Somewhere behind them, on the free side of the river, a church bell rang the half hour — a different timekeeper, a smaller god, and Marguerite thought: *I could check it. Right now. Before I cross. I could stand here and compare the two and know.* But the church bell was muffled by fog and distance and the wind's noise, and the moment passed, and Marguerite understood that they had let it pass, and filed that understanding away in the place where they kept things they did not want to look at.

"You'll be inside the walls, then," Bess said. "In the compound. Through the thaw, if the ice closes early like they're saying."

"So I'm told."

"My brother worked the north floor." She said it simply, the way you state a measurement. "Last spring. There was an accident." She stopped. The wind filled the pause with river sounds. "He was twenty-two. The record says it happened outside working hours." Her eyes came around to Marguerite's face and held there, and they were not soft eyes and they were not asking for comfort. "Outside working hours. In a shed that only opens when the tower bell rings. You think about that, clockmaker's one, while you're fixing whatever needs fixing in there. Think about what *working hours* means, and who gets to write them down, and what a clock is worth to a man who owns one."

Marguerite's hands had gone cold inside their gloves, colder than the day accounted for. "I repair clocks," they said. "I don't—"

"I know what you do." Bess shouldered her own bundle — mended linens by the look of them, bound for the compound laundry, so she crossed these walls too, in and out, in and out, one of the few with a reason and a pass. "Everybody knows what you do. That's why I'm saying it." She stepped closer, close enough that Marguerite could see the fine weave of cotton dust in her eyelashes, and lowered her voice beneath the wind as though the bridge itself might repeat what it heard. "Nothing good gets fixed inside those walls. Whatever they've told you is broken — the clock, the boiler, the ledger, anything — remember that it got broken on purpose, by somebody, for a reason, and that the reason is still in there, wearing a good coat, eating well. You fix the thing they point at, and the reason stays. That's how it works. That's how it has always worked."

And then she straightened, and her voice lifted back to its ordinary register, loud enough for the foreman ten yards off to hear and dismiss: "Mind the ice on the ramp, clockmaker. It takes ankles every year." And she walked on toward the wharf gate with her bundle of linens, and the gate wardens nodded her through, and the walls took her the way the warehouse had taken the bales.

Marguerite stood a moment longer at the bridge's end.

Behind them: the free side. Fenwick's workshop with its smell of oil and cold tea, the narrow bed, the window that faced east and caught the first honest light. Behind them lay everything with a known weight and a known price. Ahead, past the ramp and the gate and the inner court, the compound rose out of the snowfall — the mills themselves vast and windowed like prisons built by someone with taste, the counting house, the owner's house set back among bare elms, and above it all, on its stone promontory, the observatory tower, its dome furred with rime, its clock face a pale disc floating in the fog. Even from here Marguerite could see the hands. Ten minutes to eight, they read. Marguerite wound their father's old watch — the one thing they carried that predated Fenwick — and held it up beside the distant dial.

The watch said six minutes past.

Four minutes slow. Already. Today. This morning, ticking its quiet theft down into the district while the barges unloaded and the bells rang and seven thousand people arranged their lives around a lie told in brass.

Marguerite put the watch away. There was nothing to be done about it here, on the public side of the river, with the wind talking and the wardens watching and Bess Carrick's warning still hanging in the air like breath. Nothing to be done but cross, and open the case, and see with their own eyes what kind of broken this was — the honest kind, the kind that happens to mechanisms through wear and weather and the plain cruelty of friction — or the other kind. The kind made by hands. By a file. By someone skilled enough to make a lie run smoothly.

They took up the tool chest. The strap settled into its groove in their collarbone, familiar as a scar. Ahead, the ramp rose toward the gate, iron plates slick with the first real snow, and a warden stood at the top with a ledger under his arm, checking names against a list, admitting the season's last laborers into the compound before the river shut and the world outside became a rumor.

Marguerite climbed.

At the gate the warden looked them over — the chest, the gloves, the face — and asked their name, and wrote it down in a column beneath a heading Marguerite could not read upside down but could guess at. *Entered.* Not *arrived*. Entered, as into a record, as into a mechanism, a tooth engaging a wheel.

"Observatory," the warden said, jerking his chin toward the tower. "Path round the left. Mr. Harrow's expecting you."

Not Cobbett. Marguerite had been told, in the letter that came with the commission, that all instructions would come through the manager, Mr. Cobbett, whose name Fenwick had repeated that night with a particular careful blankness. But the warden said *Mr. Harrow*, and the warden said it the way people say the names of weather — something large, something overhead, something that does not negotiate.

Marguerite passed under the arch, and the walls rose on either side, brick and iron and window-glass, and the sound of the river and the free city and the church bells faded behind them as though a door had been drawn across the world. Snow fell into the inner court and did not melt. Somewhere above, invisible in the fog, the master clock ticked on, four minutes slow, keeping its dishonest time over everything.

Marguerite stood in the courtyard with the chest on their shoulder and listened to it — really listened, the way Fenwick had taught them, letting the beat enter the bones — and beneath the tick they heard, faint and rhythmic from the mill floors, the looms. Hundreds of them. Keeping their own time. Waiting to be told.

The tower door, when Marguerite reached it, was already unlocked. And standing in the stairwell's shadow, as though he had been waiting there longer than courtesy would admit, was a young man in a good coat, with his father's jaw and no one's smile, looking at the tool chest the way Marguerite had once seen Fenwick look at a watch brought in for repair — as if it might contain anything, including the truth.

"You're earlier than expected," Edmund Harrow said.
