## 4. The Observatory at Dusk

The compound swallowed the road behind me.

I had crossed a thousand thresholds in my life — shop doors, cellar hatches, the low lintel of Fenwick's workshop where I'd learned to duck without thinking — but none of them had closed with this particular weight. The gates of Harrow Mills were iron, twice my height, and when they swung shut behind the porter's shoulder they made a sound like a book closing on a page someone else was still reading.

Inside, the world reorganized itself around money. I could see it in the way the gravel was raked, in the gas lamps already lit along the carriage drive though it was barely three o'clock and the snow had not yet decided to fall in earnest. On the free side of the river, dusk arrived like weather — gradual, indifferent, settling over everyone equally. Here it arrived like an appointment, anticipated, catered to, held off by brass fittings and expensive glass until such time as the household chose to permit the dark.

A boy of perhaps twelve took my tool chest from me before I could object. He carried it the way one carries a sleeping infant or a live coal — carefully, but without reverence — and set off across a courtyard large enough to hold Fenwick's entire street, expecting me to follow. I did. There was nowhere else to go, and the bridge, I reminded myself, was still open behind me. That thought was supposed to be a comfort. It sat in my chest instead like a coin too large for its slot.

The observatory stood at the compound's far corner, apart from the mill itself, as though the building that told the mill what time it was preferred not to be seen keeping company with it. It was a squat stone tower, older than everything around it, crowned with a dome gone green with weather, and I understood immediately why Harrow Mills kept it rather than replacing it with something newer and shinier. A clock tower is a claim. *Our* hours. *Our* authority. You do not renovate a claim; you maintain it, you gild it, and you make certain no one ever questions what it says.

The boy set down my chest at the tower door, touched his cap, and left without a word. Good. Words cost nothing here, apparently, which meant they were worth less than nothing, which meant I would have to keep paying attention to everything else.

The door was unlocked. That surprised me — until I stepped inside and understood that locking this door would be like locking a church. What was there to steal? Who would dare?

* * *

I have no adequate way to describe the master clock to anyone who has not stood beneath one. I have tried since, many times, in letters and testimony and once, memorably, before a room full of commissioners who wanted plain speech and got a lecture instead. But language fails at scale. A pocket watch is a sentence. A regulator clock is a paragraph. The master clock of the Harrow observatory was — there is no other word for it — a cathedral.

It rose through the center of the tower like the spine of some enormous patient animal, weight-driven, its great drum barrels wrapped with cable thick as my finger, its pendulum hanging nine feet of polished steel and cast iron into a pit lined with brick to guard against draughts. Brass everywhere — not the thin, apologetic brass of cheap movements but plates of it, half an inch thick, engraved with the maker's name in lettering so fine I had to lean close to read it: *Whitworth & Sons, London, 1811.* Thirty-six years old and running still, or trying to run; I could hear it from the doorway, that slow deep heartbeat, tick and tock separated by a full second of deliberate silence, the sound of a machine that had never once been hurried.

The stair wound up around it, stone steps worn concave by a century of feet, and light came down through the lantern windows in long amber slants that turned the dust into drifting gold. Somewhere above, bells waited in their frame — the quarter chimes, the hour strike, the voice by which two thousand workers knew when to arrive, when to eat, when they might finally stop.

I stood in the doorway longer than was strictly professional. I will admit that. Every clockmaker has one moment, early, when the trade stops being a trade and becomes a kind of devotion, and standing there with the snow melting off my shoulders and that vast slow heartbeat filling the stone throat of the tower, I felt mine all over again, secondhand, borrowed from whoever had built this thing and loved it properly.

Then I remembered why I was here, and the warmth went out of the feeling like breath off cold glass.

Because a clock this important does not drift four minutes a day by accident. And whatever was wrong with it, I had been brought inside these walls to fix it — or to appear to fix it — and I did not yet know which, and the difference between those two things was very possibly the difference between going home in spring and not going home at all.

I opened my tool chest and began to work. Not on the clock — not yet. First, the ritual: every tool out, laid on the cloth in order, inspected. It steadies the hands. It tells the mind *you are a craftsman, and craftsmen are permitted to be here.* Files, pliers, screwdrivers with handles worn to the shape of my grip, the small brass oiler, the loupe on its cord. I was halfway through the inventory when I heard footsteps on the gravel outside, unhurried, and then a voice at the door behind me.

"You count them."

I did not startle. Three years in Fenwick's workshop teaches you not to startle; startling is a form of asking questions with your body. I finished laying down the last screwdriver before I turned.

He was younger than I expected — mid-twenties, perhaps, tall, dressed well but not ostentatiously, in the manner of a man whose tailors had been instructed to avoid giving offense to anyone. Dark hair, a face that might have been handsome if it had ever been permitted to relax. He stood just inside the door with snow on his coat he hadn't bothered to brush off, and he was looking not at me but at the cloth, at the tools arranged upon it, with an attention that made the back of my neck prickle.

"Master Fenwick's apprentice," he said. "Marguerite Eastwick." He said my name correctly, both parts of it, without the hesitation most people performed around it. "Edmund Harrow."

I gave him the bow Fenwick had drilled into me — shallow, precise, offering neither warmth nor insult. "Sir."

"Don't." He said it quickly, almost wincing. "Not 'sir.' I'm not — " He stopped himself, and something moved behind his eyes, some calculation concluded and filed away. "Mr. Eastwick, then. Or — how does your master address you?"

"'Apprentice,' mostly," I said. "When he's pleased. Other words when he isn't."

That earned me the ghost of a smile, there and gone. He came further into the tower, circling wide of the pendulum as though he knew better than to walk near a working clock — another point in his favor, and I noted it against my will. I was keeping a ledger of him already. I didn't decide to; it's simply what survival looks like when it's been practiced long enough. Every person who entered my vicinity got weighed, measured, and entered into the book. *Porter: indifferent, safe. Boy with chest: silent, safe. Edmund Harrow: unknown, therefore dangerous.*

He stopped at the base of the movement and looked up at it the way I had, and for a moment we were two people looking at the same cathedral, and I hated how much that felt like common ground.

"My father says it loses time," he said. "Four minutes a day, more or less. The men who wind it can't find the cause. They're not trained — they oil it, they raise the weights, but anything finer is beyond them." He glanced back at me. "Is it beyond diagnosis?"

"Nothing is beyond diagnosis," I said. "Some things are beyond repair. I won't know which this is until I've opened the case."

"And if it's beyond repair?"

"Then it gets sent to Whitworth's in London, and your father waits a season, and the district runs on whatever time the mill bell decides." I let that sit exactly as long as it needed to. "Unless the mill bell is the problem."

The silence that followed lasted perhaps three seconds. In it, I watched him decide several things, though I couldn't tell what they were. When he spoke again his voice was lighter, deliberately so, a man stepping back from an edge he'd approached on purpose.

"You're direct. Fenwick must despair of you."

"Fenwick values results."

"Mm." He crouched beside my open tool chest, uninvited, and I felt my whole body go still in the old way — the way I'd learned the winter Tom Ashby vanished, the way I'd learned watching overseers' hands. He picked up my finest file, the six-inch dead-smooth, and turned it over. Checked the teeth against his thumbnail. Held it up to the window light and sighted along it like a man examining a blade.

"These are good," he said. "Better than good. This one's Swiss, isn't it?"

"Lacote. From Paris, originally. My master bought it secondhand from a bankrupt jeweler."

"And this?" He lifted the loupe, turning it in his fingers.

"That you'll want to put down gently, sir — Mr. Harrow. It's older than both of us put together and it doesn't forgive drops."

He set it down. Gently. But he didn't step away from the chest, and when he spoke again, the question underneath the question surfaced, smooth as oil rising through water.

"How long have you been with Fenwick?"

"Three years."

"And before that?"

"I was born before that."

His mouth twitched. "You know what I'm asking."

"I know that it isn't about clocks." I said it evenly, without heat, the way Fenwick had taught me to decline things — as though declining were simply a task like any other, requiring only competence. "Your father hired my master's shop to repair a clock. My master sent me. If you want to know whether we're competent, watch my work. If you want to know something else, you'll have to ask it plainly, and I'll tell you whether I mean to answer."

For a long moment he simply looked at me, and I looked back, and I want to record honestly what happened in that exchange, because afterward I understood it had been the true first meeting between us, more than any introduction. Two mechanisms, unfamiliar to each other, each testing the other's escapement — probing for the point where resistance gives, where the wheel engages, where trust either catches or slips. He was measuring how much I would say. I was measuring how much he already knew. Neither of us found bottom.

"The last man who worked on this clock," he said quietly, "was a Mr. Prosser. Out of Ancoats. Very highly recommended." He set the file back in its place in the cloth, aligning it precisely where I had laid it. "My father found him difficult. He went to Liverpool in the autumn. I understand he's doing very well there."

"Everyone in Liverpool is doing very well, according to the people who send them there."

This time the smile was real, brief and startled out of him, and immediately regretted. He straightened. "You shouldn't say things like that to me."

"No, sir."

"I mean it. Cobbett hears everything. Everything said in this compound reaches him within a day, usually with improvements." He said it fast, low, glancing once toward the door — and there it was again, that flicker of something under the polish, the conscience I'd later come to know so well, showing its edge for half a second before being sheathed. "You seem intelligent. Intelligent people survive here by being boring. Be boring, Mr. Eastwick. Fix the clock. Take your fee. Go home when the bridge opens."

"Is that what you'd do?"

The question landed harder than I intended. I watched it land. Something in his face closed — not angrily; more like a shutter drawn over a window because the light coming through it was the wrong color.

"What I would do," he said, "is irrelevant. I'm leaving you to your work. Dinner is at seven in the house; Cobbett expects you. Don't be late, don't be interesting, and don't — " he paused at the door, hand on the iron ring, not quite looking at me — "don't take anything apart that you don't intend to put back together."

And he was gone, footsteps fading across the gravel, leaving me alone with the great slow heartbeat of the master clock and a sentence I would turn over for days.

*Don't take anything apart that you don't intend to put back together.*

It sounded like advice. It sounded, even, like a warning offered in my favor — a strange courtesy from the owner's son to the tradesman's apprentice. But warnings have grain, like wood, and when you examine them closely you can see which direction they grew. That one had grown from knowledge. He had not said *if* anything is wrong with this clock. He had said *don't take apart what you don't intend to restore* — as if he already suspected that what I found inside the case might be something I'd hesitate to return to its original condition.

Above me, the pendulum swung its nine-foot arc, patient, metronomic, indifferent to everything except gravity and its own law. Four minutes a day, they said. I climbed the stair with my loupe in my hand and my heart going faster than any honest mechanism should, and I told myself I would find wear, dirt, a tired suspension spring, the ordinary sins of an ordinary aging machine.

Even then — even before I opened the case — some part of me, the part fluent in silence, the part that had survived Manchester by reading what rooms did not say, already knew what I was going to find.

The file marks would be fresh.
