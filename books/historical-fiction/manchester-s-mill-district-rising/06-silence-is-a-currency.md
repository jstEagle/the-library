## 6. Silence Is a Currency

The clock ticked above me all night, and I learned to hate the sound of my own breathing.

I had not meant to stay in the observatory past dark. The work was simple enough — clean the movement, dress the pivots with a fine oil I carried in a vial no bigger than a robin's egg — and by rights I should have packed my tools at dusk and walked down through the compound gates to the lodgings Cobbett's man had assigned me in the weavers' row. But the light had gone strange and blue over the moors, and snow had started again, and there is a kind of honesty that only happens between a craftsman and a mechanism when no one else is watching. So I stayed. I sat on a stool beneath the great clock with its cathedral of brass towering over me, and I listened to it run four minutes slow, and I thought about what it costs a person to know a thing and say nothing.

Silence has a weight. Anyone who tells you otherwise has never carried one.

I held the escapement pallet in my palm and turned it toward the lamp. The file marks caught the light like scars — fresh, deliberate, cut by someone who understood exactly what they were doing and wanted it to look like wear. That was the part that frightened me most, I think, though I did not have words for it then. Not the tampering itself. The craftsmanship of it. Whoever had done this loved clocks the way I loved them, knew the anatomy of an escapement the way I knew my own hands, and had used that knowledge the way a surgeon uses a blade.

Four minutes slow each day. I had worked the arithmetic twice while the kettle heated, a third time while the snow thickened against the observatory glass. Four minutes out of every hour, shaved from every bell that governed the mill district — the start bell, the dinner bell, the stop bell. A worker paid by the hour loses those minutes the way a field loses water to a hidden leak: slowly, everywhere, without ever seeing where it goes. Multiply four minutes by six hundred hands by six days by fifty-two weeks, and you arrive at a sum that would buy the wool merchant's whole warehouse. You arrive at years of other people's lives, measured out and pocketed.

And underneath the arithmetic, darker: Bess Carrick's face in the stairwell was still two days away from me, but I already knew what the falsified hours were for. You do not need to hide time unless something happened inside it.

* * *

Three winters earlier, in Fenwick's workshop, a man named Aldous Pike asked one question too many.

He was not an apprentice; he was a journeyman, older than Fenwick almost, with a beard gone to iron-grey and hands so steady he could true a balance wheel without resting his wrist on the bench. He had come to Fenwick's from a position at a watchmaker's in Stockport that had ended — nobody ever said how — and he kept to himself mostly, ate his lunch alone on the back step even in winter, whistled nothing in particular under his breath while he worked. I was fifteen. My job was sweeping, fetching, holding parts steady while better hands finished them, and learning, above all, to be furniture.

Aldous was repairing a regulator clock for the corn exchange, and one afternoon he stopped, held the movement up to the window light, and frowned at it for a long time.

"This isn't right," he said, to no one, to the clock, maybe to himself. "Someone's been into this. These marks aren't age."

Fenwick didn't look up from his own bench. "Bring it here."

Aldous brought it. Fenwick examined it for perhaps ten seconds and set it down again. "Wear," he said. "Finish your work."

"It's not wear." Aldous's voice had that particular brightness it gets when a person has found something and wants the world to confirm it. "Look at the grain of the cuts. Someone's altered the rate deliberately. This clock belongs to—"

"Aldous." Fenwick said the name once, quietly, the way you'd set down something breakable. Then he went back to his bench.

But Aldous had found something, and finding things does that to people — it makes them want to be seen holding them. He talked about it for two more days. He said he would write to the corn exchange committee. He said a man who could prove a public clock was being falsified could make a name for himself, could get honest contracts, could finally be somebody instead of a journeyman drifting from bench to bench. I remember thinking, in the shallow way of fifteen, that he seemed taller those two days. Lit up from inside.

On the third morning his bench was cleared. His tools were gone from their drawer. Fenwick told me Aldous had taken a position in Liverpool — quick work, good money, left before dawn to catch the early packet. There was a note, Fenwick said, but it was private. I never saw it. I never asked. Asking was not among my duties.

What I remember instead is that evening, after I'd swept and banked the fire, Fenwick called me to his bench and poured us each a measure of gin, which he had never done before and never did again. He drank his own in silence first, looking at the dead grate. Then he spoke, in a low even voice, the voice he used for explaining the most important mechanisms, the ones you mustn't rush.

"Marguerite. Come here. Sit."

I sat.

"There are three rules in this trade," he said, "and the first one isn't about clocks. It's about silences. Do you understand that a silence can be a thing you make? Like a part? Cut to fit?"

I said I thought so.

"Not all silences keep you safe," he went on. "Most of them are just empty, and emptiness draws attention. No — the silences that keep you safe are *fitted* ones. When a gentleman brings in a watch and complains about the cost, you don't tell him the cost is fair or unfair. You say, *I'll see what can be done.* When an overseer collects a clock and asks if anything seemed amiss, you don't say what you saw. You say, *It runs well now.* When someone vanishes from a workshop—" and here his voice did not change at all, which is how I knew the lesson was deadly serious, "—you don't ask where they've gone, and you don't ask why, and above all you don't ask whether anyone else noticed what they noticed."

"The corn exchange clock," I said. I couldn't help it.

Fenwick looked at me for a long moment. In the lamplight his eyes were the colour of old brass, greenish, patient, and very tired.

"What corn exchange clock?" he said.

And I understood. That was the examination. That was the fitting of the part. I said, "No clock, Master Fenwick," and he nodded slowly, and refilled my glass, and told me I'd do.

I have thought many times since about whether I passed that test or failed it, and I have never arrived anywhere comfortable. Because here is what I also learned that winter, from the small mercies and small cruelties of the trade: Aldous Pike did go to Liverpool. I saw him once, two years later, across the crowded floor of a warehouse sale, alive, hale, working as a timekeeper for a shipping concern — checking other men's hours against a clock he did not examine too closely. He had learned the same lesson. He wore it differently, that was all. Some men pay their tuition in flight, and some in forgetting, and some — I would learn this soon enough — in worse coin than either.

Fear calcifies. That is the truth of it. The first time you swallow a fitted silence it goes down like a stone. The tenth time it goes down like bread. And one day you cannot recall what it was like to speak plainly, because the habit of silence has grown into your jaw the way scale grows inside a pipe, and the water that comes through you comes through changed.

* * *

Now I sat under the master clock of Harrow Mills with its falsified heart ticking above my head, and I made myself do the thing Fenwick had taught me: I made the silence fit.

I laid out the plan the way I'd lay out parts before an assembly — everything in order, everything in its place, nothing overlapping. Tomorrow I would begin the official repair. I would restore the escapement to smooth operation, polish the filed faces until they looked like honest wear, regulate the clock back to precisely the slowness it had been adjusted to show. I would do beautiful work. I would let Cobbett see beautiful work. And when he asked — and he would ask, men like him always ask, pleasantly, the knife held loosely like a fan — I would say: *It runs well now.*

That was the safe road. That was the road Aldous Pike had not taken, and he had paid for it with his position, his name on a bench, his whole settled life, scattered overnight. That was the road that led home to Fenwick's workshop in spring with my apprenticeship intact and my hands still trusted and my sleep undisturbed except by dreams, and dreams could be endured. Dreams were cheap.

Against it I set the other ledger, the one I did not want to open. Four minutes an hour. Six hundred hands. And somewhere in the mill's records, a death recorded outside working hours — I did not yet know whose, but the shape of it stood in the dark of the compound the way the chimney stacks stood against the snow sky, black and certain. If I repaired that clock honestly, in the sense that meant dishonestly — if I polished the lie back into shine — then my hands would have made it. Every wage shorted thereafter would pass through my file marks. Every misrecorded hour after mine would rest, in some accounting no commissioner could ever audit, partly against me.

I took out my tools. Not to work — just to feel them. The burnisher, the gravers, the tiny Swiss files wrapped in chamois, my father's loupe that I wore on a cord around my neck and touched the way other people touch a saint's medal. This was the whole of my inheritance: skill, and the silence it had been taught to keep. I had believed, until this week, that the two fit together naturally — that craft was quiet work, that a good clockmaker was by nature a discreet one, that discretion and integrity were neighbours sharing a wall.

But sitting there I felt the wall crack. Because the same hand that files an escapement true can file it false, and the same mouth that says *it runs well now* can say *someone has been into this,* and there is no mechanism inside a person that forces the honest setting. That choice lives outside the works entirely, exposed, unwound, waiting for a finger.

I thought of Fenwick pouring the gin. *What corn exchange clock?* I thought of Aldous on the packet boat to Liverpool, alive. Alive. People forget, when they talk about courage, that staying silent keeps you alive, and being alive is not nothing. Being alive is warm bread and your own bed and the smell of brass polish and the small daily happinesses that make up a life the way ticks make up an hour. I had clawed my way to those happinesses out of nothing. Nobody had given them to me. Nobody owed me the keeping of them.

And yet.

Above me the master clock ticked its slow, stolen tick, and I found I could not stop hearing it as a sound of theft — a purse being picked, patiently, four minutes at a time, in the most public room in the district, under the nose of God and everyone. Somewhere below, the night watchman's boots rang on the compound cobbles. Snow ticked against the cupola glass. The lamp hissed. And in my coat, hanging on the peg by the door, lay the steel blank I had cut that afternoon — ostensibly scrap, ostensibly a practice piece, ostensibly nothing — from which, if I chose, if I dared, I could machine a true pallet, an honest escapement, a heart that beat correct time.

I had not let myself finish the thought when I cut the blank. Now, in the dark, with the false clock counting out its false midnight overhead, I let myself finish it.

There was a third option, and it was not bravery and it was not cowardice, and Fenwick's three rules did not cover it. It was this: make both parts. Restore the lie for Cobbett's eyes, and build the truth in secret, in stolen hours, in the fitted silences Fenwick had taught me — and keep the truth close, unfinished, uninstalled, a loaded mechanism waiting to see what the days demanded. A craftsman always keeps the original pattern. Even when the work is delivered, even when it is signed for. Especially then.

I took the blank from my coat and set it on the bench beside the sabotaged pallet, and the two pieces of steel lay together in the lamplight, identical twins, one of them lying.

That was the moment I heard the stair door open — soft, deliberate, unhurried footsteps on iron treads, someone climbing toward the clock room who had no business climbing at this hour. I closed my hand over both parts and turned, and the lamplight swung, and Edmund Harrow stepped up into the doorway with a candle of his own and an expression I could not read at all.

"You're here late," he said.

"So are you," I said.

Neither of us moved. Above us, the clock ticked on, four minutes slow, keeping everyone's time but its own.
