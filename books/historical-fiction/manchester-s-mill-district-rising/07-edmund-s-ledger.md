## 7. Edmund's Ledger

The counting house smelled of tallow, ink, and the particular dust that accumulates around money — a dry, papery sweetness, like something once alive that had been pressed flat between ledger boards for too long. Edmund Harrow had grown up breathing it. He had learned his letters copying freight invoices at this same desk, his feet not yet reaching the floor, his father's hand heavy on his shoulder in the way of a man steadying a machine he expected to run for decades.

Now he sat alone with three years of wage books, and the candle had burned down to its last thumb's width.

He should not have been there. The counting house belonged to Cobbett during the day and to his father's authority always, and Edmund's own duties — the observatory, the correspondence, the presentable face of Harrow Mills shown to commissioners and clergymen — required no acquaintance with the wage ledgers at all. That was precisely the arrangement. His father had built it that way, or Cobbett had, or the two of them had arrived at it without ever needing to say it aloud: the son would be given the clock tower and the charity subscriptions and the speeches about Manchester's brightening future, and the son would not ask what fed the furnace underneath.

Edmund had asked. Once. He was eleven, and the question had been simple enough — why did Mr. Cobbett keep two sets of pens? — and his father had looked at him for a long moment with an expression Edmund could not then name but recognized now, thirty years on from nothing at all, as the look of a man deciding how much weight a beam can carry before it must be replaced rather than trusted.

*Some accounts are not for you,* his father had said. *A mill runs on knowing what each man is for.*

He had been given the observatory instead. The master clock above the district, keeper of official time, the thing every loom and whistle and wage book in Ancoats deferred to. A beautiful gift, in its way. A boy who loved mechanisms was handed the largest one in sight and told to keep it running, and never told what it was *for*.

Edmund turned the page.

The figures were Cobbett's — he knew the hand immediately, the small severe numerals with their flattened tops, each digit written as though it resented being written. Three years back, to begin. October 1844. He ran his finger down the weavers' column, the spinners', the piecers', the scavengers' — children, those last, hired by the hour and paid by the hour, which made them the easiest arithmetic in the building.

And there it was.

Not shouting at him. Nothing in this room ever shouted. It was quieter than that: a discrepancy of four minutes per hour, folded into the rounding, disguised in the conventions of the trade where hours were reckoned to the nearest quarter and nobody — no clerk, no worker, no commissioner — carried a watch good enough to argue. Four minutes an hour, taken at the top of every hour worked, deducted through the observatory time that governed the whole district. Sixty-six working minutes stolen from every ten-hour day. Across nine hundred hands. Across three years.

Edmund did the multiplication twice, because the first answer seemed impossible, and the second answer was the same, and both answers were obscene.

*Eleven thousand pounds.* Give or take. Per year.

He sat very still. Somewhere below, the night shift was turning over; he could feel the mill breathing through the floorboards, the deep subsonic shudder of eight hundred looms that he had heard so long it had become silence to him, the way a man stops hearing his own heartbeat. Those looms were eating four minutes out of every hour of every life they touched, and the men and women feeding them were being paid for fifty-six minutes out of sixty, and the difference went — where? Not into wages. Not into the repair fund, which his father complained of quarterly. Into a column that reconciled itself perfectly against a clock that everyone trusted because clocks do not lie.

Clocks do not lie. *People lie to clocks.*

He thought of Marguerite Eastwick up in the observatory tonight, small and careful among the brass, doing honest work on dishonest machinery. He thought of the questions he had asked them — too many, too personal, he'd known even as he asked — about Fenwick's other commissions, about whether the old man kept records of repairs. He had wanted, without admitting it even to himself, to know whether anyone outside these walls already suspected what the mechanism had been made to remember.

Marguerite would find it. If they hadn't already. Anyone with a loupe and an uncorrupted eye would find file marks on an escapement within an hour of opening the case, and Marguerite Eastwick had the finest hands Edmund had ever watched work. He had stood in the workshop doorway that first evening watching them true a pivot, and the thought had come to him unbidden and unwelcome: *here is a person who cannot help telling the truth, because the truth is the only material they work in.*

Which meant the clockmaker was now in danger, and Edmund had summoned them into it himself. Or the commission had; or his father's signature; or the whole grinding apparatus of the place. It amounted to the same thing. He had walked them across the bridge into the cage.

*

His father found him there near midnight. Edmund heard the uneven tread on the stair — the old gout had worsened since autumn — and had just enough time to close the 1844 book before the door opened.

Josiah Harrow filled the doorway the way he filled every room: massively, unhurriedly, a man who had never in sixty years needed to hurry because the world had learned to wait. His eyes went to the desk, to the single guttering candle, to his son sitting among papers at an hour when sensible men were asleep.

"Burning the midnight oil over charity accounts?" His voice was gravel poured slow. "The orphan fund can keep till Thursday."

"Freight," Edmund said. "Cotton costs. I couldn't sleep."

His father grunted, satisfied or pretending to be, and stood a moment in the warm stink of the tallow light. He looked tired, Edmund thought. He looked like a monument that rain had gotten into.

"You've met the clockmaker," Josiah said. It was not a question. "Fenwick's apprentice. What's your read?"

"Competent."

"And discreet?"

The word hung between them like smoke. Edmund held his father's eyes and understood, in one cold descending moment, that this was the real reason for the visit — not charity accounts, not insomnia. His father wanted to know whether the person currently holding the district's official time in their two careful hands could be relied upon to see nothing.

"They talk about escapements," Edmund said. "Little else."

"Good." His father nodded slowly. "That's all a clock needs. Hands that don't ask what the hour is for." He paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and added, almost gently, in the tone he used for lessons he considered kindnesses: "You'll inherit all this one day, Edmund. Every gear of it. Best you learn early that a mill is not a family. It's a machine. Machines are kept by feeding them and cleaning them and never, ever asking them what they're thinking about."

Then he was gone, his tread receding down the stairs, and Edmund sat in the dark listening to the looms breathe, and felt the full weight of the inheritance settle onto him like wet wool.

*A machine.* Nine hundred people, a machine. Bess Carrick's brother, a component. Four minutes of every hour, lubrication.

He opened the 1844 book again.

* * *

He copied until his hand cramped and the candle drowned in its own wax pool and he had to light another from the stub, shielding the flame with his palm though no one was there to see. There was no one to see. That was the horror of it, he realized somewhere past two o'clock — the completeness of the solitude. Cobbett kept the ledgers locked by day, and his father never descended to the counting house because the counting house came to *him*, distilled into a single figure every Monday morning, and the workers never saw inside these walls except as names in columns, and the columns never saw anything at all.

No one was watching. Which meant no one had ever watched. Which meant this had gone on for years not because it was hidden well but because it was hidden in plain arithmetic, in a place where the only people motivated to look had been given no lantern and no invitation and no belief that looking would change anything.

Edmund copied the figures faithfully — the stated hours, the observatory-reckoned hours, the difference, week upon week upon week, three years of it — into a cheap notebook he had bought that afternoon from the stationer's on Great Ancoats Street, paying with coin so the purchase would appear nowhere. He wrote small. He wrote in a hand deliberately unlike his own. He did not know yet what the notebook was for. Evidence, perhaps. Confession, perhaps. A letter to a magistrate who would laugh at him. A document for a day he could not imagine arriving, when the machine might finally be asked what it was thinking about.

He burned nothing. That surprised him, how easily he decided it. A weaker version of himself — the version that had accepted the observatory keys gratefully at nineteen, the version that gave speeches about progress — would have burned it, would have called prudence what was really complicity, would have slept afterward the way his father slept, heavily and without dreams. But the fire would only make him cleaner-feeling, not cleaner, and the figures would still exist in Cobbett's fair copies and in the clock's own memory, filed into its teeth four minutes deep.

Let the paper live. Let it sit in his coat pocket like a stone until he found a river worth throwing it into — or a court worth reading it aloud in.

Toward dawn he stopped copying and simply sat, and let himself think the thoughts he had been declining to think all night.

That his father knew. Not suspected — *knew*. The skimming could not survive a quarter without the master's blessing; Cobbett was clever but he was not bold enough to steal eleven thousand pounds a year from Josiah Harrow on the gamble that Josiah Harrow never checked. No. The arrangement was older than Edmund's apprenticeship, probably older than Edmund. His father had decided long ago that four minutes an hour was the price of competitiveness, that the workers would never prove it, that God would accept the usual donations, and that his son need never be told because sons who are told become either accomplices or enemies, and Josiah Harrow had not yet decided which kind he had bred.

And beneath that knowledge, colder still: that the accident file — the loom, last spring, the Carrick boy whose sister delivered linens with her chin up and her grief folded away like good linen — that file would be in the study, in the locked drawer with the insurance correspondence, and it would say whatever it needed to say to keep the death outside working hours. Outside the hours the clock recorded. Outside the hours the dead boy had actually worked, because the dead boy had been on the machine at half past five in the morning, and the clock said the shift began at six, and the clock was the law here, and the clock had been lying for years.

Bess Carrick believed her brother died after hours — believed it, maybe, or wore believing it the way she wore everything, close to the skin and out of the wind. And the instrument of the lie sat above them all in its stone tower, keeping perfect false time, and Edmund had held its keys since he was nineteen.

He put his head in his hands. The second candle had burned down to nothing while he wasn't watching, and the first grey light was coming up over the yard, bleaching the ledgers to the color of bone.

Somewhere below, the morning shift bell would ring soon — late, by four minutes, by the clock's decree, and nine hundred people would lose another day's stolen fragment without ever feeling the knife. Edmund closed the notebooks, tucked the true copy inside his coat, and climbed the stairs toward the observatory, where a clockmaker with honest hands was working on the most dishonest mechanism in Manchester.

He would watch them today. Say little. Learn what they had seen.

And if Marguerite Eastwick had found the file marks — when they found them — Edmund intended to be standing close enough that, for the first time in his inheritance, someone inside these walls would not have to be silent alone.
