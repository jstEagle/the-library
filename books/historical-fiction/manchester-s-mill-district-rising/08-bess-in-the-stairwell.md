## 8. Bess in the Stairwell

### Marguerite

The stairwell of the observatory tower smelled of stone dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of the river — a cold mineral breath that rose through the walls as though the building itself were drinking. Marguerite had learned its rhythm in four days: the way the wind found the arrow slits on the third landing and made the candle flames lean east; the way each step rang hollow underfoot except the seventh from the bottom, which had cracked sometime in the last century and gave back a duller note.

They counted the steps without meaning to. It was what clockwork people did with anything repeated — measured it, divided it, made it obedient. Seven flights down to the yard door, forty-one steps each flight if you trusted the worn numerals scratched into the plaster by some apprentice dead before Victoria's reign. Marguerite trusted them. Numbers did not flinch when spoken to. Numbers did not look at you sideways across a dinner table while telling you, pleasantly, how quickly men could find work in Liverpool.

They were carrying the day's shavings out — brass and steel curled like apple peel, wrapped in cloth so no one would see how much material a true repair consumed — and thinking of nothing, which was a skill now, a discipline practiced hourly, when they heard footsteps above.

Not Cobbett's. Cobbett descended stairs the way he entered rooms: slowly, so that everyone had time to arrange themselves. Not Edmund's either; Edmund climbed like a man apologizing for taking up space, quick and light and always half-turned, as if he might yet change his mind about arriving.

These steps were quicker than both, and heavier, and accompanied by the soft clatter of a basket against a hip.

Marguerite pressed themselves flat against the cold wall at the fourth landing and let the figure come around the spiral — a young woman with flour-pale hands and a shawl the color of wet slate, hair escaping its cap in stubborn brown curls, and a wicker basket balanced on one hip stacked with folded linens so white they seemed to hold their own light in the gloom.

Bess Carrick.

She saw them and stopped, three steps up, so that their eyes were nearly level. The river-wind moved through the stairwell and lifted the top linens; she pinned them down with one flour-pale hand without looking away.

"You," she said. "The clock doctor."

"Apprentice," Marguerite said. "Marguerite Eastwick."

"Bess Carrick. Though you knew that." Her voice was not unkind. It was the voice of someone who had decided, after consideration, not to be afraid of this particular stranger, and who wanted credit for the decision. "You crossed the bridge the day it shut. I told you nothing good gets fixed inside these walls."

"You did."

"And here you are. Still inside." She came down two more steps, unhurried, and set the basket on her hip again. "Still fixing."

"The clock is my work."

"Mm." She looked them over the way her brother's generation looked over machinery — assessing load-bearing members, noting defects, deciding whether the whole would bear the strain put upon it. "You've got the look of someone counting something. Steps, is it?"

"Everything," Marguerite admitted, before the silence rules could close their throat. "It's a habit."

"Aye. My brother counted too. Spindles. Eleven hundred a shift, he'd count them off under his breath like a rosary, and God help the girl feeding his side if she ran two spindles short." Something moved behind her eyes then, quick as a shuttle — there and gone, but not gone enough. "He said numbers kept him sane. I think they kept him from thinking. There's a difference, and it took me years to learn it."

The wind leaned the candle flame between them. Marguerite held the bundle of shavings tighter and said nothing, because saying nothing was the first rule, the oldest rule, the rule that had kept Fenwick's workshop breathing through three winters of vanished apprentices —

— and heard themselves break it anyway.

"What was his name?" they asked.

Bess went very still. Not the stillness of suspicion — Marguerite knew that one intimately, wore it daily like an apron — but the stillness of a person who has carried a name alone for a long time and has just felt the weight of it shift, slightly, onto another pair of shoulders.

"Joseph," she said. "Joe Carrick. Second frame, spinning room, since he was nine years old. He'd have been twenty-two this March." She adjusted the basket. Her knuckles whitened on the handle and then deliberately loosened, finger by finger, like someone unclenching a fist by choice rather than by exhaustion. "You'll forgive me. I don't say it out loud much. In there—" she tipped her chin toward the mill block, its windows burning yellow through the snow-haze beyond the observatory's lancet windows, "—names are expensive. You spend one wrong and it costs your place, your references, sometimes your teeth. I learned which ones I could afford."

*Which silences keep people safe.* Fenwick's lesson, three years old, delivered in a whisper over a half-stripped movement while somewhere across town a colleague's lodgings stood empty with the rent paid a week in advance. Marguerite had built their whole life inside that lesson. And here was a weaver girl on a stairwell, twenty years old at most, holding the same lesson in her hands like something she'd woven herself.

"They know me here," Bess went on, quieter. "I bring the mended linens up Tuesdays. Tablecloths, bedding for the house. Nobody looks at a laundress twice — that's the whole trick of being a laundress, nobody ever looks at you twice." A ghost of a smile. "Same as your tools, I expect. Nobody fears a clockmaker. Clocks are furniture. Furniture can't testify."

Marguerite's blood went cold and stayed cold, the way water goes in January — not sharp, just absolute. "Can't it?"

"Oh, I don't know. I'm only a weaver." But her eyes had sharpened, and she glanced once toward the upper landing where the master clock's case stood behind its locked door, and Marguerite understood that Bess Carrick had been watching more than linens on her Tuesday rounds. "Only — folk talk. Even in the mill, even low. They say the master clock's been sweet-tempered lately. Never argues. Never runs fast." She paused. "Never runs true, neither, but who's going to say that out loud? The bell rings quarter days and the overseers check their watches against it, and the watches agree, and everyone's paid by the hour the clock says. Round and round. Lovely arithmetic, if you're the one selling hours instead of buying them."

The shavings rustled in their cloth. Somewhere far below, a door boomed shut and the sound climbed the stairwell like a slow animal.

"Four minutes," Marguerite said. Their own voice sounded foreign — too loud, too bare, a bone laid on a table.

Bess didn't ask how they knew. That was its own kind of answer, and they both heard it.

"A day," she finished softly. "Forty minutes a ten-hour shift, near enough. Times six hundred hands. Times six days." She had done the sums herself; you could hear it, the arithmetic worn smooth as her brother's spindle-count. "Cobbett calls it the standard rate. My mother called it theft, once, at the pay window, quiet-like. We lost our cottage over it inside a month." She lifted her chin. "So now I carry linen and keep my mouth shut and do my sums in my head, where no foreman can dock them."

Marguerite thought of the escapement under their fingers — the fine file marks running across the pallet faces like scratches on a confession, the hand that made them skilled enough to leave almost no burr, patient enough to remove even that. Someone had spent hours making those marks. Hours stolen from other hours. The craft turned inside out and used as a knife.

And now the knife had a name attached to everything it cut.

"How did he die?" Marguerite asked. "Your brother."

Bess's face did something complicated and brief — grief crossing it not like a cloud but like a shuttle, fast, threaded, leaving pattern behind. When she spoke, her voice was level in the way of things leveled by force.

"March the ninth. Last year. He was cleaning the second frame — you have to stop the line for that, or you're supposed to, but stopping the line costs money and Cobbett had instituted a bonus scheme for full shifts, so lines stopped less and less." Her thumb moved over the basket handle, back and forth, a small loom of its own. "A drive belt caught his sleeve. They got him out alive. He lived eleven hours." She said *eleven* the way Marguerite might read a dial. "Eleven hours in the infirmary, and my mother holding his hand, and him trying to speak and mostly getting air. Then he stopped."

The wind filled the pause. Marguerite found they were gripping the banister hard enough to hurt.

"The mill recorded it," Bess continued, "as happening outside working hours. That's the phrase they used. *Outside working hours.* As if Joe had wandered in of an evening, curious, and stuck his arm in a machine for pleasure. The accident book says he wasn't on the clock. Which means no compensation, no inquiry, and no blame — because a man not working can't be killed *by* the work, can he? Very tidy. Cobbett's writing, that entry. I'd know it anywhere; he wrote my mother's eviction notice too." She laughed, one dry note, terrible. "Same pen, likely."

"Someone must have signed—"

"The shift manager signed what Cobbett told him to sign. The surgeon signed what he was paid to sign. The coroner—" Bess shrugged, and the shrug contained a whole city, whole decades, whole graveyards. "Manchester's full of men whose signatures cost less than their conscience. You'd know that better than me, working up here."

"I don't—" Marguerite began, and stopped, because the denial was a lie and lies had weight, and they were carrying enough weight.

But Bess only studied them, head tilted, and something in her expression gentled — or hardened; with grief the two are often indistinguishable. "No," she said. "Maybe you don't. Maybe you're just a clockmaker standing in a stairwell with your brass shavings, and maybe the clock upstairs runs slow all on its own, out of sympathy for the workers." She picked up the basket. "Or maybe one day somebody asks you what you found in that case, and you'll have to decide what kind of silence yours is. Because there's two kinds, clock doctor. There's the silence that keeps you safe." She started up past them, then paused on the step above, close enough that Marguerite could see the fine cracks in her chapped lips, the exhaustion worn like a second cap. "And there's the silence that keeps *them* safe. Joe's got one of those now. Six feet of it, at Philips Park, with a stone that gives the wrong date of employment and no cause of death at all."

She climbed. The linens glowed white in the dark, rising like something ascending. At the turning of the spiral she looked back once.

"Tuesdays," she said. "I come Tuesdays. In case you ever need mending done."

Then she was gone, and the stairwell was only stone and wind again, and Marguerite stood holding their bundle of shavings — the small bright corpses of honest metal — and understood that something in them had shifted gear and could not be shifted back.

Above, faint through three floors of stone, the master clock ticked on. Four minutes slow. Lying to the district with every swing of its pendulum, and lying now, Marguerite thought, over a dead man's name.

They began to climb.

### Edmund

Edmund Harrow had taken to walking the long way to the observatory.

There was no practical reason for it. The covered passage from the house would serve, and in January the yard route was a punishment — wind off the frozen river finding every seam in a coat, sleet moving horizontally, the flagstones glazed black with ice. But the passage meant passing his father's study door, and the study door meant the possibility of his father's voice behind it, and lately Edmund could not hear that voice without hearing, underneath it, the ledger figures he had copied by candlelight over five nights and burned the drafts of, one page at a time, feeding them into the grate like letters to no one.

So he walked the yard. Let the weather punish him; punishment was familiar and clean.

He was crossing by the coal store when he saw the laundress come out of the observatory's yard door, basket on hip, and stand a moment in the snow with her face turned up to the grey sky as if checking whether it had changed its mind about anything. He knew her distantly — Carrick, one of the weaving-room Carricks, the family that had lost the cottage and then the son. His father's mills were full of such subtractions. Edmund had spent twenty-six years learning not to do the arithmetic.

He was doing it now constantly. It was like a toothache of the soul.

He had not meant to follow her. He told himself he was merely going the same direction, and then he told himself he wanted word of how the clockmaker was progressing, and then he stopped telling himself things, because the truth was simpler and worse: the laundress had come out of that tower wearing an expression Edmund recognized from his own mirror. The expression of a person who has just learned something and does not yet know what it will cost.

She saw him before he reached the door. She did not curtsy. Most servants did; the ones who didn't were either new or furious, and she was neither.

"Mr. Harrow," she said. Neutral as a scale weight.

"Carrick." He hesitated. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he did not brush it off; brushing it off would be a gesture, and gestures invited conversation. "You've been up to the tower."

"Linens, sir. Tuesdays." Her eyes were steady and dark and entirely unafraid, which unsettled him more than insolence would have. Servants' fear was the mortar of his whole world, and here was a woman standing in it without any.

"How is — Mr. Eastwick finding the work? The clock."

A flicker crossed her face. He watched it the way he'd learned to watch his father's manager across an accounts table: not the words, the weather behind them.

"Finding it fine, I'd say." A pause, deliberate as a dropped stitch. "Though clocks is like families, sir. What looks sound from outside can have a deal filed away inside."

And she walked on toward the laundry block, upright under her basket, leaving Edmund standing in the falling snow with his heart beating strangely and the sentence turning over in him like a mechanism he could not stop examining.

*Filed away.*

He knew what was filed away. He had known since the night he sat with the wage ledgers and the candles guttered and the figures refused, however he rearranged them, to mean anything innocent. Four minutes a day, compounded across six hundred hands across four years — he had checked the arithmetic eleven times, hating it, unable to disprove it, copying it out in his own neat hand until the copies themselves became evidence sitting in his desk drawer like a loaded thing.

And now a laundress spoke of filing, and looked at him with eyes that weighed him, and Edmund understood with a lurch that the secret was not sealed. Secrets in this compound leaked like the roof of the north shed. The workers knew. Perhaps not all, perhaps not precisely — but the shape of it moved among them the way cold moves through a wall, and every day he stood in the study door and said nothing, the leak widened, and the thing he was protecting by his silence was not his family's honor but Cobbett's theft and his father's convenience and, underneath, beneath everything, the fact that a young man named Joseph Carrick had died at a machine that should have been stopped, and the books said otherwise.

*Outside working hours.*

His father had used the phrase at dinner once, months ago, apropos of the compensation claim — dismissed it with the ease of a man brushing ash from a sleeve, and Edmund had passed the potatoes and said nothing, and the silence had fit him so comfortably that night, and now it fit like a coffin.

He pushed open the observatory's yard door. The stairwell swallowed him, stone-cold and lamplit, smelling of oil and metal, and he climbed toward the room where the district's time was kept and lied about.

Marguerite Eastwick was on the landing below the clock room, standing motionless with a cloth bundle in their arms, staring at nothing with the fixed intensity of someone listening to something very far away — or very near.

"You're pale," Edmund said. It came out blunter than intended.

"So are you." They looked at him, and their eyes had that quality he had begun to dread and crave in equal measure: the quality of a person measuring him, calibrating, reading the play of his gears. "Mr. Harrow. Do you know a weaver named Joseph Carrick?"

The name went through him like a bolt through cloth. "The accident. Last spring." He heard his own voice go carefully neutral and hated himself for the craft of it. "Why do you ask?"

"Because his sister just told me how he died," Marguerite said quietly, "and how the books record it. And I find I want to know whether you already knew — or whether you're going to pretend, the way everyone else pretends." A pause; the wind moaned in the arrow slit. "Because I've spent my life learning which silences keep people safe, Mr. Harrow. And I'm beginning to suspect that up here, in this house, every silence keeps the same people safe. And it isn't us."

Below them, faintly, the yard bell rang the half hour — rung, Edmund knew, by the master clock overhead, four minutes later than heaven's own accounting, faithful to its tampered heart.

He looked at the clockmaker, and the clockmaker looked back, and between them, unspoken, lay the whole weight of the tower: the ledgers in his desk, the escapement behind the locked door, the name *Joseph* hanging in the cold air like frost forming.

"My father's study," Edmund said at last, and his voice did not shake, though everything under it did. "Keeps the accident files. From the whole of last year." He drew a breath that tasted of stone and iron and decision. "Tomorrow, when he rides out to the Bolton mills, I'm going to open them."

Marguerite Eastwick said nothing at all.

And it was, Edmund thought, climbing the last flight beside them toward the ticking heart of the lie — it was the most eloquent silence he had ever heard.
