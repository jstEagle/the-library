## 9. The Repair Begins

Cobbett arrived at the observatory at half past seven by the master clock's own lying face, and Marguerite noted, with the detached precision that had kept them alive this long, that he did not look up at it. He looked at his pocket watch instead — a heavy gold-cased thing, no doubt kept true in some private drawer where honesty was permitted.

"Miss Eastwick." He said *miss* the way other men said *boy*, and Marguerite had learned years ago not to correct either. "You'll work under my eye. Tools laid out each morning. Nothing leaves this room. Nothing enters it without passing through me."

"Understood."

"You'll find I'm a reasonable man." He smiled, and the smile had all the warmth of a ledger line. "Reasonable men are the easiest to work for, so long as one remembers what is reasonable and what is not."

Marguerite nodded and opened the tool chest. The brass fittings caught the grey morning light through the observatory's tall windows, and below, through the frost-furred glass, the mill yard lay steaming with early labour — hundreds of figures funneling toward the gates, breath rising from them like smoke from a hundred small fires. Somewhere down there, Bess Carrick was tying on her apron. Somewhere down there, four minutes of every hour were being quietly taken out of every wage like coins lifted from a sleeping man's pocket.

*Don't ask. Don't repeat. Don't look up.*

But looking down, Marguerite thought, had never been covered by the rules.

The clock's case stood open now, panels removed and stacked against the wall like coffin lids. Inside, the movement rose in its brass cathedral — great wheels above, the escapement cradled at the heart like something a surgeon would guard with both hands. Marguerite had spent two evenings mapping it in memory: every tooth, every pivot, every place where a file had been dragged across steel by someone who knew exactly what they were doing.

Cobbett settled into a chair near the door with a newspaper he did not read. Marguerite felt his attention the way one feels weather coming — pressure behind the eyes, a change in the air.

"Begin," he said.

Marguerite began.

* * *

The official repair was, in truth, a restoration. That was what Cobbett wanted, though he would never say it aloud: return the clock to the state he had paid to have it put into. The sabotaged escapement ran four minutes slow per day because someone had altered the pallets' geometry — shaved the locking faces, shifted the impulse just enough that each swing of the pendulum surrendered a fraction more time than it should. A beautiful crime. Marguerite could almost admire it. Almost, if not for knowing what those stolen minutes bought: threadbare dinners, docked wages, a dead weaver recorded as having died off the clock.

So Marguerite cleaned. Marguerite oiled. Marguerite adjusted the suspension spring within the narrow tolerances the sabotage allowed, and each adjustment made the clock run *truer to its lie*, and each gesture felt like pressing a coin into a thief's palm with one's own warm hand.

"There," Cobbett said on the first afternoon, when the beat error came back within the falsified range. "You see? It only wanted care. Most things do." He wrote something in a small black book. "You have your father's trade in your fingers, I think."

"Fenwick is my master, not my father."

"A distinction without much difference, in my experience." He turned a page of his unread paper. "Though fathers can be let go more easily than masters. There are laws about apprentices."

Marguerite's hands did not stop moving. That was the first discipline of the workshop: the hands keep working when everything else wants to shake. They burnished a pivot until it shone like a drop of water.

"I'll need fresh oil for the great wheels before the week is out," Marguerite said. "And wire stock for replacements. And a small quantity of hard brass — the pallet arbor's bearing shows wear, and I'd rather machine a bushing than leave it."

Cobbett considered. Marguerite watched him consider, and understood that this was the true examination — not the repair but the requests. What would the apprentice ask for? Would they ask for too little, out of fear? Too much, out of ambition?

"Brass," he said at last. "I'll have some sent up. Rod stock, quarter-inch." He smiled again. "Nothing larger. One doesn't need anything larger for bushings."

*He knows,* Marguerite thought, as the man's footsteps faded down the stair. *Or he suspects, or he simply enjoys saying it.* The words sat in the chest like swallowed ice.

That night, alone in the narrow room above the stables where the compound's tradesfolk were billeted, Marguerite took the quarter-inch brass rod from the supply crate and began to saw.

* * *

The work of making a true escapement part in secret is unlike any other work. It cannot be done quickly, for speed leaves marks. It cannot be done at night only, for night-work has a different rhythm than day-work, and any inspector worth his salt — and Cobbett was nothing if not thorough — would notice a part finished too fast. So Marguerite split the labor the way a counterfeiter splits a coin: a little honest metal here, a little hidden metal there.

By day, under Cobbett's supervision, Marguerite restored the false geometry. By lamplight after supper, with the door barred and a blanket nailed over the window crack, Marguerite machined the replacement: a new pair of pallets cut to the *correct* angles, calculated from the clock's original specification, which Marguerite had found scratched into the inside of the case by some long-dead maker who had perhaps suspected, even then, that someday his work would need defending.

The file sang softly. Outside, the wind came off the river carrying grit and coal-smoke, and somewhere below, the night watchman called the hours — wrong hours, Marguerite noted, hours read off the poisoned clock, so that even the darkness of Manchester kept the mill's time now.

On the third evening there came a knock.

Marguerite went still, file poised above the brass. The knock came again — twice, unhurried, no urgency in it. Only one person in this compound knocked like that, as though the door belonged to him.

Marguerite swept the part, the files, the lamp behind the blanket-draped shelf and opened the door.

Edmund Harrow stood in the corridor with a candle in one hand and a book in the other, dressed plainly enough that in another district he might have passed for a clerk. His eyes went past Marguerite's shoulder, unhurried, reading the room the way Marguerite read a movement — pivots, mainsprings, points of failure.

"The library sent the wrong volume," he said. "I'm told you asked after treatises on horology. This one covers escapements." He held it out. "Bordon's. It's the standard."

Marguerite took it. Their fingers did not touch; Edmund had arranged that, offering the book by its spine.

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't call me sir." He glanced at the shelf with the blanket over it — one glance, mild as milk — and then away. "I come to the observatory most evenings. My father believes I'm studying accounts. In a sense I am." He paused at the threshold. "You work carefully, Miss Eastwick. Most people work fast and call it careful afterward. You don't."

He left before Marguerite could decide whether that was praise or warning.

Marguerite closed the door, leaned against it, and listened to his footsteps descend the stair. Then, slowly, they drew out Bordon's treatise and set it beside the hidden escapement part, and understood that the book was a message in itself: *I know what chapter you're living in.*

They did not sleep well after that.

* * *

Day five brought the cold snap. Frost climbed the observatory windows in ferns, and the great pendulum swung in air gone thick and sluggish, and Cobbett arrived wearing a fur collar that made him look like something preserved from an earlier, crueler century.

"Progress?" he asked.

"On schedule. The clock will be ready for the audit."

"Good." He walked the perimeter of the movement, gloved finger hovering an inch from the brass, never touching. He stopped at the escapement. "You've been studying this part particularly."

"It's the heart of the thing. One studies the heart."

"Mm." He straightened. "My late predecessor in the manager's office had a saying: *the heart is where men hide their mistakes.* Or their intentions." He smiled down at Marguerite, and the smile was pleasant, and pleasantness from Cobbett had become, in five days, the most frightening sound in the compound. "I trust yours are clean."

"As brass, sir."

"Brass tarnishes." He settled into his chair. "Carry on."

Marguerite carried on. And beneath the floorboards of that long day, beneath the rhythm of files and oils and the pendulum's slow metronome, a second clock ticked: the counting of the secret part's progress. Two days' cutting remained. One evening of polishing. Fitting would take an hour, no more — but the fitting could not happen until the moment Marguerite chose, and choosing that moment had become, without Marguerite quite deciding it, the central act of their life.

Every honest gesture now carried a dishonest weight. When Cobbett praised the truing of a wheel, Marguerite heard the praise land on fraud. When the clock struck the hour — always late, always gently late, like a liar with good manners — Marguerite flinched inwardly, complicit in the sound. Even the tea Cobbett had sent up, unasked, tasted of obligation. There was no clean surface left anywhere in the work. Marguerite had spent a lifetime learning which silences keep people safe, and now understood that silence, kept too long, stops protecting and starts preserving — pickling a person whole inside their own caution.

* * *

Edmund came that evening while Cobbett dined below with his father. He did not knock twice this time; he knocked once, softly, and entered when Marguerite answered, closing the door behind him.

For a while he simply watched. Marguerite worked on — there was nothing else to do with one's hands in this place — fitting a bushing to the great wheel's arbor, reaming it true, checking the fit with calipers that whispered against brass.

"You hate it," Edmund said finally.

"I'm doing my work."

"You hate it. I've been watching you hate it for five days." He moved closer to the open movement, and his reflection ghosted across the polished wheel faces, doubled and distorted. "My father thinks I come here to escape the ledgers. He's half right. I come here because it's the only room in this compound where something is being done honestly."

Marguerite's hands paused. Resumed. "Is it?"

"That depends entirely on you, doesn't it." He said it quietly, without accusation, and that was somehow worse. "Four minutes a day. Do you know what that is, over a year? It's a fortnight's wages per worker. It's—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. "It's arithmetic. I've been doing a great deal of arithmetic lately."

Marguerite said nothing. *Don't ask. Don't repeat.* But Edmund Harrow was standing close enough that the rules trembled on their foundations.

"The last apprentice Fenwick had," Edmund said, still watching the wheels turn, "went to Liverpool. Did Cobbett tell you that?"

"He mentioned it."

"He didn't mention that the man wrote letters for six months and then stopped. Or that the last letter said the millowners' association had agreed among themselves never to hire him again. He repairs fishing tackle now. On the docks." Edmund turned then, and looked at Marguerite directly, and his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "I'm telling you this because I don't know why I'm telling you this. Because you're the only person in this building who touches the mechanism and doesn't flinch, and I need—"

He didn't finish. He looked at the escapement instead, hanging in its cradle, running its slow false time, and Marguerite watched him understand it — really see it, perhaps for the first time — the way one sees a familiar face suddenly aged.

"Good night, Miss Eastwick," he said at last, and left the book's successor on the table without a word, and his footsteps went down the stairs slower than they had come up.

Marguerite stood a long while in the lamplight, the coat lining heavy against their ribs where the nearly-finished part rode flat and secret as a second heartbeat.

Below in the yard, the watchman called the midnight hour — wrong, as always, four minutes adrift from the truth, and no one in all the sleeping district stirred or questioned it.

Except, Marguerite thought, reaching for the file, perhaps one.
