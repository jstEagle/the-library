## 10. Cobbett's Courtesy

The invitation arrived on a card of heavy cream stock, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than Bess Carrick likely earned in a week. *Mr. Cobbett requests the pleasure of your company at dinner, Thursday next, seven o'clock, the Counting House.* No question mark anywhere in it. It was not an invitation; it was an announcement wearing invitation's clothes.

Marguerite turned it over twice, as though the reverse might contain an escape clause. The back held only the Harrow Mills crest — a wheel within a wheel, the outer one turning the inner — and beneath it, embossed so lightly one had to tilt it to the light to read: *Time Is Our Bond.*

They had been inside the compound nine days. The bridge had closed four days ago.

* * *

Fenwick would have said nothing about such a card. Fenwick would have simply looked at Marguerite over the top of his spectacles for a long moment, then returned to his bench, and that look would have contained an entire lecture. But Fenwick was on the far side of a frozen river, and Marguerite was alone in the narrow room above the observatory's clock chamber with a card in their hand and a true escapement part hidden in a false bottom of their tool chest, and no one to tell them what the silence meant.

They dressed carefully. Not finely — fine clothes on an apprentice would be an insult, a presumption, a thing Cobbett could punish with a raised eyebrow — but cleanly. The good shirt with the collar that still held its shape. The coat brushed free of brass filings. They checked their hands three times under the lamp, scrubbing at the permanent grey shadow in the creases of their knuckles, the mark every metalworker carried like a baptism they hadn't chosen.

At six minutes to seven — because arriving early was eagerness, and late was insolence, and only exactness was safe — Marguerite knocked at the door of the counting house.

* * *

The counting house was not what they expected. From outside it was a squat brick building, unremarkable among the mill's outbuildings, but inside it had been made into a small palace of order. Ledgers lined the walls in oak cases, each spine numbered in gold leaf. A fire burned behind a brass fender. The lamps were shaded with green glass so that everything they touched took on the colour of banknotes.

And at the head of a table set for two, Cobbett rose to greet them like an old friend.

"Master Eastwick." He pronounced the name carefully, giving it its full weight, and offered his hand. His grip was dry and precise, calibrated exactly between warmth and authority. "You honour us."

He was smaller than Marguerite remembered from the workshop floor. In the clock chamber he had stood with his hands clasped behind him like a man inspecting a regiment; here, in lamplight and good tailoring, he seemed almost gentle. His face was smooth and pale as the inside of a shell, and his eyes were the palest blue Marguerite had ever seen — eyes with almost no colour in them at all, like water left standing too long in a glass.

"You've done remarkable work," he said, guiding them to a chair. "Remarkable. That clock has been a trial to us for years. Sluggish. Unreliable. A timekeeper that cannot keep time — you can see the embarrassment." He smiled as he said it, a small closed smile, and poured wine without asking. "But you have it nearly right again, I think. Nearly right."

Marguerite heard the words land strangely. *Nearly right.* As though the clock were a stubborn animal being broken to harness rather than a mechanism that had been deliberately maimed. They kept their face where Fenwick had taught them to keep it: attentive, pleasant, empty as polished glass.

"The escapement wanted cleaning," Marguerite said. "There's wear in the pallets. I'm fitting new ones."

"So I understand." Cobbett settled across from them and lifted his glass. "To honest work."

Marguerite touched their glass to his. The wine was French, dark and expensive, and tasted of nothing they could name except money.

* * *

The meal came in courses, brought by a servant who never met anyone's eyes. Partridge. A soup the colour of amber. Bread still warm from an oven somewhere in the compound Marguerite had never been permitted to see. Cobbett talked throughout, pleasantly, about everything and nothing — the weather, the price of coal, the lamentable state of the roads since the frost set in — and each subject, however innocent, curved gently back toward the mill, toward the clock, toward Marguerite's own hands.

"A craftsman of your quality," he said, carving the partridge with surgical neatness, "should not be buried in a back-street workshop off Chapel Row. No offence to Master Fenwick — a sound man, sound — but there is only so far a sound man can carry a talent like yours." He laid a slice of breast meat on Marguerite's plate with the tenderness of a man feeding something he intended to keep. "Harrow Mills rewards discretion. I want you to know that. We notice who keeps quiet and who does not, and we remember."

"I try to do my work and speak little," Marguerite said. It was the truth, which made it easy to say.

"Yes." Cobbett looked up sharply at that, and for half a second the pleasantness dropped away entirely, the way a shopkeeper's smile drops when he counts coins, and Marguerite saw underneath it something quick and cold doing arithmetic. Then the smile returned. "Yes, exactly so. You try to do your work and speak little. I find that admirable in these days of agitation. There are men going round the district — agitators, chartists, troublemakers of every stripe — telling the workers their hours are stolen, their wages shorted, that the very clocks are against them." He laughed softly at his own figure. "Superstition. But superstition spreads fast in cold weather. Which is why a reliable clock matters so much, Master Eastwick. One clock, keeping one true time, that every man in this district must accept. Do you see? Order begins in the escapement."

*I see,* Marguerite thought. *I see precisely.* They ate the partridge and said nothing, and the nothing sat in their chest like swallowed stone.

* * *

It was over the cheese that Cobbett doubled the fee.

He did it casually, the way a man might pass the salt. "I've reviewed your master's estimate," he said, dabbing his lips with linen. "We consider it inadequate. You will be paid double. Half now, half when the audit inspection concludes." He slid an envelope across the table. Through the paper Marguerite could feel the weight of notes, more money than they had held at once in their life.

"Double," Marguerite repeated. The word sounded wrong in the warm room.

"Certainly. Quality work deserves quality payment. And—" he paused, and the pause was a door opening onto a room Marguerite did not want to enter, "—there are other considerations. After the audit, when the clock is certified, there may be further commissions. Regular work. A retainer, even. The firm values continuity." He refilled both glasses himself, waving off the servant. "Continuity is everything, in my experience. Workshops rise and fall. Apprentices come and go. But the man who keeps the district's time — ah, he becomes indispensable. He becomes safe."

*Safe.* The word went through Marguerite like a needle finding the gap between ribs.

"And if the work should prove... beyond me?" they asked. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "If I should fail to satisfy?"

Cobbett considered this with genuine-looking care, tilting his head as though the question were a difficult piece of mechanism he admired.

"Oh, failure happens," he said kindly. "It happens to everyone. Why—" he set down his glass and leaned back, gazing into the green-shaded lamplight with the air of a man sharing a fond memory, "—only last year, or was it two years ago now, Master Fenwick lost an assistant. Bright young fellow. Very promising hands. And after some difficulty — I never learned quite what — he found work in Liverpool almost immediately. Remarkably quickly, considering how hard the trade is these days. Someone had spoken for him, I believe. Opened doors. He wrote back once, I'm told, grateful as anything." Cobbett smiled at Marguerite over the rim of his glass. "So you see, even failure, in this house, is provided for. Nobody starves. Nobody disappears. Doors open. All it requires is a certain flexibility."

The fire ticked. Somewhere above them, faint through the ceiling, Marguerite could hear the great clock's pendulum swinging in its chamber — *tick, tock, tick* — but wrong, wrong by four minutes in every hour, and they thought: *he knows I know. He is telling me he knows I know, and telling me he does not mind, so long as I am flexible.*

"Liverpool," Marguerite said.

"Liverpool," Cobbett agreed warmly. "A fine city. Docks. Opportunity. Though of course—" and here he reached across and briefly, paternally, covered Marguerite's hand with his own, his palm cool and dry as paper, "—one hopes you'll be with us a very long while. One hopes there'll be no need of Liverpool at all."

He withdrew his hand and rang a small bell, and the servant appeared to clear the plates, and the moment folded itself away as neatly as a letter into its envelope.

* * *

Dessert was a compote of preserved cherries, out of season, crimson as something wounded. Cobbett ate his slowly and spoke of his father — dead these ten years, a toll-keeper on the turnpike, "a man who understood that people will pay almost anything for certainty of passage" — and of the elder Mr. Harrow, whom he praised in terms so flat and frequent they curdled into parody. Marguerite answered when answers were required. Mostly they watched.

They watched the way Fenwick had taught them to watch mechanisms: not the show of it, but the working parts. And the working parts of Cobbett were these — that he never asked a direct question, because a direct question creates evidence; that every kindness he performed was performed in front of witnesses, the servant, the fire, the ledgers in their gilt-numbered rows; that he had told them about Liverpool not as a threat but as a *favour*, so that when they lay awake at night they would not be able to say *he threatened me*, only *he was kind to me, and I was afraid.*

That was the genius of the man. He built his terror the way a watchmaker builds a movement: every component deniable, the whole assembly unmistakable.

When Marguerite rose to leave, Cobbett accompanied them to the door and stood holding it open against the cold.

"One more thing," he said, breath fogging in the lamplight spilling past him. "The audit inspection comes in eleven days. The commissioners' man will wind the clock, check it against his chronometer, and sign the certificate. After that signature, everything is settled for another quarter. Wages calculated. Accounts balanced. Peace maintained." He smiled his small closed smile. "I mention it only because craftsmen sometimes grow nervous before inspections, and nervousness makes for clumsy hands. Don't be nervous, Master Eastwick. Do exactly what you have been doing. Nothing more. Nothing less. And in spring, when the bridge opens, you may walk home to Master Fenwick with your wages and my gratitude, and this whole winter will be a story you tell over ale for the rest of your life."

He extended his hand. Marguerite took it. His skin was cold from standing in the doorway, and his grip, again, was exact.

"Goodnight," Cobbett said. "Sleep well. You've earned it."

* * *

Marguerite walked back through the compound under a sky the colour of wet slate. Snow had begun again, fine and dry, hissing faintly where it touched the lamps. The mill loomed black against the blacker river, its windows dark now, its chimneys breathing smoke into the frozen air. Somewhere a dog barked twice and stopped, as though corrected.

In their room above the clock chamber, they lit no lamp at first. They stood in the dark listening to the pendulum below, feeling the false time of the building seep up through the floorboards into their bones, and they took out the cream-coloured card and read it again by the light that came through the window from the yard.

*Mr. Cobbett requests the pleasure of your company.*

Pleasure. Courtesy. Doubled fees and warm bread and a hand patted over theirs like a father's — and underneath all of it, running through it like wire through wax, the message plain as a stamped serial number: *I know what you found. I know you know. Take the money. Keep the hours false. Or Liverpool.*

Or worse than Liverpool. Because the last assistant had written back once, grateful — and Fenwick never spoke of him at all, and the colleague three years gone had not written anything, ever again, to anyone.

Marguerite put the card down and opened the false bottom of the tool chest.

The true escapement lay where they had hidden it, wrapped in chamois, machined over six nights by candlelight with files worked slow and soft as breathing. They unwrapped it and held it to the window's grey light. New brass, bright as a promise. Pallets cut true. It weighed almost nothing in their palm and everything else besides.

Eleven days until the commissioners' man wound the clock and signed the certificate. Eleven days of dinners perhaps, of courtesy, of velvet. Eleven days to decide whether they were the kind of creature that could be bought with warm bread — or whether somewhere in the arithmetic of stolen minutes there was a debt that could only be paid in truth.

Below them the pendulum swung, patient and wrong. Marguerite wrapped the escapement away, and began, quietly, to count.
