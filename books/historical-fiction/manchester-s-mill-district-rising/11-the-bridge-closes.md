## 11. The Bridge Closes

The ice came in the night, the way winter's worst decisions always do — quietly, without negotiation, while everyone slept.

Marguerite heard it before they understood it: a sound from the river like a great door groaning shut, iron plates shifting against their moorings, and beneath it a low grinding that was not wind and not machinery but water itself surrendering to cold. They lay in the narrow bed in the observatory's undercroft, where Cobbett had lodged them for the duration of the repair — *for your convenience*, he had said, and Marguerite had heard the other meaning folded inside it like a note hidden in a seam — and listened to the river die.

By morning it was done. From the observatory's high window, above the clock case with its cathedral of brass, Marguerite could see the whole grey length of the Irwell locked under white, and the bridge — that latticed spine of iron that had carried them across three weeks ago with a tool chest on their shoulder and snow beginning to fall — standing closed at both ends. Not broken. Not damaged. Simply *closed*, its gates barred with fresh timber, a lantern hung at each approach burning yellow through the morning fog. Two men in mill coats stood at the near gate, not guarding it exactly. Just standing there. Which was worse.

*Seasonal closure*, the notice said, nailed to the gatehouse in a clerk's careful hand. *By order of the River Commissioners. Until thaw.*

Marguerite had lived in Manchester long enough to know which orders were real and which were written by men who owned the paper they were printed on. The River Commissioners were real men with real seals. But the timing — the timing was Cobbett's. The quarter audits fell next week. The bridge always closed eventually; every year it did, and every year the district held its breath until thaw. But this year it had closed four days after Marguerite had begun asking quiet questions about the escapement, and two days after Edmund Harrow had been seen carrying papers out of his father's study at an hour when honest sons were asleep.

Coincidence was a currency too, in the mill district. It was simply one most people couldn't afford.

* * *

They went down to the gatehouse anyway, because there is a difference between knowing a door is shut and standing before it, and because some part of Marguerite still believed — stubbornly, foolishly, the way an apprentice believes a seized mechanism will turn if you only love it enough — that doors could be reasoned with.

The snow had stopped but the cold remained, a physical presence, pressing on the ears, stiffening the breath. The river below the bridge was a white plain scored with dark seams, and out in the middle of it a cart stood abandoned, tilted, its horse long since led away. Someone had tried to cross in the night. Someone had learned what Marguerite was learning now.

"You'll want to go back, then," said one of the men at the gate. He was young, red-knuckled, with the flattened vowels of the spinning rooms. He didn't look at Marguerite when he spoke. He looked at the middle distance, the way mill people looked at overseers' faces — directly enough to show respect, indirectly enough to show nothing else.

"I have work unfinished," Marguerite said. "My master will be expecting word."

"Word goes by the post coach, and the post coach goes over the bridge." The young man shrugged, an eloquent shrug, a shrug that contained an entire education in how things were. "Thaw's usually six weeks. Could be eight."

"Could be longer?"

"Could be." Now he did look, briefly, and in that look was something that might have been pity or might have been warning; in the mill district the two expressions shared a face. "Best get warm, clockman. Ice doesn't care who's waiting on t'other side."

Marguerite stood a moment longer, feeling the cold find its way through coat and waistcoat and shirt to the skin beneath, and understood, with the slow certainty of a weight settling onto a balance, that this was not weather. This was architecture. The bridge had not closed; it had been *closed*, the way a hand closes around a thing it means to keep. No leaving. No letter to Fenwick — or none that would arrive unopened, and both of them knew what happened to letters that arrived opened. No retreat down the stairwell of the world back to the cramped workshop and the smell of oil and the rules that had kept them alive so long: *don't ask, don't repeat, don't look up*.

The rules had kept them alive. But rules made for crossing bridges are poor shelter once the bridge is gone.

* * *

Bess Carrick found them on the return walk, or perhaps she had arranged to — Bess was a woman who arranged things quietly, mended linens appearing in the right stairwells, warnings delivered with the plausible deniability of laundry talk. She was coming up from the weaving sheds with a basket on her hip, her shawl drawn tight, her breath smoking.

"So it's shut," she said. Not a question. She'd seen Marguerite at the gate from the shed window; half the district probably had. Watching was free entertainment in a place where everything else cost wages.

"It's shut."

"And you're still here." She shifted the basket. "I told you. Nothing good gets fixed inside those walls."

"You also told me your brother—" Marguerite stopped. In the open yard, with the sheds breathing their steam around them and the foremen walking their rounds, even grief had to be carried carefully. "You told me about the record. About the hours."

Something moved behind Bess's eyes, quick as a shuttle. "Aye. I did." She glanced toward the nearest foreman, measured the distance, lowered her voice until it was nearly part of the wind. "And now you can't run off and think about it somewhere safe. That's what he'd have wanted, that Cobbett. Everyone who asks questions sealed in till thaw, like jam."

"Bess—"

"No." She said it sharply, then softened it, because softness was her armor the way silence was Marguerite's. "No, I'm not sorry I told you. I'm not sorry for anything I've said in that stairwell. You want to know the truth of it?" She stepped closer, close enough that Marguerite could see the fine cotton dust caught in her eyelashes, glittering in the flat light like frost. "Six weeks is a long time. Eight is longer. A man could learn a lot about a clock in eight weeks. A man could learn what makes it lie."

She hoisted the basket, turned, and walked back toward the sheds, and Marguerite watched her go — small and straight-backed against the vast brick geometry of the mill, a single figure refusing to be scaled down by the architecture — and felt something shift in their chest that had no name yet but had weight, and the weight was not fear.

Or not only fear.

* * *

The afternoon passed in the observatory, doing the work that was meant to be seen. Cobbett had established a rhythm of supervision that was almost tender in its attentiveness: he came at ten, at two, and at five, each visit announced by his particular tread on the iron stairs, each visit consisting of the same circuit — a glance at the movement, a glance at Marguerite's hands, a pleasant remark about the weather or the fee or Master Fenwick's reputation, and a departure that somehow left the room colder than his arrival. He never touched anything. He didn't need to. His eyes did all his handling for him.

Today he lingered at the case, watching Marguerite fit the sabotaged escape wheel into its place with the false teeth angles that shaved four minutes from every day.

"Beautiful work," he said, meaning his own. "Fenwick trains them well. Steady hands. And discreet." He smiled at the movement, not at Marguerite. "Discretion, Mr. Eastwick, is the rarest quality in a craftsman. Talent is common as dirt. But a man who sees what he sees and says what he's told to say — that's worth double any fee." He straightened his cuffs. "Thaw will come soon enough. Best not to wish your life away waiting on it."

He went down the stairs. Marguerite sat very still among the brass, listening to the tread fade, and thought: *he knows the bridge closed before I did. He counted on it. He has been counting on it since the day he filed that wheel.*

And underneath the thought, quieter, colder, another thought rising through it like water finding its level:

*Then use it.*

Because here was the arithmetic no ledger recorded: if Marguerite could not leave, could not send word, could not retreat — then neither could the truth leave. It was sealed in here with them. And a sealed thing, given time and pressure, does not dissipate. It concentrates. Fenwick had taught them that too, though he'd never said it aloud: a movement sealed in a case, wound and patient, will keep its time through anything. Through neglect. Through weather. Through the indifference of the whole world.

Eight weeks was not a prison sentence. Eight weeks was a *run time*.

* * *

That night, after the observatory lamps were officially out and the undercroft was officially dark, Marguerite worked.

They had cleared a space weeks ago — a corner of the sleeping cell behind the coal store, where the wall's damp kept any listening ear at a distance of two bricks and the pipe's warmth masked the small sounds of files. The true escape wheel lay disassembled there in a chamois roll, its parts disguised among spare pieces of ordinary repair stock: a wheel blank that was supposedly destined for a kitchen clock, pinions that were supposedly replacements for the turret's strike train. Nothing that would raise an eyebrow if discovered, because nothing was what it appeared to be only when assembled, and assembly required a bench, and benches were watched.

So Marguerite had built a bench that wasn't one. An old packing crate, its lid hinged, its interior fitted with two wooden clamps and a strip of leather to deaden sound. Closed, it was luggage. Open, working by the light of a candle shielded behind a tin plate with a slit cut in it — a watchmaker's trick, old as the trade, light for the hands and darkness for the watchers — it was the smallest, most secret workshop in Manchester.

Tonight they opened it.

The cold made the work harder; brass shrinks and steel grows brittle, and fingers that cannot feel their tools are dangerous fingers. Marguerite worked slowly, warming their hands at intervals over the pipe, flexing them, testing sensation against a needle point before touching file to metal. Outside, the wind worried at the observatory dome with a sound like someone patiently trying keys in a lock. Below, the district murmured in its sleep — the engines banked, the looms stilled, ten thousand people dreaming in rooms heated by the same coal that warmed the master's house.

Four minutes a day. Marguerite turned the false wheel in the candlelight, studying the file marks Cobbett's craftsman had left — skilled marks, beautiful marks, marks that would have delighted Marguerite in any other context, and that was the obscenity of it. Somewhere a craftsman as good as Marguerite, trained in the same tradition of patience and precision, had taken this wheel and made it lie, and had probably been paid well, and had probably slept soundly, and was probably at this moment warm somewhere, while Daniel Carrick's hours dissolved into the fiction of a slow clock and a dead weaver's last day shrank by four minutes at a time until the record showed he had never been there at all.

*I see what I see,* Marguerite thought, filing, fitting, checking the tooth profile against the template they had cut from memory of the original drawings. *And I will say what I choose to say.*

The distinction mattered. It was the whole distance between survival and complicity, and Marguerite had spent three years walking it in the dark without knowing there was a difference. Don't ask. Don't repeat. Don't look up. Fine rules for staying alive. But Bess had stood in the stairwell with cotton dust in her lashes and put a face into the arithmetic — a brother, a loom, a spring morning recorded as happening outside working hours — and now the silences had weights, and weighted things move.

The last tooth took shape under the file. Marguerite held the finished wheel to the candlelight, turning it, letting the flame's reflection travel around the rim like a tiny captive sunrise. True angles. Honest pitch. A wheel that would drive the escapement at the rate the heavens actually turned, that would make the master clock agree with the stars instead of with Cobbett's ledgers.

It was not installed. Installation was a different problem, requiring the audit inspection, requiring witnesses, requiring a moment when the clock's case would stand open before men whose signatures meant something. Marguerite had begun, without quite admitting it, to plan not an escape but a *demonstration*. To think not in terms of getting out but of getting it *seen*. The difference filled the little cell like warmth filling a room.

They wrapped the wheel in its chamois, nested it in the crate among the innocent spare parts, and blew out the candle. In the darkness the wind went on working at the dome, and the river lay locked and silent under its ice, and somewhere across that ice, in a workshop that smelled of oil and patience, an old man was presumably telling himself his apprentice was safe.

Marguerite lay in the dark and did not correct him, and did not sleep, and was not afraid.

Not only afraid.

Because the bridge was closed, yes. The trap was shut. But a clock, Marguerite thought, staring into the black, is also a sealed thing — a case with a truth inside it, keeping its time regardless of what the weather does. And thaw comes. Thaw always comes. And when the ice went out and the gates swung open and the commissioners' coach rolled across the renewed bridge for the quarter audit, the master clock of Harrow Mills would begin, for the first time in years, to tell the truth — and Marguerite intended to be standing beside it when it did, with the filed wheel in one hand and the arithmetic of stolen minutes in the other, ready to read both aloud.

Let Cobbett count the days until thaw.

Marguerite was counting something else.

Above them, faint through floors and stone, the master clock struck two — four minutes slow, lying to the sleeping district as it had lied for years — and Marguerite listened to the lie, all eleven strokes of a twelve-stroke hour arriving late and wrong, and smiled in the dark like a person setting a watch.

Soon, the mechanism promised. Soon.
