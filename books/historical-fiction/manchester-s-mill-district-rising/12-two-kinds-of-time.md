## 12. Two Kinds of Time

Edmund had learned to read his father's house the way other men read weather. The creak of the third stair meant Cobbett making his rounds. The slam of the counting-house door before nine meant the ledgers had balanced badly and someone would pay for it by morning. And the particular quality of silence that fell over the compound after the servants retired — a silence like held breath, like the whole estate lying very still so no one would notice it — meant it was safe to move.

He had not always known how to use these silences. For years he had simply lived inside them, the way a fish lives inside water, unthinking. But since the night he had sat copying figures by candlelight — Cobbett's figures, his father's figures, four minutes of every hour shaved off the lives of six hundred people with the tidy arithmetic of a man balancing his own importance — he had begun to move through the house differently. Deliberately. Like a thief in his own inheritance.

Which was, he supposed, exactly what he was.

Tonight the silence was deep enough to walk on. Snow had come again at dusk, fat slow flakes that muffled the yard and turned the lamplit windows of the mill into smudges of amber, and now the whole district lay under that soft white hush that made even the river seem quiet, though Edmund knew better. He stood at his window a long while, watching the observatory across the courtyard, its dome a darker darkness against the sky.

The apprentice was in there. Had been in there every night this week, long past any honest hour for clockwork.

Edmund had told himself he was only keeping watch. That was the lie he'd chosen, and he wore it comfortably, the way he wore his father's name. Keeping watch. A son's duty. But the truth had been sitting in his breast pocket for three days now, wrapped in a scrap of linen, small and heavy as a sin: a piece of machined brass he had found where it should never have been.

* * *

He had found it by accident, or by whatever force arranges accidents for people who are already looking. He had gone to the observatory on Tuesday afternoon to fetch a book — an excuse so thin he could see through it himself — and found the workshop empty, Marguerite Eastwick gone down to the yard for cotton waste, and the coat they had left hanging on the peg by the door sitting wrong. Too heavy at the hem. Too careful in the way it hung.

He should have walked away. Every rule he had been raised by said walk away, say nothing, notice nothing; noticing was a disease in his father's house and the cure was always worse than the illness. But his hands had gone to the coat before his conscience finished arguing, and from the lining — stitched back with thread that almost matched, almost, but not quite, because the apprentice's hands were honest hands and deception did not come naturally to them — he had drawn out a length of oiled paper, and inside the paper, a part.

An escapement pallet. Newly cut. Beautifully cut — he knew enough of mechanism to know that much, enough to feel the way a man who cannot paint can still stand before a painting and be moved. Its angles were clean and true. It was everything the escapement currently in the great clock was not.

And Edmund Harrow, standing alone in the cathedral of brass with stolen truth in his hand, had understood two things at once, with the certainty of a striking bell. First: the apprentice knew. Knew about the filing marks, knew about the four minutes, knew what those minutes bought and for whom. Second: the apprentice was going to do something about it — something quiet and irreversible and brave beyond anything Edmund had ever managed — and would very likely die for it, or worse than die, disappear the way Fenwick's last assistant had disappeared, and the world would close over the place where they had been like water over a stone.

He had put the part back. Stitched the lining closed again with fingers that were not quite steady. And then he had stood in the middle of the workshop, surrounded by all that patient gleaming machinery, and felt for the first time in his life that his father's house had given him something he could actually use: he knew the ledgers. He knew the numbers. And somewhere in the dark above him, the master clock ticked out its four-minute lie, and for once Edmund listened to it not as background, not as furniture, but as testimony.

Tonight he would go up. Tonight he would stop watching and start choosing.

* * *

The observatory door was not locked. Of course it wasn't; locked doors invited questions, and questions were currency in this compound, and everyone inside its walls spent carefully. Edmund climbed the spiral stair with his candle shielded, past floors of sleeping instruments, past the smell of cold iron and lamp oil and, faintly, solder — the smell of the apprentice's work clinging to the building like a second presence. At the top, beneath the dome, the great clock filled the room the way an organ fills a church, its case rising floor to vault, its pendulum swinging in long silver arcs that seemed to breathe.

Marguerite Eastwick sat on a low stool before the open case, tools laid out on a cloth with a precision that looked, to Edmund, like prayer. They did not startle when his light reached them. They had heard him on the stair, of course — a clockmaker hears everything — and they simply turned their head, and in the pendulum's shifting light their face went from bronze to shadow to bronze again, unreadable as weather.

"You shouldn't be here," Marguerite said quietly.

"Neither should you." Edmund set his candle down on the workbench. "Not at this hour. Not doing what you're doing."

"And what is it you think I'm doing, Mr. Harrow?"

The formality stung more than an accusation would have. He let it sit between them a moment. Then he reached into his breast pocket and drew out nothing — only tapped the pocket itself, once, where the memory of the brass part still weighed.

"I found your coat on Tuesday," he said. "I'm sorry for it. I've no right to have gone through it, and I'd undo it if undoing were possible. But I saw what you're carrying. And I've come to tell you—" His voice caught on the simplicity of it, the enormity. "I've come to tell you that you're not alone in this."

Marguerite rose slowly from the stool. In the half-light their eyes were very steady, and very tired, and older than their years in the way that Fenwick's workshop must have made them — fluent in silences, as Edmund had come to think of it. Fluent in the language he had grown up speaking without knowing it was a language at all.

"You searched my coat," they said, "and instead of running to Cobbett, you came here at midnight to say *you're sorry*. Forgive me if I don't weep with gratitude. What do you want, Mr. Harrow? Truly."

"I want—"

"No." Marguerite's voice stayed low, but something underneath it had edges now. "Think before you answer, because I'll know if it's false. I mend watches for overseers. I've become very good at knowing when a thing is false."

The pendulum swung. Tick. Tick. Four minutes slow, and the whole sleeping district dreaming inside the lie.

Edmund took a breath, and gave up the last shelter of his name.

"I want to help you," he said. "And I want you to understand why, so you can decide whether to trust me, which you'd be a fool to do, and which I'm asking you to do anyway. So. Sit down, Master Eastwick, or stand, whichever keeps your knife nearer your hand — I've seen the one in your sleeve, yes, that too — and let me tell you what I know about my father's accounts."

Something flickered across Marguerite's face. Not surprise exactly. Recognition — the look of a person who has been measuring a mechanism for days and finally sees it move.

* * *

Edmund talked for a long time.

He told them about the ledgers — how the wage books kept in the counting house showed hours that matched the master clock, and how the wage books kept in Cobbett's private office, in a drawer with a lock Cobbett thought no one else knew the key to, showed hours that matched the workers' own tallies. Two sets of truth, separated by four minutes an hour, fourteen hundred minutes a week, and every minute of the difference flowing upward like water finding its level — into Cobbett's purse first, and around it, generously, into his father's. How the fraud was old. How it predated Cobbett. How his father had built the mill on honest labor and then, somewhere in the fat years, had learned that the cheapest material of all was time, and that time could be mined from the people least able to refuse.

"I copied the figures," Edmund said. "Three nights. Everything. Both sets. The discrepancy for each quarter going back six years. It's hidden where Cobbett will never think to look, which is to say somewhere that costs money to reach." He almost smiled. "My father's vanity has one use after all. He believes no one who loves him would rob him."

"Do you love him?" Marguerite asked. It was not cruelty. It sounded almost clinical, the question of someone diagnosing a movement.

Edmund looked at the pendulum rather than answer straight. "I loved the version of him I was raised to see. That man doesn't exist. Whether I love the one who does — I honestly don't know. I know I used to wake in the morning proud of my name." He laughed, short and airless. "Do you know what that's worth? A name. You wear it thirty years thinking it's yours, and then one day you find it's a coat someone else tailored, cut to hide exactly the shape of what you are."

Marguerite said nothing. But they had stopped holding themselves like a cornered animal. Edmund pressed on, because there was worse to say and he had decided, crossing the courtyard through the falling snow, that he would say all of it or none.

"There's more," he said. "You've met Bess Carrick. She does linens for the house. Her brother worked the looms — Daniel Carrick, spring of last year."

Marguerite went very still. "I know what happened to him," they said carefully. "She told me he died outside working hours."

"She told you what she was told." Edmund's mouth tasted of ash. "Here is what actually happened, Master Eastwick, and I give it to you knowing you may carry it to a magistrate tomorrow, and knowing I deserve whatever comes of that. The loom Daniel Carrick worked — number forty-one, in the north shed — had a cracked drive shaft. The men reported it in February. Reported it twice. There's a requisition for the repair in the mill's own files, marked and refused. Refused by my father personally, with a note in his hand — I've seen the note — saying the shaft would hold until the summer overhaul and that replacement steel was 'an extravagance.'" Edmund's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man carrying something fragile across ice. "On the ninth of April the shaft let go. Daniel Carrick was at the loom. He was dead before anyone reached him, from what the surgeon wrote, though the surgeon's report also seems to have gone missing, along with the requisition, along with the note. The record now says the accident occurred during a meal interval. Outside working hours. Which meant no compensation, no inquiry, no fault — and, not coincidentally, no entry in the wage book showing Carrick on the clock at all that day, which tidied away the fact that he'd been kept at his loom through the dinner break because the order was behind schedule."

The words fell into the room and lay there among the gears.

Marguerite had lowered themselves onto the stool. Their hands were in their lap, and Edmund watched them press one thumb slowly against the opposite palm, again and again, the way a person does when they are holding themselves together by main strength.

"The clock," Marguerite said at last, very quietly. "The clock says whether the hours were working hours. Doesn't it. If the master clock shows the time, and the master clock runs four minutes slow every day, then the whole record bends around it. Wages. Hours. Whether a man was at his loom when he died." They lifted their head, and their eyes in the candlelight were bright and terrible. "It isn't just theft. It was never just theft. The clock is the witness. Your father's house bought the witness."

"Yes," Edmund said. "And now you know why I'm here at midnight instead of asleep like a sensible man. I found your true escapement, and I understood it instantly — not the mechanics, I'm no craftsman, but the meaning of it. Put that part in, and the clock tells truth. Clock tells truth, and every falsified hour in six years of ledgers stands exposed by simple arithmetic, because your repair will be dated and witnessed and signed. You aren't fixing a clock, Master Eastwick. You're forging a weapon. The only kind my father's house can't buy or burn."

Silence. The pendulum swung its silver arc, tick, tick, patient as tide.

"Why?" Marguerite asked finally. Just the word. Just that.

And Edmund, who had rehearsed a dozen answers on the walk across the snow — duty, justice, conscience, all the respectable words — found that none of them survived the asking. What came out instead was plainer and cost more.

"Because I've been standing in the room my whole life," he said, "watching, saying nothing, telling myself that noticing was innocence. Because Daniel Carrick's sister carries linens past me in the corridor and calls me *sir*, and I take it, I take the courtesy owed to my name, knowing what my name did to her brother. Because there are two kinds of time in this house — the time that belongs to the people who earn it, and the time my family steals — and I am done living on stolen hours. That's why. Take it or leave it. I know what it weighs. It isn't much. But it's mine, and it's true, and I find I'd rather hand it to you and be judged by it than keep carrying it alone."

For a long moment Marguerite Eastwick simply looked at him. And Edmund, who had faced his father across the dinner table, who had faced Cobbett's smooth grey courtesy, discovered that being measured by this apprentice was harder than both — because Marguerite measured not with contempt but with craft, the way one measures a gear for truth of cut, checking the teeth, checking the runout, deciding whether the thing would hold under load.

Then Marguerite reached into the coat on the peg — the same coat, the violated coat — and drew out the oilpaper packet, and unfolded it on the cloth beside the tools, and let the candlelight fall on the new-cut pallet, its angles clean as morning.

"Fenwick taught me that every mechanism confesses eventually," they said. "Wear, backlash, play in the bearings — a clock keeps a record of everything done to it, if you know how to read. This clock has been confessing for years. Nobody listened." They looked up. "You're listening. That puts you in rare company, Mr. Harrow. There's me, and there's Bess Carrick, and there's apparently you."

"Bess knows?"

"Bess knows her brother was robbed twice — once of his life, once of the truth of it. She doesn't know about the escapement yet." Marguerite's jaw tightened. "And she won't, until I'm certain the knowledge won't kill her. Cobbett has already dined me and doubled my fee and mentioned Liverpool to me in the same pleasant breath. When this clock strikes true for the first time, there will be a reckoning, and I intend to be standing somewhere it can't simply be buried. Which brings me to you."

"To me."

"You carry the name Harrow." Marguerite folded the packet closed, slowly. "When the audit comes, when the arithmetic lands, whose side will the world believe you stood on? Have you thought about that? Because I have. I've had nothing but time to think, locked inside these walls with the ice coming up the river. And I'll tell you what I've concluded, Edmund Harrow, since you've earned plain speech: you cannot help me halfway. Halfway, you're only the son of the house who happened to be nearby, and sons of houses survive by distance. To matter, you have to stand where I'm going to stand, in front of whatever magistrate or commissioner they drag in, with your copied ledgers in your hand, and say *my name is Harrow and here is what my name did.* Can you do that?"

The question rang in the dome like the clock's own note.

Edmund thought of his father's face. Thought of the study, the brandy, the note about extravagance written in a hand he had once imitated proudly as a boy, practicing his letters. Thought of the word *disown*, waiting out there in the dark like weather due to arrive.

"Yes," he said.

It was the easiest true word he had ever spoken.

Marguerite nodded once — and something passed between them then that neither of them named, because naming it would have made it smaller: not friendship yet, not trust entire, but the alignment of two mechanisms that had been turning against each other's grain and now, abruptly, ran true together. Love of the work. Duty to the truth. For this one midnight hour, in a room built to measure time, they wanted precisely the same thing, and both of them understood how brief such alignments were, and both of them chose, silently, not to waste it on speeches.

"Then listen," Marguerite said, drawing the stool closer and laying out the sabotaged escapement's parts in a row, each filed mark catching the light like a scar. "If you're going to testify, you'll testify accurately. So you'll learn to read what Cobbett's man did here — and you'll learn it tonight, because the quarterly inspection is three weeks off and I mean to be ready, and so are you."

Edmund pulled up a crate and sat.

Above them the master clock gathered itself and struck the quarter — late, wrong, lying — and neither of them flinched. They bent over the brass together, the owner's son and the clockmaker's apprentice, while the snow came down on the sealed district, and the river froze a little deeper, and somewhere below them Cobbett slept the sleep of a man who believed all clocks could be bought.

In three weeks, the audit.

In three weeks, the truth would begin to strike.
