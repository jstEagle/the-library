## 13. The Accident File

His father's study smelled of pipe smoke and cold authority, the way it had smelled all of Edmund's childhood — a smell that meant *no* before any word was spoken. He stood in the doorway now with a candle in one hand and his heart going like a badly balanced flywheel, and he told himself the same lie he had been telling himself for three days: that he had come only to fetch the second ledger, the leather-bound one kept behind the false panel where his father hid the brandy from himself.

It was half past eleven. The house slept the way mills slept — never wholly, always with some portion of itself turning over, groaning, keeping watch. Somewhere below, the great engine breathed its slow iron breath, and above him, through two floors of stone and timber, he could almost hear the observatory clock striking the quarter hour: four strokes, slightly ragged at the end, the way it had struck since before Marguerite Eastwick had ever opened its case. Since before anyone opened its case honestly.

He closed the study door behind him and did not lock it. There was no time for locks.

* * *

The desk was oak, older than the mill, bought by his grandfather when the district was still fields and the river ran clear enough to see trout holding in the current. Edmund set the candle on the blotter and watched the flame lean toward the window as if it too wanted out. His own reflection hovered in the dark glass — a pale smear of a face, thirty years old, wearing his father's frown without ever having earned it.

The false panel was behind the third shelf of the bookcase, exactly as he remembered from the night he was seventeen and drunk on his uncle's port and curious about everything. That night he had found only ledgers. He had assumed, in the arrogance of youth, that ledgers were all there was.

Now he pulled the shelf forward on its hidden pin and reached into the cold cavity behind it. His fingers found the brandy first — dusty, untouched, a monument to his father's self-restraint or self-deception, he had never determined which. Behind it, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string gone brittle as biscuit, lay a bundle of papers he had never seen before.

Not accounts. Accounts were what he'd expected, more of them, an endless genealogy of theft. This was something else. The oilcloth was stiff with age but the knot had been tied recently — recently enough that the string left pale grooves in the wax that sealed it, and the wax was soft under his thumbnail.

He carried it to the desk and unwrapped it there, and the candle flame shivered as though the room itself flinched.

* * *

The top sheet was a report. Not a mill document — a proper one, printed on the grey stock of the insurance assessor's office, dated the ninth of April last spring. Edmund knew the date before he read it. Everyone in the district knew the date. It was the day the number-three loom shed took Daniel Carrick, and it was the day his father had stood at the head of the dinner table and said, in the voice he used for weather and other unavoidable things, *a weaver boy was caught in the drive shaft; nothing to be done; the coroner has been spoken to.*

Edmund had been twenty-nine then and still capable of believing his father's silences were merely grief badly expressed.

He read.

*Report of inspection, Harrow Mills, loom no. 41, weaving shed no. 3.* The assessor's name was a stranger's name, a man paid to look at machines and see only machines. But this man had looked, and what he had seen he had written down plainly, in the flat unlovely prose of men whose trade is truth:

*The driving belt guard had been removed from loom no. 41 and not replaced. The gearing showed wear consistent with prolonged operation without lubrication of the third shaft bearing. I am informed by the weavers of the shed that the machine emitted noise indicative of bearing failure for no less than ten days prior to the accident. A written request for repair, signed by the shed overlooker, is appended.*

Appended. Edmund turned the page. There it was, in a clerk's careful hand: *Bearing on no. 41 screaming bad. Ask again for parts. Men afraid to work it.* Dated the twenty-ninth of March. Eleven days before Daniel Carrick died.

And beneath that, in his father's own handwriting — small, economical, the handwriting of ledgers and of Sunday grace — a single line:

*Cost of new bearing: £4 12s. Cost of stoppage: £60/day. Let it run to quarter-end.*

Let it run to quarter-end.

Eleven days. Two hundred and sixty-four hours of a machine shrieking its warning into a shed full of people who could not afford to stop listening to anything else. And then a boy of nineteen, Bess Carrick's brother, reaching across the frame for a broken end of thread because that was what weavers did forty times an hour, and the bearing seizing, and the belt, unguarded, taking what it took.

Edmund set the paper down. His hands were steady, which surprised him; he had expected them to shake. Instead they felt the way Marguerite's hands must feel at the bench — precise, deliberate, belonging to someone else. He picked up the candle and held it close, and read the rest.

There was a second letter beneath the report. Cobbett's hand this time, smooth and confident, dated the week after the burial:

*Sir — the coroner has accepted the finding of accidental death occurring outside working hours. The lad's sister has been given a month's wages and a caution. The shed has returned to full output. I would advise that the guard remain off until the new bearings arrive in bulk; ordering one part alone invites questions.*

One part alone invites questions. Four pounds twelve shillings against a life, and even that sum begrudged, deferred, batched into bulk like so much cotton.

And then the last page, and this was the one that undid him — not the arithmetic of it, for he had already learned in his father's ledgers that arithmetic could be made to eat people — but the familiarity of it. Because at the bottom of Cobbett's letter, where a signature should have been, his father had written not *approved* nor *noted* nor any of the cold administrative words, but:

*Well managed. This is how your grandfather built. Keep the file with the others.*

With the others.

Edmund looked up at the bookcase, at the innocent row of sermons and agricultural reports, and understood that the panel behind it was not a hiding place. It was an archive. A cabinet of inheritance, curated across three generations, passed from father to son like a watch — wound, maintained, kept running — except that what it measured was not hours but everything a family will pay to forget, and what it struck was always, always on someone else's body.

*This is how your grandfather built.*

He thought of Marguerite's hands on the escapement, the way they had paused over the filed marks — fresh, deliberate, skilled — the way a doctor pauses over a wound that tells them everything about the knife. He thought of what he had said that midnight in the observatory: *I think my father's mill eats people, and I think I am being fattened to run it.* Marguerite had not laughed. That was the thing he loved — had known he loved since the stairwell, since the candlelit confession, since every hour of this strange besieged winter — Marguerite had not laughed, and had not looked away either.

He folded the report along its original creases. His father's handwriting stared up at him from inside the fold like an eye.

He could burn it. That was the loyal son's move, wasn't it — feed the archive back to itself, keep the mechanism oiled, let it run to quarter-end. He could put it back behind the brandy and go upstairs and sleep the dreamless sleep of the well-managed, and in twenty years stand at this same desk and write the same words in the same hand over some other weaver's name, and call it inheritance.

Or.

* * *

He was still standing there, the papers in his coat, the candle guttering in its own wax, when he heard the door of the morning room below open and close, and footsteps on the stairs.

Cobbett's footsteps. He knew them the way one knows weather — the unhurried tread of a man who has never once hurried because hurry implies surprise, and surprise implies fault. Edmund blew out the candle. The dark came down complete, smelling of smoke and dust and old decisions.

The footsteps climbed. Paused on the landing — a silence long enough to count a heartbeat in — then resumed toward the study door.

Edmund moved without thinking, the way one moves in a workshop when a gear is about to bind: fast, quiet, along the path already known. The bookcase. The third shelf. He slid the panel home, pressed the shelf flush, and got his body between the bookcase and the door just as the handle turned.

Cobbett entered carrying his own lamp, and the light swung up and caught Edmund full in the face, and there was nothing to do but be caught.

"Mr. Edmund." No surprise in it. That was the horror of Cobbett — nothing ever surprised him, because nothing was ever permitted to have happened without his having arranged or foreseen it. "Late for reading."

"The second ledger," Edmund said. His voice came out level. He marveled at it from a distance, the way one marvels at a clock still keeping time in a falling house. "Father asked me to check the dye accounts against the quarterly."

Something moved behind Cobbett's eyes — not belief, certainly, but a kind of professional courtesy extended to lies, the way a card player acknowledges a bluff without calling it. He crossed the room slowly, lamp raised, and let the light travel along the shelves. Along the sermons. Along the agricultural reports. Along, for one suspended moment, the third shelf, where the wood grain met the wood grain in a seam no wider than a hair.

Edmund did not breathe. In his coat lining, against his ribs, the folded papers felt warm as a living thing.

"You'll find the dye figures want correcting," Cobbett said at last, turning. "They always do, this time of year. Your father is a great believer in correction." He stopped at the door, and looked back, and smiled the smile he used at wage negotiations. "Good night, Mr. Edmund. Mind the stairs. They're old, like everything else here."

The lamp receded down the passage. The front-of-house door closed. The house exhaled.

Edmund stood in the dark a long while, listening to his heart slow, and understanding — with the clarity of a man who has just seen the whole train laid out on the bench, every wheel, every escapement, every hidden spring — that Cobbett knew. Perhaps not precisely what, perhaps not tonight's particular theft, but that the son had begun to look. And that Cobbett's courtesy, like his dinner invitations to apprentices, was simply the sound a trap makes before it closes.

Which meant there was no more time for copying figures by candlelight. No more time for patience, for the long game, for waiting until thaw.

He went up to his room, lit his own lamp, and unlocked the drawer where the copied ledger pages lay flat beneath a false bottom of spare shirts. He added the accident file to them — the assessor's plain grey prose, the overlooker's frightened pencil, his father's four pounds twelve shillings, Cobbett's *outside working hours*, the words *this is how your grandfather built* — and he weighed the little packet in his hand.

Light. Absurdly light. Three generations of empire, and it fit inside a coat.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would take it to the observatory, to the one person in this compound who knew how mechanisms told the truth. And together they would decide whether to wind it — or let it strike.
