## 14. Fenwick's Letter

The coal cart came on a Thursday, grinding up the compound road with its wheels shrieking against the frozen ruts, and Marguerite knew before the driver dismounted that something had been smuggled in with the fuel.

They had learned to read deliveries the way Fenwick had taught them to read mechanisms — by weight, by rhythm, by the small deviations from the expected pattern. Coal arrived twice weekly in winter, always at mid-morning, always heaved into the cellar chute behind the kitchens with a violence that suggested the men resented the cold more than the work. This cart came late, near dusk, and the driver — a broad man with a scarf wound so high it swallowed his mouth — did not go to the chute at all. He went instead to the side door of the observatory yard, where Cobbett's under-clerk kept a ledger of incoming goods, and he stood there too long, talking too quietly, shifting his weight like a man with a stone in his boot.

Marguerite watched from the observatory window, their breath fogging the glass, and understood.

Three days later the coal scuttle in the clock room held, beneath the black heap, an oilcloth packet no larger than a prayer book.

* * *

They waited until the bell struck eleven and Cobbett's footsteps faded down the stair, until Edmund's lamp in the adjoining gallery guttered low or was extinguished — they could not tell which, and had stopped trying to tell, though the not-telling sat in them like a splinter they had learned to leave alone. Then they lifted the packet out with both hands, as if it might go off.

It was tied with waxed twine in Fenwick's particular way — a double loop pulled left over right, the knot seated flat, because a proud knot catches on things and a flat knot keeps secrets. Marguerite had watched those fingers tie that knot ten thousand times across a decade of apprenticeship: knuckles swollen at the hinge joints, a burn scar shaped like a comma across the back of his right hand from the day he'd taught them never to reach across a running lathe. Their own hands, unwrapping the oilcloth, were shaking, and they hated them for it.

Inside: a sheet of paper folded twice, and beneath it, wrapped separately, a twist of paper containing two barley sugars. Fenwick had given them barley sugars after every completed repair since they were twelve years old. *For the teeth,* he said. *Grinding is for gears.*

The handwriting was cramped and slanted, written fast, likely by lamplight with the shop door barred:

*Girl — word comes the bridge is shut and you are inside till thaw. I will not waste candle-grease pretending I am easy about it. Do what you were sent to do. Finish the work. Say nothing. Come home whole. The rest is weather, and weather passes. Fennick.*

He had misspelled his own name. Or rather — Marguerite turned the page toward the lamp — he had spelled it *Fennick*, which was how it appeared on no document, no sign, no ledger anywhere in Manchester, but exactly how it appeared on the first tool he had ever put in their hand: a burnishing stone scratched *FENNICK* on its handle by some earlier, illiterate owner, and kept by him all these years out of a sentiment he would have denied under torture. It was the mark only Marguerite would know. The letter was genuine, and it was also, deliberately, deniable — a letter that said nothing if it was read by the wrong eyes, and everything if it was read by the right ones.

*Finish the work. Say nothing. Come home whole.*

Twelve words. Marguerite counted them the way they'd count the teeth of a wheel, because counting was what their hands did when their mind refused to move. Twelve words, and every one of them chosen like a component: nothing wasted, nothing spare, nothing that could hang him.

And every one of them a cage bar.

* * *

They sat down on the floor beside the great clock's case, back against the oak plinth where the vibration of the pendulum came up through the wood and into the spine like a second heartbeat, and they ate one of the barley sugars very slowly, and they thought about silences.

There were kinds. Fenwick had made sure of it, three years ago, when Tom Averill vanished.

Tom had been the senior apprentice then, a big gentle boy with sawdust-colored hair who could file a pivot true by feel alone and who laughed too loudly for the neighborhood they lived in. In October of that year a rate-collector's clerk had come into the shop with a watch to mend, and while Fenwick worked, Tom had asked him — pleasantly, idly, the way you'd ask about weather — why the parish time bell always rang a half-minute behind the exchange clock, and whether anyone had ever minded. The clerk had smiled and paid and gone. Two days later two men came asking after Tom, saying he owed money. A week after that, Tom's mother came asking after Tom, saying he didn't owe anybody anything and hadn't come home. Fenwick closed the shop early that day, which he never did, and sat Marguerite down on the stool by the fire and gave them a barley sugar and told them the lesson in a voice like a man reading a burial register.

"There's the silence you keep because you've got nothing worth saying," he'd said. "That one's cheap. Anyone can afford it. And there's the silence you keep because what you know would get someone hurt — maybe you, maybe me, maybe some girl in a mill who never knew your name. That silence costs. You pay for it every day you hold it, and the price goes up." He'd looked into the fire with his bad eye, the milky one that never tracked right. "But hear me, Marguerite. The other thing — speaking, telling, being *right* out loud — that costs more than most people can pay. I have watched men pay it. I don't want to watch you."

"And Tom?" they'd asked. Fourteen years old, and stupid with grief the young are, the kind that still believes questions are innocent.

"Tom asked a question he couldn't afford," Fenwick said. "Don't be Tom."

They hadn't been Tom. They had filed and polished and kept their eyes down and become fluent in the local language of looking away, and the fluency had served them, and somewhere along the years the paying had stopped feeling like paying and started feeling like breathing. That was the trick of it, they understood now, sitting on the cold floor of a stolen clock room. Silence didn't calcify all at once. It set slowly, like lacquer, layer over layer, until one day you tapped it and heard stone.

*Come home whole.* As if wholeness were something the body managed on its own, like healing a cut. As if the part of them currently buried under four minutes of every hour — the part that loved this craft, that had opened the escapement and found the file marks and felt the same sickness as finding a knife in a church — could simply be carried home intact if only the limbs came through the gate unbroken.

They turned the letter over. Nothing on the reverse. Of course not. Fenwick wasted nothing, not even blankness.

*Say nothing.*

But the trouble — the thing Fenwick could not know, sealed away on the free side of a frozen river with his barred door and his burnishing stone — was that the saying had already begun. Bess Carrick had stood in the stairwell three weeks ago with her brother's name in her mouth — *Daniel, he was nineteen, and your fine machine says he wasn't even working* — and once a name like that gets into a room it does not leave. And Edmund Harrow had come to the observatory at midnight with copied figures in his coat and a dead man's file under his arm and had said, in a voice stripped of every advantage his birth had ever lent him, *"I can't unknow the arithmetic"* — and Marguerite, who had survived a decade by knowing exactly which words not to say, had heard themselves answer: *"Then we'll make the numbers speak where we can't."*

The true escapement lay finished now, wrapped in chamois, hidden in the false bottom of their tool chest among the worn stones and the spare pivots, waiting. Machined over nine nights by lamplight with the patience of the damned: steel, hardened in oil, polished to a finish better than the original maker's, because if Marguerite Eastwick was going to commit treason with a piece of metal, the metal would be perfect. It was the finest work of their life. It existed for one purpose: to be discovered doing its duty.

The clock above their head ticked on, four minutes slow each day, faithfully lying. Four minutes an hour. Sixty-four minutes in every sixteen-hour shift. Multiplied across six hundred hands, across six days, across however many years Cobbett had been filing brass — the sum climbed past wages into theft, past theft into something with a body count. Daniel Carrick's hours, recorded as leisure. A widow's pension denied on the testimony of a gear wheel.

*Finish the work.*

Marguerite pressed the letter flat between their palms, thinking: *I am going to finish the work, old man. Just not the work you mean.*

* * *

They burned the letter that night, alone, in the brazier that took the chill off the clock room. It seemed the only honest funeral available. Paper and barley-sugar wrapper curled to ash in the coals, and Marguerite watched the ink lift off the page in the heat — *come home whole* going last, the words glowing briefly legible as the paper blackened around them, like text surfacing in dark water before it sank.

No reply. What would they even send? *Dear master, I have met a dead weaver's sister and your worst student has decided to be brave; pray for the thaw.* Some letters were traps laid for yourself. If the coal cart was searched — and after Cobbett's dinner, after the velvet-voiced arithmetic of that doubled fee and that pleasant mention of Liverpool, Marguerite assumed everything was searched — a reply would kill Fenwick as surely as a file mark kills a train of wheels. No. The old man had sent protection dressed as instruction, and Marguerite would take the protection and refuse the instruction, and the difference between those two acts would live in their chest, tight and quiet, for whatever remained of the winter.

They ate the second barley sugar at dawn, standing at the window, watching the river.

The ice had thickened again overnight. It lay along the Irwell like a lid on a pot, grey-white, seamed with darker lines where the current still moved underneath — the only living thing in the whole locked view, moving without permission, patient, certain of the thaw. On the far bank the mill district's chimneys stood up into the snow-light, and beyond them, invisible at this distance but perfectly visible to Marguerite's mind, Fenwick's workshop with its one lit window, its barred door, its master asleep above the bench with his bad eye toward the ceiling, believing his apprentice safe and silent and coming home.

*I'm sorry,* they thought, not for the first time, and not meaning it less for the repetition. *You taught me the trade. You just never asked what the trade was for.*

Below, in the yard, the morning shift was assembling in the falling snow — a dark clot of figures stamping and steaming, women with shawls over their heads, boys too small for the doors they'd walk through. Somewhere among them, Bess. Marguerite looked for her and couldn't find her and understood that this, precisely, was the problem: six hundred people, and the fraud had made them indistinguishable, interchangeable, a quantity. The clock had done that. The filed brass had done that. Turned persons into hours and hours into coppers and coppers into Cobbett's soft voice saying *discretion* over the wine.

Well. In eight days the commissioners' man would come for the quarterly audit, with his seals and his spectacles and his official form, and he would stand in this room and compare the master clock against his chronometer, as the law required, and the master clock — wearing Marguerite's true escapement by then, installed in plain sight during the inspection itself, the boldest and safest moment, hidden inside the ceremony — would keep honest time to the second. And honest time, multiplied across six hundred hands, was a confession written in arithmetic that no magistrate could refuse to read.

Cobbett would sign off on it. Cobbett always signed off. The inspection was theater, the audit a ritual, and rituals were blind — Marguerite had watched enough ceremonies pass through the mill to know that the grander the rite, the less anyone actually looked. Truth would walk in through the front door wearing the uniform of routine.

Unless Edmund lost his nerve. Unless Bess talked too soon. Unless the ice broke early and Cobbett fled. Unless, unless, unless — a mechanism with a dozen points of failure, and only one chance to run.

Marguerite breathed on the frozen pane and drew, without deciding to, a small circle with spokes — a wheel — and then rubbed it out with their sleeve before it could mean anything to anyone.

Behind them the clock ticked its beautiful lie, and downstairs a door slammed, and boots began to climb the stair: Cobbett's tread, heavy and unhurried, coming up with the morning key. Marguerite put the chamois-wrapped escapement deeper into the chest's false bottom, straightened their apron, and turned to the bench with a gear in their hand and their face arranged into the fluent silence of ten years' practice.

Eight days, they thought, as the lock turned. Eight days, and then the clock tells the truth whether any of us survive to hear it.

The door opened behind them. "Morning, little clockmaker," Cobbett said, cheerful as frost. "Lovely work weather. Let's see what you've done today."
