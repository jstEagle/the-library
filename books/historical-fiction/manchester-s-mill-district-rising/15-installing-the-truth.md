## 15. Installing the Truth

The night before the audit, Marguerite slept with the true escapement against their ribs.

Not in the coat lining — Cobbett's man had taken to searching the workshop after the dinner, a courtesy of the same velvet kind as everything else he did — but sewn into the waistband of their second shirt, where the fabric lay flat against the body and no seam betrayed itself under a patrolling thumb. The brass was cold at first, then warm, then so familiar that Marguerite forgot it was there, which was the whole point. A thing you could forget was a thing that would pass an inspection.

They woke before the bell. In the compound everything woke before the bell; the mill owned even the dawn here, rationing it out through windows oriented to the counting house rather than the east. Frost ferned the inside of the observatory's lower panes, and Marguerite breathed on the glass and drew nothing in it — no mark, no initial, no sign that anyone had stood in this room wanting anything. They had become very good at leaving no evidence of themselves. It occurred to them, dressing in the dark, that this skill might soon be obsolete, and that they should enjoy it while it lasted, the way one enjoys the last quiet minutes before a clock is due to strike.

The escapement rode against their ribs like a small stubborn heart.

* * *

The audit inspection began, as all things at Harrow Mills began, with Cobbett being pleasant about it.

"Mr. Pilling comes up from Manchester on the eleven o'clock coach," Cobbett said, standing in the observatory doorway with his hands clasped behind him, a man presenting his own virtue for appraisal. "Commissioner's office sends him twice a year. He'll want the clock opened, the works examined, the rate checked against his own chronometer. You'll do what you always do, Eastwick. Show him the mechanism. Answer what's asked. Volunteer nothing."

"Volunteer nothing," Marguerite repeated. The words sat strangely in their mouth now, like a coin they'd bitten and found to be base metal.

"He's a dull man," Cobbett went on, "and I mean that as a compliment. Dull men see what they expect to see. Give him brass and arithmetic and he'll be happy as a bishop." He smiled. His teeth were excellent. Everything about Cobbett was maintained the way a machine is maintained — regularly, expensively, and without any feeling involved. "You've done well by us, Eastwick. Mr. Harrow senior remarked on it only yesterday. *A steady pair of hands.* There are worse things to be called in this district."

Marguerite thought of Fenwick's letter folded into four beneath the false bottom of their toolbox. *Finish the work. Say nothing. Come home whole.* Two instructions from two masters, and both of them, God help them, pointing the same direction: down. Keep your head down. Keep the truth down. Keep it buried so deep it comes out the other side of the world as silence.

"I'll be ready at eleven," Marguerite said.

Cobbett lingered a moment longer, looking past them at the clock case — the great dark walnut tower of it, taller than two men, its face pale as a moon over the mill yard. He looked at it the way a farmer looks at a field. Not love. Accounting.

"Four minutes slow," he said softly, almost fondly. "You know, when I first came here the old master ran three minutes fast. My predecessor's arrangement. Fast time makes the workers early, early workers make full days, and nobody complains because the whistle sounds honest." He tapped the doorframe twice, a little farewell knock. "Slow time is better, though. Slow time, they stay late and think they've been paid for it. Much cleaner. Nobody ever suspects a gift."

He left. Marguerite stood in the cold observatory with their heart going like a train across a viaduct, and understood that they had just been told the entire architecture of the crime, casually, by a man who believed the building was his.

* * *

They spent the morning doing honest work, because dishonesty of this magnitude required a foundation of perfect normality or it would not hold.

They cleaned the pivots. They dusted the movement with the soft badger-hair brush Fenwick had given them their first year, saying *dust is the memory a mechanism keeps, and you want it remembering nothing.* They checked the oil cups, adjusted the pendulum's rating nut by a hair — not enough to change anything, only enough that if Pilling watched them work he would see a craftsman attending to details, a person with nothing to hide and therefore everything hidden. Edmund came at half ten and said almost nothing, which was their agreement now. He wound the drum while Marguerite held the great wheel steady, and their hands nearly touched on the barrel arbor, and did not, and the space between their fingers was the loudest thing in the room.

"The coach was seen at Stockport," Edmund murmured, eyes on the winding crank. "It's running early."

"Then we're early too."

He looked at them then — one long look, the look of a man handing something heavy across a gap — and went back to the crank. Three years ago Marguerite would have needed words. Now they understood that Edmund Harrow's silences were the opposite of the district's silences: not walls but doors, held open with effort.

Bess passed the stairwell window at quarter past, a basket of mended linens on her hip, and did not stop, did not nod, only turned her face toward the glass for the length of one breath — the way she'd learned to signal since the counting-house vigil, the way a sentry signals: *I am here. The others are here. We have not gone away.* Below her, somewhere in the grey weave of the morning, three hundred weavers were moving toward their looms carrying a knowledge none of them had spoken aloud since the bridge closed: that today the clock would either tell the truth or it wouldn't, and that whatever happened, someone had finally decided it mattered which.

Marguerite's hands, on the tools, were perfectly steady. Their mouth had gone dry as unslaked lime.

* * *

Pilling arrived at ten minutes to eleven, a round, spectacled man smelling of wet wool and the coach's stove, carrying a box chronometer like a clergyman carries scripture. Cobbett received him in the yard with a bow calibrated to the man's exact importance. Tea was proposed, declined, proposed again, accepted for later. And then the party climbed the observatory stairs — Pilling, Cobbett, a clerk with a ledger chained to his wrist, Edmund trailing with the studied boredom of a son dragged along on his father's business — and Marguerite stood before the open clock case with the true escapement warm against their ribs, and felt the moment arrive the way ice feels a thaw beginning: not as an event but as a loosening, deep down, irreversible.

"Now then, now then," said Pilling, cheerfully, into the movement. "Master clock, is it? Regulator work? Who built her?"

"Grimsditch of London, thirty-one years ago," Marguerite said. "Rebuilt by my master, Fenwick, in '44."

"Fenwick. Fenwick." Pilling nodded as though filing the name among names he already trusted. "Good firm. Good firm." He set his chronometer on the ledge and bent to the dial, comparing, lips moving through the arithmetic. "Running slow, I make it. Slow by — mm. Consistent, mind. Nicely consistent. That's good workmanship, even when it's losing. Sloppy clocks wander. Honest ones lose at a constant rate." He chuckled at his own joke. Cobbett chuckled with him, one beat behind, a man fluent in every dialect of laughter.

Marguerite heard Pilling say *consistent* and felt the floor tilt gently beneath them, because it was true, it was horribly true — the filed pallets lost exactly four minutes a day, every day, a fraud machined to tolerance — and if Pilling certified the rate as merely *a regulation matter*, noted in the ledger, corrected next visit, then the arithmetic would never be done, the discrepancy would remain a footnote, and Bess's brother would stay dead outside working hours forever.

Or.

Or the escapement that was currently in the clock could come out, and the one against Marguerite's ribs could go in, and the clock would begin keeping true time in front of the commissioner's own witness, and when Pilling compared the mill's wage books against the audited hours — which he would, because the commissioners' new forms required it, because Parliament had begun asking questions that made Cobbett's smile thin — the numbers would refuse each other. Four minutes an hour. Forty-eight minutes a day. A year of forty-eight-minute days, multiplied by three hundred hands, summed by a clerk with a chained ledger who loved arithmetic the way Cobbett loved courtesy: completely, and without feeling.

It would not say murder anywhere in the figures. But it would say *lie*, in the only language a magistrate cannot argue with.

"Eastwick," Cobbett said pleasantly, "the gentleman will want to see the escapement. Open the plate for him."

* * *

Later — years later, in rooms with lamps and shorthand clerks, when people asked how they had found the courage — Marguerite would try to explain that courage had nothing to do with it, or rather that courage was the wrong word entirely, too grand, too much like a coat one puts on. What happened was smaller and stranger. They knelt at the movement with their screwdrivers laid out in order of size, as Fenwick had taught them, and they removed the four screws of the escape bridge, and lifted the bridge away, and there it was: the sabotaged anchor, its pallet faces bearing the fresh file marks, bright scars in the old brass, the signature of a skilled hand that had wanted to stay anonymous and had instead written its confession in a language only clockmakers read.

And Marguerite reached — casually, in the natural course of lifting the part clear for examination, a gesture performed a thousand times in a thousand repairs — into the region of their waistband, and brought out the true escapement wrapped in an oiled rag, and set the rag on the ledge beside the chronometer as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world. Which, in a way, it was. Craftsmen carried spare parts. Craftsmen prepared for every contingency. No one had ever been hanged for having the correct replacement to hand.

"What's that, then?" said Pilling mildly.

"Spare anchor, sir. I cut it against the day the original wears. A master clock wants a spare the way a ship wants a boat."

"Prudent," said Pilling, approving. "Very prudent. May I?"

And he picked up the true escapement — the part Marguerite had filed and polished and secretly tested against a borrowed seconds dial through eleven nights of trapped winter — and turned it in his blunt inspector's fingers, and admired it.

"Clean work," he said. "Who cut this?"

"I did, sir."

"Apprentice?" Pilling raised his eyebrows at Cobbett. "You keep better apprentices than I keep inspectors, Mr. Cobbett. Look at these pallet faces. Mirror-finished. Angles true within a breath." He held it to the light. The light ran along the steel and brass like water along a weir. "This is the finest small work I've seen north of Greenwich, if you'll pardon the flattery."

Beside Marguerite, Edmund made a sound that was almost a cough and almost a laugh and was, underneath, something that had no safe name in this room.

"You're too kind," Marguerite said. Their voice came out level. Inside, a voice that was not a voice at all was screaming the way a kettle screams — high, contained, and boiling.

Because here was the thing they had not planned, the thing no plan could have held: Pilling liked the part. Liked *their* part, the honest part, the witness. And a proposal assembled itself in Marguerite's head with the smooth inevitability of a weight driving a train of wheels — not to smuggle the swap at all, not to do it slyly, in the dark, with averted eyes, but to do it *now*, openly, dressed as maintenance. Because the clock was running slow — Pilling had said so himself, had entered it in his book as a *rate error, correction recommended*. And what did an inspector recommend? Correction. And who corrected? The craftsman. And what did the craftsman use?

The spare. The fine one. The mirror-finished one that had just been praised north-of-Greenwich.

"Sir," Marguerite heard themselves say, "if it pleases you — since you note the rate error — I can fit the new anchor while you watch. Ten minutes' work. Then we'll let her run against your chronometer through your tea, and you can certify the correction done under inspection. It reads better in the record than a promise."

There was a silence of perhaps two seconds. In it, Marguerite heard Cobbett breathing. Heard the clerk's pen pause above the page. Heard, distantly, the mill's engines turning over below like some vast animal dreaming of cotton.

"Done under inspection," Pilling repeated, pleased. "Yes. That's the modern way. The commissioners do like it. Very well, young — Eastwick, was it? Proceed."

"Is that wise?" Cobbett said lightly. "Opening the train without the works drawn? An error at this height—"

"Errors are what inspections are for," said Pilling, already settling onto the stool with the contentment of a man about to be entertained. "Go on, lad. I'll watch every move."

*Every move.* Marguerite bowed their head over the movement to hide their face, and began.

* * *

It was the strangest work of their life: honest craft performed as an act of war, every motion correct and every meaning inverted. They lifted the filed anchor away and set it down on the white cloth with its scars uppermost — *there*, in plain sight, for one suspended moment, the evidence lying in the light between the commissioner's man and the mill's manager — and Cobbett looked at it, and Marguerite watched Cobbett look at it, and Cobbett saw only a worn part. Why would he see more? He had ordered the wearing. To him those file marks were not a confession; they were a job well done.

But Marguerite's hands shook — one fine tremor only, at the first pivot — and they steadied them by pressing them flat against the cold movement plate, feeling the whole great machine's faint pulse, the tick traveling through the brass like a heartbeat through a chest. *Steady,* said Fenwick's ghost. *A trembling hand tells the truth, and today the truth is not yours to tell yet.*

They fitted the true escapement. Bridge down. Screws home, each turned to the same snug torque, the last quarter-turn making the small sound Fenwick called *the click of a thing finished*. They oiled the pallets — one drop, drawn fine, exactly as the manual said — and released the train, and the escape wheel stepped forward and caught, and the clock took up its new heartbeat with a sound so slightly different that only four people in the room could have heard it.

Marguerite was one of them. Edmund was another. Cobbett, Marguerite saw, heard nothing at all.

"Set her to your chronometer, sir," they said.

Pilling matched the hands to his box instrument, wrote the figure in his book, and beamed around the room like a sun. "There. Corrected under inspection, witnessed and signed. She'll keep true time now or I'm a Dutchman. Let her run through tea and I'll check her before the coach." He gathered his coat. At the door he paused, struck by a professional afterthought, and pointed his pencil at Marguerite. "That wage-book reconciliation your clerk's doing downstairs — the new forms, hours worked against wages paid? You'll want the clock's certificate attached. Commissioners are strict about it now. Arithmetic has to agree with arithmetic, gentlemen. That's the whole of reform, when you boil it down." He chuckled. "Agreement. That's all anybody wants."

"Yes," said Edmund quietly. "Agreement. That's all anybody wants."

Cobbett signed the inspection sheet without reading it — why would he read it? it said what he had arranged for it to say — and pressed his seal into the wax, and the wax took the impression cleanly, and Marguerite watched the seal rise and fall and thought: *you have just put your name at the bottom of your own unraveling.*

"Excellent work, Eastwick," Cobbett said warmly, shaking their hand at the stairhead. His grip was dry and precise. "Steady pair of hands. Mr. Harrow senior will hear of it. Perhaps — after the thaw — a permanent position. The compound suits you, I think. You seem *settled* here." The word *settled* arrived with its freight intact: settled as in comfortable; settled as in kept; settled as in a debt discharged and a mouth stopped.

"Perhaps," Marguerite said.

Below, the mill whistle blew the noon — four minutes late by the sun, four minutes late by every honest watch in the district, and on time by the only clock that counted, which until this morning had been the only clock that lied.

* * *

They stood alone in the observatory afterward, Marguerite and Edmund, listening to the new escapement tick. The sound filled the tower the way water fills a lock: level by level, rising.

"It's done," Edmund said. "There's no taking it back."

"No."

"When Pilling checks her before the coach, she'll have gained — what, a minute on the afternoon?"

"A few seconds. Enough. He'll certify her true. And then the wage reconciliation goes to Manchester with the clock's certificate nailed to it, and some clerk there does the division, and gets a number that doesn't divide, and asks why." Marguerite looked at their hands, which had finally begun to tremble now that the work was over — the body settling its accounts after the mind's had cleared. "Forty-eight minutes a day, Edmund. Times three hundred hands. Times three years. When they finish the sum it will come out in pounds, shillings, and a dead man."

Edmund said nothing. He put his hand briefly, hard, on Marguerite's shoulder — a grip like a knot tied — and then went to the window and looked out at the yard, at the weavers streaming back from the noon break into the loom sheds, at Bess Carrick standing a moment in the snow with her face turned up toward the clock tower, checking the hour against something she carried inside herself, some truer time that management had never been able to confiscate.

"She'll know," Edmund said. "By tonight, the whole district will know the clock's been changed. They won't know how or why. But they'll feel it — the whistle ringing closer to the church bells. Word travels faster than coaches."

"And Cobbett?"

"Cobbett winds her himself on Sundays." Edmund turned from the window, and his face in the cold light had the stillness of a man standing on a river just before the ice gives. "Sooner or later he'll compare her against his private records. He keeps them, you know. I've seen the book. Every falsified hour, tabulated in his own neat hand — insurance, he'd call it. Leverage over my father." He smiled without any pleasure in it. "He'll notice within a week. Maybe less. And when he notices, he will know exactly what happened, and exactly who did it, and he will come for you with the magistrate's warrant already drafted, because that's the kind of man he is. Meticulous."

"Then we have a week," Marguerite said.

"We have a week." Edmund looked at them across the ticking tower — at the apprentice who had walked into his father's compound with a toolbox and a survival vocabulary and was walking out, in every way that mattered, already gone. "When he comes — and he will come — what will you say?"

Marguerite listened to the clock. True time now, second by honest second, falling through the frozen air like a coin dropped down a well.

"I'll show him," they said, "how it's done."

Down in the yard, the looms began again, three hundred strong, beating like a heart that had finally been allowed to speak.
