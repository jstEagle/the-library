## 16. Discovery

Cobbett had always believed that a man's sins were best kept the way a miller keeps flour — in sealed sacks, stacked in order, weighed every morning against the ledger of what they cost him. He rose before the bell, as he had for nineteen years, and performed his private offices in the dark: the counting house keys tested one by one against their locks, the wage book checked beneath the lamp for tampering, and last — always last, because it required climbing — the winding of the observatory clock.

It was not strictly his duty. The winding was supposed to be done by the clock-keeper, a shambling old man named Pell whose hands had begun to shake and whose discretion Cobbett had purchased years ago with a shilling a week and the unspoken promise that no questions would ever be asked of him. But Pell had taken to his bed with the chest fever three days past, and the audit was tomorrow, and there were some things Cobbett trusted to no one else. Not out of sentiment. Out of arithmetic. Every degree the great clock drifted from its falsified rate was a degree of exposure, and Cobbett had spent half a lifetime learning that exposure did not arrive like a flood. It arrived like damp — silently, through cracks too small to see, until one morning the whole wall was soft.

So he climbed.

The stairwell smelled of cold iron and pigeon droppings and, faintly, of oil — good oil, the pale sweet kind the clockmaker used, not the cheap tallow-and-linseed stink of the mill floors. Cobbett noted the smell without thinking about it, the way he noted everything, filing it in that vast dim archive behind his eyes where he kept the district's secrets the way other men kept receipts. Snow had come again in the night. Through the slit windows the river showed white from bank to bank, the ice thickening toward the shallows where the bridge's iron legs stood sheathed and silent, locked in their winter cage along with everything else in the compound. No one in. No one out. A season sealed like a jar.

He liked it that way. He had argued for the closure policy himself, years ago, in his careful clerk's voice, framing it as a matter of security and inventory control. In truth he had framed nothing; he had simply made sure the argument reached Mr. Harrow's ear at the right moment, from the right mouth, and let the old man believe it was his own idea. That was the whole art of management, as Cobbett understood it. You never pushed. You arranged the world so that pushing was unnecessary.

The clock room opened before him like the interior of some enormous gilded instrument, which was precisely what it was. Brass everywhere, brass in tiers, brass descending into shadow — wheels within wheels, the pendulum swinging its slow patient arc above him with the sound of a held breath released over and over. Four seconds each way. He knew the rhythm as intimately as his own pulse, because he had built that pulse himself, or rather he had paid to have it built, four minutes slow in every day, a small elegant theft multiplied by six hundred workers multiplied by sixty hours a week multiplied by years. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever commissioned. He had told Mr. Harrow once that fraud was simply accounting conducted ahead of schedule, and the old man had laughed until he coughed.

He set down his lantern. He took up the winding crank from its hook on the stone wall. And then he paused — because habit is a form of intelligence, and nineteen years of habit told him something was different before his eyes could say what.

The light. That was it first. The lamplight fell differently on the going train than it should have. There was a brightness at the third wheel, a rawness, the gleam of metal recently handled, recently cut, that had not yet acquired the soft grey patina of parts that lived inside a case. Cobbett stood very still and let the pendulum count for him — one, two, three, four — while his gaze moved over the movement the way a customs officer's gaze moves over a crate.

Then he saw it.

* * *

The escapement anchor was new.

Not new-new. It had been aged, deliberately — rubbed with something, smoke or umber or the grease off a workman's thumb, a competent forgery of age. But competence has limits, and Cobbett's eye had been trained in the counting house, where the whole trade was telling forged figures from true ones, and a forged figure always betrays itself in the same way: it is too clean where it should be worn and too worn where it should be clean. This anchor's pallet faces were polished exactly where an old anchor would be polished. Its file marks ran in exactly the directions an old anchor's would run. It was an excellent imitation of a part that had been turning in this clock for eleven years.

Except that Cobbett had the original.

This was the detail that saved him, though he would never think of it as luck; he would think of it as thoroughness, which was the virtue he believed he possessed instead of paranoia. When the sabotage had been ordered, three years ago now, Cobbett had insisted — quietly, reasonably, in the name of maintenance records — that the removed parts be kept. *One never discards originals,* he had said to the man who did the filing, a journeyman from Leeds with gambling debts and no family, a man whose silence had been bought the way all silences were bought, and whose subsequent departure for Liverpool had been arranged with the same care. *If the commissioners ever query the rate, we must be able to produce the authentic mechanism.* What he had meant, and what the Leeds man had understood without being told, was: if anything goes wrong, we bury the evidence under genuine-looking doubt.

The original anchor lay wrapped in wool and waxed cloth in a locked drawer of Cobbett's own desk in the counting house, three floors below, tagged in his own hand: *OBSERVATORY — ESCAPEMENT — REPLACED 1844 — DO NOT DISCARD.*

And the anchor hanging in the clock now was not it.

He knew within four seconds of looking. The Leeds man's filing had left a burr on the near pallet — a tiny bright whisker of metal, barely visible, which Cobbett had noticed at the time and decided was harmless. The burr was absent now. Someone had dressed it. Or — no. He leaned closer, lantern raised, breath fogging the cold brass. Not dressed. *Different.* The geometry was subtly wrong, the drop of the pallets a hair shallower, the impulse face finished with a finer hand than the Leeds man had ever possessed. This was not the sabotaged part restored. This was a new part altogether, machined to the original specification — the *true* specification — by someone with a lathe, a graver, and a great deal of patience.

Someone had put the truth back into the clock.

Cobbett straightened. The pendulum swung. Somewhere below, the compound was waking — the first loom-shudder coming up through the foundations, the muffled cough of the engine house, boots on frozen cobbles. All of it proceeding on time, on the clock's time, on *his* time, the way it had proceeded for years. And he stood in the brass cathedral with his lantern shaking very slightly — only slightly, he corrected himself, only the wind through the slit window — and began, with the terrible calm that was his gift, to calculate.

The clockmaker's apprentice. Eastwick. The quiet one, the watchful one, the one who never asked questions and never looked up and had seemed, throughout the repair, to embody every virtue Cobbett paid for and prized. Too much so, perhaps. Cobbett turned back through the weeks: the dinner, the doubled fee, the pleasant mention of Liverpool — and how the little creature had received it with lowered eyes and perfect stillness, and how he had thought, walking home through the snow, *that one understands the arrangement*. He had mistaken obedience for understanding. He had mistaken fear for agreement. He had forgotten the oldest lesson of the counting house: the quiet ones are quiet because they are listening.

But when? The inspection yesterday had gone flawlessly. He had watched Eastwick's hands work — he had watched them the entire time, Cobbett did not watch idly — and seen only the honest fiddling of an honest tradesman, adjusting, oiling, wiping down. Unless. Unless the swap had been made before the official repair even began, during those first unsupervised days when the boy — the apprentice, whatever it was, Eastwick was difficult to make assumptions about and Cobbett distrusted people who were — during those days when Pell had been dying of his fever and the stairwell door had stood unlocked and Cobbett himself had been occupied with the quarter's wage books.

Before the repair. Of course. Before the repair, so that the "restoration" could be performed on the true mechanism and witnessed and signed for — so that the audit tomorrow would certify, in ink, under the commissioner's own seal, that Harrow Mills' master clock kept correct time. And then the wage books would not balance. Then the arithmetic would stand up in the audit room and testify in a voice louder than any witness. Six hundred workers. Three years. Four minutes a day. The sums would unfold page after page after page, and at the bottom of every page, in a neat clerk's hand, would be a name.

His name.

* * *

He did not run down the stairs. Running was for men who had lost control of a situation; Cobbett had not yet conceded that he had. He walked, fast, his heels striking the iron treads in a rhythm quick enough to pass for urgency but short of panic, and he was aware — distantly, contemptuously — of his heart doing what hearts do, slamming at its cage like a loom gone wild, and he overruled it. Fear was fuel. Fear was information. Fear told you where the fire was so you could get ahead of it.

In the counting house he unlocked the drawer and unwrapped the wool and laid the original anchor on the blotter beside his private record book, and the two objects together told the story more plainly than any confession: here the true part, filed down and altered to steal time; there, in the clock overhead, its replacement, machined innocent. Any magistrate with eyes would read it in a minute. Which meant any magistrate with eyes must *not* be given the chance to read it calmly. Speed was everything now. The audit was tomorrow morning; the commissioner's party was already lodged at the hotel across the river road; the magistrate could be summoned by noon if the right message went out within the hour.

He worked swiftly and in order, because order was his religion. First: the observatory was to be locked. Not later, not after breakfast — now, this instant, before the apprentice could return to "check" anything, before Edmund Harrow could drift up there with his long melancholy face and his pockets full of heaven-knew-what. He took the ring of master keys and set out through the falling snow, and at the foot of the observatory stair he met Pell's substitute, a dull lad called Naylor, and told him the clock room was closed for adjustment and no one was to enter on any pretext whatsoever, including Mr. Edmund's. Especially Mr. Edmund's.

Second: the message. He wrote two notes at his desk with a steady hand. One to the magistrate in town, respectful, precise: *a fraud upon the works' timekeeping having been discovered, and a suspect in custody pending your attendance*. One to Mr. Harrow senior, shorter still, because the old man responded best to brevity: *A problem with the clock. Handled. Come to the counting house at ten.* Let the old man arrive believing it a trifle. Let him sit down. Let the facts be laid before him in sequence, with the wage book open at the relevant pages — because Cobbett had already understood, in the cold hour of the climb back down, that the old man's protection could no longer be assumed. If this broke, it would break over both their heads. And Cobbett had spent nineteen years arranging the world so that blame, wherever it landed, landed elsewhere. That arrangement had one final service to perform.

Third: the apprentice.

* * *

They came for Marguerite Eastwick in the workshop yard at half past eight, while the frost still silvered the cobbles and the apprentices' kettle was just going on the hob. Two yard men, chosen for size and stupidity, and Cobbett behind them with his coat brushed and his gloves on and his voice pitched at the mild register he used for addressing servants and children and other categories of property.

"Eastwick. Mr. Harrow wishes a word about the clock. Bring your tools."

He watched the face across the yard with the attention of a man reading a scale. He saw — or thought he saw, and it hardly mattered which — the fractional stillness, the calculation happening behind those watchful eyes at the speed of a dropped weight. He saw the glance flick toward the gate, toward the river, toward the white sealed miles between here and anywhere, and he saw it come back empty. Good. Let the arithmetic of hopelessness do half his work for him.

"You'll want to know," Cobbett said pleasantly, as they walked, snow squeaking underfoot, "what the word concerns."

No answer. Only that infuriating silence, the silence that had charmed him at dinner and appalled him this morning.

"It concerns the escapement," Cobbett said. "You'll know the one I mean."

Still nothing. But the shoulders — ah, the shoulders gave a little, the way a mechanism gives when a pawl slips. Cobbett smiled at the frozen lane ahead of them and said nothing further. There was no need. A trap does not explain itself. It merely closes.

In the counting house he had the doors shut and the yard men posted outside, and he sat behind his desk with the original escapement anchor lying on the blotter between them like an exhibit at an inquest, and beside it his record book open to the entry in his own hand. The lamp was lit against the grey morning. Outside, the mills thundered on, indifferent, six hundred souls bending to a stolen hour.

"Yesterday," Cobbett said, "the master clock of this establishment was found to have been altered. An essential component, manufactured to my order and installed under my supervision, has been removed and replaced with a counterfeit — a part machined to restore the clock's original rate. Do you understand what that means?"

Nothing. Those eyes on him, level and dark and patient, and Cobbett felt the peculiar vertigo of interrogating a person who would not perform fear, and he pressed on before the vertigo could become anything worse.

"It means that whoever did it intended the quarterly audit — which convenes tomorrow morning — to discover a discrepancy of some thousands of hours across three years of wage payments. It means that the fraud thus exposed would be attributed—" and here he allowed himself, for the first time, a thin blade of real anger, real and cold and ancient, "—to the management of these works. To me. So I will ask you plainly, Eastwick, once: did you replace that part?"

"I repaired the clock," Marguerite Eastwick said. The voice was quiet, unhurried, and it was the first time Cobbett had heard it speak at length since the dinner. "That was the commission. The clock was running slow. I corrected it. Ask anyone in the district what a clockmaker is hired to do."

"The commission," Cobbett said softly, "was to restore the clock to its certified rate. The certification is in my safe. Signed. Witnessed. Dated."

"Then your certificate certifies a lie." Still that maddening calm — no, not calm, Cobbett amended, watching closer now, watching the hands folded in the lap with their burn-spots and file-calluses, watching the breath. Not calm. *Held.* Held the way a spring is held, wound to breaking. "I'm a clockmaker's apprentice, Mr. Cobbett. I don't certify lies. I fix clocks. Yours was broken on purpose, and I fixed it, and I'd do it again."

The silence afterward lasted four seconds. Cobbett counted them on the pendulum in his head, out of habit, and found, absurdly, that he was measuring his own composure in the units of the thing that had ruined him.

"You understand," he said at last, rising, buttoning his coat with neat deliberate fingers, "that this is theft. Removal of company property. Tampering with the machinery of a certified public timekeeper. It carries a term in Kirkdale. It carries, for a person of no trade and no character references worth the paper — well." He took up the bell-pull and rang it twice. "You are confined to the compound. Your tools are forfeit to the magistracy as evidence. You will be held in the east room of the counting house until the magistrate arrives, and you will have, between now and then, one opportunity to reconsider your account of events. Persons who assist the magistracy are treated very differently from persons who obstruct it."

At the door he paused. He could not help it; vanity was the one luxury he permitted himself, and the morning had earned him a taste of it.

"When the Leeds man went to Liverpool," Cobbett said reflectively, "he also told himself he was only fixing things."

The snow was falling harder now. Through the counting-house window Cobbett watched the yard men walk the prisoner away — a slight figure between two large ones, spine straight, head level, and around the edges of the yard, drawn by the wrongness of the scene the way crows draw to a fallen beast, the first weavers gathering at the fences with their shawls pulled tight, saying nothing, watching everything.

Bess Carrick was among them. Cobbett marked her particularly, and did not like what he marked.

He returned to his desk, unlocked the safe, and drew out the wage books — three years of them, the beautiful architecture of his life — and began, with steady hands and a cold heart, to decide which pages would burn.
