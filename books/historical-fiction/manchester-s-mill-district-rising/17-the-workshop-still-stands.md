## 17. The Workshop Still Stands

The counting house smelled of ink and cold iron, the way all rooms smell when they have been built to keep accounts rather than people. Marguerite had been brought there at dusk — or what passed for dusk behind the shuttered windows, a grey seeping through the slats like water through a failing dam — and left alone on a hard chair beneath the tall ledger shelves. A lamp burned on the clerk's desk, trimmed low, as though even its light were being rationed.

They had taken the tool chest. That was the worst of it, worse than the locked door, worse than the sound of Cobbett's boots receding down the corridor with the unhurried rhythm of a man who has already won. The chest held everything: the burnisher their father had owned before him, the jewelling files worn to the width of a fingernail, the little brass gauge Fenwick had pressed into their hand on their first morning with the words *measure twice, speak once*. Twelve years of apprenticeship in a box of oiled wood, and it sat now somewhere in this building, inventoried, confiscated, entered into evidence against them.

*Against.* The word sat strangely. They had spent three days installing truth into that clock, and now the truth was the crime.

Marguerite tested the door without much hope. It was oak, older than the mill's prosperity, fitted into its frame with the kind of joinery no one bothered with anymore. Beyond it, the corridor ran silent. Somewhere above them, muffled by floors of stone and timber, the observatory clock struck the hour — and Marguerite flinched at the sound, because they knew now exactly how true it was. Eight strokes, clean and honest, each one a small accusation aimed at the man who would come for them.

They counted the strikes out of habit. They could not stop counting. That was the whole trouble with a trade like theirs; you measured everything, and measurement made silence impossible in the end.

The chair faced the desk. On the desk lay a single sheet of paper, squared neatly with its edge, waiting.

* * *

Cobbett came an hour after dark, if dark was what it was outside; Marguerite had lost track of the sky. He came with two men Marguerite did not know, big quiet men with the flattened knuckles of those who move heavy things for a living, and he came carrying a decanter and two glasses as though he were arriving at a friend's house for supper.

"Miss Eastwick." He set the glasses down but poured only one. "I trust they've made you comfortable. I know it isn't home."

Home. The word landed in Marguerite's chest like a dropped gear. Fenwick's workshop above the river, the smell of oil and coal smoke, the kettle on the iron stove with its dented spout — all of it on the far side of frozen water, all of it suddenly very far away and very fragile.

"I'd like my tools back," Marguerite said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "And I'd like to know the charge."

"The charge." Cobbett smiled the way frost forms, slowly, evenly, over everything. He lowered himself into the clerk's chair and turned the paper on the desk so that it faced them. "Sabotage of the master timepiece. Theft of mill property. Conspiracy to defame a lawful enterprise. Any one of those would see you transported, Miss Eastwick. Together they'd see you hanged, if your magistrate were a less sentimental man." He sipped his whisky. "But none of that needs to happen. Confessions are wonderful things. So brief. So final."

Marguerite looked at the paper. It was a confession already written out in a fair clerk's hand, leaving only a space at the bottom for a name. Their own guilt, composed by someone else, in someone else's grammar. *I, Marguerite Eastwick, apprentice clockmaker, do confess that I did wilfully alter the mechanism of the Harrow Mills observatory clock...*

"You filed the escapement," Cobbett said pleasantly. "You installed a false part during the audit. My records show the clock running four minutes slow per day for eighteen months under the old part, and perfectly — *suspiciously* — perfect since yours. You'll sign, and we'll say the whole affair was the act of a disgruntled apprentice acting alone."

"Alone," Marguerite repeated.

"Alone," Cobbett agreed. "A lone malcontent. It happens in every district. Sad, really." He leaned forward, and the lamplight found the small pale eyes above his smile. "Because if you don't sign, Miss Eastwick — if you insist on some other story — then I'm afraid the story grows. Witnesses multiply. Evidence accumulates." He turned his glass slowly on the desk, watching the amber catch the light. "Evidence such as exists in certain workshops across the river. Tools, parts, papers. A great deal can be proven from a workshop, you understand. And a fire proves nothing at all except carelessness. Old buildings. Oil lamps. It's a wonder more of them don't burn."

The room went very still around Marguerite. Not the stillness of safety — the other kind, the stillness of a mainspring wound past its limit.

"Fenwick's workshop," Marguerite said quietly.

"A tragedy waiting to happen. All that lacquer and spirit. Your master is an old man; he sleeps badly; he leaves lamps burning." Cobbett spread his hands, regretful, reasonable. "Of course, none of it need occur. Sign the paper. Be transported to somewhere green and forgettable instead of hanged, and your master keeps his roof, and everyone goes on keeping whatever time they're told to keep."

Marguerite thought of Fenwick's hands, which had taught theirs every motion they knew. Thought of the letter that had come smuggled in the coal cart — *Finish the work. Say nothing. Come home whole* — and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like fever, that Cobbett was offering them the same bargain in different words. Silence, always silence, purchased at whatever price the silencer named.

Three years ago they would have signed. Two years ago. Last week, perhaps. But Bess Carrick had stood in a stairwell and said her brother's name aloud — *Tom Carrick, sixteen years old, caught in the loom they wouldn't repair* — and something in Marguerite had cracked along a line that could not be soldered shut again.

"No," Marguerite said.

The word was small. It sounded enormous.

Cobbett sighed, genuinely, the sigh of a man disappointed in a purchase. "I hoped you'd be sensible. Your master was sensible. Sensible people live long lives." He rose, straightened his coat, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch. "Take the night, Miss Eastwick. Read the paper. Ink is patient. Fire is patient too — but it does prefer dry weather, and the forecast is for a thaw."

The door closed. The two big men stayed, taking up posts on either side of it like furniture that breathed.

Marguerite sat in the lamplight with the confession squared before them, and for the first time in twelve years of learning which silences keep people safe, they spoke aloud to an empty room.

"He'll burn it," they said. "He'll burn the workshop whether I sign or not. A witness is a witness either way."

The shelves of ledgers said nothing. But saying it aloud changed something in the architecture of their chest — a small structural failure, or a small structural repair, they couldn't yet tell which.

* * *

Sleep came in fragments. The counting house grew colder as the night deepened, the lamp burning lower, and Marguerite learned the geography of their captivity by ear: the breathing of the guards, the settling of timbers, the hourly stroke of the clock overhead — nine, ten, eleven — each strike arriving through the stone like a heartbeat through a wall. *I put that voice in you,* they thought toward it. *Keep speaking.*

Somewhere past midnight — they judged it by the clock, because there was nothing else left to judge by — a sound began below the window.

At first it was only a scrape, a shuffle, easy to mistake for wind-driven snow against the shutter slats. Then it resolved into voices, low and many, and then into something with a rhythm to it: boots. Dozens of boots, shifting weight in the snow, the way crowds shift when they intend to stay.

Marguerite crossed to the window and put their eye to the gap in the shutters.

Below, in the yard between the counting house and the mill's black façade, stood the weavers.

They had come out of the workers' rows in whatever they had — shawls over nightclothes, coats buttoned wrong in the hurry, a boy barefoot inside another boy's boots. Snow fell among them and nobody brushed it off. There were thirty of them, then fifty; more came down the lane from the direction of the tenements, lanterns bobbing, until the yard was full of a quiet crowd that did not chant and did not scatter and did not go away.

And at the front of them, on the counting house steps themselves, stood Bess Carrick.

She had wrapped herself in a blanket the colour of wet slate, and her hair was coming loose from its pins, and she stood with her arms folded against the cold like a woman guarding a doorway she had decided existed. As Marguerite watched, she raised her voice — not loud, not wild, but pitched to carry, the way a shuttle carries thread.

"They think we can't count," Bess called to her people, and to the walls, and to whoever listened. "Four minutes an hour. Thirty-two minutes a week off every wage, for years. We counted. We've been counting longer than any clock in that tower." A murmur moved through the crowd, low and steady. "Tom Carrick died on mill time. The book says he didn't. Well — here are the hours now. Standing in the snow. Let them write us down."

Marguerite pressed their forehead to the cold shutter slat. Something hot was happening behind their eyes and they let it happen, because no one could see, and because some things should not be swallowed.

*Bess.* They had met her twice. Once crossing a bridge, once in a stairwell, both times exchanging only what the rules allowed. And she had gone out into the falling snow and gathered half the district's weavers to stand witness outside the walls where a confession was waiting for a signature — not because Marguerite had asked, but because a dead brother deserved arithmetic, and arithmetic needed witnesses.

One of the guards shifted at the door. "Move away from that window," he said gruffly.

Marguerite did not move away from the window.

Instead — and their heart hammered as though they were filing the escapement again, that first transgressive stroke — they lifted their voice and sang out, clear enough to reach through the gap in the shutters and down into the yard:

"Bess Carrick!"

The crowd stilled. Lanterns tilted upward.

"He means to burn Fenwick's workshop!" Marguerite called, and the words came out of them like a spring released after years under compression, twelve years of *don't ask, don't repeat, don't look up* breaking loose all at once. "Whether I confess or not — he told me tonight. If anything burns across the river, remember who said it first! Remember who wanted it!"

Silence, one full second of it, in which Marguerite heard their own life change pitch.

Then Bess turned to her people and repeated it, louder, and the crowd took it up — not a roar, nothing so tidy, but a spreading murmur, names and questions passing from mouth to mouth like current through a circuit. Someone began moving toward the bridge road before anyone stopped them. The guards outside Marguerite's door swore softly and began conferring, and boots went hurrying away down the corridor to fetch someone whose job it was to manage crowds.

Marguerite stayed at the window, palms flat against the freezing shutter, watching the lantern-light below.

Bess looked up — she could not have seen Marguerite through the slats, but she looked anyway, directly at the window, and gave a single nod. Then she turned back to her weavers and began organizing them the way a good weaver organizes warp: threads into order, order into strength. Some went to the bridge road. Some stayed, planting themselves in the snow before the counting house doors like posts driven into winter ground. A girl of maybe twelve produced a stub of pencil and a scrap of paper and began, at Bess's dictation, writing down names — witnesses, each one, a ledger of a different kind.

*Don't ask, don't repeat, don't look up.* Marguerite said the old rules to themselves, tasting them like coins gone out of circulation.

Then they stepped back from the window and looked at the confession lying on the clerk's desk, squared and patient and worthless now, and they picked it up and read it through once, carefully, the way Fenwick had taught them to read a mechanism — understanding every part before deciding what to do with it.

Then they folded it into quarters and slid it, unread by anyone but them, into the lining of their coat, where a hidden escapement had once lived. Evidence worked both ways. Let Cobbett discover, in front of the magistrate, exactly what his own confession demanded of them.

Outside, the snow kept falling on the weavers who refused to leave. Overhead, at half past the hour no one official would ever hear again, the observatory clock — Marguerite's clock, honest now, irreversibly honest — struck a single chime into the dark.

And down the corridor, coming fast, with none of last night's velvet about him at all, came the sound of Cobbett's boots.
