## 18. Edmund's Choice

The house on Ardwick Green had never felt like a home to Edmund Harrow, but he had always assumed it would feel like something — a fortress, perhaps, or a stage, or the hull of a ship his father captained through weather. Standing in the entrance hall that final morning, he understood it was none of those things. It was a counting house with bedrooms attached, and every surface in it had been purchased at a price someone else had paid.

Snow came against the long windows in slow diagonals. The fire in the grate was lit, as it always was by seven, though his father did not rise until nine; the servants kept the rooms warm for a man who occupied them the way a landlord occupies a property. Edmund stood with his hat in one hand and his satchel heavy against his hip, and he listened to the house breathe — the tick of the hall clock, the distant clatter of the kitchen, the muffled groan of ice on the river two miles off, where the bridge had been closed these six weeks and would not open again until thaw.

Six weeks. Long enough to copy every figure twice. Long enough to read a dead weaver's accident file by the light of a shuttered lamp, his hands shaking not with fear but with the effort of keeping still while Cobbett's boots passed in the corridor outside. Long enough to learn what his name weighed.

His father's study door was closed. It was always closed. Edmund knocked once and entered without waiting, because courtesy had become, somewhere in the last month, a thing he could no longer afford.

Josiah Harrow sat behind the desk like a man enthroned in miniature — a small, neat, grey-templed figure whose hands, even at rest, moved with the restless economy of someone tallying invisible columns. He looked up. He did not look surprised. He had never in Edmund's life looked surprised, and Edmund understood now that this too had been a kind of arithmetic: surprise cost nothing to suppress and everything to show.

"You're up early," Josiah said. "Or you haven't slept. Which is it?"

"Neither." Edmund set the satchel on the desk's leather top. The sound it made was small. He had imagined this moment so many times — in the observatory at midnight, in the stairwell where Bess Carrick had given her brother's death a shape and a name, in the long white hours when Marguerite Eastwick worked below him with files and truth — and in every imagining the satchel had landed like a verdict, like a hammer on an anvil. In fact it made hardly any sound at all. Evidence, he thought, is very quiet. That is how it gets into houses like this one.

His father eyed the bag but did not touch it. "You've been strange since the clock commission began. Strange at dinner. Strange in the yard. Cobbett tells me you've been asking after the audit figures."

"Cobbett tells you a great many things."

"He does. And they're nearly always worth hearing." Josiah leaned back. The chair creaked, a domestic sound, obscene in its smallness. "So. Say what you've come to say, and then go and sleep, because you look like something the river coughed up."

Edmund opened the satchel.

He laid the ledgers out first — his copies, fair-written in his own hand over eleven nights, each column checked three times against the originals hidden behind the third-floor paneling where his grandfather had once kept brandy. Then the accident file, its official seal broken, the report inside annotated in Cobbett's cramped fist: *machine left unrepaired pending budget review.* Then the wage abstracts, quarter upon quarter upon quarter, four minutes an hour shaved from every spinner and weaver in the district for six years, and beneath all of it the single page that mattered most — the record showing Thomas Carrick had died at half past two in the afternoon on a working Thursday, logged instead as occurring outside working hours, because a death within hours was compensable, and a death outside them was merely unfortunate.

He arranged the papers the way Marguerite might have arranged gears on a workbench: in order, in relation, each part explaining the next. He stepped back from them the way a craftsman steps back.

"I'm giving you until noon," Edmund said. "Go to the commissioners yourself. Confess before the magistrate's inquiry opens. Name Cobbett's scheme, name the falsified hours, name Thomas Carrick. Do it first, do it openly, and I burn my copies and stand beside you when they come. Whatever they take from you, you'll have chosen to give. That's worth more than they'll ever tell you."

For a long moment his father said nothing. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a maid went along the corridor with a jug, and the water sloshed gently, absurdly, the ordinary world continuing overhead while the floor of Edmund's life opened under him.

Then Josiah Harrow laughed. Not loudly. A short exhalation through the nose, the laugh of a man declining a bad offer on wool.

"And what," he said, "do you imagine happens to this family if I walk into a public room and hand them my own throat? You think the workers will love us? They'll tear the gates off. You think the banks will wait? You think your sister's marriage holds if the Harrow name goes into a magistrate's room on the arm of a confession?" He rose, buttoning his dressing gown with terrible calm. "You've been in the observatory too long with that clockmaker's apprentice. They've put ideas in you like worms in flour."

"They didn't need to put anything in me. I found the ledgers myself. Your manager stole from your workers with your knowledge—"

"My *knowledge*." His father crossed to the window. Snow slid down the glass. "Boy, I built every brick of what you're standing in. I came here with nothing but a strong back and a head for figures, and I bought a riverbank and filled it with looms, and every man jack of you eats because I keep the whole machine turning. Four minutes an hour. Do you know what that is, in a district of nine hundred hands? It's *order*. It's the difference between a mill that runs and a mill that drowns. Cobbett understood that. I thought you might, eventually. I waited for it the way I'd wait for a colt to fill out."

Edmund heard himself speak from a great distance, as though the words were being wound upward on a spindle by someone else's steady hand. "Thomas Carrick was seventeen. The loom was left unrepaired to save eleven pounds. He died at half past two on a Thursday, and you let them write that he died in his own time, so you wouldn't owe his sister a shilling. That isn't order, Father. That's just theft with a corpse in it."

Something moved behind his father's eyes then — not remorse, Edmund saw with a clarity that would stay with him all his life, but recalculation. The same motion Cobbett's face made over a disputed invoice. Weighing what the son in front of him was worth against what the son in front of him knew.

"You'll give me those papers," Josiah said quietly, "and take six months in Liverpool. There's a cousin there who can knock some sense into you. When you come back, none of this will be spoken of again."

"No."

"No?"

"The papers go with me. Every copy. The originals are already beyond your reach — don't waste your morning wondering where. At noon I deliver them whether you've confessed or not." Edmund's voice did not shake. He noted this the way one notes a mechanism running true. "I wanted so badly to be wrong about you. I want you to understand that. I spent thirty years assembling evidence in my own head that you were a hard man doing a hard job, and every night I lost the argument, and I kept losing it because losing it meant I got to keep my father."

The words hung in the cold air of the study. For one instant — one — Josiah Harrow's face broke open, and underneath the break Edmund glimpsed something old and tired and almost humanly afraid, a boy from a tenement who had sworn never to be poor again and had kept that vow past all decency. Edmund felt the pull of it like current under ice. He could stay. He could spend his life diving for that boy, hauling him up year after year, drowning a little more each time.

Then his father's face closed, the way a ledger closes, and whatever lived beneath was locked away with the accounts.

"You are no son of mine," Josiah said.

It was very quiet when he said it. No thunder in it at all. That was the worst part — that a sentence meant to sever a lifetime should be delivered in the flat tone of a man declining a second cup of tea. Edmund had braced for rage. Rage would have been a gift; rage would have been proof of feeling. What he got instead was bookkeeping.

"Then I'll trouble you no further," Edmund said.

"You'll leave the papers."

"I won't."

"Then you'll leave this house with nothing. No allowance, no name, no letter of introduction. You'll be a stranger in the city you were born in, and no door on Ardwick Green will open to you, and within the year you'll come crawling back and I'll pretend this morning never happened, because I am a generous man." His father picked up the fire irons, absently, needing his hands full. "That is the last kindness I will ever offer you."

Edmund repacked the satchel. Ledgers, file, abstracts, the page with Thomas Carrick's true hour of death. Each document went in square and flush. He buckled the straps. He put on his coat, and then his hat, and then he took one thing more from the mantel without asking — the small brass orrery his grandfather had made, a little engine of suns and planets no bigger than a loaf, which had been the only object in this house he had ever loved. It fit in his coat pocket. Let his father notice or not notice; it was inheritance enough.

At the door he stopped. He did not turn around.

"When they ask me why," Edmund said, "I'll tell them about a clockmaker's apprentice who showed me that a mechanism is honest even when the men who own it aren't. And when they ask you why, Father, I hope you find an answer that costs you less than this one did."

He walked out. Down the stairs, through the hall, past the ticking clock that had measured his childhood in false quarters, past the maid with her jug frozen mid-step, out into the snow.

* * *

The streets took him in without ceremony. Snow lay ankle-deep and unbroken on the lesser roads; the main thoroughfares were rutted brown slush where carts had fought through since dawn. His breath smoked. The satchel strap cut his shoulder and he shifted it and let it cut. It seemed right that it should weigh something.

He had perhaps a mile to walk and forty minutes to spend, and he spent the first ten of them discovering what dispossession actually consisted of. It was not dramatic. Nobody pointed. The city did not care yet — the city would care enormously by evening, but at half past eleven on a February morning it simply went on trading around him. He was a man walking. He had no money beyond eleven shillings in his coat, no name that would open anything, no address except a workshop that belonged to another man and might, by week's end, belong to ashes. He turned the last fact over and found he could not think about it yet. One catastrophe per day; his hands were full.

What he had was a satchel of paper and eleven shillings and a promise extracted from a girl with ink-stained fingers: *at noon, the magistrate's room, or wherever Cobbett's men have taken them.* Bess Carrick had said it standing in the coal-yard with snow in her hair, having run half the district since dawn on word that the observatory was locked and the clockmaker held in the counting house. She had looked at him — at the fine coat, the gentleman's hands — with a weariness that asked him to prove himself quickly or not at all.

"I'm coming with you," he had told her.

"You know what it costs?" she'd said. "Walking in there? Once you carry those papers through that door, there's no version of your life after that comes back to this one."

"I know exactly what it costs," he had said. "I've just been shown the bill."

Now he crossed the canal footbridge and came down toward the mill district, and the compound walls rose ahead of him through the snow-fall, black brick going grey with frost, chimneys standing idle because the works ran short-hours in the closed season. Smoke still rose from the counting-house chimney. Smoke meant a fire, a fire meant people, people meant that Marguerite Eastwick was still breathing on the other side of those walls — he held onto that the way a drowning man holds a spar, not because it bore weight but because letting go was unthinkable.

And there, at the corner where the lane met the mill road, the weavers were gathering.

They came in twos and threes out of the terraced streets, silent, unhurried, collars up against the snow, and they arranged themselves along the wall opposite the compound gate with the terrible discipline of people who had practiced patience their whole lives. Bess stood at their center. She had told them everything, he realized — the filed escapement, the stolen minutes, her brother's misrecorded hour — and they had not rioted, had not broken anything, had only put on their coats and come out into the cold to *witness*, which was a harder thing entirely. Nine hundred pairs of hands that could have torn the gates down, folded instead into stillness. Edmund stopped at the edge of them, an intruder in good boots, and Bess caught his eye across the snow.

She nodded once. Toward the gate. Toward the counting house, where a constable's hat was visible now through the frosted glass, and a carriage stood steaming in the yard with the commissioner's arms on its door.

Noon was close. The magistrate's room would be filling.

Edmund tightened his grip on the satchel — the only copy now, every figure, every page, the whole quiet arithmetic of six years riding against his hip — and walked forward alone across the snow, toward the doors he had been born behind, carrying the truth of his father's house out of it for the last time.

Inside, he knew, Marguerite was waiting with a clock's mechanism for a witness.

Outside, behind him, nine hundred people stood witness with nothing in their hands at all.

He went in through both.
