## 19. The Magistrate's Room

The magistrate's room smelled of tallow and wet wool, of ink gone sour in its pot, of the particular staleness that gathers in rooms where men decide things about other men without ever touching them. Marguerite noticed all of it — the smell, the grey light pressing against the tall windows, the way the fire had been built up on one side only so that the magistrate's bench sat in warmth while the rest of the room held the cold like a grudge. Noticing was what they were for. It had kept them alive in Fenwick's workshop through seven years of small dangers, and they assumed it would not fail them now.

It might.

They stood at the center of the room with their wrists bound before them — not rope but a leather strap, borrowed, someone had said, from the harness room, as though Marguerite were livestock to be led out to the fields after. The strap bit when they moved their hands. They did not move their hands.

Cobbett sat at a folding table to the left of the magistrate's bench, arranged there like an exhibit himself, though he did not seem to know it. He wore his good coat, the one with the velvet collar, and his hands lay flat upon the table's edge in an attitude of reasonable grievance. Beside him, laid out with terrible care on a square of black cloth, sat the escapement — the true one, Marguerite's own work, machined by candlelight over three weeks while the district slept sealed in ice. Cobbett had carried it here himself. Marguerite watched his fingers rest near it the way a man rests his hand on a dog that has disappointed him, ready at any moment to close into a fist.

At the far end of the room, behind a desk scarred by fifty years of other people's signatures, sat Magistrate Pell, old and dry as a ledger nobody balanced. And beside *him* — this was the thing Marguerite had not dared hope for — sat the factory commissioner, Mr. Aldous Crane, who had come north with the thaw because the papers said he would, because the weavers had said he must. He was younger than Marguerite had imagined. His hands were ink-stained at the cuffs. When Marguerite entered the room under guard, he did not look at them the way the magistrate did — as a problem to be filed — but the way Fenwick looked at a mechanism brought in off the street: with attention, and something underneath the attention that might, in better light, be called respect.

Marguerite looked away first. Old habits. Then, deliberately, slowly, they looked back.

*Don't look up.* That had been the rule for seven years. Fenwick had taught it gently, the way you teach anything meant to keep a child alive: *Eyes down, hands busy, mouth shut. The world will tell you everything if you never ask it a question.* But Fenwick was not here. Fenwick's letter still lived folded against Marguerite's breastbone — *Finish the work. Say nothing. Come home whole* — and Marguerite had read it so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. They understood now, finally, what the letter was. Not protection. A cage with the door painted on it.

They raised their eyes and kept them raised.

"Begin," said Magistrate Pell, and Cobbett began.

* * *

He was good at it. Marguerite had to grant him that. He had spent twenty years making numbers lie to hundreds of people at once, and lying was a craft too, and he had practiced it the way Marguerite practiced filing — until the rough edges disappeared.

"The facts are simple," Cobbett said, and his voice was warm, regretful, the voice of a man forced to do an unpleasant duty by an ungrateful world. "This person —" he did not say Marguerite's name; he made it sound as though they had no name, which was its own kind of theft — "was engaged to repair the observatory clock. In the course of that repair, they removed the clock's original escapement and substituted a part of their own manufacture. This is sabotage. It is also theft — the original component is Harrow Mills property. And it was done during the quarterly audit inspection, with my signature obtained under false pretenses." He turned his head, slow and sorrowful, toward Marguerite. "I treated this apprentice with courtesy. I doubled their fee. I invited them to my own table. And this is how they repaid the house."

Every word was true. That was the horror of it, Marguerite thought. Every single word, true as a plumb line, and hanging from those truths was a scaffold.

Magistrate Pell made a note. "The apprentice will confess," he said. It was not a question. It was a schedule.

"They have refused," Cobbett said, and let a silence fall around the refusal, letting it look like insolence rather than terror, which was skillful, because it was both.

"And the original escapement?" Crane asked. His first words. He had a northern accent ground nearly smooth but not quite, a grain of the mill towns still audible beneath the commission's polish. "You've produced the replacement. Where is the part it displaced?"

"In my possession," Cobbett said smoothly, "at the mill. Under lock. Given the — sensitivity — of the evidence."

Marguerite's heart went cold and then, strangely, calm. He didn't have it. Or worse — he'd had it and it hadn't shown him what he wanted, or he'd hidden it because the marks on it told a story he could not allow told. Either way: the original escapement, the sabotaged escapement, with its fresh file cuts four minutes deep into every working day — was not on that black cloth.

Crane saw it too. Marguerite could tell by the small stillness in him, the way a clockmaker can feel a train out of beat even across a crowded room. "I should prefer," Crane said mildly, "to examine both parts. Side by side."

"After the confession," Pell said. "Procedure first. The apprentice is charged. Let us hear the charge confessed, then discuss the machinery."

And the room turned toward Marguerite — Cobbett with his sorrowful eyes, Pell with his pen already moving, two constables by the door with their caps in their hands, and somewhere beyond the tall windows, muffled by glass and distance, a sound that took Marguerite a full breath to recognize.

Crowd noise. Weavers. Hundreds of them, standing in the thawing mud outside the magistrate's court since dawn, because Bess Carrick had stood on a cotton bale in the yard and told them her brother's name until they learned to say it back to her.

Marguerite breathed in. The leather strap creaked.

"May I speak?" they asked.

Pell looked up, irritated. "Apprentices speak when—"

"With respect, sir," Crane said quietly, "the accused may always speak. That is rather the point of all this."

* * *

What happened next, Marguerite would remember for the rest of their life in fragments, the way a broken plate remembers being whole.

"I am a clockmaker's apprentice," Marguerite said. Their voice came out low, and they made themselves lift it. "Seven years trained. I have repaired four hundred watches and eleven public clocks, and I have never stolen so much as a screw. I am not going to confess, because there is nothing to confess. But I will do something better than confessing." They turned to face Crane directly. "I will show you. Give me ten minutes and the clock's own mechanism, and I will show you exactly what was done to the observatory master, by whom, and why. Not with words. Words are cheap in this room. With arithmetic. Arithmetic doesn't take sides."

"You're proposing," Cobbett said, with a smile like oiled hinges, "to interrogate your own guilt with clockwork."

"No," Marguerite said. "To interrogate yours."

The smile stopped moving.

Pell's pen hovered. "Commissioner, this is irregular—"

"So was the fire at Hyde Mill," Crane said. "So was every inquiry this commission ever opened too late. Irregularity, Mr. Pell, appears to be the native weather of this district." He rose. "Bring the parts. Both of them. Constable — fetch the observatory escapement from the mill, from whatever lock Mr. Cobbett keeps it under. Now, please, and accompanied."

For one visible moment Cobbett weighed whether to refuse — Marguerite watched him do it, watched the calculation move behind his face like a shuttle between heddles — and then he bowed, gracious as a dinner host, and gave the order himself, because refusing would have been a confession more complete than any words.

While they waited, the room rearranged itself around the idea of what was coming. A long table was cleared. Someone fetched lamps although it was barely past noon, because the day outside had gone the colour of pewter and the light through the windows was failing into snow. Marguerite's strap was struck off — Crane insisted; *one does not demonstrate horology in fetters*, he said, and something in the absurd gentleness of it nearly undid Marguerite entirely — and they flexed their freed hands, feeling blood return like water filling a dry channel.

Bess's brother. They made themselves think it, deliberately, the way you press a bruise to be sure it's real. Thomas Carrick, nineteen years old, caught in a loom whose broken governor-strap had been reported, logged, and never replaced, because the replacement cost eleven shillings and Cobbett's budget did not forgive eleven shillings. The mill record said the accident occurred outside working hours. Outside working hours. As measured by a clock that ran four minutes slow for every hour it was asked to keep — a clock whose escapement had been cut with a jeweler's file by a hand steady enough to make the cuts look like wear.

Four minutes an hour. Sixty-four minutes in a sixteen-hour day. For five hundred workers, five days a week, three years running — wages stolen not in coin but in time, which no worker could count and no clerk would admit, because the counting instrument belonged to the thief.

Marguerite had done the sums forty times. They knew them cold. They had machined them cold.

* * *

The constables returned at half past twelve, wet to the shoulders, carrying the original escapement in a locked deed box between them as though it were a relic. Perhaps it was. Cobbett unlocked the box himself, tipped the part onto the black cloth beside its twin, and stepped back with his hands folded, wearing the expression of a man who expects a magic trick to fail.

"Explain," said Pell.

Marguerite picked up the original escapement. It was warm from the box, warm from the room, and heavier than it had any right to be — heavy with what had been done to it, heavy with sixty-four minutes a day multiplied by five hundred souls. They set it on the table where the lamplight fell full upon it and bent close, and the room bent close with them, magistrates and constables and commissioner gathering like apprentices around a master's bench, and for one strange moment Marguerite felt seven years fall away and was back in Fenwick's shop, holding a watch up to the window, learning to see.

"An escapement," they said, "is the conscience of a clock. Everything else stores power or transmits it. The escapement decides. It releases the wheel tooth by tooth, tick by tick, and each release is a small decision about how much time is real. If it is honest, the clock tells the truth. If it is tampered with—" they turned the part over, "—the clock lies, and everyone who trusts it inherits the lie."

"Pointedly," Cobbett murmured, "we await the evidence."

Marguerite ignored him. They took a loupe from their coat — their own tools had been returned that morning, another of Crane's quiet orders, another hinge swinging open — and screwed it to their eye, and held the escapement up to the lamp.

"Here." They passed it, handle first, to Crane. "The pallet faces. Look at the entrance stone, and then the exit stone. A worn escapement wears evenly — friction is fair, it takes from both stones alike. Look at the asymmetry. One face polished by three years of honest contact. The other freshly filed, within the last year, the cuts bright, no oxide in the grooves. Someone lengthened the impulse face on one side only. Each escape-tooth engagement loses a fraction of a second. The fractions accumulate. Four minutes in every hour."

Crane held it to the light for a long moment. Then he said, "Could this be wear?"

"Wear doesn't file in straight lines," Marguerite said. "Wear doesn't improve the symmetry of a cut. Wear is chaos; this is intent. And here—" they touched the retaining screws, "—these were removed and reseated. See the burr on the slot edges, bright, recent. The tampering was done with the clock assembled, by lamplight, by someone who knew exactly which thousandth of an inch mattered. That is not vandalism. Vandalism breaks. This *adjusted*. You need training to do damage this precise. You need craft."

Whose craft? The question hung in the room. Marguerite did not answer it aloud. They had their suspicions — a paid journeyman, a hired hand, someone whose name could be bought — but suspicion was not testimony, and they would not spend truth cheaply on the day it mattered most.

"Now the arithmetic," Crane said.

"Now the arithmetic." Marguerite straightened, and their voice found a register they had never heard in it before, steady and clear, the voice of a person reading a verdict rather than receiving one. "Set the master clock against the sun any day you choose — the observatory has a transit instrument and the records to prove it. From Michaelmas last the divergence grows: four minutes daily, compounding. Now take the mill's wage books. The mills pay by the clock, not the sun — every bell, every shift, every quarter-hour docked or earned, measured by that machine. Four minutes an hour is one-sixteenth of every working life in this district. Five hundred workers. Sixteen-hour days in the busy season. Over three years, gentlemen, the difference between the clock's hours and heaven's hours comes to —" they had it written, folded in their pocket, in figures checked three times — "somewhat more than thirty-one thousand working hours. At the mean wage, that is money enough to buy the loom that killed Thomas Carrick a thousand times over."

The name landed in the room like a dropped weight. Cobbett's hands, folded on the table, went white at the knuckles.

"That is assertion," he said.

"That is arithmetic," Marguerite said. "But here is what ties the knot. Bring the wage books. Any day the books show a man entering the gate before the six o'clock bell and leaving after the six o'clock evening bell, ask the sun what time it truly was. On the clock's testimony, Thomas Carrick died at twenty minutes past eight on the eleventh of April — outside working hours, as the accident record states, a private grief, no compensation owed. On the sun's testimony, corrected for your clock's four-minute theft, he died at twenty minutes past *seven* — inside the second shift, at his loom, under the mill's duty of care, owed compensation, investigation, and the truth." Marguerite turned and faced Cobbett fully, and did not look down. "Your clock stole four minutes an hour from the living. It spent some of those minutes erasing the dead."

Silence. The fire cracked on its warm side of the room. Out beyond the windows, the crowd had gone quiet too, as though sound itself were leaning in.

Cobbett rallied, because he was Cobbett. "A clever story. An apprentice's story. Show me the hand that held the file. Name the man who did this, if you can. Or confess that you yourself installed the replacement part — *you* altered the clock during my audit, *you* handled the mechanism—"

"Yes," Marguerite said.

Cobbett stopped.

"Yes. I installed the true escapement. During your audit. With your signature on the inspection sheet below my work. You signed a certificate attesting that the observatory clock keeps honest time." Marguerite let that stand a moment, let it turn over in the air like a coin catching light. "If my part is fraudulent, then your signature certified fraud. If my part is honest — and the transit records, and the sun, and every church bell in Manchester will confirm it by Sunday — then the clock is honest now, and the question the honest clock asks is: what was it doing *before*?"

Crane set down the escapement with great care. "Mr. Cobbett," he said pleasantly, "do fetch me those wage books after all. And the accident file. And your own private time-comparisons, if you keep any — clockmakers' clients so often do."

And Cobbett, whose whole empire had been built on the principle that silences could be owned, discovered in front of forty witnesses that some questions cannot be declined — and that the room had changed sides while he was watching the wrong hands.

* * *

They came for him at dusk, two constables and the commissioner's own clerk, and took him out through the yard where the weavers stood in the falling snow, silent, five hundred strong, saying nothing at all — which was, Marguerite thought, watching from the doorway with Bess Carrick's shoulder pressed warm against theirs, the loudest sound the district had ever made.

But Cobbett, pausing on the courthouse steps, bound now where Marguerite had been bound, turned his head and found Marguerite through the crowd the way one clock finds another across a dark room — and smiled.

"You've wound something you can't stop," he said, and his voice carried, pleasant as ever. "Ask yourself who taught me which thousandth of an inch matters. Ask who profits when the clock runs slow and everyone blames the ice."

Then the door of the carriage shut, and the wheels ground away through the slush, and his words stayed behind in the snow, ticking.

Marguerite stood very still, Bess's hand suddenly tight on their arm, and felt the sentence go home like a tooth drawn: *who taught me.*

Somewhere in this city, the hand that held the file was still holding it.
