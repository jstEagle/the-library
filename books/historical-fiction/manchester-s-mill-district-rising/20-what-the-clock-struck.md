## 20. What the Clock Struck

**Marguerite**

The clock struck four while the commissioner was still reading.

Marguerite stood at the long table in the magistrate's room and listened to the sound come through the walls from across the compound — muffled by snow, by brick, by the thickness of everything between — but unmistakable all the same. Four strokes, even and unhurried, each one falling into the room like a coin dropped into a deep well. The true escapement, keeping true time. Every ear in Manchester's mill district had been set to that clock for thirty years, and for thirty years it had lied to them by degrees so small no one could feel it, only live it: four minutes an hour, an hour a day, a day of every working life, gathered up invisibly like dust under a loom.

The commissioner, a dry man named Pell whose spectacles sat low on a nose reddened by cold, read Marguerite's figures twice. The room waited. Cobbett waited too, at the table's far end, gloved hands folded, wearing the expression of a man observing a tedious formality he expected shortly to end. He had not yet looked closely at the escapement part laid on the felt before him — or rather, he had looked, and Marguerite had watched him look, and had seen the moment the file marks registered. It had passed over his face like a shadow of a bird crossing a window. Then it was gone, folded away, and his hands were still folded, and he said nothing, because to say anything would be to begin.

Pell set down the papers. "You are telling me," he said, "that this clock has run slow by design. For how long?"

"The wear pattern suggests years," Marguerite said. Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "The marks were cut fresh — within the last year, I judge, when the mechanism was 'overhauled.' But the arithmetic in the mill ledgers goes back further than that. The clock is only the newest instrument. Before it, there were other instruments." They did not say what the other instruments had been. Bess would say it, if it needed saying. Bess was waiting outside with three hundred weavers who had walked off their shifts without asking leave for the first time in their lives.

"Mr. Cobbett," Pell said. "Your response."

Cobbett smiled the way frost forms on glass — gradually, beautifully, and with no warmth in it anywhere. "Commissioner, this apprentice has confessed to tampering. You have heard it yourself. A frightened child alters a clock, then invents a conspiracy to explain the alteration. I would ask why we entertain fiction in a matter touching the property and reputation of Harrow Mills —"

"The ledgers," Marguerite said quietly, "are not fiction."

And that was when Edmund stood up.

He had been seated against the wall through all of it, nameless now in every way that mattered, dressed in a coat that no longer carried his father's crest on the button. He crossed the room carrying a leather folio, and he laid it down beside the escapement part, and he opened it, and the sound it made — paper on felt, soft as breath — was the loudest thing Marguerite had ever heard.

"My copies," Edmund said. "Eleven years of wage accounts, side by side with the hours the mill reported to its workers and the hours the clock actually recorded. And beneath those—" his hand did not shake, though Marguerite saw the effort it cost him— "the accident file. March of last spring. Loom nine. The brake assembly was worn past tolerance; my father was told; the repair was deferred twice for cost. Daniel Carrick died at half past two in the afternoon, inside the sixth hour of his shift. The record says the accident occurred outside working hours. It says that because outside working hours, a death costs the mill nothing but a coffin."

Cobbett rose. Whatever moved behind his face then was not frost. It was something faster and less patient. "Mr. Harrow," he began, using the name like a blade offered hilt-first, hoping Edmund would flinch from it—

"I have no name," Edmund said. "My father made certain of it an hour ago. Which means I am nobody, Mr. Cobbett, and nobodies are remarkably difficult to threaten. Everything you might take from me, I have already lost. Everything you might do to me, I invite you to attempt in front of the commissioner."

* * *

**Edmund**

Later — much later, when the candles had burned down to stubs and the room had emptied of everyone but clerks copying testimony by lamplight — Edmund would remember most clearly not his father's collapse, nor Cobbett's face, nor even the moment Pell closed his folio and said, in the flat voice of a man who has just understood the size of what he has stepped in, "There will be an inquiry. A full one. Beginning tomorrow."

What he remembered was the clock striking five.

Because it struck five while Cobbett was still speaking — still mid-sentence, still constructing his denials with the same craftsmanship he had brought to eleven years of theft, each word filed smooth and fitted close — and Edmund watched the man hear it. Watched him glance toward the window, toward the observatory tower rising black against the snow-light, and saw him understand, in one instant of naked calculation, that the sound reaching them now was honest. That it had been honest since the audit inspection. That every worker in the district had heard the difference, even if none of them could yet name it, the way you feel a fever break before the thermometer confirms it. Time itself had turned witness. There was no instrument left in the compound that would lie for him.

Cobbett stopped speaking. He looked at Pell, at the magistrate, at Marguerite standing straight as a pendulum rod with their ruined apprenticeship hanging off them like a torn coat. And then he did the only thing left in his ledger: he apologized for interrupting, begged a private moment on grounds of ill health, and walked out of the magistrate's room with a steady, dignified gait that fooled no one who knew him, least of all Edmund, who had spent his childhood learning to read that walk and knew it meant flight.

"He's gone for the river," Marguerite said. Not loudly. To Edmund, and to Bess, who had been admitted to give her testimony and had not yet given it, standing just inside the door with her shawl still iced at the shoulders, her hands red from the walk through the yard.

"The river's frozen," Bess said. "It's frozen solid. Men skate on it."

"It's frozen at the banks," Marguerite said. "Midstream, where the current runs under the ice, it's thinner. He won't know where the thin places are. He doesn't know the river. He only ever crossed it on the bridge."

Bess looked at Marguerite for a long moment. Something passed between them that Edmund could see but not read — the old currency of the district, silence being spent at last in a cause worth its price. Then Bess pulled her shawl tight and said, "Then somebody had better know it better than he does," and went out the door, and Edmund went after her, and the last thing he heard behind him was Pell saying, wearily, to the clerk: "Note the hour. Note that the hour was correct."

* * *

**Bess**

The river lay white under a sky the color of dishwater, and out on it, small and dark and moving fast, went Cobbett — coat tails flying, hat gone, a figure from a nightmare sketched by a child. Behind him the whole compound had emptied onto the banks: weavers first, because word travels fastest among people who have learned to pass it silently, then spinners, then the curious, then the children, running along the ice-edge shouting the way children shout at anything, not knowing yet whether it was a spectacle or a reckoning.

Bess ran too. Her boots broke through the crusted snow at the bank and she kept running, out onto the ice proper, hearing it groan beneath her — a deep, whale-song moan that she felt in her teeth. She had grown up on this river. She knew it the way Cobbett never would: knew that the ice sang differently over the channel, knew that the dark patch ahead where the snow lay thinner was water moving underneath, hungry, awake all winter.

"Cobbett!" she shouted, and her voice cracked across the whiteness. "Daniel Carrick was my brother!"

He heard her. She saw him hear her — saw his stride falter, just once, a hitch like a dropped stitch — and then he ran harder, cutting diagonally for the far bank where the willows stood in their winter rags, where the ice was sheltered and thick. But the diagonal took him over the channel. Of course it did. The shortest line across a river always crosses its deepest heart.

The ice did not explode. Bess would remember that afterward, tell it to her nieces and nephews years later, and always be unable to make them believe it. There was no great crack, no drama of splitting white. There was simply a change in the sound under his feet — a note dropping, the way a clock drops a beat — and then Cobbett went down to his waist, and then to his chest, into water so cold it was not wet so much as a presence, a thing with weight and will, and his scream came up out of the hole thin as wire.

They got him out. That was the terrible part, the part Bess carried longest: they got him out. Weavers waded in on their bellies with belts knotted together, men whose wages he had stolen, whose brother-in-law's hours he had falsified, hauling him out of the black water while the commissioner's men came down the bank with ropes and lanterns, and Cobbett clung to the arms pulling him free and wept and begged, and no one let go. No one let go. Because they were not the kind of men who did that, and Cobbett, gasping and blue-lipped on the ice with the river's truth soaking through his fine coat, seemed finally to understand what kind of men he had been robbing all these years — men who would pull their enemy out of a frozen river and then stand over him, dripping, silent, until the constables arrived.

They arrested him on the bank. The charge sheet was written by lantern light: fraud, falsification of records, obstruction of inquiry. Someone brought a blanket. It was Bess's own thought to bring it, and she regretted it immediately and did it anyway, and threw it around his shoulders roughly, and he looked up at her with his lips shaking and said, "I didn't kill him. I only wrote down what I was told to write."

"Yes," Bess said. "That's exactly what you did."

She turned away and walked back up the bank, and the crowd parted for her, and somewhere in it a woman began to sing — low, wordless, the old spinning-room song that was half lullaby and half complaint — and one by one the voices joined it, until the whole frozen riverbank was humming, and above them all, faint and clear across the compound, the master clock struck six.

* * *

**Marguerite**

Marguerite did not go down to the river. They stood at the window of the counting house with Edmund, watching the lanterns swarm on the white like fireflies trapped under cloth, and when the singing started, Edmund put his hand briefly on their shoulder — the first time he had touched them since everything — and neither of them said anything about it, because some things keep better unwound.

Below, in the yard, the elder Harrow was being helped into the house.

He had come out during the arrest — come out into the snow in his dressing gown, unshaven, an old man suddenly, the empire of small thefts stripped from him like scaffolding from a finished building, leaving everyone to see what it had actually been holding up: not grandeur, only habit. He had stood at the top of the bank steps while his manager was dragged dripping from the river, and he had said nothing, done nothing, and then his hand had gone to his chest and he had sat down where he was, in the snow, without appearing to decide to. Two housemaids had borne him inside. Through the lit windows Marguerite could see the physician arriving, and the son who was no longer a son standing apart from it all, watching, his face unreadable as a locked case.

"He'll live," Edmund said quietly. "Men like my father don't die of shame. They convert it. By spring he'll be telling the commissioners he was deceived, that he grieves for young Carrick, that he intends to endow something — a school, a chapel, something with his name carved large enough to bury the rest."

"And you'll let him?"

"I'll testify to exactly what I know and nothing more." Edmund's breath fogged the glass. "That's the punishment I'd choose for him, if I got to choose. Not ruin. Accuracy. Let history keep his books the way he kept ours — honestly, to the minute, with every entry legible." He turned from the window. In the candlelight Marguerite saw that he looked lighter, somehow, despite everything — a mechanism relieved of a weight it had carried so long it had forgotten it was there. "Marguerite. When this is over. When the inquiry has taken everything it needs and Fenwick's workshop is safe and you've lost the only trade you had—"

"I haven't lost it," Marguerite said. "I've outgrown it. There's a difference, and Master Fenwick knows it better than anyone, which is why it hurts him most."

Edmund almost smiled. "Will you teach me? The lathe. The files. All of it. I have nothing now but time, and for the first time in my life, it's honest time."

Marguerite looked at him a long moment. Outside, the singing had faded; the lanterns on the river were coming back to shore, one by one, like a rosary being counted. Somewhere overhead the observatory clock ticked on through the dark, keeping its new faith, and Marguerite found they were not afraid of it anymore — had not been afraid of it, they realized, since the moment they installed the true escapement and heard it strike, correct, indifferent, magnificent.

"First lesson," Marguerite said. "Starts tomorrow, if the inquiry spares us both. Bring your hands. Leave your name at the door — you won't be needing it."

* * *

Down the corridor, in the small room where the witnesses waited, Bess Carrick sat with her testimony already given, her voice already spent, and watched through the doorway as Commissioner Pell blew out his lamp. Tomorrow the inquiry would begin in earnest: subpoenas, ledgers, looms examined, graves visited. Tonight there was only the clock, striking seven across the sleeping district, and for the first time in living memory, no one in the mill rows stirred at the sound to wonder whether it was lying.

But in the infirmary wing, the elder Harrow woke briefly in the dark, heard the stroke, counted it, and whispered to the nurse — so softly she nearly missed it — *"Four minutes fast. It was always four minutes fast. Fix it."*

And the nurse, who had a brother on the night shift at the mill, wrote the words down, and carried them to the inquiry at dawn.
