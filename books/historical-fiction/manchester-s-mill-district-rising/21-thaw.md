## 21. Thaw

The ice went out of the Irwell on a Tuesday, with a sound like a great door groaning open.

I heard it from the observatory window before I saw it — a long cracking exhale that traveled up through the stone and into the clock's frame and set the pendulum humming in sympathy, as if the master itself had been waiting all winter for permission to move. By noon the river ran brown and muscular between its banks, carrying the winter's wreckage — branches, barrel staves, a cart wheel turning over and over like something drowning — down toward the sea. By the following morning there were men on the bridge with picks and salt barrels, and by the one after that, the toll-keeper's bell rang for the first time since December, and the district breathed.

I stood at the parapet with my tool chest at my feet and watched the first carts cross. Cotton bales going out. Coal coming in. A woman with a market basket who stopped in the exact center of the span, looked down at the water as though confirming it was real, and laughed — actually laughed, aloud, at the sky. The bridge had been a wall all winter. Now it was a bridge again, and everything it touched remembered what it was for.

Bess found me there. She had a way of arriving without announcing herself; I think she'd learned it in the weaving sheds, where you could be fined for speaking but never for appearing silent at someone's shoulder.

"They say you're leaving," she said.

"I'm going home."

"Home." She tried the word the way you test a floorboard that might not hold. "Fenwick's workshop?"

"For now." I looked at my hands. They were still mine, still nicked and scarred along the knuckles from a winter of work done twice — once honestly, once in secret. "There's a letter in my pocket offering me more than that. The commissioners' office wants a journeyman to certify public clocks. Church towers, town halls, the exchange. Every clock the public trusts." I turned the letter over without reading it again; I had read it so many times the creases were soft as cloth. "They asked for me by name. Apparently," and here I smiled despite myself, "I have a reputation for keeping honest hours."

Bess snorted. It was the most elegant thing anyone had said to her all year, and she knew it, and she let me know she knew it by refusing to be impressed.

"Will you take it?"

"I don't know yet."

"You do know. You just haven't said it out loud where Fenwick can hear."

She was right, which is why I'd crossed half the district to stand on a bridge in the wind rather than say it in a warm room. I watched a barge nose through the last drifting plates of ice, its wake cutting white seams in the brown water, and I thought about how a mechanism tells the truth. Not because it is virtuous. Because every tooth engages every other tooth, and a lie anywhere becomes a lie everywhere, and the whole train of wheels carries it up to the hands, and the hands tell it to everyone who looks. There was no way to build a clock that lied gracefully. I had spent a winter learning what happened when men tried.

"Tell your new weavers," I said, "that if any clock in this district runs four minutes slow, they're to send for me. Day or night. I'll come."

"Four minutes an hour," Bess corrected, and we stood together a moment with the arithmetic of it, the stolen wages, the brother whose death had been booked outside working time because working time had been made smaller than the day. Then she squeezed my arm once, hard, the way mill folk shake hands, and went back across the bridge ahead of me, into the district, toward the sheds where she was teaching girls to read their own piece-rates.

* * *

Fenwick's workshop smelled exactly as it had in October: oil and coal smoke and the ghost of solder, the particular warmth of a room where metal has been worked for forty years until the very walls have absorbed it. He did not rise when I came in. He was bent over his bench with a loupe screwed into his eye, repairing something small, and he finished his stroke before he looked up — which was itself a message, and I understood it: *you are welcome, and nothing here has changed, and everything here has changed.*

"Marguerite."

"Master."

He took the loupe out and set it down with exaggerated care, the way he did when he wanted his hands occupied while his mind decided something. "You've grown," he said, which was absurd; I hadn't grown an inch since autumn. But he meant it some other way, and I let him mean it.

He showed me the bench he'd kept cleared. My old place, third from the window, where the light came cleanest in the mornings. My tools were already racked there — no, not my tools. His tools, laid out in my pattern, offered. On the bench beside them sat a watch case, opened, waiting for work, as though I had only been away a fortnight and the world's broken timepieces had politely held their breath.

"Fenwick," I said. "Before I sit down. There's something I have to tell you, and I'd rather tell it standing."

He folded his hands. He had taught me which silences keep people safe; he knew better than anyone how to hold one.

"The commissioners have offered me a post. Certifying public clocks. It's journeyman's work, steady pay, and it would take me out of the workshop most days. Some weeks entirely." I made myself look at him. "I'm going to take it."

For a long moment the only sound was the shop clock — his good regulator, the one true thing in the room — ticking its patient count.

"Do you know," he said finally, "when I sent that letter in January, I wrote three versions. In the first I told you to run. In the second I told you to fight. In the third I told you to finish the work and come home whole." He picked up the opened watch case and turned it slowly, studying the empty space where the movement belonged. "I posted the third because it was the kindest. I've regretted it every day since. A mentor's kindness," he added, dry as file dust, "is often just his fear wearing your coat."

"It kept me alive."

"It kept you *complicit*, for eleven weeks, in a fraud that starved six hundred families. And then it kept you brave, for one afternoon, in a magistrate's room, and that afternoon undid the eleven weeks." He set the case down. "So I find I can't claim credit either way, and neither should you. What I can claim—" and here his voice did something I had never heard it do, something almost young— "is the privilege of watching an apprentice become something I couldn't teach. Clockmaker, they'll call you. Timekeeper. Whatever title the office prints. But I know what you are. You're the person who looked inside the machine everyone else trusted, and told the truth about what was filed away in there."

"Fenwick—"

"Sit down at the bench, Marguerite." And when I didn't move: "*Sit.* You misunderstand me, child. Take the post. Take it today. I'm not losing an apprentice — I'm gaining a correspondent, and a reason to keep the kettle warm, and the considerable satisfaction of being able to say, whenever some guild master sneers at me for training a nobody off the mill lanes, *that nobody now outranks you.*" He screwed the loupe back into his eye, which ended the conversation as effectively as shutting a door. "Now. This fusee needs a new chain, and I've been putting it off for a month because my hands aren't what they were. Come and show me how it's done properly, and then go and sign whatever papers the commissioners put in front of you."

I sat at my bench. I picked up the small pliers. And I will confess here what I confessed to no one else: my eyes blurred, briefly, at the sight of my own tools waiting for me in my own places, and I let them, and then I got to work.

* * *

The commissioners' office occupied two rooms above the assize court, and it smelled of ink and cold stone, and its windows looked out on the bridge — I suspected this was not coincidence. Mr. Aldous, the chief clerk, was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the manner of someone who had waited his whole career for the world to produce a problem exactly shaped like him.

"You'll certify weekly," he said, walking me through the duties. "Every public timepiece within the parish boundaries. You log the rate against your own standard, you seal the log, you post the seal. Any deviation beyond thirty seconds a week, you investigate. Any evidence of tampering—" he looked at me over his spectacles with an expression that was almost, but not quite, a smile— "you report directly to this office, in writing, witnessed. No verbal accounts. No informal understandings. Nothing that can be denied later by men richer than you."

"That last part isn't in the statute."

"No," Aldous agreed. "It's in the job. The statute gives you authority; the writing gives you survival. I watched what happened to you this winter, Eastwick. Half this city called you a hero and the other half called you a liar, and both halves would happily see you ruined if it suited them. Paper," he tapped the ledger on his desk, "is the only witness that doesn't blink. Sign here."

I signed. My hand shook slightly, and I let it, because Aldous was watching and I wanted him to know I understood the weight of what I was taking up. Then he handed me the standard — a marine chronometer in a mahogany box, older than either of us, its certificate signed by astronomers whose names I'd only ever seen in books.

"It keeps time to two seconds a week," Aldous said. "Guard it with your life. Or rather—" the almost-smile again— "guard your life with it. A man carrying the district's standard is a man too useful to hang."

I carried it home through streets just waking from winter, past doorways where women nodded to me now — nodded, openly, in daylight — and past corners where men turned away, and I understood I would live the rest of my life between those two gestures, and that the chronometer in its box was heavier than any tool chest I had ever carried, and that I would carry it gladly.

* * *

Edmund came to the workshop on the first mild evening of March, when the light stayed gold past five o'clock and the gutters ran loud with meltwater.

He knocked — knocked, at the door of a workshop, like any tradesman's son — and when I opened it, I hardly knew him. Not because he was changed in face; the same long bones, the same careful gray eyes. But he wore a plain wool coat with no crest, and his hands were bare of rings, and he carried a canvas bag the way workingmen carry bags, against the body, ready to set it down and pick it up again.

"They struck the name from the rolls on Friday," he said, without preamble, standing in the doorway as if uncertain whether the threshold would bear him. "My father's solicitor read the instrument aloud. Disownment, disinheritance, prohibition from using the surname in trade or otherwise." He said it evenly, the way you recite figures you've copied a hundred times by candlelight. "It cost him nothing. It was, I'm told, drafted weeks ago — before I ever brought him the ledgers. Cobbett's arrest simply gave him occasion."

"What will you be called?"

"Whatever the work requires. Harrow is a mill name now; no one will hire it. I thought—" and here he paused, and for the first time since the observatory, since the midnight confrontation with the true escapement gleaming in my palm, I saw him uncertain— "I thought perhaps the man whose name is on the door might take an apprentice. If the man is willing. If the apprentice is too old, and too educated, and too much trouble."

From inside the workshop, Fenwick's voice floated out, dry as ever: "The man is listening, and the man says the applicant should come in out of the draught before the applicant catches his death and becomes someone else's expense."

Edmund stepped inside. He looked around the little shop — at the benches, the lamp-blackened ceiling, the regulator ticking on the wall — the way I had once looked at the observatory clock: as a cathedral he hadn't known counted as one. Then he set down his bag, opened it, and produced a block of brass, a beginner's graver, and a book on the lathe that must have cost him a month of whatever money remained to him.

"I want to make things that tell the truth," he said. Simply, as though it were a technical specification. "I've spent my life surrounded by mechanisms designed to conceal. I'd like to learn the opposite trade. I'm told it requires patience, honesty, and strong wrists. I have one of the three."

"Which?" Fenwick asked.

"Honesty. Patience I'll have to acquire. As for the wrists—" he flexed his fingers, and I remembered them, steady on the winding key at midnight while the whole district slept under a lie— "we'll find out."

Fenwick looked at me. It was, I realized, the first time in seven years he had ever asked my opinion about anything with his whole face. An apprentice's judgment, weighed as an equal's. I looked at Edmund — at the disowned son holding a graver like a man holding a key he wasn't sure fit the lock — and I thought about bridges, and crossings, and how the district does not forgive, and how forgiveness was never the thing we'd asked of it anyway. Only witness. Only work.

"He starts on the fusee chain," I said. "If he files his thumb in the first hour, that's traditional."

"I've already filed worse than my thumb," Edmund said, and rolled up his sleeves, and sat down at the bench Fenwick cleared for him — fourth from the window, where the evening light came longest.

* * *

That night I walked home alone along the river, the chronometer's box under my arm, and stopped at the center of the bridge the way the woman with the market basket had, to confirm the water was real. It was. Running full and free, carrying the last of the ice seaward, reflecting the first lamps of evening in long shaken ribbons of gold.

Below, on the far bank, the observatory dome caught the light — my charge now, wound and certified and logged every Sunday, keeping honest hours over a district that had paid terribly for them and would not soon forget the price. Somewhere behind me, Bess was locking up the weaving shed where she trained girls to read their own worth. Somewhere ahead, in a cramped workshop, a man with no name was learning to cut his first wheel.

I thought of Master Fenwick's oldest lesson, the one that had governed my whole life before this winter: *don't ask, don't repeat, don't look up.* I had unlearned it one clause at a time, and each clause had cost something, and I stood on the bridge in the cold spring air and tallied the account the way I'd been taught to tally — precisely, without flinching.

An apprenticeship: spent. A name Edmund never chose and never deserved: spent. Eleven weeks of complicity, one dead weaver's misrecorded hours, a friendship tested in a stairwell, a mentor's love revealed as a cage I had to walk out of with my own hands: all entered in the ledger, all paid.

And against those entries — the balance side, the side that made it worth it — a trade of my own. A standard to guard. A seal that did not blink. Six hundred wage packets, come quarter-day, carrying minutes that belonged to the people who had earned them.

The river moved beneath me, unhurried, certain, going about its long business of carrying everything downstream. I tightened my grip on the box and walked on toward the lit windows of the workshop, where two lamps now burned instead of one, and behind me the master clock above the observatory began, softly and truly, to strike the hour.
