## 22. Keeping Honest Hours

The key is cold enough to burn.

I have learned to hold it in my palm a moment before I turn it — the brass of the observatory door takes the winter's temper into itself and gives it back to whoever comes asking. One year of Sundays now, and still I stand on the step like a stranger, breath smoking, the river running loud and open below me where the ice broke apart in February with a sound like cannon fire. The thaw came late that year. It came, though. It always comes, if you can wait it out without becoming something else while you wait.

Inside, the stairwell smells of stone and lamp oil and, faintly, of Bess's lavender water, because she comes up these stairs more often than any weaver has business doing. The master clock stands at the top of everything, as it always has: a cathedral of brass in its glass case, pendulum swinging with the patience of something that has outlived every hand that ever touched it, including mine.

Especially mine.

I wind it first. That is the order of things now, the ritual the commissioners wrote into my certificate of appointment in their careful clerk's hand: *Marguerite Eastwick, Keeper of the Public Clocks of Ancoats and Beswick, to present the mechanism for inspection quarterly and to keep honest hours.* Honest hours. As though honesty were a substance you could measure, four minutes to the pound. Perhaps it is. I have seen what happens when men weigh it out short.

The great weight rises on its cable, groaning through eleven floors of geared patience, and settles at the top of its run with a small satisfied click that I feel in my teeth before I hear it. Then I set the winding crank down, and I do what I did not dare to do for most of last winter: I look at the escapement.

It keeps true time. Four minutes a day, it does not lose. The pallet stones sit in their cradles at the angles I cut them, and the escape wheel releases tooth by tooth by tooth, each one paid out in full, and when I put my ear to the case I can hear the whole district's wages ticking out evenly, fairly, one second owed and one second given. Some nights I dream of file marks. Fresh ones, bright as scars across polished steel. In the dreams I am always holding a file myself, and I wake with my hands closed into fists so tight my knuckles ache until morning.

But this morning is not a dream, and the clock is honest, and the light coming through the east windows lies along the floor in long gold bars, and somewhere below me the mill district is waking up to another Sunday — which is to say, to the only day its people own outright.

* * *

Bess Carrick meets me at the bottom of the observatory steps, as she does most Sundays, with her shawl pinned crooked and her boots leaving wet prints on the flagstones. She is broader than she was a year ago. Grief does that sometimes; it builds you up around the hollow like a tree building around lightning.

"Your face says it's keeping time," she says.

"My face says nothing. That's how I was trained."

"Trained," she repeats, and snorts, and offers her arm anyway, which is how she tells me I'm forgiven for the joke. We walk down together toward the river path, past the observatory's iron railing furred with frost, past the corner where the coal carts turn in. The district spreads out below us in its Sunday quietness — or its Sunday semblance of quietness, because even now there are looms going in three mills whose owners keep no Sabbath their workers would recognize. The sound carries over the rooftops like distant surf.

"They've taken six new girls at Carrick's old shed," Bess says. Old shed meaning her brother's shed, meaning the shed where Daniel worked the loom that killed him, meaning the shed the mill recorded him leaving an hour before he died. She says it plainly now. A year ago the name caught in her like a bobbin thread. "Two of them are barely fourteen. I've told them the first rule."

"Which rule?"

"Don't ask, don't repeat, don't look up." She glances sideways at me. "And then I told them the second rule, the one nobody taught us."

"Which is?"

"You can be silent all your life and call it survival, or you can be silent right up until the moment silence becomes a lie, and then you'd better be ready to speak with your hands full." She smiles, thin and fierce. "You taught me that one. You were too busy being terrified to notice you were teaching it."

We come out onto the river path. The bridge stands ahead of us, iron-legged, streaming with thaw-water and early sunlight, and the traffic of Sunday crosses it both ways — families walking off to the fields beyond the toll, courting couples, a man carrying a cage of pigeons who will race them home from some far rooftop and bet a week's wages on their return. The bridge is open now. It stays open all year round since the inquiry, since the commissioners ordered the tolls posted publicly and the closure schedule abolished — a small thing, a bureaucratic thing, but Bess stopped and read the notice board the day they put it up, and she stood there reading it twice, and then she laughed, once, hard, the way you laugh when something hurts less than it used to.

"The district remembers," she says now, watching the crossing crowd. "You know that."

"I know."

"Cobbett's sister still runs the chandlery on Miller Street, and she'll spit on the ground when you pass, and half of Chapel Row won't buy from Fenwick anymore, and there's a verse about the Harrow name going around the weaving sheds that isn't fit for church." She says it all without apology. "They remember. They resent. And they get up Monday and go back to the looms, because remembering doesn't fill a belly. That's endurance, Marguerite. It's not pretty, but it's ours."

"I never wanted to be remembered."

"No." Bess looks at me then, fully, with those grey eyes that watched her brother's coffin go into ground that had been sold twice before he was buried in it. "You wanted to be safe. So did he. So did I. Safe turned out to be a room with the door bricked up. Better to be remembered and free." She squeezes my arm. "Come on. Edmund will have been at the bench since dawn, and if we don't drag him out he'll forget to eat again, and then he'll be insufferable about his eyesight."

* * *

Edmund works now in the front room of the house on Foundry Lane that the three of us rent between us — two rooms and a lean-to workshop, a chimney that draws badly, a view of the canal where the barges queue like patient cattle. He kept nothing from the house on the hill except his grandfather's lathe, bought back at auction by Bess's collection among the weavers, a penny here and a ha'penny there, presented to him wrapped in sacking with no card. He wept over it. I have never mentioned that I saw, and I never will, because I know which silences keep people safe, and that one keeps him.

Today the front room smells of oil and hot metal and the bread Bess left rising under a cloth. Edmund sits bent over the bench, sleeves rolled, a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye, working on something small enough to disappear into his palm when he shifts his grip. He does not look up when we come in. He learned long ago — from me, though he'd say from Fenwick's ghost — that a craftsman's greeting is the work itself, shown when it's ready and not one minute sooner.

He is thinner than he was a year ago. Stripped of his name, he lost weight the way trees lose leaves in a bad autumn — gradually, then all at once, then regrown differently, harder wood. The lawyers took the surname and the settlement and the expectation of everything; the city took the rest, or tried to. There are still public houses where he cannot drink, still counting-house clerks who find reasons to close their doors when he passes. His father lives now in a rented house in Southport, attended by one servant, visited by no one, and the elder Harrow's collapse at the inquiry became within a month a kind of local legend — struck down, people said, by the arithmetic itself, by the sum of four minutes times ten years times eight hundred workers, calculated aloud by a factory commissioner in a voice like a ledger closing.

Edmund signs his work now with only a device: a small bridge, five strokes of the graver, arch and deck and two piers.

He finishes whatever it is he is finishing, sets down the graver, blows across the metal, and holds it out to me on his open palm without a word.

It is a watch. Small — a lady's size, or a child's — cased in gunmetal, the dial plain white enamel with black Roman figures, the movement visible through a pierced back. And engraved on the case bow, fine as a hair, so that you must tilt it to the light to see it at all: a bridge. Five strokes. Arch and deck and two piers.

"It's not commissioned," he says. His voice has changed in a year too; the drawing-room polish wore off it somewhere between the magistrate's room and the lean-to workshop, and what grew in its place is quieter and rougher and better. "Not exactly. I mean — it is, but the commission was strange. Come here, I'll show you the letter."

He fetches it from the drawer beneath the bench. The paper is good quality, mill-owner quality, and the hand is a young woman's, careful and slightly formal, the handwriting of someone educated past her station and proud of it. Bess reads over my shoulder, because Bess has never in her life pretended not to read over someone's shoulder.

*To the watchmaker at Foundry Lane* — no name, I notice; the city has decided he has none, and he has decided to agree — *My father was an overseer at Harrow Mills for nineteen years. He took the falsified wage without knowing it was false, and when he learned, it broke something in him he never mended. He died in March. I have saved for a year to pay for a watch that keeps honest time, to be buried with him, so that he goes into the ground owing nothing and owed nothing. I am told you understand such matters. Charge me what it cost you truly, and no less.*

No less. I look up at Edmund. He is watching me read it, and there is a expression on his face I have learned to read the way I once learned to read escapements — slowly, by lamplight, with great care.

"She wouldn't give me her name either," he says. "She paid in advance, in coins counted out on the counter of the coffee house, and she wouldn't sit down. But she said—" he stops, and starts again. "She said her father used to walk home over the bridge every night at six, and he used to say the bridge was the only honest thing left in the district, because it held whoever walked on it, rich or poor, and asked nothing about their hours."

The room is very quiet. Somewhere outside, a barge horn sounds on the canal, low and mournful, and the bread under its cloth continues its slow invisible work, rising.

"So I made it true," Edmund says. "The movement. I checked it against the observatory clock three times over seven days, and it lost nothing. Not a second. Not a second, Marguerite. And when it's buried with him, it'll still be running — they bury them running, did you know that? Running down into silence, but honestly, all the way down."

Bess turns away abruptly and goes to the window, and I pretend not to see her wipe her eyes, because I know which silences keep people safe, and that one keeps her.

"And yours?" Edmund asks me, nodding at my coat pocket. "Is it finished?"

* * *

Mine is finished. It has been finished for three weeks, waiting for today, waiting for the courage I keep having to re-mine out of myself like a seam that closes behind you.

I take it out of my pocket and set it on his bench beside the dead girl's-father's watch: a pocket watch of my own making, my first complete piece, every wheel cut on Fenwick's old lathe during the evenings of the long winter, every pivot turned, every jewel set by my own hand. On the inside of the case back I have engraved a single line of lettering, and I have practiced engraving it forty times on scrap brass before I trusted myself to touch the real piece, because some words must not waver:

*KEEPS HONEST HOURS.*

"That's not for sale," I say.

"No," Edmund agrees softly. "It isn't, is it."

"Fenwick wrote to me again. Last week. Third letter since the thaw." I recite it from memory, because I know it by heart the way I once knew the rules of silence. "'Come back. The bench is yours. What's done is done and the town forgets faster than you think. Come home whole.'" I look at the watch lying on the bench, at the two watches, his and mine, side by side in the grey northern light. "Whole. As if I could go back to being the person who didn't look up. As if that person still exists anywhere inside me."

"Does she?"

"I don't know. I think—" I stop. This is the hardest sentence I have ever built, harder than any testimony, harder than the arithmetic I laid out before the magistrate with the clock's own guts spread on the table like evidence, which is what they were. "I think she exists, but she can't hold the tools anymore. Someone else grew around her. Like Bess said — grief builds you up around the hollow. Only mine wasn't grief. It was the opposite. It was finding out I could speak."

Edmund picks up his watch, the bridge-watch, and closes it with a click, and for a moment we stand there in the smell of oil and bread and canal-water, two people who met across a sabotaged mechanism measuring each other like unfamiliar gears, wondering whether they would mesh. A year later I can tell him the answer: we meshed. Not smoothly — there is backlash in us yet, a rattle of old fear and old guilt that no amount of adjustment will entirely take up. But a train of wheels doesn't need perfection. It needs engagement. It needs each wheel willing to carry the next.

"Bess has been offered the training shed permanently," he says. "Commissioners' funding. She'll have thirty girls by summer."

"And the quarterly inspection is Thursday, and I'll present the master clock, and Cobbett's replacement — that nervous little man, Whitlow — will check my figures against the standard chronometer and sign his name, and the district will go on."

"And we'll go on."

"Yes," I say. "That's the whole of it, isn't it? That's the trade I apprenticed to without knowing. Not clockmaking. Going on."

Bess turns from the window, red-eyed and grinning, and announces that if the bread burns she will hold us both personally responsible, and the ordinary machinery of the day engages — cloth lifted, oven door, kettle, argument about whether Sunday counts as a working day for watchmakers (it does not; Edmund insists; Bess points out he has been at the bench since dawn). I stand a moment longer at the bench, looking at the two watches.

Then I take up my own, my first-born, keeps-honest-hours, and I do not put it in my pocket. I wind it — full, hearing the mainspring take its tension sweetly, no grit, no protest — and I carry it to the window where the light is best, and I set it beside the cheap tin clock on the mantel that runs two minutes fast because nobody has ever loved it, and I let it tick.

One year ago I stood in a gilded cage and learned that a clock could lie. Today the clock on the hill keeps true time because I made it, the bridge stands open because I spoke, a dead weaver's name is carved on a stone his sister paid for with weavers' pennies, and a watchmaker with no name is finishing a commission that will keep honest time in the dark of a grave.

The district remembers. Let it remember.

Outside, the bells of St. Clement's begin to strike the hour — and for the first time in a year, they are right.
