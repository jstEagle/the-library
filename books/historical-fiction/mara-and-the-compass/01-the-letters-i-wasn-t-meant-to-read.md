## 1. The Letters I Wasn't Meant to Read

There are three things a woman in my position must never be: noticed, needed, or believed.

I had been driving for Mrs. Marchmont for two years by the summer of 1914, and in that time I had perfected the art of being furniture with hands. When she swept out of the house on her way to another meeting of the Cornwall Women's Suffrage Society, I was already at the wheel, cap pulled low, expression arranged into that particular blankness that servants learn before they learn anything else. When she argued with vicars, I was polishing the mud off the fenders. When she wept — and she did weep, twice a year, quietly, into her good linen handkerchief behind the locked door of her study — I was somewhere else entirely, being nowhere, which is the most useful place a person like me can be.

My name is Coral Villalobos. My father was a Spanish sailor who washed ashore in Porthleven and stayed just long enough to give me a name nobody could pronounce and a face nobody forgot, which is the worst possible combination for a woman whose survival depends on forgetting. The village called him the Spaniard. They call me nothing at all, which suits me fine.

Or it suited me fine, once. I'll come to what changed.

* * *

The letters began as an accident, the way all great sins do.

I had been delivering correspondence for Mrs. Marchmont since my second week — she trusted me with it because I could read, which put me above half the girls in service, and because I drove everywhere anyway and petrol cost the same whether the tank carried one errand or five. On a Tuesday in October, 1912, I collected a bundle from the post office in Helston: three letters for Mrs. Marchmont, two for the vicarage, one for Dr. Penhale, and a small brown parcel for a widow up toward Breage whose son was in Canada and who paid me tuppence a trip to carry his letters the last mile.

It was raining, of course. It is always raining in Cornwall; the county is simply the sea's way of complaining about England. My coat was soaked through, and I sheltered under the awning of the closed bakery to wait out the worst of it, and there I made the mistake that has defined my life ever since: I looked down at the letter on top, addressed to Mrs. Marchmont in a slanted, confident hand, and I read the first line through the thin paper without meaning to. The ink had run slightly with the damp. It might as well have been written on glass.

*My dear Edith, they are calling you a danger to the peace of this county...*

I should have put it away. Any sensible woman would have put it away. But I was twenty-three years old, wet through, and hungry in a way that had nothing to do with bread, and the letter said *danger*, and I wanted to know who thought my Mrs. Marchmont dangerous, and why, and whether they were right.

I read the whole thing. Standing under an awning in the rain, holding another woman's secrets in my cold hands, I read every word.

That was the first. There have been hundreds since.

* * *

You must understand — I don't excuse myself, but you must understand — that reading other people's letters is not the vice it appears. It is not greed. It is closer to hunger, and hunger is not a sin, only a condition. A girl who belongs nowhere will eat anywhere.

Here is what I knew by June of 1914, none of which anyone had told me:

I knew that Dr. Penhale's wife drank laudanum and wrote to her sister in Truro about it in code so gentle the sister probably never understood, calling it "my headaches" and "the grey days."

I knew that the vicar's curate was engaged to two young ladies at once, one in Cornwall and one in Bath, and that he kept both engagements alive through letters of such breathtaking devotion that I sometimes wondered if he ought to be published rather than defrocked.

I knew that Widow Chenoweth's son in Canada had stopped writing for four months and then resumed without explanation, and that she never asked him why in her replies, and that her silence on the page was so loud I had to set the letter down and look out the window for a while.

And I knew everything there was to know about Edith Marchmont, which is how I came to love her, though I'd have bitten my own tongue bloody before saying so.

She received letters from eleven women across the county who were planning something for the autumn — I never learned precisely what, only that it involved railway timetables and a great deal of the word *discreet*. She received hate mail, some of it unsigned and cowardly, one piece so vile that she read it twice, folded it very small, and burned it in the study grate while I stood in the doorway pretending to ask about the car. She received letters from a prison governor's office regarding a Miss Alice Grey, remanded at Holloway, and I learned from those letters that Edith sent money every month to pay the woman's fines, and had done for years, and had instructed the governor never to tell her the source.

That last one I read on a Sunday evening, standing in the hall while Mrs. Marchmont took her constitutional around the garden, and I remember that the light was going gold on the wallpaper and I felt something crack open in my chest that has never properly healed. Here was a woman who bought strangers' freedom and forbade them their gratitude. What does a person do, loving someone like that? You can't repay her. You can't even tell her you know. You can only drive her where she needs to go and keep your mouth shut and hope that loyalty counts for something in whatever ledger God keeps.

I have driven her through storms that turned the road to river. I have stood outside halls in Falmouth and Penzance while men spat at the wheels of the car and shouted things at her through the windows, things I will not repeat, and I sat with my hands on the wheel and did nothing, because doing something would mean being seen, and being seen would mean losing the position, and losing the position would mean — well. In 1912 it would have meant the workhouse or worse. By 1914 it meant something different, though I couldn't have told you what yet. Only that the arithmetic had shifted, the way arithmetic does when you stop counting your own safety first.

* * *

Mrs. Marchmont herself — since you'll want her described, and I am the only one left to describe her — was sixty-one that summer and built like a lighthouse: broad, pale, immovable, with eyes the grey of a sea before weather. She dressed in tweed the colour of wet slate and wore a hat that could have sheltered a family of four. She spoke the way other people fired rifles: rarely, precisely, and with effect.

She was not kind, exactly. Kindness implies softness, and there was nothing soft about her. She was *just*, which is a harder metal. She paid me above the going rate and never mentioned it. She taught me to check the magneto myself rather than waiting for the garage boy in Helston, on the grounds that "a woman who waits for a man to fix her motorcar deserves to wait." She corrected my grammar once — only once, over the word *whom*, delivered with such gentleness that I went home and practiced *whom* in sentences until midnight, which tells you more about me than about her.

And once — this I keep like a pressed flower between the pages of my memory — once, after the spitting incident outside Penzance, she climbed into the car, straightened her magnificent hat, and said, without looking at me: "They shout at me, Coral, because I am a symbol. Symbols can't be hurt. But you sit there hearing it, and you are not a symbol, and that is the harder post. I see you."

Then she opened her newspaper and said nothing further for eleven miles.

*I see you.* Two words. I have been paid wages, given references, handed a Christmas goose every December, and none of it weighed what those two words weighed. That is the terrible economics of invisibility: it takes almost nothing to end it, and the people who could afford it mostly never think to spend.

* * *

Now. About Herr Brandt, because I cannot tell this story honestly without telling it, and the story refuses to begin until he is on the page.

Aldous Brandt kept the clockmaker's shop in Porthnaven, the narrow street below the church, between the ironmonger and a chandler that smelled permanently of tar and rope. He was German — Bavarian, he'd say, with a small correcting smile, as though Germany were too large a word for what he actually was — and he had lived in the village longer than most of the people who muttered about him. Thirty years. His English had Cornish sand in it. He mended the church clock without charge and refused thanks on the grounds that "a clockmaker who lets God's time run wrong will answer for it."

In the winter of 1909, when I was nineteen and running from my father's house with nine shillings and a bruise the shape of a belt buckle under my collar, I walked through Porthnaven at two in the morning because it was the only street with lights still burning. The shop window glowed gold onto the cobbles, and inside, an old man sat at his bench with a jeweller's loupe screwed into his eye, working on something smaller than a fingernail, and he looked up at me through the glass as though he'd been expecting me for years.

He didn't ask questions. He gave me tea and a chair by the stove and worked in silence until dawn, and in the morning he said, "You have the hands of a careful person. Come Saturday, I will teach you to clean a movement, and you may earn your tea honestly instead of freezing for it." I went every Saturday for a year. He taught me escapements and mainsprings and the names of tools in two languages, and he never once asked where I slept or what I ran from, and when Mrs. Marchmont advertised for a driver who could also mind accounts, it was Aldous Brandt who walked me up the hill himself and told her, "This girl reads better than your solicitor and lies worse than a child. You will not find better."

So when I say I owed him — understand that the word is inadequate. He was the first person who looked at my hands instead of my face. Everything I am runs on springs he wound.

All of which is prologue to the fact that in June of 1914, Aldous Brandt was still in Porthnaven, still winding the church clock, still beloved and tolerated in that particular English way that means *loved until further notice*.

Further notice came in August.

* * *

But I am ahead of myself, and a keeper of secrets should know better than to spend them early. Let me stay in June, the last golden month, when none of us knew anything except what we read in each other's letters.

The last week of that month, I made my usual rounds. Post office in Helston; letters for Mrs. Marchmont, the vicarage, Dr. Penhale; the widow's Canadian letter; and one item I hadn't seen before — a long envelope, foreign stamps, addressed to Mrs. Marchmont in a hand I knew at once, because I had watched it label drawers of watch parts for a year of Saturdays. Small, upright, patient lettering, each word built like it was meant to last a century.

Aldous Brandt, writing to Edith Marchmont.

Under any other sky I'd have read it in the bakery doorway as usual, rain or no rain, guilt or no guilt. But the sun was out — an omen, if I'd been fool enough to read omens — and Mrs. Marchmont was home, and something in me said *not this one*. Some instincts arrive wearing no reason at all. I put it unopened on the hall tray with the others and told myself I was growing virtuous in my old age.

Mrs. Marchmont read it in her study with the door open, which she never did with important letters, and afterward she came to the kitchen doorway — the kitchen doorway, where no mistress of that house had stood since the house was built — holding the letter folded in her fist.

"Coral," she said. "How long have you known Aldous Brandt?"

"Since I was nineteen, ma'am."

Something crossed her face then. I've thought about that crossing many times since, in the dark, in the years after, trying to name it. It wasn't surprise. It was the look of a woman watching two separate roads turn out to meet at the same bridge.

"He saved my life," she said, "in 1888. In Innsbruck. A fever, and a stranger who nursed a foreign woman through it because no one else would touch her." She smoothed the letter against her skirt, though it was already smooth. "He writes that he may need a favour of me one day soon. He doesn't say what. He says—" and here she paused, and I saw her choose, deliberately, to give me the words, "—he says he hopes it will be a small thing, but he fears it will not be, and he says I am to trust the people I trust completely, and no others."

She looked at me then, straight on, the lighthouse beam passing over me.

"I trust you completely, Coral," she said. "I want you to know that, before whatever comes."

And I — invisible, unreadable, the furniture with hands — I stood in the kitchen doorway with dishwater cooling on my skin and felt my whole careful architecture tremble, because she had spent her two words and now here were six more, and I understood that the ledger had opened, and that whatever Brandt feared was coming, and that I was already inside it.

I said, "Yes, ma'am," which was a lie, because I knew nothing of the sort, and I went back to my sink.

Three nights later there was a storm coming in off the Atlantic the way they do — black wall, no negotiation — and Mrs. Marchmont had gone up to London on suffrage business, and I was alone in the house with the lamps lit and the shutters rattling.

At half past midnight, someone knocked on the front door.

Small knock. Low down, near the bottom of the door, the height of a child's fist.

I stood in the hall with the candle shaking in my hand and thought of Brandt's letter, of *trust the people you trust completely*, and I went and opened it.
