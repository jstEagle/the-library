## 2. A Knock After Midnight

The storm came up the coast the way bad news always did — quietly at first, then all at once.

I had felt it in my knees by supper, an old ache that had nothing to do with age and everything to do with a childhood spent sleeping in a damp attic in Penzance. By nine o'clock the wind was worrying at the shutters like a dog that wanted in. By ten, the rain had turned horizontal. And by half past eleven, when I should have been asleep in the narrow bed behind the kitchen with Edith's spare blanket pulled to my chin, I was sitting up instead with a candle stub and one of her letters, reading it a second time because the first reading had unsettled me and I hadn't yet decided what to do about being unsettled.

It was a letter from London. From a Mrs. Pankhurst's office, or near enough — Edith kept her suffrage correspondence separate from the parish post precisely so that I wouldn't read it, which is why she locked it in the writing desk, and why I had learned years ago that a hairpin opens most locks made before the war. Not this war. The other one. There is always another one coming; you can feel it in the weather.

I will say plainly what I have never said aloud: I read Edith Marchmont's letters. All of them. The parish letters she leaves on the hall table for posting, the private letters she thinks are safe in their drawers, the telegrams she burns in the study grate without finishing. It began as accident — a letter left open on the passenger seat of her motorcar, addressed to her sister, describing an operation Edith was ashamed of and a marriage Edith had escaped — and continued as something between compulsion and devotion. You cannot drive a woman twice a week to Truro and back, hour upon hour of Cornish lanes, and not come to love her. And you cannot love a woman who trusts you with her life and not wonder what else she might trust you with, if she only thought to ask.

She never asked. So I took.

Not everything. I have rules. I never take money, and I never take anything that would get a person hanged, only things that would get them pitied, which is a different crime entirely. And I keep every secret as carefully as if it were my own — better, in fact, since my own secrets have never once been worth the keeping.

That night the letter was from her solicitor. It said, in the roundabout way solicitors have, that the government was considering a new measure against militant suffragists, that certain names had been noted, and that Mrs. Marchmont ought perhaps to consider a period of quiet. I sat with the candle guttering and thought: *Edith Marchmont has never once been quiet in her life.* And then I thought how strange it was that I could know a person so thoroughly — her debts, her disappointments, the name of the man in Bristol she still wrote to every Christmas and never posted — and still be, in every way that mattered, a stranger standing in her hallway holding her candle.

That is the trouble with knowing everything. It is a kind of belonging, but it is belonging to a house, not to a family. The house knows everything too, and nobody invites the house to supper.

* * *

I heard the knock over the wind, which tells you something, because the wind that night sounded like the whole Atlantic being poured through a funnel.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a person makes who is afraid of the answer.

I told myself it was a branch. I told myself it was the shutter coming loose on the north side, the way it did every November, and that in the morning I would fetch the ladder and the nails and swear at it properly. I told myself all the sensible things a sensible woman tells herself at midnight when she is alone in a large house with one candle and no lock on the front door that anyone could call honest.

Then it came again. Three knocks, and then — this is the part I cannot explain even now, though I have turned it over a hundred times since — three more, softer, almost apologetic, as though whoever stood there feared they had been rude the first time.

Edith was in London. She had gone down on Tuesday for the campaign, for the meetings and the marches and whatever it was she wasn't telling her solicitor, and she was not due back until Saturday. The housekeeper, Mrs. Pengelly, had gone home to her sister in St. Ives before the roads turned wicked, and I had waved her off with a lie about how I liked having the house to myself. Which was true. I did like it. It was the only time the house belonged to me instead of the other way round.

So it was me or nobody.

I took the poker. I am not proud of the poker; it seems in retrospect a foolish thing to bring to answer a knock made by someone small enough to be polite about knocking. But fear does its arithmetic quickly and badly, and mine said: *storm, night, alone, girl in the house, girl in the papers* — for Edith's photograph had been in the *Western Morning News* that very week, scowling splendidly under her hat — and the sum of it was *poker.*

I unbarred the door and opened it four inches, which is as far as the swollen wood would allow, and the wind came through the gap like a slap and put out my candle.

And there, in the sudden dark, was a child.

I want to describe her properly, because later — much later — there were people who asked me what I remembered of that first moment, and I found my memory had done what memory does, worn the edges smooth and kept only the strange bright pieces. The strange bright pieces were these: her boots, soaked through, making small sucking sounds on the step as she shifted her weight; her hair, dark and plastered flat, so wet it looked painted on; and her eyes, which were fixed on my face with an attention no child has ever given me before or since, as though she were checking me off against some list she'd been given.

"You're her," the child said.

"I'm nobody," I said, which was true, and which I meant as reassurance, and which I saw at once was neither. "Who sent you? Where's your mother?"

The child didn't answer. Instead she held up her hand, and in it was a note — folded once, twice, sealed with wax, which struck me as absurdly formal for a child standing in a gale at midnight — and when I reached for it she drew it back an inch.

"You have to read it," she said. "He said you'd read it."

"*He* said—" I began. But the wind chose that moment to shove the door wider on its own, and the child flinched back from the light spilling out of the hallway behind me, flinched like a creature that had been taught, recently and thoroughly, what light costs. And something in my chest went sideways. I stepped back and pulled her in by the wrist before I had decided to, the way you catch a falling cup without thinking, and shut the door against the storm, and stood in the dark listening to the rain hammer the slates and feeling the small cold hand in mine and thinking, with terrible clarity: *this is the moment everything after gets sorted into before and after.*

I relit the candle. My hands were steadier than they had any right to be. I broke the seal.

The note was short. Four words and a mark.

*Forget this face,* it said.

Beneath, where a signature ought to be, there was a little stamped impression in the wax: a circle, and inside the circle a set of gears — or perhaps it was a compass rose, the light was poor and my hands were not steady anymore — and beneath the gears, tiny, precise, unmistakable to anyone who had ever looked closely at the underside of a watch: a single letter, **A.B.**

I knew that mark.

I knew it the way you know a voice on the telephone, the way you know footsteps on your own stairs. Aldous Brandt stamped it inside the case-back of every clock he ever sold in that county, and he had sold clocks in that county for thirty years, and one of them — a small brass carriage clock, the kind with a handle like a purse-frame — had stood on the mantel of a certain room above a certain shop in Penzance on a certain night eleven years ago when a seventeen-year-old girl arrived on the doorstep with a bruise going yellow on her jaw and nowhere else in the world to go, and the clockmaker had looked at her exactly as long as it takes to wind a watch, and then stood aside and said, *You will take the room, and you will take the soup, and tomorrow we will speak of nothing.*

We had spoken of nothing. That was his gift. He had given me a month of silence in a warm room above ticking things, and soup, and a job winding clocks until my hands learned gentleness again, and he had never once asked my surname, and when I left he had pressed a coin into my palm and closed my fingers over it and said, *You owe me nothing. But if ever a door knocks for you, remember how heavy doors are, and how easy to open.*

I had spent eleven years believing I owed him nothing. Standing in Edith Marchmont's hallway with his seal broken in my hand, I understood that I had believed a comfortable lie.

* * *

The child had not moved. She stood just inside the door in a puddle of her own making, dripping on the flagstones, watching me read with the particular stillness children achieve when they have been coached — *stand still, don't cry, don't tell them your name* — and are holding onto the coaching with both hands because it's all they've been given.

"Is he all right?" I asked. "The man who sent you. Is he—"

"You're not supposed to ask." Her voice wobbled on it. "You're supposed to forget."

"Forget." I looked at the note again, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something more sensible. "Child, it is past midnight, you are soaked to the skin, and someone has walked you — walked you, in this? — to a stranger's door with instructions to *forget you*. Do you understand that forgetting is the one thing I cannot do?"

"He said you would say that." Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the way lightning is the ghost of the sun. "He said, *tell her she'll be angry, and then tell her she'll do it anyway.*"

I laughed. It came out of me sideways, surprised, half a sob wearing laughter's coat, because it was such a Brandt thing to say — such a precise, unsentimental, entirely correct thing — and because being known like that, predicted like that, by a man I hadn't seen in eleven years, undid something in me that eleven years of careful invisibility had very neatly tied.

"All right," I said. "Come away from the door. Come here, by the stove. Don't touch anything, and — no, wait. First tell me one thing, and only one thing, and I won't ask any others tonight, I give you my word. Are you followed?"

She considered this with a gravity that would have done credit to a magistrate. Then she shook her head.

"Not anymore," she said. "There was a man on the train. Papa said if a man sits too close, get off at the next station and walk the other way. So I got off. And then I walked." She paused. "It was two days."

Two days. Two days, alone, a child of — eight? nine? — walking coastal roads in November, following instructions written by a man whose seal I held in my hand. I did the arithmetic of it and stopped doing the arithmetic of it, because the answer was unbearable.

"Right," I said, and my voice came out rougher than intended, so I cleared it and tried again. "Well. You can't go back, and you can't stay in that doorway, and I am apparently incapable of forgetting your face, so it seems we're stuck with each other until morning at least. Come by the fire. There's soup."

"There's soup?" she said, and for the first time her whole face moved — the whole architecture of it, cheekbones and chin and those enormous eyes — and I thought, with a lurch: *whoever you belong to, you belong to someone who laughs, underneath it all. Someone taught you to hope for soup.*

"There is always soup in this house," I said, steering her toward the kitchen. "Mrs. Pengelly believes the world might end at any moment and one ought to meet it fed. It's the only theology she and I share."

* * *

She ate three bowls. I counted. I sat across from her with my own bowl untouched and watched her eat the way you'd watch a small fire in a dry season — with attention, and a kind of dread, and no intention whatsoever of looking away.

By candlelight I could see her properly for the first time. Thin, in the way that isn't poverty exactly but is adjacent to it — the way of children who've been traveling. A coat too big for her, good wool, cut for an adult, sleeves rolled and rolled and rolled. Boots ruined. Around her neck, on a cord, a shape under her collarbone that she touched once, unconsciously, when she caught me looking — touched it and tucked it away, the way you'd hide a letter.

And her face. I kept trying not to look at her face and failing, because there was something in it — something in the set of the brows, in the particular stubborn line of the mouth — that plucked at a string I couldn't find. As though I'd seen her before. As though I'd seen someone before, and she was the echo of him.

"Papa said you'd feed me," she said, around the third bowl, "and you'd ask nothing till morning. He said you were the best reader of letters in Cornwall." A pause. A frown of effort, a child reciting a lesson she doesn't fully understand. "He said — he said you read what isn't yours, and you keep it anyway. Like a church keeps sins."

I set down my spoon.

"Well," I said, after a moment. "Your papa sounds like a man who says a great deal."

"He doesn't, though." She looked genuinely puzzled. "He hardly says anything. He fixes clocks and he hums and he says *gut, gut* when a spring goes right. These are the most words he's ever said at once, I think. He practiced." She lowered her eyes to her bowl. "I heard him practicing in the night. Saying your — saying what to tell the lady. He got it wrong three times and started over."

The stove ticked. Rain ran down the window in ropes. Somewhere upstairs a shutter gave up its argument with the wind and banged, and neither of us jumped, which I think was the moment I decided — decided properly, in the place below decision where decisions are already made and one merely catches up to them — that whatever came through that door next, constable or storm or the whole machinery of a country gone mad with suspicion, I was not handing this child back into the dark.

"What's your name?" I asked. "You needn't give me the long version. Just the part you'd write in sand and wash away."

She looked at me for a long time. Checking me off against her list again. Whatever she found there must have satisfied her, because she said:

"Mara."

Just that. No second name offered, none asked for. In my experience — and my experience of other people's secrets was, by then, considerable — a Christian name alone is either a child too young to have secrets or a child carrying one too heavy to share. Hers sat between us on the table like the fourth bowl of soup nobody needed.

"Mara," I repeated. "Well, Mara. I'm Coral. Coral Villalobos. And I'm going to tell you three things, and then you're going to sleep, because you look like a coat hung on a nail. One: you may stay tonight, and tomorrow we'll see. Two: if anyone knocks, you go in the linen cupboard and you don't breathe loud. Three—" and here I hesitated, because the third thing was the note, which I had set beside my untouched bowl, and which I now picked up and looked at again, the wax seal, the little stamped gears, the **A.B.**, and the four words that were the strangest instruction ever handed to a stranger: *forget this face.*

"Three," I said slowly, turning the note over as if the back of it might confess something the front withheld, "your papa owes me an explanation. And I intend to collect."

Mara's eyes went wide. "You *know* him?"

I looked at the seal. At the child. At the window, black and streaming, beyond which lay a village that would, within the week, learn to hate everything this girl was and everyone who sheltered her — for the wireless had been muttering for days about ultimatums, about mobilization, about the guns that everyone said would never actually —

The guns. Oh, God. The guns.

I had read enough intercepted politics that summer to know the smell of the air before thunder. And I understood, all at once, with the clarity of the truly exhausted, why a German clockmaker who spoke of nothing and hummed while he worked might spend a night rehearsing words, and send his daughter into the dark with a command to be forgotten.

"Mara," I said, as gently as I knew how, and I did not always know how, "tomorrow, you're going to help me mend a watch. And while we work, you're going to tell me about your papa's humming. Because I believe I've heard it before — and I believe, whatever that note says, that forgetting your face would be the second-worst mistake of my life."

She blinked at me, rain-damp and fire-lit and unbearably young.

"What's the worst one?" she asked.

I looked at the note again. *Forget this face.* Signed with the mark of the man who had once opened a door for me and asked for nothing.

"The worst one," I said, "was already made, years ago, by somebody who forgot *his* face. Now — upstairs. Linen cupboard's on the landing if you hear knocking."

She went. I sat alone at the table with the candle and the storm and the note, and outside, far off toward Falmouth, the sky flickered — though whether it was lightning or something else, something man-made and arriving, I could not then have said.
