## 3. Mara

The child did not sleep.

I had put her in the blue room — the one with the iron bedstead and the quilt Edith's mother had sewn from sailcloth scraps — and I had sat outside the door on the landing for the better part of an hour, listening to the storm batter the shutters and waiting for weeping, or screaming, or at minimum some small human sound to prove the girl was not a ghost delivered by the sea.

What I heard instead was humming.

It came through the door thin as wire, a tune I did not know and yet somehow did know, the way you know a word you've read but never spoken aloud. It had that particular shape of German melody — falling, then rising stubbornly at the end, like someone insisting on hope against good evidence. A lullaby. She was singing herself to sleep in a house where she knew no one, in a country that by morning might be at war with her own, and she was doing it quietly, so as not to trouble me.

I sat on the cold landing with my knees drawn up and felt something crack open in my chest that I would spend the rest of this story trying to mend.

* * *

Morning arrived the colour of dishwater. The storm had spent itself against the cliffs, and the lane below the house was strewn with seaweed and one indignant gull feather, and the whole village of Porthcarrow would be sweeping up the night along with its doorstep grit. Edith was still in London. The train, if it ran at all, would bring her home by evening — which gave me perhaps twelve hours to decide what on earth I had taken into this house.

I found the girl in the kitchen.

Not sitting in the kitchen. *Inhabiting* it. She had dragged the footstool to the counter, climbed it, and was standing over the bread crock with the grave concentration of a surgeon. My good knife — well, Edith's good knife, everything here was Edith's, including increasingly me — lay beside a half-sliced loaf cut with more care than I have ever applied to anything.

"You're awake," she said, stating it as though she'd verified it personally.

"I am." I kept my voice level, the way you do with horses and vicars. "Did you sleep?"

"A bit. The bed is soft. Soft beds make me think of London." She said it without drama, the way children say terrible things, because they have not yet learned that terrible things require a pause and a lowered voice. Then: "There's no butter."

"There's dripping."

She considered this. "Papa says dripping is what happens when a household gives up."

"Your papa," I said carefully, "sounds like a man of strong opinions about fat."

"He's a man of strong opinions about everything. He says opinions are like gears — useless unless they connect to something that moves." She sliced the loaf further, precisely, each cut identical to the last. "Who are you?"

"Coral Villalobos. I drive for Mrs. Marchmont. This is her house."

"You're the driver." She looked me over with frank appraisal — my boots, my oil-stained cuffs, my hair escaping its pins after a night on a landing floor. Whatever verdict she reached, she kept to herself. "You drove us past the church yesterday. Before the rain."

My stomach performed a slow, cold roll. "Did I?"

"The car has a knock in the second cylinder. Left side." She said it the way another child might say *your shoelace is undone*. "You should tell whoever fixes it. It will get worse."

"It has been getting worse since spring," I admitted. "Mr. Penhale says it's character."

"Mr. Penhale," she said, with the immense authority of a nine-year-old dismissing an entire profession, "doesn't know engines."

* * *

Her name, she would give me, but only partway.

"Mara," she said, when I asked over breakfast. Just that. When I asked for a surname, she looked down at her plate of bread-and-dripping as though the answer might be written there in grease, and said, "I'm not meant to say."

"Not meant to say, or don't want to?"

"They're different questions." She glanced up, quick and shrewd. "Which one matters to you?"

I confess the child outmanoeuvred me before eight o'clock in the morning. I set down my cup and tried again, circling, the way one circles a locked drawer. Where had she come from? London, clearly. How had she come? By train, by cart, by the long wet walk from the crossroads — she wouldn't say, or didn't know how much it was safe to say. Who had left her at our gate in the teeth of a storm with nothing but a note and a nightgown too fine for tramping?

At every question she went quiet and still in a way no child should know how to do. Stillness like that is taught. It is taught by people who say *don't answer*, and *look at your shoes*, and *if anyone asks*. I had been that kind of still myself once, at fifteen, in a house with a father whose questions were worse than strangers'. I knew the curriculum.

So I stopped asking. I washed the dishes, and she dried them — standing on the same footstool, towel folded over her arm like a parlourmaid twice her age — and I let the silence do what interrogation could not.

It was she who broke it, eventually, apropos of nothing, wringing out the dishcloth.

"Papa says a person who asks too many questions either wants to help or wants to sell you. He says you can always tell which, because the helpers ask about *you* and the sellers ask about *them*."

"And which are you deciding I am?"

"I haven't finished deciding." She hung the cloth neatly on its hook. "But you asked about my boots. Yesterday. Whether they were wet."

"I did."

"Sellers never notice boots." And she climbed down off the stool and walked out of the kitchen with the air of a diplomat concluding favourable negotiations, leaving me holding a teacup and feeling, absurdly, approved of.

* * *

It was the watch that undid me.

I keep my father's watch — no. Let me be accurate, since accuracy is the whole disease of this story. I keep *a* watch that was my father's, a silver hunter he won at cards from a man he ruined, and which I took when I fled his house because spite is also an inheritance. It had not run in two years. I kept it wound anyway — winding it each morning the way you might water a dead plant, out of habit, out of some argument with time itself — and it sat in my coat pocket while I worked and told me nothing, hour after hour, faithfully.

That afternoon, rain having returned in a thinner, pettier mood, I sat in the motor shed servicing the car while Mara watched from an upturned crate. I'd let her hand me tools. There is a safety, I find, in shared machinery; you may say anything to a person while both of you look at a carburettor.

She noticed the watch when I pulled off my driving gloves. Of course she did. Children notice what adults have agreed to stop seeing.

"That's broken," she said.

"It's retired."

"May I see it?"

I hesitated. It is a private thing, a broken watch; it tells everyone exactly how long ago your life stopped going forward. But there was such naked wanting in her face — not for the object, but for the *work* of it — that I set it in her small palm before I had properly decided to.

She turned it over once. Opened the case with her thumbnail, finding the catch instantly, which Mr. Penhale himself cannot do. Held it to her ear. Frowned.

"The mainspring's snapped. Someone forced the crown." She looked up. "Do you have a lens? And tweezers? And something very clean to work on?"

I own a lens, because I use it for reading things I am not given permission to read, but that is a confession for another chapter. I fetched it, along with Edith's sewing tweezers and a white pillowcase, and I spread them on the bench in the grey light, and I watched a nine-year-old child take apart my father's watch with hands that did not shake.

I have seen surgeons at work — Edith made me drive her to a lecture once, and the surgeon demonstrated on a dead hand — and I tell you the girl had those hands. Not the steadiness only, though God knows she had that, but the *attention*: the way the world contracted to the space between the tweezers' points. Her tongue pressed against her teeth. Her breath went shallow and even. She laid the pieces out on the white cloth in rows so orderly they looked typeset — gear, gear, escapement, balance wheel, the snapped coil of the mainspring lying like a tiny dead snake.

"Where did you learn this?" I asked.

"My papa." She did not look up. "He repairs clocks. All kinds. Tall ones and small ones and ones that sing birds at you, which are the worst, because the birds get dusty." A pause. Her fingers stilled on the balance wheel. "He has a shop in Clerkenwell. It smells like metal and oil and the floorboards creak in a pattern, because he walks the same way every day, from the bench to the window to the kettle. You could tell the time by him walking."

The shed was very quiet. Rain ticked on the roof. Somewhere down the lane old Tregose's dog was barking at nothing, or at everything, the way dogs do when the weather changes and men change with it.

Clerkenwell. A clockmaker. A shop that smelled of metal and oil.

I thought of a workshop I had known seven years ago, in another town, in another life — the night I had run from my father's house with a bruised cheekbone and eleven pounds stolen from his desk, and knocked at the lit window of a clockmaker's shop because it was the only light on the street and I could not have gone one street further. I thought of a broad, careful man with sawdust on his sleeves who had looked at me — a wild-eyed girl in a torn collar — and asked no sellers' questions at all. He had fed me ham and bread. He had let me sleep in the back room among the ticking. In the morning he had driven me himself, forty miles, in his delivery cart, to the boarding house where a friend of a friend took in women fleeing things, and he had refused my money, all eleven pounds of it, pushed it back across the seat and said:

*"Keep it. One day you'll meet someone running, and you'll need it more than I do."*

Aldous Brandt. Clockmaker. The mark on the note in my apron pocket — a stylised wheel with eight spokes — matched the sign that had hung above his door.

And now his craft was lying in neat rows on a pillowcase in front of me, held in a child's hands, humming under her breath that same falling-then-rising lullaby I'd heard through the door.

Cold water has a way of arriving all at once. That is what recognition was: not a dawning but a dunking. I gripped the edge of the bench.

"Mara," I said, and my voice came out strange. "The lullaby. The one you hum. Where is it from?"

She looked up at last, tweezers poised. "Papa sings it. His mother sang it to him, in — " she caught herself, jaw tightening, that trained stillness sliding back over her face like a shop shutter. "It's just a song," she finished.

"It's a beautiful song."

"You know it?" Hope, sudden and unguarded, cracked her wide open. "Most people here don't. Agatha at school said it sounded like drowning."

"Agatha," I said, "is not to be trusted around music or much else."

Something loosened in her shoulders. She bent back to the watch, and I stood beside her in the smell of oil and rain and did not say any of the ten thousand things crowding my throat. Because if I was right — if this child was who the shape of her hands and the turn of her tune said she was — then everything had changed. The note in my apron was not a stranger's plea. It was a message from the one man alive to whom I owed a debt I had never managed to repay, and it had arrived in the form of a small girl with a broken mainspring and a mother-shaped absence I did not yet dare to ask about.

She worked another quarter hour. I handed her things before she asked — the lens, the oil, a sliver of pegwood whittled from a matchstick — and each time she took them without surprise, as though I were merely the apprentice and she had long since grown used to assistants who anticipated her.

Then she closed the case, turned the crown with two fingers, and set the watch on the bench between us.

It ticked.

I do not mind telling you that my eyes behaved disgracefully. Seven years of silence, ticking away on a white pillowcase, mended by a child's hands.

"It will keep good time now," Mara said. "Better than before. Papa says a repaired thing is stronger than an unbroken thing, because it knows where its weaknesses are." She studied my face with that unnerving directness. "Are you crying?"

"No," I said. "Yes. A little. It's the oil fumes."

"Liar," she said comfortably, and took my hand.

Her fingers were small and grease-darkened and they held on with surprising strength, and we stood together in the motor shed while the watch counted out seconds neither of us had expected ever to hear again.

* * *

We were interrupted, as one is always interrupted at the precise moment one's life changes shape, by the rattle of wheels on the lane. I tucked the watch into my waistcoat, scrubbed my face with my sleeve, and stepped out to see Mrs. Pascoe's gig drawing up before the gate, Mrs. Pascoe herself aboard with a face full of purpose and a basket full of other people's business, which was her usual cargo.

"Miss Villalobos!" she called before she'd fully halted the pony. "Is it true? They're saying at the shop there's a child at Marchmont House — a *foreign* child, left in the night — Betty Cobb saw a light in the blue room and Nance Tregose heard singing, and singing in a *foreign way*, if you follow me—"

Behind me, in the shadow of the shed, I heard Mara go still. That taught stillness. That shutter coming down.

I smiled at Mrs. Pascoe with every tooth I possessed.

"A niece of mine," I said. "From Bristol. Arrived on the late train. My family keeps her singing, Mrs. Pascoe, as most families do."

The lie came out smooth and warm as poured cream, and I hated how good it tasted, and behind me in the dark of the shed I heard a small voice resume its lullaby — louder now, deliberately louder, in plain defiant English — and understood that whatever came next, the two of us were already conspirators.

Mrs. Pascoe's eyes narrowed over her basket. Down in the village, I would learn by evening, a baker's windows had been smashed because of his Saxon vowels, and a war that existed so far only in newspapers had begun putting out roots in Cornwall soil.

Thursday's post, I remembered, included a letter for Sergeant Quill.

I would be reading that one first.
