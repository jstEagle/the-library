## 4. What Aldous Brandt Owed

There is a particular kind of silence a clock leaves behind when it stops.

I had never noticed it before I came to work for Edith Marchmont — the way a house full of ticking things hums along beneath your thoughts like a second heartbeat, and how you only hear it end. But in the days after war was declared, I began to notice it everywhere. The church clock in St. Goran still kept its time, stubborn as ever, but Herr Aldous Brandt's shop on Fore Street stood dark, its windows papered over with notices about enemy property, and inside it, I happened to know, forty-one clocks sat unwound and going slowly mad.

I knew because I had counted them once. He had made me count them.

* * *

The summer I turned nineteen — six years ago now, though some mornings it sits closer than that — I ran away from my father's house with one carpet bag, four pounds eleven shillings, and a bruise on my arm shaped like nothing so much as a hand. I will not dwell on the bruise. It has been dwelt upon enough, by me, at three o'clock in the morning, and dwelling does not improve it.

What matters is that I got as far as Truro before the money ran out, and what mattered more is what I did there, which was stand outside a clockmaker's shop in the rain, watching an old man repair a carriage clock in the window with the patience of a man who believed the world could be fixed if you simply held the right part steady long enough.

He looked up. He saw me looking. And instead of doing what most men of his age did when they caught a girl loitering — frown, or worse, smile — he opened the door and said:

"You have been standing there forty minutes. Either you love clocks or you are avoiding something. Come in either way; both are improved by warmth."

His English had edges on it, German vowels wearing English clothes, and he spoke as though every sentence had been wound that morning and would run true until nightfall. I went in because I was cold and because I had nowhere else to be, and I told myself I would stay five minutes.

I stayed two years.

Not continuously — I found work in Truro, then a position through an agency that placed me with Mrs. Pascoe in Falmouth, and eventually the extraordinary luck of Edith Marchmont. But for those first weeks, while I decided what shape my life might take, Aldous Brandt let me sit in the back of his shop and watch him work, and he never once asked me the question everyone else asked, which was *and where are you running from, then?*

Instead he taught me the names of tools. The staking tool and the broach. The movement holder, which he pronounced *movement holder* with such gravity you'd think it held up the sky. He showed me how a mainspring stores anger and releases it evenly, which he said was more than could be said for most people.

"Why are you kind to me?" I asked him once, around the third week, when the strangeness of it had begun to itch.

He did not look up from the escapement he was adjusting. "You are assuming kindness requires a reason. This is a very English suspicion." A pause. His tweezers hovered. "My daughter would be near your age now."

"Would be?"

"She is alive," he said, in a tone that closed the subject the way a case closes over its gears. "She lives in Hamburg with her mother's sister. It is better this way. Clocks run best when the parts are kept apart that want to grind against each other."

I thought then that he was talking about his marriage, or perhaps about England and Germany, which even in 1908 were already eyeing each other across the water like dogs through a fence. I did not know — how could I have known? — that he was talking about a promise, and that the promise had a name, and the name would arrive at our door one stormy night years later wearing wet boots and carrying a note that said *forget this face.*

That is the trouble with kindness from careful men. You don't see the ledger until it's presented for payment.

* * *

But I am getting ahead of myself, and a chapter that begins in the past should have the decency to walk through it in order.

When the news came that Germany and England were at war — and it came to our kitchen before it came to London, because the fisherman's boy ran up from the harbour shouting it before the wireless had finished warming its valves — Edith was in London, mid-campaign, and I was alone in the house with the wireless, the cat, and a rising sense of dread that I mistook at first for indigestion.

By evening the village had arranged itself into its wartime posture, which looks remarkably like its peacetime posture except that everyone is watching everyone else sideways. By the second day, Mr. Trenoweth at the post office had begun reading people's telegrams aloud to his wife before delivering them, on the grounds of national security, a justification I found professionally offensive, as it is *my* national security he was infringing upon.

And on the third day, two constables from Bodmin came down the hill to Fore Street, entered the clockmaker's shop, and escorted Herr Aldous Brandt out under the Defence of the Realm Act, enemy alien, interned, his stock sealed and his house locked.

I watched it from across the street, holding a parcel of Edith's correspondence I had no intention of delivering unread.

He did not struggle. That was the thing I could not shake afterward. He walked between the constables with his hands in his pockets and his chin up, and just before they turned the corner by the Ship Inn he stopped, turned his head, and looked directly at me — directly, deliberately, across forty feet of August sunshine — and inclined his head slightly, the way a man acknowledges a colleague.

As though to say: *you know where everything is kept.*

Then he was gone, and Fore Street acquired a dark shopfront, and the village acquired a story it could tell itself: *they took the German away, we're safe now.* Nobody mentioned that the German had repaired the church clock for free for eleven years. Nobody mentioned that when the fever took half the children off the coast road in 1911, it was Brandt who walked the lanes at night with medicine, because he was the only man with a bicycle and no fear of the dark.

Villages keep books too. They just write in them selectively.

* * *

That night I sat in the kitchen with Mara asleep upstairs — she had been with us four nights by then, sleeping in the box room with the ferocious practicality of a child who has learned that sleep is fuel and must not be wasted on crying — and I tried to think like a clockmaker.

Break the problem into parts. Identify the escapement. Do not shake the mechanism.

Part one: Aldous Brandt had left his daughter at my employer's door. Not at a police station, not at a church, not with any of the respectable families whose names carried weight along this coast. At ours. Which meant he trusted precisely one household in Cornwall, and the household he trusted contained a suffragist, a chauffeuse who read other people's letters, and a cat with no moral qualities whatsoever.

Part two: he was interned, and interned men do not send letters easily. Whatever arrangements he had made — and a man like Brandt always made arrangements; he struck me as a man who kept spare parts — those arrangements were now sealed behind barbed wire somewhere, possibly on the Isle of Man, where they were said to be sending them, and I had no way to reach him and no legal right to try.

Part three: the note. *Forget this face.* Signed with a small stamped mark of a clock, hands at ten past ten — the position clockmakers set hands to in advertisements because it frames the maker's name like a pleasant open face. I had seen that mark a hundred times, burned into the bottom corner of his repair tickets, pressed into wax on his parcels.

He was telling us to make the child invisible. He was telling us to do what I had done my whole life — become furniture, become background, become nobody anyone remembers — and he was telling his daughter to accept it.

Well. I had spent twenty-five years being invisible, and I had some professional opinions about it, chief among them that it is a poor way to treat a child, even if it is an excellent way to survive.

* * *

Here is the part I have not told Edith yet, and may never tell her, and am telling you only because a book must hold something back or it bursts.

In 1912, when Edith sent me to London with papers — suffrage papers, the dangerous kind, the kind that get offices raided — I stayed two extra days. I told her the train connections had failed. They hadn't. I went to Clerkenwell, to a narrow house above a workshop, because Aldous had asked me, months earlier, to deliver a letter should I ever find myself in London, and to ask after his family.

The letter was for his wife. I knocked; no answer. I knocked again, and a neighbour came out and told me, in the flat voice neighbours use for such news, that the wife had gone the previous winter, and the daughter had been sent to relatives in Hamburg.

So the letter went undelivered, and I stood in a Clerkenwell street holding a dead man's — no. A living man's letter for a dead woman, feeling the whole architecture of his quiet life rearrange itself around an absence. He had known, presumably. He had known when he gave me the letter. He had sent it anyway, the way you wind a clock that has stopped, because stopping is unbearable and winding costs nothing but hope.

I posted the letter care of the Hamburg address. I never learned whether it arrived. I never told him his wife was dead, because telling him meant admitting I had opened the door at all, and opening the door meant explaining why, and explaining why meant telling him about my father's house, and there are debts you can pay and debts you can only carry, and I had chosen to carry mine.

Now his daughter stood asleep in my box room, motherless, fatherless for the duration, carrying a lullaby in a language our new enemies spoke, and I understood — with the cold clarity of three o'clock in the morning — exactly what I owed and exactly what it might cost.

Peacetime debts come due in wartime at compound interest.

* * *

Mara appeared at the kitchen door at dawn, hair flat on one side, watch in hand — my old watch, the broken one, the one I'd left on the mantel as junk. She set it on the table. It was ticking.

"It was only the pallet fork," she said. "It needed cleaning. Papa says dirt is the most common disease."

Her voice was careful, morning-quiet, the voice of a child who has learned households are temporary. She watched me pick up the watch. Inside the caseback, when I turned it over — and I confess my hands were not entirely steady — I saw marks scratched into the metal, fine as spider silk, numbers and letters in a code I did not know but recognized instantly as clockmaker's shorthand, the private language of a man who assumed the world would be read by people like himself.

"Papa wrote in the watches," Mara said. "For emergencies. He says a good hiding place is one nobody wants to open twice." She frowned slightly, correcting herself. "He *said*. Before."

Before. The word hung between us like dust in a sunbeam.

I looked at the scratched message, and the ticking watch, and the child who had mended both the watch and something in me without noticing she'd done it, and I felt the old gratitude turn over in my chest like a mainspring tightening — gratitude curdling into purpose, purpose sharpening toward dread, because whatever that message said, whoever it named, wherever it pointed, I already knew the truth that governs all such mechanisms:

Once you start reading a hidden thing, you cannot un-read it. And once you decide to act on it, the spring is wound, and it will run down only one way.

Upstairs, the wireless in the parlour crackled with the nine o'clock bulletin. Down the lane, I could hear voices — several voices, male, unhurried, stopping at our gate.

They stopped at our gate.

Mara went very still, the way she did, folding herself small and silent, and I put my thumb over the scratched message and thought: *Edith is still in London, and someone has come to the door, and whatever happens next, I am alone with it.*

The knock came — firm, official, three raps, the knock of men who expect doors to open.
