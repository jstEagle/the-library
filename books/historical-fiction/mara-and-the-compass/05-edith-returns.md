## 5. Edith Returns

The train from Paddington had been four hours of exquisite misery, and Edith Marchmont was in no mood to be contradicted by anything, least of all her own front hall.

She had spent nine days in London doing battle with the Liberal establishment, two editors, and a landlady who believed that votes for women and hot water for lodgers were both extravagant fantasies. Her voice was half gone. Her feet ached in her good boots. And somewhere between Truro and the coast she had begun composing the speech she would deliver to Coral — something brisk and wry about how the movement would be the death of her, said with enough affection to make it clear that being killed by it was, on balance, preferable to any other death available to a woman of fifty-three.

What she had not composed — what no amount of train-bound rehearsal could have prepared her for — was the sight of a child sitting at her kitchen table.

Edith stood in the doorway of her own kitchen with her hat still on and her umbrella still hooked over her arm, and regarded the scene as one might regard a painting hung slightly crooked: recognizably familiar, unmistakably wrong. The kitchen itself was immaculate. The kettle was on. Coral stood at the stove with her back to the door, stirring something, her posture so carefully neutral that it announced guilt the way a lighthouse announces rocks.

And at the table, small hands wrapped around a mug nearly too large for them, sat a girl of perhaps eight or nine. Dark-haired. Pale. Watching Edith with the flat, unblinking appraisal of a cat deciding whether the new arrival was worth the trouble of fleeing.

"Hello," said the child.

"Good afternoon," said Edith, because forty years of breeding will produce a response even when the brain behind it has emptied entirely. She looked at Coral's back. "Coral."

"Ma'am." Coral did not turn around. "There's tea."

"I can see there's tea. I can also see there's a child. In my kitchen. Drinking from my mother's Spode." Edith set down her umbrella with great precision, as though it were a fencing foil and the room might charge. "Would either of you like to explain, or shall I simply stand here inventing explanations until one fits?"

Coral turned then. And Edith — who had employed the girl for three years, who had taught herself to read her silences the way sailors read weather — saw something she had never seen before: fear. Not the ordinary flinch of a servant caught in some small dereliction, but real fear, banked down and burning underneath.

"We should talk in the study," Coral said.

"The study," Edith repeated. "Naturally. The child cannot hear the explanation of the child."

"It's better if—"

"In my own house, Coral, I will be explained to where I please." She regretted it before she finished saying it — the voice she used for hecklers at rallies, deployed against a frightened girl holding a spoon — but pride is a stubborn animal and does not apologize mid-sentence. She pulled out a chair and sat down opposite the child. "Well. And who are you?"

The girl looked at Coral. Coral looked at the kettle. The kettle, having no opinion, merely steamed.

"Mara," said the girl.

"Just Mara? No surname?"

Another glance at Coral. "Just Mara."

"How convenient," said Edith. "I once knew a woman in Manchester who called herself only Vera, and it turned out she was keeping rather more than her surname private. But I expect yours is an innocent mystery." She leaned forward. "Mara. Where is your mother?"

Something crossed the child's face — quick, total, like a shadow of a bird over a window — and was gone. "London," she said.

"And your father?"

"Gone away."

"Gone away," Edith echoed. "How the men of this century do travel." She sat back. The girl had not touched her tea in some minutes; she held the mug the way one holds a thing entrusted to one's care. There was a bruise-yellow smudge under one eye that Edith chose, for the moment, not to remark upon. "Coral. The study. Now. Mara, my dear, there are seed cake tins on the second shelf — the round blue one. Help yourself and guard the rest against my appetite."

* * *

In the study, Coral closed the door and stood with her hand still on the knob, as though the door were a dam and the whole story pressed behind it.

"You'd best start," Edith said, removing her gloves finger by finger, "from the beginning, omitting nothing, and I warn you I am exhausted, hoarse, and fresh from a week of being lied to by men in Westminster, so my tolerance for evasion is at its historic minimum."

So Coral told her. All of it, in her level, careful voice — the knock past midnight, the storm, the note signed with a clockmaker's mark, *forget this face*. She told it standing, hands folded, eyes fixed somewhere over Edith's shoulder, and she told it in the tone of someone reading out a verdict.

When she reached the part about Herr Brandt, Edith stopped taking off her gloves.

"Aldous Brandt left a child on our doorstep."

"He left his child on our doorstep, ma'am. I'm nearly sure of it now. She mends watches. She hummed a German lullaby in her sleep. And the handwriting—" Coral produced the note from her apron pocket, and Edith took it and read it twice.

*Forget this face.*

"Oh, Aldous," she said softly. It came out before she could decide whether to let it. "You impossible, magnificent fool."

"So you knew him well."

Edith looked up sharply. There it was again — that new quality in Coral, this boldness that hadn't been there nine days ago. Nine days alone with a secret and a child, and her housekeeper-slash-chauffeur had apparently grown a spine in her absence.

"I knew him," Edith said. "Twenty years ago. Before you were driving motorcars, Coral, he pulled me out of a river in Lucerne after a carriage accident, and set the bone in my arm on a hotel billiard table because the nearest doctor was drunk. He refused payment. He said — what did he say—" She closed her eyes briefly. "He said, 'A life is a loan, Frau Marchmont. One repays it forward.' And then he went back to his clocks and I went back to my causes and we exchanged Christmas cards for eleven years until the cards stopped coming." She laid the note down on the desk. "Until now, it seems. His card has finally arrived."

"It's not a card, ma'am. It's a child."

"Yes, thank you, Coral, I had grasped that." Edith pressed her fingertips to her temples. Outside the study window, the sea did its eternal grey business against the headland. Somewhere in the kitchen, faintly, a chair scraped — the child moving, always quietly, never quite audibly. Even her presence was practiced.

"Do you understand what you've done?" Edith asked. "Do you understand what war has been declared, and what that makes him, and what it makes her, and what it makes us?"

"I understand exactly what it makes us." Coral's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "It makes us the only people in England who know she exists."

"That is precisely my point! Being the only people who know a German child exists is not a position of safety, Coral, it is a position of *gallows*." Edith rose and began to pace, because she thought better on her feet and worse seated, and just now she needed every advantage. "I have spent this week in rooms full of men explaining that suffragists are not hysterics, not traitors, not a fifth column. Do you know what happens to that argument the morning the newspapers report that Edith Marchmont, militant suffragist, was found harbouring an enemy alien's daughter? I will hand them the rope myself. They'll hang the movement with me as the knot."

"They won't find her," Coral said.

"They found Mrs. Pankhurst in a garden party."

"Coral—" Edith stopped pacing. "That's not funny."

"No, ma'am."

But something in Coral's face suggested she disagreed — that she found it grimly, privately hilarious, in the way of people who have already decided to be afraid later. Edith recognized the expression. She wore it herself most weeks.

She sat down again. "Tell me why," she said, and made it not a command but a question, which cost her something. "Truly. Not duty to Brandt — I feel that debt too, and debts can be honoured in safer currencies. Why did you keep her?"

For a long moment Coral said nothing. Then, quietly, in a different voice entirely:

"She knocked, ma'am. Past midnight, in the rain, with nowhere in the world to go. Everyone else in England would have sent her away. Someone had to be the person who didn't."

Edith looked at her housekeeper — this quiet, watchful, invisible young woman whom she had hired three years ago largely because she drove well and asked nothing — and felt the ground shift beneath one of her oldest certainties. She had believed, these three years, that she was the rescuer in the arrangement. That she had lifted Coral out of whatever silence she'd come from and given her wages, purpose, a door of her own.

It occurred to her now, uncomfortably, that Coral had been waiting all this time to be the one who opened a door.

"You realize," Edith said at last, "that I am going to say yes."

Coral blinked. "Ma'am?"

"Don't look surprised; it's unbecoming. I've spent thirty years being told by reasonable men that the risks outweigh the principle, and I have learned — slowly, expensively — that they are almost always wrong, and that the times they were right, the principle was worth the risk regardless." She tapped the note. *Forget this face.* "A man lent me my life once and told me to repay it forward. Very well. The bill has come due, twenty years late and wearing pigtails."

"Thank you, ma'am." Coral's composure cracked, just barely, at the edges — relief showing through like light under a door.

"Don't thank me yet. Conditions." Edith held up one gloved finger. "First: she becomes a niece. Mine. From Bristol. My sister Gertrude's family is tedious enough that no one will ever check. Second: we tell no one — no one — including, especially, that interfering windbag Dr. Penhale. Third—" she hesitated, and the third condition was the hard one, and they both knew it. "Third. If it comes to arrest, Coral, you take her and you go, and you leave me to face them. My name is armour. Yours isn't. Yet."

"That's not fair, ma'am."

"Fairness is for people with time. We have neither." Edith stood and straightened her jacket, restored now to operational order, the general resuming command of a very small and increasingly dangerous army. "Now. Where is she sleeping?"

"The blue room. I moved the trunk out and put the cot from the loft in. She folds her clothes every morning into a square so small I nearly cried the first time I saw it."

"Of course she does." Edith's chest did something complicated. "And school?"

"I've begun lessons. She reads English beautifully — better than half the parish, if we're honest. Her arithmetic is frightening. She won't write anything down without asking first whose eyes might see it."

*A clockmaker's daughter,* Edith thought. *Raised to keep things running and keep things hidden.* Aloud she said, "Then we shall manage. We have hidden a printing press from a magistrate; we can hide a child from a village." She paused at the study door. "One question, Coral, and answer honestly, because everything depends on it. Does anyone know? Anyone at all?"

Coral hesitated.

That hesitation lasted perhaps half a second. But Edith Marchmont had spent her life reading faces across hostile committee tables, and she caught it — caught it the way one catches a wrong note in a hymn.

"Coral."

"The postmaster saw me buying children's boots," Coral said. "I told him they were for the church jumble."

"And did he believe you?"

"He believed me," Coral said. "But he wrote something down after I left. I saw it through the window. A short note, ma'am. And he posted it."

Edith stood very still in her own doorway, listening to the sea, and to the faint sound of a child in her kitchen carefully re-lidding a tin of seed cake.

"Well," she said. "Then we had better be quicker than the post."
