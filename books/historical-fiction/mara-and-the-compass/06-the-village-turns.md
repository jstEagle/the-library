## 6. The Village Turns

The war arrived in Pencarrow not with a bang but with a bell.

I was polishing the motorcar's windscreen — a futile occupation on a Cornish coast where salt air undid any shine within the hour — when St. Petroc's began ringing. Not the Sunday peal, which is lazy and conversational, but the urgent, overlapping clamour that means *come, come, come and hear*. I stood with my cloth dripping over my knuckles and listened, and I understood before anyone told me. You cannot grow up in England without learning what that particular ringing means. It means the world has tilted.

Mara appeared at my elbow, as she had a way of doing that week, silent as a cat in socks.

"What's happened?" she asked.

"War," I said, because lying to her seemed suddenly obscene, even about this.

She considered it with the gravity of a nine-year-old weighing a new governess. "Papa said it might." Then, after a pause: "Will they ring the bells in London too?"

"I expect so."

"Good," she said, and went back inside, and I stood there a moment longer wishing I could un-teach myself the sound of alarm bells so that I might have one more hour of not knowing.

* * *

By evening the village had changed its shape.

It is a strange thing to watch a place you have known all your life rearrange its furniture. Pencarrow had always had two speeds: slow, and asleep. Now it moved at a third speed entirely — a brisk, sideways, watching sort of movement, everyone going about their errands while keeping one eye on their neighbours' errands, so that the whole village seemed to be walking through a mirror.

The shop windows went first.

Not all of them. Only Mr. Kessler's bakery on Fore Street, which had sold the best rye bread west of Plymouth for eleven years and whose windows now wore a starburst of cracks from someone's brick, thrown by someone who had presumably bought his morning roll there only last Saturday. The word *HUN* had been painted across the door in tar, the letters wobbling like teeth. Mr. Kessler himself — a round, gentle man who once gave me a stale bun every week for the simple reason that he didn't like waste — stood in his doorway looking at the glass as though he could not remember what it had shown him yesterday.

Edith saw it too. She had returned from her campaign in Bristol with a trunk full of pamphlets and a voice roughened from speaking, and she stood beside me at the window of the draper's opposite, her jaw set in the way I knew meant she was composing something.

"This is what happens," she said quietly, "when people are taught that fear is a form of patriotism."

"They've been taught nothing of the kind. They've just been taught to throw bricks."

"That's how it starts, Coral. Bricks first. Laws second. It's always been the order of things." She turned from the window abruptly. "Where is Mara?"

"In the kitchen, teaching herself the piano by ear, badly."

Something eased in Edith's shoulders. "Badly is good. Badly sounds like an English child."

That was the first time either of us said aloud what we had both been thinking since the bells: that the child sleeping under our roof was, as far as the village would soon be concerned, contraband.

* * *

The gossip arrived faster than the bricks.

Mrs. Penhale came for eggs — Mrs. Penhale always came for eggs; I suspect she keeps hens purely as a pretext — and stayed for forty minutes, during which time I learned the following: that the German baker had "got what he asked for, living here all these years, pretending"; that young Tom Curnow had enlisted and his mother was torn between pride and fury; that the vicar had prayed for victory, which Mrs. Penhale approved of, though she felt God should be given more specific instructions; and that, oh yes, she'd seen a little girl at the Marchmont house. A stranger child. Whose would that be, then?

"She's my niece," I heard myself say, before my brain had finished approving the sentence. "From Bristol. Her mother's taken poorly and Edith offered to have her down for the sea air."

"Bristol!" said Mrs. Penhale, with the satisfaction of a woman fitting a piece into a puzzle she intends to take apart again later. "And your people being from Bristol?"

"My sister married there," I said, inventing a sister with the calm fluency of a born liar, which surprised me. I had never thought of myself as one. Apparently I had simply never needed to be. "Chest trouble. Nothing catching," I added quickly, because Mrs. Penhale's eyes had narrowed in a manner suggesting she was already drafting the epidemic.

"Well," she said, gathering her basket with the reluctance of a general withdrawing troops from ground she meant to retake. "Give the little maid my regards. What did you say her name was?"

I hadn't said. I hadn't dared ask Mara's surname, and her Christian name was the only thing I had.

"Mara," I said.

"Mara," repeated Mrs. Penhale, tasting it. "Foreign-sounding, that."

"It's from the Bible," I said. "Very respectable. The sister of Moses. She watched over a baby in the reeds until it was safe."

Mrs. Penhale gave me a look that suggested she found piety suspicious in a woman of my age and unmarried condition, and departed. Through the window I watched her stop twice on the lane to speak to people — once to Mrs. Tregonning, once to the postman — and I understood that my niece from Bristol had been born into a world of instant and merciless documentation.

When I came inside, Mara looked up from the piano.

"You lied," she said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because sometimes the truth is a door you leave open, and the wind comes in."

She thought about this. "Papa says a clock only works if the case is closed."

"Papa," I said, "is a very wise man, wherever he is."

Her face did something small and complicated at that, and she went back to murdering Chopin, and I went to find Edith and tell her she now had a sister in Bristol with a weak chest, and that she had better remember it, because Mrs. Penhale certainly would.

* * *

The watching began properly on Thursday.

I noticed it first as a rhythm rather than a fact: a figure at the gate of Tresowan Cottage whenever I drove past, gone when I looked directly; a man leaning against the wall by the churchyard who had no business leaning anywhere; Mrs. Tregonning's curtain twitching with such regularity that I began to think of it as a metronome. The village had always gossiped — gossip in Cornwall is less a vice than a municipal function — but gossip had previously been content to travel. Now it wanted to *watch*. It wanted evidence. It wanted, I realised with a chill that settled somewhere behind my ribs, to be useful to somebody.

Because there were notices up now. In the post office window, on the church door. Instructions regarding enemy aliens. Registration. Restrictions. And beneath them, in smaller print, an invitation: information to be given to Sergeant Quill at the police station.

Information. As though the village had been waiting all these years for permission to hand itself over.

Edith read the notice twice and laughed — her campaign laugh, the one that made suffragist audiences sit up straighter. "'Information,'" she said. "They make it sound like charity work."

"Do you think they'll come here?" I asked. "To the house?"

"To the house of Edith Marchmont, who has been called a traitor in three newspapers for asking to vote? Let them come. I have been suspected of worse for better reasons." But I saw her glance toward the kitchen door, through which came the sound of Mara practising her scales — she had progressed from murder to mere assault — and I saw that the laugh cost her something.

"We should talk about the story," I said. "Mine will hold if it's the only one. Two stories are worse than none."

"So I'm the aunt in Bristol with the chest trouble?"

"No — you're Edith Marchmont, doing a kindness for your housekeeper's niece, because that is exactly the sort of thing you do, and everyone knows it, which is precisely why nobody will question it. Kindness is your alibi. You've spent ten years making it credible."

Edith looked at me for a long moment. "Coral Villalobos," she said, "you have missed your calling. You should have been in politics."

"I prefer driving," I said. "In politics you can't slam doors."

* * *

But the village kept turning.

On Saturday the baker's shop did not open at all, and by Sunday the Kesslers were gone — packed onto the early train, so it was said, though nobody admitted to having seen them go, which in Pencarrow meant everybody had watched them go and agreed to pretend otherwise. Eleven years in the village, and their departure took less than a morning. Their bread oven was still warm, Mrs. Penhale reported, with something in her voice I chose to read as shame, though it may equally have been indigestion.

"They left the cat," Mara said. She had heard it from the fish-boy, who heard everything and told her everything, the two of them having formed the kind of alliance children build instantly and adults pay lawyers to dissolve. "Nobody wants a German cat."

"The cat is a cat," I said.

"I know that." She scratched behind the marmalade tom's ears where he sat insufferably at home on our kitchen flagstones, having annexed us within an hour of arrival. "But grown-ups forget things when they're frightened. Papa says fear is like dust — it gets into everything and you can't see it working until the gears jam."

Out of the mouths of babes and clockmakers' daughters. I gave her bread and jam and let the tom stay, because it seemed suddenly important that somebody in this village be permitted to arrive uninvited and be fed.

* * *

Monday brought the letter.

I was doing the parish deliveries — Edith's motorcar and my willingness to drive it had long ago made me the unofficial carrier for half the parish, which is how I came to know everything I know — and among the ordinary harvest of bills and parish magazines there was an official envelope, buff-coloured, stamped with an office I didn't recognise, addressed to Sergeant Quill himself. I am not proud of what I did next. I have never been proud of it. I have also never stopped doing it. I steered the car into the shade of the elms above the coast road, opened the envelope along its seam with a hairpin, and read.

Most of it was administrative tedium — forms, returns, lists of registered persons. But halfway down the second page, in the flat language officialdom uses for the things it doesn't want to say loudly, was a paragraph concerning the internment of enemy nationals in the district, and among the names listed for removal was *A. Brandt, clockmaker, resident of Truro* — and beneath it, in a different ink, a handwritten addition that stopped my breath:

*Note: enquiries continuing re: missing minor, believed connected. Any intelligence to be forwarded immediately.*

Missing minor. Believed connected. Intelligence.

I sat under the elms for a long time with the sea going grey and glittery below me and the words arranging themselves into shapes I did not want to see. They were looking for her. Not casually, not as paperwork — *continuing*, which meant actively, which meant someone, somewhere, wanted a small girl with clever hands and a German lullaby found, and had set machinery moving to find her, and the machinery had begun to turn in the direction of Pencarrow, where a woman named Penhale was already telling anyone who would listen about a niece from Bristol whose name sounded foreign.

I resealed the envelope with the hairpin and a steadiness I did not feel, delivered it to the police station with a smile I had worn a thousand times, and drove home with my hands tight on the wheel and my mind moving faster than the engine ever had.

At the house, Mara was in the garden teaching the cat to sit, which the cat was permitting out of politeness. Edith stood at the study window with her arms folded, watching the lane, watching the watchers, wearing the expression of a general surveying ground she has decided to defend.

"Coral," she said, without turning. "How long do you think we have?"

And I, who read letters for a living and lies for a survival, gave her the only honest answer I had.

"Not long enough. We need a plan."

* * *

That night I lay awake listening to the house breathe — Edith's floorboards, Mara's small even breathing through the wall, the sea keeping its old time below the cliff — and I thought about clocks. Herr Brandt had once told me, in his careful English, that a clock's whole honesty lies in its escapement: the little mechanism that lets the gears move forward one tooth at a time, no more, never all at once. *Everything must be released gradually,* he'd said, *or the whole machine breaks.*

Well then. One tooth at a time. A niece invented today; a route found tomorrow; whatever came next, next.

Outside, a curtain twitched in the dark, and the village watched, and I began, quietly, to plan.
