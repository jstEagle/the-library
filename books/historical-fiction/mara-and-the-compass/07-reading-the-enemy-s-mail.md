## 7. Reading the Enemy's Mail

There is a particular sort of silence that falls over a village when it has decided to be afraid of something, and by the third week of August, Porthmellin had perfected it.

I noticed it first at the post office counter. Mrs. Pascoe, who had sold me stamps for four years and gossiped with me for three of them, now counted out my pennies without meeting my eye. The bell above the door rang and she flinched at it. Two fishermen stood near the window, talking in the low, careful voices of men who had learned that the walls had opinions.

"Morning," I said brightly, because brightness is a kind of armor too, and I have always preferred mine polished.

Nobody answered. Mrs. Pascoe slid my letters across the counter as though they might bite.

I gathered them up — Edith's correspondence from London, a circular from the District Council about blackout precautions that nobody in Porthmellin had asked for, and a fat official envelope stamped with an eagle and the words ON HIS MAJESTY'S SERVICE, which I held for a moment longer than strictly necessary before tucking it into my bag.

The thing about carrying letters is that people forget you are a person. They think of you as a satchel on legs, a pair of hands, a bicycle bell. In the years I had driven for Edith and delivered for the parish, I had been forgotten so thoroughly and so often that I had begun to suspect I possessed some rare talent for it. Other women worked at their invisibility their whole lives and never achieved what came to me naturally, like perfect pitch or double-jointed thumbs.

It was, I will admit, a lonely distinction.

But it meant that when I stopped the bicycle on the cliff road to open the eagle-stamped envelope with a steam kettle's worth of care — a skill I had learned from Aldous Brandt himself, who could open and reseal an envelope so cleanly that even its own mother wouldn't know it — nobody looked twice at the girl standing in the sea wind with her skirts pinned and her fingers moving.

I told myself, as I always told myself, that I only read what concerned me. It was a narrow category, conveniently elastic. Letters concerning Edith: yes. Letters concerning the parish roads: no, however tempting the scandal. Letters concerning German clockmakers interned by His Majesty's Government: well.

You can see how the category grew.

* * *

The letter inside was addressed to the Harbour Office and signed by someone at the Home Office with handwriting so upright it looked ironed. I read it standing on the cliff path with the gulls complaining overhead, and by the time I reached the third paragraph my hands had gone cold despite the August sun.

*...with reference to enemy aliens of German nationality detained under Regulation 14b, be advised that A. Brandt, watchmaker, formerly resident of this parish, is currently held at Knockaloe camp, Isle of Man. Enquiries regarding his status may be directed to the camp commandant...*

Isle of Man. Alive, then. My knees went soft with relief before my mind caught up to point out that alive and well were two different countries entirely, and the road between them was long.

I read on.

*Further, be advised that the Board is making enquiries after one Mara Brandt, a minor, believed to have been removed from the London address prior to the detention of the said A. Brandt. Any information as to the child's whereabouts should be reported immediately to the local constabulary.*

The wind took the page's corner and fluttered it like something frantic trying to escape my hands.

*Any information as to the child's whereabouts.*

They were looking for her. Not idly, not as a matter of filing — *immediately*, they had written, underlined once with a firm black stroke, the way officials underline things when somebody important has asked twice.

I folded the letter along its original creases, sealed it with the flat of my thumb, and stood for a while watching the sea do its eternal indifferent business below the cliffs. Somewhere out there were patrol boats. Somewhere up there, God, presumably, taking notes.

"I'm sorry," I said to the letter, which is a strange thing to say to an envelope but no stranger than anything else I'd been doing lately. Then I got back on the bicycle and pedalled toward the harbour office with the face of a woman thinking about nothing whatsoever, which is the most difficult face there is.

* * *

Sergeant Quill was not at the harbour office; he was outside it, talking to Mr. Trevanion the fishmonger, and both men stopped talking when I approached, which told me precisely nothing new but confirmed everything old.

"Miss Villalobos." Quill touched his cap. He had the habit of touching his cap whether or not he wore it, which gave him the air of a man perpetually arriving.

"Sergeant." I handed over the harbour office's post. "Council circular, and something official."

His eyes stayed on mine a half-second past comfortable. He had grey side-whiskers and the patient expression of a man who fishes with a line rather than a net, on the theory that the fish comes to you eventually if you're quiet enough.

"You've been on the cliff road," he said.

"The wind's good for drying washing," I said. "Also for thinking. Also for cycling downhill, which is the part I actually enjoy."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Your employer back from London yet?"

"Not yet. She writes that the campaign goes splendidly and the government remains obstinate, which she considers proof of God's sense of humor."

"Hm." He weighed the letters in his hand without opening them, which I found professionally insulting. "Give Mrs. Marchmont my regards."

"I'll give her whatever she asks for," I said, "which is generally an argument," and cycled off before he could decide whether he'd been insulted.

Behind me I felt him watching, patient as tide. I did not look back. Looking back is a confession in itself.

* * *

That evening I laid it all out on the kitchen table at Marchmont House the way Edith lays out campaign strategies: the letter's contents, the search for Mara, the name Knockaloe, the word *immediately* with its underlining like a small black fence.

Mara sat across from me doing something to the insides of Edith's mantel clock that would have horrified Edith had Edith been home to be horrified, which was why we had waited until she wasn't. The girl worked with a jeweller's screwdriver she'd produced from somewhere — children like Mara carry tools the way other children carry secrets, close and casually — and her whole face was lit from within by concentration. She hummed while she worked. The lullaby again, that slow minor-key German melody that had told me everything before she ever spoke.

She was humming it now, and I had to stop reading the letter aloud because my voice kept catching on it.

"They want to find you," I said finally, plainly, because I had decided weeks ago — or perhaps years ago, in another house, in another life — that children deserve truth the way plants deserve water: regularly and without being asked.

Mara's screwdriver paused. "Papa said they might."

"Did he."

"He said England would get frightened and frightened people look for someone to put in a box." She turned a tiny gear over in her palm. "He makes boxes for time, so he knows about boxes."

I let that sit. There was a terrible clarity in the child, the kind adults pay analysts to achieve.

"Knockaloe is on an island," she said. "In the middle of the Irish Sea. Papa told me that too. He said if anything happened to him, there was a lady—" She stopped. The screwdriver resumed. "He said things," she finished, in the tone of a child closing a drawer.

"And your mother?" I asked gently, though I already knew, the way you know the shape of a room from outside the door.

Mara didn't answer. She fitted the gear into place with a click so small and exact it sounded like a period at the end of a sentence.

"We won't let them box you," I said instead. "Whatever else happens. That's the whole of it."

She looked up then, and her eyes were her father's — I knew them from three winters ago, from a workbench lit by an oil lamp, from a man who'd fed soup to a runaway girl and asked no questions except *are you warm enough*. Those eyes, in that small face, undid me thoroughly, and I pretended to be very interested in the fire.

* * *

Here is what guilt does, if you feed it properly and exercise it daily: it becomes purpose.

I had spent years telling myself that reading other people's letters was a small sin, a private vice, like taking sugar in tea or enjoying funerals. Now the sin had a use, and usefulness, I discovered, is the great absolver. Every envelope I steamed open that week became ammunition. I learned the postal rhythms of the parish the way Brandt had taught me the rhythms of gears — everything moves in circles, everything returns, you need only patience and clean hands.

By Thursday I knew three things worth knowing.

First: that the authorities had circulated a description of Mara to every constable between Penzance and Truro — *female child, approximately nine years, foreign appearance, possibly travelling with an adult female* — which described, inconveniently, roughly every child in Cornwall including several goats.

Second: that Sergeant Quill had received this circular and, according to a note from his own superior asking why no report had been filed, had not yet acted on it. That absence interested me more than any presence. A dutiful man who delays is a man at war with himself, and wars of that sort end suddenly and one way or the other.

Third — and this arrived on Friday in a letter I nearly didn't open, because it was addressed to Mrs. Tregose of Smugglers' Lane, and everyone knew Mrs. Tregose's mail consisted entirely of seed catalogues and threats from her nephew —

Third, a letter from a woman in Falmouth whose handwriting trembled, mentioning a boat, mentioning *the usual rate doubled for war work*, mentioning a cove I had swum in as a girl, and mentioning, in a postscript that made my blood move differently through my body:

*Tell your Falmouth cousin the Manx route still runs, if he has friends inland who need the sea.*

The Manx route. To the Isle of Man. Where they kept the Germans.

I sat in the delivery shed behind Marchmont House with that letter in my lap and let the enormity assemble itself, piece by piece, like one of Brandt's movements: here the mainspring, here the escapement, here the balance wheel that governs the whole.

Letters go places. That is the entire point of them. And a letter can be answered.

If a letter could travel from Falmouth to a camp on the Isle of Man — if the smugglers' route ran both ways, as smuggling routes always do — then perhaps a letter could reach Aldous Brandt. Perhaps the man who had opened my door for me, years ago, without questions, could receive from me at last an answer to a question he had never thought to ask: *your daughter is safe, she is mending clocks in my kitchen, she hums your lullaby, I remember your face and I am keeping hers.*

And perhaps — the thought arrived quietly, the dangerous ones always do — perhaps a reply could come back. Instructions. A name. Whatever he had hidden in that careful mind of his against exactly this catastrophe.

I resealed Mrs. Tregose's trembling letter and delivered it to her cottage, where she took it from me with eyes like a gull's, missing nothing.

"You've gone thin in the face, Miss Villalobos," she observed.

"Wartime," I said. "We're all rationed."

"Mm." She looked past me down the lane, toward the distant roofline of Marchmont House. "Some rations cost more than others. You come see me when yours runs short."

I cycled away with my heart hammering and my face perfectly composed, and only allowed myself to understand, halfway up the hill, what she had just offered, and what accepting it would make me.

A courier of secrets, yes. I had been that for years.

But a smuggler's customer — that was a different trade altogether, and its prices were paid in things you couldn't steam open and reseal.

* * *

That night, Mara sat opposite me at the kitchen table, and between us lay the mantel clock, running again, ticking with the smug regularity of a job well done.

"Your father," I said carefully, "if I could send him word — a letter, somehow, through people who move quietly — what would you want it to say?"

She considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything, wiping her small hands on her pinafore as though the question might leave residue.

"Tell him the watch works," she said. "He'll know what that means."

"The watch?"

"The one I fixed. Your broken one." She looked at me with those inherited eyes. "Papa puts things inside watches. For keeping. He says paper burns and people forget, but a movement remembers if you build it right."

My broken wristwatch, which had stopped the week she arrived and started again the day after, ticking faithfully on my wrist at this very moment.

I did not take it off and pry it open at the table, because I had learned patience from a clockmaker, and patience says: wait until the child sleeps, wait until the house is dark, wait until the only sound is the sea and the ticking of things built to remember.

But my pulse had already begun counting down to that moment, beat by beat, like a mechanism approaching the hour.
