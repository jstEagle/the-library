## 8. The Clockmaker's Letter

Papa said: if you are frightened, count the gears.

So in the night, lying in the strange bed under the strange blanket that smelled of lavender and somebody else's life, I counted. Escape wheel, fourth wheel, third wheel, centre wheel. Mainspring barrel. The click that holds everything together so it doesn't all spring backwards at once. Eleven teeth on the escape wheel of Papa's best regulator, fifteen on the little travelling watch, and the travelling watch was the one I had with me now, ticking against my ear like a small stubborn heart that refused to stop even though everything else had.

I counted to one hundred and twenty gears, which is as far as the watches go, and then I started over, because starting over is allowed when you're a clockmaker's daughter. Starting over is not failing. Papa says a watch is only ever beginning again. Tick is always followed by tock, and nobody calls the tick a mistake for having ended.

The house creaked. Not the way Papa's workshop creaked, which was a friendly sound, wood settling down to sleep. This was an older house with opinions. Somewhere below, a floorboard spoke, and then footsteps, soft ones, going from the stairs to the kitchen and back again, and I knew they were hers — the lady who reads letters — because she walked like someone trying not to be heard by the house she lived in, and I thought, *she does it too*, and that made me feel less alone, though I didn't know why exactly. It is possible to feel less alone because of footsteps. Mama would have understood that. Mama could tell who was coming up the stairs by the weight of them and would arrange her face accordingly before the door opened, so that Papa never once saw her tired.

I am getting ahead of myself. Papa says I do that. He says a story is like a movement in a clock — you must let each part finish its work before you start the next, or the whole thing jams.

* * *

Here is what happened, in order, the way Papa would want it.

We lived in Clerkenwell, above the shop, in rooms that smelled always faintly of oil and brass, which other people think is unpleasant but which I think is how safety smells. My father is Aldous Brandt, and he repairs clocks, and he made watches, beautiful ones, and he came from Germany when he was a young man younger than Coral, and Mama came from here, from England, from a town near the sea whose name she sang more than said. I have both languages inside me like two springs wound in opposite directions. At home we spoke English at the table and German after, when the lamps were low, because those were the words Mama liked best to fall asleep to.

Mama's name was Liesel. She laughed with her whole body. She could carry two buckets of water up three flights without spilling, and she sang while she did it, badly, on purpose, because she said singing well was showing off and there was enough of that in the world already. She called me *Maus*. Little mouse. Because I was quiet, she said, but quiet like a mouse in a wall is quiet — listening, not absent. "A mouse knows everything about the house," she told me. "That's why the cat never catches her."

In June, men came to the shop. I remember it was June because the light came through the window in long dusty bars and lay across the bench where Papa worked, and the dust turned slowly in it like tiny planets. There were four men. Two wore ordinary coats and two did not try very hard to look ordinary. They talked to Papa in voices too friendly, the way you talk to a dog before you take it to the vet. Words like *registry*. Words like *alien*. Words like *in the event of*.

After they went, Papa stood at his bench for a long time holding a screwdriver he wasn't using, and then he said, in German, the words I will keep forever because they were the last calm words: "Then we go early."

* * *

Going early took eleven days. I know because Mama marked it on the calendar she kept for market days, drawing a small X each morning, and I watched her draw the last X with her thumb still smudged with flour.

Eleven days of Papa taking clocks apart that were not broken. That sounds like a strange thing to write but it is true and important. Every evening he dismantled something — a carriage clock, a barometer, once the big regulator that had hung in the shop since before me — and out of their insides he drew small things that were not clock parts. Papers. A thin roll of banknotes folded flat as a wafer. A photograph of Mama and me that he put inside the case-back of the travelling watch, behind the dial, where no one looks unless they are a clockmaker or a daughter.

"You are learning a lesson," he told me while he worked, and I sat on my stool and handed him tools, which I am good at, better than boys twice my age according to him, though he may be biased. "The lesson is this: people look at a thing and see what they expect to see. A clock is expected to be a clock. So it can be a letter, or a bank, or a door." He tapped the case-back shut with a little hammer, gently, the way you'd tap a bird. "Remember that, Maus. Expectation is a lock. Everything is a key, if you're patient."

I asked him what we were hiding from, and he was quiet a moment, and Mama, who had come in with tea, answered instead. "Not hiding from," she said. "Hiding *for*. We are keeping you safe *for* later, when the world has finished being stupid."

Mama swore only once a year, by tradition, on New Year's Eve, and the rest of the time she said things like "finished being stupid," which was her way of saying a very bad word without saying it. If she were writing this she would say the war was finished being stupid. But she is not writing this. Only I am writing it, and I don't swear yet; I'm saving up.

* * *

The plan was a boat. There is always a boat, in these stories; I have read enough of Papa's penny dreadfuls to know that boats are where plans live or die. Ours was to go from London down to a port, and from the port to Holland, where Papa's sister lives, Tante Greta, who sends letters with wax seals and once sent me a wooden horse with wheels. From Holland, when the world stopped being stupid, we would all come back, or not, whichever, because by then, Papa said, we would decide as a family, and family deciding was the whole point of family.

We got as far as a railway station in the dark, all three of us, with one trunk and one satchel and the travelling watch in my coat pocket. The station smelled of coal and rain. And there were men at the barrier checking papers, and there was a whistle somewhere behind us that might have been nothing, might have been a train, but Papa's hand closed on my shoulder like a gate shutting, and he turned us down a side street, and that is when the plan began to die, in instalments, the way plans do.

Instalment one: no boat from London anymore, the ports watched, a man named Ferro — I remember the name because it means iron, and iron is what he was — could get us out but not all three, not together, not soon.

Instalment two: wait. Wait in a room above a bakery that smelled wonderful and was therefore unbearable, waiting is worse when you're hungry, and we were always hungry, because money had to be saved for the boat. I learned to sleep through my stomach. It's a skill. I don't recommend needing it.

Instalment three: Mama coughed.

It started small. A cough is such a small thing. You can hold a cough in your fist, almost. But the bakery room was damp, and November came, and the cough grew a fever around itself like a pearl grows around grit, except pearls are worth money and fevers are not, and by the middle of November Mama stayed in bed, and by the end of November the doctor came and went quickly, the way doctors do when they've already written the ending in their heads and see no reason to share it.

I sat with her. I held her hand, which had got lighter somehow, as if the fever were carrying part of her away a bit at a time, taking luggage ahead of her. And on the last morning she pulled my ear close to her mouth, which hurt a little, and I let it, and she said:

"Maus. Listen. You'll go with Papa. Papa will get you to Tante Greta. And when you get there—" and here she stopped and breathed, and I counted her breaths the way I count gears, because counting keeps the fear in its case — "when you get there, you tell Tante about the blue dress in the trunk, second layer down, wrapped in the shawl. It was my mother's. Tell her it goes to whoever needs it next. That's the rule in our family. Dresses travel."

"Dresses travel," I said, to show I'd keep it.

"And Maus—" and her voice went different, thinner, the way wire goes thin before it breaks— "if you are ever alone, ever, you find the lady who reads letters."

I said, "What lady?"

But she was already somewhere between answering and not, floating on the fever, and she smiled at the ceiling and said, "There's always one. In every parish. Some woman nobody notices, who carries everyone's letters, and reads what she shouldn't, and keeps every secret in the village inside her like a hive keeps honey. Your papa will know. Your papa knows everyone. Ask your papa."

She died on the first of December, early, before the light, which I think she would have found rude of herself, since she loved mornings. Papa did not make a sound. He sat beside her and held her hand until it cooled, and then he stood, and straightened the blanket, and tucked the corners in neatly, hospital corners, the way she liked, and I understood even then, being eight-going-on-old, that tucking in the blanket was the last thing in the world he could do for her, so he did it perfectly.

He did not cry until the night after, when he thought I slept. I heard it through the wall. It sounded like a clock winding down in a room where no one can reach it. I lay in the dark and counted gears until it stopped, and I never told him I heard, and I think that was the first grown-up thing I ever did.

* * *

After Mama, Papa changed his plan the way a man changes a plan when the plan has a hole burned through the middle of it.

No more waiting for the boat that takes three. There was a boat — Ferro's cousin, a fishing boat, leaving from a cove, taking *two*, maybe, if the weather held and the patrols were lazy and God was paying attention, none of which could be relied upon. Papa looked at the arithmetic of it for one whole day, sitting at the bakery table with his hands flat on the wood, and I watched him do sums no school teaches.

On the second day he bought paper and ink, and wrote a letter, and sealed it, and then he sat me down on his knee, which hadn't happened in a year, because I'd grown too big, and he said, "Maus. Do you remember the lesson about expectation?"

"A clock is expected to be a clock."

"Good girl. Now the harder lesson." His chin was rough against the top of my head. "Sometimes the bravest thing a clockmaker can do is admit he cannot mend a thing. Sometimes the repair is bigger than the bench."

I did not understand. I understand now. Writing it down helps; that's another thing I'm discovering, that writing is a kind of bench where you can lay a thing out and see its parts.

He would take me to Cornwall. Not to Holland — the sea route south was watched, Ferro said, the ports crawling, and Papa's face, mine, a man and a girl with his eyes and her mother's colouring, would be remembered. But there was a place he knew, a house, a person. Years ago — before me — Papa had done a repair for a lady, a great lady, a suffragist, who had been hurt in a crowd, knocked down and trampled half to death outside a hall in London, and Papa had carried her to a doctor in his own cart when everyone else stepped back, because stepping back is what crowds do. She had never forgotten. She wrote to him sometimes, fierce letters full of words like *justice* and *never surrender* and once, memorably, *the Home Secretary is an ass*, which Papa read aloud to Mama just to hear her laugh.

"If anyone in England will shelter a German's daughter," Papa said, "it is Edith Marchmont. She has been fighting this country's stupidity longer than the country has been stupid in this particular way."

And the lady who reads letters? I asked him. Because Mama had said. Because Mama's last instruction sat in me like a mainspring, coiled tight, waiting to be released by some small tooth of circumstance.

And Papa smiled — a real smile, sad at the edges, the kind Mama used to call his *workshop smile*, the one he made when a mechanism surprised him pleasantly — and said, "Ah. Yes. Edith Marchmont keeps a driver. A young woman. Quiet as a wall. When Edith's post comes, that girl carries the letters, and I would stake my finest regulator that not one of them arrives unopened in any sense that matters. Your mama knew what she knew, Maus. Mothers hear the shape of a village from far away." He tapped my nose. "If anything happens to me — listen to me, Maus, *if*, which is a word we use precisely — if anything happens to me, you find Edith Marchmont, and you find her driver, and you give them the note, and you trust the lady who reads letters. Can you do that?"

I said yes. I was lying on the inside, because I didn't want to do any of it, I wanted us to go to Tante Greta together and eat proper bread and wait out the stupid years as three became two became — but no. I said yes on the outside and meant it too, underneath, the way a watch has a case and works. Both true at once. Clockmakers know about that.

* * *

The journey down was three days and I remember almost none of it properly, which Papa said was a mercy and I say was a theft. Fragments: a train window with rain running down it like the glass was crying for us so we didn't have to. Papa teaching me a new lullaby on the platform at some junction, quietly, our breath steaming, *Guten Abend, gut' Nacht*, his voice low so it stayed ours. A policeman looking at us too long. Papa's hand steady on my shoulder the whole time, a hand like a paperweight, keeping me from blowing away.

And then the last fragment, the one I keep in the front of the drawer, the one I take out most:

It was raining. Of course it was raining; Cornwall appears to be made largely of weather. We stood at the bottom of a lane with hedges tall as cathedrals on either side, black in the dark, and Papa knelt down in the mud so we were eye to eye, and he gave me the note, and he gave me the travelling watch off his own wrist, warm from his skin, ticking against my palm.

"Now, Maus. You knock. You knock and you do not run away, whatever happens, because running away is the one thing that cannot be mended." His thumb wiped the rain off my cheek, or possibly it wasn't rain, the record is unclear. "You give the note to whoever opens the door. And then you say the words."

"*Forget this face,*" I recited. "Forget this face, forget my name, forget you ever saw me."

"No." He shook his head, and his voice cracked right down the middle, and through the crack I saw everything he'd been keeping in his case. "Those are the instructions for *them*. For you, the words are different. Your words are: *my father loves me, and this was the bravest repair he ever attempted.* Say it."

"Da—"

"Say it, Maus."

"My father loves me," I said, "and this was the bravest repair he ever attempted."

"Good girl." He kissed my forehead, both cheeks, my nose. "Now. One more lesson, the last one, free of charge. Whatever happens after tonight — whatever you see, whatever you're told, however many years go by — *do not let them succeed.*"

"Succeed at what?"

"At making you forget." He pressed the watch into my hand and closed my fingers over it. "Faces are like watches, Lieselchen. They can be hidden in a pocket. They cannot be unwound."

Then he rang the bell, and pushed me gently forward onto the step, and stepped back into the hedge-shadow, and the door opened, and light fell out of it onto the wet stone, and I looked up into the face of the lady who reads letters.

* * *

That was nine nights ago.

Nine nights of lavender blankets and unfamiliar floorboards and a woman named Coral who moves through this house like a cat through a church — silent, watchful, belonging to no one, noticing everything. Nine nights of pretending to be asleep when her footsteps pause outside my door. Nine nights of eating eggs I don't taste and answering questions with as few words as I can manage, because every word is a gear, and once you start handing out gears, people can assemble you.

She hasn't asked me many questions. That is how I know she reads letters. People who ask questions are collecting information. People who read letters already have it, and are deciding what to do.

Tonight I heard her downstairs past midnight again, walking her soft walk, and I heard her stop, and stand, and I felt through the floor the exact quality of her stillness — a thinking stillness, a weighing stillness. And then she went back to the kitchen, and there was the scratch of a pen.

Writing to whom? To Papa? Is there word of Papa? Has something happened? The police came yesterday, I saw him from the window, the village policeman, talking to Coral in the lane, hat in his hands like an apology, and she stood very straight and very still, and when he left she stood there a long moment longer, looking at nothing, doing sums.

I know about sums like that. I've seen a man do them at a bakery table with his hands flat on the wood.

Tomorrow, I decide, lying in the dark with the travelling watch ticking against my ear — tomorrow I will show her what Papa hid inside it. The photograph behind the dial. The message in the case-back, scratched fine as spider silk, that I found on the third night when I couldn't sleep and took the movement apart just to have something to do with my hands. *Coordinates. A name in Holland. Keep my promise.*

Tomorrow I will open the case-back and let her read it, because she is the lady who reads letters, and because a promise addressed to *whoever brings her* is addressed to her, and because I am tired of carrying the mainspring alone.

But tonight, the pen scratches downstairs, and a floorboard speaks, and I lie counting gears in a house that is not mine in a country gone mad at my father, and I practice the words Papa gave me, silently, so no one hears:

My father loves me, and this was the bravest repair he ever attempted.

Tick, says the watch.

Tock, says the house.

And downstairs, softly, the pen keeps writing, and I wonder who the letter is for, and whether it will save us, and whether tomorrow has teeth.
