## 9. An Offer from Sergeant Quill

Sergeant Thomas Quill had been a policeman for twenty-two years, and in that time he had learned one great truth about the human race: nearly everybody wanted to confess to somebody. They simply hadn't found the right somebody yet.

He thought about this on the morning of the twelfth of September, 1914, as he stood outside the Marchmont house in the pale gold light, holding his helmet under his arm like a man arriving at church rather than a constable come to poke at a neighbour's conscience. The house looked the same as it always did — grey stone, blue shutters, Edith Marchmont's motor car sitting in the drive like a well-fed cat — but Quill's eye, trained by two decades of small crimes and smaller lies, kept snagging on details that didn't sit right.

The curtains on the upstairs left window were drawn at nine o'clock in the morning. Nobody drew those curtains except when there was something behind them worth hiding or somebody who didn't want watching. And Mrs. Penhale at the shop swore she'd seen a child's face there twice now — a small pale face, gone as quick as a minnow.

Quill was not a suspicious man by nature. He was a careful one, which was a different animal entirely. Suspicion was a fever; care was a discipline. He'd been careful about Coral Villalobos since the day he'd watched her sort the parish post on the bench outside St. Petroc's and noticed — only noticed, mind — how her thumb lingered on certain envelopes before she tucked them into her satchel. A woman who read other people's letters was either a gossip or a keeper of secrets, and Coral Villalobos struck him as neither, which meant she was both.

He knocked. He waited. He heard, distantly, a piano lid being closed with unusual haste, as though someone inside had been caught playing it with their elbows.

Miss Villalobos opened the door. She had flour on her sleeve, which interested him enormously, because Miss Villalobos did not strike him as a baking woman. Baking women had flour in their hair and on their chins and an air of resigned martyrdom about them. This flour sat on her sleeve like evidence.

"Sergeant Quill," she said. Her voice was perfectly level. That was the trouble with Coral Villalobos — everything about her was perfectly level, like water that had decided, on principle, never to ripple. "Is something wrong?"

"I wonder if I might have five minutes of your time."

"Edith isn't home. She's in London until Thursday."

"I know," Quill said mildly. "It's your time I'm after."

Something flickered behind her eyes — not fear exactly, more like a lamp turned briefly up and then down again. She stepped aside. "You'd better come in, then. There's tea. Or there will be, once the kettle forgives me."

* * *

The kitchen was warm and smelled of bread, which was interesting, because Miss Villalobos had flour on her sleeve and no one else appeared to be home. Quill sat where she indicated and accepted tea he didn't especially want, because refusing hospitality in Cornwall was a crime worse than most of the ones on his books.

"You'll have heard about Hobbs the baker," Quill said.

"I've heard about half of Cornwall," Miss Villalobos said. She poured milk into his cup without asking whether he took it, which told him she knew how he took it, which told him she paid attention to things, which confirmed what he already suspected.

"His windows came in Tuesday night," Quill went on. "Bricks through the glass, 'Kaiser's friend' painted on his door. Twenty years that man has baked our bread. His boy is in France this minute." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "I arrested nobody, because I don't know who did it, and because part of me wonders whether arresting anyone would make a blind bit of difference. But it's started, Miss Villalobos. The fever. It comes up out of the ground like damp."

"That's very poetic, Sergeant."

"My wife says I should write my memoirs." He allowed himself a small smile, then put it away. "I've come about something else. Something I'd rather say plainly than let you hear it crooked later."

She folded her hands. Waiting. She was very good at waiting; he could see that. It was probably why she was such a good reader of letters — she understood that everything important arrived eventually, provided you held still long enough.

"There's a child in this house," Quill said.

The clock on the mantel ticked four times before she answered, and Quill counted every tick, and he saw her count them too.

"Whatever gave you that idea?"

"A child's coat, too small for you, hanging behind the scullery door. Bread enough for three baked this morning. Flour on your sleeve from teaching somebody, maybe, rather than feeding yourself. A face at the window twice, according to Mrs. Penhale, whose eyes are going but whose tongue is not." He set down his cup. "And a piano played badly just now by someone with small hands, because whoever it was stopped the moment they heard my knock, and grown people in this house play better than that."

Coral Villalobos looked at him for a long moment. Then she did the last thing he expected: she laughed. Not a big laugh — a short, startled one, like a bird startled off a wire.

"Mrs. Penhale," she said. "Of course. I could hide from the Admiralty, but not from Mrs. Penhale."

"So there *is* a child."

She didn't confirm it and didn't deny it, and Quill felt a strange respect settle over him, because most people, cornered, would have done one or the other, and doing neither took nerve.

"Here's what I know," he said, leaning forward, keeping his voice low even though there was no one to hear. "Since war was declared, there are orders about enemy aliens. Registration, internment, movement restrictions. And there are orders about missing persons, and among the papers that cross my desk this week was a circular from the metropolitan police regarding a girl, aged approximately eight, unaccounted for since July. Last associated with a German name." He paused. "I am not a clever man, Miss Villalobos, but I can add two and two, and lately the sum keeps coming out the same number, and the number is standing in your kitchen drinking my tea."

"It's your tea?"

"I brought my own leaves. Habit. Prison tea is terrible, and I imagine yours would be too, after they took away your housekeeper." He sighed. "That was a joke. A poor one. My wife says all my jokes are poor ones, which is why she married me — so no one else would have to suffer them."

For the first time, something in Miss Villalobos's shoulders came down half an inch. Not surrender. Just the smallest loosening, the way ice shifts before it decides what to become.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "If you suspect us — and you clearly do — why warn me? Why not search the house?"

Quill looked out the window at the garden, at the hens scratching in Edith Marchmont's vegetable bed with the impunity of creatures who answer to no law but appetite.

"Because I've been thinking a great deal about bricks," he said. "About Hobbs's windows. About what happens when ordinary people decide that suspicion is the same thing as duty. Somebody will report you, Miss Villalobos. Not today, perhaps. But Mrs. Penhale has seen a face at a window, and Mrs. Penhale talks to Mrs. Chegwin, and Mrs. Chegwin's nephew is home from the navy with nothing to do but feel useful, and somewhere down that chain a brick gets thrown or a letter gets written to the authorities in Truro. And when it does, it won't be me standing at this door with my hat in my hands. It'll be men from out of county who don't know Edith Marchmont from Eve and don't care to. Do you understand what I'm offering?"

"Tell me plainly. You're good at it, apparently."

"I'm offering you the chance to bring the child to me yourself. Voluntarily. Before Thursday." He held up a hand before she could speak. "Hear me out. If she comes to me voluntary, I can process it gently. She'd be taken into care — properly cared for, I'd see to it personally, there's a good family upcountry, the Pentreaths, lost their own girl to diphtheria and would spoil any child sent to them rotten. No cells. No strangers shouting. No newspaper men. Whereas if she's found here — found, mark you, hidden, in the household of a suffragette already notorious in three counties — then it becomes a *story*. Enemy child harboured by militants. The papers would eat it alive, and they'd eat Miss Marchmont alive with it, and you along with her. Everything she's built. Every cause she serves. All of it dragged through the mud because of one secret kept past its usefulness."

He watched her take that in. He watched her take it in and, to his considerable surprise, not flinch.

"And the child herself?" Coral asked quietly. "What happens to her in your gentle version? An orphan, a German orphan, in 1914, handed to a family in a country that's currently throwing bricks at bakers?"

"She'd be safe," Quill said, and heard how thin it sounded even as he said it.

"Safe," she repeated. "Yes. Like Herr Brandt is safe. On the Isle of Man, I'm told, behind wire, for the crime of having repaired half the clocks in this parish and refused payment from anyone who couldn't afford it."

So she knew about Brandt. Of course she knew about Brandt. Quill filed that away and felt the case assemble itself a little further in his chest, click by click, like some mechanism he wished he could stop winding.

"The world's gone mad," he said. "I won't pretend otherwise. Half of what I'm ordered to do these days sits wrong in my stomach, and I go home and tell my wife about it, and she says, *Thomas, you are a sergeant, not a saint, do what you're told and keep your head down.* And I mostly do." He met her eyes. "But I like you, Miss Villalobos. And I liked Aldous Brandt. And whatever else is true, that child in there — and yes, I'm quite sure now there's a child — deserves better than to be discovered by strangers. So I'm giving you till Thursday's tide, so to speak. Bring her in yourself, or move her somewhere I can't find her, but don't be here when the fever arrives. Because it will arrive. It always arrives."

* * *

He walked back down the lane slower than he'd walked up it, and the shame caught up with him around the second bend, as it always did.

That was the thing nobody warned you about mercy: it was heavy. Duty was light — you just carried the regulations and put one foot before the other and never had to think. But mercy required you to choose, and choosing meant owning the outcome, and Quill stood at the gate of the churchyard looking out at the sea and thought: *if that child comes to harm either way, it will partly be mine.* He had handed Coral Villalobos a knife and called it kindness.

A gull screamed somewhere over the harbour, greedy and indifferent. Down at the quay, two fishermen were painting a boat, and one of them said something that made the other laugh, and the laugh carried across the water, easy and unguarded, the sound of a country that didn't yet know what it was becoming.

Quill's wife would ask him tonight, over supper, how his day had gone, and he would say *quiet*, and it would be the first lie he'd told in weeks that actually troubled him.

Because here was the truth he hadn't said aloud, not even to himself, not until this moment, standing in the churchyard with the tide going out below him: he hadn't given Coral Villalobos an ultimatum at all. Ultimatums were for enemies. What he'd given her was a map of the trap, drawn by the man setting it, with the times of the patrols marked in pencil.

And a man who did that, Quill reflected, turning his collar up against the wind, was a man halfway to something. He wasn't sure what. Treason, probably, if you squinted. Decency, possibly, if you didn't.

Thursday, then. He'd said Thursday, and he was a man who kept his word, God help him.

But walking home past the bakery — its boards still nailed over the broken glass, Hobbs inside kneading dough with the stubbornness of all men who rise early regardless of empires — Quill saw something that stopped him in the lane. Fresh chalk on the wall beside the bakery door, small and neat, in a hand he almost recognised: a little drawing, quick and skilful, of a clock face. And beneath it, in letters no bigger than a shilling:

*This house keeps honest time.*

Someone had been defending Hobbs in the night. Someone with a chalk stub and steady nerves and, he suspected, excellent handwriting — the sort of hand that had spent years copying addresses onto envelopes.

Sergeant Quill stood looking at that little clock for a long while. Then he took out his notebook, wrote nothing at all in it, put it away, and walked on — whistling, very quietly, a tune his mother used to hum, and trying hard not to notice that it might have been German.
