## 10. Smugglers' Lane

The letter had come through the parish post on a Tuesday, which was the day old Mr. Pascoe sent his weekly complaint to the county council about the state of the Gwennap road, and so I nearly missed it, folded as it was between his grievances and a circular about potato blight.

I want to be clear that by this point in my career — if one may call it a career, this apprenticeship in other people's business — I no longer read everything out of idle curiosity. That had been true once, when I was seventeen and hungry for any life larger than my own. Now I read the way a lighthouse keeper watches the water: steadily, dutifully, waiting for the one thing that mattered to come into view.

The letter was addressed to a Mrs. Tregose of Tregose Cottage, Porthmellin Lane, and it was from her nephew in Penzance, and it said, in a hand like a spider drowning in ink:

*Auntie — the boy is willing but the boat wants work on her seams before she'll cross anything wider than the harbour. If you mean to do what you say you mean to do, tell him soon. The season for foolishness closes early this year.*

I sat with it on my knee in the lane outside the post office for a full minute while Mrs. Chegwin's dog investigated my bootlaces, and I thought: *cross*. Cross what? The Channel to France, most likely — there were men running brandy still, war or no war, because human appetite is the one trade that never observes a blockade.

But a boat that could cross the Channel could also cross it going the other way.

And somewhere in my chest, beneath the fear that had lived there since the night Mara arrived, something small and stubborn sat up and took notice.

* * *

I did not go to Porthmellin Lane that day. Or the next. A woman who reads other people's letters learns a certain discipline, and the chief article of that discipline is *patience*, and the second article is *never look eager*, and the third — which took me years to learn — is *know exactly what you're afraid of before you go knocking on doors*.

So I spent two days knowing.

I was afraid of Mrs. Tregose herself, of whom the village said only that she had buried three husbands and outlived them all comfortably, which told me either that she was very lucky or very careful, and I suspected careful. I was afraid of the price, because smugglers do not work for gratitude and I had precisely eleven pounds saved in a biscuit tin behind Edith's spare boots. Mostly I was afraid of the arithmetic of it: that even if the boat existed and the price could be met, I was a chauffeur's daughter with no surname worth saying planning to transport a German child across wartime waters past His Majesty's patrol boats, and every single part of that sentence would hang me separately.

Edith would have called it a suffrage problem. "Every cause looks impossible until enough women refuse to accept the arithmetic," she liked to say, usually while being arrested.

But Edith was in London, and the arithmetic sat at my kitchen table eating bread and butter with the methodical seriousness of a child who has known hunger, and I found I could not wait for anyone's permission, least of all history's.

On Thursday morning I told Mrs. Penhale that I was taking the motorcar to St. Ives for engine oil, which was even true; the car did want oil, eventually, in the way that all things wanted things if you waited long enough.

"Mind the coast road," she said. "They say there's a spy signalled from the headland after dark."

"Does he signal in code?" asked Mara, who was helping shell peas and absorbing the village's paranoia with the wide-eyed delight of a child being told excellent ghost stories.

"Of course he does," said Mrs. Penhale darkly. "That's what makes it spying."

Mara looked at me over the pea bowl, and I saw the corner of her mouth twitch — the first twitch in eleven days — and I thought, well then. Whatever else happened, we had achieved irony, and irony is a kind of courage.

"I'll wave at him on my way past," I said, and drove south.

* * *

Porthmellin Lane was not so much a lane as a suggestion the land had made and then regretted. It dropped from the cliff path in a series of hairpins that had been designed, I believe, by a man with a grudge against axles, and it ended at a cottage wedged into the fold of the hill like a stone someone had pocketed and forgotten.

There was a garden gone gloriously to seed, a rowboat upside down on trestles, and a woman splitting mackerel on a bench with a knife that moved faster than my eye could follow.

She did not look up. "If you've come about the gull," she said, "it died. If you've come about the boy's wages, they're paid Saturday. And if you're selling anything, I've bought nothing since 1897 and see no reason to start."

"None of those," I said. "I've come about a crossing."

The knife stopped.

She looked up then. She was small — smaller than me, and I am not tall — built like a knot in hard wood, with eyes the pale grey of a sea just before weather. She looked at me for perhaps four seconds, which is a long time when you are being weighed.

"You're Marchmont's driver," she said. "Seen you pass on the coast road. Red motorcar, goes too fast round Churchtown bend." A pause. "Girl reads letters."

My stomach performed a slow, dignified revolution. "I beg your pardon?"

"Don't beg, it's undignified." She went back to the mackerel. "Half this parish knows. They think you don't know they know. Old Pascoe leaves his letters unsealed because he likes an audience, and Mrs. Chegwin writes her sister the same gossip every week under three different envelopes hoping someone will read it, because living alone is a disease and the post is her only tonic." The knife flashed. "You think nobody notices a girl who stands too long at a letterbox?"

I stood very still, doing sums. "Are you going to report me?"

"To who? Quill?" Something that was almost a smile crossed her face and was gone. "Nephew of mine spent six months in Bodmin for a little quiet trading. I've no love for men whose whole occupation is knowing things." She set down the knife and wiped her hands on her apron. "Sit down. You'll want tea. Nobody makes a sensible decision standing up, and you look like you've been making decisions standing up for weeks."

It was unnerving, being seen through so completely. It was also — and this shames me slightly to record — the first time in months I had spoken to anyone without translating myself first, and the relief of it went through me like warm water.

"How did you know why I'd come?" I asked.

"I didn't." She poured tea from a brown pot into cups that did not match. "But a girl drives forty minutes past her errand to end up here, alone, wearing her good coat, and asks about a crossing — she isn't after brandy. Brandy girls don't wear good coats." She pushed a cup toward me. "So. What is it you want carried, and how heavy is it?"

"A child," I said. "A girl. Eight years old. To Holland. Flushing, if it can be Flushing."

Mrs. Tregose drank her tea and looked out at the sea for a long moment, and I watched her do her own arithmetic — hers in boats and tides and fines, mine in pounds and lies — and understood that we were, in our different ledgers, the same profession.

"Holland," she said at last. "In August. In wartime."

"Yes."

"With a child who cannot swim, presumably, because children never can."

"She can steer by stars," I said, and heard how it sounded the moment it left my mouth — absurd, tender, true.

Mrs. Tregose looked at me again, longer this time. "Whose child is she?"

"That," I said carefully, "is the part I can't say."

"Can't or won't?"

"Is there a difference that changes your price?"

Something moved in her face then — surprise, maybe, that I had turned the knife back around — and she laughed, a short bark like a seal. "Oh, you're wasted on a motorcar, girl." She set down her cup. "Here's the shape of it. My nephew has a boat, the *Kittiwake*, fast enough and shallow enough. He knows the French run, and he knows where the patrol boats sit lazy and where they don't. Holland's further than his usual work, and it's a fool's route this time of year, and the fine for carrying a soul out of England without papers isn't a fine anymore, it's prison, possibly worse, because there's a war on and men in London have got a taste for examples."

I nodded, and waited, because I had learned from reading ten thousand letters that people tell you the price last, and always after telling you every reason the price should be higher.

"Fifty pounds," she said. "Thirty now, twenty on delivery. And the girl travels as cargo — meaning quiet, meaning hidden, meaning if we're stopped, my nephew doesn't know what's under the tarpaulin and neither will I, officially. You bring her to the cove below Gwinear Head, Thursday fortnight, tide turns at half past two in the morning. Be early. The sea doesn't wait and neither do patrols."

Fifty pounds. I had eleven. I felt the number settle onto my chest like a stone, and then — this is the strange part — I felt something underneath the stone, something almost like gladness. Because a sum, however impossible, is at least a shape. An impossibility with a figure attached can be fought. It was the shapeless things I had learned to dread.

"And if we're caught?" I asked.

"Then the girl goes back to whoever's hunting her, you go to prison, and I go to prison, and my nephew complains about it for thirty years, which will be worse than the prison." She shrugged. "The sea's honest that way. Everything costs what it costs."

"One more thing." I put my cup down. "Why are you doing it? You don't know me. You don't know her."

Mrs. Tregose looked at her garden, at the rowboat, at the grey line of the horizon where the war was happening without visible effect on the mackerel.

"In 1871," she said, "my mother put me in a fishing boat at this same cove because there was fever in the village and my father dead of it, and a man none of us knew carried me across to Falmouth though it cost him his licence and near his neck. I never learned his name. Never paid it back. Been carrying the debt fifty years like a stone in my shoe." She stood, briskly, conversation over. "Some debts you pay forward or they rot. Fifty pounds. Thursday fortnight. Don't be late and don't be followed."

* * *

I drove home with the engine oil I had genuinely remembered to buy, feeling like a woman who had signed a contract with the sea itself, and I suppose I had.

The trouble with hope, I reflected somewhere along Churchtown bend — taking it slowly, for Mrs. Tregose's sake and my suspension's — is that it makes you careless. All summer I had been careful: Mara's name changed at the gate, her humming confined to closed windows, my face arranged into pleasant blankness whenever Sergeant Quill lifted his hat in the village street. But a woman driving home from having solved a problem walks differently. A woman with a plan has a lightness in her shoulders that the careful can smell.

I noticed it myself in the hallway mirror that evening and schooled my face flat, but the damage, as it turned out, was already done elsewhere.

Because it was that same Thursday night — I learned this later, and the learning came like ice water — that Sergeant Quill called at the house while I was out returning the parish letters, and Edith, home unexpectedly from London for the weekend, answered the door, and Quill stood on our step in the lamplight turning his cap in his hands and said, in the voice of a man who takes no pleasure in it:

"Begging pardon, ma'am, but I wonder if I might have a word about the niece from Bristol."

He paused, and looked past Edith's shoulder into the hall, at the coat hooks, at the child's boots sitting neatly beside mine.

"There isn't a niece enrolled at any school in Bristol," he said. "I checked Tuesday."

I was not there to hear it. I was half a mile down the dark lane with the postbag over my shoulder, reading by feel the address on the topmost letter, when I saw the handwriting — the cramped, clockwork-precise hand I knew better than my own these days — and stopped dead between one hedgerow and the next.

It was addressed to Sergeant Quill. It was unsigned. And it began: *Regarding the child concealed at Marchmont House.*

The tide, I thought, standing in the dark with my heart going like a trapped bird, turns Thursday fortnight.

Someone means to make sure we never reach it.
