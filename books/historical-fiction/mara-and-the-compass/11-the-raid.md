## 11. The Raid

I had been home four days when I understood that the house had changed its voice.

A house speaks to you if you've lived in it long enough — the particular creak of the third stair, the way the kitchen door sticks in damp weather, the rhythm of Coral's footsteps overhead at half past six. I had been listening to Marchmont Cottage speak for eleven years, and in four days it had learned a new sentence: a small footfall on the cellar stairs, a hush that fell over rooms a moment before I entered them, and Coral's laugh, which had always been rare as summer snow, now arriving on schedule like a woman performing cheerfulness for an audience.

I did not ask. That is the arrangement between Coral and me, and it has served us both: she does not ask what I do in London, and I do not ask what she does with the letters. But I am not a fool, whatever Sergeant Quill's constables believe, and by the fourth evening I had assembled the facts into their obvious shape. A child was living in my cellar. Coral was hiding her. And the child, judging by the lullaby I'd heard drifting up through the floorboards one night — a German lullaby, *Guten Abend, gut' Nacht*, sung in a whisper so careful it might have been contraband — was German.

I sat with this knowledge over my tea and felt something I had not expected to feel again so soon after London: fear. Not for myself. I had been spat at in Westminster, jailed in Holloway, force-fed through the nose twice, and I had learned that fear for oneself is a fire that burns down to nothing but stubbornness. This was a different fuel entirely. This was fear for a child who hummed Brahms in a cellar in Cornwall while the whole coast howled for German blood.

So when the knock came on Tuesday morning — three knocks, official, spaced like a telegraph message — I was almost relieved.

* * *

There were four of them. Sergeant Quill I knew, of course; he removed his helmet when I opened the door, which I noted and filed away as either decency or tactics. With him were two men from Bodmin in civilian coats cut too generously across the shoulders, and a young constable whose ears went pink when I looked directly at him, which told me everything useful there was to know about him.

"Mrs. Marchmont." Quill held out a paper. "We have a warrant to search these premises."

"Have you indeed." I read it with the unhurried attention of a woman checking a grocer's bill for errors. It cited the Defence of the Realm Act, suspicion of harbouring an enemy alien, and a signature I resolved to remember. "Well," I said, stepping back, "do come in. Mind the mat; it slides."

The pink-eared constable actually looked at Sergeant Quill before crossing my threshold, as though the mat might be armed.

They spread through the house with the method of men who had done this often enough to enjoy it and not so often as to be bored. The tall one from Bodmin went upstairs. The other began pulling books from the parlour shelf and riffling them spine-first, which is a thing no one who loves books ever does, and I confess I watched him do it with a pleasure I did not trouble to conceal.

"You'll want the study," I said pleasantly. "That's where the sedition lives."

He glanced up, uncertain whether he'd been insulted.

"My pamphlets," I clarified. "*Votes for Women*, leaflets from the WSPU, correspondence with Mrs. Pankhurst herself. If you're hunting treason, Constable, you needn't dig. I keep mine on the desk where I can find it."

Sergeant Quill made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a laugh swallowed at great cost. He was studying me with those tired grey eyes of his, and I understood that he knew — or suspected with a precision that amounted to knowing — exactly why they were here. And I understood something else, watching him watch me: he was giving me time. A minute, perhaps two, bought with paperwork and protocol.

I intended to spend every second of it.

* * *

Coral, meanwhile, was moving.

I heard her above me — I had positioned myself in the hall precisely so that I could hear everything above me — a floorboard here, the pump in the scullery, the ordinary noises of a house going about its morning, all of it performed by a woman whose actual morning involved getting a child down a ladder into a cellar and making that child silent. I had seen the cellar door stand ajar when I came down for breakfast, and I had said nothing then either, but now I walked past it and let my skirt brush it shut, casually, the way one closes any door.

The cellar was Coral's idea, I later learned. She had spent the days since my return doing something I would not have thought of: she had moved my confiscated suffrage papers down there. Boxes of them — *The Suffragette* in bundles, minutes from the Penzance branch, my prison letters, petitions with four hundred signatures — the whole paper archaeology of five years of fighting. And into the middle of it she had put a camp bed, a candle, a jar of barley sugar, and a child.

It was, when I thought about it afterward, a kind of genius. No search party in England wanted to touch those boxes. Men who would turn out a drawer of baby clothes without a qualm went pale and formal before a box labelled WOMEN'S SOCIAL AND POLITICAL UNION — MINUTES, 1912. It smelled of police stations and unpleasantness and women who had gone on hunger strike. The tall man from Bodmin took one look at the cellar stairs and assigned the pink-eared constable to go down instead, and the pink-eared constable descended into five years of my life with the expression of a boy entering a church where he owed money.

I know what he found, because Coral described it to me that night with a precision I found both alarming and rather magnificent. He found a child sitting perfectly still on a camp bed with an open book in her lap — my copy of *Jane Eyre*, as it happened, chosen by Coral on the grounds that no searcher on earth would interrupt a little girl reading Charlotte Brontë among suffragist papers. Around her, stacked with theatrical carelessness, were the boxes. The girl looked up when he came down, looked at him the way children look at strangers — with that frank, unblinking assessment that makes grown men feel they are the ones being searched — and went back to her book.

"Who's this, then?" he asked Coral, who had followed him down with a laundry basket on her hip as though she were simply continuing her day in an inconvenient location.

"My employer's niece," Coral said. "From Bristol. She's staying while her mother's poorly."

And here is the thing about Coral that people underestimate, because she is quiet and because she drives a car and because she has made herself furniture-shaped for so long: the girl can lie like a vicar reading a lesson. Flat, clear, unembellished, with just enough boredom in it. *My employer's niece. From Bristol.* Not too much detail. No trembling. She stood there holding laundry in a cellar full of sedition and gave the constable a look of polite inquiry, as though he were the unusual one for asking.

The constable looked at Mara. Mara looked at her book. Somewhere above us a drawer banged shut.

He wrote something in his notebook and climbed back up the ladder, and Coral followed with her laundry, and that was that — except that it wasn't, because I could see in Sergeant Quill's face when they regrouped in my hall that it was very much not that.

* * *

"Mrs. Marchmont." Quill stood in my parlour with his helmet under his arm and his men pretending to examine the windows. "You'll understand I have to ask. The child downstairs. Her name?"

"Mara," I said.

"Surname?"

"I'm sure Coral mentioned it. Bristol way. People from Bristol." I let a small pause sit between us, delicate as pastry. "Sergeant, may I be direct with you? You have turned my house inside out for an hour. You have found a nine-year-old reading Jane Eyre and several hundred documents asserting that women are persons. If you mean to arrest someone, I'd suggest starting with Mrs. Pankhurst, only you've already tried that, and we both recall how it ended."

The pink-eared constable developed a sudden fascination with the ceiling.

"The Act is the Act," Quill said. His voice was flat, but not unkindly flat — there is a species of kindness that disguises itself as bureaucratic dullness, and I had heard it from him once before, years ago, when he chose not to notice the meeting notices pinned to our gate. "Enemy aliens are to be reported. Harbouring one is a serious matter."

"Harbouring." I tasted the word. "Sergeant Quill, I am fifty-three years old. I have been called a harlot, a hysteric, a traitor, and a bad mother by men who had never met me, in newspapers with circulations larger than this entire county. Do you imagine the word *harbouring* frightens me?" I stepped closer, and I will admit I enjoyed it — enjoyed it the way one enjoys the last mile of a long march, when the legs have stopped arguing. "There is a child asleep in my house. Whatever else she is, she is a child, and she is frightened, and she eats her bread crusts first the way frightened children do. If your warrant requires you to drag her out of my cellar and parade her through a village that smashed Herr Lehmann's bakery windows last week, then you may do it. But you will write in your report — write it plainly, in your own hand — that Edith Marchmont refused, and that she will say so publicly, at every platform from here to Truro, and that she will name names. Mine first. Then yours."

Silence. The tall man from Bodmin had stopped pretending to examine the window and was openly staring at me, and I had the satisfaction of seeing in his face the particular alarm of a bureaucrat confronted with a woman who has already been to prison and therefore cannot be threatened with one.

Quill held my gaze for a long moment. Something passed behind his eyes — not agreement, nothing so useful as agreement, but recognition. The look of a man who has realized he is not fighting a criminal but a veteran, and that the war he thinks he's fighting is older and stranger than the one in France.

"You're playing a dangerous game, Mrs. Marchmont," he said quietly.

"I've been playing it since 1908, Sergeant. I'm rather good at it."

* * *

They left at noon. The warrant was satisfied — "nothing found inconsistent with the household's accounts," whatever that means, which means nothing, which means Quill had decided to write it that way. At the gate the pink-eared constable paused, turned back, and said, very fast, to his boots: "The book's a good one, miss. Jane gets out in the end." And then fled.

When the last boot had faded down the lane, I stood in my parlour amid the wreckage of my drawers and listened to the house resume its new voice. Then I went down the cellar stairs.

Mara was still on the camp bed, still holding *Jane Eyre*, though I noticed she was holding it upside down, and had been, I judged, for approximately an hour. Coral stood beside her with one hand resting on the child's head, and when she looked up at me her face was white and steady at once, which is a combination I recognized, because I had worn it myself in Holloway.

"Well," I said. "That's bought us some days. Perhaps a week."

"A week," Coral repeated.

"And then he'll come back with more than a warrant, because he's not a fool either, and because somebody in this village will make it worth his while." I looked at the child — thin wrists, watchful eyes, a face I had been told to forget by a dead man's handwriting and had refused, on principle, to forget since the night she arrived. "Which means we stop waiting for the tide to decide for us. Coral — whoever your smuggler friend is, whoever Mrs. Tregose is — I think it's time you told me what it costs. All of it. Because I have a motorcar, a reputation I no longer require intact, and a strong preference for spending both before Thursday."

Coral stared at me. "Thursday?"

"Call it a policeman's arithmetic," I said grimly. "Now come upstairs. We're planning a voyage over cold mutton, and nobody is telling anyone to forget anyone's face ever again."
