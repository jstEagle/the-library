## 12. What the Watch Knew

The watch sat on the kitchen table for three days before I dared open it.

That sounds absurd — a pocket watch the size of a plum, sitting between the salt cellar and Edith's copy of *The Suffragette* like any ordinary object — but I had come to think of it the way one thinks of a sleeping animal. Respectful distance. Mara had mended it in an evening, working by lamplight with a jeweller's screwdriver she'd produced from her coat pocket as if every eight-year-old carried one, and when she'd finished she had wound it, set it, and placed it in my palm without a word of explanation. She'd only watched my face, the way children do, to see what they've given you.

I had thanked her and put it in my apron pocket, where it ticked against my hip all day like a second heartbeat, and at night it went on the table beside my bed, and I lay listening to it and thinking about Aldous Brandt behind wire on the Isle of Man, and about the note that said *forget this face*, and about how thoroughly I intended to disobey both instructions.

But on the third evening — a Thursday, grey rain coming down like the sky had given up on subtlety — something happened that made me pick the watch up again.

Mara sneezed.

Now, a sneeze is not in itself an event. But this was the first sound she had made all day beyond a nod, and she had been pale since morning, and when I pressed the back of my hand to her forehead she was hot as a kettle left on. The girl who could dismantle a movement and reassemble it blindfolded was asleep in the chair by the fire before I'd finished fetching the blanket, her small hands curled shut, her breath whistling faintly.

I carried her up to bed — she weighed hardly more than a bundle of kindling — and came back down intending to fetch the camphor oil from the parlour cabinet, and stopped, because the watch had been left on the table where I'd set it while lifting her, and it had stopped ticking.

Wound it myself that very morning. I was sure of it. I had taken up the habit of winding it each dawn, a small ritual, a private conversation with a man I couldn't speak to. And here it sat, silent, its little hands frozen at twenty minutes past six.

"Mara?" I said uselessly, to a child three rooms away and asleep.

I turned it over. The case-back, which I had never had reason to open — Mara had opened it herself during the repair, of course, but I hadn't thought to look inside; I am many things but no horologist — was smooth gold-coloured metal with a hinge so fine you'd miss it unless your thumb found the seam. My thumb found the seam.

It clicked open like a locket.

Inside, where the mechanism ought to have been, there was instead a shallow cavity lined in green felt, and in the felt, folded into eighths, a slip of paper thin as onion skin.

* * *

Let me tell you what I did not do, because I am rather proud of it: I did not read it standing over the kitchen table with shaking hands like a woman in a melodrama. I lit the lamp properly, trimmed the wick, sat down in Edith's good chair, dried my palms on my skirt twice, and then unfolded it.

The handwriting was small and precise, the hand of a man accustomed to fitting a great deal into a very little space. I knew it at once. Not from the note at our door — that had been hurried, the letters leaning like people running — but from the clockmaker's mark beneath it, and from a letter I had once delivered, years ago, addressed to Edith in this same careful script. Some handwriting you don't forget. It has a voice.

The paper said:

> *To whoever holds this watch —*
>
> *If it is Coral Villalobos: I hoped it would be you. Forgive the theatre. A thing hidden is a thing half-safe.*
>
> *If you are reading this, then my daughter has reached you, and you have kept her breathing, which is more than I can do from where I sit. Her name is Mara Brandt. Her mother's name was Liesel. Write them somewhere safe, in your own hand, so that she will know them when she is older and braver than she needs to be tonight.*
>
> *She must go to Holland. Flushing, or anywhere the boats land. In Vlissingen, at the address below, lives my sister Greta Brandt-van Rijn. She knows everything. She will know what to do. Give her this watch — she will know it — and say these words: the clock is mended, the hour is kept.*
>
> *51°27' N, 3°36' E. That is the water off Flushing. Mrs. Tregose's father used the same numbers for other cargo; ask her.*
>
> *I made a promise to a frightened woman on a station platform in March, and promises are the only currency that keeps its value now. Keep my promise. I cannot.*
>
> *Forget my face if you must. Never hers.*
>
> *— A.B.*

I read it four times. On the fourth reading my eyes stopped being useful, and I sat in Edith's chair with the lamp hissing and the rain on the glass, and let the whole shape of it settle over me the way cold water settles over a swimmer — not all at once, but in layers, each colder than the last.

He had known. Standing on some London platform in March with a frightened wife and a child, he had known exactly where his daughter would have to go, and he had built the route into the watch itself — into the one object small enough to be carried through checkpoints, valuable enough to be guarded, and dull enough to be ignored. He had wound the coordinates and the sister's name and the promise into brass and felt and handed it to a little girl the way you'd hand someone a lifebelt, trusting her not to drop it.

And she hadn't dropped it. She'd carried it through God knew what, across England, in a storm, to a door in Cornwall, along with a note saying *forget this face* — because he'd written two messages, hadn't he? One for the world, to keep us from looking too hard. And one for the person clever enough, or loyal enough, or foolish enough, to open the case.

He'd trusted me to open the case.

"I hope it would be you."

Well. That did for me entirely. I put my head back against the chair and cried for perhaps a minute — efficiently, the way one cries when there's work to be done — and then I got up, found pen and paper, and wrote in my own hand, as instructed:

*Mara Brandt. Her mother's name was Liesel.*

I looked at those words a long time. Then I folded the paper into quarters and slid it into the lining of my corset, which is the one place in Cornwall no constable has ever shown interest in searching, and which I recommend to any woman with documents to protect and no time to build a false wall.

* * *

Mara's fever broke toward dawn. I sat up with her through the worst of it, cool cloths and camphor and the lullaby — I'd learned enough of it by now to hum along, badly, in the wrong key, and at one point in the grey small hours she opened her eyes, registered my terrible humming, and gave me a look of such profound German disapproval that I laughed and she smiled and then we were both laughing quietly in the dark like conspirators, which of course we were.

"You fixed more than the watch," I said, when we'd settled again.

She was quiet. Then, in her careful English: "Papa says — said—" The tense caught her, the way it always did, a small door swinging open onto a dark room. "Papa says a watch is honest. It only does what you wind it to do." Her fingers moved under the blanket, tracing gears in the air. "People are not honest. They say things and hide things. But a watch—" she yawned enormously, "a watch tells you the truth even when it is broken. You just have to know where to look."

"Where did you learn that?"

"Everywhere." Her eyes were closing. "In the shop. Under the counter. Papa let me hold the broken ones." A pause, long enough I thought she'd gone under. Then, barely audible: "He said I had steady hands like him and stubbornness like Mama, and that between the two I would be unstoppable, and Mama laughed and said God help England."

She slept. I sat by the window with the watch in my hands and the sky going the colour of dishwater, and I understood, finally and completely, what I was holding.

Not a message. An instruction manual. For me.

* * *

Edith arrived home on Saturday — earlier than expected, having concluded that London in wartime was merely Cornwall with better restaurants and worse manners — and I met her at the door with tea and the composed expression I reserve for delivering news that will make her either furious or magnificent, which with Edith are frequently the same thing.

She took one look at my face, set down her bag, and said, "Out with it. Has the Kaiser written?"

"Better." I told her everything. The fever, the stopped watch, the seam, the letter. I recited most of it from memory, because I had spent two days memorising it, and when I finished she stood very still in the hall with her hat still on, holding her gloves, staring at nothing.

"The clockmaker," she said at last, softly. "Aldous. Of course. Of course it would be Aldous." She pulled off her hat with a sharp motion. "He carried me two miles through a snowdrift when my appendix burst, did I ever tell you? 1908. Before you came. He didn't stop to ask whether carrying a suffragette might cost him custom. He simply picked me up." She flung the hat onto the hall stand. "And now they've caged him on an island for the crime of being born in Württemberg, and his child is hiding in my cellar eating bread and dripping, and the entire British state would call us traitors for feeding her."

"Yes," I said. "That's roughly the size of it."

"And you mean to take her to Holland." It wasn't a question. Edith had spent fifteen years being told by men what she couldn't do, and had developed a genius for hearing intentions before they're spoken.

"I mean to try. There's a smuggler in Falmouth. Mrs. Tregose. I'm to see her Tuesday."

"Coral." Edith crossed the hall and took both my hands, which she does perhaps twice a year, and which always makes me feel like a knight being dubiously armed. "You understand what you're contemplating. If you're caught putting that child on a boat — wartime, enemy alien, aiding escape — it isn't a fine. It's prison. Possibly worse."

"I've read enough confiscated mail to know precisely how much worse," I said. "Yes."

"And yet."

"And yet he wrote *I hoped it would be you*. In a watch. Two years' worth of foresight, folded into eighths, addressed to a chauffeur he met a handful of times." I heard my own voice go steady and strange. "Edith, everyone in my life has assumed I'm furniture. Useful furniture, well-polished, moves things from room to room. He looked at me and saw the person who'd finish the job. I can't un-see that."

Edith squeezed my hands once, hard, and released them. "No," she said. "You can't. I remember the sensation." She retrieved her hat and put it back on, which meant business. "Tuesday. We'll want a cover story for the journey, money in smaller notes, and you'll take the coast road, not the turnpike — Quill's constables watch the turnpike now. And Coral—"

"Yes?"

"When you see Greta Brandt-van Rijn, tell her her brother saved my life and I never properly thanked him. Tell her I intend to spend the rest of mine repaying it sideways."

* * *

Sunday after church — Mara and I had stayed home, she on account of lingering sniffles and me on account of not trusting either of us within earshot of patriotic hymns — I walked down to the post office to collect the parish delivery, because the war had not suspended correspondence and neither had Sergeant Quill's suspicions, and a driver seen doing her ordinary work is worth ten alibis.

There were the usual letters: ration notices, a soldier's card from France, the vicar complaining about coal prices to a cousin in Truro. And at the bottom of the bundle, one envelope that made my stomach drop through the floorboards.

It was addressed to Sergeant Quill. The sender's franking was from Falmouth. And the return corner bore a name I recognised from my own researches: *Tregose*.

Someone had written to Quill about Mrs. Tregose.

I stood in the post office queue with the letter in my satchel, weighing it — not the paper, the choice. Deliver it unread, like an honest woman, and let whatever was inside arrive on Quill's desk intact. Or steam it open over the kettle, as I had done with a hundred letters before, and know what was coming.

I want to say I hesitated out of conscience. The truth is I hesitated out of arithmetic: steaming takes eleven minutes, and Mrs. Penhale ahead of me in the queue was buying stamps for her nephew's wedding invitations, forty of them, and discussing each one.

So it was decided for me, which I have learned is how fate prefers to operate in Cornwall.

That afternoon, in the scullery with the door bolted and Mara keeping watch at the window — she'd appointed herself to the post with the gravity of a sentry, and saluted when I glanced at her — I held the envelope over the kettle spout until the gum softened, lifted the flap with a knife, and read:

> *Sergeant — Re: your enquiry of the 14th. Woman Tregose, retired, Falmouth side. No convictions on record but known to harbour persons and goods without paperwork, per her late father's trade. Warned her in '09 re: excise. She'll move anything for money and ask nothing. If your interest is contraband, expect tobacco. If otherwise — she keeps a boat at Piskie Cove and doesn't log her trips. Advise discretion. — Inspector Hale, Falmouth*

Quill was already asking about Mrs. Tregose. Which meant Quill already suspected someone might want to leave Cornwall by sea. Which meant he was closer than I'd thought — close enough to be writing to Falmouth, patient enough to wait for answers.

I sealed the letter back up, dried it under the flatiron, and delivered it Monday morning with my gloves on and my face arranged into pleasant blankness, watching Quill's broad back as he took it from me and thanked me in his mild voice.

"Quiet week, Miss Villalobos?" he asked, pleasantly.

"Very quiet, Sergeant."

"Good." He tapped the letter once against his desk, almost idly. "Quiet weeks are a blessing. Shame how rarely they last."

I drove home faster than usual. Tuesday I was due at Mrs. Tregose's. Monday night, I sat with Mara at the kitchen table teaching her to play cribbage, and outside the window, at the lane's end, a figure stood in the dusk for a long moment — just standing, just looking at the house — before turning away.

Mara saw it too. She didn't say anything. She just reached over and moved my king, which was a terrible cribbage move and also, somehow, the right one.

Two days, then. Perhaps less. The tide wouldn't wait, and neither, it seemed, would anyone else.
