## 13. Quill Closes In

Sergeant Thomas Quill had been a policeman for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned one lesson above all the others: the truth in a small village did not live in houses. It lived in between them. It lived in gateways and shop doorways, at the well, on the church steps after service. A man who wanted the truth in Porthcarrow had to stand very still in the gaps between things and let it brush past him like wind.

So that was what he did.

He stood still. He drank his tea at Mrs. Chegwin's bakery counter longer than any tea deserved to be drunk, and he heard how the Marchmont house had lights burning past midnight twice this week. He loitered by the fish carts and heard how Miss Villalobos — quiet creature, drove that motor car like she was born to it — had been seen walking toward Smugglers' Lane of an evening, alone, with her collar up. And he stood outside the post office while Mr. Penhale restacked his penny novels, and heard the baker's widow say, in the particular voice people used when they wanted to be overheard, that there was a child's coat drying on the line at Marchmont's, and it was not a coat anyone recognized, and its buttons were foreign.

"Foreign buttons," Quill repeated, when he got back to the station.

Constable Tresize, a young man built mostly of enthusiasm, looked up from polishing a boot he had been polishing since Tuesday. "Sir?"

"Buttons." Quill sat down heavily at his desk. "We are investigating buttons now."

"There was talk, sir. About the German girl. The missing one."

There was talk about everything these days. Talk had replaced weather as the chief topic of the coast. But the girl was real — that much had come down from Falmouth on proper paper, an inquiry circulated through channels, a photograph so blurred it might have been anybody's daughter or nobody's. *Child, female, approximately nine years. Last seen London. Any information to be conveyed without delay.* And beneath it, the word that made every constable in Cornwall sit up straighter: *enemy alien connection.*

Quill had read it eleven times. Not because it was complicated. Because every time he read it, he saw a different face over the top of it, and none of those faces belonged to a threat to His Majesty's Empire.

One of them belonged to Coral Villalobos.

He remembered her father, vaguely — a hard man out toward St. Ives way, the sort who believed children were livestock. Quill had been young then, new to the force, and he'd been sent once to the Villalobos place about a dog complaint, and he had come away with the peculiar, unshakeable impression that the dog was the only member of that household eating regularly. Years later, when the girl had vanished from the county and turned up driving for the suffragist lady, Quill had felt something dangerously close to pride. There were two kinds of people who came out of houses like that: the kind who broke, and the kind who slipped through fences. She had slipped.

And now she was slipping again, and his job was to close the gap behind her.

* * *

Mrs. Tregose was easier to find than she ought to have been, which told Quill most of what he needed to know.

Retired smugglers, in his long experience, came in two varieties: the ones who retired loudly and never stopped talking about the old days, and the ones who retired quietly and went on practicing. Mrs. Tregose belonged to a third category entirely — the ones who had never quite admitted the profession existed, and therefore could not be said to have left it. She kept goats. She sold honey at market. She had, according to thirty years of parish suspicion, moved more contraband along that coastline than the tide itself, and had never once been charged, because proving anything against Mrs. Tregose was like proving something against fog.

He walked out to her cottage on the cliff road on Wednesday morning, under a sky the color of dishwater, and found her splitting kindling on her step with an ease that would have shamed men half her age.

"Sergeant." She didn't stop splitting. "Come about my goats? They're all accounted for. Mostly."

"Your honey's good," Quill said. "I'll give you that."

"I know it is." She set the axe down and looked at him, and her eyes were pale and flat and old as the sea. "You've come about the other trade."

"I haven't said—"

"You're standing on my path wringing your hat, Sergeant, which you do when you want something and hate wanting it. Sit down or spit it out."

Quill sat on the step because sitting seemed safer than spitting. A goat regarded him from a distance with frank contempt.

"A boat left from a cove below here," he said. "Or will leave. Or is being arranged to leave. There's a child in it somewhere, Mrs. Tregose, and there's a name on a paper in Falmouth, and if that boat sails and I've stood by watching the gulls, it won't be me that pays for it. It'll be everyone it touches."

"And if it doesn't sail?"

"Then a child goes into the system. An internment camp's shadow, at best. A motherless girl, half German, in the year of Our Lord 1914, passed from institution to institution until nobody remembers why she was ever a person." He heard his own voice go rough and stopped. "I've read the file, Mrs. Tregose. There's no happy ending written in it. Only a tidy one."

The old woman was quiet a long moment. Down in the cove below, invisible but audible, the sea worked at the rocks like a patient carpenter.

"You're telling me your problem," she said finally, "as if it were mine."

"I'm telling you as a neighbor. If a boat goes from your water and something happens to that child — weather, patrol, worse — I couldn't look at this coast again. And if I report what I believe before it happens, then a woman who has done nothing in her life but good gets ruined, and a girl who has done nothing at all gets swallowed." He rubbed his eyes. "I'm asking you to tell me it isn't true. That's all. Give me one thing I can write in my report that lets me sleep."

Mrs. Tregose smiled, and it was not a comforting smile.

"Fifty years, Sergeant, nobody's bought anything off me but honey. You think I start today?" She picked the axe back up. "But I'll tell you what a neighbor tells a neighbor. The tide turns Thursday, near midnight. Spring tide, big water. If I were arranging a thing I weren't arranging, that's when I'd arrange it. And if I were you — a man with a conscience and a uniform that don't fit either — I'd decide what I am before Thursday, because after it you'll only get to be one thing."

* * *

He spent the afternoon failing to write a report.

The page in front of him bore three lines, each crossed out. *It is believed that* — no. *Information has reached this station* — from where? From buttons and gossip and a smuggler's arithmetic? *A child may be residing* — may be. Everything in his life had come down to *may be*, and the war had made the word heavier than iron, because these days *may be* was enough to ruin a family, burn a shop window, drive a baker out of the town his grandfather had built.

Quill put down the pen. He took out the blurred photograph again and looked at the smudge that was supposed to be a child's face.

Then he put on his hat and went to see Coral Villalobos — not officially. Officially would come later, or it wouldn't; that was the decision he hadn't made yet. He went unofficially, which meant he caught her at the lane above the Marchmont house, where she was cranking the motor car's handle with the grim competence of someone arm-wrestling a machine and expecting to win.

She saw him and went very still. Not frightened-still, exactly. Braced-still. Like a woman hearing a floorboard creak overhead and waiting to learn whether it was footsteps or just the house settling.

"Sergeant Quill." She wiped her hands on a rag. "Is there trouble with the car? Edith will be sorry to hear it, she thinks of it as a member of the household."

"No trouble with the car." He came closer, boots loud on the lane, and lowered his voice though there was no one for half a mile in any direction but gulls. "Miss Villalobos. I'm going to speak plain, because I think you're a plain-speaking woman, and because we haven't much time for anything else."

She folded the rag. Folded it again. Waiting.

"There's an inquiry out of Falmouth," he said. "About a child. And there's talk in this village that would curdle milk, and some of it has come my way, and I can't unhear it. And yesterday I stood on a certain lady's doorstep and asked her a question she wouldn't answer, and her not-answering was the loudest answer I've had in nineteen years of policing."

Coral said nothing. Her face did nothing at all, and that, too, was an answer — the trained blankness of a woman who had spent a lifetime making herself unreadable. But her hands kept folding the rag, tighter and tighter, until it was a small hard knot, and Quill thought: *there she is.*

"I could go back to the station," he said, "and write down what I believe. And what I believe would bring officers here by tomorrow noon. They'd search the house. They'd take the child. And whatever happened after that would happen in rooms you never see, to a little girl who'd learn quickly enough not to cry where anyone could hear."

The rag stopped moving.

"But I was a policeman in this parish when you were a girl nobody fed properly," Quill went on, quieter now, "and I have spent nineteen years learning that the law and the right thing are relations, not twins. Sometimes they sit at the same table. Lately they hardly speak." He took off his hat, which he did perhaps four times a year. "So here is what I've come to say, and then I'll say no more. Tide turns Thursday night, near midnight. Big spring water. If that child is gone from this parish before the tide turns — gone clean, gone across, gone wherever she's going — then there is nothing for me to find, is there. Nothing ever happened here. No child was ever here. I have talked to you about the weather and the car, and you have agreed the weather is changeable."

Coral's breath went out of her slowly, like air from a punctured tire. For one moment the blankness cracked and he saw underneath it — the exhaustion, the terror, the fierce stubborn love that had gotten her into this — and then she closed it away again, but not fast enough, and he pretended not to have seen, because mercy is mostly pretending.

"Why?" she asked. Her voice was barely above the wind. "Why would you risk it?"

Quill put his hat back on and considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty even if he'd have been struck off for giving it.

"When I was a young constable," he said, "I was sent to a house about a starving dog. I couldn't prove anything. The law said the dog was property and the owner could treat his property how he liked, and the law was correct, and I have thought about that dog for twenty-five years." He looked down the lane, toward the grey sea. "I won't think about a child the same way. That's the whole of it, miss. I'm not a brave man. I'm a tidy one. And this is the only untidy thing I mean to do before I die."

"The boat may not sail," Coral said carefully. "Plans fail."

"Then plans fail, and I'll do what I must, and so will you, and God help us both. But if it sails—" He met her eyes. "Thursday midnight. After that, I can't hold the pen anymore. Someone senior will ask me questions I can't refuse to answer. Whatever I've given you ends when that tide turns. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you, Sergeant."

"One more thing." He hesitated, hating himself slightly. "Miss Marchmont. If it comes badly — if they take the house apart — she should have a solicitor ready, and papers in order, and she should say nothing without him. She'll want to fight them with words. She always does. But words in a courtroom move slower than soldiers at a door, and she needs to understand the difference." He paused. "Tell her a policeman told her that. She'll know one who means her well."

Coral looked at him for a long moment. Then she extended her hand — the rag still knotted in it, so that the handshake was half grease and half grace — and shook his hand like a man.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me. Get the child out." He turned and walked up the lane, and did not let his shoulders sag until the bend hid him from the house.

* * *

That evening he sat in the station with the lamp lit and wrote his report at last, the honest version, the one that would destroy him if it were ever read:

*Nothing to report. Parish quiet. Weather changeable.*

He signed it. He blotted it. He locked it in the drawer with all the other years.

Then he took out his watch — a good watch, kept well — and looked at the hands, and thought about clocks and tides and the small hours of Thursday, and how a man's whole life could turn on whether he had the courage to look at nothing very hard for one more night.

Outside, far off, the sea was already pulling toward the shore, gathering itself for the big water, keeping its own appointments.

Two days. One tide.

Quill turned down the lamp and sat in the dark a while longer, listening to it come.
