## 14. Thursday Comes Early

I had always imagined that the end of things would announce itself properly — with a knock, a shout, a bell at least. In my experience of other people's catastrophes, gleaned from years of reading their letters, ruin generally sent a calling card first.

It did not send one on the last Tuesday of October. It came instead as Mrs. Pascoe's washing.

I noticed it because I noticed everything; it was my profession and my affliction. The Pascoes' line ran beside our gate, and for eleven months it had carried the usual inventory: sheets, petticoats, a shirt with a collar so starched it could have stood for Parliament. On Tuesday there was a new garment among them. A man's shirt, grey, with a red band stitched at the shoulder.

I knew what it was because I read everything, including the parish notices nobody else bothered with. It was the signal the Coastal Watch had agreed upon at their meeting in the schoolhouse — the meeting Edith had attended openly, out of sheer contrariness, and reported to me over supper with a running commentary on the chairman's hypocrisy. *Red band means the household is suspected. Red band means watch them.*

Mrs. Pascoe was hanging it facing our house.

I stood at the scullery window with a teacup going cold in my hand and thought, with a strange calm, *So. Thursday has come early.*

* * *

Quill arrived within the hour. That was the thing about Sergeant Quill — he was never cruel, and he was never late, and between those two virtues he managed to be more frightening than most cruel men I have known.

He did not come to the door. He came to the garden wall, where I happened to be deadheading the chrysanthemums with more force than they deserved, and he leaned on the stone as though we were two neighbours discussing the price of mackerel.

"Miss Villalobos."

"Sergeant Quill."

"Wind's turning," he said. "Come up from the southwest. Be rough water by tomorrow night."

"I don't have any interest in the water."

"No," he said. "But you've an interest in tides, I think." He took off his cap, turned it in his hands, and put it back on. "There's been a letter written to the Chief Constable. Not anonymous — signed. Mrs. Pascoe. Very thorough woman, Mrs. Pascoe. She's noted the child's accent, the German books she saw through your window — you ought to draw your curtains, miss, you've a library like a lighthouse — and the visits to Mrs. Tregose."

My hands went still among the chrysanthemums. Somewhere behind me, inside the house, Mara was practising her English vowels with Edith, who insisted that a girl who said *w* for *v* would never pass in Bristol, and Mara insisting back that Bristol was a fiction and she saw no reason to rehearse it.

"She can't have seen German books through the window," I said. "The bookshelf faces the sea."

"That's what I told her." Quill looked out at the horizon, which was doing nothing useful. "I'm to act on it Friday. That's the order. Search, removal of the child to the authorities, statement from Mrs. Marchmont." He paused. "Friday is what the paper says. But Mrs. Pascoe also wrote to the newspaper in Truro, and newspapers are faster than constables, and there are gentlemen in this county who like nothing better than a crowd and a cause. If they come before I do—" He stopped himself, which was its own kind of sentence.

"You're telling me to run."

"I'm telling you," said Quill carefully, "that the tide turns Thursday night, and after that the weather closes in for a week. And that I have a great deal of paperwork to complete before Friday, and I am a slow writer. You may make of that whatever you like."

"Quill." I said his name without the sergeant, and he didn't correct me. "Why?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then he said, "In 1911 my sister was in a hunger strike in Walton Gaol. Nobody in uniform lifted a finger for her. A lot of people outside the walls did, though. Some of them were women who drove motorcars." He settled his cap. "I keep accounts, Miss Villalobos. Mine are overdue."

He walked away down the lane without hurrying, a man going about his lawful business, and I stood among the ruined chrysanthemums and understood that I had just been given forty-eight hours disguised as a weather report.

* * *

Edith took the news standing up, which surprised no one who had ever seen her take anything.

"A denunciation," she said, when I'd finished. "Signed. How thrilling. I haven't been formally denounced since 1908, and that time it was only for heckling Asquith." She poured three cups of tea — hers, mine, and Mara's, sweetened the way the girl liked it without anyone having to ask anymore — and set them out on the kitchen table like place settings at a dinner party. "Well. We shall be organized about it. I do hate being disorganized in a crisis; it makes one feel middle-class."

Mara sat with both hands around her cup, watching us the way she watched everything: sideways, patient, missing nothing. Eight years old and already fluent in the language of adults pretending not to be afraid.

"We'll go tonight," I said. "Tonight or tomorrow night. Tregose will want the dark either way."

"Then tonight." Edith counted on her fingers, which she only ever did when the arithmetic mattered. "Packing. Money — there's the tin in the linen cupboard and the account at the bank, though the bank won't be open till nine, and I don't trust Mr. Pengelly not to gossip. Food. The papers — no, leave the papers, let them find the papers, let them read every word and choke on them. The car—"

"The car goes with us," I said. "Tregose's price. Half of it, anyway."

"Mm." Edith sipped her tea. "And the other half?"

"I'll manage it."

"Coral." She set the cup down with a small precise click. "How much is Mrs. Tregose asking for the balance?"

"Edith—"

"How much?"

"Six pounds," I said. "And I have four, if I sell my mother's brooch, which I intend to do, and two pounds' worth of pride, which I also intend to sell, though the market for it is poor."

Edith looked at me for a long moment. Then she got up, went to the dresser, and unlocked the drawer where she kept the household ledger and, beneath it, a biscuit tin that had contained shortbread in another century. She counted notes onto the table. Ten pounds.

"That's the campaign fund," I said. "That's for the WSPU, that's for—"

"It is for getting a child out of a country that has stopped being safe for her," Edith said. "If the movement cannot survive ten pounds, then the movement deserves to lose, and I will say so at the next meeting myself." She pushed the notes across. "Take it. Argue later. We haven't time for you to be noble properly; nobility done quickly always comes out sloppy."

From the corner of my eye I saw Mara slide her hand across the table, very quietly, and cover one of the pound notes, as if protecting it. As if she understood exactly what money was and what it cost. My chest did something complicated and painful, and I made tea-related noises until it passed.

* * *

We packed in the manner of people who have rehearsed disasters in their heads for weeks, which we had. Mara folded her own clothes — three dresses, a coat, the German picture book she wrapped in a shawl and held against her chest like a small warm animal. I packed food, the letters I dared not burn and could not leave, and Brandt's watch, which lived now in my left pocket and had begun to feel less like an object than an organ.

It was while I was buckling the valise that Edith said, conversationally, "You'll want to take the coast road as far as Penryn, the main road will have a sentry post by now."

"Yes."

"And you'll want to leave the car at the top of Tregose's lane, not the bottom; the tide comes right up to the hedge at high water and you'll drown the engine."

"Yes."

"And you'll drive slowly past the church, because young Trevanion is on watch there and he sleeps at his post like his father before him."

I turned around. Edith was folding a map she had drawn herself, in her own hand, with her own annotations, in the neat upright script she used for placards. She had been planning this since the raid. Possibly since before the raid. Possibly since the day she came home from London and found a child asleep on her chaise and did not once suggest sending her away.

"You're coming with us," I said. It came out as an accusation, which was accurate.

"No," said Edith.

"Edith—"

"Coral." She smoothed the map flat and looked at me over her spectacles, and I braced myself, because I knew that look. Every suffragist in England knew that look. It was the look that preceded chains to railings and hunger strikes and other forms of behaviour that sensible people called madness and Edith called tactics. "Think it through. Three people arriving at a black cove in a motorcar at midnight draws three times the attention of two. Two women and a child walking down to a boat — well, that's smugglers' business, old as Cornwall, half the parish's grandmothers did it and the other half married them. But Edith Marchmont, the suffragist, missing from her house the same night the police come?" She shook her head. "They'd turn the whole coast over. They'd drag Tregose's cove. They might follow you onto the water."

"So you stay and be arrested."

"So I stay," said Edith, "and answer questions. Which, I remind you, is the one activity at which I am genuinely excellent. I have been questioned by magistrates, by inspectors, by a Home Secretary's under-secretary, and by my late husband's family, and I have never yet given a straight answer that hurt anyone I love. My name is worth something here — not enough to stop them searching, but enough to slow them down, and slowing them down is all you need. By the time they've finished with me, you'll be in Holland eating cheese with Brandt's sister, and Quill will be writing his report with a headache."

"And if it doesn't work? If they charge you?"

"They might." She said it lightly, the way she said everything true. "Concealment of an enemy alien child. It sounds dreadful. It will sound considerably less dreadful at trial, when I stand up in my best hat and explain that the child in question is the daughter of the man who pulled me out of the Fal estuary in 1909 when my motorcar went off the ferry road, and that I owe him a life, and that paying debts is rather the point of civilization. Juries are sentimental, Coral. So are magistrates. Everyone is, underneath. It's why I became a suffragist — I kept meeting all this sentimentality with nowhere to put it."

I sat down on the edge of the bed, because my legs had opinions of their own and they had voted to give up.

"This is my fault," I said. "All of it. I read the letters, I opened the door, I—"

"You opened the door," Edith said, "which is the single finest thing anyone has done in this house since I nailed a banner to the church roof. Don't you dare apologize for it." She crossed the room and took my face in her hands, spectacles cold against my cheekbones, and kissed my forehead the way she had once, famously, kissed the forehead of a policeman she was distracting from a window-smashing. "Coral Sterling. You were invisible when you came to me. Do you know what you are now? You are a woman crossing occupied water to keep a promise to a dead man. There is nothing invisible about you. There never was. The world just needed to catch up."

* * *

At half past eleven I woke Mara.

I had planned to wake her gently, with a hand on her shoulder and her name whispered. What actually happened was that the floorboard betrayed me, as it always does, and her eyes opened before I'd touched her — wide, instantly awake, a child whose body had learned that sleep is something that gets interrupted. For one second she looked at me with pure animal fear. Then she found my face, catalogued it, decided it was the safe one, and sat up.

"Is it time?"

"Yes, lieve. Time."

She dressed without a word, quick fingers, everything in its place — she had repacked her bundle twice that afternoon without being asked, tightening the shawl, redistributing weight. Her father's training, I thought. Clockmakers understand that everything must fit and nothing must rattle.

In the kitchen Edith was waiting with a basket: bread, cheese, apples, a slab of her notorious fruitcake, which Mara had once politely described as "a challenge." There was also, wrapped separately, a paper of boiled sweets, because Edith maintained that no journey undertaken by a child should lack sweets, and because she was right.

They stood together in the lamplight, the tall woman and the small one, and Edith crouched down to Mara's level, which she almost never did, because Edith believed children should be addressed as equals and equals meet standing up.

"Now," she said. "You will listen to Coral. On the road, on the water, everywhere. When she says hide, you hide before you finish wondering why. Can you do that?"

"Yes, Miss Marchmont."

"When you get to Holland, you will find Frau van der Meer, and she will feed you until you're sick of feeding, which will take some time, Dutch people being what they are." Something moved behind her glasses and was firmly suppressed. "And you will write to me. In English. Your w's need work but we shall persevere by post."

Mara looked up at her. And then, without any warning at all, she stepped forward and put her arms around Edith's neck and held on, hard, the fierce silent grip of a child who has already lost too many people to waste time on goodbye.

Edith's hands hovered — Edith, who faced down mounted police, whose hands had never once hovered in my hearing — and then closed, gently, on the small back, and she pressed her cheek to the girl's hair and closed her eyes, and neither of them made a sound.

I looked at the ceiling. I studied the beams. There was suddenly a great deal of moisture in the kitchen air and someone responsible for it was going uncredited.

When Mara let go, Edith straightened, cleared her throat once, and became brisk again as though putting on a coat. "Right," she said. "Car's round the front. Headlamps stay dark till you're past the crossroads; the moon will do the rest. And Coral—" she caught my sleeve as I passed with the valise "—the note on the hall table is for Quill. Give it to him yourself, when it's over. He'll understand it."

"What does it say?"

"None of your business," said Edith Marchmont pleasantly, "and everything to do with yours."

* * *

The engine started on the third try, which in October counts as a miracle. I let it idle while Edith stood on the step with the lamp behind her, one hand raised — not waving, just raised, the way you'd salute a ship leaving harbour.

Mara twisted round in her seat to watch the house recede. "Will she be all right?"

"She'll be magnificent," I said. "It's her best setting."

The lane unwound ahead of us, silver under a thinning moon. At the bend, where the hedges met overhead, I glanced back once. Edith was still on the step, still holding the lamp, a small bright fixed point in the dark like — I thought, and the thought steadied my hands on the wheel — like a light somebody keeps burning in a window so that the ones who went ahead will know the way home is still theirs.

Then the hedges closed, the lamp went out behind us, and there was only the road, the sleeping parish, the ticking of Brandt's watch in my pocket, and six miles of Cornish night between us and the sea.

We had gone perhaps two of them when Mara said, very quietly, "Someone is walking along the hedge."

I looked. She was right. A figure, keeping pace with us in the field beyond the hedge, unhurried, deliberate — keeping pace the way only someone who intends to be waiting for you further on can keep pace.

I pressed the accelerator down and prayed to the god of carburettors, whom I had lately come to believe in.
