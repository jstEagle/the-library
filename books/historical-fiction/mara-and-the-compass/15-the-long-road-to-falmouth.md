## 15. The Long Road to Falmouth

### Coral

We left by the coal door, because dignity is a luxury and the front gate was being watched.

I had dressed Mara in one of Edith's old travelling coats, the sleeves rolled back three times each so her hands could find their way out, and she looked like a small girl playing at being a giant. She did not laugh. I had hoped she might — there is a particular kind of laughter children produce when they are dressed in adult clothing, a laughter that says *the world has gone briefly absurd and therefore bearable* — but she only stood on the step while I checked the latch, her chin tucked into the coat's high collar like a wren in a teacup.

"The cart will be at the crossroads," I said. "Mr. Pascoe owes me a favour and doesn't know it yet."

"Doesn't know it yet?"

"I'm going to remind him of something he said last spring about how he'd never forget what Mrs. Marchmont done for his sister. People say things in spring they don't expect to be held to in autumn." I pulled my own coat closed. "It's a kind of magic. Reading letters teaches you that."

Mara nodded as though this were a perfectly ordinary way to conduct one's life, which, after these weeks under our roof, perhaps it was.

The night was the sort Cornwall keeps in its back pocket for emergencies: no moon, a wind off the water carrying salt and the smell of rain not yet fallen. Good for us. Terrible for the hens we startled crossing the yard, who raised such a fuss that I had to stand still in the dark for a full minute, heart hammering, waiting to see if any window would light.

No window lit. Edith was downstairs in the parlour with the lamps burning bright and the curtains open, being magnificently visible, exactly as planned. Somewhere behind those curtains she was pouring herself a whisky she didn't want and arranging her face into the expression she used on magistrates. I thought: *let them come to that house tonight and find a suffragist reading the *Times* aloud to an empty chair.* Let them spend an hour being lectured on the Franchise Bill. It was, in its way, the bravest thing anyone did that night, and she did it sitting down.

At the crossroads, Mr. Pascoe's cart materialised out of the dark with the creak of a ship in a storm, drawn by a pony named Duchess who regarded me with the flat contempt only ponies can manage.

"Miss Villalobos," he said. "This is a bad business."

"It's a necessary one."

"Same thing, mostly." He handed me the reins without being asked. "Duchess knows the Penzance road better than the parson knows scripture. Don't let her stop at the Black Dog — she'll try. There's a turnip patch."

He climbed down, touched his cap to Mara — who had ducked low among the sacking — and walked off into the darkness whistling, a man determined to be able to swear later that he'd seen nothing at all.

And so we went. Two fugitives and a contemptuous pony, rolling west through the sleeping country toward Falmouth, the tide, and Mrs. Tregose.

* * *

### Mara

The cart smelled of turnips and horse and a little bit of the sea, which was far away but coming closer every time the wind changed. I sat in the straw with my knees up and watched the stars move over the hedges. Papa taught me stars. He called them *die Uhr des Himmels* — the clock of the sky — and said you could tell the hour by Orion if you were patient, though I never got further than knowing Orion was up, or down, or half-up, looking like he'd lost something in the grass.

Mama knew the stars too. Mama knew everything and told nobody.

I thought about that for a long time, the wheels going *rumble-rumble*, and then I thought about other things, which I had been very carefully not thinking about for weeks, the way you can balance a whole tower of blocks on your head if you hold still enough.

"Are you warm?" Miss Coral asked from the front.

"Yes," I said, because lying to grown-ups is allowed when it makes them feel better. It was one of Mama's rules.

The cart stopped once, at a place where the road bent near a farm, and Miss Coral held up her hand like a soldier, and we heard voices — men, more than two, boots on gravel — and then lantern-light swung across the hedge and lit up the inside of my eyelids even through my closed eyes. My whole body went tight as a wound watch. The voices came closer. One of them laughed at something. A boot scraped stone not six feet from where Duchess stood chewing placidly, betraying nothing, because ponies have no conscience.

"*Halt!* Who goes there?" a voice barked, right over the hedge.

There was a pause. Then another voice, farther off: "It's Farmer Tredinnick's dog, you fool. Stand down."

The lanterns moved away. My heart kept going like a sewing machine for another mile at least.

When the road was empty again, Miss Coral turned round and looked at me, and I saw that her hands were shaking on the reins even though her voice wasn't.

"Well," she said. "The British Army has been routed by a sheepdog. I shall sleep easier knowing the coast is defended."

I made a sound that was almost a laugh. It came out strange, like a hiccup wearing a hat.

* * *

### Coral

The railway nearly undid us.

The plan — and I use the word generously; it was more of a sketch than a plan, the way a child's drawing of a horse is a horse — was to leave the cart at a copse outside Penzance and walk to the station, where the late train to Truro would connect with the Falmouth line. What the plan failed to account for was that the station at Penzance at eleven o'clock at night in wartime is not empty. It is full of soldiers, most of them drunk, all of them homesick, and several of them bored enough to notice a woman and a child hurrying along the platform with their collars up.

I bought two third-class tickets with money sewn into my petticoat, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me. "To Truro, please. And a newspaper, if you have one."

"We've got the *Western Morning News*."

"Then I'll have that, though it'll be nothing but bad news and shipping notices."

"That's the news, miss."

The soldiers were gathered near the buffet, singing something with great feeling and no accuracy. As we passed, one of them peeled away from the group — young, red-cheeked, barely old enough to shave — and fell into step beside me.

"Lovely evening," he said.

"Dreadful," I agreed. "Good night."

"You're out late, miss."

"And you're drunk early. Between us we make a scandal." I kept walking. Mara's hand had found mine and gripped it hard enough to hurt.

He laughed — the laugh of a boy who wants to like everyone and hasn't learned better — and, mercifully, returned to his friends. But at the ticket barrier a sergeant was checking papers, slow and methodical, working through the queue like a man shelling peas, and I felt the arithmetic of the situation settle coldly in my stomach: two tickets, no papers worth the name, a child whose accent, if she spoke, would end everything.

"Mara," I murmured. "Not one word until we're through. Not one."

She nodded against my arm.

When our turn came, I produced the tickets and launched immediately into complaint, which has saved me more often than courage ever has. "Honestly, the state of the railways. We've been standing on that platform twenty minutes and no one can tell us whether the Truro connection is even running. My sister's girl here is half asleep on her feet, we've come up from Bristol, and if we miss the connection I shall write to the company, I truly shall—"

"Platform two, miss," said the sergeant wearily, waving us through, already turning to the next traveller. "Connection's running. Mind the gap."

Bristol. The lie I'd built in the village had followed us onto the platform and put itself to work. I have never been so grateful to a fiction in my life.

We found a corner seat in an empty carriage, and as the train pulled out I watched Penzance slide away, its lights smeared by the wet glass, and I thought about Edith alone in her bright parlour, and about Sergeant Quill somewhere in the dark with Thursday ticking in him like a bomb, and I reached over and tucked the coat more closely around the small shape beside me.

Mara was asleep before the first station. Children can do that — close the day like a book and put it on the shelf. I envied her with my whole tired heart, and then I did not envy her at all, because I understood what the closing cost, and what waited on the shelf.

* * *

### Mara

I woke up crying. That is how it happened — not awake-then-crying, but crying-already, before I even knew where I was, the tears arriving ahead of me like scouts.

The train was rocking. The lamp above the seats made a yellow circle. And Miss Coral was there, sitting very straight, watching the black windows, and I think seeing her is what did it, because Papa used to sit just like that on the night trains, straight-backed, keeping watch so Mama and I could sleep.

I tried to stop. I am good at stopping. I hadn't cried since London, not properly, not since the night Mama—

But the crying wouldn't go back in. It was like the watch Papa gave me to mend once, when I was small, where all the springs had come loose and if you took off the back plate everything jumped out at once and you couldn't ever get it all back in the same order.

"Hey," Miss Coral said. "Hey now."

"I'm sorry," I said. "I don't know why—"

"There's nothing to be sorry for. Come here."

I didn't come there. I stayed where I was, stiff, because I had learned that if you go to people when you cry they sometimes send you away afterwards, and I could not be sent away, not now, not in the middle of England in the middle of the night.

Miss Coral seemed to understand this. She didn't reach for me again. She just sat there, and after a while she began to talk, quietly, the way you talk to a frightened animal.

"When I was fourteen," she said, "my father decided I was to be married to a man of forty-six who owned a fish-curing business and three opinions, all of them unpleasant. So I ran. I walked out of Plymouth with two shillings and a hairbrush, and I cried the entire first night behind a hayrick, and I swore I'd never cry again, because crying was weakness and weakness was how they caught you."

She paused. The wheels went on underneath us, steady as a heartbeat.

"Do you know what happened the second night? Nothing. No one caught me. No one even looked at me. Because I had learned to be invisible — quiet, useful, forgettable. It kept me safe for years. It kept me alive. And it was the loneliest trick I ever learned." She turned and looked at me, and her eyes were shining too, which surprised me so much the crying went quieter. "You've been invisible a long time, Mara. Since London. Maybe longer. Holding still with the whole tower of blocks balanced on your head. Well. Tonight the blocks may fall. I'll catch what I can."

So I went there. Across the space between the seats, into the smell of her coat, which was wool and coal smoke and a little bit of engine oil, and I cried the way I hadn't let myself cry since Mama put me on the boat and said *don't look back, look forward, always forward, mein Schatz*, and I cried for Mama and for Papa and for the house with the clocks and for the whole city that was gone, and Miss Coral held me the whole time and did not once tell me to hush.

Once she said, into my hair, "You're allowed to grieve her. You're allowed to grieve all of it. Grief isn't disobedience."

I didn't know until she said it how much I had needed someone to say exactly that.

* * *

### Coral

She slept at last, truly slept, heavy and boneless against my shoulder, the way only exhausted children and sailors manage. The train rattled on through the dark. At some unnamed halt a porter looked in, saw a woman with a sleeping child, and moved on without interest, and I understood suddenly that we had passed, in his ledger, from *suspicious* to merely *ordinary*. Invisibility, it turns out, comes in many costumes. Some of them are even comfortable.

Falmouth arrived grey and dripping, the air thick with the sea. I woke Mara gently, and she surfaced without fear this time, blinking up at me with her mother's eyes and something new in them — trust, maybe, or the beginning of it, which is the same substance at a younger age.

"Nearly there," I whispered.

"Where's there?"

"A cove, and a boat, and a woman named Mrs. Tregose who smells of tar and drives a harder bargain than the devil."

"That sounds terrible."

"It does rather. Keep close, and if anyone asks, you're my niece from Bristol, and you're sulky because I wouldn't buy you sweets."

"I don't have to act that part."

"No," I said, taking her hand as we stepped down onto the platform, into the wind, into whatever came next. "You're a natural."

But as we came out of the station into the grey light of the quay, I saw a figure leaning against the harbour wall ahead of us — still, patient, watching the boats — and my stomach dropped like a stone down a well, because I knew that stillness, and I knew that patience.

Sergeant Quill had not waited for Thursday.
