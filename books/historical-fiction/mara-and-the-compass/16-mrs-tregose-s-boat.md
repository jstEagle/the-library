## 16. Mrs. Tregose's Boat

The road down to the cove did not so much end as give up. One moment the cart track was a track; the next it was a suggestion; and after that it was simply the memory of a path, overgrown with gorse and littered with slate, winding down toward water that was less sea than darkness given texture.

Coral left the borrowed pony tied to a stunted elm and walked the last quarter mile alone, her boots finding purchase by feel rather than by sight. Behind her, Mara slept in the cart under sacking and fish boxes, which had been Mrs. Tregose's own suggestion — *child travels as cargo, cargo asks no questions* — and Coral had hated the wisdom of it.

She had been walking for most of two nights. Her body had passed through tiredness some hours ago and come out the other side into a strange clear buzzing state in which everything seemed slightly hilarious and slightly doomed. She thought about Edith's face when she'd driven off — that brisk nod, the one Edith used when she wanted to appear calm for someone else's benefit — and she thought about Sergeant Quill's warning ringing in her ears like a cracked bell: *Thursday, Miss Villalobos. The tide turns Thursday.* And here she was on Wednesday night, negotiating with a smuggler in the dark, because of course the tide turned on Thursday. The sea kept office hours even during a war.

The cove opened below her like a cupped black hand. A single lantern burned low near the waterline, hooded, its light the color of old tea. Beside it sat a woman on an upturned crate, smoking a pipe with the unhurried air of somebody who had been waiting since before Coral was born and intended to go on waiting long after.

"Mrs. Tregose?" Coral called softly.

"You'll be the Marchmont girl." The woman didn't stand. She was small and wide as a barrel, wrapped in a man's coat several sizes too big, and her voice was gravel rolled in honey. "Or the Marchmont *driver*, I should say. Word gets muddled coming down that hill."

"How did you know—"

"Everyone knows something in this parish, dear. Nobody knows enough to be dangerous. That's what makes it livable." She gestured at the sand beside her with the pipe. "Sit. My knees have retired and my manners went with them."

Coral sat. The sand was cold through her skirt. Out on the black water there was no boat visible at all, which she chose to interpret as professionalism rather than absence.

"My contact said you could take a passenger across," Coral began. "A child. To Flushing. Quietly."

"I can take a child across." Mrs. Tregose drew on her pipe, and the ember lit the deep seams of her face. "I've taken men across, women across, a piano once, though I don't recommend pianos. Question is never whether I *can*. Question is always what."

"What?"

"What you're carrying, girl. What's worth crossing a war for." She turned her head and looked at Coral properly, and Coral felt the look go through her like a knitting needle through wool. "You've got a child hidden in a cart up that hill dressed as fish. You've not slept in two nights. You've come to me instead of to any of the three respectable people who might've helped you legally, which means legal isn't on offer. So." She knocked out her pipe against the crate. "Forty pounds."

Coral's stomach performed a slow, dignified collapse. "Forty — pounds?"

"Thirty for me. Ten for the boy who runs the boat, and he earns it; his engine is temperamental and so is he. Fuel, food, papers if we need them, bribes if we need those instead. War's put the price up on everything, including silence."

"I have eleven shillings," Coral said, "and a hairpin."

There was a pause.

"The hairpin's included in the eleven shillings," Coral added, with dignity.

Mrs. Tregose laughed then — a short, barking sound that startled gulls out of the cliff above them, or possibly the gulls were merely commuting. But the laugh died quickly, and her eyes stayed hard and appraising.

"Eleven shillings doesn't cross the Channel, love. Eleven shillings buys you a nice walk back up the hill and a very difficult conversation with yourself."

Coral looked out at the water. Somewhere beyond it was Holland: grey, flat, indifferent, safe. Somewhere behind her was Mara, asleep under fish boxes, humming German lullabies in her sleep without knowing she did it. And somewhere between those two facts was a sum of money Coral did not possess and would never possess, because she had spent nine years making herself a woman who owned nothing — nothing that could be traced, nothing that could be seized, nothing that anyone could take away. She had thought it was freedom. Sitting on cold sand in the dark, she understood it was only emptiness wearing a sensible coat.

Except.

"There's a car," Coral said.

Mrs. Tregose's pipe stopped halfway to her mouth.

"A Darracq. Twelve horsepower. Dark green. It belongs to—" Coral caught herself. "It's mine to dispose of. Registered to my employer, but she gave it into my keeping, and she'd sooner see it sold than see a child handed to the authorities. I can bring it to you tomorrow night. It'll fetch more than forty pounds even in wartime — especially in wartime. Petrol's worth its weight in spite these days."

"A motorcar." Mrs. Tregose said the word the way another woman might say *unicorn*. "You're offering me a motorcar for one crossing."

"One crossing, and your discretion afterward. Forever. Which is the part that costs extra everywhere else."

The old woman studied her for a long moment. The hooded lantern threw their shadows huge against the cliff wall, two shapes that looked like they'd been arguing there for centuries.

"It's a fair trade," Mrs. Tregose said at last, grudgingly, as though fairness offended her. "More than fair. Which makes me suspicious, because nobody trades fair who isn't desperate, and desperate people are unreliable." She leaned forward. "So let me ask you plain, girl, because I'm old and I haven't time for mysteries: whose child is it?"

Coral hesitated.

"Don't tell me the name," Mrs. Tregose said quickly, holding up a hand. "Names are burdens. Tell me the shape of it. That's all I need to know whether I'm ferrying a fugitive or a treasure."

Coral looked at the black water. When she spoke, her voice surprised herself by being steady.

"She's eight years old. Her father taught her the stars and how to mend watches. Her mother is dead. Her father told her to find a lady who reads letters, and she did — she found me — and now everyone with authority in this country wants to lock her in a camp because of the language she dreams in. She hasn't cried yet. Not once. She's saving it, I think, the way she saves everything, the way children do when they've learned that crying costs." Coral turned back. "That's the shape of it."

Mrs. Tregose was quiet for a while. The tide made its small patient sounds against the shingle.

"My husband ran this coast thirty years," she said finally. "French brandy, Dutch gin, the occasional priest. He always said the sea doesn't care what you're carrying, but the sea does keep accounts." She stood — slowly, in stages, like a drawbridge lowering — and looked down at Coral. "I'll take the car. And I'll take the child. And I'll tell you what I told the last fool who sat on this crate trying to buy something impossible: there's room for two in the boat, and the second place costs nothing but courage, and it's the one thing I can't sell you."

Coral's breath stopped.

"I hadn't asked about a second place," she managed.

"No. But you'd thought about it. It's written on you in letters a foot high." Mrs. Tregose picked up her lantern. "Here's how it stands. Tomorrow night, you bring me the car and the girl. If you get on the boat, you leave England knowing you may never lawfully come back, and you land in Holland with nothing but a child and whatever you're standing up in. If you stay, you stay knowing Quill's men will pull this coast apart by Friday, and you'll need a story better than the one you've got, and I wish you joy of it. Either way — decide tonight. I don't take wafflers to sea. Wafflers capsize boats."

* * *

Coral climbed back up the track with the lantern held low and her mind going around like a clock with a loose escapement.

Get on the boat. Stay off the boat. Get on. Stay.

The trouble was that every version of herself she consulted gave a different answer. The sensible Coral — the one who had survived her father's house by being careful, invisible, unremarkable — pointed out that a woman with no papers, no money past eleven shillings, and no claim to the child whatsoever could do Mara no good in Holland and considerable good in England, where she could testify, negotiate, lie beautifully on Mara's behalf. The sensible Coral had kept them both alive this far.

But the sensible Coral had also spent nine years watching life happen through other people's windows, and lately — since a small hand had pressed a broken watch into hers and said *I can fix it, if you like* — the windows had begun to feel less like views and more like walls.

At the cart, Mara was awake.

Coral knew it before she saw her — the sacking was breathing wrong, too quick, too shallow, the breathing of someone pretending. She lifted the corner of it. Two enormous dark eyes looked up at her from the straw.

"You heard," Coral said.

"Some." Mara sat up. In the thin moonlight her face was pale and grave, an old soul doing its best with a young body. "Is it true? About the boat?"

"It's true. Tomorrow night. If everything goes right."

Mara considered this with the terrifying thoroughness of children. "Will you come?"

Coral opened her mouth to deliver the answer she had prepared on the walk up — the sensible answer, the *I'll follow later, someone must stay with Edith* answer — and found that her prepared answers had all quietly deserted her.

"I don't know," she said honestly.

Mara nodded slowly, absorbing this. Then, in the matter-of-fact tone she used for important things: "Papa said someone would come. He said, *find the lady who reads letters, and she will know what to do.*" A pause. "He didn't say she'd know straightaway. He said knowing takes a minute sometimes."

"He was right about that."

"He was right about clocks too." Mara lay back down, pulled the sacking to her chin. From beneath it, her voice floated up, smaller: "Whatever you decide. I'd rather it was you deciding than anybody else. Even Papa."

Coral sat on the cart's edge for a long time after the child slept, turning that over like a stone with something glinting inside it.

* * *

They came down to the cove the next evening under a sky the color of pewter, the Darracq's headlamps shuttered with sacking, the engine ticking over at a whisper Coral had coaxed from it over years of patient maintenance. Mara rode inside the car itself, wedged among blankets, and Coral drove with her heart in her mouth and her hands perfectly steady, because that was the arrangement her body had made long ago: panic inside, stillness outside.

Mrs. Tregose was waiting at the waterline with a boy of perhaps nineteen who looked like his engine was also ticking over at a whisper. Three men from the village — paid, Coral guessed, in coin and fear — helped wrestle the car onto the smugglers' flat-bottomed lighter as the tide came in, grunting, swearing gently, treating the machine with the wary respect of men handling a sleeping animal.

"She's beautiful," Mrs. Tregose admitted, circling it once on deck. "I'll get a good price. There's a farmer up-country who thinks petrol is a religion." She spat ceremonially over the side. "Right. Money's settled. Now the other business."

She looked at Coral. Coral looked at the boat. The boat looked grey and small and very far from England.

"Miss Villalobos," Mrs. Tregose said, "you've got until I count ten."

And here was the strange thing, the thing Coral would remember afterward whenever she tried to reconstruct exactly who she had been before that night: she did not deliberate. All day the question had turned and turned, sensible arguments wheeling overhead like gulls, and now, standing on wet shingle with salt wind pushing at her back, every argument simply — landed. Settled. Folded itself away.

Because she had learned her worth in the strangest school there was: the school of subtraction. Take away the letters she read, the secrets she carried, the invisibility she'd worn like armor — take away the car, the wages, the careful little life — and what remained was a woman standing on a beach who had exactly one thing of value left to trade, and it was not for sale at any price, and it was currently asleep in a motorcar, and it trusted her.

"I'm coming," Coral said.

Mrs. Tregose raised her eyebrows. "Counted faster than ten."

"I've been counting for two days. The number was always the same. I just didn't want to hear it." She turned to the boy. "How much fuel do you carry, and does the engine flood in swell?"

The boy blinked. "How d'you know engines?"

"I drive a Darracq through Cornish lanes in all weathers. I know engines the way you know your own bad temper."

He grinned despite himself. Mrs. Tregose snorted. "Lord preserve us. A chauffeur and a clockmaker's daughter. We'll be lucky to drown."

"We won't drown," Mara said, appearing at Coral's elbow, blanket-caped, entirely serious. "I checked the sky. Papa says when the stars sit low and quiet like that, the water behaves. Mostly."

"*Mostly,*" repeated Mrs. Tregose, to the heavens, in the tone of a woman filing a complaint in advance.

They pushed off as the last light went. The cove fell away behind them — the black cliff, the hooded lantern, the whole green secret country of Cornwall shrinking to a smudge and then a memory. Coral kept her arm around Mara's shoulders and felt the small warm weight of her, solid and real, and understood that she had crossed some line that maps didn't mark: behind her was everything she had been, ahead of her only what she had chosen.

Then the boy started the engine, and the engine coughed, caught, coughed again — and out of the fog bank to the south, where no fog bank should sensibly be at that hour, came the low, purposeful thrum of a different engine altogether.

Mrs. Tregose's head snapped toward the sound. Her face, in the lantern light, went carefully blank — the blankness of a woman doing rapid arithmetic with her life.

"That," she said quietly, "is either a fishing boat running late... or it is very much not. Somebody get that light out. *Now.*"
