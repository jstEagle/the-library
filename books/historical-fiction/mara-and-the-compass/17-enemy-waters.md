## 17. Enemy Waters

**Coral**

The fog came off the Channel like a held breath, and Mrs. Tregose's boat slipped into it the way a secret slips into a letter — quietly, and with no intention of ever coming back out.

Her name was *Perseverance*, which struck me as either a joke or a prayer painted on her stern by someone who'd had time for both. She was forty feet of salt-swollen timber that smelled of herring, tar, and the particular stubbornness of a woman who had run contraband through two wars already and saw no reason why a third should interrupt her retirement.

"Sit there," Mrs. Tregose said, pointing at Mara with a chin like a dock bollard. "And you—" she pointed at me, "—sit anywhere you like, so long as it's not near my engine, my wheel, or my opinions."

I sat.

The cove fell away behind us in about ninety seconds flat. One moment there were black rocks and blacker pines and the small orange eye of Mrs. Tregose's cottage lamp; the next there was nothing but grey water and grey air and the sound of our own engine working harder than any machine should have to before breakfast.

Mara sat very straight on the coil of rope Mrs. Tregose had assigned her, hands folded in her lap like a child at church, eyes enormous. She was wearing Edith's old oilskin coat, which reached past her knees and made her look like a small lighthouse that had given up.

"Coral," she said, in the careful voice she used when she'd been rehearsing a sentence in her head. "Are we going to die?"

"Not tonight," I said.

"That's what Papa said," she said. "About London."

The engine coughed. I decided not to pursue the theology of reassurance any further.

---

We had been under way perhaps an hour when I heard it: a low, patient thudding under the water-noise, rhythmic as a heartbeat that wasn't ours. Mrs. Tregose heard it too. I knew because she stopped swearing at her engine mid-swear, which I gathered from her face was unprecedented since the Armada.

She killed the lamp. She cut the engine to a whisper. And then she did something I will remember until I die: she smiled.

"Patrol boat," she said pleasantly, "coming to have a look at us. Now then, missus. You wanted enemy waters." She nodded at the fog. "There they are."

---

**Mara**

The big boat came out of the fog like a shadow having ideas.

It was long and dark and it had a gun on the front of it, which surprised me because I had thought guns would look more exciting. This one just looked like a pipe. Papa always said that most frightening things are disappointing up close, and then they are not disappointing anymore at all, and I understood him suddenly and completely.

Mrs. Tregose pushed us both down behind the wheel-house and put a tarpaulin over us, which smelled of fish and old rain. Through a gap between two boards I could see the searchlight sweeping the fog in slow circles, like a lighthouse looking for something to apologize to.

My heart went very fast. I counted to keep it company. Papa taught me counting. He said numbers are the only language that never lies to you, and if you can count, you can build anything — a clock, an escape, a new life. *One gear at a time, Liebchen,* he said. *Nobody ever built a watch all at once.*

So I counted the sweeps of the light. Fourteen. Fifteen. On sixteen it stopped, and hung on us, and I stopped breathing entirely, and somewhere under the tarpaulin Coral's hand found mine and held it so hard I nearly forgot to be frightened, because nobody had ever held my hand like I was the thing worth holding onto.

A voice came across the water. It sounded bored. It said something in English about fishing vessels and registration numbers.

Mrs. Tregose shouted back something in English that I didn't understand all of, but which included the words *herring*, *thirty years*, and *your own grandmother*, delivered in a tone that made the bored voice go away.

The light moved off. The shadow-boat grumbled away into the fog like a dog called back indoors.

Seventeen. Eighteen. I kept counting anyway. It seemed polite, somehow. To finish.

---

**Coral**

When the patrol boat's engine faded to a rumor, Mrs. Tregose pulled the tarpaulin off us and looked down at Mara with an expression I can only describe as professional approval.

"You didn't scream," she said. "That puts you ahead of most adults I've smuggled."

"She was counting," I said.

"Counting." Mrs. Tregose snorted and went back to her engine, muttering something that might have been *the young these days* or might have been a navigation instruction; with her it was impossible to say.

But I looked at Mara — this small person who had crossed half of England on railway platforms and in cart-bottoms, whose mother hadn't made it out of London, who had watched soldiers with bayonets from behind a hedge not six hours ago — and she was *counting*. Calmly. Like mending a watch. One gear at a time.

Aldous Brandt, wherever he was — interned on the Isle of Man, or dead, though I refused yet to believe it — had built his daughter out of the same materials he built his clocks: patience, precision, and parts that would outlast their maker.

I found I was gripping the gunwale rather hard.

*Keep my promise.* The words from inside the watch case, scratched tiny as spider silk. Coordinates. A name in Holland. I had read them forty times since finding them, and each time they rearranged themselves slightly in my understanding, the way a sentence does when you finally stop skimming and actually read it.

He had known. Months ago, before the war, before the internment — he had known the world might come apart, and he had hidden a map inside a watch and put it on his daughter's wrist, and told her to find *the lady who reads letters.*

Well. Here I was. Reading letters. Delivering one, in a sense, across an entire sea.

"Liebling," I said — the endearment slipping out of me in German before I could catch it; I'd caught it from him, years ago, in his workshop above the harbor — "come here a moment."

Mara came. Under the tarpaulin smell she still smelled faintly of clock oil. Some things soak in and stay.

"When we get to Holland," I began, "there may be —"

The engine died.

Not sputtered. Not hesitated. Died, the way a man dies in a story: completely, and at the worst possible moment, and with everyone pretending afterward that they'd seen it coming.

---

**Mara**

The silence after the engine stopped was the biggest silence I have ever been inside. Bigger than the church silence. Bigger than the silence in the house in London after Mama's letter came and before anyone told me what was in it.

Mrs. Tregose said a word I wrote down later in my notebook so I could ask Coral about it, and Coral said I should absolutely never ask her about it.

Then Mrs. Tregose opened the engine hatch and looked inside, and made sounds like a kettle giving up.

"Fouled," she announced, to the sea generally. "Fuel line's choked with half of Falmouth Harbor. We're adrift, ladies. Adrift in a shipping lane, in a fog, in wartime, with the tide setting us east toward France, which is currently the wrong country to drift toward by approximately everything."

I climbed up to look in the hatch, because Papa always let me look in the hatch. Inside the engine was a mess of pipes and wires and a flywheel, and it was dirty, and dirty is a problem, and problems are only puzzles that haven't been introduced to you properly yet.

"There," I said, and pointed.

Both women looked where I pointed. Then they looked at me.

"The fuel line goes through there and there's a bend and the bend has got grit in it. You can see the fuel isn't moving. Papa's workshop had an engine like this but smaller, for the polishing wheel. When it stopped, he blew through the pipe." I paused, because honesty matters even at sea. "You have to blow hard. And sometimes you get petrol in your mouth. He always did it himself. He said it builds character."

Mrs. Tregose stared at me for a long moment. Then she took off her cap, ran a hand through hair like steel wool, and said to Coral:

"Where," she demanded, "did you *get* her?"

"Bristol," I said, before Coral could lie badly. "I'm her niece. From Bristol."

Something happened in Coral's face — a whole storm and a whole sunrise in the space of a second — and then Mrs. Tregose laughed, a great rusty anchor-chain of a laugh, and blew through the pipe herself, and swore again, and spat, and blew again, and on the fourth try the engine caught with a bang like a door slamming in heaven.

*Perseverance* shivered, and woke, and went on.

---

**Coral**

Fog, I learned that night, is not empty. It is full — of water, of cold, of sound traveling wrong distances, and of other boats whose intentions you must guess from engines alone, the way you once guessed a correspondent's mood from handwriting alone. I had spent years reading what people sealed away from the world. Now I read fog.

Twice more that night engines passed near us, and twice more we drowned our own light and waited, Mara under the tarpaulin with her counting, me above it doing arithmetic of a different kind: how much fuel, how many hours, how far to neutral water, how many minutes of courage remained in my personal supply. The last figure fluctuated. I topped it up by watching a nine-year-old girl hold a compass steady while a grown smuggler swore at the sky.

Somewhere past three in the morning the fog began to tear.

It tore in rags first, and through the rags came stars — sudden, outrageous, indifferent stars, scattered over the black water like spilled salt. And Mara went absolutely still.

"Mara?" I whispered.

"Look," she said.

I looked. There was nothing there but sea and sky and the small stubborn light of our lamp. And then I followed the line of her finger and understood that she wasn't pointing at any one star. She was pointing at all of them, arranged, ordered — a mechanism. A clock face the size of everything.

"Papa taught me," she said softly. "Before. In the summer, on the roof of the shop. He said sailors used to steer by them, before compasses, and that a clock is only a sky you can hold in your hand. He said—" her voice wobbled and steadied itself, one gear catching another, "*he said the stars don't take sides. They shine on English boats and German boats exactly the same. That's why I like them better than people.*"

Neither of us said anything for a while. The engine thumped its faithful heart. Somewhere behind us lay a country that had smashed the windows of a baker who'd lived among them twenty years; somewhere ahead lay a country that was merely neutral, which after the week we'd had sounded like paradise.

"Which one is north?" I asked.

She raised her arm without hesitation, the way her father must have raised his a thousand times over a workbench, and pointed past the W of Cassiopeia, and she was right — I checked against the compass, because I am who I am, and I check everything — and she was right to the degree.

"Well," said Mrs. Tregose, from the wheel, without turning around. "If the Germans send their children over as spies, they've wasted a spy on us. Hand me that star chart, missus. Let's see if your father's sky agrees with my paper."

It agreed. Of course it agreed. It was Aldous Brandt's sky; it agreed with Aldous Brandt's daughter down to the last tooth of every gear.

---

**Mara**

After that we steered by stars and by Mrs. Tregose's chart and by Coral checking both against each other, which Mrs. Tregose said was redundant and Coral said was thorough, and which I thought was love, though I didn't have the word yet. I have it now.

Once, when the fog closed again and there was nothing to do but sit and be cold together, Coral put her coat around me and asked, very casually, the way grown-ups ask questions they've been carrying for miles:

"What else did your father teach you? On the roof. Besides the stars."

I thought about it properly. He taught me so many things that it was like being asked which drops of rain made the puddle.

"He taught me that if a spring breaks, you don't throw the watch away. You make a new spring. It takes longer, but the watch remembers being good." I turned the repaired watch on my wrist — *his* repair, done the night we met, the night I knocked on the Marchmonts' door with the note in my pocket that said *forget this face.* "He taught me that people who mend things for strangers are the best people, even if the strangers never learn their names."

Coral was quiet.

"And he taught me a song," I added, because her face had gone strange and soft and I wanted to give her something easier to carry. "Do you want the song?"

"I know the song," Coral said.

I looked up at her. Fog. Stars through fog. Her eyes shining with something she would deny later, being who she was.

"How do you know Papa's song?"

"Because," Coral said, and pulled me close against her side, and looked out at the black water as if the answer were written there in letters only she could read, "a long time ago, when I needed somebody to mend me, your father did that too."

---

**Coral**

Dawn came up the color of dishwater, and with it, land.

Low and grey and utterly unglamorous, the Dutch coast slid out of the mist like a rumor confirming itself — dunes, windmills turning their slow arms against the pale sky, and gulls screaming the welcome that no official would ever give us.

"There," Mrs. Tregose said, and her voice had gone quieter than I'd ever heard it, almost tender, the way it probably sounded in 1915 running brandy past His Majesty's revenue men. "Flushing roads. Neutral water. Congratulations, ladies — you are now, officially, nobody's enemy."

Mara stood at the bow with the wind tearing her hair sideways and her father's coat flapping around her knees, and she laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the first I'd heard from her since the cellar — and I felt something in my chest come loose that had been wound tight since the night of the knock.

We were almost through. Almost safe. Almost finished keeping a dead-or-living man's promise.

Which is, I now understand, precisely the moment at which one should become suspicious.

Because as the dunes grew nearer, and Mrs. Tregose eased back on the throttle, and I allowed myself — foolishly, forgivably — one long exhale of relief, Mara went stiff against my side.

"Coral," she said. "Who lives in that house?"

On the beach ahead, just above the tide line, stood a low grey house with a red tile roof — and beside its door, mounted on a post, swinging gently in the sea wind, hung a sign. Painted on it, in neat Dutch lettering beneath a symbol I recognized from a hundred shopfronts in Cornwall:

A clock face.

And below the clock face, in smaller letters, a name.

Mara read it aloud, her voice very small, as the keel whispered against the shallows.

"That's Papa's name," she said. "But Papa is in England. Papa is on the Isle of Man."

The boat ground gently onto the sand.

The door of the clockmaker's house opened.
