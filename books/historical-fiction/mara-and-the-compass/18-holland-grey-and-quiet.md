## 18. Holland, Grey and Quiet

The sea gave us up at dawn, and Holland received us the way a tired aunt receives unexpected guests — without complaint, and with a certain grim efficiency about towels.

Mrs. Tregose put us ashore in a mudflat creek a mile east of Flushing, where the water lay flat as pewter and the sky was the colour of dishwater left too long. She would not take us nearer. "Patrols come thick past the harbour," she said, hauling her dinghy up on the grey sand with the ease of a woman who had been doing unlawful things to coastlines for fifty years. "You'll walk. Keep off the road till you see the windmills, then look like what you are."

"And what am I?" I asked.

"A woman walking with her daughter," she said, "who has never in her life done anything interesting." She looked at Mara, who stood ankle-deep in the shallows staring at this new country as though it were a clock she intended to open. Then she looked at me, and something almost like kindness crossed her face before she could stop it. "You did all right, miss. For a landlubber."

From Mrs. Tregose, I understood, this was roughly the equivalent of a knighthood.

She was gone before the light properly came, and then it was only the two of us and the gulls and a country that smelled of wet earth and woodsmoke and — improbably, after everything — frying onions. My legs shook so badly on the sea wall that I had to sit down, and Mara sat down beside me without being asked, close enough that our shoulders touched.

"Are we here?" she asked.

"We're here," I said. Whether *here* meant Holland or safety or the end of anything, I couldn't have said, and I suspected she knew it. Children always know when an adult's answer is wearing its Sunday clothes over nothing.

We walked. The dykes ran straight as ruled lines, the cows regarded us with the profound indifference of animals who have seen refugees before and found them unremarkable, and by mid-morning we reached a lane of low brick houses with scrubbed steps and lace curtains so clean they seemed to be showing off. Somewhere a church bell counted eleven with the unhurried confidence of a country not at war.

I had the address folded inside my glove, where it had lived since the watch told it to me: *Mevrouw G. Brandt-van der Meer, Noordstraat 14, Vlissingen.* Fourteen. An even, modest number. A number for ordinary lives.

My heart was doing things no respectable heart should do. Because I had rehearsed this moment on the boat, in the fog, in the dark hours when Mara slept against my coat — rehearsed Aldous opening his own door, thinner perhaps, greyer, but with those quick clockmaker's hands and the voice that said *Coral* as though my name were a small mechanism he was pleased to find working. Rehearsed him taking Mara up into his arms. Rehearsed saying, *You kept your promise, and I kept mine, and now everyone is accounted for.*

I had not rehearsed the possibility that knocked on the door instead.

* * *

A woman answered Noordstraat 14 before I'd finished knocking — a woman of about sixty with Aldous Brandt's exact eyebrows, which was how I knew her before she said a word. Those eyebrows, raised in enquiry, made her whole face a question mark.

"Mevrouw Brandt-van der Meer?" My Dutch extended to approximately four words, none of them useful. "I'm looking for — Herr Aldous Brandt. He is your brother?"

Something happened to her face. I watched it happen, the way one watches weather change across a field: the question mark folding itself closed, the colour going out from under the skin, the hand rising slowly to the doorframe as though the house had begun to move beneath her.

"You have news," she said, in English accented but fluent. "Of Aldous."

"I — yes. I've brought—" My voice failed me, and I put my hand on Mara's shoulder instead, drawing her forward. "I've brought his daughter."

For a moment the woman looked at Mara the way people look at a miracle they haven't yet decided to believe in. And then she looked back at me, and whatever she saw in my face — some last unguarded scrap of hope, still asking its foolish question — she answered it.

"He died," she said gently. "In March. On the island. They wrote to me in April." Her hand found mine without ceremony, the way hands do in countries where grief is permitted to be practical. "You did not know. Oh — *meisje*, you did not know."

I want to report that I bore it well. That I stood straight and thanked her and conducted myself with the composure I had been practising all my life behind other people's letters. What actually happened is that the world tilted slightly, and Mevrouw van der Meer pulled us both inside, and I sat down very hard on a chair in her front room while a kettle was put on somewhere and Mara's small cold hand found mine and gripped it.

Died in March. While we were hiding in the cellar among confiscated pamphlets. While Mara was mending my watch by candlelight with his own patient fingers copied into hers. While I crossed half of Cornwall believing I was carrying a child toward her father — carrying, in fact, a letter to a man who had already stopped being able to read it.

All those miles, and he had been dead before we ever set out.

* * *

The room was full of clocks. Of course it was. Mantel clocks and bracket clocks and one tall case standing in the corner like a chaperone, and every single one ticking at its own pace, so that the silence between words was never really silent. It should have been unbearable. Instead it was oddly comforting — a room that refused to let time sit still, even now.

Mevrouw Geertruida van der Meer — she would not hear of formality, and besides, grief flattens etiquette wonderfully — set tea before us in cups so thin the light came through them, and told us everything in the order she had evidently told it to herself many times already, smoothing each fact flat as she went.

He had been taken in November, in the first round-up of German-born men along the south coast. Knockwood, they called the camp on the Isle of Man. His heart, she said, had been a bad clock for years — he'd known it himself, could hear the irregularity better than any doctor, because of course he could. The damp and the worry had done the rest.

"The last letter came in February." She rose, went to a bureau, and returned with a bundle tied in ribbon — actual ribbon, blue, the kind used on hatboxes — and untied it with fingers that trembled only slightly. "He wrote to me every week. Even when there was nothing to say. Especially then." She smiled, and it broke my heart, because it was his smile, wry and sideways. "Our family does not know how to leave a mechanism alone. We must keep everything running."

And then she paused, with the bundle in her lap, and looked at Mara for a long moment.

"You are Lotte's girl," she said softly.

Mara nodded. It was the first time anyone had said her mother's name aloud in my hearing, and I felt the child go still beside me, the way a watch goes still just before it strikes.

"She got you out," Geertruida said. "Aldous wrote me that much, and no more, because he was careful — he was always careful on paper. But I know my brother, and I know what he would have arranged. There were funds. There were instructions." Her eyes came to mine. "Instructions for whoever brought her. Which appears to be you."

"Me," I said, rather stupidly.

"You," she agreed, with a small dry note that Aldous would have recognised instantly. "He said — I will read it exactly, because he made me promise to read it exactly, and our family keeps promises the way we keep time, which is to say obsessively—"

"*Geertruida,*" I said, and heard Edith in my own voice so clearly that I nearly laughed, even then.

She laughed instead — a short, wet-edged laugh — and unfolded the paper.

* * *

It was dated the 9th of January, written on camp paper with a pencil worn nearly to nothing, and I have it by heart now, though Geertruida read it aloud and I only listened:

*Sister — if a person arrives at your door with my daughter, that person will be a stranger to you and the most important person in England. Do not ask what she risked; she will not tell you, because she is the sort who doesn't. Pay her what is in the envelope. Feed her first — she will claim she isn't hungry, and she will be lying. Tell my daughter that the watch was never broken, only resting, and that I am proud of her beyond the reach of language. Tell the person — the driver — that she once sat in my shop at fourteen years old with a split lip and would not say whose fault it was, and I knew, and I never said either, and if she has carried my girl across a war to my sister's door then the debt between us stands settled, and more than settled, and I hope she will allow an old man the liberty of calling it friendship instead.*

There was more — practical matters, the envelope, the name of a solicitor in Amsterdam — but I confess I lost the thread of it, because I had put my hand over my mouth like a girl at a funeral, which in a sense it was, and Mara had put both arms around my waist and her face into my coat, and Geertruida had stopped reading and simply sat with us among all those ticking clocks while the tea went cold and nobody minded.

The envelope, when she finally fetched it, contained a sum that made me laugh out loud — an ugly, startled sound — because it was roughly forty times what the journey had cost, including Mrs. Tregose's extortionate fee and the indignity of selling Edith's motorcar to a Falmouth fish merchant who had driven a harder bargain than any smuggler alive.

"He overestimated," I managed. "The costs."

"He estimated," Geertruida said, "that whoever brought her would refuse payment. So he made the sum embarrassing. He said shame is more persuasive than arithmetic." She folded her hands. "Was he right?"

I thought of arguing. I thought of the speech I might make — that I hadn't done it for money, that one doesn't, that the whole point of the thing was that it wasn't a transaction. And then I thought of Edith's empty garage, and of the winter coming, and of the fact that pride is a luxury item and I had been living without luxuries for thirty years.

"He was right," I said. "He was always right about mechanisms."

* * *

We stayed three days. Geertruida would not hear of less, and I discovered that resistance was useless — she fed us with the relentless strategy of a woman who had survived two wars' worth of anxiety by refusing to let anyone near her go hungry. Mara slept twelve hours the first night, woke, ate an entire loaf with cheese, and slept again. Grief, it turns out, is hungry work, and children pay its bills promptly.

On the second evening, Mara and I walked to the sea wall above the Westerschelde, where the great ships went up and down toward Antwerp, and the wind came off the water smelling of salt and tar and distance.

"My father knew you'd come," she said. Not a question.

"He hoped," I said. "Hoping and knowing are neighbours, but they don't share a house."

She considered this with the gravity she gave to all statements about mechanisms, literal or otherwise. "He said someone would come who reads letters." A pause. "How did he know that? About you?"

Because I had told him, once, at fourteen, with a split lip and a stubbornness I can still feel in my jaw — told him I wanted to be invisible, and he had said something I didn't understand until this year. I told her as much of it as a child should hear, which was most of it, and she listened the way she listened to gears: completely.

Then she reached into her pocket and produced my watch — *his* watch, the one he'd repaired in our kitchen by candlelight — and held it out on her open palm.

"He fixed it for you," she said. "Before. Before London. He said, 'If ever you meet the lady who reads letters, give her this, and tell her time is a debt we pay forward.'" She frowned slightly. "That's why I mended it again when it broke. It wasn't really mine to let stay broken."

I took it. It ticked against my palm, steady and insolent and alive, and I understood that I had been carrying a message for months without knowing it, the way one carries a seed in a coat pocket through a whole winter.

"Well then," I said, when I could speak. "We'd better not waste it."

* * *

But it was on the third morning, packing to leave, that the true hook of the thing revealed itself. Geertruida met me at the door with the ribboned bundle of letters and pressed it into my hands.

"He wrote every week," she said. "To me. To Lotte, when she lived. To friends scattered from Hamburg to Hull. Someone should have them — someone who reads." Her eyebrows, Aldous's eyebrows, arched in that familiar question. "And there is this also. The last letter arrived six days ago. From the camp. Written in March, held up by the censors, God knows why." Her voice dropped. "Coral — it is addressed to you."

The world went very quiet, except for the clocks.

"To me," I repeated.

"It says so on the face of it. *Miss C. Villalobos.*" She watched me with kind, sharp eyes. "He wrote it in March. The month he died." She put the thin envelope in my hands and closed my fingers over it. "I could not bring myself to open another man's post. But you — I think you are the exception our family has been waiting for."

I stood on the scrubbed step of Noordstraat 14 with an envelope in my hands and a dead man's handwriting on its face, and behind me Mara was saying goodbye to a great-aunt she'd known three days and would apparently love forever, and ahead of me was the sea, and Cornwall, and Edith, and Sergeant Quill, and whatever England had decided to do with the women who disobeyed it.

The letter weighed almost nothing.

I put it in my breast pocket, over my heart, unopened — because some things deserve a quiet room and a steady hand, and I meant to give it both.

"Hold my hand at the rail," Mara said, appearing at my elbow, solemn as a Sunday. "The crossing was terrible last time."

"It was," I agreed. "This time we'll be terrible together."

And we went down to the water.
