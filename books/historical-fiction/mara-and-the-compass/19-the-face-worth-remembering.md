## 19. The Face Worth Remembering

The aunt's house in Flushing smelled of beeswax and black tea and something faintly of metal — clock oil, Mara thought, though she hadn't smelled it since the workshop in London, since before. It was a good smell. It was a smell that meant someone, somewhere, kept things running.

Tante Liesel had cried when she opened the door. She had cried looking at Mara, then cried harder looking at Coral, as if Coral were a letter that had finally been delivered after years in the post. She had fed them both thick bread with butter and sugar on it, which Mara thought was a strange thing to do to bread but ate three slices of anyway, because her body had apparently decided it was finished being brave about food.

And now Tante Liesel was talking quietly with Coral by the stove, and Mara could hear only pieces of it — *Aldous*, and *the winter*, and *the fund he left* — and she understood, in the way you understand a thing your whole body knows before your mind agrees to it, that Papa was not coming to Holland later. Papa was not waiting somewhere across the water with his sleeves rolled up and his spectacles pushed into his hair. Papa had died in the winter, on an island full of gray sea, and no one had told her because no one had known where she was either.

She sat very still on the little bench by the window and held her cup of tea and did not cry. Crying was for later. Crying was a thing you scheduled, like winding a clock. Papa had taught her that much without meaning to: grief was a mechanism, it needed regular attention or it seized up entirely, but you didn't stop everything for it in the middle of the day.

The middle of the day, however, was ending. The light through the lace curtains had gone the color of weak tea itself, and Coral was putting on her coat.

Mara watched her do it. Watched the buttons go through their holes one by one, watched Coral tuck her collar down the way she always did, quick and neat, like a woman who had spent her life making herself small and tidy so nobody would look twice. Coral Villalobos was very good at not being looked at. Mara had spent eleven days in her company and had learned this the way she learned anything — by watching the gears.

"I'll be back on Thursday's packet boat," Coral was saying to Tante Liesel. "There's money enough here for the year, you said? And the school is walking distance?"

"Walking distance, and the schoolmistress owes me cards," Tante Liesel said. "She will spoil her."

"Good." Coral smiled, and it was a real smile, tired around the edges but real. "That's — good. That's what he wanted."

That was when Mara stood up.

Her legs felt strange, like they belonged to someone taller. The floorboards were warm near the stove. She crossed the room and put herself directly between Coral and the door, which was, she understood, a rude thing to do in Holland, in England, probably everywhere, and she did it anyway.

"You're leaving."

Coral looked down at her. In the last week and a half Mara had learned all of Coral's faces, and this one — this was the face she wore when she was about to say something kind that wasn't true. "I have to get home, love. There's Edith, and Sergeant Quill, and — well. A great many things I left in the lurch, if I'm honest. Lurking about in other people's post, chiefly."

"The note said to forget my face."

Coral went still. Outside, a barge bell rang on the Scheldt, slow and bronze, counting out the evening.

"That was his instruction," Coral said carefully. "To whoever took you in."

"But it was you who took me in." Mara's voice came out smaller than she wanted. She made it bigger. "You read letters even when they're not yours. I know. I saw you. On the train, and at the inn, you read everybody's letters, you can't help it, you hold them up to the window light first." She heard herself and flushed, because that sounded like an accusation, and it wasn't. It was the opposite of an accusation. It was the truest thing she knew about Coral Villalobos, and she had decided it was the best thing too. "So you read Papa's note. And it said forget this face. And you've decided to obey it."

"Mara—"

"He wrote it so people would be *safe*," Mara said. Her eyes were doing something hot and embarrassing and she blinked it back down. "He wrote it so nobody would be caught with me. He wrote it before he knew — before—" Her breath hitched. Before he knew he would die. Before anyone knew. The note had been written by a man who expected to be alive to be forgotten properly, and somehow that was the worst part, worse than the island, worse than the gray sea, that Papa had spent his last months arranging a forgetting that had failed completely, because here she was, standing in front of the one person in two countries who could not forget anything, least of all a face.

"You don't have to stay forever," Mara said. "I'm not stupid. You have a life. You have Miss Marchmont, and the car — you sold the car, Tante Liesel told me, you sold the car for me — and you have your letters." She swallowed. "But you can't leave tonight. Tonight you have to stay until I fall asleep. That's all I'm asking. That's a very small thing."

"It isn't small," Coral said, and her voice cracked right down the middle of it.

"No," Mara agreed. "It's the biggest thing there is. But it fits in one night."

* * *

They went up the narrow stairs together, Tante Liesel carrying a candle and pretending very hard not to listen, which was a Dutch skill, Mara suspected, or maybe just an aunt skill. The bedroom was small and clean, with a bed under the sloping eaves and a washstand and one picture on the wall: a windmill, naturally, painted by somebody who had never gotten over windmills and didn't intend to.

Mara got into the bed in her shift and lay stiff as a plank, staring at the beams.

Coral stood awkwardly by the door with the candle. "Shall I — do you want the candle or shall I—"

"Sit down."

"There's no chair."

"Sit on the bed then. At the end. Near my feet. Papa used to sit there and tell me about the watches while I fell asleep." Her throat closed. "You don't have to talk about watches. You don't know anything about watches."

"Not a single thing," Coral agreed. "I once called a mainspring a *main spring*, like water."

Despite everything, despite the hot lump behind her ribs, Mara laughed. It escaped before she could schedule it. "That's terrible."

"I know. He corrected me for a quarter of an hour. I understood perhaps four words of it." Coral sat down carefully at the foot of the bed, and the mattress dipped, and Mara felt the warmth of another person through the blanket, close but not touching, the exact distance Papa used to keep. Something in her chest unclenched by one notch, like a ratchet letting go.

For a while neither of them spoke. Downstairs Tante Liesel was washing dishes; Mara could hear the cups clinking, and the barge bell again, farther off now, and the wind off the North Sea worrying at the shutters the same way it worried at the ones in Cornwall, as if the sea were one long house with the same drafty windows throughout.

"Coral?"

"Mm."

"Take this." Mara reached under her pillow and brought out the watch.

Papa's watch. The one with the cracked crystal and the stubborn balance wheel, the one that had stopped in Cornwall and started again under her fingers on Coral's kitchen table, the night Coral had watched her work with her mouth slightly open, and Mara had known — known the way you know a key fits a lock before you turn it — that this woman was the one Papa meant. *Trust the lady who reads letters.* She'd carried those words from London like a stone in her shoe.

Now she pressed the watch into Coral's hand.

"This is yours," Coral said. "It was his. He'd want—"

"It's yours." Mara said it firmly, the way she said *no* to boiled onions. "It's the second time I'm giving it to you. First time was an accident, sort of, it just ended up in your pocket when I was scared. This time I mean it." She pulled the blanket up to her chin. "Papa put things inside it. Inside the case. You found them, didn't you? The address, and everything."

"Yes."

"He taught me to hide things in watches. He said the best hiding place is inside something everyone believes is already open. Everyone thinks they know what a watch is for, so they never ask what else it's for." She yawned, enormous and involuntary, the way exhaustion ambushes children at exactly the wrong moment. "You're like that too. Everyone thinks they know what you're for. Driving. Carrying. Going where you're pointed." Her eyelids were heavy and she fought them, because there was one more thing, the most important thing, and she would say it even if she fell asleep mid-word. "But you're for keeping. Things. People. Promises. You kept Papa's promise before you even knew whose it was."

"Mara—"

"And the note was wrong." Her eyes were nearly closed now. She said it into the pillow, muffled and fierce. "Forget this face. Well. You haven't forgotten it. I watched you not forget it for eleven days. So the note was wrong, and Papa was wrong, and I'm going to remember your face until I'm a hundred years old, and when I'm a hundred years old I'll tell my grandchildren about the lady who read letters and drove a motorcar and sold it for me and sat on the end of my bed in Holland, and they won't believe me, because nobody would believe anyone could be that—"

She was asleep. Mid-word, exactly as she'd feared. Her hand had found the edge of Coral's sleeve somewhere in the last sentence and stayed there, curled around the cuff like a small anchor.

* * *

Coral did not move for a long time.

The candle burned low. Below, Tante Liesel's dishes went quiet; the house settled into its nighttime creaks, and the wind kept on at the shutters, patient as tide. Coral sat at the foot of a child's bed in a foreign country with a dead man's watch in her hand and let herself, finally, look at the sleeping face.

*Forget this face.*

She had obeyed plenty of instructions in her life. Obeying was her native language, her mother tongue: sit straight, drive slowly, don't be seen, don't be heard, deliver and withdraw. Aldous Brandt's note had fit her like a glove sewn to her own measurements — forget this face, vanish, return to invisibility — and she had planned to obey it. Had packed her bag for it. Was, at this moment, wearing her coat for it.

But the face was right there. The face was asleep with its mouth slightly open and one fist knotted in Coral's sleeve, and it had a smudge of machine grease behind its left ear that three days of travel had failed to remove, and its eyebrows drew together in sleep exactly the way Aldous Brandt's had whenever a customer tried to haggle. Forget this face. As if a face could be forgotten after it had hummed a German lullaby badly in your kitchen. After it had repaired your watch with hands steadier than any adult's. After it had stood between you and a door and demanded, with the whole unreasonable authority of childhood, that you stay until it slept.

Coral thought about her father, whom she remembered perfectly and wished she didn't. Memory was supposed to be a wound, in her experience. Nobody had ever told her memory could be a keepsake — that you could carry a person around inside you the way Aldous carried messages inside watch cases, tucked into the machinery, safe precisely because no one thought to look there.

"Well," she whispered to the dark, to the child, to the man on the gray island who had saved her once and asked nothing and lost everything. "I'm afraid I must decline."

Disobedience, she discovered, felt remarkably like relief. All those years of careful invisibility, and it turned out the cure had been a child refusing to let her leave a room.

Very gently, she unwound the small fist from her sleeve and replaced it with the watch — Papa's watch, hers now, apparently, by decree of the owner — folding the child's fingers around it so Mara would find it in the morning and understand it had been given, not taken back. Then she eased herself off the bed, joint by joint, a burglar in reverse, stealing nothing but taking everything.

At the door she paused. One more look. Not to memorize the face — she hadn't needed to memorize it since the first night, storm-lit and terrified on the Marchmont doorstep. Just to look. That was allowed now. That was the whole point.

Downstairs, Tante Liesel was waiting in the kitchen with two glasses of something amber and an expression that said she had heard every word through the floorboards and intended to pretend otherwise.

"She will want you at breakfast," Tante Liesel said. "And at lunch. And she will write to you every week, and you will answer, or I will hear about it, and I am a great complainer."

"The packet boat leaves Thursday," Coral said. It came out as a question.

"Thursday," Tante Liesel agreed, and slid the glass toward her. "Tonight, you drink, and tomorrow we see about the morning boat instead. War or no war, some things are improved by staying one extra day."

Coral lifted the glass. Through the little window over the sink she could see the harbor lights of Flushing strung along the black water, and beyond them, somewhere east, a continent on fire, and beyond that, west, a cottage in Cornwall where a woman named Edith was holding the line alone against policemen and gossip and the entire British Empire's talent for suspicion.

One extra day. Then home — to whatever waited there.

She drank, and upstairs, in her sleep, Mara turned over and held the watch tighter, and did not let go.
