## 20. Return Passage

The boat that carried me away from Holland was a fishing smack called the *Grietje*, and her captain was a woman named Anke who smoked a pipe the size of a small cannon and asked no questions, which was, I was learning, the most expensive courtesy in wartime.

I stood at the rail as Flushing slid away behind us — grey rooftops, grey water, a grey sky that seemed to have been issued by the same government as everything else — and I did not look back at the house where Mara slept. If I looked back, I would not leave. It was that simple. I had spent thirty-one years making myself easy to overlook, and it turned out that all of that discipline amounted to nothing against a child asking me to stay until she slept. So I had stayed. I had stayed two whole days past every sensible deadline, sleeping in a chair beside her bed like a sentry, waking at every small sound, and on the third morning I had kissed the top of her head — an act of such recklessness that my own heart filed a complaint about it — and walked out before the sun was fully up, because cowardice is easier in half-light.

In my coat pocket, wrapped in oilcloth and then in a handkerchief and then in more oilcloth, because I had apparently become the sort of person who wraps things three times over, lay a letter. Frau Greta Brandt had written it herself, in a hand so upright and severe it could have been used to teach schoolchildren penmanship. She had written it in English, laboriously, for one purpose only: to be read aloud to officials.

*I, Greta Brandt, sister of Aldous Brandt, clockmaker of London, do testify that Coral Villalobos delivered to me the daughter of my brother, in accordance with his last wishes, which were communicated to me before his death in the camp at Knockaloe. The child arrived fed, clothed, unharmed, and able to name the stars, which my brother taught her, and which she has now taught me. Any person who says this woman acted wrongly does not know what my brother did in peacetime or what this woman did in war, and I ask them to consider whether they would have done either.*

There was more — names, dates, the account number of the funds Aldous had left, a description of the watch with its hidden compartment — but it was the last line I kept returning to as the *Grietje* pitched through the grey swells: *I ask them to consider whether they would have done either.*

It was not a plea. That was what I liked about it. Greta Brandt had not begged anyone's forgiveness; she had simply turned the question around and set it down in front of them like a clockmaker setting out his tools. Very well. Here is what was done. Now you tell me what you would have done.

The Channel took eleven hours. For eight of them I was too seasick to be afraid, which I recommend to any traveller as a general strategy. For the remaining three I huddled in the wheelhouse while Anke steered us into a cove I will not describe even now, under a fog so thick it seemed less like weather than like a policy, and a boy of perhaps fifteen rowed me ashore in silence, took my coin, and pushed off again without ever having met my eye. Somewhere behind me, Mrs. Tregose's arrangements continued their patient, criminal work. I had paid her once already with Edith's motorcar, and I suspected I would be paying her again, in some currency or other, for years. There are worse debts.

Then England. Shingle under my boots. Cold air that smelled of salt and coal smoke and, unmistakably, of home, though I had never thought of Cornwall as home until I had left it.

And then the fear arrived properly, the way it always does when the immediate danger clears: not as a flood but as a slow tide, seeping upward through the shingle, through my boots, into the space behind my ribs where worry keeps its lodgings.

*Had they taken Edith?*

I had left her standing on the front step with her chin up and her hat at its most defiant angle, insisting that no magistrate in Cornwall would dare arrest a Marchmont, and knowing perfectly well that magistrates do exactly what they please when the papers are shouting. Three weeks had passed. Three weeks in which anything might have happened. Quill had given me until Thursday; I had left on Wednesday night; the raid, if there was one, would have come Thursday morning, and Edith would have been alone in that house with nothing but her reputation and her fury, both considerable, neither bulletproof.

*Had he waited?* Sergeant Quill, with his decent eyes and his terrible patience. He had given me mercy with a deadline, and I had missed it by hours. Men like Quill forgive missed deadlines the way the sea forgives swimmers.

I stood on the shingle in the fog and made myself take inventory, because inventory is what I do instead of panicking. One letter from Greta Brandt. One mended watch — no. The watch was gone; I had given it back to Mara, and she had refused it, pushing it into my hands with that stubborn little frown, saying, *You keep it. You're the keeper now.* So: one watch, ticking against my wrist where I had worn it since, feeling like a pulse that wasn't mine. Eleven pounds, four shillings, and sixpence, which was everything I had. One change of clothes. And a face that everyone in three villages knew, because I had spent the autumn walking into police stations and post offices and a courtroom, being seen, being named, being — the word still startled me — *remembered.*

There was a time when invisibility had been my craft. I had honed it the way Brandt honed gears. A driver is furniture; furniture is safe; furniture reads everything and is read by no one. I had built that safety plank by plank, and then I had burned every plank in a single winter to keep a child warm, and here was the remarkable thing: I did not miss them. Standing in the fog with my name attached to me like a label I couldn't peel off, I felt — exposed, yes. Frightened, certainly. But also *present*, in a way I hadn't been since I was nineteen and ran from my father's house with one suitcase and no plans. Visibility, it turns out, is just fear with somewhere to go.

The road inland was long and I walked most of it, catching lifts on carts when the drivers didn't look too closely at a woman in a travel-worn coat. Every mile, the fear and the hope argued. At Truro the news-seller was shouting about something; I bought a paper with trembling hands and found, buried below the war news, a paragraph:

*MAGISTRATE'S COURT, PENZANCE — Proceedings against Mrs. E. MARCHMONT of [village name], charged under the Defence of the Realm Act with harbouring, adjourned sine die pending further evidence, the accused having been released on her own recognizance.*

Released. Adjourned. Not acquitted — *adjourned*, which meant they were waiting, gathering, deciding. But not in custody. I read the paragraph four times, standing in the street while men hurried past with kit bags, and I confess that I laughed, once, out loud, startling a pigeon. Edith free. Edith furious, certainly, and composing letters of protest even now, but free.

That left the other question, and it followed me the last twenty miles like a dog: *was Quill waiting?*

* * *

He was waiting.

Of course he was waiting. He was sitting on the low wall outside the churchyard, half a mile from the Marchmont house, with his helmet beside him and his hands folded, looking for all the world like a man waiting for a bus, except that there were no buses within forty miles and he had been there long enough to acquire a companionable crow.

My legs nearly gave out at the sight of him. Then they remembered themselves, and carried me forward, because I had decided somewhere over the North Sea that I was done with sneaking up my own lane.

"Miss Villalobos." He stood and put his helmet on, which I understood to be a formal gesture, like drawing on gauntlets. "You've been to Holland."

"I've been to Holland."

"And the child—"

"Is with her aunt. Frau Greta Brandt, of Flushing. Sister of Aldous Brandt." I took out the oilcloth parcel and held it. "She has written everything down. Dates, names, how the girl came to us, what her father asked. What he did for Miss Marchmont in 1909, and what he did for me." I let that sit a moment. "It will stand up in any court in England, Sergeant, and if it doesn't, I'll stand up with it and read it aloud until it does."

Quill looked at the parcel for a long time. He didn't reach for it.

"You know," he said, "when I gave you until Thursday, I half hoped you'd run and half hoped you'd come back. I didn't expect you to come back carrying paperwork."

"The Dutch are thorough."

"So I see." He rubbed his jaw. "The case against Mrs. Marchmont is adjourned. My superintendent wanted it pressed. But there's a letter from a solicitor in London, retained by — well, by someone who signs himself with a clock, if you can make sense of that — and between that letter and your testimony at the hearing—" He stopped, and something moved across his face that I suspect he'd deny under oath: relief. "Nobody wants to hang a respectable lady for keeping a dead man's promise. Especially not now, with the lists coming back from the camps."

The lists. I knew about the lists. Everyone along the coast knew someone on them.

"Aldous Brandt died in February," I said quietly.

"I heard. I'm sorry." And he sounded sorry, which was the thing about Quill that had made him so dangerous and so bearable at once: he meant it. He picked up his helmet again, then didn't put it on. "Miss Villalobos, I'm going to say something now that isn't official. When you disappeared, I told my superintendent you'd gone to Bristol to see family. It wasn't true. I'd be obliged if you'd had family in Bristol."

I blinked at him. "Sergeant Quill. Are you telling me you lied in a report?"

"I'm telling you," he said, "that a man who spends thirty years reading other people's letters learns to write one or two of his own." He settled the helmet on at last, squared his shoulders, and became, visibly, a policeman again — but a policeman walking *away* from my house rather than toward it. "Tell Mrs. Marchmont the adjournment stands. Tell her—" he paused, choosing, "—tell her the parish is glad to have her attention back on the cause. Good evening, Miss Villalobos."

He walked off down the lane, and the crow escorted him partway, complaining, and I stood there holding a letter that had just become unnecessary and finding, oddly, that I loved it anyway. Proof is proof, whether or not anyone demands it. Some things you carry so long they become part of your skeleton.

* * *

Edith opened the door before I knocked.

I should explain that the door was forty feet from the gate and she could not possibly have seen me coming, but I had learned long ago that Edith Marchmont perceives things by methods unavailable to science. She stood in the doorway in her grey day dress with ink on three fingers and an expression she would have described as mild concern and everyone else would have described as barely contained joy.

"You're thinner," she said. "And you've been crying on the boat, I can tell, your eyelashes do that thing. Come in before the whole village sees you standing about like a monument."

"You've seen the newspaper."

"I've seen six newspapers. I've written to four editors and one Member of Parliament. Did you know that under the Defence of the Realm Act they may confiscate your bicycle as well as your correspondence? Neither did I. I intend to find out why." She stepped back to admit me, and as I passed her, her hand came up — quickly, almost roughly — and touched my cheek, once, and was withdrawn before either of us could remark upon it. "The kettle is on. It has been on for three weeks. I've wasted a scandalous amount of coal."

Inside, the house smelled of tea and lamp oil and Edith's tobacco, and everything was exactly where I had left it, except that on the hall table, addressed to *Miss C. Villalobos*, sat a letter. I stopped and stared at it.

"That came Tuesday," Edith said, watching me with open satisfaction. "Addressed to *you*. By name. In your own house. Whatever shall you do?"

I picked it up. The hand was upright and severe, fit to teach schoolchildren. Inside, beneath the testimony I already knew by heart, Greta Brandt had added a postscript:

*My brother wrote to me in his last letter: "If a quiet woman brings my daughter across the water, tell her she was never invisible to those who were paying attention." I am paying attention now, Miss Villalobos. Write to me. The girl asks when you are coming back, and I tell her the truth, which is that keepers return.*

I stood in the hall with the letter in my hands while the kettle screamed itself hoarse in the kitchen and Edith pretended, badly, not to be watching, and I thought about the word *keeper*, and about a watch ticking on my wrist that belonged to a child across the sea, and about a door I had once opened after midnight and never really shut again.

"Coral," Edith said, from the kitchen doorway. "The tea. And then you're going to sit down and tell me everything, from the beginning, and I am going to interrupt constantly, and we are not going to sleep tonight."

"Yes, ma'am," I said — and for the first time in my life, I answered to my own name as if I expected to hear it again tomorrow.
