## 21. The Trial of Edith Marchmont

They held the hearing in Bodmin, in a courthouse with the smell of cold stone and old varnish, on a Tuesday so grey it seemed the sky itself had been asked to keep its opinions to itself.

I had travelled up from Cornwall the night before on a train full of soldiers home on leave, wedged between a snoring lance-corporal and a woman knitting something khaki at a speed I found frankly alarming. In my bag, wrapped in a spare petticoat like contraband — which, I suppose, is exactly what it was — lay a letter in German and English, signed by one Greta Brandt-Vos of Flushing, Holland, testifying that her brother Aldous Brandt had entrusted his daughter to two English women, and that those two English women had kept their word at the cost of nearly everything they had.

The magistrate's court was half-full: a scattering of local officials, two reporters from Truro, a clerk with ink-stained cuffs, and Sergeant Quill, sitting very straight at the back as though he'd been ordered there and was obeying under protest.

Edith stood at the front in her good grey coat, the one she wore to deputations. She looked, I thought, magnificent — not despite the circumstances but because of them. She had the particular stillness of a woman who has been shouted at by better men than these and has learned to let it roll off like rain off slate.

The charge, read out in a bored drone by the clerk, was harbouring an enemy alien child without licence to the detriment of national security, contrary to various regulations whose numbers I did not catch because my heart was hammering too loudly to hear over.

Edith answered each question put to her with the calm of a woman reading out a shopping list.

*Did you know the child was German?* "Yes."

*Did you conceal her?* "I gave her a bed. Concealment implies shame."

*Did you attempt to remove her from the jurisdiction?* A pause here, long enough for the reporters to sharpen their pencils. "I remained at home," Edith said, "while another person undertook that journey. If you wish to charge someone with it, sir, I would suggest you find out who, since I have declined to say."

And there was the difficulty. Edith would not name me. She had spent three weeks in custody — first at the police station, then transferred to Bodmin while they sorted out what to do with a woman whose suffrage work made her both notorious and, inconveniently, respectable — and she had not given them my name once. The authorities knew *someone* had taken the girl across the North Sea; Mrs. Tregose's boat had come back with a hole in its story if not its hull, and Quill had filed a report that was, I later learned, a masterpiece of selective vagueness.

But no name. Not mine, not Mara's, not Brandt's.

So they were going to convict her of a crime committed by nobody, witnessed by nobody, in defence of a child who officially did not exist.

I sat in the public gallery with my hands folded in my lap and rehearsed what I intended to do, and felt sick about it, and rehearsed it again.

Because here is what I had learned about myself in the past year: I am very good at waiting, and I am very bad at watching injustice being done politely. Reading other people's letters taught me that most wrongdoing happens in rooms where everyone agrees to be reasonable. This room agreed to be reasonable. Every man in it was being perfectly reasonable, in the way a trap is reasonable.

"Miss Marchmont," said the magistrate — a Mr. Penhale-Rowe, elderly, with the air of a man who had already decided everything before breakfast and considered the morning merely a formality — "you must appreciate the position the country finds itself in. Emotions are high. There have been incidents."

"There have indeed, sir." Edith's voice was dry as August. "A baker driven from his shop of thirty years. Windows broken. A family named Schmidt who changed their name to Smith and were broken into anyway. I believe one of your own constables intervened to prevent serious harm to a child. Perhaps he could tell you about the position the country finds itself in."

Quill, at the back, became very interested in his boots.

"You are not assisting yourself, madam."

"I was not aware I was on trial for assisting myself. I understood I was on trial for keeping a promise."

"To an enemy alien."

"To a clockmaker," Edith said, "who pulled me out of the Camelford coach crash in 1909 when every other soul on that road drove past. He set my arm with splints from his own workshop and stayed with me for six hours and never told anyone, because he didn't want thanks, only that I should get home safe. That is who I kept a promise to. You may call him whatever the regulations require you to call him. I shall call him what he was."

Silence. Somewhere a pigeon shuffled on a windowsill.

Penhale-Rowe cleared his throat. "There is no evidence before this court regarding the identity or whereabouts of the child—"

"There wouldn't be," Edith said. "That was the point."

It was at this moment — precisely this moment, when the magistrate's jaw tightened and the clerk reached for his pen to write down something damning — that I discovered my legs had made a decision without consulting me.

I stood up.

The gallery was not crowded, but it was not empty either, and every head turned with the smooth synchrony of a field of wheat in wind. The usher, a stout man with a moustache like a hearth-brush, started toward me. "Madam, you can't—"

"I should like to give evidence," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "I am Coral Villalobos. I was Miss Marchmont's driver and secretary for four years. I am the person who took the child to Holland. And I have a letter here that this court ought to hear before it decides anything."

*

I will be honest with you, whoever you are, whoever eventually reads this: I had planned to do it differently. I had planned some dignified, gradual intervention — a note passed to the clerk, perhaps, a quiet word with the prosecution. Standing up uninvited in a courtroom and announcing myself was not the plan.

But I had watched Edith stand alone for twenty minutes taking the weight of everything, refusing to let it shift onto me, and something in me simply refused to allow it. Loyalty, I have found, is not really a decision. It is a reflex. It lives lower than the thinking part of you, somewhere near the spine.

"Name?" said the clerk, recovering first.

"Coral Villalobos. Of Porthollow, Cornwall."

"Do you understand, miss, that by admitting what you've just admitted, you expose yourself to the same charges?"

"I had rather hoped," I said, "that exposing myself was rather the point."

Someone in the gallery laughed — a short, surprised bark — and was shushed.

Penhale-Rowe peered at me over his spectacles. "You confess to removing the child from this jurisdiction?"

"I confess to carrying out a dying man's instructions. May I read the letter? It is sworn and attested at the Dutch consulate in Flushing. It explains whose child she was, and why she came to us, and what we were asked to do. I think, sir, that if you hear it, you will find this court has been trying the wrong crime entirely."

For a long moment the old man looked at me the way men look at weather they cannot control. Then, to his eternal credit, or perhaps simply his curiosity — magistrates are human underneath, mostly — he said, "Read it."

I unwrapped the letter from its petticoat. My hands were shaking now; the steadiness had been used up. The paper crackled. The whole room leaned in.

"'To whom it may concern,'" I read. "'I am Greta Brandt-Vos, of Flushing, the Netherlands, sister of Aldous Brandt, clockmaker, late of London and of Cornwall, who died on the fourteenth of February of this year while interned at Knockaloe, Isle of Man.'"

I heard Quill make a small sound behind me. He hadn't known. Nobody had known. The letter had taken eleven weeks to reach me through channels I will not describe, and I had opened it standing in Edith's kitchen at midnight, alone, and had to sit down on the floor.

"'My brother was arrested in the first days of the war though he had lived in England nineteen years,'" I read on. "'His wife, Anna, died in London the previous winter. Before his arrest, fearing for his daughter Mara, aged nine, he placed her in the care of two ladies he trusted — Mrs. Edith Marchmont, whom he had once helped at risk of himself, and her driver, Miss Coral Villalobos, to whom also he had once shown kindness when she had nowhere else to go. His instruction to them, given in a sealed note, was simple: keep my daughter safe until she can be brought to me or to mine.'"

I paused. My eyes had gone blurry. I wiped them with the back of my wrist like a farmhand and carried on.

"'He wrote to me in his last permitted letter: *"If the girl reaches you, thank the lady who reads letters. Tell her she was never invisible to me. Tell her debts of kindness are the only currency worth carrying across a war."* My brother died believing his daughter lost. She was not lost. She was delivered to my door in April by Miss Villalobos, who crossed enemy-patrolled waters in a fishing boat to keep a promise made in peacetime to a man her country had declared its enemy. I ask your court to consider what manner of crime this is. To me it looks like the opposite.'"'

I lowered the letter. The courtroom had gone very quiet — not the polite quiet of before, but the thick kind, the kind that has weight.

"The child," I said, "is safe in Flushing with her aunt. Her father is dead. He died in your internment camp, of pneumonia, having harmed this country precisely never. The women you are trying kept his daughter alive. If that is against the law, then I have nothing further to say in my defence, because I would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, until somebody explained to me how a nine-year-old orphan becomes an enemy."

*

What happened next, I record with some surprise even now: the prosecution counsel — a young man called Tresidder who had clearly expected a routine conviction before lunch — rose and asked for a recess. Penhale-Rowe granted ten minutes, which everyone understood to mean he needed to go and decide whether he wanted this case in his courtroom at all.

In the corridor, Quill found me by the window.

"Well," he said.

"Sergeant."

"You know I'm obliged to take a statement from you. Formally."

"I assumed so."

He shifted his weight. Under his arm he had his notebook, and I saw that the pencil tucked in its spine had been sharpened down to almost nothing, as though he had been writing and erasing the same thing for days. "Miss Villalobos," he said, "when you came to me in September and I told you to give the girl up — I want it noted that I was doing my job. I want it noted that I believed it."

"Noted," I said gently. "And now?"

"And now I've had a lot of time," Quill said, "to consider the difference between the law and the right thing, and to notice that in my experience they're usually on speaking terms but occasionally they don't write to each other for months." He opened the notebook. "Statement of Sergeant William Quill," he recited, half to himself, half to me. "I was aware of the presence of a child at the Marchmont household from approximately the third week of September. I conducted enquiries. I was unable to establish that any offence had been committed against the child herself, who appeared fed, clothed, schooled, and — I'll put this in — *visibly happier* than when I first saw her. At no time did I observe any threat to national security emanating from a nine-year-old girl, her watch-repairing skills notwithstanding."

"You're going to perjure yourself?"

"I'm going to give evidence," Quill said, "which is a different thing. It's what all this—" he gestured vaguely at the wood panelling, the coat of arms, the whole solemn machinery of it "—is supposed to be for. Thought I'd try it."

"Edith will make a suffragist of you yet."

"God forbid," said Quill, with feeling, and went off to sign his statement.

*

The afternoon session was short. Tresidder, to his credit or his defeat, did not press hard. What could he press? Two women had admitted everything and were plainly proud of it; a police sergeant had testified that no threat existed; a sworn letter from neutral Holland sat on the bench like a stone in a shoe.

Penhale-Rowe delivered his judgment looking chiefly at his own hands.

"This court," he said, "is obliged to enforce regulations designed for the security of the realm in extraordinary times. It is not obliged to be blind. The regulations concerning enemy aliens were written with grown men of military age in mind, capable of signalling to U-boats and the like. They were not written, whatever certain newspapers may imagine, with reference to orphaned children or to the women who sheltered them." He removed his spectacles. "Mrs. Marchmont, the charges against you are dismissed. The conduct described, while technically irregular, reflects credit upon you and upon — upon the country whose values you appear to hold in better repair than some of its officials. Miss Villalobos, the same applies to you, though I would caution you most strongly against making a habit of interrupting courts."

"With respect, sir," I said, "it worked."

The gallery laughed outright this time, and this time nobody shushed it.

Edith turned around and looked at me across the length of the courtroom, and I saw that her eyes were wet, and I saw also that she would deny it to her grave, and I loved her completely.

Outside on the steps, the grey sky had cracked open just enough to drop a stripe of pale gold across the cobbles, and the two reporters were already arguing over adjectives, and Edith took my arm as though we were simply two ladies leaving a lecture, and said, quietly, for me alone:

"You stood up. Uninvited. In a courtroom. Four years ago you wouldn't have spoken first to a vicar."

"A great deal," I said, "has happened since the vicar."

She squeezed my arm. "We're going home, Coral. Both of us. And the first thing I intend to do is sleep for a week, and the second thing is write to a girl in Holland and tell her exactly what her Coral did today."

*Our* Coral, she had said. Or near enough.

We walked down the steps into the thin gold light together — visible, at last, to absolutely everyone — and neither of us minded one bit.
