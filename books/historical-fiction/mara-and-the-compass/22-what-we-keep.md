## 22. What We Keep

The armistice came in November, and the bells rang for it, and Coral Villalobos stood in the churchyard at Porthcarrow with her hands folded and her face arranged into something appropriately solemn, and thought: *I do not feel like celebrating. I feel like a woman who has been holding her breath for five years and is not entirely sure the air is safe yet.*

Edith stood beside her, hatless as always — a small rebellion she had never surrendered, even when it cost her — and after a moment she reached over and took Coral's hand. Just that. No speech, no tears. The bells went on ringing overhead like enormous patient clocks.

"Thursday," Edith said, which had become their private word for everything that could not be said aloud. *We survived Thursday. We survived all of them.*

"Thursday," Coral agreed.

They walked home along the cliff path, and the sea did what the sea always did, which was to go on being indifferent and beautiful, and Coral found that by the time they reached the gate she was, against all her better judgment, crying. Not for grief. For the strange luxury of standing in an open doorway with someone who knew every secret she had and had chosen, year after year, to stay anyway.

* * *

By spring of 1919 the house had learned new habits.

There were flowers on the kitchen table now, because Edith had taken up gardening with the same ferocity she'd once applied to chaining herself to railings, and there was a second chair at the desk in the study, because Coral's letter-reading had stopped being a crime and started being a correspondence. And there was, on the mantelpiece in the front room, a clock.

It was not a fine clock. It was a cheap tin thing with a chipped face, bought from a peddler in Falmouth, but inside it ticked with a heart Coral would have known anywhere — because Mara had regulated it before she left, seated cross-legged on the kitchen floor at eleven years old, tongue between her teeth, hands steady as a surgeon's.

"It'll keep better time than anything in Cornwall," she had announced. "Except me."

And then she had gone, in the grey light of a September morning in 1917, aboard the packet boat from Flushing with her aunt Greta's hand on her shoulder, waving until the fog took her. Coral had stood on the quay and done exactly what the note had told her never to do. She had memorized the face. She kept it still, behind her eyes, where no one could order it forgotten.

* * *

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right, because Tuesdays were post days and Coral had built half her life on Tuesdays.

She knew the handwriting before she broke the seal — that upright, careful script, each letter standing at attention like a well-made gear. Mara wrote the way Aldous Brandt had written. Some inheritances have nothing to do with blood.

*Dear Miss Villalobos,* (and Coral smiled at that; three years of letters and the girl still refused to call her anything so familiar as Coral, though her last one had signed itself *your Mara*, which Coral suspected was the girl's idea of a compromise) *I am fifteen now and taller than Aunt Greta, who says I will be tall as a lamppost and twice as useful. School is good. My German has come back, which made my teacher cry, though she cried about most things. I am learning English literature instead of only English, and I have read all of Dickens and some of him twice.*

*I am writing to tell you something important, so you must save it for last. But first — how is Mrs. Marchmont? Is she still growing impossible roses? Has Sergeant Quill finally proposed to the baker's widow or does he intend to spend another decade buying bread he doesn't need? Tell him if he doesn't act soon I will come home and arrange it myself, and I am very good at arranging things.*

Coral laughed out loud, alone in the study, and then read the passage aloud again because Edith was in the garden and deserved to hear it.

*Now the important part.* Here Mara's handwriting lost its parade-ground neatness, just slightly, the way it always did when she meant what she said. *Aunt Greta has received the final papers from the authorities. Papa's effects. Most of it is tools, and she has given the tools to a young man in Amsterdam who is apprenticing and needed them more than we do. Papa would have liked that. But there was also an envelope addressed in his hand, and it says on the outside: For the lady who reads letters.*

*Inside is a watch. You will know it. It is the one I repaired in your kitchen, the first thing I ever fixed that wasn't mine. He carried it through everything. Aunt Greta says he held onto it even on the ship to Man, and the men who searched his things said he wouldn't let go of it and they had to let him keep it because he looked at them in a certain way. I know that way. I have it too, apparently.*

*He put something inside the case. I won't tell you what, because he told me not to, and because I think you already know, or you will know when you hold it. But I will tell you what he told me to tell you, and I have waited two years to say it properly, so here goes:*

*Thank you for keeping my promise. A promise is not finished when you make it. It is finished when somebody keeps it, and you kept mine, and I hope you understand that this makes us family, because in our family that is the definition.*

*Your Mara*

*(P.S. The watch runs slow. It always ran slow. He said some clocks are allowed to run slow because they are thinking about other things. When you wind it, think about us.)*

* * *

The watch arrived a fortnight later, wrapped in oilcloth and newspaper from a country that had spent four years on the other side of everything.

Coral opened the case in the kitchen, in the afternoon light, with Edith watching silently over her shoulder, and there it was — the message she had found once before in the dark of a cellar while soldiers' boots crossed the floorboards above her head. The coordinates. The name in Holland. *Keep my promise.*

But there was something new now, scratched into the inner lid in letters so small she had to carry the watch to the window and tilt it against the sun:

*A.V. — E.M. — M.B. Four names are stronger than one. — A.B.*

"He engraved that in internment," Edith said quietly. "On the Isle of Man. With whatever tool he could hide from the guards." She touched the lid with one finger, very lightly, the way one touches a reliquary. "He knew, Coral. Before any of it. He knew we'd keep her, and he knew we'd keep each other."

Coral wound the watch. It caught, hesitated, and began — running, as promised, precisely forty seconds slow.

She decided she would never correct it.

* * *

In June, Greta Brandt came to Cornwall.

She arrived on the afternoon coach looking exactly as Coral might have predicted: a broad, capable woman with her brother's level grey eyes and her niece's habit of standing with her feet planted as though prepared to repair whatever fell apart next. She carried one carpet bag and a parcel wrapped in waxed paper, and when Edith met her at the gate the two women regarded one another for a moment like generals assessing an alliance — and then Greta said, "So you are the suffragette. My brother wrote to me about you in 1911. He said, *Greta, if I am ever in trouble in England, find the loud one and the quiet one, for between them they can move mountains,*" and Edith, who was not easily moved, put her hand over her mouth.

"I was the loud one," Edith managed.

"Yes," said Greta. "I had that from the newspapers as well."

That evening they sat in the kitchen — the kitchen, because Greta had taken one look at the formal dining room and pronounced it "a room for people who don't like each other" — and ate Cornish pasties that Coral had made and Greta politely declared "an interesting interpretation," and Greta gave them the news they had waited two years to hear properly.

Mara was thriving. That was the word she used, and she used it deliberately, testing it aloud like a bell. *Thriving*. She had grown four inches. She had won the school prize for mathematics, beating every boy in her class, and had accepted the medal "with an expression that made the headmaster nervous." She had begun an apprenticeship of sorts — unofficial, unsanctioned, entirely irregular — with a watchmaker in Middelburg named de Vries, an old friend of Aldous's, who had taken one look at her work and offered her Saturdays in his shop and eventually, Greta suspected, the shop itself.

"She has his hands," Greta said. "And his stubbornness. And—" she paused, choosing, "—she has your courage. Both of yours. She talks about England the way other children talk about fairy countries. She talks about the storm, and the kitchen floor where she mended the watch, and the night the soldiers came and Mrs. Marchmont shouted at them until they apologized, which Aunt Greta, I confess, did not believe until I heard it from a third party."

"They didn't apologize," Edith said. "But they did retreat, and retreat is its own apology."

"And Quill?" Greta asked, leaning forward. "Mara asks every letter. What became of your policeman?"

Ah, Quill. Coral had wondered when the subject would arrive; she'd have bet the car — the new car, the little Austin that had replaced the one traded away in a black cove — that it would.

Sergeant Quill had retired in the spring. He kept bees now, of all things, down in the village, and sold honey from a table outside his cottage with a hand-painted sign reading HONEST HONEY, PRICES REASONABLE, COMPLAINTS TO THE BEES. He had never married the baker's widow, though everyone agreed they were companions of long standing, and he came up the hill once a month on a Sunday, ostensibly to deliver honey, actually to sit in Edith's garden and argue gently about parish politics and lose.

And once a month, when he stood to go, he paused at the gate and said the same thing he had been saying since the trial:

"Well. I expect I'll have to arrest the pair of you someday."

And Coral always answered, "You'll have to catch us at something first."

And he always tipped his hat and said, "I'm a patient man," and walked down the hill whistling.

"Tell Mara," Coral said, "that Sergeant Quill keeps bees and threatens us monthly and has never once followed through, and that she may hold him personally responsible for the fact that we remain at liberty."

"I shall write it verbatim," said Greta. "She'll frame it."

* * *

Later, when Greta had gone up to the guest room — *the guest room*, which had been Mara's room, which had been empty for two years and which Edith had refused on principle to call anything but *Mara's room* until the week Greta confirmed she was coming, at which point Edith had aired it, repainted it, and purchased sheets of a quality she reserved for duchesses and causes — Coral stepped out into the garden.

The evening was doing what Cornish summer evenings do: going gold at the edges, smelling of salt and cut grass, pretending the twentieth century hadn't happened. Edith was deadheading the roses, which she claimed was horticulture and Coral knew was meditation.

"She asked me something in her last letter," Coral said. "Something I haven't answered yet."

"Mm?"

"She asked whether I ever regretted it. Any of it. Losing the car, nearly losing the house, the trial, all of it. She said, *you paid so much for me and I want to know if it was worth it, and please don't lie to be kind.*"

Edith snipped off a dead bloom with unnecessary force. "And what will you tell her?"

"The truth, obviously. Which is that I didn't pay for her. That's the mistake everyone makes about sacrifice — they price it like goods." Coral turned the watch in her pocket, feeling its slow heartbeat. "I had a life before her. It was a small life. Comfortable, the way a drawer is comfortable — everything in its place, nothing in it. She didn't take anything out of that drawer, Edith. She set fire to the whole dresser, and I have never once missed it."

Edith stood, brushing soil from her knees, and looked at Coral for a long moment with the expression she otherwise reserved for parliamentary injustice.

"You know," she said, "in 1913 I believed I would die alone in this house, having achieved a great deal and enjoyed almost none of it. And now look. A shared household. Monthly honey. A Dutch daughter who writes to us both and scolds us equally. A clock that runs slow on purpose." She shook her head. "The war took nearly everything from nearly everybody. From us it took a great deal and somehow handed back more. I've never worked out the arithmetic and I've stopped trying."

"Perhaps," Coral said, "some things aren't arithmetic."

"Everything is arithmetic," said Edith Marchmont. "Some sums simply refuse to balance honestly." And then, because she was Edith, she spoiled the moment entirely by adding, "Also, if you're going to stand there being profound, you can be profound while handing me the secateurs."

* * *

The last scene of that June, the one Coral returned to often in the years after, was small enough to fit in a teacup.

Greta had gone to bed. Edith was reading in the study, spectacles low on her nose, muttering at the government. And Coral sat at the desk in the front room — *her* desk now; when had that happened? Sometime in 1918, without ceremony, the way real things happen — with the evening's letters spread before her.

Because she still read them. Of course she still read them. The habit had survived raids and trials and a world war, and besides, the parish had simply decided: if you wanted a letter delivered carefully, and perhaps understood deeply, you gave it to Miss Villalobos. Half of Porthcarrow wrote their difficult letters knowing she would see them — the widow's letter to her son in Canada, the fisherman's proposal drafted eleven times, old Mrs. Tregose's annual Christmas card to a nephew she'd disowned in 1909 and secretly forgiven in 1910.

And among them, that evening, were two letters addressed to *her*.

One bore the Dutch stamps, the upright hand, the return address in Middelburg. One was heavier, local, smelling faintly of honey and sealed with a thumbprint rather than wax.

She opened the local one first. Inside, in Quill's blunt carpenter's capitals:

*MISS VILALOBOS — ENCLOSED FIND THE ANSWER TO YOUR QUESTION OF LAST AUTUMN, WHICH WAS WHETHER I EVER REGRETTED NOT DOING MY DUTY IN 1916. HERE IS MY ANSWER: DUTY IS WHAT YOU OWE STRANGERS. WHAT I FELT THAT THURSDAY WAS SOMETHING ELSE AND I HAVE NEVER REGRETTED IT ONE DAY SINCE. ALSO THE BEES HAVE SWARMED AGAIN AND IF MRS. MARCHMENT WANTS ANY I CAN BRING A NUCLEUS BOX SUNDAY. RESPECTFULLY, J. QUILL.*

Coral read it twice, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and laughed at herself for it.

Then she took up Mara's letter, slit it open with care, and unfolded pages and pages of a girl's life — mathematics prizes and apprenticeship hours and a description of the North Sea in February ("grey, but honest grey, not lying grey like your English sky") and a paragraph about de Vries's shop that glowed like a lamp — and somewhere in the middle of the third page, Coral stopped reading and simply sat, holding the paper, listening to the slow deliberate tick of a clock that was thinking about other things.

Down the hall, Edith turned a page. Out in the dark, the sea kept its own time. On the desk, under the lamp, the letters waited — some to be answered, some to be kept, all of them proof of the same plain miracle: that a woman who had once known everything and belonged nowhere now knew slightly less and belonged absolutely.

Coral picked up her pen, drew the lamp closer, and began to write back.

*Dear Mara —*

*First: yes. It was worth it. It was worth more than it cost, which is the only sum I have ever done that balances both ways.*

*Second: Quill says—*

And downstairs in the hall, the slow clock ticked on, keeping imperfect, faithful, unforgettable time — the way the best clocks do, the way the best families do — forty seconds behind the world and entirely content to be so.
