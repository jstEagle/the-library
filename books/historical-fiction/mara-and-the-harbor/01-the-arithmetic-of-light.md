## 1. The Arithmetic of Light

Mirabel Lockridge woke at four minutes past five, as he had every morning for three years, and lay still in the dark listening to the arithmetic.

The foghorn had sounded twice in the night — he'd logged it half-asleep, the pencil scratchy in the margin. The wind had come around to the northeast around midnight, which meant the swell would be building against the shoal by now. And somewhere below him, in the oil room, forty-one gallons of kerosene sat in their tins like a bank account that paid out only in light.

He got up without ceremony. There was no ceremony left in him; Ede had carried most of it away when she went, and what remained had been worn down by repetition into something smooth and functional, like a stone on the tideline. He dressed in the dark because he knew where everything was. He had made himself a man who knew where everything was. It was, he suspected, the only talent the Admiralty had ever accidentally cultivated in him: the talent of taking up exactly as much space as his duties required, and not one inch more.

The tower was cold in the way stone is cold — patiently, without malice. He lit the stove in the watch room first, then climbed. Ninety-six steps to the lantern. He'd counted them once out of boredom, early on, before boredom became a thing he managed rather than suffered. Ninety-six steps, four turns of the stairwell, and at the top the great lens waiting for him like a patient animal that knew the hour better than any clock.

He opened the lantern door and the smell of last night's burning came out to meet him — hot metal, kerosene, the faint char of wick. He worked through the routine with his hands while his mind did its other counting. Wick trimmed to a quarter inch. Lamp raised on its carrier until the flame sat at the focal plane of the lens, dead center, where the glass could gather it and throw it nine miles out to sea. Clockwork wound — the rotation mechanism wanted eleven full turns and he gave them all, unhurried, listening to the weight descend through its gears with a sound like a slow heartbeat.

At six minutes past six, Grayharbor Point began to turn.

He watched it from the gallery while the light swept behind him, one revolution every forty seconds, the beam walking out across the water and dragging itself back again. Northeast wind, force six maybe seven. A dirty gray sea with white water showing on the shoal already. He took out the logbook, opened it to the day's line, and wrote:

*6:06. Lamp lit. Wind NE 6–7. Swell building. Visibility moderate. Glass falling.*

The barometer had been falling since Tuesday. He noted it without drama, the way you note anything that has not yet become an emergency but intends to. Out here, weather was never an event. It was a ledger entry with consequences, and the trick of survival was reading the entries before they read you.

He went down to breakfast: tea, bread fried in dripping, an apple from the barrel that was going soft at one end and which he ate anyway, cutting away the bruise. He ate at the table by the window because the table by the window was where one ate, and the fact that there was a second chair at that table, and that the chair was empty, was not a thing the table or the chair or Mirabel had any intention of discussing.

* * *

Ede's chair was still by the window.

It was a small dishonesty of his life, and he kept it the way other men kept drink. A ladder-back chair with a woven seat gone soft in the middle, angled toward the south window where the morning came in over the harbor. Her shawl still hung over its back — he had washed it once, three years ago, and hung it back, and told himself that was housekeeping and nothing else. On the seat lay her mending basket, the needles still threaded with grey wool she'd been using on his good coat sleeve the week the cough started.

He did not look at it in the way that other men look at things. He looked at it in the way you look at a lighthouse from the sea: regularly, briefly, to fix your position.

Three years ago this month, the Admiralty's man had come out with the pension papers and asked, with the gentle clumsiness of clerks everywhere, whether the keeper wished to request reassignment to a shore station, given his circumstances. *Circumstances* meaning the grave on the mainland, meaning the silence in the second chair. Mirabel had said no, thank you, the point suited him. What he had not said: that Grayharbor Point was the last place on earth where Ede had been happy, and the only place where being unhappy had a shape to it — a schedule, a lamp, a logbook — so that grief could be folded into work the way salt is folded into dough, invisible, evenly distributed, changing nothing outwardly except the taste.

And what he had also not said, because he hadn't known it yet: that a man who asks to be forgotten will be obliged, eventually.

He finished his tea and took up the day's real ledger — not the light log, which belonged to the Admiralty, but his own book, the cheap one he kept locked in the drawer under the flour tin. In it he counted what he owned in the only units that mattered to him now: things that could be carried.

It was an old habit, older than the widowhood. Ede used to laugh at it. *You count like a man expecting a fire,* she'd say, and he'd say, *I count like a man who grew up poor, love, which is the same thing with longer notice.* But the habit had deepened since her death, the way roots deepen in thin soil, reaching sideways for whatever purchase they can find. Every autumn he made the inventory. Every autumn the inventory got shorter.

He wrote it out now, pencil moving in his small tight hand:

*Kerosene, 41 gal. Coal, roughly 1½ tons. Flour, 90 lb. Salt pork, 2 casks. Dried peas, beans, onions in the sand box. Tea, 4 lb. Sugar, 12 lb. Lamp oil for the house. Candles, two dozen. Boots, one good pair, one pair mended. Money, £14 7s., plus £3 10s. due at quarter-day. Books, nineteen. Ede's letters, one bundle. Nothing else that cannot be burned.*

He looked at the last line for a while.

That was the whole philosophy, really, reduced to a single entry. Three years ago he had begun pruning his life down to what could be carried up ninety-six steps and, if it came to it, down into a skiff. He owned no pictures worth saving. He had let the hens go two winters back because feed cost more than eggs. His furniture would break your back and nobody's heart. When the supply boat came each autumn he bought nothing that couldn't be lifted with one hand, and the boatmen had learned not to offer, and after a while they had stopped seeing him at all — he could tell, from the way they spoke across him to the packages rather than about them. A man who owns little becomes furniture himself. It had seemed, once, like safety.

Now, with the pencil hovering over the page, it seemed like something else. He wrote *Nothing else that cannot be burned,* and underlined it, and did not examine why the underline felt less like arithmetic and more like confession.

* * *

The morning's work proceeded in its appointed order, and he was glad of it. Gladness was not too strong a word — he had come to understand that routine was not the absence of feeling but a vessel for it, and that a man without routine drowned in whatever he felt, however small.

By eight the glass had fallen another tenth. By nine the swell was breaking clean over the outer shoal, white water standing up against the gray in long plumes, and he logged it and checked the fog signal's compressor and walked the gallery rail with an oiled rag, wiping salt haze off the ironwork before it could begin its slow argument with the metal. The rail ran all the way round, sixty feet above the sea, and from it he could see the whole of his jurisdiction: the point, the shoal lying just under the surface like a sleeping animal, the harbor mouth two miles north, and beyond the harbor the causeway bridge — closed now, as it always closed in November, the season's iron lid coming down on the town of Grayharbor like a hand over a candle.

The bridge mattered more than strangers would think. When the causeway closed, the point was cut off from everything — mail, medicine, company, escape. Six months of tide and weather between him and the mainland, with the supply boat making its runs only when the pilot dared. Most winters he saw no face but his own from December to March. He had planned for it the way he planned for everything: stores counted, fuel stacked, books rationed at one chapter a night so they'd last. Loneliness, too, could be entered in the ledger. *Loneliness, sufficient for the season.* He'd gotten very good at carrying it.

He was on the gallery at midday, eating a heel of bread and watching the shoal, when he noticed the boat.

Small, gray-painted, working hard against the chop — the harbormaster's gig, out of season, running a line directly for the point when no official business should exist for another fortnight. Mirabel stood very still and did the arithmetic. Annual inspection: mid-November, every year, same week, like rent. Supply boat: due in ten days. Harbormaster's gig on a rising sea in late October: unprecedented.

They were coming early. Something had changed upstream of him, in the offices where his life was decided without his knowledge, and the change was traveling toward him at eight knots with a clerk in it.

He went down and put the kettle on, because a keeper receives visitors with tea whether he wants them or not, and while the water heated he stood at the window — not Ede's window; he avoided that one when he needed a clear head — and rehearsed the version of himself he would present. The dull one. The reliable one. The man who trims wicks and logs weather and has opinions about neither. Three years of practice had made the performance nearly effortless, which was perhaps the saddest skill he owned.

The gig bumped against the landing stage below at twenty past one. One figure climbed the rock stair, and it was not the harbormaster, and it was not the usual inspection man either. It was a woman — young, sharp-eyed, wearing the harbormaster's office coat buttoned to the throat and carrying a document case the way soldiers carry rifles, slung and ready. She reached the tower door and knocked with the flat of her hand, three precise blows, and when Mirabel opened it she looked at him for a moment longer than courtesy required, the way a person reads a page they suspect contains an error.

"Mr. Lockridge. Tamsin Vole, harbormaster's clerk." She held out a sealed envelope, heavy paper, wax stamped with the Admiralty anchor. "Dispatch for the keeper, to be signed for. I'm to wait while you read it."

Mirabel signed the receipt in his small tight hand and broke the seal, and the first line of the directive told him why the gig had come early, and why the clerk had come with it, and none of it was good.
