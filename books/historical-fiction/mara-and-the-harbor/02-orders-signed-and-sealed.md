## 2. Orders, Signed and Sealed

The supply boat came on the last Tuesday of October, riding a sea the color of pewter, and Mirabel saw it first as a smudge against the horizon that refused to resolve into cloud.

He had been expecting it a fortnight later. That was the arithmetic he kept in his head alongside the lamp oil and the coal: the *Puffin* made her annual call in mid-November, weather permitting, which meant the stores arrived with the first real cold, which meant the winter began officially when the boat bumped against his jetty and men in oilskins began shouting numbers at him across the water. Three years of this. He could set his watch by it, if he'd owned a watch worth setting.

So when the smudge appeared two weeks early, Mirabel did what he always did with things that arrived early: he distrusted them.

He went down to the jetty while the boat was still making its approach through the outer reach, and stood with his hands in his coat pockets watching the crew fender off. Two men he knew — Denny Croft at the tiller, young Pask handling lines — and a third figure aft by the cargo, wrapped to the chin, who was not crew and did not pretend to be.

"Early," Mirabel said, when Denny had thrown him the bowline.

"Tide's early." Denny grinned. "Everything's early this year. Bridge closes Friday."

Mirabel felt that land somewhere behind his ribs, cold and specific, like a coin dropped down a well. "Friday? The causeway doesn't close until the third week of November. It's never closed before the third week."

"Well." Denny shrugged in the way of men delivering other people's decisions. "It's closing Friday. Orders came down from the county engineer, orders came down from the Admiralty, and we're all just the hands they pass through." He nodded back toward the figure at the stern. "Miss Vole has your papers. She wanted to hand them over herself, she said."

Tamsin Vole came down the gangway with a leather dispatch case held against her chest the way other women carried infants, and stepped onto the jetty with a sureness that surprised him every time — she was small, sharp-boned, thirty perhaps, with ink stains permanently resident in the creases of her right hand, and she moved on these wet planks as though she'd been born to them.

"Keeper Lockridge."

"Clerk Vole." He touched his cap. They had exchanged perhaps forty words in three years, all of them about manifests, and he had liked her for exactly one quality: she never wasted his time, or pretended warmth she didn't feel.

She didn't now, either. But she hesitated, which was new.

"The stores first," she said. "Then the papers."

The unloading took an hour. Flour in hundredweight sacks; salt pork in brine casks; lamp oil in tins that Pask stacked against the tower wall under Mirabel's counting eye — twelve dozen, no more, though the requisition he'd filed in June had asked for fourteen. Coal last, shot down the chute into the bunker, black and glittering and insufficient. He made his complaints aloud because complaining was part of the ritual and Denny partook of it gladly, but inside, where the ledger-keeping mind did its true work, he was already adjusting. Twelve dozen would carry him to March if the winter was ordinary. If the winter was ordinary.

Nothing about this year was arriving on schedule.

When the last sack was stowed, Tamsin Vole drew him aside, out of the crew's earshot, to the windward end of the jetty where the sound of the sea would cover anything quieter than a shout. She opened the dispatch case and handed him a single envelope, heavy paper, sealed with wax rather than gum, and he understood before breaking it that this was not the usual season-close notice. The usual notice came printed, unsigned, addressed to *The Keeper, Grayharbor Point*, in type so old the letters had begun to blur into their neighbors.

This envelope bore a handwritten direction: *To be delivered personally.*

He broke the seal there on the jetty, wind worrying the corners of the page, and read it twice because the first reading refused to make sense.

---

*By direction of the Lords Commissioners of the Admiralty:*

*1. The Grayharbor causeway bridge shall be closed to all traffic from the 3rd day of November, or such earlier date as conditions require, until spring thaw. No exceptions will be granted.*

*2. HM vessel Corvus will transit the Grayharbor approaches on or about the 14th day of November. The keeper of Grayharbor Point light is to ensure the light is exhibited and in good order during the period of transit. No further communication concerning the Corvus will be issued to this station.*

*3. The keeper is reminded of his obligations under the Official Secrets provisions applicable to Admiralty movements, breach of which carries penalties as prescribed.*

*By order,*
*(signed)*
*R. M. Halloway, Secretary*

---

He read it a third time, standing very still, letting the wind have the page's edges. Behind him the *Puffin*'s engine idled, impatient. Tamsin Vole watched him read, and he was aware of her watching the way one is aware of a draft.

"Two weeks' closure early," he said at last. "And a warship past my light, which I'm told to light for and told nothing else about. And a reminder that talking about it is a crime." He folded the page along its original crease. "That's three sentences doing the work of none."

"Yes." She said it quietly, without looking at the sea, which everyone else on the jetty was doing out of habit. "I copied the fair text myself. There were four drafts before it left the harbormaster's office. Four drafts, Keeper, for a notice that runs to three paragraphs. Somebody upstairs wanted the wording exact."

"Somebody always wants the wording exact."

"Not somebody." Her eyes came around to his face then, gray eyes, quick, and for the first time in three years of professional acquaintance he saw something in them he hadn't seen before, and couldn't immediately name. Unease was too soft a word. "I've been clerk at Grayharbor harbor office six years. I know what routine looks like on paper. Routine doesn't get hand-delivered. Routine doesn't close a causeway two weeks ahead of the ice." She paused. "Routine doesn't send a corvette through shoal water in November when the deep channel's open and empty."

"Maybe the deep channel isn't open and empty."

"It is. I file the soundings." She said it flatly, factually, and then, as if hearing herself, added: "Which is not a thing I said. You understand. I've said nothing."

"I understand," Mirabel said. "You've said nothing. I've heard nothing. This envelope is routine and I am a man who trims wicks."

Something in her posture eased a fraction — not relief, exactly. Recognition, maybe. Two people agreeing on the shape of a lie they'd need later.

"The stores are short," he said, resuming his own voice, the voice of complaints and manifests. "Twelve dozen oil against fourteen requisitioned. I'll want that noted."

"I noted it before you counted it." Almost — almost — the ghost of a smile. "Your requisition of June asked fourteen dozen. Someone reduced it in August without annotation. I flagged it. The flag was removed from the file. I re-flagged it." She snapped the dispatch case shut. "I keep copies, Keeper. Of everything. It's a fault in my character."

"A fault," Mirabel agreed.

The crew were coiling lines. Denny called something about tide and weather and being home before dark, and Tamsin Vole stepped back toward the gangway, then stopped, one hand on the rail, and spoke without turning fully around, pitched low, meant only for him.

"The bridge closes Friday. After that, nobody comes out here until thaw. Nobody checks on you, nobody brings you post, nobody knows what happens on this point except you and your logbook." She looked at him then, straight on. "Whatever that envelope means — and I think you're a man who reads envelopes properly — keep your log exact. Exact logs are the only kind that survive."

Then she was down the gangway and aboard, and the *Puffin* was turning in the outer reach, and Mirabel stood alone on his jetty with a sealed directive in his coat and the smell of coal smoke settling over everything he owned.

* * *

That evening he climbed the tower at the proper hour and lit the lamp at the proper minute, and went through the motions his hands had known for eleven years — nine of them with Ede, two without — and tried to do nothing else with his mind, and failed.

The lamp caught and steadied. He checked the clockwork, wound it its full eight days, watched the lens begin its slow rotation, throwing its beam seaward once every ten seconds. Out beyond the glass, the shoal lay dark under the darkening water, exactly where it had lain since before any chart named it, and his light crossed it faithfully, and had crossed it faithfully through every transit, every storm, every night of his keeping. That was the whole of his religion: the light does not judge what passes beneath it. The light burns. What comes through the reach comes through marked water, and what happens after belongs to the sea and to whoever sent it.

He went down to the kitchen, ate his supper standing up, and sat afterward in the room where Ede's chair still stood by the window, facing the harbor mouth. He had not moved the chair in two years. Moving it would have been a decision, and decisions were heavy, and he had spent three years learning to carry as little as possible.

He took out the directive instead and read it again by lamplight, and this time he did what the ledger-keeping mind demanded: he made columns.

*What the paper says:* light exhibited and in good order, mid-November, for a naval transit.

*What the paper costs:* the bridge shut two weeks early, cutting him off from the mainland before anyone could change their minds about anything; a secrecy clause hanging over his name like a hook over a fish.

*What the paper doesn't say:* why a corvette needs his light at all, when his light marks a shoal no naval vessel should come near. Why the closure must precede the transit. Why four drafts, why personal delivery, why a clerk with six years of files and a habit of copies looked at him like a woman handing him something fragile and hoping he wouldn't drop it.

He thought about the word *corvette*. He thought about the coal tallies he'd signed for over the years, the way naval tonnage had crept upward in the requisitions while the ships themselves stayed the same size, as though the Admiralty were buying weight that existed only on paper. He had noticed it the way he noticed everything — silently, precisely, and to no purpose whatsoever, because noticing was free and acting cost everything he had.

Three years ago he had learned the price of acting. Not his own price — Ede had paid hers long before him, and he had watched the bill arrive. A shipping magnate, a courtroom, a testimony given straight and true and on the record, and then the slow strangulation of a family that had spoken when silence was cheaper. Contracts withdrawn. Names blackened in rooms they'd never enter. Ede had died with her spine intact and their savings gone and their daughter estranged, and Mirabel had buried her and drawn the only lesson the evidence supported: *the truth is a luxury good, and we are not rich.*

So he had made himself small. He had kept the light, trimmed the wicks, logged the weather, answered inspections in monosyllables, and let the Admiralty's attention slide off him like rain off slate. Invisibility was not cowardice, he told himself. It was ballast. It kept the boat upright.

He sat in the room with the chair until the fire burned low, and then he got up, took his logbook from its shelf, and opened it to the current page.

*Oct. 28. Supply boat Puffin, annual stores, 12 dz oil (requisition 14). Wind SW fresh, sea moderate. Received official correspondence, acknowledged.*

He wrote *acknowledged* and stopped, pen lifted, and looked at the word.

An exact log, she had said. The only kind that survives.

He did not write what the letter contained. He did not write *Corvus*. He wrote nothing that could be read as knowledge of anything, because the letter itself had ordered him to silence, and obedience — he could tell himself this tonight, and believe it, mostly — was also a form of being invisible.

But underneath the acknowledgment line, in the margin, in the tiny ciphered shorthand he used for fuel counts and wick expenditures, a notation only he could read, he entered two facts and a question:

*Bridge shuts Nov 3. Warship due Nov 14. Why does a corvette need a light on a shoal she should never cross?*

He blotted it, closed the book, and put it back on its shelf between the fuel returns and the old inspection reports, one careful record among many careful records, indistinguishable, unremarkable, safe.

Then he banked the fire, checked that the lamp turned true, and stood a while at the tower window with the wind pressing at the glass, looking down the dark reach toward the harbor lights of a town he would not be able to reach after Friday.

Be invisible, he told himself. Be small. Light the lamp, log the weather, count the oil, and let November pass over this rock like weather. Whatever the Corvus is carrying, it isn't yours. Nothing out there is yours. Yours is the light and the log and the arithmetic, and arithmetic has never yet hanged anybody.

He repeated it to himself going down the stairs, and again climbing into his bunk, and it had the ring of a thing a man says to himself when he already knows how the saying will end.

Four nights later the bridge chains went up, and the mainland lights went out across the water, and Grayharbor Point became a world of one man and one flame.

And on the twelfth night of November, the barometer — which had been falling for two days like a stone down that same well — dropped through the lowest mark on its dial and kept going, and the gale that came out of the southeast was the worst in a decade, and out in the black roaring reach, right where his beam swept faithful every ten seconds over water marked and charted and warned, a ship began to die.
