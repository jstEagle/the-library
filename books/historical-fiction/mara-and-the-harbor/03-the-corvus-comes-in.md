## 3. The Corvus Comes In

The barometer began to fall at noon on the ninth of November, and by four it had fallen so far that Mirabel Lockridge stopped trusting it and went outside to look at the sky with his own eyes.

The sky confirmed everything. It was the color of wet slate, low and bruised, and the gulls had gone inland in a single ragged stream, which was the oldest warning there was. The sea beyond the point had a wrongness to it — a long, heavy swell running out of the southeast, the kind of water that carried bad news from a long way off.

He logged it. *16:40. Barometer 28.1 and falling. Swell increasing from SE. Gulls gone inland.* He wrote it small and neat, because he wrote everything small and neat, because writing things down was the only way he knew to hold the world still.

Then he checked the lamp.

The lamp itself was fine. He had overhauled it twice since October, against his own better judgment, since the Admiralty paid him for maintenance hours and he did not want to look eager. But eagerness and diligence were different animals, and he had never learned to be lazy on purpose. The wicks were trimmed to a hair's width. The lenses were clean. The oil reservoir stood full, and the spare sat beside it, and the second spare behind that, because Grayharbor Point's lamp was old and drank fuel like a dying man drinks broth, and if tonight went the way the sky said it would go, he wanted reserves.

He lit early. Nineteen minutes before sunset, which was not regulation but was not forbidden either, and he told himself — as he always told himself — that it was for the sake of the mechanism, warming the glass slowly so it wouldn't crack when the cold came up off the water.

It was partly true. It was also true that when the light came on, sweeping its slow white arm out across the darkening water, something in his chest unclenched. Three years of lighting it and the feeling had not dulled. Somewhere out there, boats were coming home to that arm of light. He would never meet them. They would never know his name. That was the whole design of the thing, and he had built his life inside that design like a hermit crab inside a borrowed shell.

*Be the light, not the keeper,* Ede used to say, laughing at him. *That's your whole philosophy, Mirrie. Be useful and be nobody.*

She'd meant it as a tease. She had not understood — or she had understood too well, and been sad about it — that after her, being nobody was the only shape left that fit him.

The wind arrived at dusk like something let out of a cage.

By eight o'clock it was blowing hard enough to make the tower hum. By nine it was screaming. He had lived through eleven winters at the point and three more at other stations before that, and this was the worst he had heard. Not gusts — a sustained, flat roar, as though the whole atmosphere had decided to move somewhere else all at once and the point happened to be in the way. Spray came over the lantern gallery in sheets and froze where it landed, building white rime on the mullions until the light had to burn through ice-furred glass.

He logged it every hour anyway. He climbed the ninety-one steps with the logbook under his arm and wrote what he saw, because that was the job: *21:00. Wind SSE, force 10, rising. Sea over shoal buoy, light obscured intermittently by spume.*

That last part bothered him. The shoal lay half a mile offshore, the reason the station existed, a fang of rock that at high water hid two feet beneath the surface and at low water showed black and innocent as a sleeping animal. There was a bell buoy on it, moored each spring and lifted each autumn, and this year — he checked the correspondence to be sure, because he checked everything — the buoy had been lifted late, on the twenty-eighth of October, and the seasonal spar marking the channel had been taken in with it. Standard practice. The harbor traffic was done for the year; nothing but coastal traders and fools moved in November.

But the bell buoy itself, the permanent one, the one that was supposed to stay out all winter — that had been reported fouled in September. He had filed the report himself. *Bell buoy No. 7 fouled, riding badly, may require replacement.* And the reply had come back in October, one line, signed by someone who signed everything with the same impatient flourish: *Noted. Rectification deferred pending budget cycle.*

Deferred pending budget cycle. He thought of those five words while the wind tried to take the roof off, and he did not know yet why they mattered, and that was the worst of it — that he could feel their weight without knowing what they held.

At midnight he went down and made tea he didn't drink and sat in Ede's chair by the east window, the one chair he had never re-covered, never moved, never let anyone sit in, and listened to the storm work on the point. The window flexed in its frame. Somewhere below, the sea was coming apart against the rocks with sounds like artillery.

He should have slept. The regulations said the keeper slept when he could. But sleep had been an unreliable business partner for three years, and tonight it did not come at all, and at ten minutes past two he was standing at the east window with his forehead nearly touching the frozen glass when he saw the lights.

Two lights. Running lights, red and green, low and close together, which meant a vessel, and close meant too close, and the angle —

His mind did the arithmetic before his fear could interfere. The bearing. The distance. The set of the current in a southeasterly gale, which pushed everything toward the point and toward the shoal that guarded it. A ship making for the harbor mouth would pass north of the shoal. This ship was passing south. South of the shoal there was no water deep enough for anything larger than his skiff.

"No," he said aloud, to the glass, to the ship, to whoever was steering her. "No, no, you're wrong, come about—"

The lights grew. They did not turn.

And then his own lamp swept its beam across the water and showed him her, for perhaps two seconds — a steam vessel, black-sided, mid-sized, funnels streaming smoke sideways in the wind, deck cargo lashed under tarpaulins, riding low. Riding very low. Low enough that the seas were coming aboard her in solid water.

Riding low. He noted it, some clerkish corner of his brain noting it even then, noting everything, the way he always did. A ship that low in that sea was carrying too much or pumping badly, and neither explanation was good.

The lights vanished behind the headland of the shoal's outer edge. He counted. Ten seconds. Fifteen. His hand found the windowsill and gripped it.

Twenty seconds.

The sound reached him through the stone of the tower — not a crash, exactly, nothing so clean. A long grinding roar, iron on granite, prolonged, obscene, the sound of a large thing being unmade by a patient older thing. Then the steam whistle, shrieking, stuck open, a scream with no breath behind it that rose and choked and died.

Mirabel was already moving.

He did not remember deciding. Later he would search the memory for the decision and not find it, only the actions, laid out in sequence like the steps of a staircase: logbook down, oilskins on, boots, the signal rocket tube from the store room — no, no time for rockets, rockets were for signaling shore stations and there was no shore station closer than the harbor, twelve miles of drowned road away. Just him. Just the skiff.

He got the skiff out on the lee side of the point, which meant rowing out into the teeth of it first, and the first hundred yards were the hardest physical labor of his life. The boat leapt and fell and the sea hit him like thrown gravel, like fists, like the whole ocean objecting to his presence. Twice he shipped water. Once he lost the oar entirely and recovered it by the leash around his wrist because Ede, God rest her practical soul, had made him rig leashes on both oars the first winter he kept this light, saying, *You'll lose one someday and die of pride, Mirrie, tie them down.*

The lamp swept overhead as he pulled clear of the rocks, and he took his bearing from it without thinking, the way you navigate by the thing you yourself tend, and put his bow across the shoal's southern shoulder into the chaos beyond.

The Corvus — he read her name later, off papers, off testimony; that night she had no name, she was just the wreck — the wreck was breaking up fast. She had struck the shoal square and swung broadside, and the sea was folding her in half with a patience that was worse than violence. Her stern section, where the engines were, still held. Steam poured off her in the wind, torn to rags. Men were moving on her deck, small and frantic, in the strobe of his own light sweeping past every fifteen seconds, showing him the scene and taking it away again, showing and taking, like a cruel lantern show.

There was a boat davit aft. Empty. Someone had gotten away in it, or tried to. There were men in the water. He could hear them — that was the part that would live in him longest, the voices, thin and human and already going strange with cold, calling numbers, calling names, calling God, calling nothing.

He could not take them all. He was one man in fourteen feet of wood, and the arithmetic was brutal and immediate: every minute he spent hunting swimmers was a minute the sea spent on them, and he could maybe pull two, three at most, if the sea allowed, and the sea did not allow things, it permitted them.

A shape caught his eye near the stern, wedged against the wreckage of the rail, clinging to what looked like a hatch cover. Close. Reachable. He pulled for it.

It was a man, half-submerged, black with oil and blood, and when Mirabel got a hand into his collar the man fought him — pure animal reflex, drowning men fight rescue — and then stopped, all at once, the way a clock stops, and went limp, and Mirabel hauled him over the gunwale by main strength with his back screaming and the sea trying to take them both.

The man was burned. Badly. Hands, face, forearms — the skin gone shiny and wrong, the smell of scalded flesh under the oil. An engineer or a stoker, then; fire below decks. He was conscious, barely. His lips were moving.

"Can't hear you!" Mirabel shouted, and bent close, shielding the man's face with his body.

The man's hand came up — burned, shaking, deliberate — and closed on Mirabel's oilskin front with a grip that had nothing left in it but will. He dragged himself up against Mirabel's chest. Strapped across his body, under one ruined arm, was a canvas satchel, oil-stained, sodden, and the man's other hand clamped over it.

"The light." His voice was shredded, barely words. "You're — the light. You lit for us."

"I lit for you," Mirabel said. "I've had it burning since dusk. Hold on."

"You lit—" and something crossed the burned face that Mirabel could not read in the dark and the spray, something between gratitude and grief so vast it looked like anger, and then the man said, "They'll say — listen — they'll say it was the light. They'll say the light failed. You have to — the bag—"

The sea took the next word. Mirabel got him flat in the bottom of the skiff, got his own coat over him, got the satchel out of the water sloshing in the bilge and shoved it under the thwart, and turned back toward the wreck for another run.

The wreck declined to cooperate. In the time it had taken him to pull one man out of the water, the Corvus had finished her argument with the shoal. The bow section slid, sighed, and went under with a rush of foam, and the stern settled deeper, and the men on her deck — he counted five, six, clinging — were suddenly not on a deck but in rigging, in shrouds, and then the mainmast went, and after that the sea made its own arrangements and he could not see individuals anymore, only wreckage, only shapes.

He made two more runs anyway. He pulled a piece of deck planking with a dead man's hand still knotted through the lashing, and he pulled an empty lifebuoy stenciled with letters he couldn't read, and the third time he turned toward the wreck there was nothing there to turn toward except debris spreading wide on the black water, and his own lamp sweeping and sweeping over it, faithful, useless, illuminating the aftermath with the same calm white arm it had extended to warn them in the first place.

*They'll say it was the light.*

He rowed for home with a dying man in his boat and that sentence in his ears.

* * *

Getting him up the rocks and into the tower took forty minutes and cost Mirabel skin he didn't notice losing. The stoker could walk, barely, leaning everything on Mirabel's shoulders, and he walked the way men walk when walking is the last order they intend to obey. At the foot of the stairs he looked up at the spiral of them and laughed once, horribly, a dry cracked sound.

"Ninety-one," Mirabel said. "I counted them my first week. It passes quicker than it looks."

Nothing passed quickly. The man collapsed twice on the stairs and both times got up again, and Mirabel understood without discussing it that getting up again was the entire project now, for both of them, and neither said so.

In the kitchen — the warm room, the room with the stove and the stores and Ede's chair — Mirabel stripped the burned hands as gently as he could and the man bore it without a sound, which frightened Mirabel more than screaming would have. He knew burns. Lighthouse men knew burns the way sailors knew rope. These were bad. Oil-fire burns, steam burns, the deep kind. He dressed them with what he had, which was not much, and put brandy into the man a spoonful at a time because a cupful would kill him.

The man's eyes tracked around the room — the stove, the stores, the logbooks on their shelf, the chair by the east window — and came back to Mirabel's face with terrible focus.

"Name," Mirabel said. "Your name. For the log."

A ghost of a smile. "For the log. Of course. For the log." He coughed, wet and long. "Brand. Elias Brand. Stoker, second class." His eyes closed. Opened. "Keeper. Listen to me. The manifests. In the bag. Coal, cargo, drafts — the true ones. They loaded her past her plates and called it ballast and the surveyor signed whatever they — I carried copies. I carried them because—" Another cough. "My girl. My Nell. If I don't come home she'll get nothing but shame unless someone — the inquiry's bought. It's bought, keeper, it was bought before we sailed. You have to be — you have to exist. Do you understand? Stop being careful. Somebody has to exist."

Mirabel's hands had gone still on the bandage roll.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"Yes, you do." Brand's eyes were fever-bright and utterly lucid, and they held Mirabel the way a cleat holds a line. "I've watched your kind my whole life. The quiet ones. The ones who keep everything running and sign nothing. They'll use you, keeper. They'll hang their sin on your lamp if you let them. You lit for us. Make them write that down."

Outside, the gale screamed on. Inside, the stove ticked. The man's grip on Mirabel's wrist tightened, loosened, tightened.

"Dawn," Brand whispered. "Get me to dawn."

Mirabel got him to dawn. He did it with brandy and bandages and the man's own ferocity, sitting beside him through the black hours while the storm beat on the tower like something demanding entry, and at ten minutes past six, with the light still turning overhead and the eastern sky the color of dirty wool, Elias Brand opened his eyes, found Mirabel's face above him, and said, clearly, conversationally, as though finishing a sentence begun hours ago:

"You lit for us. Write it down."

Then he died, with the satchel under the thwart of the skiff outside and his daughter's name unspoken in the air of the warm room, and Mirabel Lockridge sat alone in Ede's chair with the brandy bottle uncorked and untouched, looking at the canvas bag he had not yet opened, understanding that whatever was inside it, he was already no longer invisible.

He picked up his pen. He opened the logbook to the fresh page. And his hand — his careful, neat, obedient hand — hesitated over the paper for the first time in three years.

*06:20,* he finally wrote. *Survivor rescued from wreck of steam vessel, position off shoal. One man. Name given: Elias Brand.* He stopped. Then, in the same small neat script, because the record was the record and he had never yet lied in it:

*I had the light burning from 17:41. It did not fail.*
