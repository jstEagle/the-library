## 5. A Girl on the Rocks

Three days after Elias Brand died in Mirabel's bed, the tide gave back the first of the Corvus.

Mirabel found it at low water below the tower: a hatch cover, charred along one edge, riding the wrack line like a coffin lid. He turned it over with his boot. The Admiralty broad arrow was still legible beneath the scorch. He stood looking at it for a long time, longer than the thing deserved, because he could not stop doing the arithmetic. A hatch cover floats. A stoker does not. Somewhere down the coast, in the coves and the kelp and the cold green dark, were the rest of them — forty-one men by the manifest's count, forty-one names that would be read aloud at an inquiry as *lost through negligence of the keeper's lamp* or some other sentence written before anyone arrived to argue with it.

He had buried Brand above the tideline, in the thin soil behind the tower where nothing grew but thrift and gulls' quarrels. No clergyman could reach him; the bridge had been closed eleven days, and no boat would cross until spring unless it wanted drowning. Mirabel had said the words himself, the ones he remembered from Ede's funeral, standing hatless in a wind that kept trying to take them from his mouth. He had marked the grave with a length of spar and burned the man's name into it with a hot poker from the stove: *E. BRAND*. It was not much. It was what there was.

The satchel lay under the third flagstone of the hearth, wrapped in oilcloth, wrapped again in his oldest shirt, weighted down beneath a store of lamp oil nobody would think to move. He checked it twice a day now, morning and evening, the way he checked the wicks. That was the trouble with keeping things, he thought. Once you started, you could not stop counting them.

On the fourth day after the burial, he counted something new.

---

He saw her first as a smudge of color against all that gray — gray sea, gray sky, gray shingle, and then a scrap of red moving along the rocks below the old pilot path, deliberate as a needle stitching. He put the glass on her.

A girl. Sixteen, perhaps younger, wearing a man's oilskin coat cut down badly at the shoulders, hood up against the spindrift. She was working the shoreline methodically, the way salvage crews work, the way searchers work: head down, turning stones, lifting weed, looking into every hollow the retreating water had left behind. Looking for something specific. Or someone.

Mirabel's stomach went cold in a way the wind had nothing to do with.

She worked her way north along the point for the better part of an hour while he stood at the gallery rail telling himself she was a fisherman's daughter gathering wrack for the garden beds, telling himself a great many things, none of which he believed. Finally she came to the place where the rocks shelved down toward the shoal — *his* shoal, the one his lamp had swept every ten seconds of every night for three years — and stopped. She stood very still at the edge of the falling water, and then she took something from inside the coat and held it up, and even without the glass he knew the shape of a letter being read.

She read it standing there with the spray breaking over her boots. Then she folded it away inside the coat, over her heart by the motion of it, and began turning stones again.

Brand had spoken once, that last dawn, in a voice like gravel settling: *My girl. Nell. She'll come looking.* Mirabel had taken it for fever-talk, one more weight handed to him by a dying man, and he had carried it anyway, the way you carry everything. Now here was the weight walking upright along his shoreline with red yarn showing at her cuffs.

He went down.

---

She heard him on the shingle and spun, and he saw her take in the tower behind him, the light-room above, the uniform-less man in a keeper's coat coming toward her across the rocks, and he watched her decide — visibly, the calculation running behind her eyes — whether he was danger or answer.

"Are you from the harbor?" she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not; they were raw with cold and salt, chapped to cracking, the nails broken from prying at stones. "The harbormaster's office? Because they told my aunt there'd be a list. Of what came ashore. And nobody — " She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and started over with terrible care. "I'm looking for my father. Elias Brand. He was stoker aboard the Corvus."

"I know," Mirabel said.

Something happened to her face then. It did not crumple; that was the terrible thing. It organized. Every loose part of it drew tight around the question, so that when she asked it, she already knew, and asked anyway, because asking was the only door left open.

"You know," she repeated.

"He came ashore here. Four nights ago. After the wreck." He made himself hold her eyes. He had watched men lie for a living from this tower, shipmasters swearing their tonnage honest, and he knew now that he never wanted to be good at it. "He was hurt. Badly burned. I got him into the tower, but — " His voice did something he hadn't authorized. He waited it out. "He didn't live past dawn. I buried him behind the light. Above the tide mark. You can see the grave from the path."

She didn't cry. He had braced for weeping the way you brace for a wave, and instead she stood absolutely still, and the stillness was worse, because it was a working stillness, a stillness with machinery in it. When she finally moved, she moved toward the tower, past him, up the shingle, with the fixed stride of someone who has been given coordinates and intends to use them.

"Show me," she said.

---

He showed her. The spar with the burnt name. The thin mound. The thrift already creeping back flat over the disturbed earth as if the ground itself were embarrassed.

Nell Brand knelt in the wind and pulled off one glove with her teeth and laid her bare hand flat on the mound, and stayed that way long enough that Mirabel turned his back to give her the privacy of it, which was the only privacy this coast offered anyone.

"He wrote to me," she said at last, not turning. Her voice had gone flat and careful, a ledger-keeper's voice, and something in Mirabel flinched to hear it, it sounded so like his own. "Every fortnight, regular as wages. The last letter came three days before they sailed. He said the coal tallies were wrong. He said they were loading her past her marks and calling it ballast, and that if anything happened he wanted me to have it in writing, dated, so no one could say he'd imagined it after." She stood. She looked at Mirabel with eyes that had done all their crying somewhere ahead of schedule, years ago maybe, or in the nights since the news reached her aunt's house in Millhaven. "So either you've got those papers, keeper, or my father died carrying them into the sea, and either way you're going to tell me which."

*The satchel,* Mirabel thought. *Under the flagstone. Forty-one names and a rigged inquiry and a company's signature on its own arson.* He had spent four days deciding whether the thing was a treasure or a bomb, whether keeping it was duty or suicide, whether burning it would be cowardice or mercy. He had not once considered that it might have an owner.

"There are papers," he said. "His. He gave them to me. I've kept them safe."

"Safe," she said, tasting the word. "Safe from who?"

"That," said Mirabel, "is the question I've been asking since he died."

She studied him for a long moment, this girl in a dead man's coat, wind-flayed and stone-practical, and whatever she found in his face seemed to disappoint her, which he understood; he was disappointed in it too. Then she looked past him at the tower — at the closed season, at the causeway drowned under winter water, at the sixty miles of empty coast between her and anywhere — and he watched her do the same arithmetic he did every autumn, the sums of food and fuel and distance, and arrive at the same unwelcome total.

"The bridge is shut," she said. "Nobody crosses till thaw. The supply boat won't run. There's no road." Not questions. Confirmations. "And the harbormaster's clerk — Vole, she was called — told my aunt the point was uninhabited in winter except for the keeper." Her chin came up. "I'm not asking you to ferry me anywhere. I'm asking you not to make me walk the shore another day looking for a man who's already under this dirt."

"I wasn't going to turn you out," Mirabel said.

"You were thinking about it."

He was. He had been thinking about it the whole climb down from the gallery: the stores calculated for one, the tower's single warm room, the way every additional mouth was a liability and every additional witness a catastrophe given the paper chain currently sleeping under his hearthstone. He had been thinking about it right up until she knelt and put her hand on the grave, and then the thinking had simply stopped, the way a clock stops, and he understood that some part of the decision had been made years ago in a different voice.

"My wife's chair is by the window," he heard himself say. "It's the warmest seat in the room. Nobody's sat in it in three years."

Nell looked at him. For the first time something moved through the organization of her face that was not grief or resolve — something quicker, almost startled, the look of a girl who has prepared for every argument except kindness.

"All right," she said.

That was all. *All right*, in a voice like a signed receipt. She picked up her bundle — one bundle, he noted, everything she owned in the world fitting under one arm, and he thought of Ede saying *we're rich, Miri, look at all these things we can carry* — and followed him up the path to the tower, and did not once look back at the sea.

---

*Nell had expected the keeper's tower to smell of tar and cold iron, and it did, but underneath that it smelled of bread and lamp oil and, strangely, lavender, which nearly undid her more than the grave had.*

*The man — Lockridge, he'd finally said, Mirabel Lockridge, as though the name cost him something — fed her soup without asking whether she wanted any, just set it in front of her the way you'd set feed before a half-frozen animal, and she ate it because refusing would take longer than eating. The soup was barley and salt pork and something sweet she couldn't name, and it was the best thing she had tasted since the telegram, since the letter, since the platform at Millhaven where her aunt had hugged her and whispered* don't, Nellie, let the Admiralty sort it *and Nell had walked out of the house at four in the morning with her father's letters in her coat.*

*The keeper moved around his own kitchen like a guest in it. She noticed that. He knew where everything was — knew it the way she knew her catechism, without looking — but he touched it all lightly, apologetically, as though the room belonged to someone else who might come back and object. When she asked for water he brought it in a cup that matched none of the others, and when she drank from it he watched her with an expression she couldn't read until she realized he was watching her drink from* her *cup. Someone else's cup. Kept for someone.*

*There was a chair by the window. A good chair, worn smooth at the arms, with a cushion that had faded unevenly, darker where a head used to rest. Nobody sat in it. The keeper moved around it the way sailors move around a mast, without ever seeming to see it, and Nell, who had spent sixteen years learning to read rooms because her father was at sea half her life and silence filled the rest, read this one easily: that chair was a shrine, and shrines are not offered to strangers.*

*Except he had offered it. He'd said so on the path, awkward as a boy, and when they came into the warm room he'd hesitated beside it for one full second — she counted — and then stepped past it and dragged a stool from the corner for her instead.*

*Nell took the stool. She ate her soup. And then, because her father had raised her to pay debts promptly, she said:*

*"You lit the lamp for them. The night of the wreck."*

*The keeper went very still. "The log will show it."*

*"The pilot at Millhaven said the shoal was marked. He said anyone who says otherwise is lying or bought." She set down the spoon. "Then why does everyone ashore keep saying the light failed?"*

*"Because it's cheaper than the truth," Mirabel Lockridge said, and then looked startled at himself, as if the sentence had escaped custody.*

*Nell reached into the oilskin coat and brought out the packet of letters, tied with the red yarn from her cuffs, worn soft from handling. She untied it on the table between them, deliberately, an act of parliament. "These are mine," she said. "Dated. In his hand. He wrote me about the loading, weeks before. Whatever you're keeping under your floor — " his face did something complicated and confirming — "it isn't the only evidence anymore. There's me."*

*The keeper looked at the letters. Then at the window, where the light was failing early the way it did in December, and the glass was beginning to need cleaning before lighting-up. She watched him weigh something — she was getting good at reading his weights — and she watched the scale tip.*

*"You'll want the warm chair," he said at last, gruffly, standing. "For the evenings. Reading. It faces the fire and I'm at the desk anyway." And he was gone up the spiral stair to his lamps before she could answer, his boots ringing on the iron treads like a man fleeing a fire he'd started himself.*

*Nell sat a moment in the quiet room. Then she rose, crossed to the window, and settled into the worn-smooth chair that nobody had occupied in three years. It fit her better than it should have. From here she could see the whole gray sweep of the point, the wrack line, the grave behind the tower with its burnt-named spar, and the sea beyond, where forty-one men were waiting for someone to write their names down properly.*

*"All right, Da," she said quietly, to the chair, to the room, to the man clattering about upstairs among his wicks. "We start tomorrow."*

*Above her, faintly through the floorboards, she heard the keeper stop what he was doing — and then, after a moment, go on.*
