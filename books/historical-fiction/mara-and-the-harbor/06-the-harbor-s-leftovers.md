## 6. The Harbor's Leftovers

They came the way water comes through a cracked hull — one at first, a trickle, then all at once.

Mirabel counted them the way he counted everything, because counting was the only prayer he trusted. Four mouths besides his own, as of the third day after Nell Brand arrived on his rocks. Five, if you counted the gull that had taken up residence on the lamp gallery and refused to be moved. He did not count the gull. He counted stores instead: forty-one pounds of salt pork, two barrels of flour gone soft at the edges but sound in the middle, a sack of split peas, twelve tins of condensed milk, and a winter's worth of coal that had been budgeted for one careful man who ate standing up and slept like a man guarding a door.

The arithmetic did not work. Mirabel Lockridge had spent three years making sure every sum in his life came out even, and now the sums were wrong, and the wrongness sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

* * *

Tamsin Vole came first, though she would never admit to coming first.

She arrived at dusk on the second day after the storm eased, rowing the harbormaster's dinghy across the half-mile of gray water with her clerk's coat buttoned to the chin and a carpetbag held dry against her chest like an infant. When Mirabel went down to meet the boat, she did not ask for shelter. She handed him a paper.

"Notice," she said. "The bridge is closed until thaw. Officially. I'm delivering notices."

"You rowed a half mile in open water to deliver me a notice I already knew about?"

"The harbor end burned." She said it flatly, the way clerks say things they have decided not to feel. "My lodging was above it. The fire took the records room too — or it will be recorded that it did. Some things were carried out beforehand." Her eyes went past him, up the tower, then back. "I can sleep in the dinghy if it comes to that. I won't put you out."

It occurred to Mirabel, watching her stand there dripping and dignified, that this woman had been uneasy in September for a reason. That she had looked at the early closure order and the Corvus's routing and seen something in the paperwork that made her copy things by lamplight. He said none of it. He stepped aside from the door.

"There's soup," he said. "It's mostly peas. It's always mostly peas."

She stayed eleven days before anyone else came, and in those eleven days she reorganized his storeroom without asking, alphabetized nothing, and indexed everything. She found his spare ledger — the one he kept in duplicate out of a habit Ede had called paranoia when they were young and had stopped calling anything when they were older — and said only, "Good. Duplicates matter."

He should have asked what duplicates mattered *to*. He didn't. There was a wariness between them, two careful people circling the same fire, each pretending to have arrived there by accident.

* * *

The cook came next, and the cook came loudly.

His name was Ambrose Fenn, and he appeared at low tide hauling a handcart across the exposed shingle with a trunk, two sacks, and a copper pot large enough to bathe a child in. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with cook's burns on both forearms and the aggrieved expression of a man who has been misunderstood by every kitchen he ever worked in.

"They dismissed me," he announced, before he'd gotten both feet onto Mirabel's rock. "The *Maiden's Ransom*. Nine years' service. Dismissed over a stockpot."

"Was the stockpot yours?"

"It was the ship's stockpot, which is precisely the injustice. And the purser shorted my galley accounts and wrote it up as my theft, and no one reads a cook's defense, Mr. Lockridge, no one ever reads a cook's defense." He set down the trunk, surveyed the tower, the causeway, the sea, and sighed like a man surveying a battlefield. "You're the lightkeeper. Everyone knows everyone's business in a closed season. The pilot said you'd take people in."

"The pilot said what?"

But Ambrose was already hauling the copper pot toward the door, talking about salt pork and how a man could do wonders with salt pork if a man was permitted to, and Mirabel understood that he had just been volunteered for something.

The pilot came that same evening, which meant the pilot had sent the cook ahead like a sounding line.

Old Cullis — everyone called him Old Cullis; Mirabel suspected even Cullis had forgotten what stood before the Old — arrived with nothing but a canvas bag, a bottle wrapped in oilcloth, and a way of walking across wet rock that Mirabel had only ever seen in cats. Sixty years on the water, twenty of them, rumor said, running contraband through the coves below the point before he'd gone respectable-ish as a harbor pilot. He looked at Mirabel's skiff tied at its mooring and said, "That boat's got a soft plank. Port side, third from the stern. I'll fix it tomorrow."

"I didn't invite you either."

"No," Cullis agreed comfortably, coming inside. "But you lit the lamp for that ship, and the ship drowned anyway, and there's a girl upstairs eating your supper like it's church. A man who does those things is going to feed people whether he likes it or not. I've seen your type before." He set the bottle on the table. "Brandy. For medicinal purposes. Mine."

Upstairs, Mirabel heard Nell laugh — actually laugh, a short startled sound like something breaking free — and hated, with a clean and total hatred, how much he did not hate any of this.

* * *

Here is what Mirabel would not have said aloud, not under oath, not at gunpoint, not to Ede's ghost if she'd demanded it:

The tower had been quiet for three years in the way that a tomb is quiet, and he had called the quiet peace because the alternative word was unbearable. He had eaten standing. He had spoken to the log. He had kept Ede's chair by the window dusted and unoccupied, because an occupied chair would mean someone living here, and living people left, and leaving was the thing that killed you twice.

Now there were boots in his entryway that weren't his. Now there was a smell of onions, because Ambrose had found the onions and had opinions about them. Now Tamsin sat at his table every evening copying figures in a small precise hand while Cullis told lies about the smuggler years, each lie larger than the last, and Nell — sixteen, hollow-eyed, her father dead in Mirabel's bed and buried above the tideline in ground Mirabel had dug himself — Nell listened with her chin on her fists and corrected the lies with the flat authority of a stoker's daughter who knew exactly how much coal a ship burned.

"That's not how the coves work," she told Cullis on the fourth night. "If you ran brandy through Gull Narrows you'd have grounded on the ebb. Papa said—"

And then she stopped, because Papa said was a door that led down into dark water, and the whole table went still around her.

"Papa said," Mirabel finished quietly, "what?"

Nell looked at him. Something passed between them there at the table — the satchel was hidden beneath the third floorboard of the storeroom, and both of them knew it, and neither of them had said so where the others could hear — and whatever she saw in his face made her decide.

"He said the shoal markers off Grayharbor Point were the best-kept on the coast," she said. "He wrote it. He wrote that if a ship ever hit them, it wouldn't be the light's fault."

Cullis poured brandy into four cups and pushed one to Nell and pretended not to watch her drink it. Tamsin wrote something down. Ambrose announced that grief was best managed with dumplings and that he would require flour.

Mirabel sat in his own kitchen, among strangers, and felt the strangest sensation, which was that the room did not want anything from him except that he stay in it.

* * *

The stores lasted better than the arithmetic promised, and worse than comfort required. Ambrose stretched the salt pork with beans and invented a bread from the soft-edged flour that Nell declared edible and Cullis declared a crime against wheat, and every meal Ambrose cooked, the tower smelled like somewhere people lived rather than somewhere a man endured.

Mirabel caught himself doing the accounting differently. Not *how many days will this last five people* but *Ambrose can make the peas taste like intention. Tamsin drinks her tea scalded and black. Cullis takes his brandy medicinally at hours no physician would endorse. Nell eats slowly, like someone relearning that food keeps coming.*

He caught himself, and he hated it, and he kept doing it.

On the sixth evening, Ede's chair got an occupant. It happened without ceremony: Nell sat down in it with a letter of her father's — she'd been reading through them, one per night, rationing herself — and pulled it close to the window light, and the chair fit her the way it had fit Ede, and Mirabel stood in the doorway with a lamp wick in his hand and could not move for a full minute.

She glanced up. "Is this all right? It faces the water."

"It faces the water," he said. "That's what it's for."

He trimmed the wick and went up to the lamp, and on the stairs he permitted himself one sentence of honesty, logged privately, dated, filed: *I am not drowning. Note the date.*

* * *

The hook came on the ninth night, with the tide.

Cullis woke him past midnight, a hand on his shoulder, and Mirabel came down the stairs with the cold already in him, because old pilots don't wake keepers for weather.

"Boat in the cove," Cullis said. "Dark-running. No lights. Sat out past the point for two hours and now it's feeling along the shore."

"Smugglers?"

"In February? With the season shut?" Cullis shook his head slowly. "Smugglers run cargo. That boat's running a search. They've been over the wreck site twice, and now they're working the shoreline, and there's only one thing a shoreline holds that a boat like that wants." His eyes were steady and old and not frightened, which frightened Mirabel more than fear would have. "They'll come ashore by morning. Question is what we tell them we've got."

Below, in the storeroom, beneath the third floorboard, the satchel lay in the dark with its manifests and its tallies and its proof, and upstairs Nell slept in Ede's chair with her father's letters spread across her knees, and Mirabel stood at the black window and watched the shapeless dark boat feel its way along his rocks, and reached for the first lie of his life before he even knew he was reaching.
