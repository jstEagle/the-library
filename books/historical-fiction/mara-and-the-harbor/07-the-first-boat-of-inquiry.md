## 7. The First Boat of Inquiry

The launch came out of the fog on the fourth day after Elias Brand died, and Mirabel saw it before it saw him.

He was on the gallery with the glass, doing what he did every morning at first light — reading the water the way other men read a newspaper — when the shape resolved itself against the gray: a steam pinnace, Admiralty gray, twin funnel stubs breathing smoke into the murk. It should not have been there. Nothing should have been there. The causeway bridge had been closed eleven days; the supply boat would not come again until thaw; the only vessels that moved in this season were the ones that had to.

And an Admiralty pinnace braving the shoals in the tail of a gale was a vessel that very much had to.

Mirabel lowered the glass and stood for a moment with his hand flat on the cold rail, feeling his own pulse in his palm. *They know.* The thought arrived whole, the way bad news always did, without any of the slow approach he'd have preferred. *They know Brand came ashore, or they suspect it, and they've come to collect him.*

Behind him, down the spiral stair, the tower held its new sounds. Nell was in the kitchen — Ede's old kitchen — coaxing the stove to burn wet coal, and the smell of porridge was climbing the stairs like something alive. Tamsin Vole had arrived two days prior, stranded on the wrong side of the closing like everyone else, and had installed herself at the table by the window with the harbormaster's duplicate ledgers spread around her like a card game she intended to win.

Elias Brand lay in the shed, sewn into sailcloth, because the ground was iron and would stay iron until April. And the satchel lay where it had lain since the night Mirabel carried it up from the rocks: inside the oil store, wrapped in oilcloth, behind the spare Fresnel panels, in a space no inspector had ever looked into because no inspector had ever imagined a keeper who owned anything worth hiding.

*Count what you own,* Ede used to say, back when counting was a game they played on winter nights. *In things that can be carried.*

He counted now. One dead man's papers. One dead man's daughter. One clerk who knew too much about coal tallies. One light log with three years of careful, boring, unimpeachable entries — including the entry for the night of the wreck, wind northwest by west, visibility poor, lamp lit at 4:40 and burning steady, shoal buoy reported intact on the quarterly inspection of October the ninth.

That last one was the thing they'd want. He understood it suddenly, standing in the cold with the pinnace growing larger in the glass. Not just the satchel. The log. A log could be lost. A log could be replaced with a cleaner copy. A log that proved Grayharbor Point had marked the shoal properly on the night the Corvus went down was a log that made the Admiralty's story — *navigational error, master's fault, weather beyond all forecasting* — much harder to tell.

"Mr. Lockridge." Tamsin's voice from below, sharp as a cleat underfoot. "There's a boat."

"I see it."

"They'll want to board."

"Yes," Mirabel said, "they will."

He went down the stairs two at a time, which at fifty-three years old cost him a knee he'd feel by evening, and found the kitchen full of lamplight and waiting faces. Nell stood by the stove with a spoon still in her hand, and her eyes went to his face and read it the way he'd taught himself to read weather — quickly, completely, without flinching.

"It's them," she said. Not a question. "About Papa."

"It's about something," Mirabel said. "Nell — listen to me. You were never here. Do you understand? If anyone asks, this tower has held one man all winter, the same as every winter. You came ashore at—" he cast about, "—at Millhaven, with your aunt. You wrote to me asking if I'd seen your father. I wrote back that I hadn't. That's the whole of it."

"And the satchel?"

"The satchel is mine to worry about."

Her chin came up — Ede's gesture, though she'd never met Ede, and the resemblance hit him like a wave over the bow. "My father died carrying those papers. I'm not going to stand in your kitchen and—"

"Nell." He kept his voice level, the voice he used in gales, the voice that assumed obedience because panic killed faster than water. "If they find those papers on you, they don't take the papers. They take *you*, and then no one ever hears what happened to Elias Brand except the people who made it happen. You staying out of their sight isn't cowardice. It's ammunition staying dry. Can you do that?"

She looked at him for a long three seconds. Then she put the spoon down, took off her apron, folded it over the chair — Ede's chair, he noticed, the one by the window, which she'd claimed without ceremony weeks ago and which he had not objected to, a fact he filed away to be examined some other time — and said, "Where do I go?"

"The lamp room. You're my assistant. You've been my assistant since November. Assistants are invisible; nobody remembers a keeper has one until the pay list comes round." He almost smiled. "Tamsin — the ledgers. Anything with the Corvus in it goes under the floorboard in your room. The rest stays out. Let them read six months of herring landings until their eyes bleed."

"And you?" Tamsin asked. She'd risen from the table, and her hands were flat on the wood, and her sharp eyes were doing arithmetic of her own. "What will you be?"

Mirabel looked at his hands. They were steady. That surprised him. Inside, something was moving fast and cold, but the hands were a keeper's hands, and keepers' hands did not shake while the light was lit.

"I'll be exactly what I've been for three years," he said. "A dull man in a dull tower. Pray God I remember how."

* * *

The pinnace came alongside the landing stage at half past nine, throwing lines to a stage that was glazed with ice, and four men climbed up with the exaggerated care of sailors who did not trust stone. Three were ratings, young, seasick-looking. The fourth was not young. He wore a civilian coat over naval boots, and he had the particular stillness of a man who had spent his life watching other people for a living.

"Keeper Lockridge?" His voice was pleasant. His breath smoked. "Lieutenant-Commander Aldous Vane, retired. I'm attached to the Board of Inquiry into the loss of H.M.S. *Corvus*. May we come in? It's bitter on that stage."

"You may come in as far as the warmth does," Mirabel said, and stepped aside.

He watched himself do it — watched the words leave his mouth shaped and smooth, neither welcoming nor hostile, the words of a man with nothing in his tower worth guarding and nothing in his head worth extracting. And underneath the watching, a second thought, arriving with the clarity of a bell struck underwater:

*I'm lying already. And it doesn't hurt.*

Vane stamped the salt off his boots in the entrance chamber, accepted tea because refusing tea at a lighthouse in February was a cruelty even the Admiralty balked at, and sat at Mirabel's table with his hands wrapped around the cup and his eyes going everywhere at once, gently, the way a searchlight sweeps — never resting long enough to seem like looking.

"A dreadful business," Vane said. "The *Corvus*. You'll have seen it go down, I imagine. From here."

"From here," Mirabel agreed. "I lit for it. Wind northwest by west, visibility poor. My log will show the lamp was burning from 4:40 onward, and the shoal buoy was confirmed on station in October. Whatever else happened that night, the point did its work."

Something flickered across Vane's face — quick, professional, gone. *There,* Mirabel thought. *You wanted to know whether I'd say that. Whether I understood what my log meant. Now you do, and so do I.*

"Yes," Vane said mildly. "The log. That's largely why we've come. The Board requires the original light log for the period of the wreck, plus any correspondence, signals received, weather memoranda — the usual. We're collecting from every station within thirty miles." He produced a paper, official crests, a signature at the bottom belonging to someone important enough to have a flourish. "You'll understand it's a formality."

"Of course."

"We also understand," Vane continued, stirring his tea though there was nothing in it to stir, "that wreckage came ashore here. Flotsam. Possibly — " a small apologetic pause, a man embarrassed by the necessity — "possibly persons. The sea gives things back in strange ways, this time of year. If anything washed up, the Board would need to account for it. Effects, you understand. Personal effects. There are families."

"There are families," Mirabel said.

The room was very quiet. Down the spiral stair, faintly, he could hear Nell in the lamp room, moving with the deliberate noiselessness of someone who had learned silence in a house where silence had once been survival. He thought of Elias Brand in the shed, sewn into his own sailcloth, and of the satchel behind the Fresnel panels, twelve feet and one locked door away from this pleasant man with his stirring spoon.

"One body," Mirabel said. "Washed up the morning after. Dead when I found him. Burned — stoker, by his clothing. I logged it, notified the harbormaster's office by signal flag the same day, and buried him when the ground allowed, which it hasn't yet, so he's in my boat shed awaiting burial. You're welcome to view him. You're welcome to take him. He deserves better than my shed, frankly."

Every word true. That was the trick of it, he realized, and the realization settled over him with something between horror and exhilaration. Every word true, and arranged. The truth was a cargo like any other — you could stow it to list to one side, and a listing ship told whatever story its loading demanded.

Vane set down his cup. "His effects, Keeper? A drowned man often carries things. Papers, sometimes. Stokers aren't usually carrying papers, but this one—" the pause again, delicate as a scalpel, "—this one may have been unusual."

"He had a satchel strap across his chest," Mirabel said. "Empty. Whatever was in it, the sea took, or the rocks did. I searched the shoreline for three days afterward. Boots, hatch covers, a child's doll, forty yards of rope. No papers. You can walk the beach yourself if you like; the tide's been through it twice since."

*A child's doll.* He had not planned to say that. It had simply appeared, and he watched it land — watched Vane's eyes move, involuntarily, toward the window, toward the shore, toward the idea of a girl searching rocks for her father. Watched the lieutenant-commander decide, visibly, that the keeper of Grayharbor Point was a widower alone in a tower who catalogued flotsam and felt sorry for drowned strangers' daughters, and that such a man was not hiding anything, because such a man was not capable of it.

*Three years of making yourself small,* Ede's voice said somewhere in the back of his skull, dry as her humor ever was. *Turns out you built a hiding place big enough for the whole truth.*

"The log, then," Vane said. "We'll take the original. You'll be issued a receipt, and the log returned after the inquiry."

"No."

The word was out before he had decided to say it, and he let it stand there, plain as a rock in a channel.

Vane raised his eyebrows. "No?"

"The log is the property of Trinity House and the record of a working light," Mirabel said. "It doesn't leave this station while the light is manned — that's regulation, Section Eleven, and you'll find it quoted in my own hand on the flyleaf, because I'm the sort of man who quotes regulations on flyleaves. What I can do is make a certified copy, page by page, tonight, signed and witnessed, which is admissible anywhere the original is. Or you can wait for the thaw and the supply boat and write to Trinity House and be told the same thing by someone with a desk." He refilled Vane's cup, unhurried. "Or you can take the original, and when the Board asks why Grayharbor Point's records are incomplete for the quarter, I'll say an officer removed them against regulation, and I'll say it in writing, and I'll sign it. Your choice entirely. I'm a dull man; I follow whichever rule is closest."

He watched Vane weigh it. Watched the arithmetic run behind the pleasant face: a certified copy served the Board's stated purpose; seizing the original served the unstated one but created a witness with a grievance and a paper trail. And this keeper — this grey, tedious, regulation-quoting keeper — had just made himself expensive.

"The copy," Vane said at last. "Tonight, you said."

"Started within the hour. I'll send it down with the pilot boat if one runs, or hold it for the Board's next call. Whichever comes first."

Vane smiled then, fully, and for a moment the pleasantness dropped away like a coat taken off indoors, and what was underneath was not cruel, exactly. It was *patient*. It was the patience of a man who had all spring to come back.

"You're a careful man, Keeper Lockridge."

"I keep a light," Mirabel said. "Careless men drop them."

* * *

They left at noon, taking nothing but Vane's card and leaving nothing but a receipt for a copy not yet made. Mirabel stood on the gallery and watched the pinnace shrink into the fog, and did not go inside until the last smudge of smoke was gone.

Then he went down, and found Tamsin and Nell waiting in the kitchen, and told them all of it — every word exchanged, laid out on the table like charts. Nell's hands were white around a mug she wasn't drinking. Tamsin was writing, fast, recording the interview from his memory with a clerk's precision, dating it, timing it.

"You lied to him," Nell said, when he reached the part about the empty satchel strap.

"I misarranged the truth."

"That's lying."

"Yes," Mirabel said. "It is." He had expected to feel sick. He had expected, all his life, that the first real lie would taste like copper and shame. Instead he felt — awake. Sharper than he had felt in three years. It occurred to him, standing in his own kitchen with the fear still humming in him like a struck wire, that he had spent three years practicing a subtler version of this same skill: disappearing Ede's chair from conversations, disappearing his grief from his letters, disappearing himself from the world's attention. He had simply never turned the craft outward before.

"He'll be back," Tamsin said. She didn't look up from her writing. "Men like that don't lose. They regroup. He believed you today because believing you was cheaper than not. Next time it won't be cheap."

"Then next time we're ready." Mirabel looked at the two of them — the clerk with her ink-stained fingers, the girl with her father's stubborn jaw — and heard himself say, in a voice that surprised him with its steadiness: "Tamsin, I want everything you have on the *Corvus*'s coal and cargo tallies. Everything. Nell — your father's letters, if he wrote any. Tonight, after the log copy. We start building the record tomorrow."

"Why tomorrow?" Nell asked.

"Because tonight I make a certified copy of a log for a man who thinks I'm dull," Mirabel said, "and I intend to be exactly as dull as he believes, right up until the day I'm not."

Above them, faint through the floors, the lamp turned in its bath of mercury, steady as a heartbeat, keeping its own kind of record all night long.
