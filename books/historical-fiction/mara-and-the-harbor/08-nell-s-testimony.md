## 8. Nell's Testimony

The letters lived in the bottom of Nell's carpet bag, wrapped in her mother's old shawl, and she had not been able to open them since the night the harbor master's boat had brought word that the *Corvus* was lost.

Not lost, she corrected herself now, sitting in the keeper's tower with the bag on her knees and the wind worrying at the glass. Not lost. *Scuttled*. That was the word Mr. Lockridge used when he thought she wasn't listening. She was sixteen years old, not deaf.

She had come to Grayharbor Point looking for a body. What she'd found instead was a man who kept his grief folded like linen and a tower full of strangers eating his winter stores, all of them circling some truth too heavy to say aloud in front of her. They thought they were protecting her. Her father had thought the same thing, once, and it had gotten him a stoker's berth on a ship that was meant to die.

Nell unwrapped the shawl.

There were eleven letters. He had written every fortnight since taking the *Corvus* berth in September — Elias Brand was not an educated man but he wrote the way he stoked a furnace, steady and thorough, each letter a small hot mass of news pressed flat under pen. She'd read the first three after the wreck, then stopped, because reading him alive hurt worse than imagining him dead, and she had needed all her strength for the shoreline searches, the morgue lists, the long cold walk down the coast asking harbormasters if they'd seen a man with a burn-scarred left wrist.

Now she made herself read them all, in order, by lamplight, while below her the others argued quietly about coal tallies.

The fourth letter was dated the twelfth of October.

*Dear Nell — We coaled at Millhaven Tuesday and I will tell you what I told your Uncle Rennie, which is that we took on near double what the manifest says. The bosun had us trimming her bow-down and the chief says laughingly we are carrying more iron than the Navy ever gave her. Mr. Corbett the purser has me sign sheets with figures already writ on them. I signed. A man signs or a man walks, and there is no walking from this coast till spring.*

Nell read it twice. Then a third time, slower, moving her lips over the words as if her father were speaking them, because in a way he was.

*Do not think ill of me for signing. But I want it set down somewhere that I knowed, so here it is, set down, in ink, to my own girl who keeps her letters.*

He had known. Weeks before. He had written it down — written it to *her*, in ink, exactly as he'd wanted — and the letter had sat in her carpet bag for two months while she grieved, while the inquiry board met in Millhaven and called it weather, while Mr. Lockridge burned his own log pages rather than hand them to men who'd already decided what happened.

Her hands were shaking. She set the letter on her knee and looked at it without seeing it, and what she felt was not grief. Grief she knew; grief was the tide, it came and went. This was something with teeth.

He had trusted her with it. He had put the truth in an envelope and addressed it to a girl of sixteen in a rented room above a chandler's shop, because there was nowhere else in the whole world it would be safe, and she had let it sit under wool while powerful men sealed their tidy history around his grave.

She gathered the other letters and went downstairs.

* * *

They were all in the kitchen — Mirabel, Tamsin Vole, old Pilot Cregg with his bad leg up on a stool, and Cook hovering at the edge of things the way he did now, guilty as a dog that had chewed a slipper. The table was buried in paper: the light log, its certified copy, the manifests Mirabel had pulled from the satchel, Tamsin's duplicates in her cramped clerk's hand.

Mirabel looked up first. He had a way of looking at her that she was still learning to read — not pity, exactly, though there was some of that, and not fear either, though she'd seen fear in him plenty since the satchel came ashore. It was more like the way sailors watch a change in the sky.

"I need to show you something," she said.

She laid the fourth letter on the table, on top of everything, and smoothed it flat with both hands.

"Read it."

Tamsin read fastest. Nell watched her eyes go down the page and stop, and stop again, and then lift with an expression Nell had never seen on a grown woman — something between awe and hunger.

"Dated the twelfth of October," Tamsin said quietly. "In his own hand. Describing the overloading, the false manifests, the trim of the ship." She looked at Mirabel. "This is corroboration. Independent, dated, pre-wreck corroboration. Do you understand what—"

"I can read," Nell said. "I'm asking whether *you* do."

The room went still. Cook found something to study in the ceiling beams. Cregg's mouth twitched, the ghost of approval.

"Nell," Mirabel began, and his voice had the careful weight he used when he was being kind, which she had decided she hated, "your father wrote that to you. To you. Whatever happens next — the inquiry, the papers, all of it — that letter is yours, and no one—"

"No one what?" She heard her own voice go hard and strange, a voice she didn't recognize, and didn't care for, and didn't stop. "No one should make me look at it? No one should use it? You've had that satchel under your floor for weeks, Mr. Lockridge, and every day you wait, those men get further away with their story. My father died carrying your proof. Don't you dare tell me it's too heavy for his daughter."

"It's not too heavy for anyone," Mirabel said. "That's not—" He stopped. His jaw worked. When he spoke again the carefulness was gone, and what was underneath was rawer and, Nell thought, far more honest. "I watched your father drown. Did they tell you that? Not the board's version. The real one. I rowed out into a sea that should have killed me, and I got to him, and he died on my floor at dawn with his hand on that satchel. And every day since, part of me has been doing sums about how many ways this can go wrong. How many people it can ruin. I have gotten very good, these three years, at keeping things safe by keeping them hidden."

"So hide it better," Nell said. "Hide it where it counts. In the record."

Cregg laughed — one short bark, immediately smothered. Tamsin was writing already, fast, dating the top of a fresh sheet.

"You don't flinch," Mirabel said. It wasn't quite a question.

"My father didn't flinch," Nell said. "He signed false sheets because he had to, and then he wrote the truth to a sixteen-year-old girl because it was the only place he could put it, and then he carried a satchel off a burning ship because somebody ought to survive who knows. I am the only person alive he chose. So yes. I flinch at nothing that gets him his name back."

Mirabel held her gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he did when he entered something in the log — final, witnessed.

"Then we do it properly," he said. "Your statement, in your words, taken down fair by Miss Vole, describing when the letters arrived and how they've been kept. The originals stay with you — always with you — and we work from copies, so no one can take the chain away from you. And when the record goes out, it goes out under your name alongside mine. Brand and Lockridge. If it costs, it costs us both."

"And me," Tamsin said, not looking up from her pen. "And Cregg, if he'll stand to it."

Cregg spat thoughtfully into the stove. "Been ruined before," he said. "Gets easier."

Cook opened his mouth, closed it, and finally said, "I'll mind the door," which from Cook, in that moment, was a kind of oath.

Nell sat down at the table among the papers, in Ede's chair — Mirabel had moved it there weeks ago without a word, and neither of them had ever remarked on it — and began, in a clear voice, to dictate.

*My name is Eleanor Brand. I am sixteen years of age. My father, Elias Brand, stoker aboard HM vessel Corvus, wrote to me on or about the twelfth day of October last...*

Outside, the wind rose. Inside, the lamp turned overhead, and for the first time since the wreck, Nell slept without dreaming of the shore.

* * *

She woke near midnight to voices below — low, urgent, wrong. She came down the stairs with her father's letter in her pocket and found them clustered at the seaward window, Mirabel holding the lamp shutter half-closed, everyone watching the dark water.

A light out past the shoal buoy. Moving. Steady. Deliberate.

"A launch," Mirabel said. "Running dark until now. Coming straight for the point."

Tamsin was already gathering the papers. Nell stood beside the keeper at the window, her hand flat against the cold glass, and understood that whatever they had built tonight at the table, the other side had begun building too — and had started first.
