## 9. Ledgers and Lanterns

The storm took three days to spend itself. On the fourth morning the sky came back the color of dishwater and Mirabel swept the tower stair out of habit before he remembered there were four more pairs of boots to account for now, and that the sand he'd been sweeping alone for three years had been tracked up by a girl who slept in his wife's chair.

He did not mind it. That was the strange part. He kept waiting to mind it.

Tamsin Vole arrived at nine, as arranged the night before, carrying a canvas satchel that she set on the kitchen table with the care of a woman handling something explosive. Which, in a sense, she was. Paper was explosive. He'd learned that from Elias Brand's satchel, or rather from what happened to men who carried it.

"You'll want to lock the door," Tamsin said.

"The door doesn't lock."

"Then you'll want someone watching it." She glanced at the stairwell, where Nell was already descending, hair braided tight, eyes awake in a way that made Mirabel feel like a man who'd been sleeping standing up for years. "Good. The girl watches."

"I can do more than watch," Nell said.

"You can watch first," Mirabel said, and was surprised when she sat down and did it. Obedience, in his experience, came either from fear or from trust, and he hadn't done anything yet to earn the second. But there she was, at the window, counting boats the way he counted everything — methodically, without mercy.

Tamsin opened the satchel.

Inside were ledgers. Not the harbormaster's official books — those lived in Grayharbor proper, behind the harbormaster's own lock, under the harbormaster's own signature. These were duplicates. Fair copies, made in a clerk's hand so precise it looked typeset, each page dated and initialled in the corner with a small *TV* that Mirabel had seen a hundred times over the years on supply receipts and never once thought about.

"How long," he said, "have you been copying?"

"Seven years." She said it the way another woman might say how long have you been keeping this light. As if it were simply the work, and the work was simply done. "When I took the post, my predecessor told me the trick of harbor offices: the real book and the fair book are never quite the same, and your job is to make sure nobody ever sets them side by side. I thought about that for a week. Then I started setting them side by side."

She laid three volumes on the table like a dealer laying cards.

"The Corvus took on coal at Grayharbor on the ninth of October. The official manifest says forty tons." She turned the first ledger around so he could read it. Her finger stopped beneath an entry. Forty tons, signed by the Corvus's second officer, countersigned by the harbormaster. "The coal merchant's tally book says fifty-eight. I copy his tallies too. Nobody ever asks the coal man what he sold."

Mirabel read the line twice. Fifty-eight tons into a hull rated for forty-two. He felt the number settle into him the way ballast settles into a boat — low, and heavy, and necessary.

"They overloaded her by sixteen tons."

"They overloaded her by exactly enough to make her unseaworthy in weather they knew was coming, and exactly enough under the insurance valuation to make her loss worth four thousand pounds more than her salvage." Tamsin said it flatly, without drama, because the numbers did the drama for her. "The Corvus was old. The owners had been trying to sell her for two years. There's a broker's circular — I copied that too — offering her 'as is, hull tired, machinery sound.' No buyers at any price. Then suddenly she's insured for eleven thousand and sailing in November."

"And the inquiry—"

"Is already written." She produced a single loose sheet, folded in quarters. "Not officially. Officially the commissioners haven't convened. But the harbormaster received instructions last month regarding 'the anticipated findings,' and he received them from London, not from the wreck site. You don't anticipate findings, Mr. Lockridge. You write them first and hold the hearing afterward to give them a date."

Nell had turned from the window. "My father said they'd blame the stokers. Said they'd say the firemen fed the boilers wrong, let pressure build." Her voice was level but her hands weren't; she'd laced her fingers together on the tabletop and the knuckles had gone white. "There were eleven stokers aboard. Eleven men who can't testify."

"Ten," Mirabel said quietly. "Elias testified. To us."

"To one dying man and a lampkeeper," Nell said. "That's not testimony. That's a story."

"It becomes testimony when it's attached to things that can't tell stories." Mirabel pulled his own logbook across the table — the light log, the one he'd kept for six years, in columns ruled straight enough to satisfy a schoolmistress. "This is the thing that can't lie. Every night, what was lit, when it was lit, what weather it burned through. Here—" his finger found the entry for the night of the wreck, and he heard his own voice go strange, formal, like a man reading charges, "—November the third. Lamp lit at sundown, 4:41. Wind rising northeast, freshening to gale by eight bells. Shoal light — the green sector over Marrow Shoal — burning steady. Visibility: clear until midnight, then rain squalls intermittent. The shoal was marked. It was marked all night. I trimmed that wick myself at ten o'clock because the wind was guttering it."

"So a competent court reads that and asks why a marked shoal ate a ship," Tamsin said.

"A competent court would. This won't be a competent court."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "Which is why we're not building a case for a court. We're building a case for a journalist. Courts can be leaned on. Print cannot — not once it's out. Ink is the only thing in this country harder than the Admiralty."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the shape of the thing assembled itself the way a ship's frame does — not planks but a logic, ribs of paper bent to a common curve.

Manifest against coal tally. Coal tally against the broker's circular. The broker's circular against the insurance schedule, which Tamsin had copied from a file the harbormaster left open on his desk every Thursday while he went to lunch, because some men's habits were gifts to whoever watched long enough. And threaded through all of it, the spine: Elias Brand's own papers from the satchel. The internal loading plan. A letter, unsigned but initialed, instructing the master to take on "full measure" regardless of trim. And a note in Brand's hand — laborer's script, blunt as a hammer — recording the depth of water in the bilge on the second day out, and the order he'd received to pump and say nothing.

"He wrote everything down," Nell said. She'd taken over the sorting an hour ago, and no one had told her to and no one told her to stop. "Coal dust in the stokehold, the pumps, the master drinking, the second officer crying on deck the night before. Papa kept a diary the way you keep a log, Mr. Lockridge. He didn't know he was writing evidence. He thought he was writing so he wouldn't go mad."

"That's what logs are for," Mirabel said.

It came out of him before he could weigh it, and Tamsin looked at him across the table — a look that lasted perhaps two seconds longer than looks between colleagues last. Two careful people, recognizing the mechanism in each other. He understood then that she had spent seven years being invisible in her own way: the clerk nobody noticed, the small sharp woman with the excellent handwriting, filing truth in triplicate where no one would ever ask for it. They had both been keeping records against the day the record would matter. Neither of them had believed the day would come.

"Now the hard part," Tamsin said. "Provenance. Anyone can forge papers. Quill-Harrow's people will say we forged everything — the dead stoker conveniently leaves his evidence with the one witness, the clerk conveniently keeps duplicates, it's almost too tidy. So every document needs a chain. Where it came from, whose hands touched it, who can swear to it besides us." She tapped Brand's satchel. "Him, we've lost. His daughter swears to his handwriting—"

"I can swear to it," Nell said. "I'd swear it on anything."

"—which helps and isn't enough. What we need is corroboration from outside this tower. Someone the Admiralty can't dismiss as grief or ambition." She paused, and Mirabel saw her choose, deliberately, to say the next part. "Or as a woman and a girl keeping a grudge."

"The pilot," Mirabel said.

"Pilot Sorrel," Tamsin agreed. "He ran contraband through these waters for twenty years. He knows every load that ever came into this harbor unofficially. If the Corvus took on weight beyond her manifest, some of it might have gone over the side of a lighter he piloted. And more importantly — he's a man, he's local, he's got nothing to gain, and the Admiralty already hates him. Perfect witness. Disgrace is armor, if you use it right."

"I'll talk to him," Mirabel said.

"You'll feed him first," Nell said. "He's been eating like a gull since he came. Everything fast and suspicious."

And that was the other thing Mirabel was learning not to hate: being corrected in his own kitchen by a sixteen-year-old, and finding that the correction was right.

* * *

Old Sorrel came at slack water, when the tide stood still and even a guilty conscience could rest. Mirabel found him on the rocks below the tower, mending a net he had no right to and no intention of using, hands moving with the blind fluency of sixty years.

"They want me to swear to paper," Sorrel said, before Mirabel had finished climbing down. News traveled through a trapped household faster than through a telegraph office. "Paper's a coward's weapon, boy. A knife, you know who's holding it."

"A knife only kills one man at a time. This kills a lie."

Sorrel snorted. But he rolled the net, and he listened, and when Mirabel laid the coal tally in front of him — fifty-eight tons, the merchant's figures — the old man's eyes did something complicated.

"Fifty-eight," he repeated. "Aye. I brought the last twelve out myself, night launch, no lights. Paid double and told it was machinery spares." He spat thoughtfully into the tide pool. "Machinery don't weigh twelve ton. I asked no questions because questions cost teeth in my trade. But I'll sign your paper. I'll swear it before God and the magistrate both, if the magistrate can find his way off his own dock." He squinted up at Mirabel. "You understand what you're doing, keeper? You're ending yourself. Quiet men who speak — they don't get forgiven. Loud men are expected. Quiet men are *remembered.*"

"I've been remembering other people's wrecks for six years," Mirabel said. "I'd like to be present for one honest sinking of my own."

Sorrel laughed, a bark like a seal, and clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him.

* * *

In the afternoons, the lessons.

"If it comes to a boat," Sorrel said, spreading a chart on the kitchen table and weighting its corners with cups, salt dishes, Nell's thimble, "you don't go round the point. The point is where they'll watch. You go *through*." His cracked thumbnail traced a line that made Mirabel's stomach tighten just looking at it. "Marrow Shoal has a gut through its middle — six feet at low water, nine at flood, and the channel's the width of a coffin and curves twice. Every smuggler on this coast ran it drunk, which tells you it can be run sober. Then here—" the nail moved north along the coast, "—the Coves. Three of them. Gull, Widow, and Sinner. Sinner's the deep one; you can lie a boat there with a tarpaulin over her and God himself couldn't find you without falling into you."

"And the Admiralty charts?"

"Show rock. All of it. Solid rock from Marrow to Sinner." Sorrel smiled with most of his teeth. "Because I drew the survey party around the safe water myself, thirty years ago, and they thanked me for my trouble. Best map is a lie the enemy believes. Second best is a lie you remember correctly."

Tamsin copied the route three times — one for the tower, one for her person, one sewn into the lining of Nell's coat, over Nell's flat objection that she wasn't a child to be outfitted. "No," Tamsin agreed. "You're a courier to be outfitted. It's better."

They drilled it. Mirabel hated drills and loved arithmetic, and Sorrel's coves were pure arithmetic: bearings, soundings, tide tables, minutes. Nine feet at half-flood means entering the gut no earlier than such-and-such an hour; the current runs two knots against you on the ebb, so the crossing window is ninety minutes wide and closes like a trap. Mirabel worked the sums until he could do them with his eyes shut, and then, lying awake in the narrow bed under the lamp room, he did them with his eyes shut anyway.

Hope, he discovered, felt like this. Not warmth. Arithmetic. A chain of small certainties, each one checked, each one initialled, leading somewhere no one had promised you'd arrive.

* * *

One evening near the end of the week, Tamsin stayed late over the copies, and Nell had gone up to sleep, and the stove ticked, and neither of them spoke for a long time. It was the particular silence of two people who have run out of work and not out of reasons to sit together.

"My husband kept duplicates too," Tamsin said at last, to the table. "Fishing manifests, Grayharbor fleet. He caught a trawler taking undersized catch in the spawning grounds and reported it, and the owner broke him. Not loudly. Quietly — licenses delayed, berths withdrawn, until he crewed on nothing and drank on what little there was." She turned her tea cup a quarter-turn, precisely, the way she did everything. "I started copying the ledgers the winter after he died. I told myself it was spite. It took me years to admit it was hope. Spite burns hot and fast. Hope keeps a wick trimmed for seven years in a drawer."

Mirabel thought of Ede's testimony, of the magnate's lawyers, of the slow quiet ruin that had followed, and of the chair by the window that held a girl now instead of a ghost. He thought of what he'd been telling himself for three years — that silence was survival — and heard, underneath it, the newer arithmetic: survival for what?

"Tamsin," he said. "If this goes wrong—"

"When it goes wrong," she said. "Plans that can go right can also go wrong. Symmetry."

"—when it goes wrong, you should be able to say you were coerced. Say I forced the duplicates on you. Say the keeper bullied his clerk. They'll believe it of a man my size."

She looked at him then, fully, and there was no softness in it and no apology either.

"Mr. Lockridge. I have waited seven years for someone with the nerve to bully me. Do not take it away now."

He laughed — actually laughed, a short rusty sound that startled them both — and she almost smiled, which on Tamsin Vole was roughly equivalent to another woman's embrace.

* * *

It was near midnight when Nell came down the stairs without her boots, silent as her father's trade, and stood in the kitchen doorway holding a piece of paper.

"You should see this," she said. "Before anyone sleeps."

It was one of Elias's letters — the ones she'd been reading through in the evenings, building her own chain, her own provenance of grief. She laid it beside the coal tally. "Read the date. Then read the fourth line."

Mirabel read. Dated the eleventh of October — two days after the coal came aboard. *They loaded us past the mark, Nell-girl, and the master says say nothing and I say nothing because a stoker's word weighs less than coal dust, but I'm writing it down so somebody someday knows I knew.*

"He wrote it before the wreck," Nell said. "Weeks before. Sent it through the normal post. Which means—"

"Which means there may be more of them," Tamsin breathed. "Letters. In Grayharbor. In a dead-letter box, a lodging house, a cousin's drawer. Independent corroboration in his own hand, posted before any of us knew there was a story. Un-forgeable. Dated." She was already pulling a fresh sheet toward her, pen moving. "We need to know everyone he wrote to. Nell — names, addresses, every person he mentioned in every letter. Tonight if you can bear it."

"I can bear it," Nell said, and sat down, and began.

Mirabel banked the stove and climbed to check the lamp, because the lamp did not care what hour history kept. From the gallery he could see the whole dark sweep of the harbor, the black line of the causeway bridge under its snow, the far shore where the newspaper town slept and the mainland world went on believing its tidy version. Somewhere out in that dark, he thought, were letters in a dead man's hand, sitting patient in drawers, waiting — as all true things wait — for someone to come collect them.

Below him, lamplight in the kitchen windows: two heads bent over paper, a girl's quick hand, a clerk's exact one.

He was still looking at it, still letting the sight do its quiet work on him, when he saw the light.

Out past the point — not a boat's riding lamp, not a fisherman's flare. A steady white light, high-mounted, moving slowly along the coast road on the far side of the estuary where no coach ran at this hour and no fisherman owned a carriage lamp. It paused, opposite the tower. Held there a long moment, deliberate as a signature.

Then it went on, northward, toward Grayharbor, and the dark closed behind it like water over a dropped stone.

Mirabel stood in the cold with his hand on the rail and did the only arithmetic available: someone had come to look at them tonight. And someone who looks, he thought, comes back.
