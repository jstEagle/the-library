## 10. The Historian Arrives Early

The boat came on a Tuesday, in the flat gray hush between storms, when the sea lay still as poured pewter and the causeway stood under two feet of old snow. Mirabel saw it first from the gallery — a longboat, oars shipped, running under a small lug sail with the Admiralty's white ensign at the gaff. It came out of the fog bank to the north the way a thought comes out of sleep: gradually, then all at once, and unwelcome.

He counted. One man at the tiller. Two at the mast. A third seated amidships with his hands folded in his lap like a man riding a coach, not a boat. Even at that distance there was something wrong about him — something too still, too neat, in a world of salt-stiff wool and chapped hands.

Mirabel went down the stairs two at a time, which he had not done since his knee went bad in the autumn, and found the household already assembling in the kitchen the way iron filings assemble around a magnet. Tamsin stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the boat come on. Nell was on the stairs, halfway between hiding and running. The pilot, old Cregg, had his pipe going and his eyes half shut, which was how he listened.

"Admiralty," Mirabel said.

"I see that," Tamsin said.

"Three of them."

"I can count, Mr. Lockridge."

Cregg opened one eye. "That'll be the historian," he said. "Word came down the pilot's grapevine a fortnight back. A gentleman. Comes before the thaw, they said. Before anybody else can get across." He tapped his pipe out against the stove leg. "They're not sending a gentleman to recover a body, keeper. Bodies don't need gentlemen."

Mirabel felt the cold go into him the way it does through boot soles — not all at once, but rising, joint by joint. "Where is he?" he asked, and didn't say the name, and didn't have to. The satchel was where it had lived for six weeks now, wrapped in oilcloth, inside the false bottom of the coal bin, under a layer of genuine coal that Nell had insisted on, because she said a lie holds better if it's heavy.

"Where it's always been," Tamsin said. "And before you ask — yes, I checked this morning. Yes, it's undisturbed. No, the frost hasn't cracked the bin."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to ask."

The boat scraped the shingle below the tower. The still man stepped out without waiting for a hand, and Mirabel watched him stand a moment on the beach, taking off one glove and then the other, looking up at the light with an expression Mirabel could not read at that distance but recognized anyway, because he had seen it once before, on the face of the surveyor who had come to value Ede's mother's house. It was the look of a man pricing something.

* * *

Mr. Quill-Harrow came up the path with snow to his ankles and did not once look down at his boots. He was perhaps fifty, narrow as a ledger spine, with gray hair parted with a ruler's precision and a coat that had been cut for a man who never intended to haul anything heavier than a fountain pen. He carried a leather case under one arm and a tin of tea leaves in his free hand, and when Mirabel met him at the door the man presented the tea first, before his credentials, as though hospitality were a lever and he had found the end of it.

"Mr. Lockridge. Gideon Crane's office sends its compliments — no, forgive me, the Admiralty's Office of Records and Inquiry sends its compliments, and I am Mr. Quill-Harrow, chronicler attached to the inquiry concerning the loss of H.M.S. Corvus." He said it the way other men say good morning. "I understand the bridge is closed. I took the liberty of coming by water. I hope the imposition is forgivable."

"The season's closed," Mirabel said. "There's no crossing."

"Which is precisely why I came now." Quill-Harrow smiled. It was a good smile. It had been maintained the way a good clock is maintained. "Everything is easier before the thaw, Mr. Lockridge. The ice keeps the roads quiet and the record clean. May I come in? It is bitter on your step, and I promise you I drink less tea than I bring."

Mirabel stood aside. He was aware, with a clarity that felt almost like leaving his body, of the kitchen behind him — of Nell's boots drying by the stove, of Tamsin's duplicate ledger closed and shoved under the bread box not four hours ago, of the smell of the pilot's pipe soaked into the very beams. A house that had been empty for three years now smelled lived-in, and he could not decide whether that was a defense or a confession.

"Mind the third stair," he said. "It talks."

"Everything talks," Quill-Harrow said pleasantly. "That is rather my trade."

* * *

They sat at the kitchen table, because the kitchen was the only room with a chair that could bear a stranger's weight and both of them knew it. Tamsin poured the tea — his tea, the gift, and Mirabel noted that she chose to serve it, which was either surrender or theater, and with Tamsin it was always theater. Nell was sent to tend the lamp room, which was a fiction so thin it barely qualified as cloth; the lamp was trimmed and would be until dusk. She went anyway, and her boots went down the stairs too slowly, and Quill-Harrow listened to every one of them the way a man counts coins dropped into a till.

"You keep a remarkable log," Quill-Harrow said, when the tea had been poured and praised. "I've read the abstracts. The Office sends the light logs of all stations on a marked shoal, you understand, when there's a loss. Yours is — I want to say beautiful, but that would be unprofessional. Yours is *exact*. Weather, visibility, lamp character, the minute of every alteration. November the ninth, the gale. You logged the shoal light burning at full character through six hours of the worst visibility on the coast. You logged your own attempts to keep the glass clear. You logged the wreck itself at 11:41, with the bearing." He opened his leather case and produced — not the log, but a fair copy, in a clerk's hand, and laid it on the table between them like a card turned face up. "You lit for them, Mr. Lockridge. The record is unimpeachable."

"Then there's nothing to ask me."

"On the contrary. There is everything to ask you, and the fact that there is nothing wrong with your record is the first thing." Quill-Harrow folded his hands. He had not yet drunk his tea. Mirabel suspected he would not drink it at all; the tea was an instrument, not a beverage. "The Corvus was lost with forty-one souls. The inquiry will establish that she struck the Grayharbor shoal in weather no vessel could survive, that the light was properly maintained, that the keeper performed his duty with distinction. That is the history. It is a good history. It is nearly a true one."

"Nearly," Mirabel said.

"History is always nearly true. The art is in choosing which nearly." Quill-Harrow's eyes came up, and for the first time they were not polite at all. They were patient the way a fisherman is patient — the patience of a man who has already baited the hook and is willing to sit all day. "There was a stoker aboard her. Elias Brand. He was pulled from the water near this station by an unidentified boat, and he died of his burns before he could be conveyed anywhere. The Office has been unable to locate his effects. A satchel, we believe. He was seen with it on the tender at Portsmouth. It was not recovered from the wreck site. It was not found on the beach." He paused, delicately, the way a man steps over a threshold. "It was not found anywhere."

"Men drown with things," Mirabel said. "The sea takes what it likes."

"The sea does. But the sea did not take this." Quill-Harrow turned the fair copy of the log toward himself and smoothed it flat with one white hand. "I will tell you what I know, Mr. Lockridge, because I find that people lie less when they understand how little lying will buy them. I know a boat put off from this station into that gale. I know a man was carried up this path before dawn. I know he was buried — there is a grave behind your tower with no name cut in the stone, and I am told the mason on the mainland cut no stone for anyone this winter. I know that within a week of the burial, a girl of about sixteen arrived on the point, though the bridge was closed and no crossing was recorded. And I know that this station, which for three years has drawn its stores with the regularity of a tide table, drew double coal in December." He let that sit. "You have been careful, Mr. Lockridge. You have been careful for a very long time. But care leaves arithmetic, and arithmetic is my native language."

Mirabel looked at his own hands around the cup. They were steady. He was faintly surprised by that.

"I pulled a man out of the water," he said. "He was burned. I did what I could. He died. I buried him. He had nothing on him but what he wore."

"Nothing in his hands?"

"Nothing in his hands."

Quill-Harrow nodded slowly, and Mirabel understood, with a chill that had nothing to do with the kitchen's draft, that the man had expected exactly this answer, had perhaps rehearsed it, had come across the ice to hear it spoken aloud so that he could write down the date and the hour of the first lie. The interview was not an interview. It was a deposition being taken in reverse.

"Then I will ask you the question I came to ask," Quill-Harrow said, "and I will ask it plainly, because plainly is all I have. When the bridge opens in April, the inquiry will sit in Grayharbor town for eleven days. On the twelfth day the record will be sealed and the history will be published, and it will say what I said: weather, shoal, light properly kept, no fault anywhere but heaven's. Forty-one souls, Mr. Lockridge, and no one to blame but the sea. That is the history unless something should surface before the sealing. Something with signatures on it. Something in a clerk's hand. Something that a careful man — a man who keeps exact logs, a man who understands that the record is the only thing that outlives us — might have found himself unable to destroy." He stood, and took up his case, and buttoned his coat with small precise movements. "I am not a threatening man. Threats are for people who have run out of patience, and I have a great deal of patience. I am simply a man who knows where every nearly in this history lives, and I have come to tell you that I will be back before the thaw. Perhaps more than once. And that when the historians arrive in force in April, they will bring shovels."

He paused at the door. Outside, the gray light had begun to fail, and the first flakes of the evening's snow were coming down with the flat, businesslike steadiness of a season that meant to stay.

"Your tea is very good, Mr. Lockridge," he said. "You may tell your clerk she pours like a woman with something to hide, which is a compliment in my trade. And the girl —" he looked out at the tower, up toward the lamp room, where a small silhouette stood against the darkening glass — "the girl has her father's hands. I knew him slightly. A good stoker. He kept the fires exact." He settled his hat. "Exact men are the hardest to buy and the easiest to break. Do think about which you'd rather be. I'll see myself down the path. Your stair talks, you said. I'll mind it."

The door closed behind him with the softness of a page being turned.

* * *

Mirabel stood in the kitchen for a long time before he moved. The fire ticked. The tea went cold in the cups. Somewhere above him, Nell's boots crossed the lamp room floor, and he listened to them the way a drowning man listens to a bell.

Tamsin spoke first. She had not sat down through the whole of it; she stood at the window with her back very straight, watching the longboat pull away into the fog it had come out of.

"He knew about the coal," she said.

"He knew about the coal."

"He knew about the grave. He knew about Nell. He knew about the boat." She turned. Her face was the color of the snow outside, and her voice was level, which Mirabel had learned was how Tamsin sounded when she was most afraid. "Mirabel. He didn't come to find out if we have it. He came to tell us that he knows we have it, and to tell us his deadline. Eleven days. Sealed on the twelfth." She laughed, once, without any humor in it at all. "He gave us the date. Why would a man give us the date?"

Mirabel looked at the fair copy of his own log still lying on the table, face up, exact, unimpeachable. He looked at the cold tea. He thought about the word the man had used — *shovels* — and about the false bottom of the coal bin, and about how a false bottom is only as good as the day someone decides to look.

"Because he wants us to run," he said slowly. "A man who runs is a man who can be followed. And a man who runs with papers —" He stopped. The arithmetic finished itself in his head, cold and clean as his own light log. "Or because he wants us to think he's given us time, so we wait, so we sit on it until the thaw like sensible people, and then the historians arrive with the shovels and the record seals and it's over before we've moved a foot."

"Which is it?"

"I don't know." He picked up the fair copy and read his own handwriting as if it belonged to a stranger. "That's what frightens me, Tamsin. I don't know. Three years I've known every current on this coast, every shoal, every hour the fog comes in. And I can't read that man at all."

Above them, the boots stopped crossing. Then came the sound of feet on the stairs, fast, and Nell came into the kitchen with the wind still on her and her eyes bright and hard as the sea under ice.

"He's wrong about one thing," she said.

Mirabel looked up. "What's that?"

"He said exact men are easy to break." She put her hand flat on the table, over the fair copy of the log, over her father's name in the abstract of crew. "He's never met a girl who keeps accounts."

Tamsin and Mirabel looked at each other across the cold tea, and Mirabel understood that the race he had been pretending was not a race had already been run halfway to its finish while he was trimming wicks and minding his own small life. The thaw was coming. The historians were coming. And somewhere under a layer of honest coal, the truth sat in the dark, waiting to find out what kind of men — and what kind of girl — would get to it first.
