## 11. What Ede Would Say

The storm had spent itself by morning, and what remained was the kind of silence that made a man hear his own thoughts too clearly.

Mirabel stood at the north window with the log book under his arm and did not write anything down. Below him the tower was full — Nell asleep in the room that had been the oil store, Tamsin's lamp still burning in the gallery where she worked over her duplicates, the pilot snoring like surf in the corner by the stairs, Cook clattering pans in the kitchen because Cook could not be still without his hands shaking. The tower was full and Mirabel stood alone at the window anyway, because there were things a man could not say to any of them.

He said them instead to a chair.

It sat where it had always sat, angled to catch the light from the east glass, and it was empty, and it had been empty for three years and four months, and he had never once moved it to another wall. Visitors — the supply boat men, the clerk on her first rounds, the boy who'd come once to sweep the chimney — all of them looked at the chair and looked away again quickly, the way people look at a grave that hasn't been kept up, or one that has been kept up too well.

"Ede," he said, to no one. "They're going to take it."

He didn't have to explain. He never had to explain to her. That had been the whole architecture of their marriage — two people who kept accounts so carefully they could read each other's ledgers at a glance.

*The Corvus,* she would have said. *You mean the truth.*

"I mean my pension," he said out loud, and heard how small it sounded in the stone room. "I mean this post. I mean staying alive."

*Alive,* she would have said, and he could hear the exact weight she'd put on that word, the way she'd put it the last year of her life when the doctors used it about her. *Is that what you call it?*

He set the log book on the sill and did not open it. Downstairs, Cook dropped something metal and swore with feeling, and the pilot's snoring hitched and resumed, and the tower went on being a household around him while he stood in front of an empty chair having an argument with a dead woman.

He was losing the argument. He had been losing it for eleven days, since Elias Brand died on his floor with a satchel pressed into his hands and a name half-spoken — *my girl, tell my girl* — and Mirabel had promised nothing, which was its own kind of lie.

"You'd have opened the satchel the first night," he told the chair.

*The first hour.*

"And then what? You'd have marched to the mainland with papers nobody wanted and come home to find us turned out of the light. I remember how that goes. I *lived* how that goes." His voice came up harder than he meant. "Twenty-two years old and you stood up in a hearing room full of men in good coats and told them Aldous Vane's ships were rotten, and you were right, you were right about every plank of it, and what did being right buy? It bought us Grayharbor. It bought me learning to keep my head down so well that twenty years later I can watch a ship burn on my own shoal and think first about whether the Admiralty will question my log."

The chair said nothing. The chair always said nothing. That was the cruelty of it, and the mercy.

* * *

She had been twenty-two and certain, and he had loved her for it before he understood what certainty cost.

That was the memory that came now, unbidden, the way the bad ones did when the wind dropped: the spring of '71, the hearing at Merrowport, Ede in her one good dress with ink still on her fingers from copying manifests late into the night. She'd worked as a tally clerk for Vane & Sons for two years. She'd found the pattern in the numbers the way she found patterns in everything — the same hulls surveyed and passed, the same surveyor's initials, the same ships going down insured to triple their worth while the crews' wages were docked for lost gear.

"They'll laugh you out of the room," Mirabel had told her the night before. He'd been nineteen and already careful. Already practicing smallness, though he hadn't had a name for it yet.

"Probably," Ede said. She was mending a net by candlelight, and she didn't look up. "And then they'll fix it."

"What makes you think that?"

"Because they'll know someone's watching." Now she looked up, and her eyes were the gray-green of harbor water over sand, and she smiled the smile that had undone him completely at sixteen and was undoing him still. "Mira. A light doesn't stop the rocks from being there. It just means the rocks can't pretend they aren't."

He had not known then that she was describing her own death in advance. Not the dying — she'd die twelve years later, slow, in the bed under this very window, of nothing anyone could name — but the other thing. The part where a woman stands in the dark and shows everyone where the rocks are, and the rocks do not move, and some of the ships keep steering for them anyway.

She testified. She was laughed at, then questioned, then accused — first of incompetence, then of theft, then of being paid by Vane's rival, though no rival existed. Her testimony was entered into the record and then buried under three hundred pages of procedural objection. The inquiry cleared Vane & Sons. And within the month, Ede Lockridge was out of work in every port on the coast, because Vane's cousins sat on every shipping board from here to the capital, and her name traveled faster than she did.

They came to Grayharbor because it was the cheapest post and the farthest from anything. She took the keeper's assistant job that should have been his alone, and learned the lamps faster than he had, and for twelve years she kept the books of Grayharbor Point in a hand so exact that inspectors copied her formats into their own manuals without ever saying whose they'd been.

She never said she regretted it. Not once, not even at the end, when the coughing was bad and the money was worse and Mirabel sat by the bed doing sums he couldn't make come out.

But she'd said a thing, near the end, that he had spent three years pretending not to understand.

* * *

It had been October. She'd known by then — she'd stopped pretending otherwise before he had, because she was better at facing numbers than he would ever be. The window was open a crack because she wanted the smell of the sea even in the cold, and her hands on the blanket were thin as gull wings.

"You're angry at me," she'd said. Not asking.

"I'm not angry at you."

"You've been angry at me for eleven years. Since Merrowport. You're kind about it, Mira, you're kind the way weather is kind, slowly, but you've been angry since the day we got here and unpacked your father's clock and I saw you put it face to the wall."

He had put the clock face to the wall because its ticking sounded like hearings, like clerks reading objections aloud. He hadn't known she'd noticed. He hadn't known she noticed most things.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever it is. Say it while I can answer."

And because she was dying, and because she had asked, he said it. All of it. That her courage had cost them everything. That he had spent eleven years paying her debt — the poverty, the exile, the way doors closed when their surname came up, the way he had learned to make himself so quiet, so exact, so harmless that the Admiralty forgot Grayharbor Point had a keeper at all. "You spoke," he said, "and we drowned for it. Slowly. For eleven years. So don't ask me to admire it. Don't you dare ask me to call it a light."

Ede had listened to the whole of it without flinching, the way she listened to storm reports, taking the measurement of it. Then she was quiet a long time, and he thought she'd gone to sleep, and he was gathering the lamp keys off the hook when she spoke.

"The ruin didn't come from the speaking," she said. "I want you to actually hear this, so I'll say it twice if I have to. The ruin came after. The ruin was Vane's cousins closing their little doors, one by one, in the dark, where nobody could see them do it. That's not the price of testimony, Mira. That's the price of *unwitnessed* testimony. One voice, one record, and then silence — silence is what let them bury it. If ten tallies had stood beside mine. If one newspaperman had printed it. If one inspector had resigned rather than sign the finding." She coughed, and waited out the coughing with her jaw set, and finished. "I wasn't punished for telling the truth. I was punished for being alone with it. Those are different sentences, and everyone in this country pretends they're the same so that no one else will stand up."

He had carried that sentence for three years like a splinter too deep to dig out. He carried it now, standing at the window with the satchel hidden under honest coal below, and the empty chair in front of him, and Nell Brand sleeping one floor down.

* * *

Because that was the thing. That was the thing that had been sitting in his chest since the night the Corvus burned, getting heavier, refusing to be logged or filed or trimmed down to size.

He had done exactly what he'd sworn to do. He'd kept his head down. He'd made himself invisible, and it had *worked* — three years of invisibility, a warm tower and a full coal store and a pension accruing somewhere in an office he'd never seen, all of it bought by being so small that no one looked at him.

And a ship had come through his marked channel, overloaded and condemned by its own owners' arithmetic, and burned on his shoal in front of his lamp, and died men had washed up on his rocks, and his first thought — his honest, first, unwitnessed thought — had been *what will this cost me.*

His second thought had been for the log. Whether his entries would hold. Whether his light had been recorded properly, trimmed properly, lit at the proper minute. Even in guilt he was doing paperwork. Even drowning he was checking his own ledger.

"You'd have hated that," he said to the chair. "Not the fear. You'd have understood the fear. You'd have hated what I let the fear make. An exact man. Exact men are easy to break — that's what Quill-Harrow said, and he's right, and you knew it thirty years ago, didn't you. You married one anyway."

*Someone had to keep the accounts,* she would say. *Someone had to be the rock the light stands on.*

"And what good is the rock," Mirabel said, "if it lets the light go out?"

Downstairs, a door. Footsteps on the spiral stair, light ones, quick ones — Nell, up early, or never really down. She appeared in the doorway with her arms crossed against the cold and her father's jaw set hard, and she looked at Mirabel, and then at the empty chair, and something passed over her face — recognition, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes.

"He talked about you," she said. "In the letters. The keeper at Grayharbor Point, he wrote. *An exact man,* he wrote, *you could set clocks by his lamp.*" Her voice caught on the last word and steadied itself. "He trusted you before he ever met you. Because of the light. Because it never failed."

"Nell—"

"He trusted the wrong thing, maybe." She came into the room and stood beside the chair, not sitting in it, but nearer to it than Mirabel had managed in three years. "Or maybe there isn't a wrong thing. Maybe there's just what a person does when the light's the only witness left." She put her hand on the chair back, briefly, the way you touch a rail at a graveside. Then she looked at him straight. "Tamsin says the historian's launch was seen at the harbor mouth yesterday. Taking soundings. Mr. Quill-Harrow is measuring how soon he can come back across the ice. So whatever you and this chair are deciding—" her chin came up, and it was Ede's chin, Ede's exact angle, though the girl couldn't have known it and Mirabel would never tell her— "I'd decide fast."

She went back down the stairs. Mirabel stood alone with the chair and the window and the long gray water, and for the first time since the wreck, he took out his pen.

Not the log book. A plain sheet. And at the top of it, in a hand he hadn't used for anything but figures in twenty years, he began to write:

*I, Mirabel Lockridge, keeper of Grayharbor Point Light, do attest as follows—*

The words came slowly. But they came. And below the window, at the edge of the tide line, small and patient against the pale morning, a figure stood looking up at the tower — a man in a good coat, hatless in the cold, making no motion to knock, only watching, the way a man watches a house whose door he intends to enter soon.

Quill-Harrow had not waited for the thaw after all.

He was already on the point.
