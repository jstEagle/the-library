## 12. Betrayal at the Coal Store

The thing about guilt, Josiah Finch had learned over thirty years of galleys and griddles, was that it kept best in the dark, like root vegetables. Brought into the light it went soft and stank.

He had kept his guilt in the dark for nine years. It was a small guilt as such things went — a dropped lamp aboard the *Meridian*, a galley fire, two men burned badly enough that one never worked again, and Josiah's name struck from the rolls with a black mark he could feel against his skin on cold mornings like a scar that hadn't earned itself. He'd been drunk. He'd been careless. He had told himself both those things were the same thing, which was its own kind of lie.

Now he stood in the coal store at the base of Grayharbor Point with a shuttered lantern in his hand, and the guilt was out of the dark and growing by the minute.

The store was Mirabel's, technically — or the Admiralty's, technically, but nobody from the Admiralty had crossed the ice-locked causeway since November, so it was Mirabel's in every way that mattered. Six households' worth of winter stores stacked in barrels and crates: flour, salt pork, coal, lamp oil, dried apples, tea enough to make Tamsin Vole civil before her second cup. The stranded ones came down twice a week to fetch rations, and they trusted him with the key because he cooked for them now, and cooking, Josiah had found, was a kind of apology you could keep making three meals a day.

He turned the lantern shutter open a finger's width. The light fell across the coal sacks, the crates, and — wedged behind them where no casual eye would find it — the sea chest.

He didn't open it. He'd made himself a promise about that, back when the promise was easy. The chest held the dead man's papers; everyone in the tower knew it and nobody spoke of it above a whisper, the way you don't speak loudly near a grave. Elias Brand's daughter slept twenty feet from it every night. Josiah had watched the girl eat breakfast with her father's death still wet on her face, and something in his chest had gone hard and permanent about it.

Which was why what he was about to do needed doing fast, before the hard thing set.

He climbed the iron ladder to the store's seaward vent — a rusted grate above the high-water line, put there once upon a time for ventilation and useful now for nothing except what Josiah intended. Through the grate he could see the harbor mouth, gray under a sky the color of dishwater, and beyond it the long white teeth of the shoals where the *Corvus* lay broken.

And there, at the edge of visibility, riding at anchor in the lee of Gannet Rock: a launch. Navy gray, steam up, smoke standing straight in the cold air.

Quill-Harrow's boat. It had been coming and going for a week now, polite as a tax man, and every time Josiah saw it his heart did something shameful.

Because Mr. Quill-Harrow had spoken to him. That was the whole of it, and Josiah hated how small the whole of it was. On the chronicler's first visit to the point — tea in Mirabel's kitchen, all courtesy and dry remarks — Quill-Harrow had paused at the door on his way out, looked at Josiah's hands, and said: *You were Meridian, weren't you? I read the inquiry. A tragedy of circumstance.* And then he'd smiled, and said, *Men of circumstance deserve better than exile,* and gone down the path whistling.

Nine years, and nobody had said *circumstance*. They'd said *negligence*, said *drunk on duty*, said *struck off*, and Josiah had swallowed all of it. One sentence from a stranger in a good coat and he'd been carrying this rotten plan around for a week like a hot coal in his pocket.

*A reinstatement can be arranged,* the note under his door had said. No signature necessary. *A word about where Brand's effects are kept would be sufficient. Your discretion will be remembered.*

His discretion. His discretion would be remembered. He almost laughed. Nine years of being remembered for nothing but the worst hour of his life, and here was a man offering to remember him kindly if he'd just sell out a sixteen-year-old girl's dead father.

He raised the lantern to the vent.

Stopped.

Lowered it.

Raised it again, hand shaking, and thought of Nell Brand passing him the biggest potato in the pot last Tuesday without a word, because she'd noticed he always got the small ones left over after serving six people from five people's stores. She'd noticed. Nobody had noticed anything about him in nine years except the fire.

Then he thought of the inquiry aboard the *Corvus* already rigged — that's what the papers said, that's what the whispers among the stranded said — and he thought: if they'll rig an inquiry, what do they need with the papers? What do they need with *witnesses*?

The lantern shook worse.

He thought about the alternative. Spring thaw, bridge opens, and the Admiralty writes its clean little history: overloaded ship, tragic weather, unfortunate stoker, case closed. Nell goes to an aunt somewhere with a sealed record and a dead man blamed for his own murder. And Josiah goes back to the mainland with a black mark and no reference and cooks for strangers until his hands give out, having sold the only thing anyone ever offered him for his silence, and gotten nothing real for it either way.

Or—

Or he did the cowardly thing properly, all the way through, and lived with the smell of it forever.

The lantern went up. Three flashes toward the launch, shutter clicking, click-click-click, the old navy signal for *have information, come alongside*. He'd learned it as a boy. Some things stay in the hands even after they've left the heart.

Down in the harbor, a shape moved on the launch's deck. A lamp answered. Two flashes.

Done. There was no taking it back, the same way there was no un-dropping the lamp on the *Meridian*. All that remained was the fire and who got burned.

Josiah shut his own lantern, sat down on a flour barrel in the dark, and waited to see what kind of man he'd find out he was.

* * *

They came at dusk two days later, which surprised him — he'd expected dawn, or midnight, some theatrical hour. Instead it was the gray hour when the light in the tower was lit and everyone downstairs was at supper, and the launch slid into the cove below the coal store with her steam muffled and six men in plain coats stepping ashore like men getting off at a fishing village.

Quill-Harrow led them himself. Up the cliff path, past the store — Josiah flattened himself behind the coal sacks, heart slamming — and on toward the tower, walking not hurrying, the way men walk when they already own the ground.

Josiah should have stayed hidden. Should have let it happen at arm's length, the way you don't watch the slaughter if you want to keep eating the meat.

Instead he went up after them, because some animal part of him needed to know how bad it would be, and because — though he wouldn't understand this until later, much later — some other part of him was already looking for the exit from the thing he'd done.

The tower door wasn't locked. It was never locked; Mirabel Lockridge had opinions about locks on a lighthouse, namely that a keeper's door stands ready for the drowning. So the six men simply walked in, boots dripping on the flagstones, into the warm round room where the family — and it was a family now, God help them all, though not one of them would have said so out loud — was finishing a stew.

Tamsin Vole stood up first, slow, setting down her spoon with a click like a cocked hammer. Old Pilot Wren didn't stand at all; he kept working on his pipe, watching the door with eyes like a gull watching fish. Mirabel rose last, unhurried, and Josiah saw him take in the number of them, the cut of their coats, and arrive at the arithmetic instantly, the way he arrived at everything.

"Mr. Quill-Harrow," Mirabel said. "We're at supper."

"So we are." Quill-Harrow removed his hat, which somehow made it worse. "I apologize for the hour. An opportunity presented itself, and opportunities in winter are like daylight — brief." He glanced at the stairs winding up toward the lamp room, then at the sea chest sitting in plain view by the wall — the decoy, Josiah realized with a lurch; there had been a decoy chest since December, filled with ballast and old charts, while the true papers traveled nightly between hiding places according to a schedule only Mirabel and Tamsin knew. "I wonder if I might impose. We're recovering effects from the *Corvus*. Official business. Sad business."

"You've recovered effects twice before," Tamsin said.

"And each time found less than we hoped. Hope is persistent that way." Quill-Harrow smiled at the room. His eyes went around it slowly — Tamsin, Wren, Nell, Josiah in the doorway — and rested nowhere longer than politeness required, and Josiah understood suddenly that the man had counted heads on previous visits and was counting them again now, and finding the same number, and drawing whatever conclusion he drew.

Nell had gone very still, her spoon down, her hands flat on the table. Sixteen years old and she had the stillness of someone much older who has already decided what she'll do if it comes to running.

"The girl shouldn't hear this," Quill-Harrow said gently.

"The girl," Nell said, "is the daughter of your drowned stoker, and she'll hear every word."

Something flickered across the chronicler's face — not quite discomfort. Recalculation. "Of course," he said. "Forgive me." And he turned to Mirabel. "A search, then. With your permission."

"You have no authority on this rock," Tamsin said. "The Admiralty's writ stops at the causeway, and the causeway's been under four feet of ice since November. You're trespassing."

"I am a historian, Miss Vole, in pursuit of sources." Quill-Harrow spread his hands. "Historians trespass everywhere. It's practically our defining trait." He nodded to his men. "Begin below."

* * *

They searched the way professionals search — methodically, silently, destructively where destruction was cheap. Mattresses slit. Barrels opened. The coal store emptied sack by sack into the trampled snow. Wren's skiff, hauled up above the tideline, was turned over and thumped along her strakes listening for false bottoms. Tamsin's trunk of duplicates was dumped out onto the floor, and she stood over the scattered pages with her jaw tight and her hands folded, letting them read everything because everything in that trunk was exactly what it appeared to be — harbormaster's copies, boring as gravel, damning only to a patient reader, and Quill-Harrow's clerks skimmed them and moved on, and Josiah watched Tamsin watch them do it and understood that the boredom was engineered.

Upstairs, the lamp room. Josiah heard their boots on the iron stairs, heard Mirabel go up after them — keeper's privilege, keeper's supervision — and heard the voices fade into the wind noise under the lantern.

That left the main room, and the decoy chest, and Quill-Harrow, who had not gone upstairs. He stood at the window looking out at the darkening sea, hands clasped behind him, waiting, and the waiting was worse than the searching.

"You were *Meridian*," he said, without turning around.

Josiah's blood went cold. "I was."

"The fire wasn't your fault. I've always thought so. Cargo stored wrong, ventilation sealed for weather — a tragedy of circumstance." He turned then, and his face was kind, and kindness from that man was the most frightening thing on the whole frozen point. "Circumstances change, Mr. Finch. Men get second chances. Records can be amended, when the right people are persuaded."

In the corner, Nell had risen from the table. Wren's hand came out — slow, casual, a man reaching for his pipe — and closed around her wrist, and held it, and she stopped.

Boots on the stairs. The searchers came down shaking their heads. Nothing in the lamp room but a lamp, they said, and oil, and glass, and a log book. Quill-Harrow received this news the way a man receives news he expected, and looked at the decoy chest for a long moment, and then — and Josiah would replay this moment for the rest of his life — walked away from it.

"Well," he said pleasantly, putting on his hat. "Nothing today. The sea gives up its dead slowly, and its secrets slower. Thank you for the hospitality, Keeper." At the door he paused. "Mr. Finch. When the bridge opens, look me up. I'm easy to find. I find things."

They went down the cliff path into the dusk, and the launch's smoke stood up against the last gray light, and steamed away east toward the mainland, and the tower was silent except for the wind.

Nobody spoke. Then Wren released Nell's wrist, and Nell walked — steady, terrible, sixteen years old with her whole body shaking — across the room to where Josiah stood by the door.

She looked up at him. She didn't raise her voice. That was the worst part; he'd braced for screaming.

"You signaled them," she said. "From the coal store vent. I saw the light when I was bringing up the washing. Two days ago. Three flashes."

The room went colder than the sea outside.

"Nell—" Mirabel started.

"How long?" she said to Josiah. "How long have you been theirs?"

And Josiah Finch, disgraced cook, struck-off man, betrayer, stood in the doorway of the only home anyone had given him in nine years and found that the lie wouldn't come. Not the denial, not the excuse, not the *tragedy of circumstance*. He opened his mouth and what came out was:

"They offered to strike the mark off my record."

The words hung there, small and shabby, the truth finally in the light where it stank.

Tamsin said, very quietly, "You told us the launch couldn't have seen a signal through that haze."

"I know."

Wren took the pipe out of his mouth. "You know the coves," the old pilot said. "You helped me chart the run. Every shoal, every landing." He said it without heat, which made it heavier. "So they know our route. Or they know half of it. Which half, Josiah?"

And Josiah, who had spent two days telling himself he'd only flashed a signal, only sold a direction, only traded paper for paper — felt the floor of the thing drop away beneath him, because he hadn't thought about the coves, hadn't thought that everything he'd learned helping plan the escape had been learnable from the other side too, and he looked at the faces of the people whose bread he'd baked all winter and said, "I never told them anything else. I swear it on the fire. I swear it on the two men I burned. Just the signal. Just the signal."

"Swearing on the fire," Nell said, "isn't swearing on my father."

Mirabel crossed the room. Everyone expected anger — Josiah expected it, braced for it, wanted it, wanted to be hit and pay something, anything, cash on the nail. Instead the keeper stopped in front of him and studied him the way he studied weather.

"Why didn't you take the chest?" Mirabel asked.

"What?"

"When they searched. You were alone with the decoy for ten minutes. You could have opened it, shown them, been on that launch drinking their brandy tonight." Mirabel's voice was flat, tired, curious. "Why didn't you?"

And Josiah opened his mouth to say *because I couldn't*, and found that wasn't it either, and heard himself say instead, in a voice cracked down the middle:

"Because you gave me the biggest potato. And I've been trying to figure out for two days how a man sells that."

Silence. Wind under the eaves. Somewhere overhead, the lamp turning, turning, turning.

It was Nell who broke it. She looked at Josiah for a long moment, and whatever moved behind her face was older and more complicated than forgiveness — grief and fury and arithmetic, the family resemblance to the keeper showing through. Then she turned to the others.

"He knows their launch schedule," she said. "He knows what Quill-Harrow looks like when he's lying. And he owes us." Her voice didn't waver. "We're going to need all three before spring. So somebody decide what we do with him — because I've decided I'm not deciding."

Wren looked at Tamsin. Tamsin looked at Mirabel. And Mirabel Lockridge, who kept accounts of everything, picked up the ladle and refilled Josiah's bowl and set it back in front of the empty place at the table.

"Eat," the keeper said. "Then you'll tell us everything Quill-Harrow said to you. Every word. Because a man who betrays us once and confesses is worth more than a man who never betrayed us and might." He sat down. "And Josiah — the deadline just moved. He came early because he's leaving soon. Which means the bridge opens sooner than we thought, and the run through the shoals happens on his clock now, not ours."

Josiah sat. The stew was cold. It was the best thing he'd ever eaten.

Above them all, the light turned, and inside the lamp mechanism — bolted to the frame that rotated the lens itself, where no searcher would ever think to look, where Nell had hidden her father's papers weeks ago on the one night no one had thought to count her whereabouts — the truth of the *Corvus* revolved slowly through the dark, shining out to sea.
