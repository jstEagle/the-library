## 13. The Cook's Confession

I hadn't slept. That was the first thing anyone noticed about me the next morning, and the last thing I would ever be able to hide again: a man who hasn't slept wears it on his face like grease he can't scrub off.

My name is Ambrose Fitch. I was second cook aboard the *Corvus* for eleven months, and before that I cooked in a hotel in Portsmouth where the silverware was counted twice daily and nobody ever drowned. I am not a brave man. I want that on the record too, since we're apparently keeping records now — papers in lamp rooms, lies told to launches, a girl of sixteen carrying her dead father's secrets inside a mechanism full of hot glass and brass. If they're keeping records, let mine say plainly: Ambrose Fitch, not brave.

The night after the search, after Quill-Harrow's men had gone and Nell had stood in the lamplight with soot on her knuckles and the satchel hidden somewhere above all our heads, I lay in my bunk in the store room and listened to the tower breathe. Old towers breathe. The wind finds the joints and the joints answer, and if you lie still enough you can pretend it's a ship, which is the only way I have ever been able to sleep in a building.

But I didn't sleep. Because at some point past two o'clock, I heard boots on the iron stair, slow ones, and then the pilot — Silas Crane, seventy years old if he's a day, hands like cured rope — sat down on the step outside my door without knocking, without speaking, and stayed there.

He didn't say anything. He just sat. For an hour, maybe more. And somewhere in that hour I understood that he knew. Not suspected — knew. Knew what I'd done with the lantern signal, or guessed it close enough, and had come to sit outside my door the way you sit with a sick animal you haven't decided whether to shoot.

That's when the shame turned from something I could carry into something that carried me.

* * *

In the morning I found him in the kitchen, because there is always a kitchen and I always find it; it's the one room in any structure where I know the rules. He was sitting at the table with tea he hadn't touched, watching the storm hammer the seaward glass.

"I need to tell them," I said. "Before you do."

Silas looked at me for a long moment. His eyes are the pale gray of old ice, and they don't move much.

"Do you?" he said.

"Yes." My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Because if you tell them first, I'll lose whatever chance I have of being believed, and if they find out later, I'll lose everything anyway, and I'd rather —" I stopped. Started again. "I'd rather it came from me. It should come from me."

"Why?" he said. Not cruel. Genuinely asking, the way an engineer asks about a load-bearing wall.

"Because I'm the one who did it."

"And why did you do it?"

And there it was, the question I'd been walking around all night like a hole in a deck. I put my hand flat on the table to steady myself.

"Because they offered to reinstate me," I said. "Full certification. A galley on a coastal packet out of Wexmouth. My own kitchen, Silas. Do you know what it's like to cook eleven months on a stoker's watch, in a swell, with a first cook who watered the stock and blamed me for the taste? Do you know what it's like to be disgraced over one incident — one — and carry it for six years?" I was talking too fast now, the words coming up like bilge. "They knew all of it. Quill-Harrow knew my file better than I did. He said my name and my last posting and the date of the incident and then he said, *we can make that go away*, and God help me, Silas, I wanted a kitchen of my own more than I wanted to be a good man."

The storm filled the silence. Somewhere above us the lamp turned, patient as a heart.

Silas nodded slowly, as if I'd answered a navigation question correctly.

"All right," he said. "Now go say that part to them too. All of it. The wanting-a-kitchen part especially. They'll forgive the signal. It's the lying-about-why they won't forgive."

"They might not forgive either."

"No," he agreed, standing, joints cracking like rigging in a blow. "Might not. But Mirabel Lockridge kept a dead man alive through a night no boat should have launched in, and the girl hid papers in a burning lamp rather than give them up, and Tamsin Vole has been stealing government ink for three years out of pure spite for bad bookkeeping. These aren't people who forgive easy. But they're people who understand wanting a thing so badly you'll burn your own life down for it." He paused at the door. "Go on, then, Cook. Kitchen's yours. Say your piece in it."

* * *

They came because Silas fetched them, and they came into the kitchen because I asked, and I think that mattered — that I stood in the room where I fed them and said it there, among the pots I'd cleaned and the bread I'd baked, rather than in the lamp room where their real work lived.

Mirabel sat with his ledger closed in front of him, which I understood even then was significant: a man who keeps books had chosen not to open them for this. Tamsin stood by the window with her arms crossed, sharp-eyed as ever, and I saw her glance once at the ceiling, toward the lamp, toward the satchel, and look away again — protecting its location even from her own face. Nell sat nearest the stove. She'd taken to sitting near heat since the wreck; her father had died cold, I think, or she feared he had, and none of us ever said so.

"I signaled the launch," I said. "Two nights ago. The night of the search. I hung a lantern in the coal store window — three flashes, pause, three. It's a packet-boat code. It means *all well, proceed*. Quill-Harrow taught it to me the day he came for tea, before any of us knew who he was. He shook my hand and held it a half-second too long and said, *Mr. Fitch, if circumstances here were ever to become — navigable — there are those who'd be glad of a signal.*"

Nobody moved. The wind hit the glass and the glass held.

"He came back after the search failed. Yesterday, while the rest of you were checking the ice on the causeway path. He found me splitting kindling. He said the offer stood. Certification, reinstatement, a coastal packet, my own galley. In exchange for one thing." I made myself look at each of them. "He wanted to know where the papers were. And whether Brand said anything before he died. Names. Whether he named anyone besides the manifest officers."

"You told him nothing?" Tamsin said. Not a question — a check, like testing a mooring line.

"I told him I didn't know where anything was. That part was true. I told him Brand never spoke." My throat closed. "That part wasn't. Nell — your father talked. At the end. Not much. He said a word. He said —"

"*Mara,*" Nell said quietly. "I know. Mirabel told me."

"Mara," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I let it crack because hiding it felt like more lying. "Yes. And I sold that word's worth of silence for the promise of a kitchen, and the launch came back that same night with a search party, and if Nell hadn't thought faster than all of us, they'd have torn this tower apart and taken everything and none of you would ever have known it was me who —"

"We'd have known," Mirabel said.

It was the first thing he'd said. His voice was quiet and level, the voice of a man reading his own logbook aloud.

"How?"

"Because the coal store window faces the channel, and I trim that wick every evening, and I count things, Mr. Fitch. It's what I do. Three nights ago there was a lantern in a window that has never held a lantern. I noted it. I didn't understand it yet. But I noted it." He turned the ledger slightly, and I saw the entry, dated, timed, in his small exact hand: *Lantern observed, coal store, 21:40. Unexplained.*

"You knew," I whispered. "You've known for two days and you let me eat at your table."

"I let you split wood and haul water and feed five people through a blizzard," Mirabel said. "I wanted to see what you'd do with the knowing. Most men run. You didn't run. You came to Silas first, which took either courage or desperation, and I've learned in three winters on this rock that the difference matters less than people think."

"That's not forgiveness," I said.

"No," he agreed. "It's arithmetic. We're short-handed, the bridge opens in weeks, and the man hunting us owns a clock. I can't afford to throw away a pair of hands over a signal I already logged." He folded his hands on the closed ledger. "But hear me, Cook, and hear me once. You'll get no second signal. If Quill-Harrow asks you anything again, you'll come to this kitchen and repeat it word for word while we all listen. Your shame stops being currency the moment you spend it honestly. Understood?"

Understood. God, yes, understood — and the terrible thing, the thing I've turned over a hundred times since, is that being caught and kept was the first mercy anyone had shown me in six years. Punishment with a place at the table attached. I could have wept. I nearly did. I gripped the edge of the sink instead and looked at the pots, my pots, the only honest things I owned.

Nell spoke then. She'd been staring at the stove door, and when she looked up her eyes were dry and hard and sixteen going on forty.

"The kitchen," she said. "He promised you a kitchen."

"Yes."

"And you believed him."

"I wanted to believe him."

"My father wanted to believe the Admiralty would pay the insurance honestly and share it with the widows' fund, because the purser told him so." Her voice didn't rise at all, which was worse than shouting. "Believing men like that is how the *Corvus* went down with four hundred tons of extra coal in her hold. So here's what happens to your kitchen, Mr. Fitch. You don't get it. What you get is this one — ours — until spring. And you earn it back pot by pot, and when the bridge opens, if we win, maybe I'll tell my father's ghost you helped. And if we lose—" she stopped, and for one second the hardness cracked and I saw the child underneath, the one rowing a shoreline looking for a body. "If we lose, it won't matter whose kitchen anyone has. Because he'll seal the history and my father stays a drunk stoker whose negligence killed his own ship, forever, in print, in ink that never dries."

The stove ticked. Above us the lamp turned.

"What seal," Tamsin said suddenly. "What do you mean, sealed. Sealed how?"

And I realized I'd been saving the worst of it, hoarding it like a coward hoards the last of the brandy, and that if I didn't say it now I never would.

"He told me yesterday," I said. "After the offer. When I hesitated — and I did hesitate, I want that known, I stood there with an axe in my hand and hesitated — he stopped pretending to be friendly. He said, *Mr. Fitch, consider your position carefully, because the official account closes when the causeway reopens. The inquiry findings will be entered and sealed the day the first official crossing makes the point. After that date, no new evidence is admissible, no testimony is taken, and the record is permanent.*" I swallowed. "He said it like a man giving directions to a train. *The record is permanent.* Then he wished me good morning."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears like deep water.

Tamsin moved first. She pushed off from the windowsill and started pacing, three steps, turn, three steps, her mind visibly outrunning her feet. "The thaw," she said. "When does the survey party come? When does the bridge actually open?"

"April," Mirabel said. "First week, weather permitting. The engineer's schedule said the ninth."

"The ninth of April." Tamsin stopped pacing. "Then everything — the manifests, the duplicates, the light log, Nell's letters, all of it — has to be out of this harbor and in front of someone who prints things before the ninth of April. Not after. Not on. Before."

"It's February," Silas said from the doorway, where he'd been leaning with his arms folded, watching us all like weather. "Causeway's shut. Channel's running ice. Nothing crosses but what swims."

"Then it goes by sea," Tamsin said.

"By sea," Silas repeated, and something moved behind the old ice of his eyes — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile used to be before the sea wore it off. "Through the shoals. In winter. In a boat."

"In what boat?" Nell asked.

And Silas Crane looked at her, and then at Mirabel, and then at me, standing guilty and useful by my stove, and he said the sentence that I believe rearranged every life in that kitchen:

"There's a boat," he said. "There's always been a boat. I didn't survive sixty years on this coast by owning nothing worth seizing." He pushed off the doorframe. "But nobody charts a run through the Serrant shoals off a conversation in a kitchen. Give me three days and a blank sheet of paper, Keeper. I want your light log — every bearing you've ever recorded on the point, every fog signal, every current note. If we're going to run the enemy's deadline, we're going to navigate by your arithmetic."

Mirabel looked down at the closed ledger under his hands. When he opened it, I swear the sound of it was louder than the storm.

"Three days," he said.

* * *

That night I cooked for six people who knew the worst thing about me and fed them anyway, and it was the best meal I'd made in six years, and I don't fully know why, except that maybe a kitchen isn't a room. Maybe it's whoever eats what you make and doesn't poison you for what you've done.

Near midnight, Silas appeared at my elbow while I banked the fire. He set something on the shelf beside the salt: a small tin of pilot biscuits, salvaged, ancient, hard as slate.

"For the boat," he said. "Whoever goes. Start thinking about provisions like it's real, Cook, because by Friday it will be." He paused at the door. "And Fitch. The signal you made. Three and three."

"I know what it meant."

"So does Quill-Harrow. Which means when we run, he'll be watching every window on this point for lanterns." The old ice flickered, almost warm. "So you'll stand the watch that night, Cook, right where he can rely on you. And you'll show him three and three, all well, proceed — while we're thirty fathoms deep in the coves with his whole case in an oilskin bag." He tapped the doorframe twice, like a man knocking on fate's door. "Your shame bought us our decoy. Sleep on that."

I did sleep, finally. And dreamed of kitchens, and woke at dawn already planning menus for a voyage nobody had agreed to yet — through the Serrants, in February, against a sealed and permanent record — and for the first time since Portsmouth, Ambrose Fitch was not cooking to forget.

He was cooking for the crossing.
