## 14. Storm Season, Deepening

The blizzard came on a Tuesday, if Tamsin's almanac was to be believed, and it came like something with an opinion about us.

Mirabel had seen weather all his life. He had logged it in columns and decimals, wind speeds reduced to figures, seas described in the flat vocabulary of the Admiralty form: *moderate*, *rough*, *very rough*. But this was not weather. This was a siege. The wind came down the channel from the northeast with nothing between Grayharbor Point and the Arctic but open water, and it found the tower and leaned on it for fourteen days without once taking its weight off.

By the third day the world outside had ceased to exist. The gallery rail wore a foot of ice. The sea itself seemed to have been abolished — no horizon, no waterline, only a white roaring dark in every direction, and the lamp above it all, turning, turning, because that was the one law left.

"We're not going anywhere," Ambrose announced from the kitchen, with the satisfaction of a man who has been granted permission by God. "Nobody's going anywhere. Nobody can. So we may as well eat properly."

He had been different since the night he'd confessed. Smaller somehow and larger at once — the swagger gone out of him, and underneath it something Mirabel hadn't expected: competence. The man cooked as though the stove were a ship's wheel and he'd finally been given his heading back. Stores were counted, menus drafted, the pilot biscuit soaked and resurrected into things that were almost food.

"Properly," Tamsin said, "on what?"

"On arithmetic," Ambrose said, and looked at Mirabel. "His arithmetic."

So they did the sums at the kitchen table, all five of them, lamplight and pencil stubs. Flour: forty pounds. Salt pork: eleven. Dried peas, tea, sugar going hard in its tin, a sack of potatoes beginning to think about sprouting. Old Coss the pilot sat with his hands wrapped around his mug and recited rations from thirty years of contraband winters — what a man could live on, what a man would merely survive on, and where the line between them lay.

"Two weeks of this," Coss said, nodding at the window, where snow moved horizontally, "and we'll be down to the bone meal. Three weeks and we're eating the gulls."

"The gulls are all dead," Nell said. "I haven't seen one in two days."

That was the other thing about the storm. It was quiet in a way storms aren't supposed to be quiet. No ships. No boats. No gulls, no seals on the rocks, nothing moving in the world but them, five people in a stone cylinder with a light on top of it, and the wind trying to decide whether to take the roof or just take their minds.

Mirabel wrote it all in the log. *Storm, NE, force 11 by estimation. Station confined. All hands accounted for.* He paused over the phrase. *All hands.* It had meant himself and Ede, once. Then himself alone, which was the whole point of him. Now the log said *all hands* and meant five, and he let the words stand there like a fact he wasn't ready to argue with.

* * *

It was Nell who discovered that the glass was dying.

She came up the spiral stair two steps at a time on the fourth night, during her watch, and her voice came down ahead of her, tight and strange. "Keeper. You need to see."

The lamp room was cold enough that breath hung in it. And the lantern panes — the great curved sheets that turned the flame into the Grayharbor light — were frosting over from the inside. Not the clean white frost of a window in winter. A gray, greasy rime, salt-laden, building in feathers and scales along the lower curves of each pane, thickening even as you watched, eating the light.

"Ice," Nell said. "On the inside. How is it on the inside?"

"Breath," Mirabel said. "Condensation freezing against the glass. The storm drove spray so far up the tower it got into the ventilators, and now—" He put his palm flat against a pane and felt the cold come through like a hand closing. Every hour the rime climbed higher. Every hour the light behind it dimmed, went soft, went blind.

A lighthouse that doesn't show its light isn't a building. It's a rock with pretensions.

"There's a scraper," he said. "There's always a scraper. And spirits of wine for the worst of it." He heard his own voice go into the register he used for procedures, the flat competent voice, and was grateful for it. "You can't clear it from outside in this. You clear it from in here. Every hour, both faces. More, if it builds fast."

"How long does the storm last?"

"As long as it likes."

Nell rolled up her sleeves. She was sixteen and half-starved and had buried her father six weeks ago in a grave she'd had to dig herself, and she looked at the fouled glass the way Mirabel imagined she'd once looked at whatever stood between her and breakfast. "Then we'd better start," she said. "Which end?"

* * *

They fell into the shifts without discussing it, the way tides fall into a coast.

Mirabel took midnight to four; Nell took four to eight; and through the days they traded off, whoever was free climbing the hundred and twelve steps whenever the call came up the stairwell — *"Glass!"* — a word that within three days had acquired the gravity of a fire alarm. The others learned it too. Tamsin could scrape a pane to a shine with the patience she brought to ledgers. Even Coss, whose shoulders were seventy years old, took a shift when the wind screamed hardest, muttering to the panes like old friends he owed money to.

Ambrose cooked for the watch changes. That became part of the ritual too: hot tea at midnight, cocoa at four, a plate of something left on the warming shelf with a cloth over it, because, he said, a keeper coming off the glass should never have to see an empty shelf. Nobody thanked him out loud. Everybody ate everything.

And hour after hour, in the small hours, in the roar, Mirabel and Nell scraped ice off the inside of a lantern while a flame burned behind them and the sea tried to unmake the world below.

It was work that required no thought and allowed all of it. The scraper made its small rhythmic sound — *shk, shk, shk* — under the wind. The rime came away in curls. Behind the cleared glass the beam swung out and struck the blizzard and vanished, struck again, vanished. Somewhere out in that blindness were shoals with names, and a channel, and perhaps — though no sane vessel was out — some fool in trouble who needed the light anyway, because the light didn't get to choose its customers.

"My father hated storms," Nell said, on the sixth night, working the lower curve of the landward face. "Not being in them. Being ashore in them. He said a stoker in a storm at least knows why the ship is rolling. Ashore you just lie there imagining it."

"He was right."

"He was right about most things and wrong about the important ones." She scraped. *Shk, shk.* "He knew the Corvus was wrong, Keeper. He told me. In letters. I've read them so many times I could say them asleep. And he stayed aboard anyway, because a man has to eat, and because he thought maybe it wouldn't matter, and because—" Her scraper stopped. "Because leaving would have made it real."

Mirabel said nothing. There was a version of this conversation where he confessed his own three years of leaving-things-unreal, his own practiced smallness, his own arithmetic of silence. He wasn't ready for that version yet. Maybe by spring.

Instead he said, "Your father pulled one man's worth of truth out of a sinking ship. That's more than most men manage in a whole life afloat."

"It isn't enough."

"No," Mirabel agreed. "So we'll add to it."

She looked at him then — a long look, lamplight and frost-light on her face — and something passed between them that had no paperwork attached. An enlistment, maybe. Or an adoption. He honestly couldn't have said which frightened him more.

*Shk, shk, shk.* They went back to the glass.

* * *

On the ninth day the stores crossed the line Coss had drawn, the one between living and surviving, and Ambrose started making soup out of things that had previously been theoretical. Pea flour stretched with potato water. Tea leaves boiled twice. He served it all with such ceremony — the good cloth, the spoons laid straight — that it took them a week to notice there wasn't much under the ceremony.

Nobody complained. That was the thing Mirabel kept noticing, the thing he turned over at night like a coin he suspected of being foreign currency: nobody complained. Tamsin mended clothes by lamplight and taught Nell double-entry bookkeeping on scraps of wrapping paper. Coss told stories about running brandy past revenue cutters in fog so thick the stories themselves seemed to navigate by feel. Nell read aloud from a novel missing its first sixty pages and its last twenty, and everyone argued about how it must end.

Five people in a tower, getting thinner, and happier. It made no sense. Mirabel checked the sums twice. Happiness was supposed to cost.

On the eleventh day he found Ede's chair occupied.

He came down from the glass shift near dawn and there was Nell, curled in it with her father's letters, feet tucked up, the worn velvet accepting her the way it had accepted Ede for nineteen years and then accepted nobody. Mirabel stopped on the stairs like a man who has walked into a door he forgot was there.

"She won't mind," Nell said, without looking up. She said it plainly, not asking permission, which was the only way it could have been asked.

"I know," Mirabel said.

And found, checking himself carefully, as he checked all his accounts, that it was true. The chair had been waiting for someone who read by lamplight with her feet tucked up. He had kept it empty for three years on the theory that emptiness was respect. It turned out emptiness was just emptiness. The chair had wanted occupying.

He put another log on the stove and sat across from her and listened to the storm lean on the tower, and neither of them spoke for an hour, and it was the best hour he'd spent since Portsmouth.

* * *

On the thirteenth day the wind changed its mind, which was worse.

A northeast gale that backs suddenly doesn't die; it turns around and comes back with a grudge. The barometer — Mirabel's own, Ede's father's brass, faithful as a dog — fell all morning like something dropped. By afternoon the sea, which had been a white roar, developed a new sound underneath the roar: a long, heavy boom, boom, boom, swell arriving out of deep water, the kind of swell that moves rocks.

Coss stood at the seaward window with his mug forgotten in his hand. "That's groundswell," he said. "That's a storm still out there, a big one, and it's sending its regards ahead of it. Wind'll swing full south by midnight and drive all that water straight onto the Serrants."

"The Serrants," Tamsin said. "That's the route. For the run."

"That's the route," Coss said. "And by tomorrow noon there'll be a cross-sea on those shoals that no boat ever built should meet." He turned. "But that's tomorrow's problem. Tonight's problem is that swell will be putting spray over the top of this tower like a whale blowing, and our glass—"

"—will foul faster than we can scrape it," Mirabel finished.

He looked around the kitchen table. Five faces, thin, chapped, lit low because they were saving oil. His household. He had assembled it out of wreckage and clerical error and one act of treachery, and he would have fought anyone who tried to take a single piece of it away.

"All hands," he said, and the old phrase came out sounding like what it had become. "We keep the light. Whatever it costs tonight, we keep the light. Because somewhere out in that mess, if any vessel is fool enough to be moving, it will be looking for exactly one thing — and we are going to be it."

They climbed together. All five, up the hundred and twelve steps into the shaking lantern, and set to the glass with scrapers and spirit and bare hands when the tools froze, and the swell arrived at midnight like a debt collector.

The spray went over the gallery in tons. It hit the lantern roof like shot. Ice built on the panes faster than two people could clear it, faster than five, and they worked in relays — scrape, retreat, thaw fingers over the vent, return — while the beam swung and drowned and swung, and below them the sea stood up in shapes that had no business standing.

Near three in the morning, Nell, scraping with bleeding hands, stopped and pressed her face close to a half-cleared pane and stared out into the black.

"Keeper," she said. "Light. Off the port bow. Not ours."

Mirabel was beside her before the word finished. Out in the chaos, far off, intermittent, half-swallowed by spume — a light. Small. White. Moving. Not a star; stars didn't move against the swell. Not the Serrant beacon; that was fixed and red and miles beyond.

Something was out there. In this. In a sea that could crush a launch like an eggshell, something with a lantern aboard was picking its way toward the point — or toward the shoals — or toward them.

Tamsin appeared at his elbow with the telescope, already uncapped, and her voice when she spoke was very quiet.

"That bearing," she said, "is the mainland roadstead. And Mr. Quill-Harrow's launch went missing from its moorings two days ago."

The light blinked. Once. Twice. Coming on.

Below them, faint under the wind, Coss was already on the stairs, heading down, saying something about the skiff and the lee of the coal store, and Mirabel understood that the storm season had just stopped being about survival at all.
