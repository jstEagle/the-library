## 15. Tamsin's Duplicates

The coal store had a back room that smelled of lamp oil and mouse, and Tamsin Vole had been going there alone for eleven years.

Not every night. That was the first thing people never understood when she finally told them, years later, if she ever told them at all. They imagined a conspiracy — cloaks, midnight, a locked drawer with a key on a ribbon around her neck. The truth was smaller and worse. The truth was Tuesdays.

On Tuesday evenings the harbormaster, Mr. Pell, went to the Anchor and drank three pints of small beer and one of gin, in that order, always, because he believed the order mattered to his digestion. On Tuesday evenings Tamsin stayed behind in the harbormaster's office with the fire banked and the ledgers open, and she copied.

She copied everything.

Coal tallies that said one thing while the delivery receipts said another. Crew lists signed by men who'd signed aboard ships that had no record of them. Insurance valuations filed six weeks before the losses they insured against. She wrote it all out in her own small, square hand — the hand that had won her the clerk's position in the first place, because Mr. Pell liked tidy figures and did not look closely at what they added up to — and she folded the pages into oilcloth and carried them home in her boot, and later she moved them somewhere better, and later still she stopped telling even herself where they all were, so that no amount of questioning could shake loose what she didn't consciously hold.

Eleven years of Tuesdays.

People would ask her afterward — the journalists, mostly, the ones who came sniffing after the Corvus inquiry like gulls after offal — how she'd borne it. How she'd sat across from Mr. Pell for a decade, taken his dictation, fetched his spectacles, laughed at his jokes about the fishwives, all while building the case that would hang him. And Tamsin would say: it wasn't hard. Sitting was easy. What was hard was the waiting. Eleven years of copies in oilcloth and no one to give them to. Because papers are only paper until someone has the nerve to use them, and nerve, she had learned, was the rarest cargo on any coast. She could have walked into a magistrate's office in '89 with what she had on the *Merrow* and ended two careers. She hadn't. Not because she was afraid of the ending. Because she'd watched what happened to the last person in this harbor who'd spoken, and she understood that a witness without shelter is just a target with a name attached.

So she waited. She kept her hand square and her face pleasant and she counted Tuesdays the way other women counted rosaries, and she told herself that someday someone would come along who'd looked at the water and decided not to blink.

She had not expected it to be the lighthouse keeper.

* * *

Mirabel Lockridge was, in Tamsin's professional assessment, the most successfully invisible man she had ever met, and she had made a study of invisibility the way other clerks made a study of shipping rates.

She'd noticed him properly for the first time three winters ago, at his wife's funeral, which was also the last time he'd stood in front of more than two people at once. He'd stood at the graveside with his hands folded like a man waiting for a train that had already left, and when the vicar asked if anyone wished to speak, Mirabel had opened his mouth, closed it, and shaken his head — and Tamsin, standing at the edge of the small crowd in her good coat, had seen something pass over his face that she recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting in someone else's ledger. It was the look of a person deciding to become weather instead of a man. Something everyone notices and no one remembers.

After that she'd started watching him the way she watched everything — sideways, through paperwork. His supply requisitions came in neat, modest, slightly under-asked. He ordered less lamp oil than his station warranted and never petitioned for the raise the Admiralty schedule said he'd earned. When the annual inspection forms crossed her desk, his answers were so blandly correct they were almost an apology for existing. *No irregularities. No requests. All well at Grayharbor Point.*

All well. Year after year. She'd once caught herself wondering what it cost a man to write *all well* eleven hundred times in a row, and then she'd stopped wondering, because wondering about other people's costs was a habit she couldn't afford. She had her own.

Then the Corvus died on his shoal, and the dying man washed up carrying a satchel, and the girl came looking for her father, and the outcasts gathered in the tower like iron filings around a magnet nobody had asked to be a magnet — and Mirabel Lockridge, invisible man, keeper of the most correct logs on the coast, had stood in his own doorway with storm-blown hair and said, in a voice like a door opening after years shut: *I lit for them. My log will show I lit for them.*

Tamsin had gone home that night and sat in her cold room above the chandlery and felt something she hadn't felt since her first Tuesday: hope with teeth in it.

Because here was the thing about Mirabel's log. Here was the thing she'd known since the night of the wreck, when she'd pulled the harbor's duplicate weather records to check a date and found herself reading his entries by lamplight with her heart going like a hammer: his log proved the shoal was marked. It proved the light burned. It proved, entry by entry, hour by hour, that the Corvus had been steered onto rocks that a blind man couldn't have missed in fair conditions — which meant either catastrophic incompetence or deliberate scuttling, and the manifests Elias Brand carried settled which. But a light log alone could be disputed. A light log could be called a forgery, a keeper covering himself. What it needed was corroboration. What it needed was the harbormaster's own tallies, the coal receipts, the crew lists — the paper chain, link by link, from the insurance filing to the empty lifeboat racks to the rocks at Grayharbor Point.

And Tamsin Vole had been building that chain, link by link, for eleven years, without knowing what it would finally be for.

She hadn't told him. Not yet. Not through the weeks of Nell and the cook and old Petcher moving into the tower, not through the night the Admiralty launch came asking its polite questions and Mirabel lied like a man who'd been lying all his life, not through the long February evenings when the four of them bent over the satchel's contents by lamplight while she sat a little apart, as always, taking notes in her square hand. She hadn't told him because telling him meant showing him the back room of herself, and she was forty-one years old and had learned exactly what happened to women who showed people the back room.

But tonight — tonight the blizzard had eased to a whisper, and the household had gone to bed, and Mirabel was awake, because Mirabel was always awake at the hours when the truth got done — and he'd found her in the kitchen with the duplicates' index spread out, the one page she allowed herself to keep where others could see it, written in cipher so mild it was almost shy. And he'd looked at it for a long moment and then looked at her, really looked, the way nobody had looked at her since before her mother died, and said:

"You've known. This whole time. You've had—" He touched the page, not quite picking it up. "How much?"

"Everything," Tamsin said. "Since 1882."

* * *

He sat down slowly, the way old men sit down, though he wasn't old — forty-some, she supposed, gray coming into his beard early, grief doing what time does to some men twice as fast.

"Say it again."

"The Merrow, 1889. The Petrel coal contracts. The Corvus manifests match a pattern I've got nine years of." She laid her finger on the index, line by line, and watched him read. "I copied it all. Every Tuesday for eleven years. It's hidden in places only I know, and I'm the only living person who can reconstruct the whole of it, and until three months ago I fully intended to die with it, because there was never anyone to give it to."

"Why me?" he said. "Why now?"

"Because you lit the lamp," she said. "Every night. In a gale that should have killed you, you climbed that tower and you lit it anyway, for a ship you suspected, maybe, of being sent to die — and you logged it. You didn't decide afterward whether it was safe to be honest. You were honest first and let the safety sort itself out." Her voice did something she didn't authorize; she let it. "I've been waiting my whole adult life for somebody who did it in that order."

Mirabel looked at her across the kitchen table, and Tamsin watched him do the thing she'd already learned he did — the arithmetic. She could nearly hear it running: the risk to her, the risk to the papers, the odds of the chain holding, Quill-Harrow's patient eyes, the bridge reopening in April. He weighed it all, this man who counted what he owned in things that could be carried, and then he set the arithmetic down.

"What do you need from me?" he said.

That was it. That was the whole of it. No *are you certain*, no *it's too dangerous*, no *leave it to the men*. He asked what she needed, as though she were obviously the one who'd know, as though the plan were hers to command and his to serve — and Tamsin Vole, who had spent eleven years being useful and unseen, felt her eyes sting and was furious about it.

"I need your log," she said, when she could speak evenly. "The original. Not the fair copy you keep for the inspectors — the working log, with your corrections and your ink blots and the smudge where you spilled the tea on the fourteenth. Forgers make clean documents. Truth is messy, and messiness is verifiable. Your log plus my duplicates plus Brand's manifests is a chain no inquiry can call coincidence."

"You'll need somewhere to work."

"I'll need somewhere to work."

"My tower has a room that isn't used," he said. "It used to be—" He stopped. Started again, steadier. "There's a desk in it. Good light."

Ede's room. Tamsin knew whose room it was; the whole harbor knew the keeper had kept his wife's things untouched three years, the chair by the window, the shawl still on its peg. She understood what he was offering, and what it cost, and she understood that he was handing it over without ceremony because ceremony would have made it impossible.

"Tuesday nights," she said. "I've always worked best on Tuesdays."

Something moved through his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile lived before a man forgets the address. "Then we start tomorrow."

* * *

They worked, after that, the way two careful people work when they've finally found each other: silently, efficiently, and with a growing horror of how well it goes.

She taught him her cipher and he cracked its logic in an evening, faster than anyone she'd ever shown, and seemed embarrassed by it. He taught her the light log's rhythms — how a real keeper's hand fatigues over a watch, how the ink changes when the tower's cold, details no forger would think to fake and no skeptic could dismiss. They built the chain link by link on Ede's desk: Merrow, Petrel, Corvus, the insurance filings dated ahead of their disasters, the coal tallies that showed the Corvus loaded past her marks twice over, Brand's manifests corroborating all of it from inside the engine room.

And somewhere in the third week of it — she couldn't have said which evening, that was the maddening thing, there was no moment — Tamsin noticed that she'd stopped keeping a little apart. That she took her notes at the table now, shoulder to shoulder with him, close enough that their sleeves touched when they reached for the same page, and neither of them moved away. That he'd begun leaving the kettle filled for her in the mornings, knowing she took her tea bitter, without ever having asked. That she'd memorized the sound of his boots on the stair and could tell his step from Petcher's from three floors up, and that this knowledge pleased her in a way she refused to examine under lamplight.

Love, when it came for Tamsin Vole, arrived without announcement, the way she'd arrived at every job and every danger of her life: quietly, through the servant's entrance, already halfway to being indispensable before anyone thought to ask its name. There was no declaration. There was a night in late March when the wind dropped and the whole harbor went glass-still, and they finished a section of the chain at two in the morning, and Mirabel stretched, and looked at her across the candle, and said, "You know they'll come for you too. Once this starts. Quill-Harrow. They'll dig up everything you've ever filed."

"Yes," Tamsin said.

"And you're not afraid."

"Oh, I'm afraid," she said. "I've been afraid since 1882. Fear and I have an arrangement — it sits in the corner and keeps quiet while I work." She squared her pages and looked at him straight. "What I haven't been, these eleven years, is *accompanied*. That's the difference. You can be brave alone. I've done it. It's overrated."

Mirabel held her gaze, and whatever passed between them across that candle needed no words and got none, and was, both of them understood without saying, more binding for it.

"Nell knows something's changed," he said at last, half-smiling. "Petcher says we've started finishing each other's sentences, which he finds disgusting."

"Petcher ran contraband for twenty years. He has no standing to find anything disgusting."

"No," Mirabel agreed. And then, softer, in the voice he used for true things: "Thank you. For the Tuesdays. All of them. I know what they cost."

Tamsin looked down at her square, neat, eleven-years-deep hand and found she couldn't answer, so she did what she always did with what couldn't be spoken: she wrote it into the record. She pulled the fresh index toward her and began a new heading, in ink that wouldn't fade, and beneath it, where the cipher usually went, she wrote plain:

*Grayharbor chain, complete as of this date. Witnesses: M. Lockridge, keeper. T. Vole, clerk.*

Two names, side by side, in a document that might hang them both.

Mirabel read it upside down, and reached over, and beneath her name he added his — not initials, not the abbreviated, apologetic *M.L.* he'd used on a thousand inspection forms, but the whole of it, spelled out, unhidden: *Mirabel Nathaniel Lockridge.*

Tamsin stared at it. "You never sign the full name."

"I know," he said. "First time for everything."

* * *

They heard the boat at dawn — oars muffled, hull kissing the ice-edge below the tower, the particular wrongness of a craft moving quiet where nothing had cause to be quiet.

Tamsin was at the window before Mirabel finished buttoning his coat. Through the thinning snow-haze she made out the shape pulling away from the point toward the mainland channel: a launch, Admiralty lines, and standing in the stern, hatless in the cold, a figure watching the tower with the patience of a man who has all the time in the world and knows exactly how little of it is left.

Quill-Harrow. Come again — earlier than any schedule, in weather no sane launch would sail — and leaving, not arriving.

"Tamsin," Mirabel said quietly. "If he's sailing *out* at dawn—"

"—then he's not coming to ask questions," she finished. "He's coming from somewhere. Someone on this coast talked."

Below them, the tower slept on — Nell, Petcher, Cook — and out on the white harbor the launch grew small, carrying its news toward the bridge, toward April, toward the sealing of the official history.

Tamsin's hand found Mirabel's on the cold windowsill, and gripped it.

"We're out of Tuesdays," she said.
