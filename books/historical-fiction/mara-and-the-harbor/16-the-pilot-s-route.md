## 16. The Pilot's Route

The pilot's name was Aldous Renk, and he had spent forty years learning the water between Grayharbor Point and the mainland, which meant he had spent forty years learning how the water lied.

He sat now in Mirabel Lockridge's kitchen with a chart spread across the table, weighted at the corners by a salt cellar, a tin of lamp oil, Ede's thimble, and Nell's fist. Outside, the wind had dropped to that low grumbling register that meant it wasn't finished with them — just thinking about what to do next. The tower creaked above like an old man shifting in his sleep.

Renk put one blunt finger on the chart and dragged it along the coast.

"Everyone runs the shipping lane," he said. "Deep water, marked channel, nice and honest. That's why Quill-Harrow's launch will be sitting in it."

"And you don't run the lane," Tamsin said. She stood at the window with her arms crossed, the duplicate ledgers already wrapped in oilcloth behind her, as if she couldn't stand to have them more than arm's reach away.

"I haven't run the honest channel since I was nineteen years old," Renk said, "and the excise men taught me otherwise with a boarding hook." He tapped the coast south of the point, where the chart showed nothing but hatching and a warning in faded copperplate: *shoal water — unmarked — foul ground.* "There's a way through there. The Gullet, we called it. Runs between the Old Wife and her sisters. Six feet on a spring tide if you know where the six feet is. Four if you don't."

"Four feet of water under our keel," Mirabel said quietly.

"Under most of the keel. There's a rock called the Cobbler comes up to nine foot below the surface at half tide, and she doesn't show herself unless the swell's wrong." Renk looked up at him. "I've taken boats through there maybe sixty times. Lost two. Both times I got greedy with the tide."

The kitchen went quiet. In the corner, the cook — Hobb, who hadn't spoken much since the confession, since the launch came to the tower and left again empty-handed — set down the pan he'd been scouring and didn't pick it up again.

Nell's fist was still flat on the chart. She was sixteen and had her father's jaw and none of his patience. "When does the tide serve?"

"Spring tides come round Thursday. High water at the point a little past four in the morning. That gives us slack water in the Gullet around five, first grey light. We'd want to clear the Old Wife before the ebb makes against us." He drew a line with his finger, slow, so they could all follow: out of the harbor notch, south along the rocks, hard in through the gap where the chart promised death, then the long open stretch to Marsh inlet, and from Marsh, the mail road inland to Ashford, where the newspaper office sat three streets up from the quay. "Twelve miles of bad water, then thirty of good, then the truth gets printed."

"And the bridge opens Saturday," Tamsin said. "Quill-Harrow seals the history Monday morning."

"So we go Thursday," Nell said.

Renk leaned back. His knees cracked like kindling. He looked at each of them in turn, the way he used to look at crews before a run, deciding who would freeze and who would hold.

"There's the other matter," he said. "The boat."

* * *

The boat lay in the harbor notch under a tarpaulin gone gray with lichen, and when Renk pulled the tarp off, Hobb made a sound like a man at a funeral.

She was a gaff cutter, thirty feet on deck, built in 1861 for running brandy and tobacco, and every plank of her remembered it. Her name had been scraped off the transom long ago — Renk claimed it was the *Merrow*, claimed he'd sold her to a man in Falmouth, claimed a great many things. Her seams were open at the garboard. Her mast step was soft. Her sails were folded below in a state Renk described as "resting" and Tamsin described as "rotting."

"She'll float," Renk said.

"That is not the same as being seaworthy," Mirabel said.

"No," Renk agreed. "It isn't." He ran his thumb along a seam and came away with oakum dust. "But seaworthy costs money and a fortnight's work, and we have neither. What we have is four days, my hands, your hands, and whatever's in your stores that can be boiled into pitch. So the question isn't whether she's sound. The question is what we're willing to trust her with."

He let that sit.

Because everyone at the rail knew what she'd be trusted with. Not just four people. The satchel. Elias Brand's manifests, Tamsin's duplicates, Mirabel's light log with its three winters of careful entries proving the shoal had been lit and marked the night the Corvus died. The whole paper chain, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed in a biscuit tin, worth more per ounce than anything ever carried through the Gullet — including, Renk thought wryly, himself.

"We should take the papers inland by road," Hobb said suddenly. They all turned. The cook hadn't offered an opinion since the night he'd signaled Quill-Harrow's launch, and his voice came out rusty. "Once the thaw starts, once the causeway's passable on foot—"

"The causeway opens to traffic Saturday," Tamsin said. "And Quill-Harrow will have men on it from the first cart. He told Mirabel as much. The moment the bridge opens, this point belongs to them. Anything not gone by then is gone forever."

"So it goes Thursday or never," Nell said.

"It goes Thursday or never," Renk confirmed.

* * *

They worked the boat for two days.

Renk drove the work the way he did everything, without hurry and without mercy. He re-caulked the garboard seams himself, lying inside the bilge in the dark with a caulking iron and mallet, because no one else could be trusted to strike a seam right and he said so, twice an hour, cheerfully. Hobb cooked for the crew and found, to his visible surprise, that cooking for people who needed feeding was different from cooking for a captain who despised him, and something in his shoulders began to come down from around his ears. Tamsin copied out the paper chain one final time, dividing it — originals to go by boat, her own hand-copies hidden in the tower as insurance, in case the sea decided differently. Nell stripped and patched the sails, sitting cross-legged on the stones with sail twine and a palm guard, humming something her father must have sung her.

And Mirabel worked alongside all of them, and Renk watched him do it.

That was the thing about Renk. Forty years of reading water had taught him to read people nearly as well, and Mirabel Lockridge was the easiest read on the coast. The man moved through the days like a keeper still — trimming, logging, counting, rationing — a life organized entirely around the principle that he was temporary here, that the real owners would return, that he should leave no mark. Three years of making himself small enough to slip through the Admiralty's fingers, and now the fingers were closing anyway.

On the second evening, with the mast stepped and the rigging set up, Renk found him alone on the harbor wall, looking at the boat.

"She'll hold," Renk said.

"You said lost two of sixty."

"I said got greedy. I'm not feeling greedy." He came and stood beside him. Below them the boat swung gently on her new mooring, white patch on the dark water, small as a promise. "But she's marginal, Lockridge, and you know it, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. Twelve miles of foul ground in late February. A boat that barely floats, a tide that won't wait, and a launch that may or may not have guessed our route." He paused. "Which brings us to the arithmetic."

Mirabel didn't answer. He was good at silence; it was practically his trade.

"The papers weigh about four pounds," Renk said. "You weigh about eleven stone. Every stone in that boat is freeboard we can't spare, ballast we didn't choose, and a mouth that needs feeding if we get weathered in at Marsh. The Gullet doesn't care about courage. It cares about weight." He turned and looked at the keeper straight on. "So here's the honest arithmetic, and I'll only say it once. Either the papers go through the shoals, or you do. Not both. Not safely."

The wind came off the water and pushed between them, cold enough to feel like a decision.

* * *

Mirabel stood a long time without speaking, and Renk let him, because some calculations have to be done alone.

Renk knew what the man was weighing, because he'd weighed it himself once, in another life, on another coast, with a cargo of brandy and a partner who'd volunteered to stay behind and face the revenue men. Renk had gone. His partner had hung. For thirty years Renk had told himself the arithmetic was clean — one man's freedom bought with another man's rope — and for thirty years the sum had never once come out even. He wasn't going to hand Mirabel a lie about it. That was the pilot's whole value: he told you what the water would actually do.

"You're asking me to send my testimony into those shoals without me," Mirabel said finally. "Everything I logged. Everything I saw. If the boat founders, there's no witness left. Just paper that anyone can call forgery."

"If the boat founders with you in it," Renk said, "there's no witness either, and no one left ashore who knows the route well enough to try again." He nodded back toward the tower, where lamplight showed gold in two windows. "You've got people now, Lockridge. People need keepers alive more than they need keepers brave."

"They need the record more than either."

"Do they?" Renk spat thoughtfully over the wall. "Tamsin kept duplicates. Your log's copied. Nell's letters are copied. The chain survives you, friend — it always has. It's been surviving you since the day Brand washed up. You're not the load-bearing beam. You're the man who wrote the numbers down." He shrugged his big shoulders. "Numbers float. Men sink."

Mirabel looked at the water a long time. When he spoke again his voice had changed — flatter, quieter, the voice of a man reading his own ledger aloud.

"Three years I've practiced disappearing," he said. "Made myself small. Unnoticed. I told myself it was survival, and maybe it was. But I'll tell you what it looks like from in here." He touched his own chest briefly, as if checking something was still stored there. "It looks like drowning. Slow, comfortable, well-rationed drowning. And I have discovered, these last months, that I am not willing to go back under. Not for safety. Not for pension. Not for anything Quill-Harrow puts on a table." He turned to Renk. "If the papers leave this point, I leave with them. Or neither goes."

"That's not the arithmetic."

"No," Mirabel said. "It's the answer to it."

* * *

Renk argued it all the way back up the path, and lost, and was not entirely displeased to lose.

In the kitchen, the household took the news in their several ways. Hobb swore softly and started listing stores. Tamsin said nothing at all, but she went to the oilcloth bundle, unwrapped it, checked the seals, and rewrapped it, and her hands were very steady and her eyes were not. Nell looked at Mirabel for a long moment — the look of a girl who had buried a father and did not intend to bury a second one — and then said only:

"Somebody has to keep the lamp while you're gone."

And that settled it better than any argument, because it was true. The light would burn Thursday night whether its keeper stood beneath it or not. The light had always burned regardless. That was the whole lesson of the place, if a man was willing to learn it: you were never the light. You were only its hands, for a while.

They laid the plan out on the chart one final time. Thursday, high water, four o'clock. Papers in the biscuit tin, tin in the oilcloth, oilcloth lashed under the stern sheets where a boarding hook would miss it. Renk at the helm, Hobb forward, Tamsin amidships with the tin literally tied to her wrist, because she had insisted and no one was fool enough to argue. Mirabel aft beside Renk, navigating by memory and by the numbers in his own log — the same careful arithmetic that had proved the shoal was marked now steering them through the shoals themselves. Nell staying, with the old shotgun and the duplicates and the lamp.

"And if we're taken?" Tamsin asked. It was the question no one had asked aloud. "If Quill-Harrow's launch finds us in the Gullet?"

Renk answered, chewing on a pipe he wasn't smoking. "Then the tin goes over the side, weighted, in deep water, and we're four fools out fishing in a rotten boat. Nothing on us, nothing to find. The copies stay ashore. The story stays alive." He looked round the table. "Nobody drowns for paper. Paper can wait. People can't."

"Except the paper is people," Nell said quietly. "My father drowned carrying it. Tamsin risked her post copying it. Mirabel—" she stopped, and started again, plainer. "Everyone in this room gave something for it. It's not paper. It's them."

No one argued with her. There was nothing to argue with.

Renk rolled the chart, tied it, and stood, joints cracking. At the door he paused, and looked back at the keeper.

"Sleep tonight, Lockridge," he said. "Tomorrow we bend on the mainsail, and Thursday we find out what the Gullet thinks of us." He grinned, and it was the old contraband grin, sharp at the edges. "One more thing. Tide serves at four. But the fog —" he tilted his head toward the window, toward the black glass and the wind beyond it, "— the fog's coming early this year. And there's someone on the shingle tonight, down by the notch, who's been looking at my boat for the better part of an hour. So whatever you mean to decide, decide it fast."

Below, faint on the wind, came the creak of an oar in a rowlock — patient, unhurried, coming closer.
