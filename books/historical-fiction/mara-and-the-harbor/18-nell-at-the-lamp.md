## 18. Nell at the Lamp

The offer was still sitting in the kitchen like a third person nobody would look at.

I'd heard it through the floorboards. I hadn't meant to — the tower carried sound the way a hull carries water, everything seeping down to where you didn't want it — but I'd heard every word. Quill-Harrow's voice, smooth as varnish: *your pension, Mr. Lockridge, your post, and passage for the girl to her aunt's in Bristol. A comfortable life. No one needs to know what you kept.*

And then Mirabel's silence. Long enough that I could hear the stove ticking.

He didn't say no.

That was three days ago, and he still hadn't said no, and every hour he didn't say no was an hour my father got quieter in the ground. Elias Brand had walked into fire and out of a drowning sea with those papers pressed against his burned chest, and he'd held on through a night of Mirabel's rowing just long enough to make me an orphan with a job to do. And the man who'd pulled him from the water was up in his kitchen weighing a warm room against my father's last word.

I found him at the window in the late afternoon, watching the barometer fall. The glass had dropped all morning like something dying, and the wind had come around to the northeast, and by the way he stood — shoulders set, jaw working — I could tell he was doing sums in his head. He did that. Everything was arithmetic with Mirabel Lockridge. Coal by the hundredweight, oil by the gallon, risk by the ounce.

"Say it plain," I told him.

He turned. "Nell—"

"You're thinking about taking it." My voice came out flat, which surprised me. I'd expected it to shake. "The pension. The post. My aunt's house in Bristol, where I'd sit by a window and knit until I died of being nobody's daughter."

"It isn't like that."

"Then what is it like?" I crossed the room. He was tall and I wasn't, so I made myself stand where he couldn't step around me. "Because it sounds exactly like that. It sounds like a man who lit this lamp for eleven years deciding the dark is fine, actually. Comfortable, even."

"Nell." He said my name the way you'd set something fragile down on a shelf. "You're sixteen years old. There is a launch coming for us if we run, and the pilot's boat is a coffin with a sail, and Quill-Harrow doesn't lose. I have watched men like him work my whole life. They don't lose."

"My father lost," I said. "On your shoal. In sight of your lamp."

The words landed. I watched them land — watched his face go gray around the mouth the way it always did when anyone mentioned the Corvus. And some small mean part of me wanted to press harder, because grief makes you cruel and I had decided to stop apologizing for mine somewhere around the second week of December.

"He lit for you," I went on, softer now, which was worse. "He trusted the light. That's what a stoker does below decks, did you know that? You can't see anything from the stokehold. You trust the keeper. He trusted you, and then he trusted you again, at the end, when he gave you the satchel instead of throwing it overboard. Because he thought — " my throat closed and I pushed through it — "he thought he'd found the one honest man on that water."

"I am trying to keep you alive."

"No. You're trying to keep yourself asleep." I heard it come out of me and knew it was true the moment it did, and hated him a little for making me say it. "Three years, Tamsin says. Three years of making yourself small so the Admiralty would forget you. Well, they haven't forgotten, have they? They came here. They sat in your kitchen. Being invisible didn't save you, Mirabel. It just meant that when they finally looked, there was nothing of you left to see."

He flinched. Actually flinched, a man built like a lighthouse, and I understood suddenly that no one had ever said it to him — not Ede, not Tamsin, not God Himself in whatever weather God used. Everyone had been gentle with his grief because grief deserves gentleness. But grief was not the same thing as surrender, and he'd spent three years confusing them.

"The lamp's due at half five," I said.

"It's blowing forty knots out of the northeast with sleet behind it. I'll take the first watch."

"No." I picked up my coat off the peg. "You'll sit here with your arithmetic. I'll light her."

"You can't run the gallery alone in a gale like that."

"You taught me yourself." I buttoned the coat to the chin. "November. When my hands were too cold to hold the key and you put yours over them and said, *again, girl, the sea doesn't care how you feel.* Well. The sea doesn't care how you feel either. Neither do I, right now. Half five."

I went up the stairs before he could answer, and I did not run, because running would have meant I was frightened, and I had decided to be furious instead. Fury holds heat better.

* * *

The gallery door fought me like a live thing. Wind tore it open and slammed it against the rail and for a moment I stood in the full weight of the gale with my hood snapping and the whole world gone to salt and noise, and I thought: *Da, you absolute fool, you were down in the dark the night this started, and you never once saw what I'm seeing.* The harbor a churn of white. The point running with spume. The causeway bridge a black line under water that hadn't been a bridge since October.

I went to work.

Trim the wick. Check the oil feed — the cold had thickened it and I warmed the reservoir with my hands, both palms flat against the metal, breathing on it like I was waking something up. Wind the clockwork for the rotation. Clean the storm panes, which the spray had already begun to foul, salt freezing to the glass in feathers. This was the ritual he'd drilled into me, and my hands knew it without me, and there was a strange comfort in that: that some part of me belonged to this tower whether I forgave its keeper or not.

At half past five I struck the match.

The flame took, steadied, grew. Through the lens it became the great slow wheel of light I had lived inside for three months — the sweep, the dark, the sweep — and out past the shoal the sea answered it the only way the sea answers anything, which was not at all. But I imagined it answering. I imagined Da in his stokehold, sweat and coal dust, feeling the ship heel wrong, and above him and ahead of him a light turning, turning, saying *here, here, the safe water is here.* He'd died believing in that sentence. The least I could do was mean it.

By seven the sleet had come horizontal and the panes were fouling faster than I could clean them. I worked the outside walk with the safety line clipped to my waist, cloth in one mittened hand, wiping each pane in strokes that the wind tried to steal. Twice the gusts shoved me against the rail hard enough to bruise through the coat. Once my feet went out from under me and the line caught and I hung there over sixty feet of black air with the sea roaring underneath like a train, and I laughed. I actually laughed, hanging there, because the alternative was understanding what I was doing, and I wasn't ready to understand it yet.

At eight I went down for more lamp cloths and found the kitchen empty and the stairs to the lantern deck creaking under a weight that wasn't mine.

Mirabel stood at the lamp room door with his coat on and his cap in his hands, soaked through, sleet melting in his gray hair. He must have climbed the outside stair in it. His face was wrecked — not angry, not pleading. Just open, in a way I'd never seen it, like a door left unlatched after years of locks.

"I've been watching you from the headland path," he said, over the wind. "For two hours."

"Come to relieve me?"

"Come to be ashamed," he said. "There's a difference."

I wiped the pane I was working on and didn't answer. He came out onto the walk anyway, clipped his own line beside mine, and took a cloth from the basket without asking. We worked the gallery together for a while, side by side, the gale screaming, neither of us speaking, and it was the closest thing to a conversation we'd ever had.

"She was like you," he said at last. "Ede."

"Don't."

"She testified against Halloran Shipping when everyone told her what it would cost. She had proof — real proof, bills of lading, a dead bosun's letters — and she walked into a courtroom in Plymouth and read it aloud with her hands shaking. She said afterward it was the only time in her life she'd felt entirely steady." He wiped a pane. Sleet immediately began feathering across it again, and he wiped it again anyway. "We lost the house. We lost the case. Her witnesses were bought or frightened, and Halloran's historians wrote her down as a hysterical widow, and she died three years later with the loss still on all of us like weather." He stopped. His hands weren't shaking. "I built my whole life since on the idea that she'd been wrong. That the truth is a thing you carry quietly or it kills you."

"And?"

"And I sat in my kitchen three days ago and listened to a man offer me my own cowardice back with interest, and I caught myself *counting*." He turned to face me, rain streaming off his jaw. "Do you know what I was counting? The years to pension. The tonnage of comfort. I was doing sums, Nell. While your father's papers sat in the lamp mechanism ten feet above my head, I was doing sums."

"Then stop," I said. "Sum it properly. What do you actually own? Write it in your ledger. One column: what they give you for silence. Other column: what Da gave you for free, in a satchel, with his last hours. Tell me which entry weighs more."

The lamp wheeled above us, sweeping the dark, sweeping it again. In the sweeps I watched his face change — not soften; settle. Like ballast going into a hull.

"You hung off that rail tonight," he said. "Don't think I missed it."

"You taught me the line. Use it or lose it."

"If you fall, I fall. That's the arithmetic of a safety line, and it turns out it's the arithmetic of everything else too." He looked out at the black water where the Corvus lay, where Da lay, where the truth sat rusting on a shoal because powerful men had needed it to. "Quill-Harrow comes back tomorrow evening. He expects an answer."

"He'll get one."

"Not from you." Something had come into his voice I hadn't heard before — not the careful keeper, not the invisible man. A third man, older than either, the one Ede must have married. "From me. And it won't be the answer he brought a contract for."

I should have said something. I want to tell you I said something wise, something worthy of the moment. Instead I reached over and finished wiping his pane for him, and he let me, and that was the whole speech.

* * *

We came down near midnight, soaked to the skin, and the kitchen was full of people pretending they hadn't been listening at the stairs. Tamsin had her duplicate ledgers stacked on the table already — she'd heard the wind change the way she heard everything, and she'd heard something else too, apparently, in the way Mirabel and I came down together instead of separately. The old pilot sat by the stove with a chart weighted flat under four mugs, and Cook stood at the range with his back to all of us, stirring a pot of nothing in particular, which was how he apologized.

Mirabel dripped on the flags and looked around the room at the strangest crew the harbor had ever assembled, and I saw him do it — actually do it, deliberately, the way you strike a match — stop counting.

"Pilot," he said. "Your route through the coves. Walk me through it again. Every shoal, every tide gate."

The old man's eyebrows went up under his wild white hair. "That run's a killer in weather like this."

"It's going to get worse before it gets better," Mirabel said. "And Quill-Harrow seals the record the day the bridge opens. So we don't wait for the bridge." He put his hand flat on the chart, on the black thread of water between the point and the mainland, and I watched three years of invisibility drain out of his shoulders like water out of a raised lock. "We go before dawn. While the gale's still covering us. And while it does—" he glanced up the stairwell, toward the lamp still turning overhead, "—somebody keeps the light."

Tamsin was already reaching for her pen. The pilot was already turning the chart to face him.

And I stood in the doorway with salt drying stiff in my hair and my father's fury finally, finally quiet in my chest, and thought: *there he is.* The honest man Da found on the water. Late — but lit.
