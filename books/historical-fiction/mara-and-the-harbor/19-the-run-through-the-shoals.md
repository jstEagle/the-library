## 19. The Run Through the Shoals

The pilot's boat was called the *Maggie B*, and she was a lie of a vessel — fourteen feet of tarred larch and borrowed nails that had spent twenty years running brandy through the coves and the last ten rotting under an overturned hull behind the coal store. Old Brannock had turned her over at midnight, scraped her seams with a knife, and paid the gaps with tallow from Mirabel's stores and the cook's apology, rendered down in the lamp room like something penitent.

"She'll float," Brannock said, which was not the same as *she's sound*, and everyone standing on the shingle understood the arithmetic.

Mirabel counted what they carried. He could not help it; he was made of ledgers. One oilskin satchel, wrapped in sailcloth and bound with tarred twine, holding the manifests, Tamsin's duplicates, Nell's father's letters, and eleven years of light logs copied out in his own hand because he would not trust the originals to salt water. Two lanterns, one shuttered. A compass whose card wobbled but did not lie. Bread, cold tea, a horn of brandy for shock or celebration, whichever came first. Oars, a stub of mast, a lugsail patched with flour-sack cloth.

And three people. Himself, sixty-one years old and stiff in every joint the weather owned. Tamsin Vole, who had never in her life been on open water after dark and said so twice, precisely, so no one could later claim she hadn't been warned. And Brannock, seventy if he was a day, who claimed he could run these shoals blind and drunk and had once done both.

Above them the tower stood black against the racing clouds, its lamp burning — Nell up there now, alone by choice, keeping the light because someone had to and because she had told him, in the flat voice she'd learned from him, that a keeper's place on the worst night of the year was wherever the light was. She would keep it until they were past the point. Then she would put it out, as agreed, so the launch behind them would have nothing to steer by either.

"You understand," Mirabel said, "that if we're taken, the papers go in the sea. Not into their hands. I'd rather drown them than deliver them."

"Cheerful man," said Brannock, and spat over the side.

"I'm serious."

"Aye. That's why we're going tonight instead of waiting for the thaw." The old pilot put one boot on the gunwale and held out his hand for the painter. "Quill-Harrow's launch has steam up. Cook saw the smoke before he went to make his confession, God forgive him, and smoke means they mean to come to you tomorrow morning with the thaw tide and take everything polite-like. Tonight they're still anchored off the harbor mouth thinking they've won." He grinned, all brown teeth and no mercy. "Let's not correct their thinking till we're gone."

Tamsin climbed aboard without help and sat exactly where Brannock pointed, which told Mirabel more about her fear than anything she'd said. She had the duplicate ledgers strapped inside her coat, against her body, the way a woman carries letters from a lover. When the boat lurched she did not cry out. She put her hand flat on the deck and kept her eyes on the black water and said, "I've balanced books in worse conditions than this," and Mirabel believed her, and loved her, and had not yet found a safe way to say so, and suspected there wasn't one.

They pushed off at half past three.

* * *

For the first mile they ran the inside channel, oars muffled with rags, the tower light sweeping overhead in long white strokes — once, twice, the rhythm Nell had promised, two flashes and a dark, two flashes and a dark, the signal they'd invented at the kitchen table like children playing at war. Each sweep lit the cliffs, the boiling water at their base, Brannock's face set forward like a figurehead.

Then, on the fourth sweep, the light stuttered. Held dark. Held dark again — longer than any interval they'd rehearsed.

"She sees something," Tamsin whispered.

Or the ice-fouled glass had failed, or the storm had finally beaten the flame out, and Mirabel felt the old terror climb his throat: *the light is out, the light is out, and it is my fault.* Three years of small careful darkness and here was the true dark at last, and it was worse than any inspection, any inquiry, any ruin.

Brannock shipped his oar and listened. Under the wind, faint, came the thing he was listening for: a steam launch's engine, working hard against the swell, somewhere north toward the harbor mouth.

"They've moved early," he said. "Damn fool cook must've signaled more than we knew. Or Quill-Harrow never sleeps." He turned the boat's head toward the open coves. "Inside channel's done. We go the smugglers' way, and we go now, and nobody prays where I can hear them."

The smugglers' way ran outside the shoals — *through* them, rather — a labyrinth of sunken rock that only existed on charts drawn from memory, in heads, in songs. Brannock steered by nothing visible. He steered by smell, by the taste of the water off his fingers, by the different sound the sea makes over sand versus stone, and by counting.

"Two hundred strokes to the Kettle," he muttered. "Then the water goes green even in the dark — you'll feel it, girl, colder on your hands. Port oar then, hard, and hold till she screams."

*Till she screams.* Meaning the rocks. Mirabel took the starboard oar and did the only thing he knew how to do, which was count.

It occurred to him, somewhere around stroke ninety, with spray freezing in his beard and the satchel lashed between his feet like a third passenger, that this was the strangest hour of his life, and also the most honest. For eleven years he had written numbers in a book: hours burned, gallons of oil, vessels passed, weather observed. Numbers that proved things. Numbers that no one read. Now those same numbers were under his hands as wood and water — the distance to the shoal he'd logged a thousand times, the depth of water at low spring tide off the Kettle, the set of the current that dragged everything east toward the Teeth. His whole careful invisible life had been, without his knowing it, a chart. Every entry he'd ever made was a reason he might live through this night.

He had spent three years making himself small enough to be forgotten. The forgetting had not saved him; the *record* had. There was a sermon in that, and Ede would have preached it, and he let himself hear her voice say it once, brief as a flash of light, and then bent to the oar.

* * *

The Kettle nearly killed them.

It happened the way disasters happen at sea: quickly, quietly, and with terrible courtesy. One moment Brannock was counting; the next the water around them went green-black and cold as a hand, and a swell lifted the *Maggie B* and set her down six feet to port of where she should have been, and the rock that should have passed to starboard rose instead dead ahead, black and glistening, tall as a church.

"Hard astern!" Brannock roared, and Mirabel and Tamsin backed water like people trying to unmake a mistake with their arms, and the boat checked, hung, and slid sideways along the rock close enough that Mirabel could have touched it, could have chipped a piece off and kept it in his pocket forever. Barnacles clicked against the planking. Somewhere below, he knew, the rest of the shoal waited in tiers, a staircase of drowning.

The current had shifted. Of course it had. It was in his logs — the easterly set strengthening after four bells in storm conditions, noted forty times over eleven years, and he had never once needed it to be true until now.

"Set's carrying us east," he shouted into the wind. "Half a point! Maybe more!"

Brannock looked at him across the thwart, and in the dark Mirabel saw the old pilot recalculate — not angrily, but the way a man looks at a lock he's just discovered someone else knows how to pick.

"How much more?"

"Three quarters by the drift on the wreck night. Less now, the tide's younger." He was computing it as he spoke, oil and hours and tons of water, arithmetic learned at a desk being spent at sea. "Bear south. South and a hair west. Trust me to the second shoal, then I can't help you."

"I can't *see* the second shoal."

"No," Mirabel said. "But I know where it isn't."

There was a silence of perhaps two seconds, filled entirely with ocean. Then Brannock laughed — a short bark, half fury, half delight — and put the helm over. "South it is, Keeper. Count for me."

So Mirabel counted. Out loud, into the storm, in the flat voice he used for log entries, while Tamsin bailed with the tin pan and the launch's engine note swelled somewhere behind them like a second storm learning their name. Two hundred strokes south. A hundred and fifty southwest. At each number Brannock adjusted, and the sea tried to argue, and the numbers won. Once — only once — the keel kissed something soft, sand or weed, a long shudder through the hull, and all three of them froze, and the boat slid on, and Tamsin said nothing at all, which was the loudest prayer Mirabel had ever heard.

Behind them, the launch blundered where they had been. They heard it strike — not the killing blow, but a glancing hit, iron on rock, a shriek of metal and a sudden chaos of shouted orders carrying flat across the water. Brannock didn't even turn his head.

"Fog's coming down off the moor," he said. "That's our friend now. Row."

* * *

They cleared the last of the shoals as the sky went from black to the color of wet slate, and the fog arrived like a curtain drawn across the world, and the pursuit became a sound and then a guess and then nothing. Brannock raised the patched lugsail and the *Maggie B*, miraculously, forgave him, and ran northeast before the wind with water coming over the bow in quantities Tamsin described, between bailings, as "within tolerance."

Mirabel got out the bread and the cold tea and made them eat, because a helmsman who hasn't eaten steers badly and he had read that somewhere or learned it in thirty years of watching men come ashore. His hands shook so badly pouring the tea that Tamsin took the flask from him without comment and poured, and their fingers touched on the metal, and neither of them mentioned it, and it stayed in the air between them anyway, warm as the tea.

"The papers are dry," she said. "I checked. Twice."

"I know. I checked too."

"We're going to be all right, aren't you going to say it?"

He looked at her — salt-crusted, exhausted, ledger-strapped to her ribs, absolutely certain of the figures. "We're going to be right," he said. "All right comes later."

She smiled for the first time since the shingle. "That's the most romantic thing anyone's said to me in a decade."

"It wasn't—"

"Don't ruin it, Keeper."

Ahead, gray through the gray, the mainland coast began to assemble itself out of the fog — first the headland, then the chimneys of the fisher rows, then, blessedly ordinary, the pier lights of Harrowgate burning steady yellow, the newspaper town, the town with telegraph wires and a printing press and a man named Aldous Verrity who owed Tamsin a favor from some old story she refused to tell and who, she swore, would print the truth if the truth arrived before the Admiralty's version did.

The bridge was still shut. The road around the bay was a day's journey in this weather. Whatever Quill-Harrow did next — and he would do something; a patient man with a sunk scheme does not stop being patient — whatever he did, they had the hours. They had the ink.

Brannock ran the boat at the little beach under the pier, and she touched gravel with a sound like a closing door, and none of them moved for a moment. Just sat, three drowned people in a floating coffin, breathing.

"Well," said Brannock at last. "Keeper. You count well."

Mirabel lifted the satchel out from between his feet. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. Eleven years of light. A dead stoker's proof. A girl's inheritance. He slung it across his shoulder and stood up into the fog, onto land he had not stood on since the bridge closed, and looked up the hill toward the town where the presses slept.

"Tamsin," he said. "How fast can your journalist dress?"
