## 21. What Can Be Carried

The letter came on a Tuesday, because the Admiralty believed ruin was best delivered in writing and the post crossed with the morning tide.

Mirabel read it standing in the doorway of the tower, letting the wind take the envelope's edge, reading it twice because the first time he had not believed it and the second time he had not felt it. *By order of the Commissioners: your services as Keeper of Grayharbor Point are discontinued effective the first of May. Pension forfeit under Section—* He did not finish the clause. It didn't matter which section. They had found one.

He folded the letter into quarters, the way he folded everything, and put it in his coat pocket, where it sat against his chest like a coal that hadn't caught yet.

Downstairs, Nell was at the table with the light log open before her, copying out the final entries in her careful schoolroom hand — his words, her ink. She had taken to doing that without being asked, weeks ago, and neither of them had ever discussed it. Tamsin stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the harbor road, where the first official crossing had come over the bridge that morning: three carriages, a wagon of clerks, and a photographer's cart whose driver kept looking up at the lamp room like a man sizing a rival's shopfront.

"Well?" Tamsin said, without turning.

"They've let me go."

"Post?"

"Post."

"Pension?"

"Gone."

She turned then. Her face did what it always did — went very still, very level, the face of a woman who had spent years making sure nothing showed. But her hands came apart from their fold, and one of them found the back of a chair and gripped it.

"The inquiry," she said. "It opened anyway. The journalist got there first. You saw them scramble."

"I saw."

"So they punish the witness instead of answering him." She said it flatly, cataloguing it, filing it. That was how she grieved; he'd learned that about her. In ledgers. "And Nell?"

"Nell keeps her passage rights to her aunt's. That much Quill-Harrow conceded. In writing." He almost laughed. *In writing.* The man had a religion and its scripture was carbon copies.

Nell had stopped copying. Her pen hung above the page, dripping a small black star onto the margin. "They can't do that," she said. "The inquiry vindicated the crew. Your log proved the shoal was lit. Everyone in that room heard it read aloud."

"Yes," Mirabel said.

"Then why—"

"Because I was right," he said. "That's the whole of the crime, Nell. Being right is expensive. Someone has to pay for it, or other people might try it."

She looked at him for a long moment, sixteen years old and older than the sea, and then she bent back over the log and finished the entry, and blotted it, and signed beneath it, and pushed it across the table to him.

"Sign your name," she said. "All of it. Mirabel Josiah Lockridge. If they want you gone, make them spell you first."

His hand shook when he took the pen. Not from fear. He hadn't shaken from fear since November. From something else, something with no ledger line of its own.

He signed.

* * *

Packing took two days, and taught him more than the Admiralty ever had.

He began with the stores, because that was the arithmetic he knew. Oil: requisitioned, therefore theirs. Coal: same. Flour, salt pork, tea, the winter's remainder — all of it logged, all of it stamped, all of it belonging to the Crown down to the last weevil. He set it aside without bitterness. It had never been his; he had only been its custodian, the way he had only been the light's custodian, and there was an odd freedom in handing the key across and owing nothing.

Then the rooms themselves. And here the counting failed him.

Three years in this tower, and what did he own? Ede's chair by the window — hers, but the Admiralty had bought the chair with the keeper's allowance eleven years ago, so by their paper it was theirs too. He sat in it once, briefly, the way you visit a grave rather than live in a house, and decided the chair stayed. Let some other keeper sit in it. Let them wonder why the seat was worn on the left side, why whoever sat there always faced the water.

His clothes fit in one canvas bag. His tools in another. His books — Ede's Milton, his navigation tables, the Bible nobody had opened since her funeral — wrapped in oilcloth, tied with twine. The tin photograph of Ede on the quay at Falmouth, laughing at something outside the frame, went into his breast pocket next to the dismissal letter, so that they rode together, the sentence and the appeal.

On the second day he emptied the drawer under the stairs where he kept things that were not records and not tools and not, strictly, possessions: a child's wooden bird Nell had carved in February during the blizzard, terrible and perfect, its wings uneven. A dried sea-lavender sprig Tamsin had put in his coat pocket in March and pretended afterward she hadn't. A clay pipe the old pilot had left on the doorstep one night with no note, still smelling faintly of tobacco and contraband. The cook's recipe for storm bread, torn from a flour sack, grease-spotted, annotated in a furious hand.

He laid them all on the table and looked at them and understood, with the slow cold clarity of a tide turning, that this was the inventory. This was everything. Twenty-two years of keeping lights, three years of making himself small enough to be forgotten, one winter of being found — and it came to a bird with crooked wings and a flower and a pipe and a bread recipe.

The stores had never been his life. The stores had been the wall he hid behind.

"You're counting again." Tamsin stood in the doorway with two mugs of tea. "I can hear it. There's a sound you make."

"There's no sound."

"There is. Like an abacus falling downstairs." She set the mugs down, picked up the wooden bird, turned it in her fingers. "You're taking this."

"It's not mine to leave behind."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She set it gently into the tool bag herself, wings up, and said nothing else about it, and drank her tea looking out at the harbor, where the pilot's boat sat at the quay looking exactly as unseaworthy as it had every day of its long criminal life.

* * *

Word traveled through Grayharbor the way word always traveled there — faster than the mail, slower than gossip in a decent town, but with more consequences.

He should have known. He had fed half the harbor's leftovers out of these stores all winter. He had lied to an Admiralty launch for them. He had stood in an inquiry room and read his own log aloud until the commissioners' faces went the color of candle wax. Grayharbor did not forget accounts. Grayharbor kept them.

The cook arrived first, hauling a handcart up the cliff path with his collar undone and his dignity left at home. "Right," he announced, dumping a crate of salt fish, a ham, and four jars of pickled onions inside the door. "You're not going to the mainland to starve in a garret while you wait for some newspaper to decide if you're worth paying. I've seen how you people eat. It's a scandal. It's practically mutiny."

"Cook—" Mirabel began.

"Don't 'Cook' me. I've a name. You've all been using it wrong for months anyway, so we'll start fresh on the mainland." The man was already repacking the handcart with the ferocious competence of someone who had fed crews through hurricanes and disgrace both. "Where's your pans? Tell me you're not bringing those Admiralty pans. Those pans have boiled nothing but despair since 1888."

The old pilot came next, saying nothing, which was his way, simply appearing at the door with his sea chest and sitting down on it as though furniture had been arranged for him. When Mirabel raised an eyebrow, the pilot shrugged and produced, from inside his coat, a folded paper: a bill of sale, dated March, for a boat. Not the wreck he piloted through the coves. Another boat — uglier, slower, drier. Bought months ago. Before the inquiry. Before the verdict.

"You knew," Mirabel said.

The pilot shrugged again. "Knew you'd need a boat eventually. Knew you'd never buy one. Man who counts everything won't spend on himself." He tapped the paper. "She's yours. Registered to me, so they can't touch her, but she's yours. Consider it freight charges. You carried my secrets twenty years ago and never sent a bill."

"That was different."

"It never is."

Tamsin came in from the lamp room at dusk and found the tower full of crates and men arguing about ballast, and stood in the doorway with the strangest expression Mirabel had ever seen on her — something between exasperation and the thing people mistake for grief but isn't. She crossed to the table where his inventory lay, picked up the sea-lavender, and put it deliberately into his breast pocket, over the tin photograph, over the letter, patting it flat with two fingers like sealing an envelope.

"There," she said. "Now you're carrying everything that matters. Everything else was ballast."

"And you?" he asked. Because she had not said. Because she had packed nothing he could see, and her eyes had not said either, and he had spent a whole winter learning to read this woman's silences and still could not bring himself to ask plainly.

"I've given notice at the harbormaster's office," she said, to the window. "Effective Friday. My duplicates are going to the mainland archive regardless; it seemed inefficient to send papers without a courier who could vouch for them." A pause. The length of a wave. "Also the courier position includes lodging. Apparently. If the lodger is tolerable."

"Tolerable," Mirabel repeated.

"Borderline," she said, and finally looked at him, and whatever was in her face made the whole tower feel suddenly smaller and warmer, like a lamp turned up one notch.

* * *

The last night, Nell climbed to the lamp room alone, and he let her.

He heard her overhead — the familiar sounds he knew better than his own heartbeat now: the wick trimmed, the glass wiped, the mechanism wound, the small ceremonial striking of the match. At eight o'clock the beam swung out over the dark water, steady as ever, though after the first of May it would be somebody else's steadiness, somebody else's arithmetic.

When she came down she had the old log book under her arm — the finished one, closed and signed — and a new book, blank, cheap paper, harbor-shop binding.

"This is yours," she said, giving him the old one. "For the archive. For the record. Whatever they do to you, this stays true."

"And that one?" He nodded at the blank book.

"For the next light." She said it the way she said everything now — plain, unbending, her father's daughter down to the marrow. "Because there will be one. I've thought about it the whole way through this winter. A light doesn't stop existing because the Admiralty takes away one lamp. Somebody always needs lighting home. And I'm not going to my aunt's, by the way. I'm coming with you. Don't argue. I've already written to her explaining that I'm needed elsewhere and she'll agree because she argued with the Admiralty herself in '71 and lost everything and never once regretted it. It runs in families, apparently. Both kinds."

He looked at her — Elias Brand's girl, who had walked into his life on the rocks in December carrying nothing but grief and a satchel's worth of truth, who had hidden papers in a lamp mechanism with steady hands, who had stood in front of Quill-Harrow and not blinked — and he could not find a single ledger line against her staying. He tried. He was, after all, a counting man.

The column came up empty.

"Then we'll need provisions," was all he said, and she grinned, and somewhere below them the cook shouted something profane about onions, and the pilot laughed his silent laugh, and Tamsin called up the stairwell that the tide turned at six and anyone not aboard by five-thirty could swim.

* * *

He woke before dawn on the last morning, out of habit, and went up to the lamp room out of habit, and stood out of habit in Ede's place by the south window while the light burned down toward its extinguishing hour.

The harbor lay gray and still. The causeway bridge stood open at last, spring water running free beneath it, and beyond it the mainland hills were green the way they only were for two weeks each year, as if the world rehearsed its forgiveness annually whether anyone accepted it or not. Down at the quay the pilot's ugly boat waited with her hold full of crates and salt fish and pickled onions and one canvas bag of clothes and one wooden bird with crooked wings.

Twenty-two years. Three winters of silence. One winter of noise.

He had come to the point with a wife and a trunk and a salary, and he had built himself a life so small it fit through the eye of any needle the Admiralty cared to thread, and it had taken a shipwreck, a dead stoker, a furious girl, a clerk with stolen duplicates, a traitor-cook-turned-honest, a smuggler's boat, and the worst gale in a decade to show him the sum.

He took out the little notebook he had kept since his first posting — the one with every possession listed, valued, weighed — and he turned to a fresh page, and he wrote:

*Mirabel J. Lockridge. Effects: one bag clothes, one bag tools, three books, one photograph, one bird (wooden), one flower (dried), one pipe, one recipe. Persons: one keeper's daughter by adoption of circumstance, one clerk of exact habits, one cook reformed, one pilot retired-ish. Debts: none. Owed: everything.*

He considered it a moment. Then he added a final line, in the space at the bottom where accountants write the thing the columns can't say:

*Balance: carried forward.*

Below the tower, the door banged, and Nell's voice rang up the stairwell, impatient and alive: "Keeper! Tide's turning! Are you coming or aren't you?"

He closed the notebook, put it in his pocket beside Ede and the lavender and the letter that had ended everything, and took the stairs down two at a time — out of the dark, into the morning, toward the boat, toward the mainland, toward whatever light came next.
