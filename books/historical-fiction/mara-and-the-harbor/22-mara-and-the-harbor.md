## 22. Mara and the Harbor

The new logbook smelled of sizing and salt, the way all fresh paper did before a lighthouse got its hands on it.

Nell Brand sat at the keeper's desk with her boots flat on the floorboards and her spine straight, because her father had been a stoker and stokers did not slouch, and because Mirabel Lockridge was watching from the doorway with that particular expression he wore — half suspicion, half something warmer he hadn't learned to say out loud yet — when she was about to do something official for the first time.

The desk had been Ede Lockridge's. Nell knew this the way she knew everything in the tower now: not from being told but from absorption, from eleven months of living inside another family's grief until it became partly hers. The wood was worn pale where elbows had rested for thirty years. A groove near the right edge where a pen had been tapped, tapped, tapped through long winter watches. Nell had caught herself tapping pens into it without thinking, and had decided that was not desecration. That was inheritance.

She opened the logbook to its first page.

The old book — the one that had recorded every night of the Corvus disaster, every lie told smoothly to an Admiralty launch, every shift she'd climbed alone through the March gale with her hands frozen to the rail — sat on the shelf behind her, fat with significance, officially retired. The inquiry men had wanted it. They had gotten copies instead, notarized by Tamsin Vole's own careful hand, delivered into the *Grayharbor Sentinel*'s offices four hours ahead of Mr. Quill-Harrow and his historians, which was enough. It had been just enough. The truth had crossed the line first by the length of a newspaper column, and the inquiry could not be sealed over a record already in print.

Four hours. Her father's whole reputation — cleared of blame, his name read aloud in the House without the word *negligence* attached to it — had hung on four hours and an old smuggler's knowledge of shoals.

Nell dipped the pen.

*April 9th, 1894,* she wrote. *First entry, new book. Light: Mara Point, re-established. Lamp lit 6:41 p.m., wick trimmed, glass clean, oil full. Weather: clear, wind southwest, moderate sea.*

Her handwriting had gotten better. Mirabel made her copy pages of his old logs as practice, and she had complained about it for a month before admitting she liked the way a clean page felt under her hand, the way numbers behaved themselves if you asked them properly. She kept accounts now the way he did — everything counted, everything carried — though she counted different things. He counted what could be packed into two cases. She counted nights the light had burned without failure. Forty-one since the new lamp was mounted. She intended to make it thousands.

Below the weather, she wrote the line they had argued about for two weeks, all five of them around the table, arguing the way families argue, which is to say loudly and with bread involved.

*Mara Point,* she wrote. *So named.*

* * *

Her father's last word had been Mara.

She had not known that for certain until the letters came back from the mainland, after the inquiry, after the newspapers, when a clerk at the shipping office finally located the effects bag that had gone astray between Grayharbor and the city. Inside, among the manifests and the spare shirt, was a letter to her, dated six weeks before the Corvus sailed, the letter that had given the inquiry its corroboration — Elias Brand, stoker, writing to his daughter that they were loading her past her lines and calling it ballast. But below that, cramped into the margin where ink had run with seawater, was a single line she hadn't noticed the first hundred times she read it:

*If anything goes amiss, mind the light at your grandfather's point. Your grandmother's name was Mara. Remember that.*

Nell had never met her grandmother. Her father rarely spoke of his mother, except once, drunk on New Year's porter, when he said she'd been born within sight of Grayharbor Point and had loved a man who drowned there, and had named her daughter — Nell's aunt, the one in the city who still sent grudging parcels — Mara, before dying young like women did. So the name ran backward down the family like a vein of ore, and her father had carried it across the water in his head, and the last thing he'd thought of, drowning or burning or both, had been a woman who loved someone lost off this very point.

It seemed right. It seemed more than right. It seemed like arithmetic, and Nell had learned arithmetic here.

Mirabel had resisted the name for exactly one evening. "Lights are named by the Admiralty," he'd said, in his ledger voice, "and the Admiralty will not thank—"

"The Admiralty," Tamsin had said, not looking up from her duplicates, "can put it in their next sealed history."

That had settled it. Tamsin settled most things lately, in the quiet way she settled them, by existing adjacent to a problem until it gave up.

* * *

The tower itself was not the same tower. That was the strangest part, how much had changed while remaining in the same footprint.

The old light had come down in February, officially condemned — unofficially, everyone agreed, as punishment, though no order ever said so. The Board had offered Mirabel a transfer to a mainland light, a demotion dressed as mercy, and he had refused it in writing so politely that the refusal was later read aloud at a public meeting to considerable applause. What nobody at the Board had anticipated — what Quill-Harrow himself, Nell suspected, had privately hoped for — was what happened next.

The harbor bought the point.

Not the harbor council. Not the port authority. The harbor. Fishermen and chandlers and the widows of pilots and the cook — Silas Penn, reinstated nowhere, employed everywhere, who cooked at the mission hall now and brought up whatever the tower needed twice a week in a cart drawn by a pony he swore was borrowed — had passed a hat, then a ledger, then a subscription list, and by March there was a new lantern room on the old tower, fitted secondhand from a decommissioned light up the coast, and a trust deed signed by forty-three households naming the Grayharbor Point Light Association as its keepers. The first entry in the trust ledger was one shilling, contributed by Nell, earned by mending nets. The second was ten pounds from a woman in the city named Mara, Nell's aunt, whose covering letter said only: *He would have laughed at you all. I enclose what my sister would have sent.*

So the light burned again, and the Board could do nothing about it, because the light stood on land the harbor owned and served a channel the harbor used, and the Admiralty's writ apparently stopped where private charity began. Mr. Quill-Harrow had visited once more, in the autumn, to look at the new lantern with his head tipped like a heron considering a frog, and had said, "You understand this is unprecedented," and Tamsin had said, "We've had a year of unprecedented," and he had almost smiled, and left a donation of two guineas, which Silas spent on mutton out of pure spite and declared delicious.

Nell wrote the last line of the entry: *All in good order. N. Brand, assistant keeper.* Then she paused, pen lifted, and looked toward the door.

"You're hovering," she said.

"I'm inspecting," said Mirabel Lockridge.

"You're hovering with a clipboard."

"I'm inspecting," he repeated, coming in anyway, "with a clipboard." He set it down unopened, which was his way of conceding the inspection was theater. He had insisted, in the beginning, on formal inspections, formal drills, formal everything — as though rigor could hold the tower together against the world. Eleven months had loosened him the way the sea loosens a mooring knot: not undone, just shaped. He still logged everything. But he'd started logging jokes too, in the margins, in pencil, small enough that only someone reading closely would find them.

"Read me the entry," he said.

She read it. He listened the way he listened to weather reports, eyes shut, and when she finished he opened his eyes and said, "You've spelled *established* correctly, which puts you ahead of three previous keepers of this station," and Nell grinned, because praise from Mirabel came disguised as pedagogy and everyone in the household had learned to unwrap it.

"There's one thing missing," he said.

"The signature line?"

"The signature line," he agreed.

They had designed the page together, weeks ago — space for the keeper and the assistant, both names every night, because Mirabel had opinions about accountability the way other men had opinions about religion. Nell signed hers with a flourish she'd been practicing. Then she slid the book across the desk.

"Keeper signs first," she said. "I'm the assistant."

"I signed the old book first for nine years." He picked up the pen. "This is a new book. Assistant opens it. Keeper closes the watch." And beneath her name, in a hand that was steadier than it had been a year ago, less like a man writing small enough to disappear, he wrote:

**M. Lockridge.**

Then, below it, without being asked, without ceremony, the way a man does a thing he has decided to do and will not discuss afterward:

**Mirabel James Lockridge. Keeper.**

Nell looked at it a long moment. In the old logs — she had copied hundreds of pages — his signature had always been initials and surname, cramped into the corner of each entry like a man signing a confession. Full name, written large, on the first page of a new light: it meant nothing to the Board, and everything to her, and she thought Ede Lockridge would have understood it completely.

"He'd have hated the name," Nell said. "My father. Too sentimental for him. He'd have wanted the light called something practical. *Corvus Shoal Light.* Rubbing it in."

"He'd have wanted," Mirabel said, "his daughter safe, and the truth printed, and a pension. He got two of the three."

"We're working on the third," Nell said. "Tamsin says the association can petition for a stipend once we've a full year's service record. She's already drafted it. There are fourteen appendices."

"Of course there are."

"She's very happy. You know that, don't you? This is what happiness looks like on Tamsin Vole. Fourteen appendices."

"I'm aware." Something moved behind his face that Nell had learned to read this year and never comment on, because some things in a household were load-bearing walls and you didn't knock on them. Tamsin still kept rooms in town, technically. Technically. Her duplicate ledgers lived in the tower now, in a locked drawer beside the oil stores, and her coat hung on the fourth peg, which had been empty for three years and was empty no longer, and neither she nor Mirabel had said one word about it, and the whole harbor had said words about it for months, and the two of them went on not saying it with the serene obstinacy of people who had survived worse than gossip.

* * *

Evening came down over the water the way it did in April — slowly, apologetically, the light lingering like a guest who knows the season of goodbyes is over. From the gallery, Nell watched the harbor do its evening arithmetic: boats in, nets up, smoke from Silas's kitchen chimney lying flat along the water. Old Pellar — the pilot, seventy-one years old and still convinced every vessel in the channel was personally attempting suicide — stood on the causeway end directing traffic with a boat hook, though the bridge had been open since the thaw and traffic consisted of two dinghies and a very confused swan.

The causeway. Nell still felt it sometimes, that closed-season feeling, the island-ness of the point when the water took the road away. But the road was open now, and had been for a year, and the difference was that none of them needed it open to feel free. That had been the lesson, though Mirabel would have phrased it as a ledger item: survival bought with invisibility was its own kind of drowning, and the cure wasn't the bridge. The cure was the household. Five people and a pony and a locked drawer of true ledgers, and a light with a dead woman's name burning over the same shoal where the Corvus had gone down with its cargo of lies.

She went down to the lamp room for the lighting. Mirabel followed, because he always followed, because even now, a year on, he did not entirely trust a lamp to be lit without him watching, and Nell had stopped taking it personally and started finding it funny.

The new burner caught on the third match. She trimmed the wick, checked the clockwork that turned the lens — secondhand, slightly temperamental, needing encouragement every eighth rotation, which Pellar claimed proved the light had character — and watched the beam swing out over the water, steady, white, reaching.

"Mara Point," she said aloud, to the lamp, to the sea, to her father wherever stokers went. "Burning."

The glass was clean. The oil was full. The wind was southwest and moderate, and somewhere beyond the horizon ships were moving on darkening water, and every one of them would pass this point tonight and see the light and adjust their course, and none of them would ever know what it had cost, and that was correct. That was the whole job. Lights were not supposed to be thanked. They were supposed to burn.

But downstairs, when she came back, there was supper smell rising up the stairwell — Silas had arrived with the pony and a leg of mutton and opinions about her gravy technique — and voices, Tamsin's dry and Pellar's booming and Mirabel's rare low laugh, the one that still startled everyone including him. The table was laid for six, because the table was always laid for six now; they had stopped setting it for fewer sometime in the autumn and never discussed it.

Nell stood a moment longer at the window where Ede's chair still sat — reupholstered now, Tamsin's doing, in a blue that would have scandalized no one — and looked out at her light sweeping the water, and did the evening's counting the way she'd learned it here. One tower. One family, assembled from wreckage, none of it related by blood and all of it related by choice. One truth, printed, permanent. One name on the first page of a new book, written large enough that no historian could ever shrink it again.

Everything that mattered could be carried. Everything that mattered, they were carrying together.

She went down to supper.

Outside, the light turned, and turned, and turned, keeping its faithful count of the dark — and far out on the southwest water, small as a star but growing, a sail appeared where no sail was expected, running for the point with all canvas set, as though it carried news that could not wait for morning.
