## 1. The Last Boat Before Ice

The wind had turned in the night.

Hazel Nystrom felt it before she heard it — a change in the way the draft moved through the lamp room, sliding under the door instead of rattling it, coming down from the north with a dry, mineral smell that meant the river's temper was turning. She lay awake in the narrow bed beneath the stairs and listened to the light turn above her, the great Fresnel lens riding its bath of mercury, and she thought: *three days early this year.*

By the time she climbed the ninety-one steps to the gallery, the sky was the color of unpolished pewter, and the water below the point had gone strange and glassy at its edges. Not ice yet. Not quite. But the river was rehearsing.

She trimmed the wick first, as her hands had done every morning for eleven years, pinching the carbon from the flame's edge so the light would burn clean through the coming dark. The work required no thought, which was why she loved it. There were perhaps forty motions between waking and a lamp made right, and her fingers knew all of them the way they knew Mara's hair, the way they had once known Elias's shoulders. The body keeps what the heart cannot afford to carry.

Below, the cove lay flat and gray as slate. And out on it, small and black and stubborn, the ferry *Corvid* sat at her mooring with steam rising off her deck like breath off a beast, taking on the season's last crossing.

Hazel stood at the rail and counted.

She could not help counting. It was what eleven years of keeping a light did to a woman: you learned to number things. Ships. Days. The turns of the lens. The men who went out and the men who came back, and the terrible arithmetic of the difference.

The *Corvid* would make one run today, downriver to Millbrook Landing before the channel shoaled with frazil ice, and then she would be hauled out and tarped until thaw. Whoever crossed today crossed for good. Whoever stayed stayed until the river gave them leave — four months, five, if the winter came down hard the way old man Soderlund swore it would. Hazel had watched the bridge close nine times now. She knew the ritual of it the way she knew the trimming of the wick: which families stood at the landing with their trunks strapped and their collars up, and which ones stood on the bank with empty hands, watching other people's departures like watching a funeral they hadn't been invited to.

The ferry whistle blew — two short notes, flat in the cold — and the crowd at the landing shifted and stirred. Even at this distance Hazel could pick them out. Eleven years of watching had given her a kind of sight.

There went the Halloran sisters, Edda and Pearl, matching gray coats, going down to spend the winter with their sister in Millbrook because last year's cough had frightened them and neither would admit it. They'd been at the point since before Hazel; they'd taught school when Elias was a boy. Edda looked back once at the town, just once, and did not wave.

There went Doctor Pram, bag in hand, called away to a lying-in at the mill town — a woman expecting twins, they said, and no midwife within thirty miles who'd take the case. He'd be back by Christmas if the weather allowed, or not until spring if it didn't. Half the point's children had been born into the world with only his absence between them and God. Hazel had stitched Mara's knee herself two summers ago rather than send for him across open water, and the scar still showed white as a fishbone.

There went the Okafor brothers with their instrument cases — violin and accordion — hired for the winter dances at the hotel in Millbrook. Music leaving the point like blood leaving a limb. What would the long nights be without it? Someone would drag out the mouth organ at the tavern and someone else would complain about it, and that would be the whole of winter's song.

And there, standing apart from the others with a single carpetbag and his hat held to his head against the wind, went Mr. Callum Reeve, agent for the Meridian Shipping Company.

Hazel's hands stilled on the rail.

Reeve had been at the point three days, "inspecting the wharfage," he said, "assessing winter tonnage," though there was no tonnage to assess; the barges had been laid up a fortnight past. He had dined at the tavern each night and bought rounds he did not drink, and he had asked questions the way some men ask directions — already knowing where they're going. How many men wintered at the point now, exactly? Did the widow Ruth Alden-Quist intend to stay through the thaw? Had anyone heard from the company's former clerk, Mr. Fenwick, who'd left so sudden in October?

Hazel had answered him with the flat pleasant nothing she gave to inspectors and salesmen. *Cold enough for you. River'll freeze soon. We get by.* She had watched him write in a small black book, and she had thought, with a calm that surprised her: *He is counting us. He is counting us like I count ships.*

Now he stepped aboard the *Corvid* and the pilot took his bag, and Hazel let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding. Whatever Reeve carried downriver in that little book, at least he carried it away from here. At least for a season.

The whistle blew again, longer this time, a note that seemed to come up through the soles of her boots, and the mooring lines came aboard dripping. The *Corvid* backed water, swung her stern toward the channel, and began to move — slow, then steadier, her wake unrolling behind her like a sash being drawn off.

And on the bank below the light, a girl was running.

Hazel saw her before she heard her — saw the red scarf, the one she'd knitted from yarn bought at the fall market, flying out behind Mara like a pennant. The girl tore along the shingle path toward the landing, arms pumping, boots throwing up sand and frozen spray, and even before the words came up the bluff on the wind, Hazel knew them.

"Mama! Mama — wait —"

Hazel closed her eyes for exactly one second. Then she went down the stairs.

* * *

She met Mara at the turn of the path above the landing, and the girl arrived like weather — breathless, cheeks scalded red, eyes too bright.

"They haven't cast off," Mara gasped. "Mama, they *haven't*, I can see them, the pilot's waiting on the tide or something — please — I asked Mrs. Halloran and she said there's room, she said she'd sit with me the whole way, she promised —"

"Mara."

"I could stay with Aunt Edda till spring. Till *thaw*, Mama. She said — she wrote to me in the summer, she said there's always a bed —" The girl's voice cracked on the word *bed*, and Hazel understood suddenly that this had been planned. Planned in letters, in the summer, while Hazel trimmed lamps and counted ships and imagined she knew everything that happened under her own roof. "Please. I don't want to be shut in. I can't breathe when it's shut in."

"You can breathe," Hazel said. "You've done it sixteen winters."

"That's not fair."

"No," Hazel agreed. "It isn't."

Down at the landing, the *Corvid* hung in the channel, holding station against the current, waiting on her last passenger the way ferries will — grudgingly, briefly. The pilot raised a hand. Somewhere on the deck, Edda Halloran's gray figure turned and lifted an arm, whether in farewell or summons Hazel couldn't tell.

"Mama, the whistle's gone twice—"

"And it'll go a third time, and then they'll go, and you'll stand here and watch them, and by supper you'll have forgotten why you ran." Hazel kept her voice level, the keeper's voice, the voice she used in storms when the lamp wanted steadying and panic wanted feeding. Inside, beneath the level of it, something churned — the thing she never named, the thing shaped like a bridge gate swinging shut and a river going silent and white and final, and herself on the wrong side of it with everyone she loved. "The answer is no, Mara. It was no in July when you first asked, and it's no now."

"Why?" The word came out raw. "Just say why. You never say why. You just — you keep things. You keep everything. You keep the light and the stores and the accounts and you keep *everything* behind your face and nobody ever gets to know —"

"Hold on to that," Hazel said quietly.

"What?"

"How it feels. When someone won't tell you why." She looked at her daughter — at the furious wet eyes, the scarf wound crooked, the fists balled at her sides — and felt the whole weight of what she could not say settle onto her tongue like snow. *Because I am afraid,* she might have said. *Because the last boat out is the last boat out, and I have buried enough people the river has taken, and I will not put you on the water in November to spare you a winter you were born to survive. Because your father went downriver on a day like this and the river kept him, and I have spent fifteen years learning that the safest place for the people you love is where you can see them.* None of it would fit through the door of her mouth. It never did. "Because you're my daughter," is what came out, "and the light needs keeping, and I need you."

"The light doesn't need *me*. The light needs oil. You love the light more than —" Mara stopped herself. Hazel watched her daughter decide not to say the rest of it, watched the girl swallow the cruelest word available to her, and loved her fiercely for it and feared her fiercely too, because sixteen-year-olds do not swallow those words twice.

On the water, the *Corvid*'s whistle blew a third time, long and mournful, and the hull began at last to gather way. The wake widened. Edda Halloran's gray coat grew smaller.

Mara turned and watched it go. Her shoulders shook once, twice — whether with cold or crying Hazel couldn't say, and didn't ask, because asking would break something and winter was long.

They stood together on the landing as the ferry dwindled to a smudge, then to a suggestion, then to nothing but a fading smear of coal smoke against the pewter sky. The river took the sound of her engines and folded it away. And then there was only the water slapping the pilings, and the gulls working the shallows, and the wind coming down from the north with that dry mineral smell, patient as arithmetic.

"Well," Mara said at last. Her voice was thick but steady. She wiped her nose with the back of her glove and stared out at the empty channel. "That's it, then. That's the last boat."

"That's the last boat."

"Until thaw."

"Until thaw."

Mara turned to go back up the path. Then she stopped, and without looking at her mother, she said: "Mrs. Quist's chimney wasn't smoking this morning. Ruth's. I walked past on my way down and there was no smoke at all, and her shutters were closed, and yesterday she told me she was nearly out of coal."

Hazel looked at her daughter. Sixteen years old, furious ten minutes ago, and already — already — doing what the women of the point did, which was notice everything and carry it home.

"She'll come up to us tonight," Hazel said. "Ruth always does, when the stores run low. She's proud, but pride freezes faster than coal burns out."

"If she comes," Mara said, "I'm making the good bread. Not for her. For me. Because *I'm* staying, apparently, and I'm allowed to want things." And she started up the path, red scarf snapping, spine straight as a mast, leaving Hazel alone on the landing with the gulls and the closing water.

Hazel stood a moment longer. Out in the channel the first skin of ice had formed overnight in the eddies below the point — thin, gray-white, trembling plates of it, rocking on the swell like broken dishes. By morning they would be solid. By week's end a man could walk on them, and after that the river would belong to whoever was bold enough, or desperate enough, to cross.

She thought of Mr. Reeve's little black book. She thought of the question about Fenwick the clerk, asked too lightly, twice. She thought: *nothing happens at Gullwrack Point in winter; that's what we tell ourselves; that's what we tell ourselves every year.*

Then she climbed the ninety-one steps, and lit the lamp early against the coming dark, and set the lens turning over a river that was already beginning to forget it was water.
