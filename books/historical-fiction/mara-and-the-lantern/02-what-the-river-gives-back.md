## 2. What the River Gives Back

The river gave back what it took. That was the old saying along Gullwrack Point, and Hazel Nystrom had spent fifteen years learning it was a lie dressed up as comfort. The river gave back only what it had finished with — the splintered oar, the boot with no foot in it, the gull too frozen to rot. It did not give back men. She had stopped waiting for it to do so before Mara learned to walk.

But on the third morning of December, 1893, with the ice forming in grey lace along the cove's edge and the wind coming down out of the north like something with an errand, the river gave back a man.

She saw him first as a dark seam in the water, the way she'd been trained to see anything wrong against the current. Twenty years of keeping this light had taught her eyes to sort the river's grammar: whitecaps meant weather, brown streaks meant the mill upriver dumping its dye, and a long black line moving against the flow meant wreckage or worse. She had the glass up before she knew she'd reached for it.

A body, face-down, arms trailing. Drifting into the cove on the slack tide like a leaf that had given up arguing.

Hazel set down the glass. Her hands were steady. They were always steady now; that was the one thing grief had taught her that she was grateful for. She pulled on her oilskins, wound her scarf twice around her throat, and went down the spiral stair with the lamp still burning above her, because a keeper does not leave the light unattended even for the dead. *Especially* not for the dead. Elias had taught her that rule too, back when his rules still made sense to her, back when he was a man she believed she understood.

The cove was a quarter mile down the cliff path, and she took it at a pace that would have frightened Mara if Mara had seen. The wind came off the water sideways, carrying the smell of iron and coming cold. At the shingle line she stopped, boots in the wet stones, and looked at what the river had delivered.

He was young. That was her first thought, absurd and useless: *he is young.* Nineteen, twenty perhaps, though the river had already begun erasing the years from him, swelling the face, greying the lips. His coat was good wool once, city wool, now black with water. One shoe missing. His hands were bare and torn, the nails split from climbing something — rocks, she thought, or a ladder, or another man's shoulders.

She knelt and pressed two fingers beneath his jaw.

There it was. Faint as a moth against glass, but there. A pulse.

"Damn you," she said quietly, to the boy or the river or God, she wasn't sure which. A dead body she could have managed. She knew the procedure for a dead body: cover him, mark him, send word when the bridge opened. But a living man half-drowned in December water was a different arithmetic entirely, and every term of it was dangerous.

She hooked her hands under his arms and hauled. He weighed nothing and everything at once — the terrible loose weight of a body that has stopped helping itself. The stones rolled under her boots. Twice she nearly went down with him on top of her, and both times she swore through her teeth and kept pulling, dragging him above the wrack line where the winter storms wouldn't reach him, and then, after a moment's rest with her breath tearing in her throat, began the longer work of getting him up the path.

It took the better part of an hour. By the time she reached the top of the cliff her shoulders burned and her gloves were shredded at the palms, and the boy had not stirred once. In the boathouse she found the handcart she used for hauling oil drums, and it was there, wrestling him onto it among the coils of rope, that she felt it: a hard rectangular weight against his chest, under the soaked coat.

She should have left it. Even then, kneeling in the dark of the boathouse with the wind worrying the door on its hinges, some part of her — the part that had kept fifteen years of careful silence — said *leave it, Hazel, it is none of yours, the less you know the safer everyone will be.* That part of her had kept the household fed and the questions unasked for a decade and a half. That part of her was tired.

Her fingers found the oilskin wrapping anyway. Tied tight, sailor's knots, the kind you learn so the sea can't pick them apart. Whatever was inside had mattered enough to bind and waterproof, and to hold against the chest while drowning.

"Not yet," she told herself aloud, and her voice sounded strange in the boathouse, and she didn't listen.

She left it bound. She carted the boy up to the house instead, through the side door, past the kitchen where Mara's schoolbooks lay open on the table, and laid him on the settle before the stove, which she fed until the iron ticked and glowed.

* * *

Mara came home at four, cheeks scarlet with cold, stamping snow off her boots in the doorway, and stopped dead.

"He's dead," she said. Not a question. Fifteen-year-olds always know the worst first.

"He's breathing."

Mara came closer, wary as a cat approaching something that might be either food or threat. Her eyes moved over the boy the way they moved over everything now — cataloguing, measuring, looking for the catch in it. She had her father's way of looking, Hazel thought, and then put the thought away where she kept all thoughts of Elias, in the locked drawer behind her ribs.

"Who is he?"

"I don't know."

"You dragged a stranger you don't know up the cliff path." It wasn't quite an accusation. It was Mara's new habit of stating facts as though their mere statement constituted a moral position.

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because the light must be tended. Because a keeper keeps. Because fifteen years ago I stood on a dock and let a boat leave without asking where it was going, and I have been paying the interest on that silence ever since, and maybe this is how it compounds. None of these were answers she could give a girl of fifteen.

"Because he needed dragging," Hazel said instead, and wrung out another cloth.

They worked over him together through the evening, mother and daughter, and it was almost — *almost* — like the old days, before Mara turned thirteen and began keeping her own silences. They stripped the ruined coat. They rubbed whisky between his blue hands. Hazel cut away his shirt with her sewing shears and found, strapped against his ribs by a leather harness worn through at the shoulder seams, the oilskin bundle. It had ridden there through the river like a second heart.

Mara watched her untie the knots this time. There was no stopping it; the girl's eyes were on her hands the whole while.

Inside the oilskin was a ledger. Leather-bound, brass-cornered, swollen with damp but whole. No name stamped on the cover. Inside, in a small clerk's hand gone wavery with water damage, columns of figures — tonnage, manifests, dates — and beside certain entries, initials. And dates she recognized. Hazel recognized them the way you recognize a face in a crowd you've been avoiding: suddenly, sickeningly, without wanting to.

October, 1878. The month the *Meridian Maid* went down off the shallows with eleven men aboard. The month Alden drowned. Her sister-in-law Ruth's husband, whose wages had fed this family's winters ever since, whose name Mara still spoke at supper when she wanted to wound her mother about something.

Hazel closed the ledger. Her hands were not steady anymore.

"Mama?" Mara said. "What is it?"

"Nothing. Get the broth."

* * *

He woke near midnight.

Hazel was alone in the kitchen by then; Mara had gone up grumbling, and the stove had burned down to a red eye. Hazel sat in the chair she'd dragged close to the settle, mending a sail patch she did not need mended, because her hands required occupation the way other women's hearts required prayer. The wind had come around and was pushing hard at the shutters, and beyond them the great lamp turned and turned above, sweeping its six-second beam across the river ice, faithful as a heartbeat.

The boy's eyes opened. Grey eyes, unfocused, swimming somewhere far below the surface of himself. He looked at the ceiling beams. He looked at the fire. He looked at her, and something moved across his drowned-white face that she could not read — pain, or recognition, or the simple animal confusion of waking in a strange place.

Then he spoke.

"Elias," he said. Just that. A whisper cracked down the middle, barely air at all. "Elias—"

The mending fell from Hazel's hands.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years since anyone had spoken that name aloud in this house. She had buried it the way the town buried the *Meridian Maid* — officially, respectfully, with a stone in the churchyard and no questions asked. The neighbors called her Widow Nystrom and she answered to it. Even in her own head she had taken to thinking of him as *my husband, dead these fifteen years*, a category rather than a man, a closed account. She had gotten so good at the silence that she had fooled even herself, most days, most hours.

Now a half-drowned stranger lay on her settle saying her dead husband's name into her kitchen, and the silence cracked open like river ice in spring, and everything underneath came up gasping.

"Who are you," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice.

His eyes found hers again. There was fever starting behind them — she could see the brightness building, the flush crawling up his neck — but beneath the fever, beneath the drowning, there was something deliberate. Something he had carried up the cliff path inside his chest along with the ledger, heavier than the oilskin, heavier than the water.

"Nystrom," he whispered. "My name is—" And then the cough took him, deep and rending, folding him double, and blood showed bright on his lips, and she was on her feet with the cloth and the whisky and the kettle before she could think, because whatever he was, whoever he was, he was dying in her kitchen and the tending of things was the one law she had never broken.

She worked over him until the coughing eased and he slid back under into a sleep that looked, from the outside, dangerously like the other thing. Then she stood in the middle of her kitchen, in the turning light of the lamp overhead, holding a ledger full of drowned men's names against her breastbone the way he had held it against his.

*Elias,* she thought. *What did you do? What did you do, and what did you leave washing ashore fifteen years later to find me?*

Outside, the ice groaned along the cove — a long, singing crack, the sound the river made when it decided something. Hazel wrapped the ledger back in its oilskin, tied the sailor's knots tight, and did not sleep at all.
