## 4. The Boy Who Knows My Husband's Face

### Hazel

The fever broke on the third night, and by morning the boy was asking for water in a voice like a rusted hinge.

Hazel had not slept. She had sat through the dark hours with her back against the wall and the lamp turned low, watching him fight whatever river-ghosts had ridden him to her cove, and now that he was surfacing into sense she found she did not know what to do with her hands. They wanted work — trimming wicks, splitting kindling, anything but this: sitting still while a stranger's breathing slowed from panic into something like sleep, knowing what lay wrapped in oilskin at the bottom of her flour bin.

She brought him watered broth. He drank it in small, careful swallows, the way a man drinks who has learned that his stomach is not to be trusted, and when the bowl was empty he looked at her for the first time with clear eyes.

They were brown eyes. That was the first cruelty of it.

"You're the keeper," he said. Not a question. His voice had an upriver flatness to it, city vowels worn thin over something older, something from downriver, from the docks where Elias had grown up before she'd ever met him.

"I'm Hazel Nystrom. This is Gullwrack Point."

"I know." He said it simply, without apology, as if arriving half-drowned at a woman's door were a thing one might reasonably plan. "I meant to come here."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind had turned north overnight and carried with it a smell like iron — the smell of the river deciding, slowly, to become ice.

"Meant to," Hazel repeated.

"I've been following you since Duluth. Asking after the keeper of Gullwrack Point. Most people wouldn't say much about a woman keeping a light alone, but they'd say enough." He shifted against the pillows, and she saw him catalog the pain of it, file it away, refuse to let it show. He was perhaps nineteen. Nineteen and already practiced at swallowing hurt. "I needed to reach you before the bridge closed. I nearly didn't."

"Nearly didn't," she said again, and heard how her own voice had gone flat as slate. "You came through the water in November to reach me. Why?"

He was quiet a long moment. The stove ticked. Somewhere below the floorboards, under the ice forming at the shallows, the current moved with its long patient sound, like something turning over in its sleep.

"Because my father's name is in that ledger," he said. "And because I thought you'd want to know what happened to him. Really know. Not the story they gave you."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way the lamp room tilted in a gale when you forgot for a moment that the floor was solid and the world was moving. Hazel set the bowl down on the table with exaggerated care so that her hands would have nothing else to do.

"My husband drowned fifteen years ago," she said. "Off Sandbar Flats, they said. A squall came up fast."

"A squall came up fast," he agreed. His voice was gentle, which made it worse. "That's the story they gave everyone. It isn't what the ledger says."

### Tomas

He had rehearsed this speech for six hundred miles. On the train, in the freight cars, walking the towpath when the trains slowed enough to jump, lying awake in hay barns with his heart going like a paddle wheel — he had said it aloud to the dark until the words wore smooth as river stones. *My name is Tomas Verrone. My mother was Lucia Verrone, a washerwoman off the Meridian docks, and my father was Elias Nystrom, and I can prove it because he signed his own name to my baptismal record and sent money every quarter for eleven years until the quarter he died.*

But saying it now, to this woman with her gray-streaked braid and her father's chin, all the smooth words went out of him like heat from an open door.

"He knew about me," he said instead. Which was not the speech. "My mother worked the Meridian laundry. He used to come by the drying yard in the evenings when the barges laid over. I don't remember a time when he wasn't — not a father exactly, I'm not saying that. But he taught me knots. He taught me to read the water. He'd sit on the pilings and tell me about the lights along the whole river, every one of them, and how a lightkeeper's whole job is to be there when nobody else can be." Tomas stopped. His throat had closed. "He told me about you. He told me you could read weather three days off, and that once you'd kept the light burning through a night when your hand was broken and the lamp mechanism jammed, and you held the shutter open yourself till dawn so the grain boats could find the channel."

Something crossed the woman's face. He couldn't name it. It was gone too fast.

"That was the year Mara was born," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, folded in her lap, were not entirely. "Elias told you that?"

"He told me everything except where home was. I thought he lived on the boats. I thought the whole river was his house." Tomas looked down at the quilt, patched in a dozen fabrics, mended by someone who refused to let anything go to waste. "It wasn't until after he died that my mother told me the rest. That there was a wife. A daughter. A lighthouse. That we were the other life, the secret one, the one he kept downriver."

"And after he died?" Hazel asked. "You were what — four?"

"Four when he died. Fourteen when my mother told me everything. She was dying then. Consumption." He said it the way he always did, quickly, the way you pull off a bandage. "Before she went, she gave me a packet of his letters. Every one he ever wrote her. And in the last ones — the last year — he was frightened. You could see it even in how the handwriting changed. He wrote that Meridian was loading barges past any safe draft and falsifying the manifests, and that he'd been made to sign papers saying the tonnage was true. He wrote that men would die of it. Then he wrote that he'd been moved off the loading crews onto the Sandbar run, and after that the letters stopped."

The stove had burned low. Tomas watched Hazel rise, feed it two splits of birch without looking at them, her body doing the work while her mind stood somewhere else entirely. He had imagined this moment a hundred ways. In most of them she wept. In some of them she struck him. In none of them did she stand so perfectly still, feeding a fire, like a woman holding a door shut from the inside.

"The ledger," she said at last. "You said his name was in it."

"Not only his." Tomas made himself sit up straighter despite the pull of the stitches in his side — she'd sewn him up with sail twine and lamp oil for antiseptic, and he'd bitten down on a leather strap and not made a sound, and he suspected she'd noted that about him the way she probably noted everything. "Meridian kept duplicate books. Official ones for the insurance men, and real ones for themselves. A clerk named Osgood kept the real ones. When the *Marguerite* went down last month — the barge with eleven men aboard—"

"I read about it," Hazel said quietly. "In the papers before the ferry stopped running. Eleven men. They said she shipped water in the storm."

"She shipped water because she was loaded four feet over her marks and her seams had been condemned twice and patched with the cheapest oakum money could buy. And the company knew. Osgood's ledger has the survey reports, the load orders, the instructions from the supercargo — *load her deep, the rates won't wait.*" Tomas's voice cracked on the last words and he hated it. "Eleven names in the back pages, under the entry for October ninth. Wages owed. Wages *owed*, like they'd stepped out for supper." He pulled a breath. "My father's name is there too. Not for the Marguerite. For the Sandbar Flats barge, fifteen years ago. Same handwriting. Same entries. *Draft beyond survey. Signed per E. Nystrom.*"

The wind rose outside and the window shivered in its frame, and far off, downriver toward the bridge, something boomed — the ice shifting, or a barge horn, or just the winter announcing itself.

"They killed him," Tomas said. "Fifteen years before the Marguerite, they were doing the same thing, and they put his signature on the lie to take the blame if it ever surfaced, and when it did surface they drowned eleven more men the same way and called it weather." He leaned forward. The room swam and he ignored it. "I took the ledger out of Osgood's office the night before the Marguerite inquiry closed. Osgood helped me — he's the one who told me where you were, told me if I had to trust anyone on this river, trust the keeper at Gullwrack. The agents caught up with me at the cut below the falls. I went into the water with it strapped to my chest rather than give it up. I came here because—" 

He faltered. Because why? Because a dead man had described a light on a point, and a boy who'd never had a whole father had thought maybe he could inherit a whole family? He couldn't say it. Not yet.

"Because it was the last house before the ice," he finished.

### Hazel

Eleven men. She turned the number over and felt its weight, the way you feel the weight of a stone before skipping it. Eleven men, and beneath their names, in a dead clerk's careful hand, the proof that the river hadn't taken them — that men had fed them to it.

And beneath those, older ink, browner: *Signed per E. Nystrom.*

She had spent fifteen years not asking questions. She knew the shape of that silence the way she knew the shape of her own bed. There had been the trips downriver Elias never explained. The extra money in quarters when quarters were lean. The way he'd come back from Meridian's offices white around the mouth and pour himself a drink with a hand that wasn't quite steady, and how she had decided — deliberately, knowingly, choosing it the way you choose a course through shoal water — not to ask. Because asking might break the boat they were all riding in. Because Mara was small and Alden was alive and the wages came regular, and silence seemed then like a kind of shelter, a roof built of things unsaid.

She had believed she was protecting her family. Now a half-drowned boy sat in her kitchen telling her the roof had been made of paper all along, and that the fire had come through anyway, and had come through twice.

"Elias signed a false manifest," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, level and distant, like someone calling across open water. "For the Sandbar barge. The one that killed — the one my brother-in-law's crewmates talked about for years. Eleven years later they made him do it again, or he did it again, and then they moved him to the dangerous run."

"It looks that way," Tomas said. "From the entries. I've read them a hundred times. I've had a hundred miles to read them."

"And the agents who chased you. Meridian's men."

"Two of them. A big one called Dree and a quiet one called Fenn. Fenn does the talking. Dree does the rest." The boy's jaw tightened. "They'll come here. Maybe not today, maybe not tomorrow, but when the river freezes hard enough to walk, they'll come. I cost them a season's hiding of their worst sin, and I'm a thief in their ledgers now, and thieves get recovered."

Hazel looked at him — at this boy with Elias's stubborn set of the shoulders and none of Elias's face, at this stranger who was not a stranger, who had lain three days in her guest bunk bleeding river water into her sheets while she turned over and over the question of what he was and why God or the river had delivered him to her door wrapped in her husband's sins like a swaddling cloth.

"Mara will be back from the Kettles' before dusk," she said. "She doesn't know anything yet. Not about you, not about the ledger, not about—" She stopped. Started again. "Alden. My brother-in-law. He works the Meridian docks. His wage is half of what keeps this light and this household. Everything you're telling me lands on his family too."

Tomas went still. "I know. Osgood told me. He said—" The boy hesitated, and she saw him choose honesty over comfort, and recognized the effort of it, and hated how much it reminded her of Elias. "He said the truth would cost people you love. But he said the lies were costing them already, they just couldn't see the bill."

Outside, the light turned on its clockwork above them, sweeping its long arm across the graying water, marking the channel, warning off the rocks, faithful and blind. Hazel listened to it — tick, sweep, tick, sweep — the rhythm she had lived inside for fifteen years, the rhythm that had outlasted grief and doubt and every silence she'd ever kept.

The ice boomed again, closer this time.

"Then we'd best decide," she said, "what we do before the bill comes due."

Above them the lamp swept round, and down the stair came the sound of boots on the gravel path — Mara, home early, her voice carrying ahead of her through the cold, calling for her mother.

* * *

### Tomas

He heard the girl before he saw her — a bright, quick voice thinned by wind, growing nearer — and watched the color leave the keeper's face like tide going out.

"That's Mara," Hazel said. It came out half-strangled, and she cleared her throat and tried again. "My daughter. She cannot find a strange man in her house and a stolen ledger under the flour and learn in one breath that her father was a liar and her uncle's masters are murderers. Not in one breath. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Tomas said. And he did understand; he'd spent his whole childhood being the thing that couldn't be said in one breath.

"Then get into the bunk and look half-dead," Hazel said, already moving, banking the stove, sweeping the oilskin wrapping into the woodbox with one motion so practiced it was clearly older than this morning. "Because as far as she knows, you are. A wrecked sailor I pulled from the cove, and nothing more. We'll find the rest of the truth slowly. Truth keeps. It's lies that spoil quick."

The door banged open on a gust of snow-smelling air, and the girl came in shaking frost from her hood — sixteen, maybe, all elbows and sharp dark eyes, Elias's eyes though she'd never guess it, chattering about eggs and the Kettles' dog and the ice at the bridge already thick enough to hold a stone.

Then she saw him.

Tomas arranged his face into fever-weakness, which required no arrangement at all, and watched the girl stop mid-sentence, watch the sharp eyes go from him to her mother and back with a suspicion that was swift, total, and — he thought, with a sinking feeling he'd carry for many chapters of his life to come — thoroughly deserved.

"Who," said Mara Nystrom, stamping the snow off her boots, "is *that*?"

The lamp overhead swept its beam past the frosted window, once, twice, steady as a pulse. Under the floorboards the river ground its teeth against the coming ice. And Hazel Nystrom opened her mouth to tell the first new lie of the next fifteen silences.
