## 6. Alden's Widow Comes Calling

The snow came down in that particular way it did before a hard freeze — not falling so much as hanging, each flake suspended between sky and river like a held breath. Hazel saw Ruth Alden coming up the path from the boathouse road long before she reached the door: a dark shape laboring through the drifts, one hand pressed to her bonnet, the other clutching a market basket she had clearly carried from town out of habit rather than need. There was nothing left to buy at the point. The ferry was gone. The stores were shuttered until thaw.

Hazel stood at the window with the lamp-trimming shears still in her hand and felt something cold move through her that had nothing to do with the weather.

She had not seen Ruth since October, since the Sunday when both of them had sat in the same pew and sung the same hymn and neither of them had said what they were thinking, which was *Alden should have been sitting there.* Fifteen years Hazel had practiced the discipline of silence, but grief made liars of everyone on Gullwrack Point, and Ruth was the worst of them now — or the bravest, depending on how you counted it, because Ruth went on wearing her widow's black like a uniform she refused to be discharged from, went on walking to the company office every second Tuesday to ask after the pension they had promised.

They had promised it in November. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs had come to her door with his hat in his hands and said, *Mrs. Voss, the company means to do right by you,* and Ruth had believed him because believing was cheaper than the alternative, and because eleven widows cannot eat their anger.

By the time Ruth reached the porch steps, Hazel had composed her face into what she hoped was ordinary welcome. She opened the door into the wind.

"Ruth."

"Hazel." Ruth's voice was hoarse, wind-scoured. She stamped snow from her boots without being invited in, which meant she intended to be invited, which meant she had walked all this way for a reason she could not say standing up. "I was passing."

No one passed Gullwrack Point in January. The point sat at the end of everything, past the last farm, past the last fence post, where the land gave up and the river began. You came here because you were coming here.

"You were passing," Hazel repeated, and stepped aside.

* * *

Inside, the kitchen held its usual winter warmth — the stove banked, the kettle murmuring, the smell of yesterday's bread still living in the plaster. Mara was out at the woodpile, mercifully; Hazel heard the rhythmic thunk of the axe through the walls and prayed silently that her daughter would stay at it a good long while.

Ruth set her basket on the table, looked around the room the way people look around a room when they are looking for courage, and then took the chair Hazel offered, perched on its edge like a bird about to fly off again.

"I'll take tea if it's no trouble," she said. "I've walked from town."

"In this?" Hazel filled the kettle properly, though it needed only topping up. The work gave her hands something to be doing while her mind ran ahead of her, down all the roads this visit might take. "The bridge road's drifted to your knees."

"The bridge road's drifted every January for thirty years." Ruth unpinned her bonnet and set it beside the basket. Her hair beneath was iron-gray and flat with sweat, and her face —

Her face was what stopped Hazel's breath for half a second. It was thinner than October. Not merely tired but *worn*, the way stone steps wear, from the same foot placed in the same spot over and over. There were shadows under her eyes the color of the river ice, and her hands, folded on the table, had a fine tremor in them that had not been there in autumn.

"They've missed another payment," Hazel said. It wasn't a question. It didn't need to be.

"The second one." Ruth's voice stayed level, which cost her; Hazel could see it costing her, could see the muscles working along her jaw. "Mr. Calloway says the company's accounts are under review. He says come back after the thaw. He says—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "He says it as though the thaw is a small thing. As though February isn't coming first, and March behind it, and my pantry is not arithmetic I can do on my fingers anymore."

Hazel poured the tea. Her own hands were steady, which seemed an obscenity. She set the cup before Ruth and watched the other woman wrap both palms around it without drinking, holding it the way you'd hold a coal in winter, for warmth rather than taste.

"Have you been to the parish?" Hazel asked. "Pastor Lindqvist keeps a fund—"

"I know what Pastor Lindqvist keeps." Something flickered across Ruth's face, too fast to name. "I'm not proud, Hazel. But I'm not begging yet either. I came about something else."

Here it comes, thought Hazel, and braced herself the way you brace against a wave you can see gathering beyond the breakwater.

"The wreck," Ruth said. "The barge. The *Meridian Girl*."

The name went through Hazel like a fishhook finding flesh. Eleven men. Alden among them. And somewhere downstream of that truth, wrapped in oilskin, lying in the chest under her bed where Tomas had hidden it at her instruction three nights ago, the ledger that proved the company had known — had weighed the storm against the freight and sent the men out anyway.

"What about it?" she said. Her voice came out flat. Too flat. She heard it herself, heard the door slam shut inside her own sentence, and watched Ruth hear it too.

"The company paid out burial money," Ruth said. "Four dollars a man. Four dollars, Hazel. For eleven men. They put a number on Alden that wouldn't cover his boots." She drank finally, mechanically, scalding herself and not appearing to notice. "But there's talk. Down at the landing, before the ferry stopped. Men saying the barge wasn't sound. Saying the manifest was signed wrong, that she went out overloaded in weather no captain would choose. Saying—" Ruth's eyes came up, and they were terrible, they were steady, they were the eyes of a woman who had spent two months turning a single night over and over like a stone in her palm until every facet of it was worn smooth with handling. "Saying somebody knew."

The kettle ticked on the stove. Outdoors, faintly, the axe rose and fell.

"Somebody always knows things after a drowning," Hazel said. "It's the way of towns. Grief makes people talk, and talk makes people certain, and certainty doesn't bring anyone home."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have."

Ruth set down her cup with a click that sounded very loud in the kitchen. "Elias knew the river," she said, and Hazel's whole body went still, the way the cove goes still when the wind dies and the water holds whatever has sunk in it. "Your Elias. He worked Meridian's barges for six years before he took the light. He'd have known the *Meridian Girl*'s condition. He'd have known her master. If there was something wrong with that boat, someone who loved you might have known it too."

"Elias is fifteen years dead, Ruth."

"Yes." Ruth looked at her, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse than accusation. "And I've noticed, these fifteen years, that you never once spoke ill of the company. Not when they shorted the light's coal allotment. Not when they let the channel markers go unlit two seasons running. Not when they docked your pay for the storm damage and called it depreciation. Most women would have cursed them. You kept your mouth shut like a woman keeping a room tidy — everything in its place, nothing out where it might be seen."

"I keep my mouth shut because I have a daughter and a light to tend and cursing pays no wages."

"No," Ruth agreed softly. "But it does suggest a woman knows more than she says."

* * *

Hazel got up to feed the stove, because her legs wanted doing something and sitting was becoming impossible. She lifted the lid and fed in split birch, and the flames came up orange and greedy, and she knelt there longer than the task required, watching them.

Behind her she heard Ruth shift in her chair. Heard the small sounds of a woman deciding something.

Then Ruth said, "What are those?"

Hazel turned.

Ruth was not looking at her. Ruth was looking at the line above the stove, where Hazel had hung the wash that morning because the indoor line was all she had in deep winter — Mara's stockings, a towel, and, clipped at the end where she had thoughtlessly hung them last night and forgotten them this morning, the linen bandages. Boiled, bleached, dried once by the fire and rewetted with the salve, and now drying again, curling slightly at their edges, unmistakably bandages. Unmistakably recently used.

"My hands crack in the cold," Hazel said.

"Both of them?" Ruth asked. "Deep enough to bind?"

"It was the lamp glass. A pane cracked in the blow two nights back. Glass cuts deep before you feel it." The lie assembled itself fluently, smoothly, all its parts machined to fit, and it was the smoothness that betrayed it — Hazel could hear it, could hear how easily it came, and knew that Ruth could hear it too, because the truth never comes out dressed that neatly.

Ruth looked at the bandages. Then she looked at Hazel kneeling by the stove, and her face changed. It was not suspicion that crossed it. It was something older and sadder — recognition, the look of one woman reading another woman's fear in a language only widows and keepers of secrets are fluent in.

"There's someone here," Ruth said quietly.

The axe outside had stopped. Hazel realized she could hear Mara's boots crunching back toward the house across the frozen yard, and the sound of them was suddenly the most urgent thing in the world.

"Ruth—"

"I'm not asking who." Ruth rose, took her bonnet from the table, and began pinning it with hands that trembled only a little. "I walked four miles through a white world to ask about money, and I find bandages drying by your stove in a house where you live alone with a girl, and you lie to me about lamp glass, and I understand — do you hear me? I understand that there is a thing in this house you are protecting, and I understand why you won't say it, because I have spent two months learning exactly what the company does to things that say too much." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she steadied it with visible effort. "But Hazel. Whatever it is. Whoever it is."

Outside, boots on the porch boards. The door latch lifting.

"Whatever it is," Ruth whispered, "it had better be worth more than four dollars a man."

The door opened and Mara came in on a cloud of cold, cheeks blazing, arms full of kindling, saying something cheerful about the drifts — and stopped. She saw her mother kneeling at the stove. She saw Mrs. Voss standing by the table with her bonnet pinned crooked and her eyes wet and fierce at once. The kindling tumbled from her arms onto the floor, and in the silence that followed, the fire popped once in the stove, and Ruth pulled her gloves on finger by finger and said, in a voice gone perfectly pleasant and perfectly hollow:

"Don't get up, Hazel. I'll see myself out."

At the threshold she paused, her back to the room, snow swirling in past her shoulders, and said — so softly that only Hazel caught it, only Hazel was meant to catch it:

"He was alive when they found the boats, you know. Alden. The papers didn't print that part. He was alive, and calling for help, and the company's tug stood off because going closer risked their hull." She did not turn around. "So whatever you're keeping safe in this house — keep it better than they kept my husband."

And she went out into the snow, and the door closed behind her, and Hazel knelt by the stove with the heat pressing against her face like a hand, listening to the footsteps diminish down the drifted path, knowing that by nightfall Ruth Voss's walk home would carry her past every lit window on the point, carrying more than a market basket — carrying questions, spreading like spilled grain, and that there was no way now, none, to gather them all up again before the company's men came asking theirs.
