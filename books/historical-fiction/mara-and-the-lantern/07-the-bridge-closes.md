## 7. The Bridge Closes

Tomas felt the cold come up through the floorboards before he ever saw the river change.

He had been three weeks at Gullwrack Point by then — long enough for his ribs to knit from a deep ache into something merely sore, long enough to know which stair tread groaned and where the drafts lived in Hazel Nystrom's house like unwelcome relatives. He was splitting kindling behind the boathouse when the wind shifted, swinging around out of the north with a sound like a struck bell, and he stopped mid-swing because every fisherman's son knows what that wind means.

The axe hung in his hands. Somewhere below the point, the river went quiet in a way it hadn't been quiet all winter.

* * *

You could hear ice form if you stood close enough to the water on a still night. That was a thing Tomas's mother had taught him when he was small, back when they still lived within reach of the river and before she took them inland to her sister's farm. She'd walk him down to the shallows in late November and hold one finger to her lips and say, *Listen, Tomi. The water is saying its prayers.* And there would be a sound like distant silk tearing, or like someone whispering through their teeth very fast, and that was the river skinning over, crystal by crystal, mile by mile.

He heard it now, faintly, under the wind's new note. The river was closing its throat.

He set down the axe and climbed the path to the lantern gallery, taking the iron stairs two at a time until his ribs complained and then ignoring them. From the gallery he could see the whole gray sweep of the water: the cove where he had washed ashore half-drowned and half-dead, the black teeth of the breakwater, and far off — so far it seemed more memory than structure — the bridge.

The Meridian Bridge was a mile of iron and timber thrown across the narrows, and everything in Tomas's life ran across it or didn't. The company's barges passed beneath it in the shipping season. The company's paymaster crossed over it twice a month. The company's agents came and went over it as freely as weather, and for fifteen years it had carried Elias Nystrom's secret downriver in the breast pocket of a man who made the crossing every Thursday and never once looked back.

And now the ice was taking it.

He watched the ferry make its last run that afternoon — he and Mara both watched it, standing at the gallery rail without speaking, though they had hardly spoken at all since the night she found the ledger. The little boat sat low in the water, overloaded the way boats always were on last runs, stacked with trunks and crates and a piano lashed under canvas. People waved from the deck. Someone played a mouth organ, thin and brave against the wind. On the far shore the bridge loomed, and Tomas could see the work crews already on it, small as ants, knocking the frost-plates loose from the spans so the expanding ice wouldn't shear the pilings apart.

"They'll pull the swing span after dark," Mara said. Her breath fogged and tore away. She didn't look at him when she spoke; she hadn't looked at him properly in days. "Papa used to help with it, before. Before Alden." She said the name carefully, like setting down something fragile, and then she turned and went inside, and the door shut with the particular softness that meant anger.

Tomas stayed at the rail until the ferry was a smudge, until the light behind him swung round and round, throwing his shadow out across the water in long slow spokes, like the hand of an enormous clock counting toward something.

At dusk, the signal flag went up on the bridge tower.

He couldn't read flags — that was keeper's knowledge, not clerk's knowledge — but he knew this one anyway, because Hazel came out onto the gallery a moment later and stood beside him, and whatever she saw made her face go still in a way he was beginning to learn meant bad news arriving without words.

"Red over white," she said quietly. "River closed. Bridge closed. No crossing until thaw."

Neither of them said anything for a while. Below them the lamp turned, patient as a heart. Out on the black water, the ice was singing.

* * *

That night Tomas lay awake in the narrow bed in the storeroom, listening to the house tick and settle, doing arithmetic that would not come out right.

Here is what he knew, laid out in the dark like tools on a bench:

The ledger was real. It was here — wrapped in oilskin again now, hidden inside the false bottom of the flour bin, because Hazel had said no one hides anything in a lightkeeper's house better than a woman who has spent fifteen years hiding things, and Tomas had learned not to ask how she knew. Eleven names in that book. Eleven men sent onto a rotten barge in a November storm by men who knew exactly what they were sending them onto, and who had signed a false manifest afterward and paid a widow's pension at half the promised rate and called it settled.

His father's handwriting was in that book too. That was the part Tomas could not stop turning over. Not just the signature on the manifest — the whole careful record of it, kept week after week, cargo tonnage and hull condition and the orders handed down from Meridian's superintendents, all of it copied out fair in Elias Nystrom's neat clerk's hand, annotated in the margins with dates and initials. His father had been keeping evidence against himself along with everyone else. Or insurance. Or confession. Tomas didn't know which, and Hazel, when he asked, had looked at him with those gray eyes and said, *Maybe all three. Men are rarely one thing.* And then she had gone back to trimming the wick as though she had said nothing at all.

And here was the rest of the arithmetic:

The agents had already come once. Two of them, in good coats, smiling, asking about wreckage and survivors, and afterward Hazel had found her stores disturbed, the oil cases moved a half-inch from their chalk marks, the trapdoor above the cellar tested and left a hair too clean. They knew someone had come ashore. Maybe not whose name. Maybe not yet.

But when the river froze solid — and it would, it always did, by January the narrows became a white road you could drive a sleigh across — the agents would not need the bridge. They would not need the ferry. They would simply walk.

Tomas pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw sparks.

Three hundred miles of frozen river between him and the federal courthouse in the city, and every mile of it passable to the company's men and impassable to him. He was a stranger with no papers worth the name, a face the agents might have seen posted in any river town, a body still mending. He could not outrun anyone yet. He could not swim anywhere. He was sealed into this house like a fly in amber, and somewhere south of the narrows, men were being paid to find him, and they had all winter to do it.

He got up. He dressed in the dark. He went down the stairs past Mara's door, where lamplight showed in a thin line beneath it — she wasn't sleeping either, then — and out into the yard.

* * *

The cold hit him like a door. It was the true cold now, the January kind, the kind that made the iron railing burn through your gloves and the stars stand out hard as nailheads. He walked down to the edge of the cove, boots crunching on the first crust of shore ice, and stood looking at the river.

It was changing in front of him. That was the terrible thing about it — you could watch it happen. Sheets of frazil ice rode the current in the channel, gray-white and restless, grinding against each other with a sound like crockery. Closer in, the pans had begun to lock together, rafting and ridging, building the white armor outward from both shores. By morning the cove would be closed. By the end of the week, probably, the narrows. In a month a man could walk from the far bank to the light itself without wetting his boot, and the company's men would come strolling across the ice with their lanterns and their warrants, and there would be nowhere —

There would be nowhere.

Tomas realized his fists were clenched so hard his nails had gone through his gloves into his palms. He made himself open them, finger by finger. His mother had taught him that too: *when the fear takes your hands, unmake it slowly, Tomi, joint by joint.* But his mother was forty miles inland behind a closed bridge herself, and did not know where her son was, and might be learning soon enough from a company man's questions instead of a letter.

"Ice won't hold a man till mid-January," said a voice behind him.

He turned. Hazel stood at the top of the cove path, wrapped in her husband's old coat, a storm lantern hanging from one hand. She must have seen him leave. She came down the path unhurried, the way she did everything, and stopped beside him, and looked out at the river with the expression of a woman reading a letter she had received many times before.

"You're thinking they'll walk across," she said.

"Aren't they?"

"Yes." No softening, no comfort. She had never lied to him, not once since she dragged him out of the cove, and he understood that this was its own strange mercy. "They'll wait till the ice is safe for horses, because agents don't like getting wet any more than anyone else. That gives us four weeks. Perhaps five."

"Four weeks." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Mrs. Nystrom, I can't cross a mile of open water. I can't ride a horse. I can barely carry wood. In four weeks I'll be —"

"Mended," she said. "Or near it. I've seen worse than you mend faster. You were young and strong before the river got hold of you, and the river gave you back, which means the river isn't finished with you." She lifted the lantern slightly, and its light moved across the locked pans of ice, turning them briefly gold. "But you're right to be afraid. Fear with numbers attached is just planning wearing a mask."

"That's not —" He stopped. Started again. "I keep seeing it. The bridge closing. My whole life it's been the way out. Everything goes over the bridge — the mail, the money, the truth. And now it's gone, and it's not coming back until spring, and by spring they'll have found me, or found the ledger, or —"

"Or neither," Hazel said, "because you'll have stopped standing out in the open at midnight looking like exactly what you are."

Her voice wasn't sharp. That was the unnerving thing about her — even her corrections arrived gently, like a hand on the shoulder steering you away from a drop. But he flushed anyway, and pulled his collar up, and she let the silence sit between them for a moment, the two of them watching the ice build itself pan by pan, patient and remorseless, the way the company built its case, the way grief built its walls.

"My brother-in-law drowned in November water," she said at last, and Tomas went very still, because Ruth's husband was a subject she touched the way you touch a bruise — to test whether it still hurt. "Alden. You'll know the name from the book. The company told us the river was at fault. Acts of God, they called it, in the letters. My sister-in-law believed them for a long time because believing them was easier, and because the pension came regular, half what it should have been but regular, and a woman with children learns to swallow arithmetic like that." She turned to look at him, and in the lantern light her face was all planes and shadows, older than her years. "I am telling you this so you understand what's at stake on that ice. If those men walk across and take that ledger, it isn't only eleven dead men staying buried. It's Ruth. It's her girls. It's every widow on this river being told forever that the river did it, that God did it, that nobody did it. Ice closes roads, boy. It doesn't have to close mouths."

"No," Tomas said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "No, ma'am."

She nodded once, satisfied, and started back up the path. At the top she paused.

"There's bread on the board and tea in the pot," she said. "Eat something. A frightened man who doesn't eat makes poor decisions, and I've buried enough poor decisions for one lifetime."

Then she was gone, and Tomas stood alone at the edge of the freezing river with his heart hammering and the stars wheeling overhead, and thought about what she had not said, which was also what she had said: that she had known, all these years, that the accident was no accident. That she had swallowed her own arithmetic. That the woman who had saved him had spent fifteen years practicing the exact silence he was now trapped inside.

* * *

He did not sleep. Toward dawn he found himself in the kitchen with a candle, kneeling at the flour bin with the false bottom lifted, the ledger open on his knees, reading it again the way you press a bruise.

Elias Nystrom's handwriting was better than his own. That was somehow the worst detail of all — the fine, upright, schoolroom-perfect letters, the tidy columns, the marginal notes in a smaller hand. *Hull planking sprung amidships, reported 14 Oct. Order from Supt. Greer: load as scheduled.* *Barge Meridian No. 7 cleared 3 Nov., tonnage 212 tons over certified limit.* And then, on the final page, the manifest, signed, dated — and beneath the signature, in the same neat hand, almost invisible unless you knew to look, a single line added later, pressed hard enough to emboss:

*I am sorry. E.N.*

Tomas put his fingers over the words. He had done this every night for a week, as if touching it might tell him when it was written — before the wreck, after, minutes after, years? Had his father written it standing on the bridge itself, looking down at the water where eleven men were drowning? Had he written it at a desk with a cup of tea going cold?

Outside, the sky was going the color of dishwater, and the wind had dropped to nothing, and in the perfect stillness Tomas could hear the river clearly now — that whispering, tearing whisper, running on and on under the gray light, mile after mile, bank to bank.

The ice was closing. In four weeks it would be a road, and the road would run straight to this door.

He was still kneeling there, the ledger open on his knees, when the knock came — three blows, heavy and official, at an hour when no honest traveler knocks — and from the top of the stairs, already awake, he heard Mara's voice go sharp with fear, and Hazel's answer, low and level:

"Light's lit. Come up slow, gentlemen. The ice is treacherous this time of year."

Through the window, red-eyed, heart slamming against his healing ribs, Tomas watched two figures in dark coats start up the path from the direction of the river — from the direction of the ice, which had already, impossibly, begun to hold.
