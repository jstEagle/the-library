## 8. Company Men

The knock came at midmorning, when the wind had dropped and the light's glass needed washing and Hazel had allowed herself, for one hour, to believe the winter might be ordinary.

She knew before she opened the door that it would not be ordinary. No one knocked at Gullwrack Point in January unless they had come for something, and no one crossed from town in this cold unless they had been paid to do it. She heard the horses stamping in the lee of the boathouse — good horses, city horses, shod for ice — and she took a moment to fold her hands into her apron, to set her face into the shape it needed to be: tired, courteous, unremarkable.

Two men stood on her step. The elder was broad and ruddy, with a fur collar up to his ears and the settled flesh of a man who ate well in warm rooms. The younger was thin as a pike, with a leather case under his arm and eyes that moved over everything without appearing to look at anything. Both wore the dark coats and brass buttons of the Meridian Shipping Company, and the younger carried a document folder sealed with the company's red wax, which he held slightly forward, like a lantern, so she could not fail to see it.

"Mrs. Nystrom." The elder removed his hat, releasing a smell of pomade and coal smoke. "Mr. Callum Voss, company inspectorate. This is Mr. Pell. We've come to inspect the light."

"At your service," Hazel said, and stepped aside. "Though I'll tell you now the mechanism is sound. I keep my logs current. You're welcome to read every page."

"We don't doubt it." Voss smiled the way a man smiles at a horse he intends to buy cheap. "We inspect all the river lights each winter. Routine."

Routine. The word sat wrong in her mouth as she led them through the kitchen, where the stove ticked and the kettle waited and Tomas lay hidden above their heads, fevered still but quieter than yesterday, breathing in the narrow space behind the lamp-room stores where she had made him a bed among the spare wicks and the tins of kerosene. She had carried broth up to him twice since dawn. His eyes had followed her to the door with an animal watchfulness that broke something in her chest each time.

She put the kettle on because a keeper offers tea; refusing hospitality would be noted, and she intended to be noted only for her competence.

"You'll want to see the log first," she said, taking down the volume from its shelf by the door. "November through this morning. Weather, visibility, lamp hours, oil consumed. All entered nightly."

Pell took it without thanks. His gloved fingers moved down the columns quickly, too quickly to truly read, and Hazel understood that the log was not what he'd come for. He turned pages the way a man turns pages while listening to a room — to the creak of the floorboards overhead, to the pause in her voice, to anything at all that might answer a question he hadn't yet asked.

"Fine establishment you keep here," Voss said, wandering. He looked at the photograph of Elias on the mantel — taken the year before he died, squinting into sun on the ferry deck — and then at Hazel, with a small adjustment of sympathy that she recognized as technique. "You've been alone out here a long while."

"Fifteen years."

"And your daughter. Mara, is it? A fine age. Fourteen?"

"Fifteen in spring."

"She'll make a fine wife someday, a girl raised to work." He said it comfortably, as though complimenting livestock, and Hazel poured the tea and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of answer, and Voss received it as such, because his smile thinned by a degree.

* * *

They went up to the lamp room together, the four of them if one counted the silence overhead as a presence, and Hazel performed her craft the way she had performed it ten thousand times: the Fresnel lens turning in its bath of mercury, the wick trimmed to the sixteenth of an inch, the clockwork that governed the rotation wound and greased against the cold. She spoke about focal length and flash character and the freezing point of mercury, and she watched Pell's eyes travel instead to the storage lockers, to the cot in the corner where she sometimes slept during storms, to the hatch that led down into the stores.

"The river's been giving things back lately," Voss said, conversationally, watching the lens turn. "Wreckage. The *Meridian Pride*'s boats have been finding planking, barrels, a ship's bell washed up near Miller's Bend. Terrible business. Eleven men."

"Terrible," Hazel agreed. Her hands did not stop moving. She had learned long ago that hands are the truth-tellers, and hers belonged to her work.

"They're saying some of the crew might have gotten off. On planks. That the river gives back more than timber, some winters." He turned to look at her directly, and up here in the white light of the glass his face lost its warmth entirely. "A body would be company property, Mrs. Nystrom. Effects, papers. There are widows waiting on pensions, and pensions require documentation. If anything came ashore here — anyone — the company would want to know at once."

The lamp turned between them, throwing its beam across the ice-locked river, across the town's smoke rising small and gray beyond the frozen bridge, and in the second of shadow that swept past Hazel let herself think of Tomas's fevered breathing three feet above Voss's head, muffled under wool, under the drone of the clockwork, under God's own mercy if there was any left on the river.

"Nothing comes ashore here but driftwood," she said. "And once, years ago, a piano. You may ask the ferryman. Ask anyone in town. I keep the light. I don't go beachcombing."

"A piano," Voss repeated, amused. "Well. The river is generous." He produced a card and laid it on the ledge beside the log shelf. "If anything does wash up — timber, personal effects, a man — you'll send word downriver immediately. There's a reward for information regarding survivors or documents recovered from the wreck. Substantial." He let the word *substantial* hang in the cold air of the lamp room like smoke. "The company takes care of friends, Mrs. Nystrom. It has a long memory for them. And for others."

"I keep the light," Hazel said again. "That's all the memory I have room for."

* * *

They stayed two hours. They read the logs properly after all, both men bent over the pages while Hazel stood at the window with her back straight and her heart going like a hammer wrapped in cloth. They asked to see Mara's schoolwork, asked where Mara was — gathering firewood, Hazel said, a lie that cost her nothing because it was half true, the girl had gone to Ruth's an hour before the knock came, thank God, thank whatever watches over foolish mothers. They asked when the last boat had crossed, and who had been on it, and whether any stranger had presented himself at the light seeking shelter, and Hazel answered each question with the flat competence of a woman reciting lamp hours: the boat crossed November the ninth, eleven passengers, names in the ferryman's book not hers; no stranger had come; the bridge closes; the world ends; she keeps the light.

Voss drank his tea standing, looking out the kitchen window at the cove, at the boathouse, at the tracks in the snow — Hazel's own, going down to check the mooring line, one set of prints, only one — and she saw him cataloguing the prints the way Pell had catalogued the lockers, and she understood that these men were not inspectors. They were hounds. And somewhere under the ice or in the reeds or in the drowned grass of the far bank lay the thing they scented: a boy, alive, who should have died with the *Meridian Pride*, carrying papers that could hang the men who signed her manifests.

At the door, Voss paused with his hat in his hand and gave her the smile again, warmer this time, which frightened her more than the other one.

"You've kept this light alone fifteen years," he said. "Alone is a hard country, Mrs. Nystrom. The company values loyalty in its keepers. There's a comfortable post in the harbor office for a woman of your experience, come spring. Warm rooms. Good wages. A girl like your Mara could do well in the city."

"I'm needed here," Hazel said.

"For now, perhaps." He settled the hat. "Winter changes things. Rivers change things. We'll see you again before the thaw, I expect."

Then they were gone, hooves crunching down the track toward the closed bridge road, and Hazel stood in the doorway until the sound of them had gone entirely, until the wind had filled their tracks with snow, and even a while longer, counting her breaths.

* * *

She found it within the hour, because she went looking.

She started with the stores — Pell's eyes had lingered there, and a searcher always returns to what tempted him first. The hatch to the under-floor cellar stood as she'd left it, but the hasp had been lifted and set down a hair askew, the kind of detail only a woman who lived alone with her own habits would ever notice. She went down with a candle and found her winter flour sack shifted two inches left, the kerosene tins reordered by weight rather than size, and the false bottom under the potato bin — the one where Elias had kept his private papers, where she had hidden nothing, where there was nothing now but dust — opened and closed. Opened and empty-handed.

He had looked for the ledger there. In her house, in her husband's old hiding place, and he had found nothing, and he would report that he had found nothing, and Voss would know that meant the ledger was either destroyed or elsewhere, and neither answer would end the hunting.

She climbed back into the kitchen and stood a long time in the middle of the floor, the candle shaking in her hand before she steadied it. Then she checked the rest, methodically, the way she trimmed a wick. Her bedroom: drawers eased open and shut, the quilt folded differently, Elias's old coat — dead fifteen years in a cedar chest — moved, searched, replaced. The Bible: its ribbon marker shifted from Psalms to Revelation, as though a man had hunted through it for hollow pages. Mara's copybook: read. Her letters from her sister in Duluth: read, and refolded wrong.

They had gone through her whole life the way water goes through a hull, patiently, finding every seam. Not because they suspected her — because suspicion required evidence, and they had none. They had done it because it was cheaper than believing in her innocence, because a company that drowns eleven men cannot afford to believe anyone innocent.

Hazel set the candle on the table and sat down across from it and let herself feel the fear fully, all of it, the way she let a wave strike the tower — braced, upright, letting it pass through. Fear of Voss's warm smile and his harbor-office bribe, fear of Pell's quick gloved fingers, fear for Tomas coughing quietly above her head, fear for Mara walking home from Ruth's along the shore road where those same hoofprints were already filling with snow.

Then, beneath the fear, something else rose — something she recognized from the night she had pulled a half-drowned boy out of the cove, something with the same dumb stubborn heat as the flame she trimmed every dusk. It was not courage exactly. Courage implied choice, and this felt older than choice. It felt like the light itself: a thing lit because the dark was intolerable, tended because someone had to tend it, burning not because it was safe but because it was true.

Elias had signed the false manifest. She had kept his silences for fifteen years, had buried her questions with him, had taught herself that quiet was love and love was quiet. But Voss had put his boots in her house and his hands in her Bible and his price on her table, and Hazel found that the silences had a limit, and the limit was this kitchen, this hour, this candle.

She thought: *they searched the potato bin.* She almost laughed. Fifteen years of keeping a dead man's secrets, and the company had known precisely where to look — which meant the company had known Elias's habits better than his own wife did, or else someone in town had told them, and either way the knowing was its own indictment.

She snuffed the candle, climbed the stairs to the lamp room, and knelt at the hatch to the stores loft.

"Tomas," she whispered.

A rustle. His face appeared in the lamplight leaking through the slats, gray still, feverish, eyes too old for nineteen. Behind him, wrapped in oilskin at the bottom of his coat where the river hadn't managed to take it, the shape of the ledger pressed against the boards.

"They came," she said. "Company men. Inspectors, they called themselves. They went through everything — the stores, the chest, the Bible. They know you didn't drown, Tomas. Or they suspect it hard enough to act like it. And they'll be back before the thaw. Voss said so himself."

The boy's jaw worked. When he spoke his voice was scraped raw. "Then we have to move the ledger. Tonight. Somewhere they'd never—"

"No." Hazel heard her own voice come out level and cold as the river ice, and was surprised by it. "No more hiding. Hiding is what killed the men on that barge — everyone hiding what they knew. The ledger stays close, and we get ready, because next time they won't come with tea and compliments."

She looked out through the lantern glass at the darkening river, at the first flakes of the evening snow starting down, and below, far off along the shore road, a lantern bobbed — Mara coming home through the blue dusk, small and steady as a star.

"They'll be back," Hazel said again, softly, sealing it like a vow. "And this time, we'll be ready for them."

Above the frozen river, the great lamp swung into its turn and threw its long white blade across the ice — once, twice, patient as judgment — and Hazel Nystrom went down to light the stove, and wait.
