## 10. Ruth's Testimony

Ruth Alden had spent eleven months learning the shape of an empty chair.

She knew it the way other women knew their husbands' footsteps — the particular hollow of it at the head of the table, the way the morning light fell across it without interruption, the creak it did not make because no one leaned it back on two legs anymore. She had learned to cook for one and a half, the half being what she could persuade herself to eat. She had learned that grief was not a storm but a tenant, quiet most days, rearranging her furniture when she wasn't looking.

And she had learned, in those eleven months, to watch.

The widow of a drowned man learns to watch. The company pays its pension money late and calls it bookkeeping, and so you learn which windows stay lit and which go dark, whose smoke rises from whose chimney, who walks to the point and who does not. The whole shore of Gullwrack was stitched together with watching. It was the only currency the river left them.

So when Hazel Nystrom began drying bandages by her stove in the second week of December — linen strips boiled white and hung on the line behind the lighthouse where anyone with a spyglass could see them, though no one but Ruth seemed to be looking — Ruth noticed. And when the ferryman's boy mentioned that Keeper Nystrom had bought twice her usual flour from the trading post, and sugar besides, and a jar of the good honey she'd never once indulged in during fifteen years of careful widowhood-by-marriage, Ruth set down her cup and looked out her window toward the light, and thought: *Someone is living up there.*

She did not tell anyone. That was the first decision, made before she even knew it was a decision — the way all her important choices seemed to arrive already made, fully dressed, waiting for her to catch up.

* * *

She waited nine days. Nine days of watching the smoke, counting the figures that moved past the kitchen window of the keeper's house — two now, sometimes three, one slight and quick, one tall and unsteady, one with Hazel's deliberate stride. Nine days of telling herself it might be nothing. A trapper caught out early. A cousin. A hired hand.

On the tenth day she put on her good black coat, wrapped her throat in the gray wool scarf Alden had given her the Christmas before he died, and walked the shore road to the point with a basket of dried apples she had no intention of offering as anything but what it was: an excuse, a key, a small honest lie to open a door she suspected was hiding a large dishonest silence.

The wind came off the river flat and mean, the kind of wind that found every gap in a woman's clothing and made its home there. The ice had begun to build along the shallows in gray plates, grinding against each other with a sound like slow applause. Ruth hated that sound. It had been the last sound the river made before it closed over her husband's world forever — or near enough; they had never recovered him, and some part of her, the part she fed at night, believed the river was still holding him, turning him over like a coin it hadn't decided to spend.

The lighthouse rose white and salt-stained against the low sky. Ruth stood at the bottom of the path a moment, letting the basket swing from her hand, gathering herself the way you gather yourself before cold water.

Then the door opened, and Hazel Nystrom stood in it.

They regarded each other across ten feet of frozen ground — two women who had shared a funeral, a church pew, a town, and almost nothing else. Hazel had always been a closed book to her, the keeper's wife with the keeper's stillness, a woman who answered questions with weather reports. But Ruth saw now what she had never let herself see before: the shadows under Hazel's eyes were the shadows of someone who wasn't sleeping, and her hands, hanging at her sides, were the hands of someone holding something too tightly even when they appeared to hold nothing at all.

"Ruth," Hazel said. Not warm. Not cold. Bracing, the way you brace a door against wind.

"Hazel." Ruth lifted the basket. "Dried apples. I've more than I can eat, and apples keep better than secrets."

Something flickered across Hazel's face — quickly gone, but not quickly enough. Ruth had spent eleven months reading faces the way she'd once read Alden's letters, searching the lines between the lines, and she did not miss it.

"You'd best come in, then," Hazel said quietly. "It's bitter standing."

* * *

Inside, the keeper's house smelled of woodsmoke and tar and, beneath it, something medicinal — the ghost of boiled linen. Mara sat at the table with a slate, working sums, and looked up with an expression Ruth recognized from her own mirror: the look of a child doing arithmetic about betrayal. The girl nodded to her, polite and distant, and went back to her numbers with the exaggerated concentration of someone listening to everything.

Hazel hung Ruth's coat and poured tea without asking, and for a while there was only the sound of the stove ticking and Mara's slate scratching and the wind worrying the eaves.

Ruth set her cup down. "I'll say my piece plain, Hazel, because I'm too old and too tired to do it any other way. You've got someone staying here. A man. Young. He doesn't come to the window and he doesn't go to the post, and your bandages are drying on your own line where the whole river can see them." She paused. "I watched this house for three days before I came. If I found him, others will."

Mara's slate stopped scratching.

Hazel stood very still by the stove. When she spoke, her voice was level as a shelf. "And if I told you it was none of your concern?"

"I'd say nothing has been my concern for eleven months, and I am sick to death of it." Ruth heard her own voice crack and hated it and kept going anyway. "Alden drowned in March. They gave me forty dollars and a letter signed by a man whose boots cost more than my year of coal, and everyone in this town tells me to be grateful. Grateful!" She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "I have eaten gratitude until it tastes like mud. So yes, Hazel, if there's a stranger under your roof, it is my concern, because the only thing I have left in this world is the truth about how my husband died, and I will claw it out of anyone who's keeping it."

The silence that followed was long enough for the fire to shift, for the wind to change pitch, for Mara to rise silently and slip through the back door into the gray afternoon without either woman acknowledging it. Some exits are understood.

Then Hazel said, "His name is Tomas," and sat down heavily, as if the name had weight enough to sit on her shoulders. "He washed into the cove twelve nights ago. Half drowned. He had this strapped to his chest."

She reached into the pocket of her apron and set the oilskin bundle on the table. Even now, wrapped and tied, it had presence — the way a gun has presence, or a Bible.

"He hasn't told me everything. He can barely walk. But I know why the agents came asking questions last week, and I know why they searched my stores, and I know that whatever's in this ledger killed eleven men, and one of them—" Hazel's voice did something it had clearly never done in front of Ruth before; it bent. "One of them was Alden."

Ruth did not touch the bundle. She looked at it the way you look at a grave.

"I know," she said.

Hazel stared at her. "You *know*?"

"Sit down, Hazel. Pour yourself tea. Because I've been carrying this since April, alone, and I'm done carrying it, and you're going to hear the whole of it whether your hands shake or not."

* * *

What Ruth told her, she had never told anyone entire — not the minister, not her sister in Oshkosh, not the pages of the journal she kept and burned page by page in the stove because paper could be seized and memory could not.

It began the night after the wreck, when the town still thought it was rescue work and not recovery. A knock at her door near midnight, and there stood Mr. Pell, the company clerk — a small damp man with ink-stained fingers and a face like a man who had swallowed something that was still alive inside him. She'd known Pell for years; he'd taken Alden's wage orders often enough, had once brought her a ribbon from the city as a courtesy, harmless as a sparrow.

He came in dripping and would not take off his coat, and he stood in her kitchen and wept. Not politely. Ugly, gasping weeping, the kind men do only when something has broken in them that cannot be mended by apology.

"I signed it," he kept saying. "I signed it, Mrs. Alden, I signed the manifest."

She had gotten him into a chair. She had put whiskey in front of him, the bottle Alden had kept for medicine. And piece by piece, between the sobs, the story came out of him the way blood comes out of cloth — slowly, terribly, staining everything it touched.

The barge had been condemned in the fall survey. Pell had the survey in his office; he'd seen the inspector's notes himself — planking rotted at the seams, the centerboard trunk leaking, freeboard too low for loaded work. The report recommended scrapping her. But the season's contract with the lumber concern carried penalties for short delivery, and Meridian's district manager had made a telephone call, and the order had come down: load her, send her, log her as sound. And Pell — Pell, who had a wife and four children and a salary he could not survive losing — Pell had copied the manifest clean. No defects noted. Cargo listed within limits when the holds were stuffed past them. Eleven names signed onto articles of employment that read like a promise when they were a sentence.

"The storm was real," Pell had said, over and over, as if that mattered, as if the sky could be blamed for what men wrote on paper. "But she never should have floated, Mrs. Alden. She never should have *floated*. I did the arithmetic myself. I knew the tonnage. I knew." He had looked at her then with eyes so wretched she had almost pitied him, and said the words she would hear for the rest of her life whenever the river wind blew: *"Your husband asked me. At the dock. He looked at the waterline and asked me straight out if she was sound, and I lied to his face."*

Ruth stopped there. She had to. Her hands were flat on the table and shaking, and the tea in her untouched cup had gone cold hours ago in feeling if not fact.

Hazel had not moved. Her face in the lamplight was terrible and still, the face of a woman watching a door close over a country she'd once lived in.

"And then?" Hazel said finally. Barely a whisper.

"Then I did nothing." Ruth said it plainly, because it deserved plainness. "That's my confession, and you may judge it. He begged me — said if it ever came out, his children would starve, said the company would ruin him before it ruined itself, said — God forgive him — he said the dead were dead either way, and what use was there digging? And I let him go. I let him walk out into the rain with it all still inside him, because I was afraid, and because part of me — the coward part — wanted to believe him. That the dead were dead either way."

She drew a breath that hurt all the way down.

"But I didn't forget. And I didn't burn what he gave me. Because at the door, when I asked him — God knows why I asked, maybe to shame him, maybe just to watch him flinch — I said, *there must be papers, Pell. There's always papers.* And he stopped. And he came back into my kitchen, and he told me."

Ruth reached into the inner pocket of her dress and drew out a folded paper, soft at the creases from months of being carried against her heart, and laid it beside Hazel's oilskin bundle. It was a page torn from a hymnal flyleaf, covered in Pell's cramped clerk's hand.

"He wouldn't give me the documents themselves. Too dangerous, he said — they'd search him first thing if anyone whispered. But he wrote this. The second copy of the true manifest — the original survey manifest, the one that was supposed to go to the federal shipping office in Milwaukee and never went — exists. He hid it. Hid it where nobody from the company would ever dare rummage, because it would look like sacrilege and start talk worse than any scandal." Her mouth twisted. "He hid it in the church records. Behind the register of baptisms, in the parish chest at St. Brendan's, under the sexton's lock. He said the company men would sooner read the Book of Revelation cover to cover than ask Reverend Marsh to open the vestry cabinet. And he gave me the position of it, and the sexton's name, and then he left, and I have held that knowledge for eight months like a coal in my chest, and I am *tired*, Hazel. I am tired of being the only one who knows."

* * *

For a long moment neither woman spoke. Outside, the wind had risen, and somewhere below the bluff the ice plates were grinding their slow applause. In the lamp room overhead, faintly, came the rhythmic sweep of the great lens beginning its turn — Hazel had lit it early, though the sun wasn't fully down; Ruth wondered at that, and filed it away.

"You could have sold us all," Hazel said at last. "Pell's story, the agents, the boy upstairs. One word to the company and your pension arrives on time for the rest of your life."

"My pension." Ruth examined the word like something fished out of a drain. "Forty dollars for a husband. Do you know what the company pays a widow per month, Hazel? Four dollars and sixty cents. Less than they spend on the agent's cigars." She pushed the hymnal leaf an inch closer to Hazel. "No. I didn't come here to trade. I came because I guessed — weeks ago, watching your windows — and because if there's a fight coming over the truth of how those men died, then I am *in* it, and I should have been in it since April, and I will not be left standing on the shore again while the men decide things and the women carry the weight in secret."

Hazel looked at her for a long time. Then, slowly, she untied the oilskin and opened the leather ledger between them, and turned to the page with the cargo figures, and the page with the survey notes copied in a stranger's hand, and finally — Ruth watched her do it, watched her finger tremble — to the list of names.

Eleven names. Fourth from the top: *Alden R. Voss.*

"Alden's middle name was Raymond," Ruth said. Her voice came out very steady, which surprised her, the way the sea stays calm right before it breaks. "Nobody ever used it. He hated it. Said it sounded like a tax collector." She touched the ink with one fingertip, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "There he is. Raymond and all."

Hazel's hand came across the table and covered hers — the first time in fifteen years of neighborliness that the keeper's wife had touched her — and the two women sat with their hands stacked over the dead man's name while the lamp turned above them, sweeping its beam out over the darkening ice.

"There's something else," Hazel said quietly. "Tomas. The boy. Ruth—"

But she never finished, because at that moment the back door opened and Mara came in white-faced with cold and something worse than cold, and behind her, leaning on the doorframe with a blanket around his shoulders and a face like a man returning from the dead — which, in a manner of speaking, he was — stood the stranger.

He looked at Ruth, and Ruth looked at him, and the breath went out of her all at once.

Because she knew that face. She had known it across supper tables and church pews and a hundred dockside mornings. The set of the jaw, the crooked left eyebrow, the way the hair grew in a double crown — it was Elias Nystrom's face, young again, wet from the river, wearing a dead man's expression.

"You're his son," Ruth said. It was not a question, and the shock in it was not for the boy at all. It was for Hazel — for the fifteen years of silence that suddenly explained themselves like a lock clicking open. "Dear God, Hazel. He's *Elias's* son."

The boy flinched. Mara's head snapped between them. And Hazel Nystrom closed her eyes as if the light above them had finally, after all these years, found her out.

"Yes," she said. "And that's not the half of what this house is holding. Sit down, Ruth. Both of you. Because if we're going to do this — if we're really going to open the church records and dig up the dead and aim it all at Meridian — then there can't be one more silence under this roof. Not one."
