## 11. Walking on the Frozen River

The ice sang at night.

Tomas had learned its language in the weeks since the bridge closed — the long groaning notes when the cold deepened, the sharp cracks like whips when the frost flexed the river's spine, the low muttering underfoot that meant the water was still moving somewhere beneath, patient and hungry. He lay awake in the narrow room above Hazel's kitchen listening to it and trying to imagine what it would sound like to a man walking out on that black glass with a lantern in his fist.

It would sound like a warning. Or it would sound like nothing at all, which was worse.

He heard the door below before he heard anything else — the soft iron knock of the latch, then Mara's voice, pitched low but urgent enough to climb the stairs on its own. He was out of bed before his mind finished the arithmetic. His boots were where he always left them, toes pointed toward the door, because for six weeks now every night had begun with the question: *how fast?*

Hazel met him at the bottom of the stairs with the lamp unlit in her hand, her face a pale oval in the dark of the kitchen.

"Signal flag," she said. "The ferry house across the narrows. Three red over white."

He knew the code by now. She had taught him the first week, sitting him at this same table with a slate, as if she were schooling him along with Mara. *Three red over white means trouble moving on the river.* Trouble moving on the river meant men. Men meant agents.

"They've come onto the ice," Hazel said. "From the east landing. Mara counted four lanterns."

"Four." His mouth had gone dry. Four was not an inspection party. Four was a search.

"The wind's from the northwest," Hazel said, and something in her voice made him understand she had already done more than count lanterns. She had been reading the night the way she read weather, the way she read water. "They'll have the wind at their backs and the light in their faces. They'll walk straight for us, because there's nowhere else to walk to."

* * *

The lamp room sat forty feet above the point, reached by ninety-one iron steps that turned inside the tower like the coils of a shell. Tomas knew the number because Hazel had made him count them aloud on his second day upright, back when his legs still shook and she had stood behind him on each landing saying, *again*, until the counting became breathing and the breathing became strength. She was not a woman who wasted anything. Not oil. Not words. Not fear.

Now she climbed ahead of him with a hurricane lamp shuttered to a coin of light, and he followed, one hand on the cold rail, his heart hammering against the ribs she had once set herself.

"You'll go up into the lantern," she said over her shoulder. "Not the watch room. The lantern itself, behind the lens."

He nearly laughed. "They'll see me. Glass on all sides —"

"They'll see light." She stopped on the landing and turned, and even shuttered, the lamp in her hand put a faint gold rim along the edge of her jaw. "Listen to me, boy, because I'll only say this once. A keeper's light is the most honest thing on this river, and that is exactly why nobody looks twice at it. Every night for fifteen years I have burned that lamp in fog, in storm, in the dead flat calm when no soul is on the water. The company men have seen it burn every night of their lives going home from the yards. It is furniture to them. It is the sky." Her eyes caught the coin of lamplight and held it. "Tonight it will be furniture again. You will be inside it."

*Inside the light.* He thought of the lens — he had helped her polish it, learned its names the way she insisted on names: the central drum, the bull's-eyes, the brass carriage that let the whole apparatus float on a bath of mercury and turn all night on the weight of nothing but its own slow rotation. He thought of standing among those great curved prisms while the beam swung past, again and again, a blade of brightness sweeping the world clean every eight seconds.

"And if they climb?" he said. "If they come up the tower?"

"Then you'll hear their boots on ninety-one steps long before they reach you, and you'll come down the other side of the gallery and take the service ladder to the oil room, and I will meet them at my own door with a logbook full of nothing and a face full of boredom." She resumed climbing. "I have been bored at dangerous men before. I can manage it again."

* * *

The lantern room smelled of kerosene and hot brass and, beneath it, the river's cold breath seeping through the vents. Hazel worked without hurry — trimming the wick, checking the mercury bath, winding the clockwork that turned the lens. Tomas stood in the shadow of the watch-room doorway and watched her hands move and tried to memorize them, the way a drowning man memorizes a rope.

"When did you decide this?" he asked. "That I'd hide up here?"

She didn't look up from the burner. "The day the flag went up for the bridge closing. I knew they'd wait for solid ice. Men like that don't swim and don't sail; they wait for the world to make them a road." She seated the chimney and adjusted the flame until it burned white and steady. "Everything since has been arranging the furniture."

"You could have sent me downriver before it froze."

"I could have drowned you, too. The ice takes boats this time of year, and the agents watch the landings." Now she looked at him, and her face was tired in the way stone is tired — worn smooth by weather, not by weeping. "You are safest where they cannot imagine you. That is the whole art of keeping people alive, Tomas. Not walls. Imagination. Theirs must fail where yours does not."

She opened the door of the lens carriage and stepped aside. "In. Quickly now. They're halfway."

* * *

Inside the lens was another country.

The great bull's-eyes rose around him like the flanks of sleeping animals, curved glass taller than a man, each panel ground and polished until the dark beyond it seemed not absent but stored — kept, the way Hazel kept everything, against the moment it was needed. The brass frame was warm near the burner and icy at the floor. There was just room to stand between two panels, back against the carriage, arms folded, if he folded them tightly.

Through the glass he could see the whole river laid out below: the frozen channel running silver-black toward the eastern landing, the drifts heaped against the point like waves that had forgotten how to fall, and far out on that black plain, four small flames walking.

Lanterns. Held low, the way men hold them when they're watching their feet and not the horizon. Even at this distance he could see the rhythm of them, four beats swinging through the dark, growing slowly larger, and between them and the tower the ice stretched unbroken except where the frost smoke curled off a seam and hung in the air like breath.

The lens began to turn.

He had seen it turn a hundred nights from outside — had watched the beam sweep the point from his window in the room above the kitchen, that slow white revolution that marked the hours better than any clock. But he had never been *inside* it. The panel before him brightened, gathered, and then the beam took him with it, flung his shadow out across the ice, and for eight seconds he was part of a light so vast and indifferent that the four walking lanterns seemed to gutter in its passing.

Then darkness swept back around him, and the lanterns were still coming.

*Eight seconds.* He began to count without meaning to. Eight seconds of blazing exposure, twenty of dark. Again. Again. He pressed himself tighter into the shadow between the bull's-eyes and understood, with a lurch, what Hazel had built here: not hiding, exactly. Rhythm. The light revealed him and concealed him, revealed and concealed, over and over, and anyone watching from the ice would see only what they had always seen — Gullwrack Point, burning faithful and blind, sweeping its beam across the frozen river as it had every night since before any of them were born.

*The most honest thing on this river,* she had said. *And that is why nobody looks twice.*

His father had lived in this tower's shadow for years and never told him about it. His father had signed a false manifest with this light probably burning overhead, and eleven men had gone into black water, and Alden Nystrom — Ruth's husband, Mara's uncle, the wage-earner whose death had fed this household in grief-money — had been one of them. Tomas pressed his forehead against the cold brass and felt the machinery tick through its rotation like a heart that never slept.

Below, very faint, came the crunch of boots on snow. Then voices. Then the knock.

* * *

He heard it all. That was the torture of it — the lantern room conducted sound the way the lens conducted light, gathering every word from the gallery and the stairwell and delivering it to him polished and clear.

The door below. Hazel's voice, unhurried, pitched with just the right grain of irritation: "Company business at this hour? The light's lit and logged, gentlemen, as it is every night."

A voice he recognized — Voss, the tall agent from the autumn inspection, the one who smiled with his mouth only. "Routine follow-up, Keeper. Ice makes the river a highway, and highways want patrolling. We're looking for a stowaway. Young fellow. Half-drowned when he came ashore, we hear. Bad leg."

"My cove gives back what the river takes," Hazel said. "Mostly kindling and dead fish. If a man came ashore here, he came and went, or the river reconsidered."

"He'd be lame. Favoring one side."

"Half the men on this point favor one side, Mr. Voss. Ice does that to a body. So does age. So does work."

Boots on boards — they had pushed past her courtesy into the watch room, and Tomas's stomach clenched as the voices climbed. He flattened himself deeper between the panels. The beam swept out, returned, swept out. In its light he could see his own breath hanging inside the glass, a small ghost betraying him to no one.

"Mind the mercury bath," Hazel called up after them, mild as milk. "It's worth more than your warrant."

Voss laughed — a short bark with nothing in it. Boots on the top steps now. Tomas counted them. Eighty. Seventy-five. His hand found the release of the service hatch behind him, the little iron ring Hazel had shown him, the ladder down to the oil room and the passage to the stores. *If they open that door,* she had said, *you go and do not look back and do not wait for me.*

Seventy. Sixty-eight.

Then Mara's voice, ringing up the stairwell bright and indignant: "Sir! You're tracking river ice on my mother's clean floors, and the last time men did that she made them scrub it themselves, and they *were* company men, and they *did*!"

Silence. Then Voss again, closer, amused: "Is that so, miss?"

"That is so. Ask Mr. Callahan. He cried."

A laugh from one of the other agents — genuine, involuntary — and the spell of authority cracked just slightly, just enough. Boots paused. Descended. Tomas let his breath out through his teeth in a thread.

But Voss was not finished. From the gallery outside, his voice carried around the tower's base, casual and terrible: "Fine view from up there, Keeper. Your boy ever help with the glass?"

"I have no boy," Hazel said.

"No. No, I suppose you don't." A pause, and Tomas could picture the man turning his lantern slowly, letting its light play across the tower stones, across the lens windows above where a shadow might or might not stand. "Funny thing about ice, though. It keeps records. A man crossing from the east landing leaves prints. And those prints, Keeper — well. They end at your boathouse, and they don't leave."

The wind moved. Somewhere below, the ice sang its long groaning note, and no one spoke.

"Prints," Hazel said at last, and her voice was a masterpiece of dullness. "On my river. In winter. You don't say. Next you'll tell me snow falls upward."

"It falls downward, Keeper. Same as men fall when a company decides a barge is seaworthy. Same as evidence falls when it's inconvenient." The lantern-light below swung away from the tower. "We'll be back. Ice is good for walking, and we've got all season."

Boots crunched away across the snow, four sets of them, shrinking toward the eastern dark. The lens turned. The beam swept the ice and found the four lanterns retreating, small and stubborn as sparks off a grinding wheel.

* * *

Tomas stayed in the lantern until Hazel came for him, because she had told him to stay until her knock — three soft taps, her knuckle on the carriage door, the signal they had agreed on. When he stepped out into the watch room his legs were shaking and sweat had frozen cold at the back of his neck, and Hazel stood holding a fresh-trimmed wick and looking at him with an expression he was beginning to know: not tenderness exactly, and not pride, but the fierce quiet attention of a woman confirming her work is holding.

"They'll come back," he said.

"Yes."

"On foot. Over the ice. Whenever they like."

"Yes." She set the wick down and looked past him, through the glass, to where the last of the four lights winked at the edge of sight. "Ice is good for walking. They said so themselves." Her jaw tightened, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, faster, carrying something he hadn't heard in it before. "And they'll not come alone next time, and they'll not come polite, and they'll bring papers if they're clever, warrants and seizures, the law dressed in company wool. Which means whatever we mean to do, Tomas, we had best stop meaning and start doing. Before the river finishes freezing. Before spring. Before they think to look in the one place on this point where everyone can see and no one does."

"What place is that?"

She looked up at the great lens turning slowly beside them, throwing its patient blade of light across the broken world.

"Plain sight," she said. "Come downstairs. There's a woman across the point I need to see at first light, and church records that won't read themselves."
