## 14. The Agents Return with Warrants

The wind came around to the north on the third of January, and with it came the smell of the city — coal smoke and iron, carried up the river like a rumor. Hazel smelled it first from the gallery, where she had gone at midday to check the ventilation cap, and she stood a long while in the cold letting it settle into her understanding. Smoke meant steamers. Steamers meant men with business. And the only business left on this stretch of frozen river, now that the bridge was closed and the light was all that remained of the season's traffic, was her.

She told herself she might be wrong. She had been telling herself that for eleven days, since the ice had taken the bridge and Tomas had pressed his forehead against the lamp room glass and said, *when the river freezes solid enough to walk, they'll come.* Eleven days of sweeping the point with field glasses, of watching the far shore where the pines stood black against the white, of teaching Mara the new signals and saying nothing about why.

On the morning of the fourth, she saw them.

Two figures on the east bank, small as punctuation marks against the snowfield, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been late for anything they intended. They reached the old ferry landing at nine o'clock and stood there, absurdly, as if expecting a boat. Then one of them raised an arm, and Hazel understood: they would walk the shore path north along the bluff, the long way, because the direct crossing — the river itself — was not yet safe even for them. Even the company's men respected the ice. It was the one honest thing left between them.

"Hazel." Tomas's voice behind her, tight. He'd seen them too; he'd probably seen them before she had. He had a way of knowing things a half-second early, some inheritance from his father or from whatever hard school had made him. "How long does the shore walk take?"

"Three hours if the drifts are bad." She lowered the glasses. Her hands were steady. She made a study of that steadiness, the way she made a study of the flame each evening — checking it, trimming it, refusing it permission to gutter. "We have three hours."

"What do we do?"

It was Mara who answered, coming up the stairs two at a time with the ledger hugged against her chest like a child she'd rescued from a well. "We burn it."

"No," Hazel said.

"Mama—"

"We do not burn it." She took the book from her daughter's arms — Mara let go too easily, and Hazel understood that the girl had wanted her mother to hold it, wanted someone older to carry the weight for a moment — and set it on the chart table. "Tomas. The lamp room. There's a space behind the fresnel's service panel, under the counterweights. It's dry and it's dark and no inspector has ever looked there, because inspectors think keepers keep nothing but wicks."

"And if they search the house?"

"Then they search the house and find what I want them to find." She was already moving, pulling open the drawer beneath the chart table, taking out Elias's second-best logbook — the one with the blank pages at the back — and a pencil. "Mara, get the stove hot. Not warm. Hot enough to brown paper."

By half past ten the kitchen smelled of scorched flour and the logbook lay open on the table bearing, in Hazel's careful imitation of her own hand, four days of weather entries copied forward from December, dated through the present hour. A keeper's alibi written in weather. If the agents checked her logs against anything, they would find a woman who had done nothing but record wind and visibility while the world went about its crimes elsewhere.

She hated how good she had become at this. Fifteen years of practice, and here was the harvest.

* * *

They arrived at one o'clock, boots crunching, and Hazel met them on the porch with her coat buttoned and her face arranged into the mild competence of a woman interrupted at her chores.

Mr. Voss again — she remembered him from the autumn inspection, the soft-spoken one with the eyes like coins left in shallow water — and a new man beside him, younger, broader, carrying himself the way men carry legal paper: carefully, importantly, as though the folder under his arm might detonate.

"Mrs. Nystrom." Voss removed his hat. Snow slid off its brim. "Forgive the second visit. The season's given us some unexpected duties."

"Duties," Hazel said.

"A matter of stolen property." He said it gently, the way one mentions a death in a family. "A company vessel, the *Corvid*, lost in November. Certain documents went missing from her before she sailed. We've reason to believe they came ashore somewhere near your light."

"The river gives back all kinds of things." Hazel stepped aside to let them in, because refusing entry would tell them more than any search could. "Driftwood. Fish barrels. Once a piano."

The young one smiled without warmth and produced the folder. "Ma'am, we're authorized by the district court. These are warrants."

Hazel took the papers and read them standing, by the window, letting them see her read every line. *Warrant for the arrest of one Thomas Merrow, alias unknown, charged with theft of corporate property valued in excess of four hundred dollars.* And below it, in smaller type, the sentence she had known was coming and still felt enter her body like cold water: *Warrant issued likewise for the apprehension and questioning of one Hazel Nystrom, keeper of Gullwrack Point Light, suspected accessory after the fact.*

"Questioning," she said.

"Standard procedure," said Voss.

"Is it standard procedure to name a woman an accessory in print before you've asked her a single question?" She handed the papers back. Her voice did not rise. She had learned, long ago, that rising voices were a kind of confession. "That seems like putting the cart considerably ahead of the horse."

"The court moves quickly when property is involved." Voss folded the warrants away. "Where is the boy, Mrs. Nystrom?"

"There's no boy here."

"You had a visitor in December. The ferry manifest lists a young man, age approximately nineteen, disembarked at your landing."

"The ferry lands wherever there's a gap in the ice. Half the county got off at my rock last month." She moved toward the stove, lifted the kettle, poured — hospitality performed like a barricade. "You may look anywhere you like. This house has nothing in it but flour and lamp oil and a girl who wants her mother to stop entertaining strangers in January."

They looked.

They were thorough about it. The young one took the upstairs; Voss walked the ground floor slowly, touching nothing at first, then everything — running a finger along the pantry shelves, opening the flour bin, lifting the lid off the rain barrel in the scullery. Hazel sat at the table with her mending and watched him work and felt something in her chest that was not fear exactly, or not only fear. It was the particular anger of a woman watching a stranger paw through the ordinary architecture of her grief: Elias's coat on its hook. Mara's schoolbooks. The chair Alden used to sit in when he came to argue politics, back when Alden was alive to argue anything.

In the corner by the stove, drying on their wooden stretchers, hung a set of bandages.

She saw Voss see them. Saw his eyes rest there a half-second longer than the rest of the room deserved. Saw him move on without comment, cataloguing, filing. She turned a hem with unnecessary force.

Upstairs, drawers opened and shut. Mara's voice drifted down, high and bright, performing indignation — *that's my diary, you can't* — and then silence, and then footsteps moving on. Good girl. Whatever else the winter had made of Mara, it had taught her to be loud in the right places.

After forty minutes they regrouped in the kitchen, empty-handed, and Hazel allowed herself the smallest internal note of triumph, which lasted until Voss said, pleasantly:

"Your stores are low, Mrs. Nystrom."

"My stores are what they are in January."

"Flour, salt pork, kerosene, lamp oil, coal oil, wicks, bread, beans." He recited it like a hymn, and Hazel understood with a lurch that he had memorized her inventory during the autumn inspection — that the company had counted her barrels months ago, patiently, the way a lender counts. "I mention it because the company holds the supply contract for this station. In circumstances where cooperation is... unclear... the contract permits suspension of deliveries pending resolution."

The word *resolution* sat in the air like a held breath.

"You'd starve out a lightkeeper," Hazel said, "in winter."

"I'd feed my own family first, ma'am. Wouldn't you?" Voss put on his hat. "Deliveries resume when the documents come back to us. Or when the boy does. Whichever arrives first. The light itself we'll leave alone — federal statute, and we're lawful men." His coin-colored eyes flicked, once, toward the bandages by the stove. "Mostly lawful. Do give our regards to your household."

They crunched away down the path, and Hazel watched them from the window until the pines swallowed them, and only then did she discover that her hands were shaking so badly she could not thread her needle.

* * *

"They'll come back across the ice," Tomas said that night. The lamp above them turned its slow orbit; in the watch room the shadows wheeled like the hands of a vast clock. He hadn't eaten much at supper. None of them had. "When it freezes solid — two weeks, maybe three — they won't bother with warrants. Warrants are for houses with doors. On the ice there's just the point and the dark."

"Then we have two weeks."

"To do what? Walk to the city? The river's closing behind us faster than it's opening ahead." He paced the little room, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, a caged thing wearing a borrowed sweater. "They don't need to catch me, Hazel. They just need to wait. Starvation doesn't need a warrant. Cold doesn't need a warrant. They've turned the whole winter into a jail and we're standing in the cell admiring the view."

"Tomas—"

"They named you an accessory!" He stopped pacing. In the lamplight his face had Elias's bones — she had never let herself see it before this winter, and now she couldn't stop seeing it, the jaw, the brow, the whole architecture of a dead man rebuilt in living flesh and furious. "Do you understand what that means? Not questioned — *named*. That takes money. Someone paid a clerk to write your name into a warrant, and whoever paid that clerk will pay another one, and another, until either you or I am standing in a courtroom being found guilty of something. That's how it works. That's how it always works. My mother learned it the hard way and it killed her."

Hazel let the fury spend itself. She had learned that too — from fifteen years of raising a daughter, mostly — that some storms you trim for and some storms you simply batten down and wait out.

When he finally sat, spent, she said quietly: "Then we don't wait for them to choose the ground. We choose ours."

"How?"

But before she could answer, there were footsteps on the stairs — quick ones — and Mara came through the door holding the ledger.

Hazel's stomach dropped. "Mara. I told you—"

"It was in the lamp room, Mama. Behind the panel. I saw Tomas put it there and I *watched* you pretend you didn't know I knew, and I'm so tired—" Her voice cracked and reassembled itself. "There were men in my room today. They touched my diary. And you sat downstairs doing your *mending*."

"I was keeping them calm."

"You were keeping yourself calm! There's a difference and I'm old enough to see it!" Mara thrust the ledger out at arm's length, toward Tomas, toward both of them, her hand trembling. "Here. Take it down to the landing tomorrow and leave it on the ice where they'll find it. Give them what they want and they'll leave us alone. They said so. Deliveries resume, they said so, Mama, and we have flour for six weeks and coal for less, and Ruth has children, and Uncle Alden is *dead* either way, and burning it or hiding it or reading it aloud changes nothing except whether the rest of us freeze!"

The words rang off the glass and died.

And then Hazel watched her daughter's arm extend further, watched the ledger tremble in her grip, watched her actually take a step toward the door — toward surrender dressed up as sense — and something in Hazel rose up that was older than fear and harder than love.

"Mara Nystrom."

Her daughter stopped.

"Look at the book."

"It's a book full of dead men, Mama—"

"*Look at it.*"

Slowly, sullenly, Mara looked down at the leather cover in her own hands. Hazel crossed the room and laid her palm over her daughter's knuckles, over the ledger, over eleven names and one false signature, and pressed until the trembling stopped.

"Elias signed that manifest," Hazel said. "Your father. He knew the barge was overloaded and he signed his name anyway because a man from the company stood next to him and he was afraid. Eleven men went out because of that signature, and eleven men stayed under the ice because of it, and your uncle was one of them, and your father carried it for a year and then went into the river with it, and I have spent fifteen years not asking why because asking felt like digging his grave deeper." Her voice held level. Only her grip betrayed her, tightening on her daughter's hand. "That book is the only true sentence anyone has ever written about what happened to those men. And you want to hand it to the people who bought the lie. You want to trade eleven true sentences for six weeks of flour."

"He's *dead*, Mama." But Mara was crying now, angry tears, the worst kind. "Alden is dead and Ruth is hungry and I'm scared, and you keep choosing the dead over the living like it's noble, and it isn't, it's just *cruel*—"

"It isn't noble," Hazel agreed, and the admission seemed to stagger her daughter more than any argument could have. "It's arithmetic. Those men can't speak. So someone who *can* must stand up and do it for them, or the company drowns the next eleven, and the next, and someday it's your husband on a barge with a false manifest, and there's no book and no witness and nobody left to say *this was wrong*. That's what I'm choosing. Not the dead over the living. The living over the comfortable."

The lamp beam swung overhead. Shadows revolved. Nobody spoke.

At last Mara drew her hand free — not snatching, just withdrawing, the way tide withdraws — and set the ledger on the chart table between the three of them, deliberately, like laying down a weapon or picking one up.

"Then what?" she asked. Her eyes were red and hard and, God help her, she looked more like Hazel in that moment than Hazel had ever dared see. "If we don't give it back and we can't run — what's your arithmetic say comes next, Mama?"

Hazel looked at the ledger. At the boy who wore her husband's face. At her daughter, who was right about the flour and wrong about everything else, which was the most dangerous combination a person could be.

Outside, far off across the black river, the ice sang — a long groaning note, the sound of the season locking its final bolt — and Hazel thought of Ruth's kitchen, of the sexton's careful hands, of a second copy of a manifest sleeping behind a church register, and of how many quiet women it took to make one loud truth.

"It says," she said, "that we've been waiting for spring. And we're going to stop."

* * *

She climbed to the gallery at midnight to trim the wick and found herself, instead, standing at the rail looking south, toward the city, toward the federal courthouse whose name she didn't yet know, toward whatever machinery of justice might or might not exist beyond the reach of Meridian Shipping's money.

The warrant naming her an accessory was folded in her pocket. She'd taken it — Voss had left a copy, courteous as a calling card. She unfolded it now in the lamplight spilling from the watch room below and read her own name again, printed in official type among the guilty.

*Hazel Nystrom, suspected accessory.*

Well. Let it stand. Let it be the first true thing the law had said about her in fifteen years — because if she had kept silent while Elias lied, if she had kept silent while Alden drowned, then accessory was not slander. It was a beginning. It was the truth arriving late, the way the river gave things back.

Below her, faint through the glass, she heard the scratch of a pencil: Tomas, writing by lamplight, copying names, dates, tonnage — making his own copy of the ledger, page by page, so that no fire, no thief, no frozen winter could ever reduce it to one recoverable object again. She had not told him to. He had simply begun, the way she had simply begun, years ago, to learn the shape of every silence in this house.

Hazel folded the warrant away, trimmed the wick to a clean white flame, and turned the lens to its mark.

Three hours' walk to the ferry landing, the agents had taken. Two weeks, perhaps, before the ice bore a man's weight all the way across. Somewhere south, a clerk was already writing the next warrant, and somewhere closer, in her own kitchen, her daughter was lying awake deciding what kind of person she would be.

The light swept the water, and the water was turning to stone, and Hazel Nystrom — accused, outnumbered, and for the first time in fifteen years entirely unafraid of her own name — began, quietly, to plan.
