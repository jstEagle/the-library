## 16. The Thaw Begins

Tomas heard the river before he saw it.

He had been sleeping in the lamp room for three weeks now — Hazel's arrangement, made after the agents walked the ice with their lanterns — and in the small hours of that March morning the sound came up through the floorboards like a voice calling from under a door. A long, groaning note, deep as a church organ, followed by a crack so sharp it might have been a rifle shot. He sat up in his cot beneath the great lens and listened, and the sound came again: *crack*, then a singing, high and thin, the sound ice makes when it begins to let go of itself.

He climbed the last few steps to the gallery, barefoot on iron crusted with frost, and looked down at the river.

The moon was up, three-quarters full, and it lay along the water in a broken road of silver. All winter the river had been a white plain, silent and absolute, a thing you could walk across if you were fool enough or paid enough. Now it was ruined — no, not ruined, Tomas thought, *repaired* — split into plates and pans of ice that ground against one another at the channel's edge, tilting, shoving, shedding their snow into the black water between. The cracks sang. That was the only word for it. Somewhere out in the dark a fissure ran a quarter mile with a sound like a wet finger drawn around the rim of a glass, rising and rising until it broke off into silence.

The thaw had begun.

He stood there a long time, letting the cold bite through his shirt, and felt something move in his chest that he had almost forgotten the shape of over these long months of winter. It took him a moment to name it, because it had been so long since he'd been allowed to feel it without immediately burying it under caution.

Hope. It was hope, and it frightened him more than the agents had.

* * *

By breakfast the whole point knew. The fishermen who had wintered in the shanties below the bluff came up the path stamping snow off their boots, and old Halvard Bergstrom — seventy years old, hands like tree roots, a man who had read this river longer than anyone alive — stood in Hazel's kitchen and delivered the news like a man reading scripture.

"Channel'll be open inside two weeks. Maybe less if the rain holds off." He wrapped his cracked knuckles around the cup of coffee Mara handed him. "First boat up from Millhaven will be the *Corinne*. Aldous' packet boat. She always comes first."

"When?" Hazel asked. She stood by the stove with her bandaged hand held against her apron, and her voice was level, but Tomas saw her other hand close slowly around the back of a chair.

"Two weeks. Maybe sixteen days."

"And she carries passengers?"

Halvard looked at her over the rim of his cup for a long moment. Everyone in that kitchen understood what was being asked, and everyone understood why it could not be asked plainly. "She carries one berth," he said at last. "Aldous sold passage ahead, back in January, before the bridge closed. Before anybody knew what kind of winter this would be." He set the cup down. "One berth, Mrs. Nystrom. Already spoken for."

"Spoken for by whom?"

There was a silence. Mara, at the sink, went very still with her hands in the dishwater.

"By you," Halvard said gently. "You bought it yourself, back in the fall. Don't you remember? The day before the ferry stopped running. You sent word down to Aldous with the pilot — asked him to hold a berth on the first spring boat north, no matter what it cost."

Tomas watched Hazel's face. He watched her remember. He saw the moment arrive in her eyes — the arithmetic of it, the terrible simple arithmetic — and he saw her refuse to let any of it show.

"I remember," she said quietly. "I'd forgotten what I bought it for."

"You bought it for getting out," Halvard said. "That's what everybody bought theirs for, that week. Half the town wired relatives downriver asking after rooms." He rose, joints cracking louder than the river. "Question is what it's for now."

He let himself out, and the door closed behind him, and the four of them — Hazel, Mara, Ruth, who had come up through the drifts an hour earlier and said nothing about why, and Tomas himself — sat in the kitchen with the news lying among them like something dropped from a great height.

* * *

Afterward, Tomas walked out to the cove.

He told himself it was to check the boathouse, which was true; the fire in February had taken the south wall and half the roof, and they had rebuilt it badly, with green lumber and borrowed nails, and every storm threatened to undo their work. But the truth was that he needed to be alone with the sound of the ice, because hope, for Tomas Vidal, had always been a dangerous animal, and he wanted to look at it carefully before he let it off its chain.

The cove was filling. Water lapped where there had been solid footing all winter; the tide of the thaw crept up the shingle twice a day now, leaving its dark tideline higher each morning. He stood on the stones where Hazel had dragged him out of the river five months ago — five months, it hardly seemed possible — and tried to imagine himself as that half-drowned thing she had found, the ledger wrapped in oilskin against his chest like a second heart.

He still carried the ledger everywhere. It slept under his shirt, against his skin, oilskin and leather gone soft with the warmth of his body. Some nights he woke certain it was beating.

Eleven men. He knew their names now the way he knew his own — Alden Nystrom, whose widow had become his ally and something like an aunt; Peter Lund, the deckhand, nineteen years old; the Okafor brothers; the rest. Eleven names written in a dead clerk's careful hand, beside tonnages and load lines and a date in November fifteen years past, and beside them, in another hand, the signature that had authorized the crossing.

His father's signature. *Elias Nystrom.*

Tomas had spent the whole winter learning how to hold that fact. There had been nights in the lamp room when rage had burned so hot in him he'd wanted to feed the ledger to the stove just to stop knowing it. There had been other nights — worse ones — when he'd understood, finally, what Hazel had tried to tell him in chapter upon chapter of her quiet, patient honesty: that Elias had signed because refusing meant losing everything, and that cowardice and love can wear the same coat, and that a man is not only his worst hour. He did not forgive his father yet. But he had stopped hating the ghost long enough to start hating the company instead, which was, he suspected, where the hatred had belonged all along.

And now the river was opening, and somewhere down in the city a federal court waited — a court, the sexton insisted, that the company had not yet bought, though everyone said it with the cautious tone of people who have been wrong before. And the evidence had to reach it. And the evidence could not walk.

It needed a person. One person. One berth.

Tomas stood on the stones and did the arithmetic he knew everyone in that kitchen was doing, and hated every line of it.

If he went, he left Hazel alone with the agents, who had warrants naming her an accessory and a demonstrated willingness to burn buildings. If he went, he left Mara — and Mara, God help them all, had been looking at him lately with an expression he couldn't read, some mixture of resentment and calculation that had been gathering all winter like pressure in the ice. If he stayed, the ledger might never leave the point at all; the agents would find a way, they always found a way, and eleven men would stay drowned and unnamed forever.

One berth. Two weeks. Three people who needed it, counting Ruth, who needed justice for Alden more than any of them and would never say so.

The ice sang out in the channel, long and high, and broke off into silence.

* * *

Ruth found him there near dusk.

She came down the path with her shawl pulled tight and her breath smoking, and she stood beside him looking at the water without preamble, the way she did everything now. The winter had carved her thin. Grief had done the first carving and purpose the second, and what remained was a woman made mostly of edges — but her edges, Tomas had learned, were turned outward, toward the company, never toward the people sheltering under her roof-line of loyalty.

"You heard about the berth," she said.

"Halvard announced it to the whole kitchen."

"He announced it because Hazel wouldn't." Ruth's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "That woman has spent fifteen years saying things sideways. It's a wonder she keeps a light at all — you'd think she'd signal in code."

Tomas laughed despite himself, and the laugh cracked something open, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Out in the channel the ice pans knocked together with a sound like distant dishes.

"She bought it in the fall," Tomas said. "Before any of this. Before she even knew I was alive, probably. She bought a way out and then she stayed anyway, all winter, with the agents and the warrants and the fire." He shook his head. "Why does a woman buy a ticket and then refuse to use it?"

"Because she's a keeper," Ruth said simply. "Keepers don't leave. Ask her sometime what happens to a light when nobody tends it. She'll tell you it doesn't go dark all at once. It goes dark by degrees, night by night, and by the time anyone notices, the ships are already wrecking." She pulled her shawl tighter. "She thinks she's the light, Tomas. She thinks if she leaves, all of us go dark."

"And what do you think?"

Ruth was quiet a while. When she answered, her voice had changed — lower, rougher, the voice of the woman who had wept over Alden's name in the church records and then dried her eyes and started copying documents.

"I think she's wrong. I think the light isn't a person. I think it's the truth, and the truth needs carrying, and carrying is a job for someone young enough to run." She turned and looked at him straight on, and her eyes were wet and hard at once. "But don't tell her I said so. She'll only dig in. Hazel digs in like a post set in frost."

* * *

That night the family gathered in the kitchen, though nobody called it a gathering. Ruth brought bread. The sexton came up from the village through the freezing rain, arriving with his coat steaming and his face grave, saying the roads below were running with meltwater and the churchyard smelled of mud for the first time since November. Even Halvard drifted back, uninvited, and sat by the door mending a net that did not need mending.

Hazel set the ledger on the table between the bread and the lamp.

Tomas's stomach tightened at the sight of it outside his shirt — it looked smaller there, somehow, and more vulnerable, a worn brown book among ordinary things. Mara stared at it from across the table with that unreadable expression again, her arms folded, her jaw set. Fifteen years old and already carrying more than most people carry in a lifetime; Tomas both pitied and feared her.

"The *Corinne* comes in two weeks," Hazel said. "She has one berth. It's mine — I bought it in October, and Aldous honors his sales." She laid her good hand flat on the table. "So we have two weeks to decide who takes it. And I want us to decide together, because whatever we choose, we'll all have to live inside the choice."

"Then let me say it plainly," Ruth began—

"No." Hazel's voice was quiet but it stopped the room. "Not yet. Tonight we only speak facts. Facts first. Feelings after." She looked around the table. "Fact: the evidence must reach the federal court in the city. Fact: the agents know the thaw is coming as well as we do, and they will watch the landing at Millhaven, and they know all our faces. Fact: whoever goes must be able to travel fast, lie well if they're questioned, and testify clearly if they arrive. Those are the requirements. Nothing else matters."

"Nothing else," Mara repeated, in a voice that made it a question and an accusation both.

"Mara—"

"No, say it again, Mother. Say *nothing else matters*. Not who's safest. Not who's—" She stopped herself, bit down on whatever the next word was, and Tomas watched her knuckles whiten where her arms crossed. "Fine. Facts. Here's a fact nobody wants to say out loud: I'm the fastest walker on this point, I'm unknown downriver, and I've memorized every page of that book since December. Every page. I read it when you were sleeping, and I've read it fifty times since, and I can recite the manifest numbers in my sleep."

The room went still. Hazel's face did not change, but Tomas saw the blood leave her lips.

"You read it," Hazel said, "when I was sleeping."

"Someone in this house had to know what we're bleeding for."

"That is not your burden to carry—"

"It became my burden the day Uncle Alden's name showed up in it!" Mara's voice cracked across the kitchen like the ice outside, and then, horribly, her eyes filled, and she fought the tears the way she fought everything, furiously, and lost. "Eleven men. His wages fed us. His pension was supposed to feed Ruth. And you sat on that book for a month before you even told me it existed, and now you want to decide everything again, alone, the way you always—"

"Mara." Ruth's hand covered the girl's fist on the table. "Enough. Not tonight."

But it was too late for enough; Tomas could see that. Something had been said that could not be unsaid, and it hung in the steam above the lamp between mother and daughter, and Hazel sat very straight with her bandaged hand pressed flat against her sternum as though holding shut a door.

The sexton cleared his throat and offered a grace so brief it was barely a sentence. They ate bread in near silence. Outside, the river sang and cracked and sang again, and the sound went on all night, and Tomas lay in the lamp room listening to it and thinking: *two weeks.*

Two weeks until the *Corinne*. Two weeks until the choice that Hazel had already, he was almost certain, made in the privacy of her own heart — and that Mara, judging by the light burning in the kitchen long past midnight, had already decided to fight for with everything she had.

He fell asleep with the ledger warm against his chest and dreamed of open water.

In the morning, Mara was waiting for him at the foot of the tower stairs, dressed for the cold, her mother nowhere in sight.

"We need to talk," she said, "before she decides for all of us."
