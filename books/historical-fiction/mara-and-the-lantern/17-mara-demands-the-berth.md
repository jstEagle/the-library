## 17. Mara Demands the Berth

The wind had come around to the north in the night, and it carried the smell of open water — that thin, mineral scent beneath the ice-smell, the river remembering what it was. Mara felt it the moment she stepped out of the tower door, and she took it for an omen. The thaw was beginning to argue with winter. Soon there would be a boat. And someone would be on it.

She found Tomas at the woodpile behind the keeper's cottage, splitting logs with the careful, economical strokes of a man who was used to working where he wasn't supposed to be seen. He looked better than he had in December — filled out a little, color back in his face — but when he saw her expression he set down the axe and gave her his whole attention, the way he always did, as if nothing else in the world were happening. It was one of the things about him that made her angriest. He listened like her father might have.

"Your mother," he said carefully, "hasn't told me anything."

"She won't tell you because she hasn't decided." That wasn't true, and they both knew it, and Mara pressed on anyway. "Or she decided weeks ago and is letting us pretend otherwise so we'll behave ourselves until spring."

"Mara—"

"The berth is mine." She heard how it came out of her, too fast and too raw, like something torn rather than said, and she hated that, but she didn't take it back. "I've been thinking about nothing else since the flag went up on the Corinne. I'm the one who should go."

Tomas leaned the axe against the chopping block. Snow squeaked under his boots as he turned to face her fully. "You want to walk into the federal courthouse in the city," he said, "carrying documents you can't explain, to testify about a wreck that happened before you were born, in front of lawyers who will eat you alive before lunch."

"I'm fast. I'm young. Nobody knows my face."

"That last part isn't true, and you know it isn't."

"It *is*—" She stopped, because it wasn't, not entirely, and the knowing of it sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. "I only came to Meridian's offices twice in summer. Once with Mother, once alone to collect Alden's wages when Ruth was ill. The clerks barely looked at me. I was fifteen."

"You were tall and you have your father's jaw," Tomas said quietly, "and Mr. Calloway's man watched you the whole time you stood at his counter, because that's what company men do — they watch everyone who comes near the money. You think agents who have spent three months hunting a survivor across frozen water won't remember a girl from Gullwrack Point?"

Mara wrapped her arms around herself. The cold out here was honest, at least; it did not pretend to be anything other than what it was. "And they know yours? They know your face well enough to pick it out of a crowd?"

"They know my description from every port between here and Chicago. But descriptions are cheap. Faces are harder." He hesitated, and she saw the thought cross him before he could stop it — saw it land and settle, the way hard thoughts do. "Besides, it isn't only about faces. It's about who they're willing to touch. If they catch me, they can call me a thief, which they already have. If they catch you—"

"If they catch me, what?" Her voice cracked upward, and she let it. "I'd like to hear you say it. If they catch me, what happens that doesn't happen to you?"

He didn't answer. Downriver, the ice groaned — a long, low complaint, like a floor settling in a house where no one was walking.

* * *

She waited all day for her mother to come to her, and Hazel didn't, and by evening Mara understood that this too was a strategy: silence as arithmetic, patience as pressure. Her mother was very good at waiting. She had kept a light burning through fifteen winters; she knew better than anyone alive that most storms pass if you simply refuse to move.

So Mara went to her instead.

The kitchen was warm and yellow-lit, smelling of bread and lamp oil, the two smells of her entire childhood braided together. Hazel sat at the table mending a sailcloth jacket that belonged to no one in the family — she mended things for half the fishermen on the point, and never spoke of it — her needle moving in small, even strokes, her eyes down. She did not look up when Mara came in. She knew, though. Mothers always knew.

"You've made your choice," Mara said. "About the berth."

Hazel's needle paused, then resumed. "Have I?"

"Don't do that. Don't answer a question with a question like I'm a child asking for sweets. You've chosen Tomas. Everyone knows it. Ruth knows it. The sexton knows it. Probably the agents know it. The only person you haven't told is me."

Now Hazel set the jacket down. She folded her hands over it — those hands, scarred and wind-cracked, hands that could trim a wick blindfolded in a gale — and finally lifted her eyes. There was no apology in them. That was the thing people got wrong about her mother, Mara thought, the thing the town had always gotten wrong. They saw the quietness and mistook it for softness. There was nothing soft in Hazel Nystrom's face at all. Only patience, and underneath the patience something forged and load-bearing, like a beam inside a wall.

"Yes," Hazel said. "It's Tomas."

"Why?"

"Because he's the witness. Because he carried the ledger out of Meridian's own offices with his bare hands. Because the law listens to the man who was in the room." She said it evenly, each word placed like a stone in a breakwater. "Because the magistrate will ask him questions for six hours and he will still be standing at the end of them."

"And I wouldn't be."

"You're seventeen years old, and you'd be testifying against the men who pay the wages that feed this point, and they would take you apart, Mara, not because you're weak but because you're young, and youth looks like weakness to men like that even when it isn't."

"Then let them look. Let them underestimate me." Mara pulled out the chair across the table and sat, deliberately, the way her mother sat — squared, unmovable — and saw Hazel register the mimicry and flinch almost invisibly. Good, she thought, with a cruelty that surprised her. Feel that. "You keep saying strategy. Strategy. As if it were weather. But I've done the arithmetic too, Mother, and your sums don't come out clean. You say the agents know my face. Fine. Say it costs me something. Now count what it costs if Tomas goes. Count what happens if they stop him on the ice, or at the landing, or in some boarding house in the city where a man matching his description was seen buying a ticket. Everything we've done since December — the manifest, Ruth's testimony, the sexton hiding papers in the church like contraband — all of it rides on one exhausted stranger crossing two hundred miles without being recognized. That's not strategy. That's prayer."

Hazel's jaw tightened. For a moment the lamplight moved in her eyes and Mara thought she saw the argument land — truly land — and then her mother said, in a lower voice, "There's more to it than strategy."

"Then say it."

"You're my daughter."

"There it is." Mara laughed, and the laugh came out ugly. "Say the rest of it. Say you can't risk me. Say you'd rather send a boy you've known for three months into their hands than your own child, and dress it up in tactics afterward."

"Yes." Hazel said it flatly, without flinching, and somehow that was worse than any denial. "Yes, I cannot risk you. I have buried a husband and lost a brother-in-law to that company's carelessness, and I have spent fifteen years keeping my mouth shut because I believed silence kept people safe, and I am done spending my family the same way. I will not hand you to them to prove a point."

"It isn't a point, it's a *berth*, it's the truth, it's eleven men—"

"Four of whom I knew by name!" The crack came suddenly, out of nowhere, Hazel's voice breaking its own banks, and Mara went still. In seventeen years she had heard her mother raise her voice perhaps five times. "Alden I fished with. Soren Lindqvist taught me to read the water. I sewed the collar onto Elias's coat the week before they ruled his death an accident, and I sat in that church and said nothing, *nothing*, while men who signed false manifests shook my hand and called me the widow Nystrom as if it were an honor." Her breath caught. When she continued, her voice had dropped to something scraped and quiet. "I have paid my silence in full, Mara. Every year of it. So don't stand in my kitchen and tell me I don't understand what that ledger costs."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above them the lamp turned in its great slow circle, throwing its shadow across the ceiling like a second hand.

Mara found, to her horror, that she was crying — not sobbing, just leaking, tears sliding down her face while her expression stayed furious, so that she cried the way she did everything, stubbornly and against her own will. "Then why does it feel like you're choosing the dead over the living?" she said. "You want to know what I think? I think you like it. The tending. The staying. I think part of you has been waiting fifteen years for a fire worth burning yourself on, and now you've got one, and you get to be noble, and I get to stay here trimming wicks until I'm you."

The words left her mouth and she knew instantly — the way you know a split second after dropping a glass — that they were unforgivable. She watched them land. Watched her mother go white, then very still, the stillness of deep water.

"You think I *like* this," Hazel said at last, and her voice was so level it frightened Mara more than shouting would have. "You think I chose to stay on this point while the bridge closed and the ice walked up the river and company men searched my stores like I was a smuggler. You think I chose to burn my hand saving papers in a boathouse fire. Child, I have been choosing to stay since the day your father died, and I stayed because somebody has to hold the light, and hold this family, and hold the truth until it can be spoken somewhere that matters. That is not nobility. Nobility would be easier. What it is, is work."

"I could do the work."

"You could. God help me, you could — you're better at the mechanism than I ever was; you climb that tower like you were built for it." Something moved through her mother's face then, grief and pride tangled past separating, and her voice gentled in a way that undid Mara worse than anger. "That's exactly why I can't send you. Do you understand? Not despite it. *Because of it.* Because you are the keeper this light will need when I'm gone, and I will not spend that future to buy Tomas a train ticket. The dead need their truth told. But the living need the light kept. Both are true, and I have to hold both, and holding both means you stay."

"So I'm the light and he's the truth." Mara wiped her face with her sleeve, roughly, hating the wet heat of it. "And nobody asked either of us which we wanted to be."

"No," Hazel said softly. "Nobody asked me, either. Not your father, not the company, not the river. That's the inheritance, Mara. Nobody asks."

Mara stood. Her chair scraped back and the sound seemed enormous in the small warm room. She wanted to slam out, wanted the door to bang, wanted her mother to hear it for an hour. Instead — because she was her mother's daughter, because even her exits were strategic — she left quietly, closing the door with a click that was somehow crueler.

Outside, the cold took her breath and she welcomed it. The stars were hard and close. Downriver the ice sang its long cracking song, the thaw arguing with winter, the river remembering itself. Two weeks, maybe less, until the *Corinne*. One berth. Her mother had said *both are true* as though that settled anything, as though truth had ever fed anyone or warmed a single room.

Mara walked down toward the cove without deciding to, her boots punching through the crusted snow, and stopped where the ice began, black and silver under the stars, running away south toward the city and the court and everything that had been decided without her.

She was still standing there when she heard footsteps behind her — slow ones, deliberate ones, ones she knew.

Her mother had followed her out. Or someone else had.

She turned.
