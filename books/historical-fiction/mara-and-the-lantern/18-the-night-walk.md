## 18. The Night Walk

Hazel smelled the smoke before she saw the fire.

It came to her on a wind out of the southeast, thin and wrong — not the clean pitch-and-pine smell of the stove flue but something sharper underneath it, kerosene or coal oil, the smell of a thing burning that had no business burning. She was at the kitchen table with her mending basket and a single lamp turned low, and for three full seconds she did nothing at all. She simply listened, the way one listens in the dark of winter when every sound has been catalogued so long that an unfamiliar one stands up out of the silence like a stranger entering church.

Then she was moving.

"The boathouse," she said, and didn't wait to see whether Tomas heard her from the back room. She heard him — the scrape of his chair, his bare feet on the boards — and she was already through the door and into the cold.

The night was clear and bitter, the kind of cold that made the stars seem close enough to burn you. Snow squeaked underfoot. The river below the point lay locked in its grey armor, and out on that armor, small and bright as fallen embers, moved two lanterns.

Two men walking away from her boathouse, unhurried, their breath rising in white plumes. And behind them, where they had come from, the boathouse door stood open like a mouth, and light was breathing out of it — orange light, living light, the kind of light Hazel had spent twenty years keeping contained behind glass.

She ran.

The path down to the boathouse was steep and crusted with ice, and she took it without thought for her footing, which at fifty-one years old was a foolishness she would pay for later, though not nearly so dearly as she might have. The heat reached her before the doorway did — a wall of it rolling out across the snow, pushing against her face, carrying inside it the sound the fire made: not roaring, not yet, but eating. A low, industrious chewing, like cattle at a trough.

They had soaked the south wall. She could see it in the way the flames climbed — too fast, too greedy, fed by something poured. Inside, the boat's canvas cover had caught, and the tarred seams were spitting black smoke, and along the workbench where Elias had once planed cedar until his forearms ached, the fire was walking hand over hand toward the shelf.

Toward the shelf where the ledger lived.

Not the ledger itself — no, after the warrants, after the agents had seized the stores and left them with flour gone short and oil rationed by her own careful hand, Hazel had moved everything. The ledger, Ruth's copied pages, the manifest pulled from behind the church register: all of it wrapped in oilcloth and wool and packed into the old signal-box, the tin-lined case that once held the spare wicks and the red lens, and the box had gone into the floor beneath the workbench, under a board Elias had always meant to replace and never had. The fire wanted the shelf because the fire wanted what the shelf represented. But the papers were three feet below it and eight inches of seasoned plank between.

Three feet. Eight inches. It seemed very little, standing there with the heat pressing her back like a hand flat against her chest.

"Hazel!" Tomas's voice behind her, high and cracked with sleep and fear. "Don't — Hazel, don't go in—"

But she was already going in.

Later, she would try to reconstruct the decision and find that there hadn't been one. There was only the knowledge, older than reasoning, that those papers were the eleven men — Alden among them, and the boy Soren Lindqvist who was nineteen, and the engineer Voss who had argued with the loading master and been overruled, all of them, their names in ink in a dead man's careful hand — and that names do not survive fire unless someone carries them out. The company had sent two men with lanterns and a can of coal oil to make sure those names never reached a court. They had walked across the frozen river to do it, calm as men checking traplines.

She went through the doorway into the heat.

* * *

Inside, the world was red and roaring. The canvas cover of the boat had become a sheet of flame hanging in the air, and the smoke banked down from the rafters in a slow black tide, and Hazel dropped to a crouch where the air was still breathable and crawled toward the workbench with her skirts hauled up and her heart slamming like surf.

The board. Her fingers found the edge of it, the gap Elias's chisel had left, and she hooked them in and pulled, and the board came up stiff and unwilling, and beneath it the signal-box sat in its nest of dust and mouse-nest wool, cool as a grave.

Cool as a grave. She would remember thinking that, afterward. She would remember being ashamed of it.

She got the box into her arms. It was heavier than she remembered, forty pounds of tin and paper and dead men's testimony, and she rose with it hugged against her chest and turned for the door, and that was when the wall gave.

The south wall — the one they'd doused — surrendered all at once, a section of it folding inward with a sound like a tree falling, and the fire came down with it in a bright avalanche, and a tongue of flame caught her sleeve, then her skirt, then her right hand where it gripped the box's hot tin handle.

The pain arrived a half-second behind the fact of it, the way thunder follows lightning, and when it arrived it did not knock. It simply moved in. Hazel felt the skin of her palm and fingers sear against metal heated past bearing, felt the flesh of her forearm scorch where the flame had taken her sleeve, and she heard herself cry out — a short, hard sound, nothing like a scream, more like the noise a rope makes when it parts under load.

She did not drop the box.

That was the thing she would hold onto in the weeks after, turning it over like a stone found on the beach: she did not drop it. Her body, burning, kept faith with the purpose her mind had set it. She stumbled two steps through the smoke, blind now, eyes streaming, the whole boathouse a furnace around her, and the door was somewhere ahead and to the left, or maybe to the right, and for one black moment she understood exactly how a person could die ten feet from safety, turning in circles in the dark—

Hands closed on her shoulders from behind.

"Here — here, I've got you—" Tomas, coughing, half-dragging her, his arm around her waist like a hoop of iron. He had come in after her, this boy who had nearly drowned in October, who knew better than any living soul what water and cold and panic could take from a person, and he hauled her bodily through the smoke with the box still crushed between her scorched hand and her ribs, and then the doorway was around them and the cold air hit like a plunge into the cove and they went down together into the snow.

She remembered rolling. Someone had told her once — a fisherman's wife, decades ago — roll in the snow if your clothes catch, and her body obeyed while her mind stood apart and watched, and the fire in her skirt died with a hiss and a smell like pork crackling that she would not be able to eat pork again for a year. Tomas beat at her sleeve with his bare hands, swearing softly, over and over, words she'd never taught anyone, and then his hands stopped and closed around hers instead, around the ruined ones, and he said her name twice, three times, and she realized she was making a sound, a low continuous note like the foghorn winding up, and she made herself stop.

"Tomas." Her voice came out sanded flat. "The box."

"You're holding it. You never let go of it, you mad woman, you never—" His voice broke. He was nineteen years old and shaking, and snow was settling on both of them, and above the point the great lamp turned and turned in its room of glass, sweeping its steady beam across the ice, indifferent and faithful, lighting the two of them and the burning boathouse and the whole white world beyond.

And the two men with the lanterns, halfway to mid-river, had stopped.

* * *

They stood perhaps two hundred yards out on the ice, and the firelight and the lamplight together showed them plainly: the taller one, Mr. Corliss, whom Hazel had served coffee in her own kitchen eleven days ago while he asked his pointed questions about wreckage and survivors; and the shorter one whose name she had never learned, the one who had lingered by her stores and handled her tinned meat as if pricing it. They stood with their lanterns raised, and they were looking back at the boathouse, and at her, and they must have known — the distance was nothing, the night was clear — that she could see them as well as they could see her.

Corliss did not run. That was the remarkable thing. A guilty man would have run, would have turned and fled across the ice into the dark and hoped the darkness swallowed him. Corliss stood quite still for a moment, lantern lifted, like a man admiring his own work, and then he turned, unhurried, and began to walk — walk — north along the shore, toward the landing road, his companion falling in beside him. As if they had every right to be leaving. As if a burned keeper and a burning boathouse were merely weather.

"They'll say it was accidental," Tomas said. His teeth were chattering; he had given his coat to her without asking, and she was too far gone in pain to protest. "Oil stove tipped. Faulty flue. They'll swear they were never here."

"They crossed open ice with lanterns," Hazel said. "On a night when half the town sits at windows facing the river because there's nothing else to look at in February." She looked down at her hand.

It did not look like her hand. The palm was seared white across the pads of the fingers, and already the edges of the burn had gone the deep red of raw meat, and blisters were rising along the thumb like small pale eggs. The pain came off it in waves, each one arriving with a delay, so that she experienced her own hand slightly in the past. She had tended burns before — lighthouse keepers collect them the way farmers collect scars — but never her own, and never like this.

"Mara will have the wagon out," Tomas said. "I woke her. She's hitching Bess now." He hesitated. "Hazel — I should have stopped you. I should have gone in first."

"No." She looked at him. In the firelight his face was young and appalled and absolutely certain, and she saw Elias in it suddenly, not the tired man of the last years but the young Elias, the one from the courting days, and she had to look away. "If you'd gone in first, we'd have two burned people and the same box. And you'd have dropped it. You'd have done the sensible thing." She managed something that was almost a smile. "Sensible is why we're in this trouble to begin with."

He didn't laugh. He took the box from her arms instead, gently, prying her scorched fingers loose one at a time while she hissed through her teeth, and set it in the snow, and stamped out a thread of smolder on her skirt hem with his boot.

Down the point, hooves on frozen ruts. Mara driving the wagon at a pace that would have earned her a scolding in any season but this one, the horse blowing steam, Mara herself half-standing on the bench with her shawl flying, and behind the wagon — Hazel saw it then, and felt something shift in her chest that had nothing to do with burns — lights appearing in windows. The Halvorsens'. The Ostergaards' at the head of the lane. Old Mrs. Brandt's candle, then her figure in the doorway in a nightcap, then her son pulling a coat over his shoulders. Doors opening all down the shore road, one after another, the way lamps come up along a channel at dusk.

They had seen. All of them. Two men had walked across the ice of Gullwrack Cove on a February night and put coal oil to the lighthouse keeper's boathouse, and the lighthouse keeper had gone into the fire and come out of it with the proof in her arms, and the whole town had watched it happen by the light of the lamp their wages from Meridian Shipping had failed to buy.

Mara reined in and was on the ground before the wagon stopped. "Mother — Mother, your hand, oh God, your hand—" She caught herself, the way she had learned to catch herself all winter, grief and fury and love all fighting for the same small space behind her face. "Get her up. Get her up gently. We need snowmelt and the lanolin, and the clean linen, and — Tomas, carry the box. Don't argue. Carry it."

Hazel let them lift her. The pain was a country she was traveling through now, vast and slowing, and from somewhere inside it she watched the boathouse burn — watched Elias's plane and his augers and the cedar strips he'd milled for a boat he never finished go up in a brightness that would have offended every instinct she owned. Twenty-two years a keeper, and she had never once lost a structure to fire. She had kept the lamp, kept the oil, kept the wicks trimmed and the chimneys clean. Fire had been her servant, her whole working life.

These men had made it their weapon, and they had done it in front of witnesses, because they believed — she could see it in the memory of Corliss's unhurried walk, in the set of his shoulders — that witnesses on Gullwrack Point counted for nothing. That fear was the law here. That the town would watch and weigh and conclude that silence was safer, the way it had concluded such things for fifteen years, the way she herself had concluded them, and gone back to bed.

* * *

The kitchen, an hour later, was full.

That was the thing Hazel kept returning to, lying propped on the settle with her hand bandaged in linen soaked in cool boracic solution, Mara's hands moving with a competence that startled her — when had her daughter learned this? — while the kitchen filled with neighbors. Mrs. Brandt and her son Nils. The Halvorsen brothers, who fished the spring run and signed the company's manifests every year without reading them, until this winter. Even Peter Lund, the ferryman's brother, who owed the company his berth and everyone knew it, standing awkwardly just inside the door with his cap in his hands.

Ruth had come too. She sat near the stove saying nothing, her face lit orange by the firebox, and her stillness had a different weight than the others'. Ruth had buried Alden. Ruth had spent fifteen years being polite to men like Corliss because politeness was all a widow was issued.

Nils Brandt spoke first, in his slow way, addressing the middle of the room rather than any person in it. "We seen them cross out. Me and my mother both. Lanterns, and the tall one in the good coat." He paused. "I'd swear to it. Before whoever asks."

"So would I," said Mrs. Brandt, from her chair, in a voice that permitted no discussion.

Peter Lund cleared his throat. "The ferry company'll want my statement wrote proper," he said, to his cap. "Signed. Dated." He looked up, and his eyes were miserable and resolved at once. "My brother'll be furious. I want that understood. He'll call me a fool, and he may be right." He straightened. "But I seen them too."

One by one, in the firelit kitchen smelling of lanolin and woodsmoke and scorched wool, they said their pieces. Not eloquently. Not bravely, even — several of them looked frightened, and were frightened, and stayed anyway. Hazel lay on the settle and listened with her bandaged hand resting on her breastbone like something precious and broken, and understood that something had happened tonight larger than a fire and larger than a crime. The company had bet everything on the town's silence, and the town had answered by filling a kitchen.

When they had gone, trailing out into the cold with murmured instructions about the doctor at the bridge settlement and offers of milk and firewood, Ruth remained. She rose, crossed to the settle, and looked down at Hazel with an expression Hazel had never seen on her before — not grief, not gratitude, but something harder and cleaner underneath both.

"Fifteen years," Ruth said quietly. "Fifteen years I kept my mouth shut because I had four children and no husband and the pension money came regular, and I told myself that was wisdom." Her jaw worked. "They tried to burn it tonight, Hazel. In front of us. They're done pretending, and so am I."

From the back room came the sound of Tomas turning pages in the rescued box — reading the names aloud to himself, soft as a prayer. Mara stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the boathouse smoke itself out against the stars, and when she turned, her face was wet and set.

"The magistrate comes with the thaw," Mara said. "Fourteen days, maybe less. What do we do until then?"

Hazel looked at her bandaged hand, and at Ruth, and at the tin box glowing faintly with reflected firelight, and heard herself answer with a steadiness she did not entirely feel:

"Now? Now they learn what it costs to make an enemy of the whole point."
