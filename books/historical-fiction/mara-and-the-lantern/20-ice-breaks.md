## 20. Ice Breaks

The river had been talking for three days.

Tomas heard it first from the lamp room, where he'd taken to standing the dawn watch with Hazel — not because she needed him, but because neither of them could sleep anymore, and because the glass above Gullwrack Point gave the best view of the ice. The sound came up through the floorboards and into his chest, a low groan like a giant turning over in its sleep, followed by a crack that ran the length of the river and echoed off the far bluffs. Then silence. Then, an hour later, another groan, deeper than the last.

"It's letting go," Hazel said beside him. She said it the way she said everything now — flat, economical, her eyes never leaving the water. The burn on her hand was wrapped in clean linen, but he could see how carefully she held it away from her body, how every movement cost her something she refused to name. "Three days of warm rain off the south. By tomorrow night there'll be open leads. By the day after, the channel will run."

"And the boat."

"The *Maren* comes up from the mouth with the morning tide. One berth. I bought it in January, when you were still too sick to know your own name." She turned to look at him then, and in the gray light her eyes were the color of the river before freeze-up — dark, moving, impossible to read all the way down. "You'll be on it."

He wanted to argue. He had argued, for weeks, in the kitchen and on the stairs and once, badly, out on the ice itself until Mara shouted at them both to come inside before they fell through and settled the matter for good. But he had stopped arguing two nights ago, after the boathouse fire, after he pulled Hazel out of the smoke with her sleeve burning and her hand already blistered, after the whole town stood in the snow watching the company men walk away with their lanterns swinging like they owned the night. After that, argument felt like a luxury left over from some other life.

"I'm frightened," he said. It was easier to say than *I don't want to leave you*, so he said that instead, or tried to.

"Good," Hazel said. "Frightened men check their knots twice." She went down the stairs to make coffee, and Tomas stayed at the window and watched the ice groan and shift, and thought about the ledger wrapped in oilskin under his shirt, warm against his skin like a second heartbeat.

* * *

The *Maren* appeared just after seven, riding the tide up the opened channel, her hull scarred white along the waterline where she'd ground through the lake ice at the mouth. Tomas saw her from the point and felt something turn over in him that wasn't quite hope and wasn't quite grief. Behind her, far downriver, the shore haze hid the city, but not what the city meant: the federal courthouse, the magistrate who would listen, the testimony he had rehearsed a hundred times against the ceiling of his bunk in the Nystrom house. *My name is Tomas Vey. My father was Elias Nystrom. I carry the manifest of the barge* Corinne *, which sailed overloaded from the Meridian dock on the fourth of November, 1892—*

"They'll be on the shore road by now," Ruth said behind him. She had come up before light with her good coat and a parcel of bread and salt pork, moving with the brisk competence of a woman who had decided that if the world intended to break her heart again it would have to work for it. She pressed the parcel into his hands. "Alden's brother saw lanterns past the narrows an hour ago. Six of them, maybe eight."

"On foot?"

"On the ice, where it still holds. They won't wait for the ferry landing. They mean to take you off the boat, or stop it from leaving." Ruth's face did something complicated then — the widow's face, the face that had learned this winter to hold grief and fury in the same expression without either one winning. "Hazel's gone down to the lamp."

Tomas followed the stair down two at a time and found Hazel in the lamp room already, working. She had the great lens open, its brass ribs gleaming, and she was doing something he had never seen a keeper do in daylight: trimming the wick high, filling the reservoir to the brim, testing the draft until the flame stood tall and steady in the middle of the morning.

"It's daylight," he said stupidly.

"It is." She struck the match one-handed, shielding the flame against her body, and touched it to the wick. The lamp caught with a soft roar, and the lens gathered the light and threw it out across the breaking river in a long white blade. "Daylight's exactly when they won't expect it. And daylight's when their aim matters most." She nodded toward the shore, where — yes — small figures were visible now, dark against the gray-white ice, moving fast toward the channel. "They've got rifles. I watched them load them from the church tower yesterday. A man shooting across open water needs to see his target, and a man looking down a rifle barrel at noon doesn't expect the sun to come find him."

"You'll blind them."

"I'll mark the channel," she said. "Same thing, done politely." And there it was, just for a moment, the ghost of a smile — the same crooked half-smile he had seen in the photograph of Elias that she kept face-down in a drawer, the smile he carried in his own mirror. He looked away from it because looking at it felt like drowning gently.

"Mara's on the landing," Hazel said. "She'll row you out when the *Maren* signals. You don't touch the ledger except to feel it's there. You don't speak to anyone on the boat about why you're traveling. You go straight to the federal building and ask for Magistrate Corliss by name — Ruth has the letter sewn in your coat lining, with the sexton's seal. You give them everything, and then—" Her voice caught, just barely, a hairline crack in masonry. "Then you write to us. First ferry of every month. The light will be burning."

"Hazel—"

"Don't." She turned back to the lamp, adjusting the wick with her bandaged hand, not flinching, though he saw the tendons stand in her wrist. "If you say goodbye properly I'll lose my nerve, and I haven't got any to spare."

* * *

The signal came at half past eight — three blasts of the *Maren*'s whistle, hanging in the cold air like something physical. Below, Mara was already pushing off from the landing in the dory, oars biting the black open water of the lead. Tomas went down the tower stairs with Ruth's bread under his arm and the ledger against his heart and the letter against his ribs, and every step sounded in him like a nail going home.

At the landing, Mara held the dory against the current, her face set in the hard, furious composure she had worn since the night her mother ruled the family council. She hadn't forgiven him. He didn't think she'd forgiven Hazel either. But she took his arm as he stepped down into the boat, gripping hard enough to bruise, and said, "You fall in this water, I'm not coming after you. The water's forty degrees and my mother would kill me twice."

"Noted."

"She means it, you know. The letters. First ferry of every month. She'll pretend she doesn't count the days, and she'll count them wrong on purpose so nobody catches her counting." Mara shipped the oars and pulled for the channel, and the *Maren* grew out of the mist ahead, her crew already lowering a rope ladder. "And Tomas — when you testify. Say Alden's name loud. Ruth won't ask you to, so I'm asking."

"I will."

On the shore, distantly, a rifle shot cracked across the ice.

It hit nothing — a ranging shot, a warning — but the sound broke over the water like a whip, and the *Maren*'s crew began shouting, and Mara pulled harder, the dory leaping through the chop. Another shot, closer. Tomas turned and saw the figures on the ice running now, spread out along the shore road and out onto the last solid pan near the point, lantern-light useless in the growing brightness, and above them — impossibly, gloriously — the beam of the Gullwrack light sweeping the river in full daylight, blazing off the broken floes, a white wheel of fire turning and turning, so bright that the men on the ice threw up their arms and shielded their eyes and stumbled, blinded by the very instrument they had come to extinguish.

"She's burning the lamp at noon," Mara said, half laughing, half sobbing, pulling for the ship. "Look at her. Look at her *go*."

The ladder came down. Hands hauled him up. The deck rolled under his feet, and somewhere above him a captain was shouting about tide and ice and damn the torpedoes of the shore battery both, and Tomas stood at the rail with the ledger against his chest and watched the point fall away behind him: the tower, the keeper's house with its thread of stove smoke, the small figure of Mara rowing back through the drifting floes, and above it all the great lens turning, turning, throwing its blade of light across the whole broken river like a hand raised in oath.

A third rifle shot cracked out. The ball skipped off a floe twenty yards astern, and the captain swore and rang for more steam.

But the light held the channel. Every floe between the *Maren* and the open lake stood lit and marked, every lead of black water gleamed, and the men on the shore fired twice more into glare they couldn't see past, and hit nothing but morning.

Tomas kept his hand flat against the rail and the ledger flat against his heart, and he did not look away from the light until the point itself slipped behind the headland, and even then he could see it — miles on, past the narrows, past the last black pines — a star burning in broad daylight, stubborn and unashamed, saying to the whole frozen river: *I am here. I am tended. Come and try.*

He was still watching when the first letter-shaped thought formed in him, the one he would write that very night in the ship's pitching cabin by lamplight:

*Dear Mother. Dear Keeper. The light was visible for six miles downriver. Everyone aboard asked whose it was. I told them: my mother's.*
