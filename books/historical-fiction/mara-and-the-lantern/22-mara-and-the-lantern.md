## 22. Mara and the Lantern

The first ferry of spring came on a Tuesday, riding high and empty in the brown water, its hull streaked with the winter's ice scars like a man come home from a long illness.

Mara Nystrom saw it first from the lamp room, because she saw everything from the lamp room now. She had learned to read the river the way her mother read it — not as water but as weather, as ledger, as book of accounts that never quite balanced. The ferry was late by an hour, which meant the channel had shifted again below the narrows, which meant the buoy at Miller's Reach had dragged, which meant there would be work before supper. A year ago she would not have known any of this. A year ago she had been a girl who begged to leave on the last boat before the ice and cried herself to sleep when her mother said no.

Now she was sixteen years old and an apprentice keeper of the Gullwrack Point light, and she knew where every one of the two hundred and eleven steps down to the cove creaked, and she knew the smell of the oil, and she knew — this was the strangest knowledge, the knowledge that had come to her slow as frost coming up a windowpane — that she did not want to be anywhere else.

"Mara!" Her mother's voice rose through the stairwell, worn smooth by the winter but still carrying the way it always carried, clear across water and across silence both. "Ferry's making for the dock. You'll want your coat."

Mara already had it half on. She took the stairs two at a time, past the third-floor landing where the spare wicks were kept in their tin box, past the second floor where the logbooks lived — forty years of them, her father's careful hand in the earliest volumes, then Elias's, then her mother's, and now, in the newest one, in ink that was still learning its own character, hers. *March 14th. Wind NW, freshening toward evening. Lamp lit 6:12 p.m., burned steady. Ice gone out of the cove.* She had written it last night by lamplight while her mother sat mending a net by the stove, and her mother had read it after, and said nothing, and nodded once, and the nod had been worth more than any words.

She burst out into the morning air and stopped, as she always stopped, because the point in spring was a thing you could not walk through without stopping. The willows along the cove were budding yellow-green. The air smelled of thawed earth and fish and the last ghost of woodsmoke. Down on the dock, Ruth Alden's widow no longer, Ruth Kessler of the pension and the apology and the new hat she wore to church every Sunday as if daring anyone to comment — stood waiting with her hands folded, and beside her the sexton, Mr. Ostrander, who had seen two women take a manifest from behind the church register in a blizzard and had said nothing then and had never spoken of it since except once, at midwinter, when he brought them a sack of flour and said only, "Some silences are load-bearing," and went home.

And out on the water, growing larger, the ferry.

Tomas had written all winter. That was not true — Tomas had written since November, when the first boat out had fought the breaking ice and the agents had run along the shore firing pistols that the daylight-bright lamp had blinded, and Hazel had stood in the lamp room with her bandaged hand on the valve and turned the flame up until the whole point blazed like a held breath, and the boat had slipped the channel and gone. He had written from the river towns, from the rail junction at Corvane, from a boarding house in the city whose address he copied twice in his letters because he did not trust the postal service and did not trust the company's reach and trusted, most of all, that Hazel would keep the paper. She kept them all. They lived in the drawer of the kitchen table beneath the oilcloth, tied with string, and Mara had read every one so many times she could recite the opening of the first — *Dear Mrs. Nystrom, I hardly know how to begin a letter to a house I left under gunfire* — and the opening of the last, which began simply, *I am on the train,* and ended with four words that Mara had thought about more than she would ever admit: *Tell her I'm sorry.*

Not *tell your mother*. Not *tell the keeper of the light*. *Her.*

Her mother never spoke of the letters. She read them at night, alone, and put them away, and in the morning her face was the face it always was, which Mara had learned was not a lack of feeling but a discipline of it, a keeping, the same way the lamp was kept — trimmed and housed and shielded from the wind not because the wind must not see it burn but because the burning must go on regardless of the wind.

"Steady now," her mother said, coming up beside her on the dock path, and Mara realized she had been walking too fast, nearly running, and made herself slow. Her mother's right hand was healed crooked. The burn had taken the skin from wrist to knuckles and the doctor from the spring inspection had said she was fortunate to keep the hand at all, and Hazel Nystrom had thanked him politely and asked whether he'd looked at the lamp's clockwork while he was here, because the governor spring wanted adjusting. The hand worked. It worked slowly, and it ached in damp weather, and Mara had watched her mother learn to do everything — trim, wind, write, braid her own hair — with the ache folded in, unmentioned, the way she folded everything in.

But today her mother's face was not quite the usual face. There was something underneath it, moving, like fish under ice.

* *

The ferry bumped the dock fenders and the deckhand threw the line and Ruth caught it and made fast, and then the gangway came down and the passengers came off one by one — a surveyor with his tripod, two men in city coats who looked at everything as if pricing it, an old woman with a birdcage covered in cloth. And last, carrying a canvas valise and wearing a suit that fit him better than the ones he'd left in, his jaw shadowed with a beard he hadn't managed in the winter, his eyes finding the dock and finding them — Tomas.

He stopped halfway down the gangway. Mara understood why later; she understood it in the moment, too, standing there with the wind pulling at her collar. He had left this place pursued. He had left it with agents running the shoreline and his father's widow's hand burned to the bone holding the light against them, and he had spent eight months in courtrooms giving testimony against men who owned judges, and he had come back anyway. Coming back was the whole of it. Anyone could be carried away from a place. Returning was a thing you chose.

Hazel walked to the foot of the gangway and stood, and did not speak, and Tomas came down the last steps and stood in front of her, and the two of them regarded each other with the strange family likeness that Mara had stopped being able to unsee months ago — the same set of the brow, the same way of holding the chin slightly back as if listening to something behind the world.

"You look well," Hazel said.

"I look like a clerk." His mouth twitched. "The federal attorneys liked me better when I looked drowned."

"The federal attorneys," Hazel said, "would have liked you better as a corpse. It's harder to argue with."

And then, because neither of them was made for ceremony, or perhaps because both of them had learned the same lesson about how much ceremony a body can carry before it buckles, Hazel stepped forward and put her good arm around him, briefly, hard, the way you tie off a line in weather, and let go, and that was the whole reunion, and Mara saw that it was enough. It was more than enough. It was the kind of thing that would be taken out and looked at in private for years.

Then Tomas turned to her, and Mara found she did not know what to do with her face.

"You've grown," he said, which was the stupidest possible thing, and they both heard it was the stupidest possible thing, and she laughed, and he laughed, and whatever else needed saying between them — the berth, the choice, the night she had screamed at her mother that she hated her, the fact that his existence had rearranged her entire childhood like furniture moved in the dark — all of it stepped back and let the laughter pass through first.

"They're saying at the courthouse," Tomas said, walking up the dock path between them, valise in hand, "that my testimony closed it. But it isn't true, and I told them so, and I'll tell everyone here so. The testimony rode on papers other people risked for. A widow who guessed everything. A sexton who saw and stayed silent. A town of fishermen who signed their names knowing what signing meant." He glanced at Hazel. "And a keeper who wouldn't give me the berth until I'd earned it, and gave me the truth instead, which was heavier and worth more."

"The truth," Hazel said, "wasn't mine to keep. I just didn't drop it."

"No," Tomas said quietly. "You tended it. There's a difference."

* *

They ate supper at the long table, all five of them — Ruth and the sexton and Tomas and Hazel and Mara — and the talk went around like the lamp beam goes around, touching everything once, warming what it touched. Ruth told about the pension arriving in January, the envelope with the government seal, and how she had read it standing in the churchyard and then gone inside and sat down in the pew where Alden used to sit and wept until the sexton came in to trim the lamps and pretended very thoroughly to be trimming lamps. The magistrate had read the company's apology aloud from the pulpit in April, all of it, every word, including the part where Meridian Shipping acknowledged that the barge *Corinne L.* had been sent out overloaded on the ninth of December, fifteen years ago, in weather no prudent master would have sailed, and that eleven men had died of it, and that the manifests had been falsified, and that Elias Nystrom, assistant agent, had signed the false manifest under pressure that the inquiry described, in language the whole congregation heard, as *coercion amounting to threat.*

Cowardice, Hazel had called it, that night in the lamp room when she finally told Tomas everything. Cowardice, plainly, without varnish — because he deserved the truth and not a shrine. But the inquiry had called it coercion, and the law had weighed it against the testimony he had also given, secretly, to a clerk named Whitcombe, the record that had survived in the church register's hiding place, and the law had decided that a coward who kept evidence was worth more than a brave man who kept nothing, and Mara supposed that was justice, imperfect, real, the kind you could actually live inside.

After supper the others went down to Ruth's for coffee, and the house settled into its evening sounds — the stove ticking, the river working at the shore, the small persistent conversation of the building itself, which Mara knew plank by plank.

Her mother was already climbing the tower stairs when Mara finished the dishes. She followed.

In the lamp room the light was not yet lit. The sun stood a hand's width above the western bluffs, and the room was full of gold, the brass fittings glowing, the lens throwing slow wheeling shards of it across the floorboards like something alive. Her mother had the lamp housing open and was going through the lighting ritual with the unhurried precision of long practice — checking the oil level, seating the wick, trimming the charred top of it with the little scissors that had been her husband's and before that her father-in-law's, wiping the chimney glass inside and out until it vanished.

"Watch the wick," Hazel said, which was what she always said, and Mara leaned in.

This was the inheritance. Mara understood it suddenly, wholly, the way you understand a sentence you have been reading all year — not the ledger, which lay in the federal archive now in the city, catalogued and famous, the document schoolchildren would someday study; not the name Nystrom, or the scandal, or the story the newspapers had told for a season and discarded. None of that was passed hand to hand in this room. What was passed was this: the trimming of the wick. The wiping of the glass. The winding of the clockwork that turned the lens, done at the same hour each day whether anyone watched or not, whether anyone thanked you or not, whether your hand ached or not. Fifteen years of silences had been her mother's wrong inheritance, hoarded like oil in a leaking can. This was the right one, poured out fresh every single evening: memory kept alight is not a possession. It is a chore. It is a devotion. Somebody has to stay and do it.

"Somebody has to stay and do it," her mother said, as if Mara had spoken aloud.

"I know," Mara said. "That's what I was thinking."

"I know." Hazel struck the match. The wick caught, and she turned the key and the flame rose, and outside the sun touched the bluffs and the great lens began its turning, sweeping the beam out over the brown spring water, over the narrows, over Miller's Reach where the buoy still needed setting, over the whole darkening country that the light claimed and served. "It doesn't feel like enough, some nights. Tending. You'll want to be doing something bigger. Everyone does."

"Do you still want that?"

Hazel considered it honestly, which was her way. "Less each year," she said. "Because I've seen what the big things cost the people who won't tend anything." She closed the housing and latched it. "Your brother—"

She stopped. She had never once called him that. Mara held her breath and did not move, the way you hold still when a wild thing steps into the open.

"—Tomas," Hazel corrected herself, and Mara saw that the correction cost her something and that she paid it anyway. "He asked me, on the dock, whether he could stay. Through the summer. Through longer, maybe. He says the city makes him feel like a witness and the point makes him feel like a son." Her mother's voice was level, but her crooked hand rested on the lamp housing, on the warm brass, as if steadying itself against something. "I told him the light takes apprentices. Both of you, if he wants it. There's work enough. There always has been."

"And if the company—"

"The company is a corpse that hasn't heard the news." For the first time all day, Hazel smiled fully, and it transformed her face into someone younger, someone Mara had only ever glimpsed in the oldest photographs. "Let it rest."

* *

Later, when the sky had gone fully dark and the stars were out in their cold multitudes and the lamp turned and turned below them, Mara stood at the parapet with her elbows on the stone and looked down at the cove, where the ferry lay moored overnight before its return run at dawn.

A light showed at the boathouse window. Tomas was down there — she could see his silhouette moving against the curtain, unpacking, hanging things, making a home in the place he'd almost died and from which he'd been sent away and to which he had chosen to come back. Tomorrow he would climb the two hundred and eleven steps and learn the clockwork and burn his fingers on a hot chimney glass and complain about it, and her mother would tell him that complaining was not in the job description, and he would laugh, and it would be ordinary, and ordinary was the thing none of them had dared hope for through that whole long winter of ice and warrants and fire.

There would be letters still, of course. Letters from the attorneys, from the families of the eleven, from strangers who had read the newspapers and needed to tell someone their own version of a drowned father or a falsified manifest. The river gives things back; it had taught her that. Some of what it gave back would be heavy. Some of it would arrive, as the worst of it always seemed to, by the first boat of a season, when the ice had barely gone and the channel was uncertain and nobody was ready.

But the channel would be marked. The buoy at Miller's Reach would be set tomorrow, she and her mother together, rowing out in the cold bright morning with hammer and chain. The wick would be trimmed. The glass wiped. The clockwork wound. The logbook written, entry by entry, year by year, in a hand that was becoming its own.

Down in the cove, Tomas's lamp went out. Above her head, their lamp turned on, patient as forgiveness, sweeping the water.

Mara stayed at the parapet a while longer, watching the beam go around, learning the rhythm of it the way she had learned everything that mattered — by staying, and watching, and doing it again.

Then she went down the stairs to write the log.
