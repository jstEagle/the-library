## 2. What the Seal Conceals

The wax arrived before the sun did.

That was the order of things in the Verrone ledger house: the wax first, then the light, then the clerks, in descending ranks of importance, until at the very bottom of the morning came Dominic Quintana with her ink-stained cuffs and her face like a shut door. She had learned to arrive second-to-last of all—the last being old Ferro, whose hands shook too badly now to be trusted with anything but sweeping—and she had learned it the way she had learned everything worth knowing in this building: by watching what happened to those who arrived otherwise.

Arrive too early and you were eager, and eagerness invited errands. Arrive too late and you were lazy, and laziness was written down somewhere, in some margin, in some hand that would one day consult its own notes against you. But arrive precisely when expected—no sooner, no later—and you became part of the furniture of the place, and furniture was never blamed, never promoted, never noticed.

Twelve years she had practiced being furniture.

The sealing room sat on the second floor, a narrow chamber that smelled of hot resin and cold stone, where the light came through one tall window glazed with glass so old and warped that everything seen through it seemed underwater. Fitting, Dominic always thought, for a company whose fortune had been pulled from beneath the waves. The window looked out over the harbor, where the Verrone flagship—*La Perla Blanca*, all white canvas and black trim, the proudest thing ever launched from these docks—lay at her moorings like a sleeping animal. In three weeks she would sail for the capital with the season's revenues, and until then the whole ledger house moved around her departure the way a household moves around a wedding.

Dominic set the wax bricks to warming over the spirit lamp and laid out the day's correspondence in a row: fourteen letters, three manifests, two internal requisitions, and one item apart from the rest, wrapped in oiled paper and carried up from the director's office by a boy who had not met her eyes. The boy never met anyone's eyes. That was how you knew a thing mattered—that it traveled blind.

She unwrapped the oilcloth with dry fingers.

Inside lay a letter already composed—not in the director's hand but in that of his private secretary, Señor Aldaya, whose script Dominic knew better than her own mother's face, having copied it faithfully for six years. It was folded once, sealed at the fold with plain wax, unmarked. Around it, a covering instruction in Aldaya's precise capitals:

*Prepare for the Director's signature. Seal under the great arms. Nothing leaves this room unsigned and unsealed.*

Nothing unusual in that. Letters went out under the great arms every week—the Verrone crest, the pearl in the shell, the motto *Ex Profundis*, from the depths, which Dominic had privately always found less inspiring than ominous. Her task was mechanical: warm the letter just enough to soften the temporary seal, extract it, present it flat for signature, return it to its fold, seal it fresh, press the great arms into the cooling wax, log it in the dispatch book. She had done this perhaps four hundred times. She could do it in her sleep, and sometimes suspected she did.

She warmed the letter's edge above the lamp.

The trick was heat without flame, patience without greed. Too much heat and the paper itself betrayed you, browning at the edges, releasing the smell of scorch that could not be disguised. Too little and the wax held, and pulling meant tearing, and tearing meant questions. Questions were the one currency the ledger house never devalued. Questions spent lives here.

Dominic counted her breaths—one, two, three, four—and lifted the softened seal away in a single clean motion, the way her father had once lifted pearls from their shells, whole and unblemished. The memory surfaced and sank again, as it always did, quick as a fish.

She opened the letter flat.

It was addressed to no one. That alone should have told her something; letters that go nowhere are the most dangerous kind, because they are not meant to be read but filed, and files are where companies keep their sins the way other families keep portraits of the dead. The body of the letter began in Aldaya's careful voice, all preambles and formalities, references to the season's accounts, to the audit due before sailing, to certain *irregularities*—

Dominic's eye, trained by twelve years of copying other men's sentences, had learned to move ahead of her attention. It was reading faster than she was thinking. And so she saw the words before she understood them, saw them the way you see a shape in deep water before your mind consents to name it a shark:

*...the settlement of Puerto Ceniza, whose divers have long defrauded the company through false declaration of catch weights...*

The room went very quiet.

Not silent—it was never silent; there was always the tick of the lamp, the creak of the building settling on its pilings, the far-off gull-cries off the harbor—but quiet in the way a room goes quiet inside the skull, all outer sound suddenly muffled, as though she had gone underwater herself.

*Puerto Ceniza.*

Her village. Her mother's village, her father's grave-ground, the place of salt and smoke and the low white church where they kept the boats' names carved into the doorframe going back four generations. The place she had left at nine years old with her mother's bargain still wet on both their cheeks, and had not returned to in twelve years, and thought of, if she was honest, mostly at night, mostly in the shape of a kitchen, mostly in the smell of a particular fish stew she could no longer quite reconstruct.

She read on.

The letter laid it out with the terrible tidiness that Aldaya brought to everything—a mind like a well-made drawer, everything squared, everything hidden. The divers of Puerto Ceniza had falsified their declarations. The shortfall across twelve seasons was considerable. The company, regrettably, would be forced to recover the debt against the village's future earnings; indeed, arrangements had been concluded. The matter would be documented fully before the *Perla Blanca*'s departure, such that the record would be *complete and beyond dispute* when the ship reached the capital.

Beyond dispute. Dominic turned the phrase over. Documents that are complete and beyond dispute do not need to be timed to a tide. Unless the timing is the point. Unless whoever is accused cannot answer until the accuser is safely at sea.

Her hands had stopped moving.

That was the danger. She knew it even as it happened, knew it with the detached clarity of a person watching someone else make an error. A clerk's hands must never stop. Hands that stop are hands that think, and thinking hands draw eyes, and eyes were the one thing Dominic Quintana had spent twelve years learning to avoid. Somewhere below her, in the counting room, pens scratched. Someone laughed briefly at some clerkish joke. The harbor glittered through the warped glass, indifferent, beautiful, full of drowned things.

*Whose divers have long defrauded the company.*

But that was backwards. She felt the wrongness of it in her body before her mind assembled the argument, the way a diver feels a current change before he can say why. Because she had copied the catch ledgers. She had copied them for years—the weekly returns, the weight certificates, the tally reconciliations. And she remembered—her memory was the one extravagance she permitted herself, the hoard she built in secret the way a poor woman builds a secret purse of coins—she remembered that Ceniza's declared catches ran *low*. Consistently low. Season after season, year after year, the numbers arriving at the ledger house already smaller than any other station's, as though the sea around Puerto Ceniza gave less generously than the sea everywhere else.

She had assumed poverty. She had assumed depletion. She had assumed what she had been taught to assume: that her people's waters were tired, their luck thin, their skills fading with the old divers.

Or she had assumed nothing at all, which was safer, and called it assumption afterward.

Now a letter in Señor Aldaya's immaculate hand was telling her that those low numbers were fraud—fraud by her family, her neighbors, her dead father's fellow divers—and that the debt thus conjured would be settled against them before the flagship sailed, documented, sealed, made permanent. Made true by ink. Made true by her own hands, if she did what she had done four hundred times before: carried the letter to the director, took his signature, brought it back, and pressed the great arms into the wax.

Her hands were shaking.

She watched them shake as though they belonged to another clerk entirely—a careless girl, a girl who had not yet learned the first lesson of the ledger house, which is that paper does not care what is written on it. Paper takes confession and slander with the same patience. Paper will hold a lie for fifty years and present it to a magistrate as fact, yellowed and authoritative, while the truth goes unrecorded and therefore unborn.

*Ledgers remember what people are forbidden to say.* Old clerk Mena had told her that when she was nine and new, teaching her the trade the way you teach a child to swim: by pushing her in. He had meant it as comfort, or as warning, or as both—he had been a man who dealt in both. She had repeated it to herself for twelve years like a prayer. Ledgers remember. Be the hand that writes, not the name that's written.

And now she stood in the sealing room holding a letter that would write her mother's village into the record as thieves.

The lamp ticked. The wax cooled on her palette, going dull.

She thought of Beatriz downstairs in the kitchen, beating eggs for the directors' breakfast, humming the song about the sailor who comes home to find his sweetheart married to the tax collector. Beatriz, who slipped her the heels of loaves and the ends of cheese and pretended not to notice when Dominic ate standing up like a starving gull. Beatriz, who called her *niña* though Dominic was twenty-one, because kindness keeps its own calendar.

She thought of her mother's hands. Serafina Quintana had hands like rope, knotted from thirty years of nets, and the last time Dominic had seen them they had been gripping the gate of the company compound, not waving, because waving would have meant admitting what was happening, and her mother had never in her life admitted to anything that hurt. *Be useful,* her mother had said instead, which in Serafina's language was *I love you, survive.*

Useful. Yes. She had been useful. She had copied ten thousand pages of other people's business without leaving so much as a fingerprint of opinion among them. She had been the safest clerk in the building, the invisible one, the furniture. And all that safety, all those twelve years of silence, had purchased exactly nothing for the people whose blood she carried—had perhaps been spent, without her consent, as the raw material of a lie like this one. Because a scheme needs clerks. A lie of this size needs faithful hands. Perhaps hers had been chosen long ago, quietly, the way you choose a tool: the reliable one, the unnoticed one, the one who asks nothing.

The thought arrived with a physical sensation, a cracking somewhere behind the sternum, small and precise, like river ice giving way at the start of thaw. Not a breaking. A beginning of breaking. Twelve years of safe silence, and this was the sound it made when it first failed.

She looked down at the letter.

She had read it twice now—once fast, once slow—and she made herself read the closing lines a third time, because a clerk's discipline dies hard even mid-drowning. There was a schedule appended. The documentation of the Ceniza debt was to be finalized within the fortnight. The relevant ledgers—the original catch records, the reconciliation books—were to be collected and reviewed *in preparation*, which meant, translated from the language of offices, *removed, adjusted, or destroyed*. And at the bottom, where instructions to subordinate stations usually sat, a line that raised the hair on her arms despite the warmth of the lamp:

*Ensure the cooperation of local personnel. The matter must appear settled by consent.*

By consent. As though her mother had signed anything. As though a village that could barely afford lamp oil had somehow swindled the richest trading house on the coast out of a king's ransom in pearls, season after season, undetected—until, conveniently, three weeks before the flagship sailed with everything aboard that anyone might wish to inspect.

Arithmetic, said a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like Tomás Iriarte, the archivist who had fallen, whose disgrace was still whispered about in the corridors five years after his dismissal. *They'll tell you it's complicated. It's never complicated. Follow the numbers and the numbers will tell you who ate.*

She had never spoken to Iriarte. She had only heard him speak, once, at a hearing she had been sent to copy—a thin man with net-mender's hands and a voice like a file drawn slowly across iron, saying that the pearl weights were wrong, that the weights were wrong systematically, that wrongness with a pattern is not error but policy. They had laughed him out of the hall. Then out of the building. Then out of the record entirely, which was the company's preferred method of murder.

She pressed her palms flat against the desk until the shaking slowed.

Then she did the thing she had done four hundred times. She folded the letter along its existing crease, warmed a new seal of wax, set the temporary fold-seal back in place over it, smoothed it with her thumb, and stood the letter upright beside the dispatch tray, awaiting the director's signature. Everything exact. Everything ordinary. A clerk's work, flawless.

Only her hands were wrong. Only her breathing. Only the fact that she now stood in a room full of company property feeling like a stranger who had wandered in off the street—no, not a stranger. A daughter. For twelve years she had managed to be neither, to be nobody, and the strain of it had felt like rest. Now it felt like what it had been all along, which was drowning, slowly, in shallow water, where no one thinks to look for a drowning woman.

She logged the letter in the dispatch book. *One item, confidential, prepared for signature.* Her handwriting came out steady. She was almost proud of that. Almost horrified.

Downstairs, the day proceeded. Directors arrived smelling of coffee and bay rum. Pens scratched. Somewhere a boy was beaten for a misposted figure, and everyone pretended not to hear, because pretending not to hear was the rent everyone paid for lodging in this house. Dominic moved through it all in her usual way—present, punctual, blank—copying manifests, initialing folios, answering questions with the minimum number of words that courtesy allowed.

But at midday, carrying a folio down to the counting room, she passed the open door of the map room, and on the wall there hung the coastal survey, and her eyes found it without permission: Puerto Ceniza, a small curl of ink on a small curl of coast, ninety miles north, two days' travel, a lifetime away.

*Niña,* Beatriz had said that morning, pressing bread into her hand, *you've gone gray as a herring. Eat.*

She had eaten. She had said nothing. Saying nothing was her craft, her profession, her armor, and it had fit her perfectly for twelve years.

It did not fit anymore. She could feel the seams straining.

At dusk, when the clerks filed out into the blue evening and the lamps were lit one by one along the harbor road, Dominic remained at her desk a moment longer than usual, hands folded, looking at nothing. Tomorrow, the director would sign the letter. Within days, the "review" of the original ledgers would begin, and whatever those books truly said would be edited out of existence before it could ever be weighed against the accusation. After that there would be only the lie, sealed in wax, sailing east on a white-sailed ship, and a village full of rope-handed people who would learn what had been decided about them when the debt collectors came.

Unless someone read the numbers first. Unless someone followed them north.

Dominic put on her shawl, blew out her lamp, and walked home through the fish-smelling dark with the letter's words repeating themselves behind her eyes like a bell that would not finish ringing—and with a single thought forming underneath the ringing, slow and dangerous as a swell coming in from deep water:

She needed to see the catch ledgers. All twelve years of them.

And she needed to do it before anyone else touched them.
