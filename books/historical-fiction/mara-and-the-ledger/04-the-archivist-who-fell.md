## 4. The Archivist Who Fell

The nets were Tomás Iriarte's penance and his prayer.

He mended them in the blue hour before the market woke, when the harbor smelled of tar and cold salt and the last of the night's fish had gone stiff on the scales. His fingers knew the work the way they had once known the ordering of a card catalogue: shuttle through, loop, draw tight. A net was only arithmetic made visible, he thought—every mesh a small promise that something would be held, that nothing valuable would slip through into the dark. He had spent eleven years inside the Verrone archive believing the same thing about paper. He had been wrong about both, or right about both, depending on how cruel a day it was.

His hut stood at the landward end of the fish market, where the cobbles gave up any pretense of being laid straight and became mud packed with oyster shell. He kept his mending frame by the door so that anyone passing could see him at it: an old man, half-blind in one eye from a lantern accident he did not discuss, doing honest work for honest coin. Invisibility, he had learned, could be manufactured as carefully as any ledger entry. The company had taught him that much before it discarded him. They had not intended it as a gift, but he had taken it as one.

The girl came on a Thursday, in the hour when the gulls went quiet and the tide turned.

He saw her before she meant to be seen—that was the first thing he noticed, and it told him nearly everything. She walked with the particular economy of someone who had trained herself out of existence: shoulders slightly folded, gaze fixed on the middle distance rather than on faces, steps timed to fall between other people's steps. He had watched clerks move like that through the archive corridors for years. The Verrone house bred them the way a cellar breeds mushrooms—in darkness, without soil, fed on whatever dripped down from above.

But this one had come looking for him. That was not mushroom behavior. That was behavior with a spine in it.

"You're Iriarte," she said. Not a question. Her voice was low and even, the voice of someone who had practiced saying little.

"I'm whoever you need me to be," he said, "if you're selling fish. If you're anything else, I'm an old man mending nets, and you've mistaken my hut for the chandlery."

She didn't leave. She stood at the edge of his frame's shadow and looked at the nets instead of at him, which he appreciated; directness was a luxury for people whose names still opened doors. Up close he could see the ink stains on her right hand—not the casual smudges of a shop clerk but the deep, permanent staining of someone who wrote eight hours a day, year after year, until the ink got into the skin like a dye. A copyist. A good one, probably. The Verrone house employed perhaps forty such hands, and he had known none of their names, because knowing names was not part of what the house paid for.

"They said you were archivist once," she said. "Eleven years. Under Archivo Director Balmaseda."

"Balmaseda is dead," Tomás said. "I am not. These are the two facts most people require."

"And then you accused the company of falsified weights."

The shuttle stopped in his fingers. He let it stop. Around them the market was beginning its slow ignition—a cart rattling over the shells, a woman crying the price of sardines two lanes over, the first smoke of the cook-fires threading up through the gray air. Nobody looked at a net-mender's hut. Nobody ever had.

"Sit down or go away," he said. "Standing there, you make a shape against the sky, and shapes get remembered."

* * *

She sat on the upturned crate he kept for exactly this purpose, though no one had used it in three years, and she introduced herself in a way that told him she understood the rules of this conversation better than most: she gave no name at all. Just "I work in the ledger house. Correspondence room." And then, after a silence long enough to have been measured deliberately: "You filed a formal accusation in the spring of '39. Underweighting of diver catches. Systematic. You claimed the shortfall was being routed somewhere."

"Claimed," Tomás repeated. He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, mostly to give his hands something to do while the old anger found its old shape. "Yes. I claimed it. Do you know what happened to the claim?"

"It was dismissed."

"It was *received*," he corrected. "That's different, and the difference matters. A dismissal means someone read it and said no. What happened to mine was worse. It was received, stamped, docketed, and filed—and then it ceased to exist. Not rejected. Erased. I went back for my own copy six months later and the docket number belonged to a shipment of olive oil. Eleven years of my life in that archive, and the archive ate my one true sentence the way a dog eats a pill—wrapped in something bland enough to swallow whole."

The girl—woman, really, he amended; the ink stains said twenty-five at least—watched him with a stillness that unsettled him more than argument would have. Most people who came to ask about the old affair wanted a story they could repeat in a tavern. She wanted something else. He couldn't yet tell what.

"The weights," she said. "How did you find it?"

"Because I counted what nobody counts." He set the spectacles back on his nose and looked at her properly for the first time. One eye gave him her sharp and clear; the other gave her back to himself, blurred and doubled, which felt appropriate. "Everyone audits the money. Money is loud. It has guards and seals and men with ledgers of their own. But pearls arrive at the counting-house by weight, and weight is quiet. A pearl weighed at four carats and recorded at three loses nothing visible. No coin goes missing. No seal is broken. The divergence lives entirely in the space between the scale and the page, and the only person who can see it is someone who reads the same diver's name across ten years of entries and notices that his eyesight, his luck, and his honesty all declined at precisely the rate the company's margins improved."

"And you noticed."

"I noticed because I was the one who copied the entries." He laughed, and it came out with rust in it. "Do you understand the joke of it? The theft was invisible because men like me made it invisible—faithful copies, faithful copies, thousands of faithful copies, each one a small brick in a wall. And the proof was invisible for the same reason. It was all there, in the record, in my own handwriting, waiting for anyone with the patience to read it as a single document instead of ten thousand separate ones. The truth doesn't hide in archives, girl. Archives are where truth goes to be buried alive. It stays breathing. You just have to know which grave to dig."

Something moved behind her face then—the first crack in the clerk's composure, quick as a fish turning under water. He had seen that expression before, on his own face, in a tarnished mirror, the morning he'd understood what the numbers were telling him. Recognition. The terrible relief of recognition.

"How much?" she asked quietly. "Over the years. Ballpark, if the exact figures are gone."

"Gone." He said the word and tasted its ash again. "The originals were consolidated in '41. New bindings, new folios. The old loose sheets were burned as superseded—I supervised the burning myself, which I consider the company's finest joke at my expense. But before they died, I memorized the pattern. Not every figure—I wasn't a fool, only a slow one—but the shape of it. Across twelve seasons, the recorded take ran short of the divers' declared catch by a fraction that varied between nine and fourteen percent, always rising before a major shipment, always falling after an audit was announced. Random loss doesn't keep a calendar. Theft does."

"Nine to fourteen percent," she repeated, and her voice had changed—flatter now, careful, the voice of someone holding something very heavy very steadily. "Routed where?"

"That I never proved. The shortfall didn't sit anywhere I could follow. It entered the general accounts and dissolved, the way blood dissolves in seawater. My suspicion—" and here he lowered his voice, though the market noise had risen around them like a wall, "—my suspicion was always that it became debt. Someone else's debt. A village, a partner, a dead man's estate. You can't steal twelve percent of a pearl fleet year after year without giving the missing mass a name to live in. Numbers abhor a vacuum. So does bookkeeping."

The woman closed her eyes for the length of one breath. When she opened them, she asked the question he had known was coming since she sat down, maybe since she had started walking toward his hut across the shell-strewn mud.

"If the figures existed—if someone brought you the pattern again, fresh, current, provable—would you swear to what you remember? Before a magistrate. On the record."

Tomás put down the shuttle.

He looked at his hands, which had done this mending for three years and had once, briefly, held the most dangerous knowledge on the coast. He thought about the night in '39 when he had walked home from the archive with his accusation drafted fair in his breast pocket, feeling like a man carrying a lit lamp through a powder store, certain of his own courage, certain of the arithmetic, certain that the institution he had served for eleven years would prefer truth to rot. He thought about the letter of dismissal that followed, worded with such exquisite courtesy—*restructuring of archival functions*—and the pension that was not quite a pension, and the way former colleagues crossed the street when they saw him coming, and the way the silence around his name grew so complete that within a year he genuinely could not prove he had ever worked there at all. The archive had erased him as thoroughly as it had erased his docket. He was, officially, a man who had never been.

"Documents without protectors are just paper," he said at last. "I learned that. Paper burns. Paper gets re-bound, mis-docketed, superseded. Paper has no arms, no voice, no friends in high places. I had the best evidence on this coast and it protected me exactly as much as a sheet of paper protects a man in a storm—which is to say, it kept the rain off one square foot of him while the rest drowned."

"So that's a no."

"That's a *lesson*," he said sharply. "Listen to it, because you didn't walk all this way to hear an old man say yes. Evidence needs a protector. Better, it needs protectors—plural, scattered, unconnected, so that killing one copy or one witness leaves the others standing. And it needs to be *sworn*. An accusation is an opinion. A deposition before a notary, with names attached and seals affixed, is a weapon. The difference between the two is the difference between shouting in the market and firing a shot. I shouted. Nobody is obliged to hear shouting."

The market had come fully awake around them now. Fishwives argued over barrels; a boy drove a handcart of ice past close enough to breathe its cold across the crate where they sat; somewhere a bell marked the hour, and the sound went out over the roofs toward the harbor, where the great ships lay at their moorings like sleeping animals, including—he did not look toward it, he never looked toward it—the Verrone flagship, white and vast and going nowhere until it went somewhere forever.

"Why now?" he asked her. "Why does a correspondence clerk come to a fallen archivist on a Thursday morning? Something has a name in it that shouldn't. Something with a place-name, I'd guess, or a person's. Am I close?"

She didn't answer. But her hands, folded in her lap, pressed together hard enough that the knuckles whitened, and Tomás Iriarte, who had spent a career reading what records said by studying what they refused to say, read her hands like a folio.

"Yes," he said softly. "Well."

* * *

She stayed another hour. He did not mend much net during it.

She told him almost nothing, and told him a great deal. She asked questions with the precision of someone who already knew half the answers and needed only confirmation: whether the underweighting had touched specific divers' families, whether the tally customs of the fishing villages were reliable, whether a sworn statement built on memory alone had ever survived a magistrate's scrutiny. To that last one he answered honestly: rarely, but not never, and never alone—memory needed corroboration, corroboration needed documents, documents needed witnesses, and the whole structure needed a notary brave enough or angry enough to attach a seal that the company couldn't simply buy or burn.

"Notaries are cautious creatures," he said. "Their seal is their livelihood. You'll need one with a grudge."

"Then I'll find one with a grudge."

He believed her. That was the strange thing, sitting there in the fish-smell and the morning noise: he believed her, this unnamed copyist with ink in her skin and grief banked behind her eyes like a coal seam. He had spent three years teaching himself not to believe in anything, the way a man teaches a limb not to feel after the wound. And here was this quiet, careful, erased person asking him—with no promises, no payment, no guarantee except the quality of her attention—to believe again.

When she rose to go, he spoke before he had decided to.

"The tally-sticks," he said. "If your place-name is a diving village—and it is, don't trouble yourself denying it—the divers keep count. They've always kept count. Notches on wood, knots in cord, marks chalked inside church walls where the company's clerks never think to look. The company weighs their catch short and tells them their memories are wrong, and so they write their memories down where memory can't be argued with. Find those sticks. Memory plus sticks plus a notary's seal—" he shrugged, and the shrug cost him something, some last plank of the careful numbness he had built. "That's not paper without a protector. That's a fortress with three walls."

The woman looked at him for a long moment. Then she inclined her head—not a bow, nothing so submissive, but the acknowledgment one professional gives another across a gap of years and ruin.

"Thank you," she said. And then, at the edge of the shadow, pausing: "They erased you. I understand that now. But you kept the pattern in your head for six years anyway. Why?"

Tomás picked up the shuttle. Ran the twine through his fingers. Thought about lying, found he had lost the knack for it somewhere along with everything else.

"Because forgetting is also a record," he said. "It's a record with their names written all over it. And I refuse to be their last copyist."

She left. He watched her go the way he had once watched important documents leave his custody—tracking her path through the market crowd, noting the direction, the pace, the places she might be intercepted. Toward the harbor quarter. Toward the notaries' lane, unless he missed his guess, or toward the church registers, or toward whatever grave she had decided to dig.

He sat back down at the frame. The net waited, torn and patient, needing every broken mesh drawn tight again before it could hold anything.

He worked until his fingers ached, and all the while the old arithmetic turned over in his head like a body turning in sleep: memory, sticks, seal. Three walls. And somewhere in the ledger house above the harbor, a fourth wall was walking around in a copyist's body, carrying a name in her chest that the company had already chosen to bury.

Tomás Iriarte mended his nets, and waited to learn whether he had just helped build something—or merely volunteered for a second funeral.
