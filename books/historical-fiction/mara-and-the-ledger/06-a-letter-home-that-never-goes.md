## 6. A Letter Home That Never Goes

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Dominic finally pulled a clean sheet of paper toward her across the scarred oak of her desk.

She had been sitting there for an hour without writing anything. The ledger house was quiet around her—the great room below locked and dark, the clerks gone home to their suppers and their wives, the building settling into itself with small wooden complaints as the night cooled. Only her lamp moved, throwing her shadow long against the wall of pigeonholes where twelve years of correspondence slept in their labeled slots. Somewhere below, in the courtyard, the watchman made his slow circuit, his boots ringing on stone at intervals so regular she could have set her heart by them.

Paper was never scarce for her. That was one of the small ironies of her life: she who owned nothing had access to more blank pages than anyone in the province. Fine rag paper for the director's letters. Coarse stock for the shipping manifests. The thin, almost translucent sheets used for carbon copies, which she pressed beneath the original with a stylus so that every word written above was duplicated below—every promise, every price, every order given in daylight preserved in ghost-script for the company's memory.

She chose a coarse sheet. It would not be missed.

*Madre*, she wrote, and then stopped, because the word looked enormous on the page, larger than any figure she had ever entered, larger than the totals that ran down the right-hand margins of a thousand ledgers.

Her pen hovered. Ink gathered at the nib like a drop about to fall from a cave roof.

Twelve years since she had written to her mother. Twelve years since she had written to anyone outside this building. Outbound post from the Verrone house went through Señor Alcázar's office, through his hands, through his reading glasses, and if a letter contained anything he judged irregular—a complaint, a plea, a name spoken too warmly—it did not go out at all. It went into a drawer. Dominic had seen the drawer. She had even, in her early years, been ordered to file some of its contents, letters from other indentured families that had died somewhere between a child's hope and the harbor post. She had filed them alphabetically by sender, because that was the system, and she had not let herself think about what the system meant.

She knew better now. Knowing better changed nothing about the drawer.

*Mother.* She tried again, in her mind. *I have seen your name.*

But she could not write that. To write it was to sign it. Every letter left this house bearing, implicitly, the company's seal of approval, which meant that everything in it belonged to the company. If she wrote *Puerto Ceniza* on this page, then Puerto Ceniza passed into Verrone custody, entered the record, became evidence—and not evidence for her family, but evidence *about* them, evidence the company might one day need, might one day use. She had watched names become entries for twelve years. She knew how easily an entry became a verdict.

And yet her hand ached to move. The letter wanted out of her the way a tide wants out of a bay.

*I am well,* she wrote at last, the oldest lie in the correspondence of the unfree. *The work is steady. I eat enough. Do not worry for me.*

She stared at those sentences. They were true, in the way that arithmetic is true—accurate, verifiable, empty. Steady work. Enough food. And underneath them, the truth that could not be said: *I have found your village in a sealed letter, Mother. I have found your village named as a thief, marked for ruin, timed to drown in blame just before the flagship sails, and I sat at my desk and copied it with my beautiful invisible hand because for eleven hours I did not know what else to do.*

Her pen scratched on, small and determined, filling the middle of the page with weather and health and the price of salt cod, all the neutral cargo of a letter designed to pass inspection precisely because it carried nothing. It was, she realized with a strange cold clarity, the same skill as copying the sealed letter: the ability to make words mean nothing. She had spent twelve years perfecting the craft of meaning nothing. Tonight it felt less like survival and more like a kind of dying done slowly, in installments.

Then, near the bottom, her hand betrayed her. Without deciding to, she wrote:

*I remember the sound of the water at Ceniza. I remember you singing the mending song when the boats came in. I remember your hands.*

She stopped breathing.

Her mother's hands. That was the thing memory kept returning to—not her mother's face, which had softened and blurred these twelve years into something half-invented, but the hands. Serafina Quintana's hands, cracked at the knuckles from salt, quick with the mending needle, strong enough to pull a net heavy with catch over a gunwale while the swell tried to take it back. Hands that had once braided Dominic's hair with oil to keep the sea-lice away. Hands that had held hers for one last moment on the dock while a company clerk counted coins into her father's widow's palm and said the word *indenture* as though it were a kindness, as though it were rescue, and perhaps it had been, and perhaps that was the cruelest thing about it.

Dominic put down the pen.

She read the letter through once. It was a good letter. It said nothing dangerous. It would pass under Señor Alcázar's spectacles without raising a single hair on his gray head. Her mother would receive it—or a version of it, or perhaps none of it—and read that her daughter was well, and steady, and fed, and learn nothing else, and answer nothing, because answering was not something the company encouraged in villages it was preparing to ruin.

And that was the moment she understood what she was actually doing.

Not writing home. Writing the company's letter. Performing, one last time, the fluency of silence she had spent a lifetime mastering. This page was not a bridge between her and her mother; it was another wall, well-built, plastered smooth, painted the color of obedience. Her mother would hold it and feel exactly what Dominic had felt for twelve years—that someone was alive out there, and unreachable, and speaking a language made only of surfaces.

*No,* she thought. Not like this. Not ever again, if she could help it.

She took up the candle.

For a long moment she simply held the flame near the corner of the page, watching the light tremble against the paper, watching her own handwriting glow faintly translucent where the heat touched it—the words becoming visible in a new way just before they ceased to be words at all. There was a smell of warm sizing, of animal glue in the paper's bones, and then the corner browned, curled, caught.

Fire moves quickly through a letter. It has so little to burn that isn't already air. Dominic held the page by its unlit edge over the clay dish she used for spent matches and watched her mother's hands disappear first—of course they did, they were written nearest the flame—and then the mending song, and then the water at Ceniza, and then *I am well*, curling into black lace, collapsing into ash that settled in the dish like gray snow, soft, weightless, deniable.

She burned it thoroughly. She had learned thoroughness from ledgers: a record half-destroyed is worse than one destroyed completely, because fragments invite reconstruction. When the last fleck had gone dark she crushed the ash with the pestle from her ink set until it was powder, and rinsed the dish, and stood a moment watching the gray swirl vanish down the drain into the pipes that ran beneath the city, carrying everything away to the harbor, to the sea her father had drowned in, to the sea that kept every secret ever committed to it.

Her hands were shaking. Not from fear, exactly. From the effort of having decided something and knowing there was no longer any path back to the person who hadn't decided.

Because burning the letter was not the decision. Burning the letter was only the clearing of the desk. The decision was what came next, and it had been forming in her all evening, all day, all the slow terrible week since the sealed letter, gathering like pressure behind a seam of rock.

If she could not speak to her mother through paper, she would have to go to Ceniza herself.

* * *

She worked out the pretext the way she worked out everything: in figures.

The Ceniza accounts were due for reconciliation—this was true, or true enough. Coastal collection stations were reconciled on a rotating schedule, and Puerto Ceniza had not been audited in four years, which was itself unusual, though not so unusual that anyone would question it now. Stations went unaudited for years when the regional factor was lazy, or when the station's books were known to be tidy, or—as she suspected in this case—when no one in the capital particularly wanted a clerk's eyes on those particular numbers before a certain flagship sailed.

That was the beauty and the horror of it: the company's own neglect was her door. Aurelio Verrone had left Puerto Ceniza unwatched because he believed it already handled, already doomed, a village that existed now mostly as a line item awaiting its transformation into a scapegoat. He would not suspect an audit. Audits were dust, were tedium, were the business of clerks like Dominic, who were furniture with hands.

She drafted the requisition in her head while she lay awake that night, and committed it to paper the next morning between the routine correspondence, buried in the flow of ordinary documents so that it seemed to arise naturally, one more sheet in the daily tide. *Requisition: field audit, coastal collection station PC-4 (Puerto Ceniza), reconciliation of catch weights against remitted revenues, period covering current fiscal year plus three prior. Clerk assigned: D. Quintana.* She signed it with the small precise hand that no one ever questioned, because questioning it had never occurred to anyone. She routed it through the channels that required no superior's countersignature for audits under thirty days' duration. Thirty days. She had built the rule herself, in effect, years ago, updating the operations manual after the Salgado audit, because someone had to keep the systems current, and the invisible are always given the housekeeping.

There was a bitter elegance in it. The machinery that had erased her would now carry her home.

She told herself it would be simple. Travel up the coast, present the audit writ, examine the station books—which she already knew were false, knew the shape of the falseness intimately, had traced its arithmetic in the capital ledgers until she could draw it blindfolded—and return. Ten days, perhaps twelve. In and out like a tide. She would say nothing to her mother beyond what an auditor says to a station keeper. She would look at her mother's face from behind the mask of her function, gather what she needed, and leave.

She wrote this plan out and examined it and knew it for what it was: the letter again. A document saying *I am well, the work is steady, do not worry for me*, addressed to herself.

Because she knew what would happen. She knew that she would stand in front of Serafina Quintana, and that her mother would look at her—twelve years grown, in company clothes, carrying company papers, escorted by company men—and that something in that look would demand more than an audit. And she knew that she would either give it or refuse it, and both choices would cost more than she had. That is what choosing a side meant, she was beginning to understand. It was not a door you walked through once. It was a debt you took on, compounding daily, payable in coin you did not yet own.

There would be no more invisibility after this. An audit drew attention—a little, a tolerable amount—but an audit conducted by the daughter of a Ceniza diver, discovered, whispered, noticed? The company read outbound letters. What would they make of a clerk whose family appeared suddenly, inconveniently, inside the very accounts she had chosen to examine?

She pushed the thought down. There would be time to manage it. There was always time to manage things; managing was her whole life.

* * *

Beatriz found her at midday in the kitchen corridor, where Dominic had gone ostensibly to collect the tray for the director's office and actually because Beatriz's domain was the one place in the building where the air smelled of something other than ink and dust.

"You've the look," Beatriz said, not turning from the pot she was stirring. She was a broad woman with forearms like mooring rope and a face that had decided decades ago not to waste expression on trifles. "The look of a girl rearranging furniture inside her own skull."

"Do I get bread with my errand, or only diagnosis?"

"Both." Beatriz cut a heel off yesterday's loaf and handed it over, then lowered her voice to the register she used when the corridor might have ears. "You didn't eat breakfast. I notice things."

"You notice everything. It's why I trust you and why you frighten me."

Something in Dominic's tone must have carried farther than she intended, because Beatriz turned then, wiping her hands on her apron, and studied her with those flat brown eyes that missed nothing in any kitchen in the world.

"It's the Ceniza thing," Beatriz said. Not a question.

Dominic's mouth went dry. "What do you know about—"

"I know nothing. I know kitchens." Beatriz glanced down the corridor, both directions, an old habit of servants everywhere. "I know that the director's table has been quiet as a funeral for two weeks, and quiet directors cook up louder storms than loud ones. I know you've been walking around all week like a woman counting stairs in the dark." She fixed Dominic with her gaze. "Whatever stair you're standing on, girl—don't skip steps, and don't fall alone."

It was the closest thing to an offer of alliance that either of them could safely make in that corridor, and Dominic received it the way she received all gifts: carefully, with both hands, hiding how much it weighed.

"I'm being sent up the coast," she said. "Reconciliation work. Routine."

"Routine." Beatriz snorted softly and returned to her pot. "In this house, nothing's routine. Some things are just slower than others." Then, stirring, without looking up: "Take the blue shawl from the linen press. Coastal nights are cold, and the company won't issue you warmth."

It was such a small sentence. Dominic carried it back upstairs like a coal cupped in her palms.

* * *

The requisition cleared on Thursday. On Friday morning she stood in the great room while Señor Alcázar stamped the travel writ and remarked, without interest, that the coastal stations bred mosquitoes and bad bookkeeping, and that she should be thorough and quick. She bowed. She thanked him. Inside, some part of her marveled at how easy it was—how twelve years of practiced nothing had polished her into an instrument smooth enough to slide past any hand.

On Friday afternoon she went to the linen press and took the blue shawl, and folded it into the bottom of her traveling satchel beneath the audit forms, and understood, pressing her hand flat against the folded wool, that she was packing not for an audit but for a reckoning, and that the woman who boarded the coast packet on Saturday morning would not be entirely the woman who returned.

That night, before sleeping, she allowed herself one indulgence. She sat at her desk in the dark, no lamp lit, and composed in her head the letter she would have written if letters were free—if outbound post traveled unopened and mothers could hear their daughters across distance without the company's spectacles in between.

*Madre. I have found your name in a place it should never have been. I am coming home—not as your daughter, because I don't yet know how to be that, but as close to her as I can manage. Keep the tally-sticks safe. Keep everyone quiet. I am bringing proof, and I am bringing myself, and I no longer intend to be unheard.*

She held each sentence in the dark like a stone warmed by a fire, and then, as with the paper letter, she let it go—released it downward, inward, into that sea inside her where she kept everything the company must never find, alongside her father's face and her mother's hands and the sound of the mending song.

Outside her narrow window, the harbor lamps burned in their rows, and among the black hulls at anchor rode the flagship, *La Perla Blanca*, sails furled, waiting on the tide and on a departure date that powerful men were even now shortening in their tidy minds. Dominic looked at it a long moment.

Three days, she told herself. In three days she would be on the road north, and whatever waited in Puerto Ceniza—her mother's face, the divers' silences, the truth wearing its twelve years of salt—would begin arriving whether she was ready or not.

She blew out the candle stub, and in the darkness her heart kept time with the watchman's boots, steady, steady, like a tide going out toward home.
