## 7. The Road to Ceniza

Rivas counted the mules before dawn, because counting was the only prayer he trusted. Four animals, two saddlebags of company paper, one clerk, one escort. The clerk was a woman named Quintana, small and gray as a moth, with hands that had spent too many years around pens and not enough around rope. He had been given her name on a slip at the harbor office, along with his orders, which were written in the director's own cramped hand: *See her to Puerto Ceniza. See that she sees what she is meant to see. See nothing else.*

He had read that last line four times by lamplight before he understood it, and then he wished he hadn't.

The road out of the capital went north through cane country, and the cane was already tall enough to hide things in. Rivas rode point with his carbine slung loose across his back—not for bandits, though there were bandits, but because an escort who rode unarmed looked like a companion, and companions were harder to lie to. Behind him came the clerk on the second mule, upright as a fence post, her ledger case strapped to her hip like a sidearm. She had not spoken since they passed the last gate of the city except to say good morning, and she said it in the flat voice of someone reading aloud from a document.

By midmorning the cane gave way to scrub, and the scrub to the long gray coast road where the wind came off the water smelling of salt and something older, something like iodine and rot and fish scales dried in the sun. Rivas had grown up on this road. That was the thing nobody at the company remembered about him: he knew every league of it.

They stopped at noon at a roadside venta whose owner owed Verrone money, which meant the food was free and the owner's smile cost him more than the meal. Rivas watched the man's hands shake as he set down the plates—fried plantain, salt cod gone soft, coffee stretched with toasted corn until it tasted of apology—and he saw the clerk watching too. She ate little. She thanked the owner twice, in a voice so quiet it barely reached the table, and Rivas noticed that the second thank-you made the man's eyes go wet, as if kindness were a currency so rare in these parts that even small change of it could make a man weep.

*She sees,* Rivas thought, and then corrected himself: *She notices.* There was a difference. He would learn later how narrow the difference was, and what it would cost him.

* * *

Past the venta the country changed its mind about being beautiful. The hills folded in close to the sea, and the villages that clung to their skirts were poor in the way that poverty is practiced rather than suffered—deliberately, generationally, like a trade handed father to son. Thatch roofs patched with tin scavenged from somewhere. Goats grazing on the leeward slope of a rubbish heap. Children running alongside the mules for half a mile, barefoot, laughing, holding up limes for sale, and when Rivas shook his head the laughter didn't stop but it changed key, became something performed for themselves instead of for the riders, which was worse somehow.

At the third village a woman stood in the doorway of a house no bigger than a woodshed, nursing a baby, watching them pass with an expression Rivas recognized because he had worn it himself once, years ago, standing in this same doorway of a different house. It was the look of someone measuring a uniformed rider not for danger but for paperwork. *What have you come to take?* the look said. Not *will you*, but *what*, and *how much*.

"Quintana," he said, without turning in the saddle. "How much do you know about the villages we're riding through?"

The silence behind him lasted long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Their names. Their populations as of the last census. Their assessed pearl revenues." A pause. "Which are considerable."

"And what do you see?"

Another silence. This one had weight to it, the way a held breath has weight.

"Houses," she said finally. "Roofs that leak. No church bell—the bell at the last village was sold, or never existed. No school. Children working the shallows." Her voice stayed level, but something underneath it had begun, very quietly, to move, like a current under ice. "The census says these districts produce a third of the company's pearl revenue."

"They do," Rivas said.

"Then where is it?"

He didn't answer. He let the mule carry the question forward down the road, and behind him the clerk rode with it, and the question grew heavier between them with every league, the way weather grows heavy before rain.

* * *

They slept the first night in the open, because the only posada within reach had burned the previous winter and been rebuilt as three walls and an argument. Rivas made the fire while the clerk sat apart with her ledger case open on her knees, writing by firelight, her pen scratching like a small animal in the brush. He pretended not to watch her. He was good at pretending; it was most of his job.

But he found himself cataloguing her anyway, the way he'd catalogue any traveler under his protection: how she sat with her back straight even exhausted, how she checked the strap on the ledger case before sleeping, how she laid the case inside her bedroll so that her body curled around it like a dog around a bone. Whatever was in that case mattered more to her than sleep. More than safety, maybe. People carried documents like that only when the documents were dangerous—to someone.

"You've ridden this road before," she said suddenly. It wasn't a question.

"When I was young. Before the company."

"What did your family do?"

"Fished. Farmed a little. My father dove for pearls two seasons, when the fishing failed." He fed a stick to the fire and watched the sparks climb. "Then he stopped diving."

"Why?"

"Because the men who dove came back with less than they'd earned and more than they could carry, if you understand me. The weights at the company scale were always lighter than the weights in a man's hand. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it, because saying it got you blacklisted, and blacklisted divers don't eat." He glanced at her. In the firelight her face was all planes and shadow, unreadable, but her pen had stopped moving. "You're a ledger clerk, señora. You'll know better than I whether the weights were honest."

"I copy correspondence," she said. "I don't audit."

"No," Rivas said. "Of course not."

The fire cracked and settled. Somewhere down the beach a night bird called, three notes, like a question asked twice and then abandoned. The clerk wrote one more line and closed her book, and Rivas lay back against his saddle and stared up at the stars through the smoke and understood, with a slow cold clarity, why he had really been sent on this journey. Not to protect her. To witness her. To be the pair of company eyes that would report, upon return, exactly how far the little clerk had looked into things she should not have looked into—and whether she had flinched.

He turned over and slept badly, and dreamed of scales.

* * *

The second day they came down into the coastal flats where the pearl camps were. Rivas had requested—quietly, through channels—that their route avoid the active camps, and the request had been granted, which told him everything about what the director wanted seen and unseen. But roads bend toward water the way rivers do, and you cannot ride the Ceniza road without passing the edges of the industry that feeds it.

They passed a sorting shed first, a long low building open on one side, where women sat at tables picking through baskets of shell. The smell hit them a quarter mile out—rotten flesh and lime and the sweet mineral stink of opened oysters—and Rivas heard the clerk breathe in sharply through her nose and hold it, not against the stench, he realized, but against something else.

Inside the shed the women worked without looking up. Their fingers moved with terrible speed, sorting pearls by grade into cups, and beside each cup stood a slate with numbers chalked on it, and Rivas saw the clerk's head turn toward the slates the way a compass needle turns toward north. One of the women, older, her hands scarred white from years of brine, caught the clerk looking and did something strange: she tilted her slate, briefly, deliberately, so that its numbers faced the riders. Then she looked down and went on sorting.

"What did she show you?" Rivas asked, when they were past.

"Nothing," said Dominic Quintana. "A number."

"A number meaning what?"

"Meaning," she said slowly, "that forty arrobas of pearl went into that shed this morning, according to the tally chalked on the wall outside. And that the slate inside says twenty-two." She was quiet for a moment. "That's arithmetic, señor Rivas. Arithmetic doesn't need my opinion."

He said nothing. His hands had gone cold on the reins. Because he knew—Irivas knew, from his father, from his whole drowned childhood on this coast—that the number eighteen missing from that shed would be divided among men in the capital whose shoes never touched shell mud, and that none of it would appear in any book the divers were permitted to see. And he knew something else too, something that arrived in him now not as knowledge but as shame, arriving late like a letter from a dead friend: that the ledgers this quiet woman had copied for twelve years were not neutral paper. They were the machine itself. Every fair-handed figure she had transcribed had helped the unfair ones stay hidden, the way a crowd hides a thief.

He looked back at her riding behind him, gray and small and silent, and thought: *she doesn't know yet that she was part of it.* And then he thought, colder: *and when she knows, whose side will she find herself on?*

And after that, the coldest thought of all, the one that would keep him awake in the nights to come: *and whichever side she chooses, I am the one who has to write the report.*

* * *

Near dusk of the third day the land began to change in ways Rivas felt in his body before his eyes confirmed them. The air thickened. The palms leaned inland, bent by years of storms coming off the water. The soil went dark and loose. And the villages lost even the pretense of prosperity—the last settlements before Ceniza were places where the company's presence was visible only in its absence: no warehouse, no agent, no scale, no school, no priest with a functioning roof. Just people. Divers' widows mending nets with twine so old it was more knot than string. Boys hauling water from a well that had gone brackish, drinking it anyway. An old man sitting in a doorway carving something out of driftwood, and when Rivas passed close enough to see, it was a tally-stick—a plain stick crossed with notches, dozens of them, hundreds—and the old man met his eyes with a look of such flat, ancient patience that Rivas had to look away first.

"Stop," the clerk said.

Rivas reined in. Ahead of them, where the road dropped toward a river mouth and the distant smudge of Puerto Ceniza's roofs, a funeral procession was crossing. Six men carrying a bier on their shoulders, no coffin he could afford, just cloth-wrapped shape. Women walking behind with uncovered heads. And no priest—no priest anywhere in the line—only an old woman singing, in a language Rivas half-remembered from his grandmother, a song for the drowned.

"Diver?" the clerk asked quietly.

"Or fisherman. Out here it comes to the same." He watched the procession dwindle down the track toward the shore, where a grave would be dug above the tide line because below it the ground belonged to the water, and the water took enough already. "Drowned men get buried facing the sea here. So they can find their way home."

"Does it work?"

"It works for the living," Rivas said. "It gives them somewhere to put their grief. The company doesn't offer anything to put it in. Except—" he stopped himself.

"Except a debt," the clerk finished. Her voice was very quiet. "Every village on this road owes the company something. I've copied the entries myself."

"Yes."

"And did you ever wonder, señor Rivas, how fishermen who own nothing come to owe so much?"

He turned in the saddle and looked at her fully for the first time since the capital. Her face was still that gray moth face, composed, professional—but her eyes had changed. Something had broken open in them on this road, some door sealed for twelve years, and behind the door was not weakness but a kind of terrible arithmetic, the same arithmetic as the slates in the sorting shed, the same sums done in grief instead of chalk.

"The company sends auditors to Ceniza perhaps once a decade," Rivas said carefully. "Each time, the village debt grows. Each time, the revenue reported falls. And each time, the divers dive deeper for less." He hesitated. "Why does the company send *you*, Señora Quintana? Clerks of correspondence don't ride audits."

For a long moment she didn't answer. The evening wind came off the water, carrying the funeral song, thin and high, almost lost under the sound of the surf. When she spoke, she looked not at him but down the road, toward the village where she was going, and her voice when it came was stripped of everything professional, down to the grain of it.

"Perhaps the company thinks I'm going home," she said. "Perhaps it's wrong."

Rivas sat with that as the light died. He thought about the slip of paper with the director's handwriting. *See that she sees what she is meant to see. See nothing else.* He thought about his father's hands, scarred white like the sorter's, like every diver's hands on this coast. He thought about the report he would be expected to file in the capital, and the blank pages waiting for his honest words, and what those words might cost, and whom.

That night, camped within sight of Puerto Ceniza's lights—few lights, oil lamps burning precious fuel only at the doors of houses with sick inside them—Rivas took out his escort log and began the day's entry. *Departed the sorting district at midday. Route uneventful. Clerk conducted herself per instructions.*

His pen hovered over the page.

Then, in the margin, in letters so small they would pass for ink blots under any hurried inspection, he added a private notation he had never made in eleven years of service, a notation that was either treason or honesty, and he no longer knew the difference:

*Clerk asks questions the route cannot answer.*

Below the village, in the dark, the sea kept its own ledger. And in the morning, when they rode down into Puerto Ceniza together, Rivas would see the clerk stop dead in the road at the sight of a woman standing in a doorway—a woman with her face—and he would understand that everything he thought he was escorting had been wrong from the start.
