## 9. The Divers' Count

Serafina Quintana had kept three kinds of silence in her life, and she knew them the way a diver knows the different darks of the sea.

There was the silence of grief, which came after her husband drowned — a silence like deep water, pressure on every side, that you learned to breathe inside because there was no other air. There was the silence of the company, which was not really silence at all but a noise so constant it became a kind of quiet: the weighmaster's voice calling out false numbers, the clerk's pen scratching, the overseer's boots on the dock, all of it saying *this is how things are* until even the gulls seemed to call the lies back down to the water. And then there was the third silence, the one she had made herself, the one she had built plank by plank for twelve years behind the altar wall of the church of Santa Marina — and that one was not empty at all. That one was full.

It was full of sticks.

She thought about them now as she walked the path from her house down toward the shore, the morning still gray and cool, the tide low enough to show the black rocks where the men launched their rafts. In her basket she carried bread and salt fish and a clay jug of water, the ordinary cargo of an ordinary day, and beneath the ordinary cargo, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom where the bread crumbs would fall and hide it, lay the thing she had come to show her daughter.

Her daughter. The word still did not sit right in her mouth, like a stone she could not swallow or spit out. Dominic. Twelve years gone into the company's ledger house and returned wearing a clerk's plain dress and a stranger's posture, walking through Puerto Ceniza with an escort at her heel and her eyes doing what eyes did in this village: counting everything, saying nothing. Serafina had known her at once on the road — known her by the way she held her head slightly forward, listening before speaking, the way Domingo had listened to the sea before he ever went under it — but knowing was not the same as holding. She had stood in the dust of her own doorway and looked at her child across twelve years and four paces of escort, and neither of them had moved.

*Later*, Serafina had said with her hands, the old way, the way divers' wives spoke when the company's ears were near. *When the light is right.*

The light was right now. Or as right as it would ever be.

* * *

The church of Santa Marina sat above the harbor on a shelf of rock, whitewashed once and weathered since to the color of bone left in sun. Its bell had cracked in a storm nine years ago and the company had never sent money to recast it, so the village told time by the tide instead, which was more honest anyway. Inside, the air smelled of wax and salt and old incense, and the light came down in dusty shafts through windows whose colored glass had been sold off piece by piece in the hard years — blue gone first, then red, then everything but a few panes of green near the roof, so that God's light fell on Puerto Ceniza mostly in shades of the sea.

Father Anselmo was not there; he was at the far house sitting with the widow of Benicio Cruz, who had come up from his last dive coughing blood and died between one tide and the next. It did not matter. Serafina had never needed a priest for what she came to do. What she kept behind the wall was not a sacrament, though she had come to think of it as one.

She went to the altar rail and knelt as if in prayer, because a kneeling woman draws no eye, and reached beneath the rail's base where the masonry had been cut away long ago and mortared back with a mixture of lime and crushed shell that Father Anselmo — the old father, the one before, who had understood — had made himself. The block came free with a sound like a sigh. Behind it, the cavity ran back into the wall itself, a hand's depth, an arm's length, cool and dry and dark.

She drew out the bundle.

Oilcloth, tied with tarred cord, and inside the oilcloth, wrapped again in linen against the damp, the tally-sticks. She unwrapped them here in the green light, kneeling, and let herself look at them the way she looked at them perhaps twice a year, no more, because looking cost something and she had learned to ration her costs.

There were two hundred and eleven of them.

Each one a slat of guayacán wood, dense and pale, cut to the length of a forearm. Each one carved — not written, carved, because carving does not fade and ink can be denied — with the record of a single weighing. The date in notches along the top edge, moon-counts and season-marks, the old system the pearl divers had used before the company brought its paper. The diver's name in the old knot-script, each family having its own sign: a wave for Quintana, a cross-hatch for Cruz, a spiral for the Maldonado sisters. And below the name, two rows of numbers. The true weight of the catch, measured on the church's own balance scale with weights cast from a broken ship's bell, and beside it the weight the company's man had called out, and between them, the difference — the theft, notch by notch, dive by dive, year by year.

Two hundred and eleven sticks. Twelve years. Every catch weighed twice: once on the company's crooked scale on the dock, once on the honest one here in the dark, in secret, by lamplight, by women and old men and children too young to be suspected, while the divers themselves were still wet from the water.

Serafina lifted the top stick and read it, though she did not need to. The first one. Her husband's last full season. *Domingo Quintana, wave-sign.* True weight, forty-one pearls and six ounces of shell. Company's count, twenty-six pearls and three ounces. The difference carved deep, deeper than any other mark, because she had cut it the night after they told her he was dead, and her grief had wanted the chisel to go deep.

They had called him a careless bookkeeper of his own catch, afterward. They had said his accounts were short, that the debt of it would pass to his family, that his daughter could work it off in service to the Verrone ledger house. Nine years old. They had put the arithmetic of his drowning on a child.

Serafina closed her hand around the first stick until the edges pressed lines into her palm.

The poor keep ledgers too, she thought. Not paper ones. Paper burns, paper rots, paper can be seized by the very men who wrote the lies on it. The poor keep their ledgers in wood and in walls and in the bodies of their children, in names given and grudges nursed and stories told low by lamplight, generation to generation, interest compounding. The rich write their histories in ink and the poor write theirs in endurance, and only one of the two can be erased — and it is never the one the rich believe.

She rewrapped the sticks, all but a handful, and put the bundle back behind the wall, and set the masonry block home, and knelt a moment longer with her forehead nearly touching the rail, not praying exactly but making the same promise she had made a thousand times: *Not yet. But soon. And not forgotten — never that.*

Then she took the handful she had kept out, wrapped them small in the oilcloth, and hid them beneath the bread in her basket, and went down to find her daughter.

* * *

Dominic was at the drying racks above the north beach, alone — the escort, the young one, Rivas, standing off at a distance with the studied idleness of a man pretending not to watch. Serafina had watched him all day, the way she watched weather. He was company-made, company-fed, and yet there was something wrong with him for a company man: he looked at the village too long, and he looked away from certain things — the children without shoes, the lean of the boats, the way the whole settlement flinched as one body whenever a company sash caught the light — and a man who looks away is a man who knows what he is seeing.

She climbed the path to the racks. The wind came off the water carrying brine and the sweet rot of kelp, and the drying fish turned slowly on their lines like a congregation of small silver souls.

"Your mother's bread," Serafina said, holding out the basket. "You were too thin at breakfast."

Dominic took it. Their fingers touched over the woven rim, and Serafina felt her daughter's hand startle — felt the exact moment Dominic registered the weight beneath the bread, the shape of it, the faint knock of wood against wood.

"Mother—"

"Eat it on the walk back," Serafina said, loud enough for the escort to hear, ordinary as salt. Then, lower, in the old hand-speech and half-voice together, the way divers' families spoke under the overseers' ears: "The wall behind the rail. You remember the loose stone in the sacristy floor? Beneath it, the second hollow. Your father's sign is on the first stick. Count them, girl. Count every one. Then tell me what your ledgers in the city say, and we will see which of us has been keeping the true books these twelve years."

Dominic's face did what faces did in this village: nothing, outwardly. But her eyes — Domingo's eyes — went bright and wet and fierce all at once, and her hand closed over the basket rim hard enough to whiten the knuckles.

"Twelve years," she whispered. "Someone counted for twelve years?"

"Every catch. Every lie. On our own scale, with our own weights." Serafina looked out at the water, at the black rocks, at the place beyond the point where the channel dropped away into the cold dark her husband had gone down into and not come up from. "The company thinks poverty makes people forget. Poverty makes people *remember*. Forgetting is for those who can afford to lose something."

* * *

That evening the village gathered, and Serafina watched her daughter be received into the oldest ceremony Puerto Ceniza possessed — older than the church, older than the company, older than the bells.

It happened in the house of Rosa Maldonado, the elder of the two sisters, whose floor was swept sand and whose lamp burned whale oil the company had never taxed because the company had never noticed it existed. The divers came in from the water as the light failed, and their wives came in from the racks, and the old men came in leaning on the young, and no one announced anything, because announcement was a city habit. They simply assembled, the way water finds its level.

Dominic sat among them with the tally-sticks spread on a cloth before her, and Serafina watched her read them — truly read them, the knot-script and the notch-dates, the language of the wall — and saw something move through her daughter's body like a current under calm water. Twelve years in the company's house, and the old script had not left her. Of course it hadn't. Serafina had taught it to her at six years old, on this same kind of floor, with this same kind of lamp, before the company ever took her.

"The scale on the dock," Dominic said quietly. "It has a false beam. I've suspected — there are entries, patterns, the shortfalls always run to the same fraction—" She stopped. Recalibrated, the way she did, translating herself from clerk back to daughter. "I found it in the numbers. You found it in the wood."

"We found it in our men coming up light-handed," said Rosa Maldonado flatly, "and going down heavy-hearted. The rest is just keeping score."

An old diver named Urbano, hands ruined white with salt, leaned forward into the lamplight. "Say it plainly, clerk-girl. What happens if the papers in the city match our sticks?"

Dominic looked around the room — at the divers, at the wives, at Serafina — and Serafina saw her daughter make a choice, saw her set down the last of her silences like a tool no longer needed.

"Then the Ceniza debt is a fiction," Dominic said. "A theft dressed as a loan. The indentures taken against it are void. All of them. Mine included." Her voice did not shake. "And the men who built the fiction answer for it — if, and only if, the record survives them. They have already burned one archive's worth of truth, or will. Paper is their weapon and their weakness both. Which is why your sticks matter more than anything I copied in twelve years. Wood doesn't burn easy. And memory—" she touched her own temple, "—they haven't found the trick of seizing that yet."

"Yet," said Rosa, dry as driftwood.

"Yet," Dominic agreed. "So we move before they learn."

The room settled into the particular silence Serafina knew best of all — not grief, not fear, but the silence of decision, the held breath of a village about to do the dangerous arithmetic of hope. Outside, the tide was turning; you could hear it change against the rocks, the long suck and shift of the water deciding to come in.

Urbano spat into the sand floor, ceremonially, sealing it. "Twelve years we carved," he said. "One more night won't drown us."

But Serafina, watching the lamp flame bend in the draft from the door, was not so sure. Because she had seen, on her walk up from the church, a sight that had sat in her chest all evening like a swallowed hook: lantern-light moving along the cliff path where no one walked at night, and beneath the lantern-light, briefly, the gleam of a company sash.

The escort's sash. Rivas, out after dark, moving not toward the guest house but toward the dock — toward the company's signal post, where a lamp hoisted high could speak to the patrol boat anchored beyond the point faster than any rider could ride.

Or toward nowhere at all. A man walking off his conscience. She had raised a daughter among clerks; she knew how many ways a man could carry a message, and how few of them required honesty.

Serafina rose, and began, quietly, to help Rosa bank the fire, and while her hands worked she made the old sign against bad tides, low against her apron, where only the dead and the determined could see it.

Whatever the young man in the sash chose by morning — witness, informer, or something in between — the sticks would need to move before dawn.
