## 10. Rivas Chooses

The rain came to Puerto Ceniza the way debt came to the poor—slowly at first, then all at once, and always with a bill attached.

Emiliano Rivas stood beneath the church eaves and watched it hammer the harbor flat. The escort log was dry under his oilskin, tucked against his ribs where he kept the things that mattered: the log, a stub of pencil, and a letter from his wife that he had read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. *Come home when the work is done,* it said. *Come home whole.* He had been in the company's employ for eleven years, and he was no longer certain which of those two requests the company intended to honor.

Across the square, the church of San Telmo leaned against the wind like an old man leaning into a gale he had long since stopped expecting to survive. Its bell had no clapper—hadn't for years, the sacristan said; the company had taken it for the war effort, or the debt effort, or whatever effort the ledgers required that season—and so when the hour changed, the tower said nothing at all. Rivas had begun to understand that this was the village's condition in miniature. A bell with no voice. A harbor with no boats worth the name. A people with no entry in any book that counted.

He was supposed to be watching the clerk.

That was the honest word for it, though the orders had dressed it up in softer cloth: *accompany the auditor, ensure her safety, ensure the smooth conduct of the records inspection.* Rivas had escorted enough Verrone men to know what smooth conduct meant. It meant the inspection found what the inspection was meant to find. It meant the village signed what it was handed. It meant that when the flagship *Nuestra Señora del Mar* sailed on the spring tide, whatever happened to Puerto Ceniza would already have a name in the books, and the name would be the village's own.

Except the auditor was not behaving like a Verrone man.

* * *

He had watched her for three days now, and everything he saw troubled the picture he had carried out of the capital. Dominic Quintana moved through Ceniza like someone reading a letter from a dead friend. She touched the doorposts. She knew which step of the church was split down the middle. When the fisherwomen spoke to her in the old dialect—the one the company had fined out of its own clerks a generation ago—she understood them before they finished, and Rivas saw her jaw tighten each time, as though understanding were a wound being cleaned.

She was small. She was quiet. She had the particular stillness of people who had learned early that being noticed was expensive. Rivas knew that stillness; he'd seen it in debtors, in widows, in the men who came to the company office to plead for an extension and stood with their caps in their hands as if the cap were the last thing they owned. But there was something else under it in her, a wire pulled taut, and he had learned in eleven years of escort work that the taut ones were the dangerous ones—not dangerous to him, dangerous to themselves, dangerous to the tidy machinery that preferred its people loose and pliable.

On the third evening she told him she would work late in the sacristy, collating the parish registers against the company's copies, and that he need not stand in the rain on her account.

He stood in the rain anyway. Not out of duty. Out of the particular curiosity that had been growing in him since the road, since the villages they'd passed through, since the child with the swollen belly who had watched their mules go by with eyes that held no hope at all, only arithmetic. Rivas had done the arithmetic too, in the nights on the road, lying under the stars with the escort log under his head for a pillow. He had carried company paper for eleven years. He had never once asked what was written in it.

* * *

He told himself he was only walking the perimeter.

The church had two doors and one window that latched, and the sacristy sat behind the altar like a thought behind a face. Rain ran off the eaves in ropes. Through the window, lamplight moved—someone pacing, someone bending—and Rivas found himself at the glass before he had decided to be there, the way a man finds his hand on a knife before he has decided to be angry.

Dominic Quintana sat at a table littered with paper, and she was copying.

Not the parish registers. Rivas had seen her copy registers; her pen moved in the quick, bored rhythm of a clerk doing time. This was different. This was slow. This was the copying of a woman transcribing scripture, or a confession, or a sentence of death. Before her, laid out on the cloth like relics, were sticks—wooden tally-sticks, dozens of them, some black with age, some still pale at the notches, each one carved with marks that meant nothing to him and everything to whoever had cut them.

She was copying the notches into columns. Weights. Dates. Names, in the old dialect, spelled the way the divers spelled them, not the way the company spelled them. Rivas watched her lips move as she worked, reading each name aloud to herself like a prayer for the dead, and he understood, with the slow cold certainty of a man watching the tide come in over his boots, that he was looking at twelve years of theft made visible in wood.

He knew about the weights. Everyone in the company's service knew about the weights, the way everyone in a house knows about the smell in the walls—known and unspoken, known and undiscussed. The company's scales at the receiving stations ran light. The divers' catches were entered at what the scale said, not what the sea had given. The difference went somewhere. Rivas had never asked where. Asking was how clerks ended up mending nets, like that Iriarte fellow whose name the older men still used as a cautionary tale: *careful, or you'll Iriarte yourself.*

Now here was a clerk, in a rain-soaked church in a village the company had already chosen as its scapegoat, copying the divers' own tallies into a record that would hang somebody. And Rivas stood at the window like a thief at his own house, and understood that the somebody it would hang was the company, and that he was the one man in Puerto Ceniza whose duty was to stop it.

* * *

He did not knock. That was the coward's part, and he owned it later, turning it over in the dark like a stone with a hole through it: he did not knock, because knocking would have made him a participant, and standing silent kept him, in the fiction he needed, merely a witness. He stepped back from the window into the rain and walked the perimeter as he had told himself he would, and the rain washed the square empty, and the bell tower said nothing, and inside the church a small woman with ink on her fingers wrote down the true weight of everything the company had ever stolen from the sea.

He was still walking when she came out, near midnight, the satchel flat against her body the way people carry things they cannot afford to lose. She stopped when she saw him. The lamplight from the sacristy door caught her face, and he watched her do the calculation he had seen a hundred debtors do: *how much does he know, and what will it cost.*

"Señor Rivas." Her voice was level. Her hands were not; he saw them tighten on the satchel strap.

"Señora Quintana." He tipped his hat, because the ritual was all he had. "Late work."

"The registers required collating."

"Of course." He looked at the satchel. He looked at her. The rain filled the silence between them like water filling a hull. "The escort log," he said at last, "requires my attention as well. I'll be at the posada until the second bell. After that, I sleep like the dead, and I write poorly when I'm woken."

Something moved in her face—too fast to name, gone before it settled. "That sounds like an ailment," she said carefully. "You should see someone about it."

"I should," Rivas agreed. "No one in the company has found a cure."

They stood in the rain and lied to each other with perfect courtesy, and both of them knew it, and both of them let it stand, because the lie was the only shelter either of them had to offer.

* * *

The escort log waited on the table of the posada's back room, and Rivas sat before it for a long time with the pencil in his hand.

He thought about his wife's letter. *Come home whole.* He thought about the child in the road village, and the bell with no clapper, and the way the divers had looked at him—not with hatred, which he could have borne, but with the flat patience of people who had learned that hatred was a luxury. He thought about Tomás Iriarte, whom he had never met, whose name was a warning, whose crime had been arithmetic. He thought about the eleven years of paper he had carried, all of it blank on the side that faced him, all of it dense with consequence on the side that faced away.

He thought about what happened to escorts who failed. He had seen one, once, in the capital—a man named Ochoa who had lost a prisoner and been broken back to dock labor, then broken further, then gone, the way the company made people go, not dismissed but *dissolved*, his name simply ceasing to appear on any roster until it was as if he had never drawn breath in Verrone employ. Rivas did not fear death. He feared dissolution. He feared his wife going to the company office to ask after her husband and being told, with perfect courtesy, that no such man was employed there.

The pencil hovered.

He wrote: *Third day. Auditor conducted collation of parish registers at the church of San Telmo, 6th hour to 11th. Escort maintained perimeter. No irregularities observed. Village cooperative. Roads poor. Rain.*

*No irregularities observed.* Five words. He looked at them and understood that he had just committed the smallest, largest act of his life. Not a mutiny—nothing so grand, nothing a man could be hanged for. A blank space. A door left unlatched. He had falsified nothing and omitted everything, and the omission was the falsification, and it was worth his name, his wages, his wife's whole future, and the only honest thing he had done in eleven years of carrying the company's paper.

He added, because the log demanded a report on the inspection's progress: *Auditor thorough. Village records in disrepair, as expected. Recommend extension of inspection by three days to ensure completeness.*

Three days. He was buying her three days, and he did not know for what, and he did not let himself know, because knowing would make him a conspirator and not-knowing kept him—kept him what? He laughed aloud in the empty room, a short ugly sound. It kept him nothing. It kept him exactly what he had always been, a man standing in the rain outside a window, except that now he had chosen to stand there.

He blotted the page. He signed it. *E. Rivas, escort, by hand.*

Outside, the rain had stopped. The clouds were tearing open over the harbor, and the moon came through in pieces, laying a broken silver road across the water toward the deep anchorage where, far out, a dark shape sat at anchor—the *Nuestra Señora del Mar*, riding her lights low, taking on stores. He could see the glint of lanterns on her decks. They were loading her fast. Faster than a schedule should allow.

Rivas stood in the posada doorway with the wet wind on his face and did the thing he was best at, the thing the company had paid him eleven years for, the thing he now turned against them with a craftsman's care: he counted. Stores for a long voyage, taken on in haste. Water casks doubled. A crew signed on in the capital, not here, so that no local man would be aboard to hear local talk.

The ship was leaving early.

And whatever Dominic Quintana was copying in that church, whatever the divers had cut into their sticks and hidden in their walls, she had three days—three days he had bought with his own name, written in his own hand, in the company's own book—and the tide would not wait longer than that.

He went inside and lay down and did not sleep, and in the morning, when she passed him in the square with her satchel and her level voice, he touched his hat and said, "The roads are poor, señora. I'd not advise rushing. But I'd advise not lingering past the week."

She looked at him for a long moment, and this time the thing that moved in her face stayed long enough to be named. It was trust, or the first plank of it, laid down between two people standing on a floor that was already burning.

"Thank you, señor Rivas," she said. "I'll be quick."

Far out in the harbor, unseen in the morning haze, the flagship's lanterns went dark one by one as she made ready to take on the last of her cargo—and Rivas, watching, felt the three days in his chest like a fuse already lit.
