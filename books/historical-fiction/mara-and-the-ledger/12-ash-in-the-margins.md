## 12. Ash in the Margins

Beatriz had worked the Verrone kitchen for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned that a house speaks, if you know how to listen. The ledger house spoke in footsteps on the stair, in the particular drag of the director's boots when he was pleased and the clipped tap of them when he was not. It spoke through the dumbwaiter ropes, which trembled differently under a tray of documents than under a tray of food—paper was lighter, but carried with more urgency. It spoke at night, when it should have been silent, in the scrape of chairs above her ceiling where no one had any honest business sitting after dark.

On the morning everything changed, the house said nothing at all. That was the first wrong thing.

She noticed it while kneading bread, hands buried to the wrist in flour and the cool give of dough, and she stopped with her knuckles sunk deep because the absence of sound pressed against her ears like water. No clerks crossing the upper hall. No drawer sliding. Above her, in the counting room where forty men bent over their columns from dawn bell onward, there was only a hush so complete she could hear the fat popping in her own pan.

Then the boots came down the stairs—not the director's, but the runner's, Tomás's replacement, a boy called Ferro with quick feet and quicker eyes—and they did not go toward the door. They went toward the records wing.

Beatriz wiped her hands and stood very still in the warm yeast-smell of her kitchen, and let nineteen years of listening arrange itself into an answer.

*They are taking out the originals.*

She could not have said how she knew. She knew the way she knew a storm before it showed over the water: the gulls going quiet, the light going green, the air acquiring a weight that had nothing to do with heat. Something was about to be taken out of the world, and the house had gone silent to make room for it.

She thought of Dominic.

The girl had been strange for weeks—stranger even than her ordinary strangeness, which Beatriz had long ago decided was simply what intelligence looked like when it had nowhere safe to stand. She ate less. She watched doors. Twice Beatriz had caught her standing in the courtyard looking up at the laundry lines with an expression Beatriz could not read, until one evening, months back, over burnt sugar and whispered talk, Dominic had taught her the lines.

*If ever I need you to know something without anyone knowing we know it,* the girl had said, folding a shirt with the precision of a woman defusing a charge, *I'll hang the washing wrong.*

It had seemed like a game then. A small conspiracy between the cook and the invisible girl, the kind of secret that made the long days bearable. Beatriz had laughed and said, *And how will I answer?*

*Blue cloth on the second line means danger, get out. White turned inside out means wait. And if you ever see my red shawl hung where the sheets should be—* Dominic had paused, and the lamplight had made her face older than her years, *—that means run, and don't come back for anything.*

Beatriz had thought of it since as a charm against loneliness. Two women with a language of linen, when the whole house treated them as furniture that happened to breathe.

Now she dried her hands slowly, untied her apron, and went out into the courtyard as if to shake the mats.

* * *

The morning was gray and close, the sky the color of unwashed wool, and the harbor smell lay thick over everything—brine, tar, fish scales drying to silver on the cobbles. The courtyard between kitchen and records wing was narrow, walled, its flagstones worn into shallow basins that held last night's rain. Three lines crossed it, sagging under pegs.

Dominic's washing was already out. Beatriz saw it from the doorway and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

A blue cloth—her good indigo, the one reserved for feast days—hung crooked on the second line. Beside it, white shirts turned inside out, seams showing like wounds. And at the end of the line, where the great bed-sheets should have billowed, hung the red shawl.

Run, and don't come back for anything.

But Beatriz Montoya had been running calculations her whole life instead of running anywhere, and she stood in the doorway with wet eyes and a dry throat and did the arithmetic that mattered. If Dominic wanted her gone, the girl would have left the signal where only Beatriz would see it, early, with time to spare. This was not early. This was desperate. Which meant Dominic herself might still be inside—which meant the message was not *run* but *warned*, and warned people do not flee; warned people act.

Besides. Nineteen years in this kitchen. She knew every jar, every loose brick behind the flour bin, every servant's passage that the company believed sealed and she knew otherwise. If the originals were being pulled from the records wing this morning, then whatever Dominic had found in them was worth burning a building over, and Beatriz had decided, somewhere between the silence upstairs and the red shawl, whose side the fire would find her on.

She went back inside and began, calmly, to bake.

Baking gave her reason to be in the courtyard, carrying trays, moving between kitchen and ovens with her sleeves rolled and her face set in the pleasant blankness of a woman thinking about crusts. From the yard she could see the records wing's high windows, and twice she saw shadows cross them—men moving with purpose, passing boxes hand to hand. Not carrying boxes *in*. Carrying them *out*, toward the side gate where the company kept its carts.

At midmorning the director's carriage arrived without the director. Aurelio Verrone had not been seen in the city for a week—the servants' talk said the capital, said the flagship, said preparations for sailing—but his carriage wore his crest all the same, and out of it stepped two men Beatriz had never seen before, in coats too fine for clerks and faces too smooth for honest work. They walked the records wing's length slowly, the way a man walks a field he is deciding whether to burn.

One of them stopped at the kitchen window and looked in at her.

Beatriz held his gaze the way she'd learned to hold a landlord's, a customs man's, any man's: chin level, eyes mild, hands busy. She lifted her basting brush slightly, a small salute of the harmless. He looked away.

Her heart hammered so hard she could feel it in her teeth.

That afternoon she sent Ferro's boy to the market with a list she did not need, and while the kitchen emptied she took down the blue cloth and the red shawl and hung them properly, and hung in their place a signal of her own invention, one she and Dominic had never agreed upon but which Beatriz trusted the girl to read anyway: every garment she owned, hung out at once, a full wardrobe of warning, a woman shouting in cotton and wool.

*They are emptying the wing. Men I don't know. Tonight or tomorrow, I think, there will be fire.*

She had no proof. She had nineteen years of listening and the green weight of the air, and she had watched those two smooth-faced men measure the distance from the records wing to the nearest water butt and find it, with a kind of professional satisfaction, inconveniently far.

* * *

Night came down heavy and starless, the clouds keeping their own counsel. Beatriz did not sleep. She sat in the kitchen with a single lamp turned low, mending a shirt she had no intention of finishing, and listened to the house.

At some hour past midnight—she would never be able to say exactly when, only that the church bells had long since given their last quarter—a new sound entered the night. Not loud. That was the horror of it. Not a crash, not a shout. A soft, industrious crackling, like a great many pages turning at once, and beneath it a low roar gathering itself the way wind gathers in a chimney.

She was at the door before she had decided to move.

Fire was climbing the records wing's eastern wall. It had begun low—she could see that even from here, see the way the flames leaned inward, feeding upward from the base as if poured—and it moved with the unnatural speed and the wrong-colored light of oil-fed flame. Someone had done this properly. Someone had done this the way the company did everything: efficiently, quietly, with attention to detail.

For one long moment Beatriz stood in her doorway and simply watched a building die.

She had hated that building for nineteen years, in the diffuse way the poor hate the houses of the rich—hated its ledgers that weighed pearls lighter than God made them, hated its contracts that turned children into column entries, hated the tidy handwriting in which other people's lives were filed away. Now it burned, and the light of it was beautiful, gold and orange and roaring, throwing dancing shadows across the courtyard, and she hated herself for finding it beautiful, and she wept without quite knowing for what—for the paper, maybe, for all those recorded lives going up as smoke, for the truth that had lived in those pages and now lived nowhere, nowhere, nowhere—

Except.

She turned from the door and ran, not toward the fire but toward the servants' stair, up into the choking heat, because somewhere in that building or near it was a girl who hung her washing wrong, and Beatriz Montoya had lost enough people to the sea and the company and the slow grinding years, and she would not—she would *not*—lose this one to fire.

* * *

The corridor outside the records wing was already unbreathable, smoke lying along the ceiling in a black tide, and the heat came off the burning door like the open mouth of an oven. Beatriz pressed her apron over her nose and mouth and shouted into it—Dominic's name, once, twice, her voice swallowed by the roar—and was turning, coughing, blind, when hands closed on her arm and dragged her sideways into the relative clean of the stairwell.

Dominic. Soot-striped, hair escaping its knot, eyes enormous and streaming, with a canvas satchel strapped across her chest and another bundle wrapped in oilcloth clutched under one arm like a child.

"You came *up*," Dominic rasped, incredulous, furious, holding her at arm's length. "I signaled you to *run*."

"You hung your washing wrong," Beatriz gasped. "Nineteen years I've hung washing right. You think I can't read?"

Something broke open in the girl's face—fear and laughter fighting for the same ground—and then the window at the stair's landing burst inward with a soft glassy sigh and the fire found its second breath, and there was no more time for anything. Dominic shoved the oilcloth bundle into Beatriz's arms. "Tally-sticks. From Ceniza. Whatever happens to me, whatever happens tonight—these cannot burn. Do you understand? Everything else is ash by morning. These and what's in my head. That's all that's left."

"What's in your—" But Dominic was already moving, pulling her down the stairs, through the kitchen, out into the courtyard where the night was lit like an obscene festival, and there was no time, there was never any time, only the bundle in her arms and the girl's hand hard around her wrist and the question she would carry unanswered into the smoking dawn.

* * *

They stood in the fish market square with half the city, watching the ledger house burn.

It burned thoroughly. That was the thing Beatriz could not stop noticing, the thing that turned her terror cold and hard as ballast stone: it burned *thoroughly*. The bucket chains came, the watch came, the harbormaster's men came, and everyone arrived in time to watch. The eastern wall fell inward at the precise moment the fire reached the records vault, as if the building had been designed to feed itself to the flames in the correct order. By the time the sky went gray, there was nothing to save and nothing left to save it from, and the company's men were already sweeping the ruins with rakes, sorting the ash into piles, and Beatriz understood that they were not cleaning. They were confirming. Making sure that every page, every original, every inconvenient number had been converted into a substance no magistrate could read.

Around her the crowd murmured its proper noises—terrible, tragic, twelve years of records, thank God no one hurt—and Beatriz stood among them with an oilcloth bundle hidden under her shawl and felt the strangest sensation of her life: grief and certainty arriving together, like twins.

Because she had seen Dominic's face in the stairwell. She had heard the girl say *what's in my head*. Twelve years of copying other people's letters, and the company had taught her so well, trained her memory so ruthlessly, that they had built their own witness. They had burned the record and left the recorder standing in the fish market square, soot-striped and alive, with the numbers folded away behind her eyes where no rake could reach them.

And in Beatriz's arms, wrapped in oilcloth and smelling faintly of salt and church stone, were twelve years of tally-sticks cut by divers' hands—the true weights, the true catches, the true names. The poor keep their own ledgers. She had told Dominic once, laughing, that a cook keeps accounts too: who eats, who doesn't, who is hungry in a way food won't fix. Now she held the divers' account against her ribs and swore to it, silently, in the smoke-smelling dawn, with the same gravity she'd have used on a saint.

"They'll say it was a lamp," Dominic said beside her, very quietly. Her eyes stayed on the ruin. "An overturned lamp, a drunk watchman. There will be an inquiry, and it will find negligence, and the loss will be called a misfortune." A pause. "By tomorrow there will be no originals. Only copies—and copies can be disputed."

"Can yours?" Beatriz asked.

Dominic finally turned to look at her, and in the gray light her face was stripped of everything young. What remained was not fear. Beatriz had expected fear, had braced to comfort it. What she saw instead frightened her more and steadied her more, both at once: it was the look of a woman stepping off a ledge who has finally stopped pretending she has wings and started trusting the fall.

"No," Dominic said. "Mine can't. Because mine aren't paper anymore." Her hand came to rest on the oilcloth bundle, on the tally-sticks, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, meant for Beatriz alone beneath the crowd's murmur and the hiss of the dying fire. "Tomás first. Then the ice cellar. We rebuild it—all of it, every figure, every name—before they realize the fire didn't finish the job. Can you hide us?"

Beatriz looked at the burning house that had fed her for nineteen years, at the ash drifting down like filthy snow, settling in her hair, on her shoulders, in the creases of her working hands. She thought of the smooth-faced men measuring distances to water butts. She thought of the red shawl, and of every person she had ever failed to warn.

"Nineteen years," she said, "they thought I was just the cook."

She shifted the bundle higher against her chest, turned her back on the ashes, and led Dominic into the waking streets—toward Tomás, toward the ice cellar, toward whatever thin ice of chance remained—while behind them the company's men raked the ruins of their own crime, and the smoke rose straight up into the empty sky like a letter no one would ever dare to sign.
