## 13. Fugitive Clerk

The fish market at dawn was a country Tomás Iriarte had never visited, though he had lived in this port city for fifty years. It had its own laws, its own weather, its own language—a dialect of shouts and gull-cries and the wet slap of hake on marble—and it had, most importantly for a man who had spent the last three days learning how to be hunted, a thousand doors and none of them locked.

He came down the ramp behind the ice house with his collar up and his hands in the sleeves of Beatriz's borrowed coat, which smelled of onions and tallow and something sweeter underneath—cinnamon, perhaps, from the kitchens of the Verrone ledger house, from the life that had burned behind them in the night. The coat was too small across the shoulders. He wore it like a penance.

Below the market floor, beneath the stalls where women gutted mackerel with quick silver knives, the ice cellar ran the length of the building: a vaulted brick tunnel, older than the company, older than the colony's charter, dug by men whose names no ledger had troubled to keep. Sawdust lay knee-deep along the walls. Blocks of lake ice sat shrouded in burlap, sweating in the dark, breathing their cold breath into the gloom. The air tasted of salt, iron, and winter kept prisoner underground.

Tomás had hidden in worse places. He had hidden, for eleven years, inside the Verrone archive itself—in plain sight, among ten thousand boxes of correspondence, a man who knew too much pretending to know nothing. That hiding place had failed him eventually. All hiding places did. But he was old now, and tired, and his knees ached with the damp, and he had decided on the walk down from the net-mender's shed that if this one failed too, it would fail while he was doing something instead of nothing.

That was new, for him. He wanted to mark it.

* * *

They had divided the cellar into territories, the way fugitives do, the way children do. Beatriz claimed the corner nearest the drain, where a trickle of meltwater kept a channel clear and she could hear footsteps on the floorboards above—her ear, she said, was better than any sentry's, trained by nineteen years of listening to clerks decide whether to be kind today or not. Dominic had taken the middle stretch of wall, where the lamplight from their single shielded candle fell strongest, because her eyes were young and the work needed them.

And Tomás—he had the far end, near the ice itself, where the cold crept through the brick and settled into his bones like an old debt coming due. He did not mind. Cold sharpened him. He remembered being twenty-six and furious in the archive, reading the pearl manifests against the divers' own tallies, feeling that same chill climb his spine: *the numbers are wrong, the numbers have been wrong for years, and everyone above me has signed their name to the wrongness.* He had thought then that being right would be enough. That a man need only hold the true figures up to the light and the world would blink and correct itself.

Eleven years ago they had dismissed him for "irregularities of temperament." The irregularity, as he understood it, was that he had spoken.

Now he sat on a crate of sawdust with paper on his knees—paper scavenged, begged, stolen. A merchant's wrapping sheet gone soft with grease. The blank half of a butcher's invoice. Three leaves torn from a hymnal that Beatriz had liberated from a chapel poor-box, God forgive them both, though Tomás suspected God had larger concerns than the defacement of psalms. On these scraps, by candle stub, in the cold, they were rebuilding what fire had eaten.

"Again," Dominic said. She did not look up. Her pen moved in the small, swift strokes he recognized from the ledger house—a hand trained to be legible but unremarkable, a hand that had learned invisibility so deeply it had carried the lesson even onto smuggled paper. "From the top. 'Fifteen.'"

"Fifteen," Tomás said. "October of thirty-six. The Ceniza catch comes in at four hundred and twelve marks by the divers' count. The receiving clerk logs two hundred and ninety."

"Two hundred and ninety," she repeated, writing. "And the difference?"

"One hundred and twenty-two marks, entered as—" he closed his eyes; the archive lived behind them still, whole and burning-bright, "—entered as an adjustment against the Ceniza debt. Which did not exist. There was no debt. There had never been a debt. The debt was invented the same month the shortfall began, a vessel built to carry stolen weight, and every clerk who touched the entry initialed it without question because the entry had been initialed before them. That is the genius of it, you understand? No one stole anything. The ledger simply *drifted*, year over year, the way a ship drifts when everyone aboard assumes someone else is holding the tiller."

"And twelve years of drift," Dominic said quietly, "comes to how much?"

Tomás told her. The number sat in the dark between them like a third person.

Beatriz, at the drain, said nothing, but she had stopped wringing the cloth in her hands.

* * *

They worked in watches. It could not be helped—the candle was short, the food shorter, and above them the market opened at first light with all its clatter and traffic, and a fugitive must sleep sometime. But Tomás found he did not want to sleep. Sleep was where the fire lived. He dreamed of the archive burning, of ten thousand boxes curling at their edges, of the index cards lifting off their shelves in a black snowfall of ash—and woke each time reaching for a card catalog that no longer existed anywhere in the world except inside three heads and a church wall in Puerto Ceniza.

So he stayed awake and talked instead, low, in the dark, while Dominic copied and Beatriz listened, and somewhere in the second night of it he understood that what they were doing down here was not arithmetic at all.

It was testimony. They were building a witness out of paper.

"You should rest," Dominic said once, when his voice had gone hoarse. "You're seventy-one."

"I am sixty-eight, and flattered." He flexed his stiff fingers. "Rest is for people whose memories are written down elsewhere. Mine are written nowhere but here." He tapped his temple. "Which, I grant you, is not archival-grade storage. But it is the only original left."

"That isn't true anymore." She glanced up, candlelight catching the edge of her face. "There are the tally-sticks. Serafina will bring them."

"Serafina." He said the name carefully, tasting it. "Your mother."

"My mother." Something crossed the girl's face then—grief, or its cousin—and she bent back over the greasy wrapping paper. "She'll come. She swore she'd come."

Tomás watched her a moment. He had spent a lifetime around records; he knew a document under strain when he saw one, knew the way ink blots where a hand has hesitated too long. But he also knew—this was the thing the archive had taught him, the thing worth eleven years of ruin knowing—that some entries take years to post. Some debts sit unrecorded in the heart's marginalia, accruing interest in silence, until one day someone demands a full accounting and there is nothing to pay it with but words.

He did not ask. Instead he said: "When your mother arrives, she'll need to give sworn statement. And sworn statement requires a notary, and notaries cost money we don't have and courage most of them weren't born with."

"Then we find one who wasn't born with less," Beatriz said from the dark.

Tomás laughed—actually laughed, a dry rusted sound that surprised them all, himself most of all. "Listen to her. Nineteen years in that kitchen and they never audited *her* books."

* * *

On the third day, a boy came down the ramp with a basket of fish heads—for the cats, he said, loudly, to no one—and left beside the ice blocks a parcel wrapped in oilcloth: bread, hard cheese, a twist of salt, and a folded note in Beatriz's sister-in-law's hand. The boy was paid in nothing. The boy asked nothing. This was another law of the fish market, Tomás was learning: the poor keep their own networks of credit, invisible, unwritten, and more reliable than any bank. He thought of the tally-sticks in the church wall and wondered why he was ever surprised. The poor had always kept accounts. It was only the powerful who pretended otherwise, and only because they intended to default.

Beatriz divided the bread with a fish-gutting knife borrowed from a stall above, wiping the blade first on her apron with a ceremony that made Dominic almost smile. They ate sitting on crates, knees nearly touching in the narrow vault, and the cheese was terrible and the bread was stale and Tomás found his throat tightening over it, absurdly, because the last time he had shared a meal it had been alone, standing, over the sink in the net-mender's shed.

"You're crying," Dominic said. Not cruelly. Observantly, the way she observed everything.

"I am eating," Tomás said, "in company. It has been some time. The condition presents similarly."

Beatriz snorted. Then, because she was Beatriz, she reached over and put a second piece of cheese on his paper plate without a word, the way you'd correct an error in a ledger—not discussed, simply amended.

He looked at the two of them—the disgraced archivist, the fugitive cook, the runaway clerk—and thought: *this is what they took from me and called it discipline. Not the position, not the pension. The table. The people across the table.* Eleven years he had eaten standing up. Now, in an ice cellar, wanted by the richest company in the colony, he ate seated, among family.

Not blood family. Better. Blood, in his experience, was a matter of accident. This was a matter of choice, entered into voluntarily, witnessed by candlelight, binding upon all parties.

A contract, in other words. He was an archivist. He recognized one when he sat inside it.

* * *

It was during the fourth watch, deep in the small hours, that Dominic stopped writing.

Tomás heard it—the cessation, the way you hear a clock stop. He looked up from his recitation. She was staring at the scrap of butcher's invoice in front of her, at the column of recovered figures, and her face in the candlelight had gone very still, the stillness of water before it freezes.

"The letter," she said.

"What of it?"

"The sealed letter. The one they burned the house to find." Her voice was flat, controlled, a voice walking carefully across thin ice. "I copied it before I resealed it. Every word. I have it—" she touched her temple, mirroring his gesture of the night before, and he felt the symmetry land in his chest like a dropped coin, "—in here. Word for word. Aurelio's own instructions, in his own phrasing, naming Ceniza, naming the timing, naming the flagship."

Tomás set down his pencil very slowly.

"Do you understand," he said, "what you just told me? I spent eleven years failing for lack of a witness. Figures without a witness are a dispute. Figures with a witness are a case. And you—" he had to stop, had to press his knuckles briefly to his mouth, because sixty-eight years of composure were threatening, suddenly, to come apart over a greasy butcher's invoice in a freezing cellar, "—you have the director's own letter in your head, girl. We don't need the archive anymore. We don't need the originals. We *are* the originals."

Above them, faintly, the market was waking. Gulls screamed. The first cart wheels ground over cobbles. Somewhere a woman began calling the price of sardines in a voice like a bell.

Dominic looked at the columns of reconstructed figures, at the psalm-leaves and the invoices and the wrapping sheets, all of it fragile, all of it contraband, all of it true.

"Then we need it certified," she said. "Sworn, signed, sealed. Paper without a seal is just paper—you taught me that."

"I did," Tomás agreed.

"So who certifies a fugitive's memory?"

Beatriz turned the knife slowly on the cutting board. When she spoke, she spoke to the blade rather than to them, in the tone of a woman opening a door she has been keeping shut for a long time.

"There's a notary," she said. "Down by the harbor gate. Solano, her name is. Inés Solano." The knife paused. "Her brother dove for Verrone. They brought him back on a Tuesday, weighted differently than he went out."

* * *

The candle had burned to a thumb's width. Outside, morning was establishing itself in full—carts, cries, the smell of frying fish drifting down through the floorboard gaps, ordinary life proceeding in cheerful ignorance one story above the conspiracy in its cellar. Somewhere out there, Rivas was missing, and the company was hunting, and Serafina was on the road with twelve years of tally-sticks in a bundle against her chest.

Somewhere out there, Inés Solano was keeping a notary's office by the harbor gate, not yet knowing that three strangers in a hole beneath the fish market were about to arrive and ask her to commit professional suicide.

Tomás gathered the scattered pages, squared their edges, and bound them with a string pulled from the hem of his borrowed coat—an archivist's gesture, automatic, tender. A provisional file. Unofficial. Uncertified. Alive.

"We go at dusk," he said. "Market crowd for cover. Beatriz leads—she knows the streets' opinions of us better than we do." He looked at Dominic, at the exhausted young face above the columns of recovered truth, and added, gently: "Sleep now. Both of you. What remains cannot be done tonight, and tomorrow we ask a grieving woman to risk everything on our word."

"And if she says no?" Dominic asked.

Tomás blew out the candle. In the dark, the ice breathed its long cold breath, and he answered with the only doctrine he had ever trusted:

"Then we knock on every notary's door in this city until one of them remembers what the seal is *for*."
