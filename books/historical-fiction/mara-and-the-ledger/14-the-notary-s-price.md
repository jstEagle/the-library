## 14. The Notary's Price

The bell above Inés Solano's door had not rung in nine days.

She kept count the way she kept count of everything now—by marks in a small notebook she carried in her apron pocket, a habit so deep it had become a form of breathing. Nine days without a client who needed anything certified, attested, or sworn. Nine days since the harbor magistrate's clerk had come by with his soft apologetic cough to tell her that, pending certain inquiries, it might be best if Señora Solano refrained from taking on new commissions involving maritime contracts. *Involving maritime contracts.* In this port, where every second document smelled of tar and salt, that was not a restriction. It was a burial.

She sat at her desk with her back to the window because she did not need light for what she was doing. She was sharpening her pens. All fourteen of them. She had bought good goose quills over the years and kept them like a surgeon keeps blades, and each morning since the clerk's visit she had trimmed and cut and pointed them until the desk looked like the aftermath of some small, orderly violence.

Her brother Joaquín had been dead eleven years, four months, and six days. She knew the arithmetic to the day because she had entered it herself, in her own hand, in the parish register, when the company's man came with the death notice already written out and asked her—her, the notary, the one person in the district whose word was supposed to be iron—to countersign a date that was wrong.

*Eleven days wrong,* she had told him. *He died on the eleventh of March, not the twenty-second.*

*The company's records show the twenty-second,* the man had said, with the mild patience of someone explaining weather to a child.

*Then the company's records are wrong,* she had said, and had not moved her pen.

They had paid her for the delay in ways that took eleven years to arrive. Small refusals first: no more company work, then no work referred through any office the company touched, which was most offices, until her practice thinned to widows' wills and fishwives' debts and the occasional sailor too drunk or too desperate to care whose seal he got. Then the quieter things—a lease not renewed, a complaint about the state of her shutters filed with the port authority, a rumor seeded in two taverns that Solana the notary was honest but unlucky, and unlucky was worse than dishonest in a town that lived off the sea's moods.

Inés had survived all of it the way a whetstone survives: by being hard, flat, and useful to anyone willing to drag their dullness across her. But there were mornings—and this was one—when she woke with Joaquín's face behind her eyes and her hands already reaching for the pens before she remembered there was nothing left to write.

The bell rang at half past eight.

Not a customer's ring—the hesitant double-tap of someone testing whether a door would even open—but a firm pull, the ring of a person who had decided before crossing the street. Inés capped her inkwell out of pure professional reflex and said, "Come."

Three people came in, and the room changed temperature.

The first was a woman she recognized by silhouette before face: broad-shouldered, flour-white at the cuffs, moving with the deliberate calm of someone who had spent her life in kitchens where sudden movement meant burns. Beatriz, the ledger house cook—or former cook; the ledger house had burned four nights ago, and the whole port had watched the smoke stand up over the rooftops like a black tree. The second was an old man, stooped, net-mender's calluses visible even from across the room, with the particular stillness of a person who had once held a position and never fully set it down. Tomás Iriarte. Inés had heard the name whispered for years—the archivist who accused Verrone of false weights and was broken for it, the cautionary tale told to every junior clerk in the district: *this is what honesty costs here.*

And the third was a young woman, slight, dark-eyed, standing slightly behind the other two as though invisibility were a garment she wore well and could shed when needed. There was ink on the side of her right hand—not a smudge but a stain, the permanent shadowing of someone who wrote twelve hours a day—and she stood the way clerks stand in front of superiors, weight settled low, hands folded, eyes down and yet somehow taking in everything.

"You're closed," Inés said. It was not a question and not quite an accusation. "Or you haven't heard."

"We've heard," said Beatriz. Her voice was low and unhurried, kitchen-voice, the voice of a woman who had fed frightened people before. "We've come because you're the only notary left who'll do it."

"Do what, exactly?" Inés leaned back. "I should tell you plainly, so no one wastes your time or mine. I have been advised against maritime commissions. Which means any document touching the Verrone Trading Company is, for me, a door painted shut. If you want a bill of sale certified or a widow's will witnessed, I'm your woman until sundown. Anything else—" she spread her hands, "—you'd be asking me to seal my own ruin into yours."

"Señora." The old man spoke for the first time, and his voice was dry paper, careful, a voice that had rehearsed being discredited. "What we carry is not a commission. It is a deposition. Sworn testimony from eleven divers of Puerto Ceniza regarding systematic underweighting of pearl catches over twelve years, together with the original tally-sticks recording each divergence between the company's ledgers and the actual take. Certified copies of reconstructed figures, prepared from memory by myself and by—" he paused, and the young woman stepped forward, "—by me," she said.

Inés looked at her properly then. Up close the stain on her hand was darker, and there was something else: a scar along her thumb, old, the kind a pen knife gives a child learning to cut quills. A lifer. An indentured clerk grown to womanhood inside the ledger house itself.

"Dominic Quintana," the young woman said. "Formerly of the Verrone counting floor. I copied the sealed correspondence ordering the falsification. I can reproduce its contents from memory. And I am the daughter of Serafina Quintana, of Puerto Ceniza, whose name appears in the company's books as debtor to the sum of—" she recited it without looking at any paper, "—four hundred and twelve pesos, seven reales, which debt does not exist."

The silence after that had texture. Inés found she was gripping the edge of her desk.

"You understand," she said slowly, "what you are describing. You are not asking me to certify a debt. You are asking me to make war."

"Yes," said Dominic Quintana, simply, as though confirming an address.

* * *

Inés made them wait while she thought, because thinking was part of her craft and she would not be hurried in it, not even now, especially not now. She walked to the window and looked out at the street, at the ordinary morning—the fish carts, the gulls arguing over a dropped sardine, two boys chalking a hopscotch grid on the cobbles. The world going on as if none of it touched it. As if the smoke over the harbor last week had been a chimney fire and nothing more.

She thought about the shape of what they were asking. Not the danger—that she could price; danger had a rate like anything else, and she had been paying installments on it for eleven years. She thought about the *form* of it. A deposition was not a letter, not a grievance shouted into a tavern. A deposition was architecture. Testimony given before a notary, reduced to writing, read aloud to the deponent, corrected, confirmed, signed, sealed—it became a thing with load-bearing walls. Tear down a letter and it was gone. Tear down a deposition improperly taken and the tearing itself became evidence of guilt.

But only if it was taken *perfectly*. Only if every figure matched every stick, every stick matched every diver, every diver stood by every word under oath. One soft line—one merciful rounding-down, one generous omission, one phrase written to protect a witness rather than record them—and the whole structure cracked, and men like Aurelio Verrone, who could smell a hairline fracture in a document the way sharks smelled blood in three fathoms of water, would drive their entire fortune through it.

"Before I say yes or no," Inés said, turning from the window, "you should know what I will require. Because I will do this, if I do it, in one way only. My way."

Beatriz glanced at the others, a small motion like a cook checking her station, and nodded.

"First." Inés raised one finger. "No softening. I have read enough company papers to know how they write—they love the passive voice the way sinners love confession without penance. *Mistakes were made. Discrepancies arose. Weights were recorded.* No. In my record, divers will be named. Their names, their fathers' names, the dates of their dives, the weights they brought up, the weights the company wrote down, and the difference between them, to the grain. Every divergence stated as what it is: a theft. I will not write *irregularity*. I will not write *shortfall*. If a man named Emiliano Reyes brought up forty ounces and was paid for twenty-six, my record will say the company stole fourteen ounces from Emiliano Reyes on the fourth of June in the year of our Lord eighteen hundred thirty-nine. Does that frighten you? Because it should frighten you. Every named man becomes a target the moment my seal touches the page."

"They know," said the old diver's daughter quietly. "My mother has known for twelve years what naming herself would cost. They kept tally-sticks in a church wall for twelve years waiting for exactly this."

Inés studied her. There was no tremor in the girl, and that itself was remarkable—Inés had seen captains weep over less—but there was something underneath the steadiness, something banked rather than absent, like coals raked over at night. Grief, she thought. Old grief, well-managed. She knew the breed. She was the breed.

"Second." A second finger. "Every deponent swears in full knowledge. I will read each testimony aloud, word for word, and each diver will confirm it or correct it before signing, and I will note the corrections in the margin with the date and hour. No blanket oaths. No 'we all agree.' Each hand, separately, on each page. If one man later recants under pressure, the rest must survive him."

"That's why we came to you," Tomás Iriarte said, and something in his voice cracked slightly, the dry paper dampening. "Twelve years ago I took my figures to a lawyer who wanted to help me. He softened everything. He wrote 'discrepancies.' He wrote 'possible error in the weighing process.' By the time the company's advocates were done, my accusation sounded like a quarrel over arithmetic. They didn't have to destroy the evidence. The evidence destroyed itself, politely." He looked down at his net-mender's hands. "You are offering the opposite of mercy."

"I am offering accuracy," Inés said. "Mercy is God's department. I keep the other office."

"Third." A third finger. "The originals of everything stay with me. Copies go wherever you like—harbormaster, magistrate, a hole in a church wall if it comes to that—but the sworn originals, under my seal, remain in my custody, and I decide who sees them. That is not pride. That is chain of custody. If this ever reaches an inquiry, the first question will be who held these documents and whether they could have been altered. My answer will be: the notary who took them, who has nothing to gain and everything to lose, and who has already lost everything once and did not bend." She paused, and heard her own voice go quieter, which she hated. "That is also the answer to your real question, which you have all been too polite to ask. Why me. Why a ruined notary with a painted-shut door. Because, señores and señoritas, they cannot threaten me with ruin. I have been living in it for eleven years. Ruin and I are old acquaintances. It holds my coat while I work."

Beatriz laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly pressed flat, but real.

"And fourth," Inés said, and here she stopped, because the fourth condition was not a condition at all but a wound she had been carrying to this desk for eleven years, four months, and six days, waiting for someone to arrive upon whom she could finally set it down. "Fourth. Joaquín goes in the record."

The young clerk went very still.

"My brother dove for Verrone for nine seasons. Eleven years ago they brought me a death notice dated eleven days after he died, and they wanted my countersignature, because a notary's mark makes even a lie load-bearing. I refused the date and signed nothing, and they buried him anyway on their date, in their register, and every document since has agreed with theirs and contradicted me." Her voice did not shake; she had spent a decade making sure it would not. "If I certify this deposition, my brother enters it. His full name, Joaquín Solano Reyes. The season he drowned—because he did not die of natural causes on a boat that came home light, whatever the company's paper says. The weight he brought up his last dive, which I know because he told me the night before, laughing, because he'd finally found a bed of pink pearls big enough to buy his own boat. Forty-three ounces. The ledger records nineteen. And the difference between those numbers is the size of the grave they put him in."

She stopped. The room held still around her. Outside, a gull screamed and was answered.

"So," Inés said. "Those are my terms. Whole truth, no softening. Individual oaths. Custody with me. And Joaquín in the record, by name, with his forty-three ounces, so that when this is read out—inquiry, courtroom, gallows steps, wherever it ends—I will hear his name spoken aloud in an official proceeding at least once before I die." She picked up her finest pen, turned it in her fingers, and looked at the three of them across the desk that had been bare for nine days. "Now. Do you accept a notary with a grudge? Or do you want someone gentler, if you can find one?"

Dominic Quintana stepped forward and laid a folded paper on the desk—not the tally-sticks, Inés saw, those were wrapped separately in oilcloth and handled like relics, but a fair copy of figures in a small, disciplined, beautiful hand.

"We don't want gentle," the girl said. "Gentle is what they hired."

Inés uncapped the inkwell she had closed an hour ago, and the smell of it rose up, iron-gall and old, the smell of every true thing she had ever written.

"Then we begin," she said. "Sit. All of you. This will take the night, and I warn you—" and here something lifted in her chest that she had not felt since before the clerk with the soft cough, something with edges, something like a blade finding its angle against the stone, "—I do my best work angry."

It was nearly dawn when the knock came—not the bell, but a fist on wood, urgent, twice, then again—and Beatriz went white as flour and crossed the room in three strides.

Through the door, breathless, a boy's voice, the fish-market boy who ran messages: *"Señora—the harbor—the flagship—they've moved up the sailing. Tomorrow's tide. And they're asking at every gate after a woman clerk."*

Inés looked at Dominic Quintana across the lamplight, at the ink-stained hand frozen above a page already half-sworn, and reached for a fresh sheet.

"Then we finish tonight," she said. "Every name. Every ounce. Before their tide comes in, we give the truth a tide of its own."
