## 15. What Serafina Swears

The sea had never frightened Serafina Quintana. It had taken her husband, yes, and it took a man from every third house in Puerto Ceniza, and still she rose before dawn each morning of her life to watch it turn from iron to pewter to the pale green of young leaves, because a woman who fears the sea in a fishing village is a woman who has already drowned. But the city — the city frightened her. The city was the sea's opposite: it did not take men cleanly, with a single great breath, but slowly, in small coins and small papers, until what walked out of it barely resembled what had walked in.

She stood at the harbor gate with her bundle over her shoulder and salt drying white on her forearms from the coastal packet's deck, and she looked up at the warehouses stacked along the waterfront like tombstones that had learned to stand, and she thought: this is where they weigh my daughter's life out in grams.

Twelve years since she had seen Dominic. Twelve years, one month, and nine days — not that Serafina counted. A mother counts nothing so carefully as the thing she refuses to count.

* * *

They had come for her daughter on a Tuesday in the season of the north wind, when Dominic was nine years old and still smelled of the sea no matter how often Beatriz — no, not Beatriz then; some other cook, some earlier kind hand in the company kitchen — scrubbed her with lye soap. Serafina remembered the smell because she had held the girl once, hard, at the gate of the Verrone counting-house, and breathed her in like a woman memorizing a psalm, and under the soap there had been salt, and under the salt there had been her own milk, twelve years gone and still, impossibly, present.

Her husband had been three weeks drowned when the company man came. Three weeks, and the pearl ledgers already open on the table between them like a wound.

"Your husband's account stands in arrears," the man said. He was not cruel. That was the terrible thing about him, the thing Serafina would spend years trying to explain to people who had never sat across such a table. He was kind the way a scale is kind. It weighs what is placed upon it and feels nothing, and its nothingness is precisely its function. "Two hundred forty pesos against his advances. The boat is company property. The gear is company property. There remains the matter of the child's keep."

"She has a mother."

"The mother cannot service the debt." He turned a page. "Indenture is available. Domestic and clerical training. Room, board, instruction. Wages applied against principal." He looked up then, and his eyes were tired and human and empty as two washed shells. "You may visit her. Quarterly."

Serafina had signed. She had signed with the name she was given at her baptism and had never needed on paper before, and her hand had shaken so badly the clerk had steadied the page for her, courteous as a man holding a stirrup for a lady mounting a horse she does not want to ride.

Quarterly visits lasted two years. Then the company reorganized its coastal routes and the packets stopped calling at Ceniza, and the visits became letters, and the letters — she learned this only now, standing in the shadow of the harbor gate, an old woman with a bundle and a grievance — the letters had likely never gone anywhere at all but into some clerk's fire. She had written her daughter every quarter for ten years. She had written about the weather and the church roof and the new priest who could not dive and the old priest who could, and about the tally-sticks going into the wall behind the altar stone, wrapped in oilcloth, one for every year, every diver, every short-weighted catch. She had written, I am well, do not worry, we are keeping count. She had never once written, I miss you, come home, forgive me, because she understood even then that whatever read those letters before Dominic did would not pass along a mother's heart, only a mother's information.

And now Inés Solano's letter had reached Ceniza by ways the company could not intercept — carried in a fish crate, sewn into a dried cod's belly like a message from God delivered by the humblest of prophets — and Serafina had read it twice, folded it into her bodice, and told the divers' council: I will go and swear it myself.

Not because the tally-sticks were not proof enough. Because a record without a witness is an accusation, and an accusation without a face is easy to burn. She had buried a husband and surrendered a daughter to paper, and she knew exactly what paper was worth, and what it was not.

* * *

Beatriz met her at the gate, a broad brown woman smelling of onions and woodsmoke, with hands like bread dough and eyes that went soft the moment they found Serafina's face among the crowd of disembarking passengers.

"You have her chin," Beatriz said, by way of greeting. "And her stubborn mouth."

"I have my own mouth."

"That too." Beatriz took the bundle without asking, which Serafina allowed, because in a port town a woman carrying nothing looks like a woman going somewhere, and a woman going somewhere can be followed. "Come. Not through the main streets. Tomás says the director's men are asking after arrivals from the coast. Asking polite questions with impolite eyes."

"Then they know."

"They suspect. There's a difference, and the difference is us staying alive long enough to make it a knowing." Beatriz shifted the bundle onto her hip as if it were a child, and something in Serafina's chest twisted at the sight, at the ordinary domestic tenderness of it, at the thought that some other woman had been doing this small labor of carrying near her daughter for twelve years while she herself had carried only the memory of the weight.

They went through the fish market, down steps slick with scales and brine, into a cold room where ice came up from the mountains in winter and lay packed in sawdust like snow that refused to die. Lamplight. The smell of old fish and clean cold. And there, at a trestle table scarred by knives and years, bent over loose sheets of paper covered in a small fierce handwriting —

Dominic stood up.

Serafina had imagined this moment perhaps ten thousand times, and in none of the imaginings had it happened like this: no falling into arms, no flood of tears, no words at all at first. Just her daughter across a cellar, grown tall and thin and watchful, dressed in a clerk's plain gray, her hair pinned back like any working woman's, her hands — Serafina noticed the hands first, always the hands, a diver's observation — her hands ink-stained and steady, and her eyes wide and wet and terrified, and behind the terror something else, something Serafina recognized because she had raised it there herself:

the look of a child waiting to learn whether she is welcome.

"You've her father's shoulders," Serafina said, because the true words would not come, and false ones at least could carry a body across a room.

"And whose temper?"

"His. God help you." And then she was crossing the ice-cold floor and her daughter was crossing it too and they met in the middle like two swimmers meeting in deep water, gripping forearms first, the way divers greet each other surfacing, testing, confirming the other is real and solid and breathing — and only then did Serafina pull her in, and Dominic came apart against her shoulder with a sound Serafina had not heard since the girl was small, a sound like a keel scraping bottom, and Serafina held her and rocked her and said nothing, absolutely nothing, because anything said now would be said too soon and could never be unsaid.

Behind them, quietly, the others withdrew. Even Beatriz, who was not good at withdrawing from anything. The lamplight guttered. Somewhere above, the market roared on, indifferent, selling its silver fish.

* * *

Later — hours later, after food, after the first storm had spent itself — they sat across the table from each other, and Serafina understood that the second conversation, the harder one, was waiting for them like a patient animal.

"You visited," Dominic said. Her voice had recovered its flatness, the clerk's voice, the voice of a woman who had trained feeling out of her speech the way other women trained calluses into their palms. "Twice a year, the first two years. Then you stopped."

"The packets stopped calling."

"So I was told. Eventually. By omission." Dominic's fingers flattened on the table, pressing the paper beneath them as if to hold the world still. "I wrote you. Every quarter. For ten years."

"I wrote you too." Serafina's throat closed. She forced the words through. "Every quarter. For ten years."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Dominic stared at the table, and Serafina watched her daughter perform arithmetic — she could see it happening, see the columns adding themselves behind those gray eyes — and arrive at the sum neither of them wanted.

"They burned them," Dominic said. "Both directions. They burned twelve years of letters."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you come? After the routes changed. You could have walked. You could have sold the boat—"

"It was not our boat. You know it was not our boat." The words came out sharper than Serafina intended, and she saw them land, saw her daughter flinch, and she hated herself for it and continued anyway, because this was why she had come, this was the whole reason she had made Inés put it in writing: not the testimony for the magistrate, which any literate diver could have given, but this, here, the accounting between mother and daughter that no notary could certify. "Dominic. Listen to me. I could not come. Do you understand what I am saying? If I had appeared at that ledger house demanding my daughter, they would have found a way to add my demand to your debt. Everything in that house was arithmetic. My love was arithmetic. Your grief was arithmetic. They would have weighed it and entered it and charged interest on it, and you'd have served twenty years instead of twelve. So I stayed away, and I let you hate me if hating me kept you safe, and I wrote letters into a fire I didn't know was a fire, and I kept count."

"Kept count." Dominic laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as a broken shell. "The tally-sticks. Yes. I've seen them now. Twelve years of notches in palmwood behind a church wall, and not one word sent to me. Not one warning. You knew what they were doing — the underweighting, the debt — you must have known, the whole village knew, and you let me grow up inside the machine that was grinding you, and no one — not you, not the priest, not one of those brave divers with their brave sticks — no one ever put a scrap of truth where I could find it and understand what I was copying all day long."

"We couldn't reach you!"

"You didn't try!" It burst out of her, loud in the cold room, and she clapped a hand over her own mouth like a girl caught swearing, and above them, faintly, a market porter paused mid-shout. When she spoke again her voice was low and shaking. "You didn't try. Because trying is dangerous, and silence is safe, and I know — God, Mamá, I *know*, I lived it, silence was my trade for twelve years, I was the best silent creature that house ever owned. But don't stand there and tell me it was strategy. Some of it was fear. Some of it was that it was easier to notch a stick than to write your child the truth, and I need — I need to hear you say which parts were which, or I cannot stand beside you in front of that notary, because she'll ask, Inés asks everything, she asks like a knife asks, and if you lie to me you will lie to her, and if you lie to her we all hang."

The lamp flame leaned. Serafina looked at her daughter — this stranger with her face, this clerk with her father's shoulders and her mother's unforgiving clarity — and she did the hardest thing she had done since the day at the signing table.

She stopped weighing her words.

"You're right," she said. "Some of it was fear. Most of it was fear. I will not dress it as strategy, though strategy wore fear's clothing and I let them be confused because confusion was comfortable." She laid her hands flat on the table, palms up, the way she had seen the priest do at consecration, though she believed in no sacrament but the sea's. "The day they took you, I swore I would get you back. That was the vow. Not expose them, not fight them — get you back, whole, alive. And every year that vow got smaller, because you got further away, because the letters stopped coming back, because a woman can carry a vow only so long before it becomes a stone and she sets it down just to walk straight. The sticks started as part of the vow. Evidence, for the day we bought you free and sued them for everything. Then the day kept not coming, and the sticks became something else. A prayer. A habit. A way of saying *this is not right* in the only language they couldn't confiscate." She drew a breath that hurt. "And then, last month, a fish arrives with a letter in its belly telling me my daughter has broken the silence alone, untrained, unprotected, and every year of setting the stone down came due at once. So I came. Not absolved. Not asking absolution. I came because the count ends here, with me, sworn, in my own name, and if my own daughter despises me afterward then that too goes in the record, because Inés Solano wants the whole truth, and the whole truth is that your mother was a coward in useful ways and you deserved better and I will say so under seal."

The cold room held its breath. Ice ticked in its packing.

Dominic reached across the table. Not for an embrace — for Serafina's hands, turning them over, reading the palms like a fortune-teller, the rope scars, the net cuts, the diver's callus along the thumb-edge that Serafina knew her daughter had inherited and then worn smooth with pens instead of lines. She held them a long time.

"Not a coward," Dominic said finally. "A survivor who survived wrong, in ways I also survived wrong. We'll swear it together and let the record sort us out." A ghost of her father's crooked smile. "That's what a ledger is for, isn't it? Not forgiveness. Accuracy."

"Your father would have said the same."

"I know." Quietly. "I remember him."

And Serafina wept then, briefly, without shame, the second storm of the day, shorter and cleaner than the first — and when it passed they bent together over the loose pages, mother's rough hand and daughter's precise one, building the deposition line by line: dates, catches, weights, names, the fictitious Ceniza debt traced backward year by year like following a vein to the heart.

* * *

It was near dawn when footsteps came down the ice-cellar stairs, quick and uneven, and Rivas appeared in the doorway with his escort coat buttoned wrong and his face gray as the ice.

He looked at the three of them — the old woman, the clerk, the broad cook rising with a cleaver she had apparently been holding all night — and he said, in a voice scraped raw:

"They have Tomás. The harbor guard took him an hour ago, off the mole, with papers in his coat. And there's a wagon outside the notary's house that's been parked there since midnight." He swallowed. "We have till morning tide. Maybe less. Whatever we're doing — we have to do it now."

Serafina felt her daughter go still beside her, that diver's stillness, the body conserving everything for the single necessary motion — and she reached over and gripped her daughter's ink-stained hand, hard, the grip she had used on the gunwale of every boat she had ever trusted with her life.

"Then we finish the page," Serafina said.
