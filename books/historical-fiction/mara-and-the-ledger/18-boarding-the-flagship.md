## 18. Boarding the Flagship

The harbor at night smelled of tar and salt and rot, of fish guts and lamp oil, and beneath it all—faint, unmistakable to a daughter of pearl divers—the cold mineral breath of the sea itself. Dominic stood in the shadow of the customs shed with Beatriz's kitchen satchel against her chest and felt her heart beating against it as though it wanted out, as though some part of her already knew what her mind refused to say: that once she stepped onto that gangway, there was no arithmetic that would carry her back.

*La Perla Blanca*. The Verrone flagship rode at anchor beyond the mole, three masts black against the moonlit water, lanterns strung along her rail like a string of false pearls. Twelve years Dominic had copied manifests for this ship. Twelve years she had written *La Perla Blanca* into ledgers with a hand so steady no one had ever looked twice at her. Now the name hung over the water like a verdict.

"You're pale," Beatriz whispered beside her. The cook had come down through the fish market alleys with a basket of provisions on her hip—bread, salt pork, dried figs—and beneath the provisions, wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of the hamper, the deposition. Forty-one pages in Inés Solano's fierce hand, sworn before her seal, witnessed by Tomás Iriarte's memory and Serafina Quintana's scarred thumbprint. The tally-sticks' figures, transcribed and certified. Every underweighted catch from twelve years of theft, every phantom entry of the Ceniza debt, laid out in ink that could not be burned twice.

"I'm always pale," Dominic said. "It's why they never see me."

Beatriz's laugh came out cracked. She set down the hamper and gripped Dominic's hands—her hands smelled of onions and lye soap and the kitchen that had been the only warm room in Dominic's world for twelve years. "Listen to me. The second mate owes me for every meal I ever slipped him past the purser. He'll wave you up without counting heads. The galley steward is my cousin's son; he expects a supernumerary clerk bringing the captain's stores manifest. You are that clerk. You walk like a man who is bored and underpaid and wants nothing but his bunk."

"And if someone asks my name?"

"Give them Tomás's old name—Ferrán. Half the clerks who knew him are dead or dismissed, and the rest won't remember his face." Beatriz squeezed harder. "And Dominic—" Her voice dropped until it was barely more than breath. "You come off that ship before dawn. Promise me. Whatever you leave aboard, you leave it, and you walk back down that gangway, and we burn the whole rotten company down from dry land."

Dominic looked at her friend—at the flour dust still caught in the creases of her knuckles, at the fear she was carrying as steadily as the hamper—and lied, because the truth would have kept them both standing on the dock until the tide turned and everything drowned.

"Before dawn," she said.

* * *

The gangway plank flexed under her boots. That was the first thing—the small give of it, wood and water and nothing solid, the whole ship breathing beneath her like something asleep that might wake angry. She kept her eyes down, her shoulders rounded, her stride just short enough to suggest a man who resented being sent out after dark. Bored. Underpaid. Invisible. She had been rehearsing invisibility since she was nine years old; tonight she only had to perform it one last time.

At the head of the gangway stood a sentry with a musket slung lazy across his chest. Behind him, the second mate—Oyarzun, broad and red-faced, a man who had eaten forty of Beatriz's smuggled meat pies and never once asked where they came from.

"Stores clerk," Dominic said, holding up the satchel and letting the hamper swing visibly from her other hand. "Manifest for the captain. Kitchen sends its compliments." She pitched her voice low, flattened the vowels toward the capital accent, and added the universal currency of clerks everywhere: "They've got me running errands at midnight, so if we could be quick about it."

Oyarzun squinted at her, then at the hamper, then at the figs visible through the basket weave. "Montrose woman still sending her fancy bread?"

"She'd box your ears if she heard you call it fancy."

That got a grin. "Go on, then. Purser's below if he wants a count—but between us, he's drinking with the first mate and nobody's counting anything tonight." He stepped aside, and the deck of *La Perla Blanca* took her weight.

For a moment she simply stood there, in the shadow of the mainmast, and let the strangeness of it move through her. She had written this deck into existence a thousand times—in manifests, in cargo tallies, in the neat columns where pearl lots became revenue and revenue became somebody else's fortune. She had built this ship out of numbers, and now the numbers were going to sink it.

The galley steward—a boy of perhaps sixteen, with Beatriz's same heavy brows—met her at the companionway and took the provisions without a word, exactly as promised. But his eyes lingered on the satchel strap across her chest, and Dominic saw him make the small calculation boys made: not my business, not my neck. He jerked his chin toward the stern. "Captain's cabin's aft. Director's using it till we sail. He's awake—been awake two nights." A pause, and then, quieter, almost kindly: "Whatever you're delivering, don't linger."

"I never linger," Dominic said, and meant it in a way the boy could never understand.

* * *

She found the chartroom first, unattended, lamplit, smelling of ink and varnish and the particular staleness of paper stored too long in damp air. It was the smell of the ledger house. It was the smell of her entire adult life. For a moment the familiarity nearly undid her—how many nights had she bent over paper exactly like this, copying other people's decisions into permanence while her own life stayed unwritten?

She slid the oilcloth packet from the satchel. Forty-one pages. Inés's seal, red wax gone dull in the lamplight. On the top sheet, in the notary's unsoftened hand: *Testimony concerning the systematic falsification of pearl weights and the fabrication of the so-called Ceniza debt, by the Verrone Trading Company, sworn this day...*

Below it, her mother's mark. Not a signature—Serafina had never learned letters—but the pressed print of a thumb scarred by twelve years of knife-work shucking oysters, a mark that said *I was here, I counted, I remember.*

Dominic took out the second packet—the harbormaster's copy, identical, sealed, wrapped in its own oilcloth—and stowed it flat against her ribs beneath the borrowed coat. That one would go ashore again with her, or it would go nowhere. Insurance, Tomás had called it. *A document without protectors is just paper,* he had warned her once, mending nets in the gray light. *But a document in two places—that's a witness that can't be arrested.* One copy for the record aboard this ship, forced on Aurelio Verrone himself, entered where he could not quietly bury it. One copy to the harbormaster's office at first light, should she fail.

She tucked the primary deposition into the canvas dispatch tube marked for the captain's cabin—supernumerary clerks delivered sealed tubes; it was the most ordinary act in the world—and went aft.

* * *

The passage to the stern grew quieter with each step, the ship's noise falling away behind her until she could hear the water sliding along the hull, close enough to touch. Lamplight leaked from under the great cabin door. From within, the scratch of a pen. Aurelio Verrone, awake, working, tidy as ever—she thought of Beatriz's account of the ledger house fire, how neatly the "inspection" had been arranged, how tidy villainy always was when it wore an accountant's cuffs.

Her hand stopped at the door. Trembling—not the fine tremor of fear but the deeper shaking that comes when the body understands before the mind does that a door, once opened, cannot be unopened.

She thought of her father underwater. Of her mother's bargain struck in a company office while nine-year-old Dominic sat unseen on a bench, learning the first lesson: *be nothing, want nothing, be missed by no one.* Of twelve years of blank pages, of copying, of silence practiced until it fit her like skin.

Then she thought of the church wall in Puerto Ceniza, and the tally-sticks pulled from their hiding place, each notch a catch the company stole and a diver who remembered anyway. *The poor keep their own ledgers,* her mother had said, pressing the sticks into her hands. *Now you keep ours.*

Dominic Quintana knocked on the director's door.

The pen stopped scratching.

"Enter."

* * *

The cabin was larger than she had expected, hung with charts and lit by a swinging lamp that threw slow shadows across a writing desk buried in correspondence. Aurelio Verrone sat behind it, silver at his temples, reading spectacles pushed up onto his forehead, and he looked at her with the mild, unhurried attention of a man who has never once in his life been interrupted by anything that mattered.

"The stores manifest?" he said. His voice was pleasant. Everything about him was pleasant. That was the horror of him, Dominic thought—he was not a monster in any shape the stories prepared you for. He was a man whose evil balanced perfectly, column against column, every cruelty entered neatly and carried forward.

"Not precisely, señor." She set the dispatch tube on his desk, upright, square, the way a clerk delivers documents. Then, with hands that had finally gone still—stillness itself a kind of weapon, learned over twelve years—she broke the company seal on the tube and drew out the deposition, laying it before him with Inés's red wax facing upward like a wound.

Verrone looked at the wax. At the pages. Then, slowly, up at her face—and she watched the exact moment recognition arrived, moving through his eyes like a tide finding its level. The invisible clerk. The Quintana girl. The daughter of the village he had chosen, by arithmetic alone, to destroy.

"Well," he said softly. "You have been busy."

*End of Chapter 18.*
