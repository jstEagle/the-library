## 20. Morning Tide

The cell they kept Rivas in smelled of tar and old rope, and the light came through a slit no wider than a hand's breadth, so that he measured the night not by hours but by the slow migration of a pale stripe across the floor stones. When it reached the iron ring bolted to the wall, he knew it was near dawn.

He had not slept. He had done something worse than not sleeping: he had talked.

All night he turned the words over in his mouth like pebbles he could not swallow—the name of the ice cellar, given up under the lantern's swing; the look on Tomás Iriarte's face when they took him, which had been not fear but something quieter and more terrible, the face of a man who had expected this for thirty years and was almost relieved to stop waiting. Rivas had given them the cellar in eleven words. Eleven words, and a man mended nets no more, and a girl with ink-stained fingers was running through the fish market with soldiers at her heels.

He pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw stars that were not stars. *I did what I had to do,* he told himself, and then, because lying to oneself is the one lie that always shows its stitching: *No. I did what I was afraid not to do.*

The lock turned.

Two men came in, and behind them a third—Corporal Enríquez, who commanded the harbor gate watch, a soft-bellied man with a voice like wet gravel and the eyes of an accountant counting other people's losses. He carried a chair, of all things, set it down in the middle of the cell, and sat, arranging himself as though settling in for a sermon.

"You're released," Enríquez said.

Rivas looked up. The stripe of light had reached the ring. Dawn, then. The tide would be turning.

"Released," he repeated.

"The director has no further need of you." Enríquez smiled without warmth, the way a ledger balances without mercy. "You'll be paid through the month. You'll surrender your company token. And you'll be at the mole by seven o'clock—not to work, mind you, but to watch. The *Estrella del Mar* sails on the morning tide, a day early, with the director aboard and every crate of pearls the house could gather before—" he paused delicately— "before certain irregularities are resolved elsewhere."

"Elsewhere," Rivas said.

"The ledger house had a fire last night. Very sad. Old buildings." Enríquez stood, brushed off his coat. "You understand your position, escort? You've been useful once, badly. Be useless now. Quietly. There's a place for quiet men in Verrone's service, even disgraced ones. The director forgets nothing except, when convenient, people."

They left him the chair.

* * *

Rivas walked out through the harbor gate into air so salted it stung his throat, and stood a long time among the morning carts, letting the crowd decide where he went. His feet, traitors or prophets, carried him toward the mole.

The *Estrella del Mar* rode at anchor beyond the breakwater, three-masted, black-hulled, her rails hung with new bunting for the occasion of her early departure. Lighters were already ferrying crates out to her—pearl crates, Rivas guessed, packed in haste overnight while the city slept and one wing of the company's memory burned to ash. On the quarterdeck, small at this distance, moved a figure in a dove-gray coat, hands clasped behind him, surveying the harbor as a man surveys a field he owns down to the last stone.

Aurelio Verrone.

Rivas found a place against a bollard and watched, and the watching was a kind of penance. Around him the port woke into its ordinary music: gulls quarreling over fish guts, a woman crying the price of bread, the groan of winches, children racing along the quay after a dog with a stolen sardine. Ordinary life, all of it resting—though none of these people knew it—on the patience of a girl who had spent twelve years learning to be invisible, and who had chosen, at last, to be seen.

He thought about the escort log. He thought about the false entries in his own neat hand, the days bought for her in Ceniza with ink instead of blood. He had told himself then it was a small treason, forgivable, practical. Now Tomás sat in a cell and the reconstructed ledger was ash or confiscation, and his small treason lay in pieces around him like a broken net that would catch nothing ever again.

*Unless.*

The word arrived quietly, the way dangerous words do.

He looked at the flagship. He looked back toward the town, toward the squat stone building by the customs stairs where the harbormaster's office kept its records—and where, two nights ago, a clerk disguised as a supernumerary had asked him, in the shadow of the gate, to witness a sealed packet placed in the office's strongbox. *Insurance,* she had called it. Dominic Quintana, who spoke so seldom that when she finally raised her voice it sounded like weather changing.

If the ship sailed, the deposition aboard it became a hostage document, a thing Aurelio could lose or burn at sea. But if there was a copy—if the harbormaster held a certified second original, stamped and sworn—then the sailing of the *Estrella del Mar* was not an escape. It was a confession with sails.

The question was whether anyone in authority would dare read it. The magistrate owed his appointment to the trading houses. The port captain drank Verrone wine. The whole colonial machine was oiled with exactly the money that had been scraped off the divers' catches for twelve years, and machines so oiled do not jam easily.

But they jam.

Rivas knew this because he had seen it once, years ago, as a boy—a grain cart whose axle seized on the mountain road above his village, and how forty men with levers and cursing and one old woman's knowledge of exactly where to strike had freed it by sundown. Not strength. Position. You had to know where to strike.

He straightened away from the bollard.

* * *

The harbormaster's office opened at six bells, and Rivas was first through the door, dripping sea fog, his discharge papers in his fist as though they were a weapon. The clerk behind the desk—a young man with a chilblained nose named Ferrer—recognized him and stiffened.

"Escort Rivas. You're not on today's manifest."

"No. I'm here on older business." He laid his hands flat on the counter to keep them from shaking. "Two nights past, a packet was deposited in your strongbox. Sealed. Certified. Left by a supernumerary clerk with the harbormaster's own countersign. I witnessed the deposit. I need to know it still lies where it was put."

Ferrer's chilblains went white. "Strongbox contents are not discussed with—"

"With discharged escorts, no." Rivas leaned closer. Under the smell of tar and fear, he found the old parade-ground steadiness that had gotten him through fifteen years of standing in doorways watching other men's crimes. "Listen to me, boy. In an hour, a ship worth half this colony is going to weigh anchor with the director of the Verrone Trading Company aboard, and by nightfall every man in this office will be asked what he did between dawn and the sailing. Some will say *nothing*. Some will wish to God they'd said something. Which answer do you want carved on you?"

"That packet," Ferrer said slowly, "is not mine to release."

"It isn't yours to keep silent about, either. There's a difference, and it's the width of your neck." Rivas pulled the discharge papers from his coat and set them on the counter. "I'm nobody now. That's my advantage. A nobody can carry paper to the magistrate without anyone thinking to stop him until it's too late. Your part is smaller. Open the box. Let me see it's real. Then let me walk out with it under my arm while you discover, later, with great regret, that you were overpowered."

"I wasn't—"

"You will have been," Rivas said. "By me. I'm bigger than you and I have a reputation for violence I never earned and am suddenly grateful for."

For a long moment Ferrer stared at him. Somewhere outside, six bells finished ringing, and beneath the ringing came another sound, faint but unmistakable: the moan of the flagship's cable being hove short, chain rattling through the hawse-hole like the teeth of some enormous creature being fed.

Ferrer got the key.

* * *

The packet was real. Rivas held it and felt its weight—paper, wax, the notary's ribbon in a green he did not know but understood instinctively to mean law rather than commerce. Inés Solano's seal, though he didn't yet know her name. What he knew was that the wax had been pressed deep and true by someone whose whole grief was in the stamping of it.

"Magistrate's residence is on the hill," Ferrer whispered, wringing his cap. "But he won't receive before nine, and the tide—"

"The tide turns at half past seven. I know." Rivas tucked the packet inside his coat, against his chest, where he could feel it with every heartbeat like a second heart. "That's why I'm not going to the residence."

* * *

Don Baltasar Urrutia, colonial magistrate, took his chocolate each morning at a café on the Plaza de la Pescadería, at a corner table under the awning, because he believed—correctly—that a magistrate seen among the people inspired confidence, and incorrectly, that the people thus observed would tell him anything worth hearing. Rivas had learned this in fifteen years of standing in doorways. Standing in doorways teaches a man everything except how to act.

He crossed the plaza while the market roared around him, and the packet beat against his ribs, and he thought, absurdly, of his mother, dead twenty years, who had once told him: *Mijo, courage is only fear that has found its errand.* He had been eight. He had not understood. He understood now, crossing thirty paves of sunlit stone with his legs made of water.

Don Baltasar looked up from his chocolate with the expression of a man interrupted between sugar and destiny.

"Magistrate." Rivas bowed, then, before propriety could recover itself, set the packet on the table beside the silver pot. "My name is Emilio Rivas. Until yesterday I was an escort in the service of the Verrone Trading Company. This is a certified deposition, sworn and notarized, concerning the theft of pearl revenues over twelve years, the falsification of weights, the invention of a fictitious debt laid upon the village of Puerto Ceniza, and the intended scapegoating of that village's divers for the company's own shortfall—all timed to be buried by the departure of the flagship *Estrella del Mar*, which weighs anchor within the hour."

The plaza noise seemed to arrange itself around his words like water around a dropped stone.

"A copy of this deposition rests aboard the flagship itself, in the hands of the clerk who assembled it," Rivas went on, his voice low and steady and strange to his own ears. "The originals were destroyed last night in a fire at the company ledger house—a fire ordered, we believe, by Director Aurelio Verrone, who is presently aboard that vessel. What remains of the proof stands on three legs: this document, the testimony of divers who have kept tally-sticks of their true catches for twelve years in the wall of their church, and the memory of a dismissed archivist whom your officers currently hold in the company's cells."

Don Baltasar had gone very still. His eyes moved from the packet to Rivas's face and back. "Who wrote this?"

"A clerk. A woman. Dominic Quintana." Saying her name aloud felt like setting down a weight he had carried so long he had mistaken it for his spine. "She was indentured to the company at nine years old. She copied their letters for twelve years without anyone once looking at her. She saw her own family named as criminals in a sealed letter, and she broke the only silence keeping her safe." Rivas's throat tightened. "I helped her and then I betrayed her, under questioning, and a good man was taken because of it. So understand me, Magistrate—I'm not here as an honest man. I'm here as a guilty one trying to make the guilt buy something before it expires."

Silence. A gull screamed somewhere above the awning. Don Baltasar picked up the packet, weighed it, turned it, read the notary's seal, and something shifted in his face—the shift of a man realizing that the paper in his hands, whatever else it was, was *real*, certified, lawful, and therefore impossible to unsee.

"If I act on this," the magistrate said quietly, "and it proves exaggerated, I destroy myself. Verrone's friends sit on the audiencia."

"And if you don't act," Rivas said, "the ship sails, the evidence goes to sea, the divers hang for a debt invented in an office, and you spend the rest of your career knowing you had it in your hands." He nodded at the packet. "You're holding twelve years of stolen weights, Don Baltasar. Nobody exaggerates arithmetic."

The magistrate looked out across the plaza—at the market, the gulls, the harbor glittering beyond the roofs—and Rivas watched him perform the oldest human calculation, the one every man in power performs sooner or later: *which way do I want history to remember I was facing when it happened?*

Then Don Baltasar stood, scattering his chocolate money, and his voice cracked across the plaza like a whip.

"Ferrer! From the harbormaster's office—run to the port captain. Wake him if he's abed. Tell him the magistrate orders the *Estrella del Mar* held at anchor pending investigation, under the colonial revenue act, and if any sail is hoisted before my writ arrives, I will charge every officer on that deck with obstruction." He was already moving, robe flying, clerks scattering before him like startled birds. "And someone bring me paper. Now. *Now!*"

* * *

Rivas ran too.

He ran not uphill with the magistrates but downhill, toward the water, because some animal part of him refused to learn the outcome from a distance. Through the fish market, past the ice cellar whose door he had sold, down the customs stairs two at a time, onto the mole where the morning crowd had begun to gather and murmur, sensing theater. Beyond the breakwater the *Estrella del Mar* lay with her topsails loosed but unfilled, and around her, absurdly, anticlimactically, a port launch was pulling away from her side carrying a single officer with a white envelope held high like a lantern.

On the flagship's quarterdeck, the figure in the dove-gray coat had stopped pacing. Even at this distance Rivas fancied he could see the moment the order reached Aurelio Verrone—the slight tilt of the head, the hands coming together behind the back again, the stillness of a man reading a column of figures that refuses to balance.

The ship did not sail.

The tide turned at half past seven, right on its ancient schedule, sliding green and gold through the harbor mouth, and the great ship swung gently on her cable to meet it, bowsprit pointing at the open sea, pointing at freedom, pointing at everything twelve years of stolen weight had purchased—and stayed. Held. A machine of empire, of ledgers and bribes and burned buildings and drowned villages' reputations, jammed at anchor by a clerk's handwriting, a notary's wax, a magistrate's vanity and courage in equal parts, and the eleven-word cowardice of a discharged escort converted, at the last possible bell, into something that might, with luck and paper, be called testimony.

Rivas stood on the mole with the spray hitting his face and his legs finally giving out, and he sat down hard on the wet stone among fishermen and urchins and women with market baskets, and he laughed, or wept, or both, he couldn't have said which.

Nearby, an old woman selling oranges looked at him with the frank appraisal of her trade. "Bad news?" she asked.

"No," Rivas said, wiping his face. "For the first time in fifteen years—good news."

* * *

They took him back into custody by noon, of course. Verrone was not a man who accepted defeat quietly, and his first act, from his held ship, was to demand the arrest of everyone connected to the deposition—Rivas included, on charges he didn't bother to make coherent. Rivas went with the soldiers peaceably, almost gratefully. A cell, now, was a small price, and cells, he had learned, were temporary. Paper endured.

But as they walked him up from the mole, past the harbor gate where he had stood guard through so many indifferent years, a runner overtook the escort—a breathless boy with Ferrer's chilblained face written all over his errand—and thrust a folded note into Rivas's bound hands before the soldiers could think to stop it.

The note was unsigned, in a cramped clerk's hand he recognized from a hundred forged escort logs:

*Tomás alive. They've moved him to the flagship—Verrone wants him where the fire can't reach. She knows. She's already going aboard.*

Rivas closed his eyes. Below them in the roadstead, the *Estrella del Mar* gleamed at her anchors, black hull bright with morning, and somewhere on her decks a girl disguised as a supernumerary clerk was walking toward the captain's cabin with twelve broken years in her arms—and now, somewhere below decks, the one man who could corroborate every figure in her deposition, locked in the enemy's floating fortress.

The tide had been stopped. The next move belonged to whoever was brave enough to board.
