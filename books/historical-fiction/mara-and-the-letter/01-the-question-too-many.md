## 1. The Question Too Many

The census man came on a Tuesday, which Coral Ashgrove thought was fitting. Tuesdays were the days nothing happened — the days the island breathed between its Sundays and its market mornings, the days she could scrub the flagstones and count the coal and pretend that a life could be made entirely of small, orderly things. He came up the cliff path at half past ten, when the mist had burned off to a thin silver and the sea below was the color of hammered tin, and she saw him from the lantern room window while she was polishing the brass, and her hands went still on the cloth.

She had known he was coming. The harbormaster had sent word a fortnight past — *census taker from the mainland, will call at every dwelling, nothing to fret over* — and Coral had done nothing to fret over. She had swept the cottage, aired the beds, set the table with the good cloth she kept for funerals and officials. She had told Wren to wear her clean pinafore and to answer politely and to say nothing she wasn't asked. She had gone down to the cellar door, stood before it with her hand on the iron latch, and said, in a voice low enough that only the dark could hear, *Tobias. Tuesday. A man from the mainland will be in the house. You'll hear his voice through the floor. You'll make no sound.*

And from the dark had come that voice she knew better than her own grief, dry as old rope: *Seven years I've kept quiet for you, Coral. I can keep quiet one Tuesday.*

So she was ready. She had told herself she was ready all morning, right up until the moment she saw him climbing the path with his leather satchel and his bowler hat and his careful, counting stride, and her hands stopped on the brass, and her heart performed a small, treacherous stumble, like a clock that has caught on its own spring.

*You are ready*, she told herself. *You have been ready for seven years.*

She wiped her hands, set down the cloth, and went down to open the door before he could knock.

---

He was younger than she expected. That was the first thing. She had imagined some grey functionary, a man made of ink and rubber stamps, and instead the man on her doorstep was perhaps five-and-thirty, with a long, serious face and spectacles that had fogged in the sea air, and eyes — she noticed this at once, the way one notices a rip in a familiar garment — eyes that *looked*. Not the way islanders looked, quick and sideways, taking in the weather and the mood and the lie all in one glance and letting it pass. He looked the way a man looks at a page he intends to copy out exactly.

"Mrs. Ashgrove." He removed his hat, and she saw the band of sweat on the brim from the climb, and thought absurdly: *he is not used to hills.* "Edmund Vane. I've the census of the realm to take. I'm told yours is the last dwelling on my list — the light."

"You've saved the steepest for last, Mr. Vane."

"I'm told it's also the highest." A small, dry smile, there and gone. "May I come in? I'll not keep you above a quarter hour. Most of the questions are the same as every year — names, ages, trades. The form does the work. I only hold the pen."

*The form does the work.* She stood aside and let him in, and thought: *no. The form does not do the work. The form has never once done the work. The work is done by the woman standing at the door deciding, in the space of one breath, what the form will be allowed to know.*

---

The cottage was small and fiercely clean. He sat where she bade him, at the deal table by the window, and set out his papers with a precision she found herself watching despite everything — the form squared to the table's edge, the inkwell set to the right of it, the pen laid parallel like a surgeon's instrument. He had a way of arranging things that made the whole room feel briefly audited.

Wren was at the hearth, ostensibly mending a net, actually listening with her whole spine. Coral caught her daughter's eye and held it for a half-second, a look that meant *polite, quiet, nothing you aren't asked*, and Wren's chin dipped the smallest degree, and she bent to her net.

"Now, then." He dipped the pen. "Name of the head of the household?"

"Coral Ashgrove. Widow."

"Occupation?"

"Keeper of the light. I hold the appointment since my husband's death. The Board of Trade will confirm it."

"I don't doubt it." He wrote, the nib scratching in the quiet. "The light's a fine thing. I watched it from the inn last night — that long sweep every few seconds. There's a comfort in it, I imagine. Knowing something so steady is up here in the dark."

"There is," Coral said. "For me and for every sailor who'd rather not die on my rocks."

He glanced up at that, briefly, and she could not read what passed behind the spectacles. "And the others in the dwelling? Names and ages, if you please."

Here it was, then. The question she had answered every year for six years without a tremor, in the years when the answer had been true and simple: *myself, my daughter, and my sister-in-law's boy until he was apprenticed off to a Falmouth chandler, and God keep him gone.* The question that was only arithmetic. She had the answer ready. She had had it ready since before the kettle boiled.

"Myself," she said. "Forty-one. My daughter, Wren Ashgrove, eleven years come Midsummer. And—"

And the beat came. She would know afterward, replaying it in the small hours as she replayed everything, exactly where it fell — in the pause between *Wren* and the name she had prepared, the pause no longer than the space a wave takes to gather itself before it breaks. One breath. One and a half. In that breath the fire ticked, and Wren's needle stopped in the netting, and somewhere below the floor — below the floor, below the cellar, below the cellar the old smugglers' tunnel ran down toward the sea like a buried vein — somewhere in all that darkness a man who had not made a sound in two years was, she knew with a certainty that felt like falling, holding his breath too.

"—my late husband's brother," she said. "Tobias Ashgrove. Forty-four. He keeps to himself. He helps with the light."

The pen moved. The nib scratched. *Tobias Ashgrove, 44, brother, occupation: assistant keeper.*

She had done it. The word was on the form, in the census of the realm, in the neat and terrible permanence of official ink: Tobias Ashgrove, dwelling at the light, alive, accounted for. And the truth — that there was no Tobias Ashgrove in any register of the living, that the man below her floor had drowned on paper seven years ago along with three other men in a boat that never came home, that his name had been read out at a memorial service in the harbor chapel while he sat in her cellar eating bread she'd carried down in a covered basket — that truth sat in the room with them now, patient as the sea, wearing the shape of her own steady voice.

"Assistant keeper," Edmund Vane repeated. He wrote something else — a small note in the margin, she thought, though she could not see it from where she stood. "That must be a help to you. A woman alone can't work a light this size without help."

"No," she agreed. "She can't."

"And your late husband? He was keeper before you?"

"He was."

"Lost at sea, I'm told." He said it gently, the way mainland men did, the way that was meant as kindness and landed as an audit. "The wreck of 'eighty-six. A hard winter."

"The hardest," Coral said, and her voice did not change at all, and she marveled at it from a small distance, at the way grief and guilt and seven years of practice could be poured into four words and come out sounding like weather talk. "Four men. The island still doesn't talk about it."

"No," he said, and dipped his pen again. "I noticed that. It doesn't."

---

The rest went as she had known it would. Ages and births and years at the present address; Wren's schooling (*instructed at home by her mother, scripture and figures*); the state of the dwelling (*stone, slate, sound*). He asked Wren two questions directly — her age, and whether she could read — and Wren answered in her careful company voice, the one that made her sound five years younger and twice as obedient, and Coral's heart ached with pride and fear in equal measure, because the child was good, the child was *so good* at the silence, and no eleven-year-old should have to be.

Then he closed the form, blotted it, squared it with his inkwell, and began — with the unhurried thoroughness of a man packing up after a job well done — to gather his things. And it was in the gathering, in the moment when the official part of him had already stood down and only the man remained, that his eyes went past her shoulder to the sink in the corner, where the morning's washing stood drying on the rack.

Four bowls.

She saw him see them. She saw the counting happen behind the spectacles — a flick of the eyes, quick and involuntary as a blink, the reflex of a man who counted things the way other men breathed. One, two, three, four. And then the flick of the eyes back to his form, where three names stood written in his careful hand, and Coral watched — with the peculiar clarity of a woman watching her own house begin to fill with water — as the arithmetic refused to close.

He said nothing. That was the worst of it. He put on his hat, and thanked her for her time, and said the forms would go to the registrar at Penzance by the week's end, and that she had been most helpful, and his voice was exactly as courteous and exactly as flat as it had been on his arrival. He paused at the door with his hand on the latch, and looked back once — not at her, she thought, but at the room itself, the way a surveyor looks at ground he intends to measure again — and said:

"Fair weather for the crossing back, at least. The innkeeper tells me the storms will start soon. They always do, this time of year."

"Sooner every year," Coral said.

"Then I'll wish you a calm season, Mrs. Ashgrove. You and yours."

*You and yours.* The words hung in the doorway after him, ordinary as salt, and she stood in the middle of her fiercely clean kitchen and listened to his footsteps go down the cliff path, unhurried, counting.

Wren looked up from her net. "Mama? Was that all right? Did I do it proper?"

"You did it perfect, my love." Coral crossed to the sink, and took down the fourth bowl — the chipped brown one, the one that had lived in the cellar for two years, the one she had forgotten, *forgotten*, in the simple morning arithmetic of washing up — and she held it a moment, and then, because the house had ears now and she knew it, she said lightly, "This one's mine, I've been using it for the lamp grease. Silly of me to leave it out."

Wren's eyes went to the bowl, and then to her mother, and something moved behind them — quick, wordless, eleven years old and already fluent in the family language of things not said. "Yes, Mama," she said, and went back to her net.

Coral set the bowl on its shelf, and stood with her hand on it, and through the floorboards, faint as a pulse, she heard the sound she had been listening for all morning: nothing. The cellar holding its breath. Tobias keeping his Tuesday's silence, as promised, as always.

*It's done*, she told herself. *The name is on the form. He wrote it down. Whatever he counted in that sink, the paper says what the paper says.*

But she had watched his eyes. She had seen a man who counted things, and she had seen the count come out wrong, and she had seen him say nothing about it — and in seven years of keeping silences, Coral Ashgrove had learned the one lesson the sea taught best: it is never the wave you see that takes you. It is the one still gathering, out past the light, where the water looks calm.

She went up to the lantern room and began to polish the brass, and did not stop until her arms ached and the sun went down.
