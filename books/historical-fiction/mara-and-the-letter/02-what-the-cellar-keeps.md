## 2. What the Cellar Keeps

The bread knife had a loose handle.

Coral noticed it the way she noticed everything now — in the flat, inventorying way of a woman who had spent two years pretending her house held only what it was supposed to hold. She turned the knife over under the lamp, thumbed the rivet that had worked itself proud of the horn, and set it on the sill to be mended tomorrow, when the light was better and the census man was gone and the island could go back to being the shape of its own lie.

Below her feet — three floors down, past the oil store, past the coal chute, behind a door she had painted the same grey as the wall around it so the eye slid off it like rain off slate — someone was awake. She couldn't have said how she knew. There was no sound. There was never any sound. But the house felt different when Tobias was awake in the dark, the way a tide feels different when there's a moon behind cloud: nothing you could point to, only a pull you'd learned to read or drown by.

She finished the washing-up. Four bowls this time, because the census man had taken tea with them, and one bowl too many had already been seen drying by the sink once today. She dried each one slowly, thoroughly, listening with her whole spine.

Then she took the loaf from the crock, cut four slices thick as her thumb, laid cheese between two of them, and went down.

* * *

The stairs below the oil store were stone, worn hollow in the middle by forty years of feet that had mostly belonged to her husband. Jonah had used them every day of their marriage, first to fetch lamp oil, later — in those last eighteen months she still could not think about without her hands going cold — for other comings and goings at other hours. He had told her it was stores. He had told her it was nothing. And she had believed him, because she had loved him, and because believing was easier than counting the boats that came to the north cove on nights when no honest boat came anywhere.

That was the thing about the cellar. It didn't keep Tobias. It kept everything else.

At the bottom, she knocked: twice, pause, once. The old signal, Jonah's signal, though Jonah had never used it for anything so innocent as delivering supper.

The bolt drew back and the door opened on darkness that smelled of paraffin, damp stone, and tobacco he wasn't allowed to smoke but smoked anyway, carefully, over a dish of sand when the wind wasn't strong enough to carry the smell up through the floorboards.

"You've brought the good cheese," said the dark.

"I've brought whatever was in the crock." She set the plate on the shelf just inside, where his hand found it without needing light. "There was a census man here today."

A long quiet. Then, dryly, from the black: "I heard. You shouted his name loud enough through the window. Edmund Vane." A pause, and the sound of him sitting back against something — the old sea chest, probably, the one that had come over with their mother from Penzance before either of them could walk. "Vane. Like weathervane."

"He's from the mainland. Truro office. He'll lodge at the inn a few days while he does the outlying farms."

"And did you tell him how many souls dwell at the Ashgrove Light?"

Coral leaned her shoulder against the doorframe. Down here the storm sounds came through differently — not the whistle of wind around the tower but a deep, tidal breathing, the sea moving in the rock beneath the rock. In winter, in the worst gales, Tobias said he could feel the whole island groaning around him like a ship. She tried not to imagine what that was like. Two years, and she had never once asked him.

"Four," she said.

Another silence, longer than the first. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — flatter, careful, the voice of a man handling something with a broken edge.

"Four," he repeated. "And which four would those be?"

"You know which four."

"I'd like to hear you say it."

"Tobias—"

"No." The word cracked across the dark like a whip-crack, then softened immediately, the way he always softened, the way he'd been softening himself into smaller and smaller rooms for two years. "No, I'm sorry. Come in where I can see your face, or don't come in at all. I'm tired of talking to the door."

She stepped inside. Her eyes had adjusted enough now to make him out: a shape rising from the chest, tall even stooped, with their mother's same dark hair gone early grey at the temples and Jonah's same heavy brow — the family face, hers included, worn by three different people into three different expressions. His was the one that looked like it was apologizing.

He lit the stub of candle he was permitted himself after full dark, cupping the flame, and in its small circle his face appeared the way faces do in cellars — older than it was, carved deeper than it should be. Thirty-four years old. He looked fifty.

"Say it," he said quietly.

"Jonah," she said. "Me. Wren. And you. But the paper says four, and the fourth name is Jonah's brother, fisherman, drowned off the Skerries in the winter of '91." She met his eyes. "That's what the paper says. That's what I told him."

Tobias nodded slowly. Then he laughed — one breath of it, no humor in it at all — and sat back down.

"Drowned off the Skerries," he murmured. "You know, I can almost feel it. Cold water. That's a fine grave they gave me. Better than the one I earned."

"Don't talk like that."

"Why not? It's true." He picked up the plate, turned it in his hands without eating. "Two years, Coral. Two years I've been dead, and do you know what I've decided being dead means? It means my sister-in-law lies for me to government men. It means an eleven-year-old girl prays at night for her Uncle Toby's soul because everyone's told her he's at the bottom of the sea. It means—" His jaw worked. "It means my niece thinks I'm a ghost story."

"She doesn't pray for you."

"What?"

"She prays *to* you." Coral sat down on the upturned crate by the wall, the one she always sat on, the visitor's seat in a room with no other furniture worth the name. "Wren talks to you. At night, when she thinks no one hears. She tells you things. What she learned at her letters. What Marta Pengelly said about her. Whether the hens are laying." She watched that land on him, watched his face do something complicated and painful. "So you're not a ghost story to her, Tobias. You're a ghost she trusts. Which is more than most living men get."

He stared at her. The candle flame stood very straight between them, the air down here too still to bend it.

"She talks to me," he said. Not a question. Something rawer than a question.

"Every night. So eat your cheese and stop feeling sorry for yourself, because you're the best-listened-to dead man in Cornwall."

* * *

Later — after the plate was empty, after the candle had burned down to a thumb's width, after the conversation had circled twice around the census man and twice around nothing at all, the way their conversations did, avoiding the center of the maze the way a tongue avoids a sore tooth — she asked him the question she asked perhaps once a season, and always regretted asking.

"Do you remember the wreck?"

The cellar went very quiet. Even the sea seemed to hold off a moment, the great breathing pause between waves.

"No," Tobias said finally.

"That's not what you said last spring."

"Last spring I was lying."

"Tobias—"

"Of course I remember it." His voice came out low and level, which was worse than shouting; she had learned that much about him since the cellar. "I remember all of it. I remember the Merrow going out with a cargo she had no papers for, and I remember Jonah laughing on the quay because the fog was coming in and fog meant money. I remember the wind turning. I remember Halloran saying we should stand off, wait for morning, and Jonah saying the tide wouldn't wait and neither would the revenue sloop." He was looking at the candle flame, not at her. "I remember the rocks sounding like the end of the world. And I remember the water, Coral. I remember thinking, as I went under the second time, that I deserved it. And I remember being angry at God when I didn't."

She said nothing. There was nothing she hadn't said. Grief has its own tides and you cannot argue them down.

"Four men drowned," Tobias said. "Halloran. The Pellow brothers. And Jonah." His hand closed slowly around the empty plate. "And the man who put them all in that boat — the man who owned the cargo, who set the night, who knew the weather better than any of us and sent them out anyway because his debts mattered more than their lives — that man slept soundly in his own bed and got up in the morning and helped organize the search parties. They gave him credit for it. People still thank him for it."

"I know."

"You don't know. You keep the light. You don't sit in the harbor office buying rounds for men whose brothers I helped drown." He set the plate down with exaggerated care, the care of a man keeping himself from throwing it. "I should have gone to the authorities that first week. While the inquiry was still open. I should have walked into Penzance and given them everything — names, dates, the cove, the ledgers Jonah hid. Instead I let you hide me. Because I was afraid. Because being dead was easier than being hanged."

"They'd have hanged you and called the ledger evidence forged," Coral said. "The inquiry closed with four names on the drowned list and no questions asked, and do you know why? Because the one man who could have answered questions wanted exactly that. No questions. Ever." She stood, brushing off her skirt, wanting to be upright, wanting to be above him for once, wanting to be the older one though he had two years on her. "You didn't fail those men by hiding, Tobias. You failed them by sailing. Hiding is just what the failing costs afterward."

He looked up at her, and for a moment he looked so like Jonah that her chest seized — same brow, same way of taking blame onto himself like a coat, and look where that coat had carried Jonah, down to the cold water with three other men in it.

"I'm sorry," he said. "About the cheese remark. About all of it. You shouldn't be down here carrying this."

"I've carried heavier." It was almost true. "Lock your bolt. And Tobias — if the census man comes back, if he asks anything, anything at all—"

"I know. I'm not here."

"You're not here," she agreed, "and you never were."

* * *

She climbed the stairs slowly, letting her eyes adjust at the top before she opened the door into the kitchen, because the lamplight after the cellar's dark struck like a blow.

Wren was supposed to be asleep. Wren was eleven, and eleven-year-olds were supposed to be asleep, and for seven years — no, for two years, since the cellar had acquired its tenant — Coral had built her evenings on that supposition the way a bridge is built on pilings you never examine too closely.

Wren was sitting on the bottom stair in her nightgown, knees drawn up, hair a pale tangle, looking at her mother with wide, unchildlike steadiness.

"You were down there a long time," Wren said.

"The oil store needed checking. We go through more in autumn."

"Mm." Wren's gaze drifted, briefly, toward the grey door that was the same grey as the wall. Then back. "Mama, is Uncle Toby cold, where he is?"

The floor did not open. The house did not fall. Only Coral's heart, which had learned to survive such blows by absorbing them silently, the way the tower absorbs the sea.

"Cold water is quick, love," she managed. "He wouldn't have suffered."

Wren nodded, accepting this with terrible seriousness. Then she stood, kissed her mother's cheek — smelling of soap and childhood and something else, something faintly of lamp oil, though the child hadn't been near the lantern room — and climbed the stairs to bed without another word.

Coral stood alone in the kitchen a long moment.

Then she saw it. On the third step up, half-hidden in the shadow of the banister, where a small hand might have left it on the way down earlier and meant to retrieve it on the way back:

A heel of bread. Wrapped in a scrap of cloth.

She picked it up. It was stale — left there yesterday, or the day before. She stood holding it, hearing the sea breathe under the house, hearing her daughter's footsteps overhead crossing to bed, hearing, far below, the faint unmistakable sound of a bolt drawing shut.

Her daughter was feeding the ghost.

Coral put the bread in her apron pocket, banked the fire, and blew out the lamp — and stood a moment longer in the dark, doing the only arithmetic that had ever truly frightened her: one secret becoming two, becoming three, multiplying in the walls of her house like damp, until the day would come when the whole structure gave way at once, and there would be no choosing which silence to save.

Out past the light, where the water looked calm, something was gathering.
