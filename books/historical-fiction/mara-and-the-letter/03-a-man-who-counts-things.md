## 3. A Man Who Counts Things

Edmund Vane had been counting things since he was six years old.

It began with the gravestones in his father's parish yard — one hundred and fourteen of them, not counting the three whose inscriptions the weather had eaten. His father had laughed at him for it, a warm, absent laugh, the kind a man gives a child who is amusing himself while the adults work. But Edmund had not been amusing himself. He had been doing what came to him as naturally as breathing: putting the world into columns so that it would hold still.

Thirty years later, the habit had made his career and unmade everything else. He was, by the Treasury's own assessment, the most accurate census taker in the western district. He was also forty-one years old, unmarried, and known in every inn from Penzance to Padstow as *that quiet fellow with the ledger*. Accuracy was a fine quality in a man you only needed once every ten years. It was a poor quality in a friend. Edmund had never quite worked out how to be anything else, and so he had stopped trying, and told himself each spring, when the forms arrived in their oilcloth wrapping smelling of government ink and other people's lives, that this year he wanted nothing more than the work itself.

He almost believed it.

* * *

The inn on Carrick Isle had eleven rooms, though its landlady swore thirteen and her late husband's will said twelve, and Edmund had spent a pleasant quarter hour on his first evening reconciling the discrepancy until she'd asked him, gently, if he was quite well. He took the corner room facing the harbor because it had a desk — a real desk, scarred oak, bolted to the floor against storms — and because from its window he could see the lighthouse standing off on its black promontory like a finger raised in permanent admonition.

The lamp there turned all night. He had watched it the evening he arrived, its beam sweeping the water in long patient strokes, and found the rhythm oddly soothing. A light that never slept. A light that counted nothing, needed nothing, asked nothing. He envied it in a way he could not have explained to anyone without embarrassment.

Now, on his second night on the island, he sat at that desk with the day's forms spread before him and a single candle burning down beside an inkwell shaped like a cannonball. Rain had begun around dusk, the first real rain of his visit, ticking against the glass like a clerk tapping his pen. Below, in the inn's low-beamed common room, the fishermen were drinking loudly and arguing about mackerel prices, and their noise rose through the floorboards in a warm indistinct rumble that Edmund found, if he was honest, comforting. Other people's company was best enjoyed through a floor.

He worked left to right, household by household, the way he always did. The Pellow family at Trenoweth Farm: seven souls, five children under ten, one grandfather who was, according to young Mrs. Pellow, "mostly gone but still eats." The two families sharing the net loft: nine souls, occupations recorded with a frankness Edmund appreciated — fisherman, fisherman, fisherman's wife, fisherman, *none*, fisherman, fisherman, fisherman, and a baby entered simply as "infant, loud." The schoolmistress, lodging alone above the chapel schoolroom: one soul, occupation schoolmistress, condition as to marriage — widow, which Edmund noted without comment because noting things without comment was the whole of his profession.

And then the lighthouse.

Coral Ashgrove. Widow. Keeper of the light these seven years, which the Trinity House man in Falmouth had confirmed by letter with something like reverence — *Mrs. Ashgrove has kept the station without serious fault since her husband's drowning; I would call her the most reliable keeper on the coast, sir, and she is a woman, which I mention only because you will not believe it otherwise.* Edmund believed it. He had met her that afternoon.

She had come down from the cliff path with her daughter trailing behind like a small shadow, and she had answered his questions with a courtesy so precise it had felt less like conversation than like fencing. Yes, she was Coral Ashgrove. Yes, widowed, seven years this autumn. Yes, the child was Wren, aged eleven, born on the island. Occupation — keeper. Condition as to marriage — widow. And then the question that mattered more than any other on the form, the question the whole census existed to answer:

How many souls dwell at your house?

"Four," she had said.

Edmund looked at the word now, written in his own hand in his own careful script, and felt the thing he always felt when a number was wrong — a faint wrongness behind his sternum, like a picture hung a half-inch crooked. Because the number was wrong. Not obviously. Not in any way the London men who checked his totals would ever catch. But he had watched her say it, and he had counted, and Mrs. Ashgrove had hesitated.

One beat too long.

It was such a small thing. Most people hesitated over the census; they miscounted children, forgot lodgers, argued about whether a grandmother living half the year with each of two daughters should be entered twice or once or one-and-a-half. Hesitation was the salt of his profession. But there were hesitations and there were hesitations, and hers had been of a particular kind he recognized the way a physician recognizes a fever — the pause of a woman reaching for a number she had rehearsed, and finding it just slightly too heavy to lift smoothly.

Three souls lived at the lighthouse. She had said four.

Or — and here Edmund set down his pen and pressed his fingertips together and stared at the candle flame until it doubled — four souls lived at the lighthouse, and she had said four, and the hesitation meant something else entirely.

He did not know which. That was the trouble. That was the itch beneath his skin.

Because he had done what he always did after leaving a household: he had stood at a distance and counted what could be counted. Windows lit. Laundry lines. And there had been laundry — he remembered it now, turning the memory over like a coin to see both faces — a line strung along the sheltered side of the tower, and on it, shirts. Not a woman's clothing, and not a child's. Men's shirts, coarse linen, two of them, and trousers pegged out beside them, and the garments had been worn, mended at the knees, sized for a grown man.

Widows did not generally wash and mend a grown man's clothes.

Unless the man was a son — but Wren was the only child, a girl, eleven years, too tall perhaps for childhood but not for a man's shirt, no, that didn't serve. Unless the clothes were the dead husband's, kept and washed out of grief, but grief did not usually mend trousers at the knee, and those patches had been fresh, sewn within the season. Unless —

Unless there was someone in that house the census was not meant to find.

* * *

Edmund got up and walked to the window. The rain had thickened; the harbor lights below had gone soft and smeared, and beyond the breakwater the lighthouse beam swung round and round, steady as a heartbeat, laying its white stripe across the sea and the rain and the dark and then taking them all back again.

He thought about accuracy. He thought about it the way other men thought about God — not constantly, but always underneath, the ground everything else stood on.

There was a version of himself, younger, that would have said: *it is not my business.* The census counted heads; it did not judge them. If a woman sheltered someone off the books, that was between her and the law, and his job ended at the sum at the bottom of the column. He had told himself versions of this before. In Bodmin, in '81, there had been a household where a girl of sixteen was entered as a servant but wore a wedding ring turned inward, and Edmund had written *servant* in his neat hand and walked away and thought about her for eleven years.

Accuracy, he had come to understand, was not the same as innocence. A true number could still be a betrayal. A false one could still be a kindness. The forms did not care which they carried; they were only paper. It was the hand that filled them that decided what the paper meant.

But that was philosophy, and philosophy did not pay his salary, and tomorrow morning he had six more households on the east side of the island and a ferry back to the mainland on Thursday if the weather held. The sensible thing — the professional thing — was to accept four as an honest error, note it in the margin, correct it at the final review, and let Mrs. Ashgrove keep whatever arithmetic she preferred.

He returned to the desk. He picked up the pen. He wrote, in the margin beside her entry, a small notation: *verify.*

Then he sat looking at it for a long time.

Verify. As if it were a routine matter. As if his hand hadn't trembled slightly making the mark. Edmund Vane was a man who knew his own handwriting better than his own face, and he knew what that little word cost him. Because verification meant returning. It meant climbing the cliff path again and knocking on that door again and asking her again — *how many souls dwell at your house?* — and watching her face while she lied to him a second time, or didn't.

And some part of him, a part he examined with the same clinical attention he gave everything else, wanted very much to go back.

Not for the number. He was nearly sure it was not for the number.

When she had answered his questions that afternoon, standing in her doorway with the wind pulling at her grey shawl, there had been a moment — a moment so brief he might have invented it — when their eyes met and he saw, clearly, unmistakably, a woman performing calm the way he performed indifference. Two people, standing three feet apart on a doorstep, each running an act for the other's benefit, each seeing straight through to the machinery underneath. He had felt *seen*. He had not been seen, properly seen, in longer than he cared to count.

That was dangerous. He catalogued the danger and filed it and told himself, as he told himself every time: it is only accuracy you want. Only the satisfaction of a corrected column, a reconciled total, a world that adds up. You are a man who counts things. Counting is all you have ever had, and all anyone has ever needed you for, and if you want to climb a cliff path to ask a lonely woman a question she has already answered, well — that is diligence, Mr. Vane. That is thoroughness. There is a difference between thoroughness and longing, and you are a man who knows the difference, because knowing differences is what you do.

Below, the fishermen's argument about mackerel collapsed into laughter. Someone began a song. The candle guttered and steadied.

* * *

He finished the east-side households first — that was discipline — but his mind kept drifting back up the cliff path, and by the time he reached the last form, the schoolteacher's, he had already composed and discarded three ways of phrasing a follow-up question. *I find a small discrepancy in my records.* No — accusatory. *In reviewing my returns—* No — bureaucratic, and worse, it invited her to be bureaucratic back, and Mrs. Ashgrove struck him as entirely capable of out-bureaucrating a government man before breakfast.

He was still drafting when he noticed the smell.

It came faintly under the door and up through the floorboards — woodsmoke and wet wool and, beneath it, something richer. Bread. The landlady baked on Wednesdays; tonight was Tuesday. Which meant someone else on the island was baking at ten o'clock at night, and baking enough that the smell of it reached across half the village.

He leaned out the window. The rain had eased to a drizzle, and the village lay below him in its scatter of lamplit windows, and he counted them without meaning to — he always counted them — and stopped at the far edge of his sightline, where the cliff rose black against the blacker sky.

At the top of the cliff, at the lighthouse, a window glowed. Not the lantern room — that burned its great wheeling beam overhead, indifferent. This was lower down, a small yellow square in the tower's flank. A kitchen window, he supposed. A kitchen window, lit at ten at night, in a house where a woman baked bread for—

For how many?

He stood there a long while, cold air moving over his face, the beam sweeping the sea below him, and let himself do the thing he was best at in all the world. He counted what he knew. One widow. One daughter. Four souls declared. Men's washing on the line. Bread for more than three. A hesitation one beat too long.

And then he counted what he didn't know, which was everything else, and understood that the difference between the two lists was the whole shape of whatever was hidden inside that white tower on the point — and that the government had sent him here to close exactly that kind of gap, and that sooner or later, gaps closed themselves, whether he held the needle or not.

Edmund went back to the desk, dipped his pen, and drew a line through his marginal note. Beside it, in smaller script, he wrote a new one, and this one did not tremble, because by now the decision had hardened into something smooth and cool and official-looking, the way decisions did when he dressed them in procedure:

*Return to Ashgrove household. Confirm count. Report discrepancies per schedule.*

Per schedule. As if the schedule demanded it. As if his heart weren't going like a boy's.

He blew out the candle. In the dark, the beam came round and round, and he lay listening to the rain and thinking, despite everything, of the look on her face — that flicker of recognition, woman to counter, keeper to keeper — and he told himself one last time, the way a man locks a door he knows he'll open again before morning:

Only accuracy. It is only accuracy I want.

Somewhere below, faint through the floor, the fishermen sang a song about drowned men coming home with the tide, and Edmund Vane fell asleep counting verses, and lost track somewhere past twenty.
