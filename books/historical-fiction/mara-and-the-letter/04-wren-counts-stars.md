## 4. Wren Counts Stars

There were two hundred and six stars over the island that night, and Wren Ashgrove had counted every one of them.

She counted from her bedroom window, elbows on the sill, chin in her cupped hands, because counting stars was not like counting sheep. Sheep were for sleeping. Stars were for knowing. If you knew the sky — really knew it, the way Mama knew the sea and Papa had once known the sea before the sea took him — then nothing up there could ever surprise you. And on an island where surprises came wrapped in fog and delivered by wind, knowing things was the whole of safety.

Two hundred and six. She'd started at the chimney of the inn across the water and worked outward, quadrant by quadrant, the way Uncle — the way *someone* had taught her to count a flock of gulls once, sweeping left to right so no bird got counted twice.

She did not say his name, even inside her own head. Names were heavy. Names could be heard through walls if you thought them loudly enough. That was one of the rules she'd made for herself, and she had eleven years of rules, more than most grown women, though nobody had ever written them down for her.

Rule one: Mama didn't know everything.

This was the biggest rule, the load-bearing rule, the one all the others stood on. Mama was brave and clever and could steer a boat through weather that made fishermen weep, and she kept the light burning better than any man the Board of Trade had ever sent to check. But Mama believed that Wren still believed the story about the ghost.

The ghost story was good. Wren gave Mama credit for that. *A drowned man walks the island,* Mama said when the cellar door creaked, or when the lamp downstairs burned past midnight in a house where everyone ought to be abed. *Old sailors say the light attracts them. Pay him no mind.* And she'd said it in her telling-stories voice, warm as cocoa, and little-girl-Wren would have believed it down to her bones.

But Wren had been eight when the stories started, and she was eleven now, and three years is long enough for a girl to notice that ghosts don't cough.

* * *

The coughing was how it began.

It was last winter, the night of the first hard frost, when the windows wore lace and her breath made ghosts of its own on the glass. She'd woken needing the privy, which meant the cold walk down the passage, past the kitchen, past the narrow door behind the flour shelf that Mama always said was locked because of the damp.

Wren had been halfway down the passage when she heard it. A cough. Not a rattle, not the wheeze of wind under a door, but a real, human cough — the kind Papa used to make when he'd caught a chill and Mama dosed him with honey and whisky and scolding. It came from below the floor. From behind the flour-shelf door.

And then, softer, a sound Wren had never heard before and had spent three years trying to describe even to herself: the sound of someone trying very hard not to cough again. The swallowed kind. The kind that shakes a body silently in the dark.

She'd stood in the freezing passage in her nightgown, heart going like the fog bell, and she had not screamed, and she had not called for Mama. Because here was the thing she understood even then, standing barefoot on cold stone: whatever was down there, Mama knew about it. Mama's footsteps went to that door some nights. Mama carried trays down on Sundays when she thought Wren was asleep, and came back up with empty ones, and washed dishes after with her face doing something careful.

Mama knew. Which meant it wasn't danger. Or rather — Wren was honest with herself, another rule — it might be danger, but it was *their* danger, hers and Mama's together, and you don't scream about your own family.

So she'd gone back to bed, and lain awake till dawn counting the cracks in the ceiling, and said nothing.

That was Rule two: keep what you know until keeping it can help somebody.

* * *

After that she watched. Watching was easy; watching was what she did best. She learned that the flour-shelf door opened only between midnight and four, never twice in one week. She learned that whoever was inside liked his bread toasted, because the toast fork came out at odd hours and the crumbs in the grate weren't Mama's. She learned that there was a smell that clung to the stairwell sometimes — tallow and wool and something green, like a man who lived near the sea without ever getting to stand beside it.

And once — this was the great one, the one she turned over like a bright stone for weeks — she had seen him.

It was in June, near midsummer, when the nights barely get dark at all and the sky stays the color of pewter until nearly ten. Wren had crept down for a drink of water and stopped dead on the bottom stair, because the flour-shelf door stood open, and framed in it, holding a candle, was a man.

He was tall and stooped, like a question mark that had given up asking. His hair was dark going grey, and his beard was trimmed short, and he wore a jersey knitted in a pattern Wren knew intimately, because Mama made that pattern, because Mama had made three of them once, two big ones and a small one, and had put the small one away in a chest after the wreck and never spoken of it.

He looked like Papa.

Not exactly — Papa had been broader, and laughed bigger — but around the eyes, and in the set of the mouth, and in the way he held his head as though listening for weather. And Wren had done the arithmetic right there on the stair, quick and cold, the way she counted stars: Papa's brother Tobias, lost at sea, Mama said, gone these two years, drowned with the others. But drowned men don't wear Mama's knitting. Drowned men don't hold candles with steady hands. Drowned men don't look at the open door of a lighthouse kitchen with an expression like a man looking at the sun after years in a mine.

He hadn't seen her. He'd blown out the candle and shut the door, soft as falling snow, and Wren had drunk her water with shaking hands and gone back upstairs and added the most important rule of all:

Rule three: Uncle Toby is alive, and Mama is protecting him, and therefore I am protecting him too.

* * *

The trouble with secrets, Wren had decided, was that they were lonely. Mama carried hers alone; Wren could see the carrying, the way her mother's shoulders sat a little lower on Sundays, the way her laugh came a half-second late sometimes, like a boat arriving against the tide. Wren couldn't take the secret off her shoulders — Rule two forbade revealing that she knew — but she could do something else. She could lighten the cargo in the only way available to a girl of eleven with no money and no boat and no permission to know anything at all.

She could feed him.

It started with the heel of a loaf. Tuesday evening, after supper, Mama had cut the loaf and left the crust-end on the board while she saw to the lamp oil, and Wren had wrapped it in her handkerchief and, moving as quietly as she'd ever moved anything, laid it on the third step of the cellar stairs, just inside the flour-shelf door, where the shadows pooled thickest.

Then she'd gone back to her chair and picked up her sampler and sewn six crooked letters of *HOME SWEET HOME* while her pulse hammered in her ears like surf.

In the morning the handkerchief was folded neatly on top of the bread box, rinsed clean and dried by the banked fire, and the bread was gone.

Wren read that folded handkerchief like other people read letters. He hadn't just taken the bread. He'd returned the cloth. Washed. Pressed flat. Set where Mama would find it and understand, and where Wren — if Wren happened to be the one who found it, and she arranged matters so that she usually was — could read its whole meaning: *I see what you did. I will not waste it. I am grateful, and I am careful.*

Well. Two could be careful.

* * *

By October the arrangement had grown into a system, and Wren prided herself that it was a proper system, with rules of its own.

Bread went down Tuesdays and Fridays, because those were baking days and a missing heel would never be noticed. On Saturdays she added whatever the island's gulls and her own ingenuity provided: a boiled egg cooling forgotten on a windowsill; a handful of dried apples from the croft store; once, gloriously, a mackerel tail she'd filleted herself with Mama's small knife, and she'd been so pleased with the mackerel that she'd nearly burst wanting to be thanked.

The thanks came sideways. Things appeared. A knot tied into the handkerchief's corner that she didn't know — a rolling hitch, though she wouldn't learn its name for months — so neat it looked machine-made. A smooth grey pebble, sea-worn into a perfect oval, left on the stairs like an egg in a nest. Once, a feather: gannet white, longer than her hand, and she'd slept with it under her pillow for a week until it went brittle and she moved it to the tin with her treasures.

They were having a conversation, she and the man in the cellar. Nobody spoke, and it was the best conversation of her life.

* * *

"You're miles away tonight, chick."

Wren jumped. Mama stood in the doorway with the lamp-trimming scissors in her hand and her sleeves rolled, watching her with that look — the fond look with a question buried in the middle of it, like a stone in a loaf.

"Just counting," Wren said.

"Counting what?"

"Stars." She nodded at the window, where the early dark was coming down over the water in sheets. "I do it every night. Did you know there's different numbers? Some nights two hundred, some nights two hundred and fifty. Tonight there'll be hardly any. Storm coming."

Mama crossed to the window and looked out, and Wren watched her mother's face do the thing it always did when she read the horizon — the narrowing, the settling, the captain coming up behind the woman's eyes. "Barometer's fallen a finger's width since noon," Mama said. "You've a good eye, Wren Ashgrove."

"I get it from you."

"You get it from both of us." Mama said it lightly, but her hand came down on Wren's hair and stayed a moment, heavy and warm, and Wren felt the weight of everything unsaid pass between them like current under a calm surface. Then Mama straightened. "If the glass keeps dropping we'll have work tomorrow. Big work. I may need you on the winches."

"May?"

"*Will* need you. My crew of two." Mama smiled, and the smile was real, but Wren saw her glance — quick, unconscious, guilty — toward the flour-shelf door. Toward the third member of the crew who couldn't be mentioned, couldn't be fed openly, couldn't stand at a window and count stars or anything else.

And it landed in Wren then, sudden and physical, like cold water down the collar: winter was coming, and winters were cruel, and Uncle Toby had been two years in a hole under the sea-wall with the damp in his cough and the dark in his eyes. How many more winters? Until when? Until what? Mama never said, and Wren never asked, and the silence between them had gotten so wide you could sail a revenue cutter through it.

*That night*, Wren resolved, cutting her bread at supper into precise thirds and giving away none of it, *that night I leave extra.*

* * *

She waited until well past midnight. She listened for Mama's footsteps on the lantern-room stairs, heard them fade upward, counted to two hundred — stars, in her head, out of habit — and then she slipped from bed.

Down the passage. Past the kitchen, where the banked fire breathed red. Her hands knew the work now: the heel of Thursday's loaf, saved and hidden in her sewing basket; half an apple, wrapped in cloth; and — new, this time, the boldest thing she'd ever done — a stub of pencil and a scrap of paper torn from her copybook.

On the paper, in her best round hand, she wrote:

*I KNOW YOU ARE THERE. I WILL NOT TELL. THE STORM IS COMING TOMORROW AND MAMA WILL NEED HELP WITH THE WINCHES. EAT MORE. YOU ARE TOO THIN. — W.*

She stared at the words a long time. Writing it down made it real in a way three years of silence hadn't. Real, and dangerous, and hers. If Mama found the note — no. Mama wouldn't. Mama checked the stairs less often than Wren did; Wren had verified this with a thread across the fourth step, undisturbed nine nights running, which meant either Mama trusted the arrangement completely or Mama had her own quiet ways of knowing, and either answer was fine by Wren.

She folded the note around the bread, laid the parcel on the third step, in the deepest shadow, and stood there a moment in the dark with her heart going like a gull in a gale.

Below her, faint through the old wood, she heard it: a cough, quickly swallowed. Then a shifting, the creak of a body turning over on a cot. The sounds of a living man, keeping watch on his own small night-sea.

"Goodnight, Uncle Toby," Wren whispered, so quietly it was barely breath at all, saying the name aloud for the first time in three years, feeling it fit her mouth like a key fits a lock.

And then, from the darkness below the stairs, so softly she almost missed it — a voice, low and rough as beach gravel, unused to itself:

"Goodnight, little Wren."

She flew back up to bed without touching a single step, and lay in the dark grinning at the ceiling, and outside the first gusts of the coming storm began to rake the island, and somewhere far out over the black water, beyond the counting-stars dark, a ship's bell was ringing — though she couldn't have known whose ship, or why, or how the sound of it would change everything by morning.

She only knew that she was no longer alone in her secret.

Neither, now, was he.
