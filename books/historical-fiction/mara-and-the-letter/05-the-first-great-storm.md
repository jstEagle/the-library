## 5. The First Great Storm

The barometer had been falling since Tuesday, and by Thursday evening it fell like a man letting go of a rope.

Coral watched the needle slide through the last of the green and into the red while the kettle sang on the stove, and she did not need the instrument to tell her what was coming. She could taste it. Thirty years of salt air had taught her tongue to read the weather before her eyes did — a metallic edge to the wind, a heaviness in the swell that came up the channel like something walking. The gulls had gone inland at noon, all at once, as if called by name.

"Wren," she said. "Bring your slate down from the loft. You'll do sums tonight."

Wren appeared in the doorway with her hair half-plaited and her eyes already bright with argument. "It's going to blow."

"It is."

"And you'll be up the tower all night."

"I will."

"Alone?"

The question hung there a moment longer than it should have. Coral kept her hands busy with the tea — two cups, one for Wren, one to carry up the spiral stair — and made her voice easy as sand. "Alone as always, my love. The light doesn't mind company but it doesn't need it."

Something moved behind the wall. Not a sound, exactly — the house was old enough that every wall spoke when the wind found it — but a stillness, a held breath in the timbers, as though the cellar below were listening. Coral did not look toward the pantry door. She had learned, over two years, never to look toward the pantry door.

"Uncle Toby would help," Wren said, in the way children say things they have been told are stories. *Your uncle Toby was a sailor lost off the point*, Coral had told her once, three years ago, because a child who asked too many questions needed an answer that ended questions. *He went down with the* Mary Ellery. *That's why we keep his name off our lips.* It had been a cruel kindness, a lie built on real graves, and it worked too well: now Wren spoke of him the way she spoke of saints or ghosts, someone holy and gone, someone whose knots she claimed to have learned from a book.

"The dead don't haul winches," Coral said. "Drink your tea."

Above them, the first gust hit the tower like a fist on a door.

* * *

By nine o'clock the sea had turned the color of wet slate and was coming at the island in ranks, each wave taller than the last, each breaking with a sound like masonry falling. Coral climbed the hundred and twelve steps with the tea cooling in her hand and shut herself into the lantern room, where the great lens turned its slow white wheel and the wind tried, as it did every storm, to take the roof off the world.

She loved this room. That was the truth she never said aloud, even to herself: beneath the dread and the duty and the seven years of careful silence, she loved the lantern room the way other women loved a chapel. Here her hands knew exactly what they were for. Here there was no census man asking how many souls, no cellar door, no name she couldn't speak. There was only the lamp, the clockwork, the glass, and the sea — and the sea, whatever else it was, was honest.

She trimmed the wick. She wound the clockwork that drove the rotation, checking the weight descent against the marks she'd cut into the casing herself, because the Trinity House inspector's marks were a quarter-inch optimistic and optimism killed sailors. She wiped salt haze from the storm panes with a rag and vinegar. Outside, the island's rocks were already vanishing under spray thrown fifty feet up the cliff face, and beyond them the channel was a churning black nothing where fishing boats should have run for harbor hours ago.

At ten minutes past nine, the light caught something.

A sail — no, the ruin of one, a triangle of torn canvas standing where a mast had snapped, driving fast on the wind straight toward the Teeth, the sawtooth reef east of the point. Coral's heart did what her hands did not: it lurched, it raced, it pressed against her ribs like a trapped bird. Her hands checked the lamp pressure, confirmed the rotation, counted seconds between flashes. Her heart screamed into the dark: *turn, turn, you fool, come about.*

The boat did not turn.

She watched it strike. Even above the wind she heard it — a crack like the world's spine breaking — and then the sail was gone and there was only wreckage in the white water, and a single dark shape clinging to something that floated.

Coral was already moving. Down the stairs, two at a time in the dark, her body knowing every tread the way it knew her own heartbeat. In the kitchen she hauled the breeches buoy box open, dragged the rocket apparatus toward the seaward door, and then stopped, one breath, one beat, her hand flat against the wood.

"Tobias!" she called, low and hard, toward the pantry. "There's a wreck on the Teeth. One man in the water. I can't work the line alone."

Silence. Long enough that she thought he wouldn't come — thought two years of hiding had grown over him like scar tissue, sealing him into the dark.

Then the pantry door opened, and her brother-in-law came out into the light for the first time in fourteen months.

* * *

He looked terrible and he moved beautifully. That was always the shock of it. Two years in a cellar had bent him gray and hollow-eyed, his beard grown wild, his clothes hanging off him like rigging off a derelict — but the moment his boots hit the stone floor outside, the sea took the years back. He ran beside her along the cliff path with the wind trying to peel them both off the earth, and his hands, when they reached the rocket apparatus, knew their work without being told.

"Line's fouled," he shouted, untangling it in the dark. "Who coiled this?"

"You did. Last winter. Through the floor."

A ghost of a grin crossed his ruined face and was gone. He braced the rocket tube against the tripod while Coral sighted along it, judging the wind's drift the way her husband had taught her, the way Tobias had taught her, back when all three of them had been young and stupid and alive on this same rock.

"On my mark!"

The rocket went up trailing its fire, arced over the black water, and dropped its line across the wreck site. Somewhere out in the foam a hand found it. Coral felt the pull come down the line like a pulse, like a second heartbeat laid over her own, and together she and Tobias began to haul.

The man they pulled out of the sea was young, twenty perhaps, blue to the lips and shaking so hard his teeth clicked like dice. They got him up the cliff path with Tobias carrying him piggyback through the worst of the spray, and into the kitchen, and stripped of his soaked wool, and wrapped in blankets before the stove. Only then, in the yellow lamplight, with the storm hammering the windows like a creditor at the door, did Coral see the boy's face clearly — and saw Tobias see it too.

Recognition moved across the fisherman's features like weather.

"You're—" the boy began, and stopped, blinking water and confusion. "You're Ashgrove. You're the keeper's—"

"Cousin," Coral said.

The word landed in the room and sat there. Tobias stood very still by the stove, half in shadow, his face turned away, his hands hidden in the folds of the blanket he was pretending to warm. Coral put herself between them, ladling broth she didn't remember making, and kept talking, because talk was a wall and she had built walls all her life.

"Coral Ashgrove's cousin. Come to help with the winter maintenance. He arrived Tuesday." A lie with a date in it; dates made lies solid. "What's your boat, lad? Who else was aboard?"

"Just me. The* Kittiwake*. Da's got the fever, so I ran the crab lines solo." The boy drank the broth in desperate swallows, and Coral watched the recognition recede behind exhaustion and shock, drowned by warmth and food and the simple animal business of being alive. "I seen the light go over me and I thought, well. Either I make the point or I don't. Then the mast went."

"You made the point because the light was burning," Coral said. "Remember that. When they ask you in the village how you lived — the light was burning, and the keeper kept it."

"Aye." The boy's eyes drifted again toward the shadowed figure by the stove, and Coral's stomach tightened like a cleat taking strain. "Mister — cousin, sir. I know you. Don't I? From somewhere. Your voice—"

"He was at the funeral," Coral said smoothly. "The whole coast was at the funeral. Four men drowned; everyone came."

"Not that funeral." The boy frowned, groping through fog. "Another—"

"Another time," Tobias said.

It was the first word he'd spoken aloud in the boy's hearing, and Coral nearly flinched at the sound of it — rough from disuse, sanded down by two years of whispering, a voice like a footstep in an empty house. But the boy only nodded, swaying, and his eyes closed, and within a minute he was asleep sitting up, the empty bowl tilting in his hands.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The storm threw itself against the house. Down the passage, faint through stone, the clockwork of the lens ticked its patient circle.

"He knows me," Tobias said quietly. "Tom Pellow's boy. I fished beside his father for six years. He'll wake tomorrow remembering everything."

"Then he wakes remembering a cousin." Coral took the bowl from his slack fingers. "Tobias. Look at me. By morning he'll have told the story forty times and changed it thirty-nine, and the version the village keeps will be the one I gave them tonight. That's how stories work. That's how they've always worked."

"That's how lies work," he said, but without heat. He was looking at his own hands, flexing them in the firelight, hands that had spent fourteen months doing nothing and remembered, tonight, everything. "God, Coral. I'd forgotten what it was to be useful. My arms are singing. I feel like a man let out of prison."

"You *are* a man let out of prison," she said. "Which is why you're going back down those stairs before dawn, and you're not coming up again until the sea forgets your face."

He didn't argue. That was the worst of it, sometimes — how readily he submitted, how completely he had accepted the shape of the hole they'd dug for him. He stood, swaying with exhaustion, and paused at the pantry door with his hand on the frame.

"The boy's right, though," he said. "He does know my voice. And voices are like faces, Coral. They wash ashore when you least want them." He managed something that was almost a smile. "Keep the light burning. I'll listen for it. It's the only clock I have down there — I count your steps on the stairs. Hundred and twelve. I know when you're tired by how slow they get."

Then the door closed, and he was a ghost again, and Coral stood alone in her kitchen with a sleeping fisherman, a dying storm, and the sudden, unwelcome knowledge that her brother-in-law had spent two years counting her footsteps in the dark.

* * *

She went back up. Of course she went back up. The light did not keep itself, and the storm was at its height now, the true height, the kind of night the old keepers had named — she'd found her husband's logbook once, after, and read his entries from the great gale of '86, and understood that men measured their lives here against storms the way other men measured them against wars.

Midnight. One o'clock. The wind passed through some invisible door around two and came out the other side screaming, and the rain went horizontal, and the tower itself leaned and groaned like a ship. Coral wound the clockwork, trimmed the wick, wiped the glass, wound again. Her world shrank to a circle of brass and flame turning steadily against the black, and she lost track of whether her arms ached from hauling the line or from the winding, and it occurred to her, somewhere in the small hours, that this was the only hour of her life when she was entirely herself. No widow. No liar. No sister of a fugitive. Just the keeper. Competence as armor; she wore it like her husband's old coat, too big at the shoulders and warm where it mattered.

But armor cracks. That was the thing about armor nobody told you.

It cracked at four in the morning, when the wind dropped just enough that she could hear, rising up through a hundred and twelve steps of stone, a sound from the kitchen below. Not the boy — the boy slept on. Something smaller. A voice, thin and sleepy, asking a question. Wren, woken by the storm, padding down to check on the rescued sailor, as any child would.

And Tobias — Tobias, who should have been behind the pantry door, who had promised, who had *counted her steps* and known she was safely in the tower — Tobias answering her.

Coral took the stairs so fast she nearly fell. She came into the kitchen with the storm still roaring at the windows and found her daughter sitting cross-legged on the hearthstone, wrapped in her mother's shawl, listening with wide awake eyes while a gaunt man in the shadows showed her, slowly, with seaman's patience, how to tie a bowline one-handed.

"—and the rabbit comes up the hole, round the tree, and back down the hole," Tobias was saying. "Now you."

"Uncle Toby," Wren said, "you tie it better than the book."

The words went through Coral like cold iron. *Uncle Toby.* Not the ghost. Not the saint. *You.* Present tense. As if the child had known all along — as if the bread missing from the loaf, which Coral had blamed on rats for months, had never been rats at all.

Three faces turned toward her in the firelight: her daughter's, unafraid and luminous; the fisherman's, still drugged with sleep and seeing nothing clearly; and Tobias's, stricken, caught, the look of a man standing on a beach watching the tide come in over everything he loves.

Coral closed the pantry door softly behind her, though it was already shut, and said the only thing there was to say:

"Wren. To bed. Now."

The girl went, pausing at the door to look back — at both of them, Coral saw, at *both* of them — with an expression far too old for eleven years old. Then the loft ladder creaked, and the small feet faded, and Coral stood in the ruins of seven years of careful silence with the storm still hammering the walls and dawn still hours away.

"She knew," Tobias whispered. "Coral. She's known the whole time."

Outside, far to the east, the sky had begun, very faintly, to bruise toward morning — and with it would come the mainland boats, the gossip, the boy Tom Pellow telling his tale in every doorway of the village. Coral looked at the sleeping fisherman, at the frayed rope still coiled by the door, at the knot Wren had left half-finished on the hearthstone, perfect as scripture.

And she understood, with a certainty colder than the sea, that the storm outside had been the least of what this night had broken.
