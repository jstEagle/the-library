## 6. Ledgers and Wrecks

The harbor office smelled of tar, old paper, and the particular dampness of buildings that had never once been warm. Edmund Vane stood in its doorway for a long moment before entering, his boots dripping on the flagstones, letting his eyes adjust to the gloom. Rain came off the channel in slanting gray sheets, hammering the single window that looked out over a harbor gone the color of pewter.

No one was there.

He had been told — by the innkeeper's wife, who told him everything whether he asked or not — that the harbormaster kept irregular hours, and that the office was left open on principle. *Anyone with honest business may walk in,* she'd said, wiping down a table that didn't need it. *We're not London here.* She had said it with a small pride, as though disorder were a virtue peculiar to the coast, and Edmund had smiled the smile he kept for such remarks and said nothing at all.

The census forms were finished. That was the trouble. He had come to this island to count it, and he had counted it, and the numbers were written fair and square in his neat hand: one hundred and forty-three souls, distributed across thirty-one dwellings, from the inn to the fisher cottages to the lighthouse at the island's western tooth. One hundred and forty-three. The figure sat in his ledger with the finality of a stone dropped into a well.

Except that it did not sit quietly. It had not sat quietly since Tuesday.

Edmund removed his coat, shook the rain from it, and hung it on the peg by the door — the only courteous thing in the room, he noted, besides himself. Then he crossed to the great cabinet against the far wall, the one with drawers labeled in faded copperplate: *Tonnage. Duties. Wrecks.*

Wrecks.

He had not planned to open it. He told himself, standing there with his hand on the brass handle, that he had merely come in out of the rain, that a census man lodging at the only inn on an island of forty houses was entitled to an hour's shelter while the weather spent itself. But his hand was already turning the handle, and he knew — with the same clarity with which he knew his own arithmetic — that he had been walking toward this drawer since the moment Mrs. Ashgrove hesitated.

*Four souls dwelling at the light.*

She had said it smoothly enough. Smoothly, and then — nothing anyone else would have caught — a pause. A fraction of a second, no longer than the time it takes a lamp to gutter in a draft, but Edmund Vane had spent eleven years listening for exactly that kind of silence. People lied to him constantly; that was the nature of the work. They lied about ages and occupations and the legitimacy of children, and most of those lies were small and human and none of his business beyond the form. But a hesitation like hers was different. A hesitation like hers was a woman hearing her own voice say a number and feeling, in that instant, that the number was wrong.

And then there was the bowl. A brown earthenware bowl, drying upside-down on the rack beside the sink, too large for porridge for two, too worn for ornament. He had seen it in the half-second before she closed the door of the kitchen, and he had thought about it every day since, the way a tongue returns to a loose tooth.

The drawer stuck, then gave. Inside lay the sediment of decades: bundles tied with string, ledgers swollen with salt-damp, a dead gull's feather used as a bookmark in what appeared to be a record of kelp dues. Edmund lifted the bundles out one by one, setting them on the desk with the care of a man handling bones, until he reached the bottom and found a folder thicker than the rest, its cover stamped in ink gone brown:

*INQUIRY — LOSS OF THE *MERROW'S PRIDE* — 1886.*

Seven years ago. The year Coral Ashgrove became a widow.

* * *

He read it standing up, because sitting felt too much like settling in, and he did not want to be comfortable with what he was doing. Outside, the rain thickened. The window went from gray to the color of dishwater.

The file began with the facts, laid out in a clerk's careful hand. On the night of the ninth of November, 1886, a lugger called the *Merrow's Pride* had come onto the rocks below the western cliffs of the island in a full gale. Four men aboard. All four drowned. Their bodies — three of them — were recovered over the following week and buried in the churchyard above the cove. The fourth was never found.

So far, so ordinary. The coast ate men the way the tide ate sand, and every file in this drawer would tell some version of the same story. Edmund turned the page.

Then the tone changed. The next sheets were in a different hand — a harder one, pressed deep into the paper — and they asked questions rather than recording answers. *Whence came the cargo? Whither bound? Who financed the venture?* Because the *Merrow's Pride*, when she broke apart, had not been carrying fish. The rocks below the cliff, for two days after the wreck, had been strewn with kegs and bales: French brandy, silk, tobacco. Enough contraband to outfit half the county's Christmas.

A smuggling run, then. The inquiry knew it. Everyone on the island had known it, presumably, and everyone had said nothing, because islands are places where saying nothing is a trade skill, practiced and perfected and passed down like net-mending. The statements collected — a dozen of them, from fishermen and cottagers and the innkeeper's late father — were masterpieces of vacancy. *Saw nothing. Heard nothing. The night was black. The wind was from the southwest.* Over and over, the same polite wall.

But names surfaced anyway, the way things surface after a wreck. Three of the drowned men were named plainly: Tregose. Pascoe. Renoden. Local men, all of them, young or youngish, and the file noted their trades without comment — which was itself a comment, since two of them had no visible trade worth the name. And the fourth man, the one the sea never gave back, was named once, in the first sheet, in the clerk's original hand:

*Master and owner: J. Ashgrove.*

Someone had drawn a line through it later. Not redacted, precisely — the name could still be read beneath the stroke, if one wished to read it — but struck through, set aside, made officially invisible. And beside it, in the margin, in that harder pressing hand, someone had circled the surname alone. *Ashgrove.* Circled twice, actually, the second circle overlapping the first, the way a man circles a thing when he is not finished thinking about it.

Edmund stood very still.

He was not a fool, whatever the harbormaster's smirk might suggest. He understood what a circled name meant in a file like this. It meant suspicion without proof. It meant *we think there is more here, and we cannot reach it, and we are leaving the thread hanging for whoever comes next.* Except no one had come next. The file ended abruptly, mid-inquiry, with a final memorandum dated the following spring: *Matter closed for want of evidence. Surviving parties removed from the district or otherwise unavailable.*

*Surviving parties.* Plural.

Edmund read the phrase again. Then a third time. Then he set the folder down on the desk, took off his spectacles, and polished them on his sleeve, which was what he did when his mind needed his hands occupied.

Three bodies recovered. Four men aboard. The sea kept one — that happened, that was the sea's privilege. But the memorandum did not say *the fourth man was lost.* It said *surviving parties removed from the district or otherwise unavailable.* As though the clerk writing it had known something the burial register did not. As though somewhere between the wreck and the closing of the file, someone had stopped looking for a drowned man and started looking for a living one — and had failed to find him, or had chosen, for reasons of their own, to stop.

He thought of the bowl drying by the sink.

It was absurd. He made himself say so, aloud, to the empty office, and his voice sounded strange among the shelves. A spare bowl proved nothing. Widows kept large bowls; widows cooked for neighbors; widows had lodgers and cousins and charity cases. A hesitation over a number proved less than nothing — people flustered over censuses for a hundred innocent reasons, fear of the tax man, fear of the parish, fear of a stranger's pen. He had built whole careers — well, one career, but a long one — on refusing to leap from a paused breath to a buried man.

And yet.

He turned back to the file and forced himself through the remainder with the discipline he applied to everything: line by line, date by date, no skipping. The witness statements. The inventory of recovered cargo, itemized down to the individual keg — someone had cared about that inventory enormously, and Edmund noted that the inventory was signed not by a revenue officer but by the harbormaster himself, in a younger and more flourish-prone version of the signature he had seen on the notice board outside. The valuation. The claim that the cargo was *of no declared ownership* — which meant it vanished into official custody and, presumably, from there into unofficial pockets.

And near the end, folded into the back cover where it had lain untouched for seven years, a single sheet in yet another hand — hurried, unpracticed, the letters leaning like grass in wind. A letter, unsigned, addressed to no one:

*Whoever reads this — there were five meant to sail that night and four went out. Ask why the fifth stayed ashore. Ask who paid the crew and who will pay the widow. The sea did not act alone.*

Edmund read it four times. His heart was doing something he disapproved of.

There was no record of any follow-up. No interview conducted, no widow questioned, no fifth man sought. The letter had been received and filed, the way a stone is dropped into the same well as everything else, and the matter had been closed for want of evidence — when here, folded in the very back of the file, was evidence, wanting nothing but a reader.

*Ask who paid the crew and who will pay the widow.*

Coral Ashgrove had been a widow for seven years. Seven years of keeping a light at the edge of the world, alone with a child, on an island whose men had sailed with her husband and drowned with him. Seven years of the community's charity or contempt or both — Edmund had seen how the innkeeper's wife spoke of her, a warmth with frost under it, *poor woman, keeping that light all alone, and after what they say her Tom was mixed up in.* After what they say. Islands said things. Islands said things for seven years and longer, and the women they said them about learned to answer questions with a smoothness that broke only for a fraction of a second, over a number, over a bowl.

He closed the folder. He did not put it back in the drawer. He stood holding it, weighing it — it weighed almost nothing, a few ounces of paper, seven years old — and understanding that it was the heaviest thing he had ever carried.

Because here was the difficulty, and he faced it squarely, as he faced all difficulties: none of this was his business. He was a census man. He counted heads; he did not open graves. The inquiry was closed, the file was dust, the drowned were drowned, and whatever Mrs. Ashgrove was hiding behind her careful arithmetic was a matter for the revenue men or the magistrates or God — not for Edmund Vane, who had a schedule, and a return ticket, and a reputation for exactness that was the only possession he had ever valued.

But another voice in him — a dry, precise, unanswerable voice, the voice of the ledger itself — said: *You are a man whose entire life rests on the proposition that the record must be true. You have corrected vicars and shipmasters and lords of the admiralty over a miscounted soul. Are you now to stand in a harbor office holding an unfinished inquiry and a false arithmetic and call yourself satisfied?*

He looked out the window. The rain was easing; the harbor was emerging from it in patches, the boats knocking gently at their moorings, the cliff path above the cove running dark and wet toward the western point where the lighthouse stood. Somewhere up there, past the curve of the headland, a widow was going about her evening — lighting lamps, feeding a child, washing dishes — and drying, perhaps, a bowl for a mouth that appeared in no record anywhere on earth except, possibly, in the space of a held breath.

*Surviving parties removed from the district or otherwise unavailable.*

Or hidden, Edmund thought. Or sheltered. Or forgiven.

He put the folder back in the drawer, exactly where it had lain, aligned as he had found it, the gull's feather bookmark restored to the kelp dues. Then he wrote — because he could not help himself, because the habit of record was the spine of him — a single line in his pocket notebook, in pencil, small enough to be erased:

*Ashgrove household: stated four. Doubt one. See Merrow's Pride file.*

He looked at the line for a long time. Then he did not erase it.

Outside, the storm had passed out to sea, taking the light with it, and the first lamp of evening was being lit in the lighthouse window — a pinprick at the end of the dark headland, steady, patient, burning over water that had swallowed a man seven years ago and never said a word. Edmund buttoned his coat, settled his hat against the freshening wind, and started down toward the inn, telling himself with every step that tomorrow he would correct the census and take the morning boat and think no more of it.

He did not believe a word of it. And at the foot of the cliff path, passing the churchyard where three unnamed-in-life stones stood over three smugglers, he stopped, quite without meaning to, and counted the graves.

Three.

He walked on faster, a man carrying a number that would not stay still.
