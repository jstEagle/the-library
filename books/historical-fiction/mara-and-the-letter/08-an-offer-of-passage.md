## 8. An Offer of Passage

The Meridian came in on the morning tide, black-hulled and patient as a heron, and Coral knew before her feet left the top step that the season had turned against her.

She had seen the cutter twice before in seven years — once to deliver lamp oil, once to carry off a sick child — and both times it had come with the loose, easy manner of a vessel about its ordinary work. This morning there was nothing loose about it. It stood in through the harbor mouth with its guns run out and its ensign snapping hard in a wind that had teeth in it already, and the men at the rail did not wave. They watched. There is a particular way government men watch an island they have been sent to measure, and Coral had learned it from her husband, who had spent his last years learning to recognize the shapes of his own ruin coming across the water.

"Wren," she said, without turning. "Boil water."

Her daughter was already at the stove. Eleven years old and she read a sail faster than most men read a page; she'd been up since first light, and Coral suspected she'd been up on the cliff path watching the cutter anchor long before her mother's door opened. Wren said nothing, only set the kettle down harder than the task required, which was her way of asking questions without spending them.

Coral dressed for the harbor. She chose the grey dress, the plain one, the one that made her look like what she was supposed to be: a widow who kept a light and asked for nothing. She pinned her hair back so severely it hurt a little. Armor came in different weights for different weather.

* * *

The harbor was full by half past eight, which for Carrack Island constituted a crowd. The fishermen had come in off the water early — too early, Coral noted, unless someone had told them to be ashore today — and they lined the quay with their hands in their pockets, wearing the studied blankness Cornish men put on like oilskins when strangers started counting things.

The harbormaster stood at the head of the steps to receive the officers, resplendent in his best coat, his beard combed to a shine. Mr. Trenance. The man had wept openly at her husband's funeral, Coral remembered. He had pressed her hand and called Josiah the soul of the island. She looked at him now, at the careful arrangement of grief still worn about him after all these years, and felt the cold move up through her boots into her knees.

The cutter's captain came ashore in the second boat. He was younger than Coral expected, thirty perhaps, with a face like a drawn map — every line accounted for, nothing left blank. He carried a leather case under one arm and did not smile at the harbormaster's welcome.

"Captain Quiller," the harbormaster announced to the quay, "of His Majesty's revenue service." A scattering of nods. "Come ahead of the storms to see us safe provisioned."

That was not what he'd come for, and everyone on that quay knew it, and nobody said so, and Coral understood in that moment precisely how a community survives: by agreeing, all together, to pronounce false words in unison.

Quiller let the pleasantries run aground on their own. Then he opened his leather case and drew out a paper, and his voice, when he raised it, carried flat and clear over the water like a line thrown to a drowning man.

"I'll say this once, and I'll say it to all of you at once, because I'm not a man for saying things twice. There is reason to believe a fugitive has been sheltered on this island. Persons with knowledge are required to come forward. Persons found concealing—" he paused, and looked along the row of faces, unhurried, letting each man feel himself counted— "will answer for the concealment as though they'd committed the original crime themselves."

The quay went very quiet. Gulls screamed somewhere out past the breakwater, absurd and indifferent.

"But," Quiller went on, and here his voice changed, softened, became something almost kind, which frightened Coral far more than the threat had, "the Crown is not without mercy. Any resident of this island who claims fear of the person sheltered — any soul who wishes removal to the mainland for their own protection — will be given a berth aboard this ship. Free passage. Protection guaranteed." He folded the paper away. "We sail on the morning tide tomorrow. No questions will be asked of anyone who takes that berth. No questions at all."

He closed the case. The offer sat there among them like bait sitting on clean sand.

Coral stood very still at the edge of the gathering, one hand resting on the stone of the harbor wall, and worked it through, because working things through was what she did instead of panicking, and it was the only discipline that had kept her family breathing these two years.

It sounded like mercy. It was shaped like mercy. A boat out, a fresh start, no questions asked — and God, how those three words rang in her like a struck bell, *no questions asked*, when her whole life had become a series of questions she could not afford to answer.

But it was a trap, and she saw the shape of it plainly, the way you see a rock under green water when the light comes round right.

Because no questions asked meant no questions *recorded*. Anyone who fled was confessing fear, and fear required a cause, and the cause was standing in their cellar. Whoever took that berth would be marked forever as the one who ran — and more than that, they would be taken aboard a revenue cutter, where names would be written down, descriptions taken, statements quietly gathered later, gently, over tea, in a cabin with the door shut. The offer wasn't a door out. It was a funnel. Every frightened soul who stepped aboard would narrow the search. And if nobody stepped aboard — if the whole island stood silent and stayed put — then the authorities would know the fugitive was protected, beloved even, woven into the place like a root under a wall, and they would stop being polite about it.

And beneath all of that, simpler and colder: Quiller had not come to catch a smuggler. Smugglers were a shilling a dozen on this coast. He had come to make the island betray itself. To stand on that quay and watch which faces flinched.

Coral made her own face do nothing at all. She had gotten very good at nothing.

* * *

Trenance walked her partway up from the harbor, which he had never done before, and that told her more than anything the captain had said.

"A terrible business," the harbormaster said, shaking his head, his voice pitched for anyone nearby to hear. "Smuggling, wrecks, drowned men — and now a fugitive under our roofs, they say. I told Captain Quiller the people of this island are honest folk. I told him we'd sooner turn out a snake ourselves than harbor one." He smiled at her, and his eyes were pleasant and blue and utterly without warmth. "You'd know if anything were amiss up at the light, Mrs. Ashgrove. You keep such careful accounts of everything."

"Do I?" Coral said mildly.

"You do. Seven years alone up there with the child. Remarkable." He patted her gloved hand where it lay on his arm. "A woman alone learns to notice things. Who comes. Who goes." The pat tightened, just slightly, just enough. "Who eats at her table."

The wind came off the water and moved the hem of her dress and she let it.

"If I learn anything worth knowing, Mr. Trenance," she said, "I'll be sure to bring it down to you. You always did keep better ledgers than the rest of us."

Something crossed his face then — quick, gone before it finished arriving — and Coral filed it away in the room inside herself where she kept everything dangerous. Because she had seen that flicker before, years ago, in another man's face, in another conversation about other ledgers, and she had spent seven years telling herself she had imagined it. She had not imagined it. She was done pretending she had.

They parted at the fork where the cliff path began. Trenance tipped his hat. Coral climbed.

* * *

Wren met her at the door with the kettle screaming behind her and her eyes full of a question she wouldn't ask indoors, so Coral walked past the house and up the path toward the light itself, and Wren fell in beside her, and only when they stood in the lee of the tower with the sea booming below did the girl finally speak.

"There's a boat tomorrow."

"Yes."

"For anyone who's afraid."

"Yes."

Wren looked out at the cutter riding at anchor, black against the grey water. "Are we afraid?"

Coral crouched down so their eyes were level. Her daughter had Josiah's chin and her own stubbornness and a silence in her that was older than eleven years ought to allow, and looking at her was sometimes like looking into a mirror hung in a dark room — familiar, and lit from nowhere.

"We are going to be the least afraid people on this island," Coral said. "Do you understand me? Tomorrow, when they ask, we are going to walk about in broad daylight and buy bread and mend nets and laugh at something, if we can find anything to laugh at, and we are not going to look at that ship once. Can you do that?"

Wren considered it with the seriousness she gave everything. "Why would they ask if we're afraid?"

"Because afraid people talk," Coral said. "Afraid people leave. And whoever leaves leaves a shape behind them, the shape of who's missing, and there are men on that ship whose whole trade is measuring shapes." She tucked a strand of hair behind her daughter's ear. "So we stay exactly the size we've always been. Four bowls. Four chairs. Four souls at this light, same as the census says."

Wren's eyes flickered — just once, just sideways, toward the cellar grate — and Coral's heart turned over slowly in her chest like a stone in a tide pool.

Four souls at this light. She heard her own words come back to her, and understood, with a lurch, that her daughter had counted better than she had. That the arithmetic of this house had been wrong for two years and every soul in it knew it, and now there was a warship in the harbor come specifically to count.

"Mama," Wren said quietly, "if someone wanted to go — someone who was afraid — would you stop them?"

Coral held her daughter's gaze and felt the trap close another notch, because the question was not really about fear, and they both knew it, and neither of them could say what it was about.

"No," she said at last. "But I'd ask them to wait for the right boat. The Meridian sails toward the law, Wren. Someday there'll be a boat that sails away from it."

Below them, muffled by stone and distance, or perhaps only imagined, a floorboard settled in the cellar. Or perhaps it was nothing. Or perhaps Tobias had heard the guns run out, had seen the cutter anchor, had been listening at the grate all morning the way he listened to everything, keeping his silence like a man bailing a boat.

Coral rose and looked out at the Meridian one last time. Tomorrow at dawn it would weigh anchor, empty or full, and whichever it was would tell the authorities everything they wanted to know. If it sailed full, someone had broken. If it sailed empty, the net would draw tighter around the island, around the light, around the cellar where a guilty man ate bread left by a child who believed in ghosts.

Hope had come into the harbor this morning dressed in the Crown's uniform, offering free passage and no questions asked. Coral had looked it in the eye and recognized it for what it was: menace with a kind voice, a funnel with a painted door.

She turned for the house. Behind her the cutter's flag cracked in the rising wind, and far below, faint but unmistakable, she heard the ship's bell strike the change of watch — counting hours, counting souls, counting down.

There were twenty-four hours until the morning tide.

Somewhere between now and then, Coral knew, someone was going to have to decide who got on that boat — and the worst of it, the thing that sat in her chest like swallowed lead, was the small treacherous voice whispering that maybe, just maybe, the right answer was *someone should*.
