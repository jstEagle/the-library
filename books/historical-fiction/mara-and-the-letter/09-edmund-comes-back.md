## 9. Edmund Comes Back

The correction form was a small thing, no larger than a prayer book, and Edmund Vane had carried it in his breast pocket for six days like a stone he could not put down.

Household of the lighthouse, Ashgrove Island: one soul. That was what the schedule said in his own careful copperplate, the ink gone brown at the edges where sea damp had reached it. One soul — Mrs. Coral Ashgrove, widow, keeper of the light, aged thirty-four. And beneath it, added later in the same hand but with a hesitation he could feel even now, a second entry struck through and rewritten: *four souls*.

He had not been able to explain to himself why he had written it that way. The rules were simple. A census taker recorded what was true on the night of enumeration, or he recorded nothing at all. He did not revise from memory. He did not let a woman's pause — one beat too long, the space of a swallowed word — rewrite his arithmetic for him. And yet there it sat, four souls, in ink now dry beyond recall, and every day the number had seemed less like an error and more like an accusation aimed at whoever had made it.

He told himself, as he had told himself all week over the inn's cold mutton and worse beer, that it was only accuracy he wanted. Only the clean satisfaction of a column that summed correctly. He was a man who counted things; it was the whole of his profession and most of his character, though he had never before considered whether those were the same thing. He counted the steps down from the inn to the harbor (one hundred and twelve). He counted the gulls wheeling over the fish cellar (nine, then eleven, then nine again, because gulls refused to be governed). He counted the days since his wife had died (two thousand one hundred and forty, though he had stopped writing the figure down after the first year, because numbers that large looked like something other than devotion).

He did not count friends. There had never been enough to bother with.

So when the morning came clear and windless — the first such morning in a fortnight, the island lying flat and golden on its water like something tame — he took up the correction form and walked out along the cliff path toward the lighthouse, telling himself the walk was administrative and nothing more.

* * *

The path was better than he remembered, or he was better at it. Six days ago he had come up it white-knuckled, certain the sea below meant to have him, and Mrs. Ashgrove had watched him arrive with a politeness so perfect it had felt like mockery. Today his boots found the grooves worn by a century of keepers' feet, and he allowed himself, once, to look down at the water instead of away from it. It was the green of bottle glass. Somewhere under that pleasant color lay the rocks that had taken four men seven years ago, and somewhere above it stood the woman who knew exactly how they had died, and said nothing, and kept the light burning anyway.

He had read the inquiry file. He should not have — it was in the harbor office, unattended, and curiosity had overcome him with the ease of an old habit — but he had, and the names redacted in the margin had circled back around to *Ashgrove* like gulls returning to a gutting table. Four drowned. The file accounted for three bodies recovered. The fourth man had gone into the water and stayed there, officially, forever.

Edmund had spent one sleepless night considering what that meant, and had concluded, near dawn, that it meant nothing provable, which was the worst kind of meaning.

The lighthouse resolved itself out of the haze as he climbed: white tower, black gallery, the keeper's cottage clinging to its base like a barnacle. Smoke rose from the chimney. Gulls argued on the roof ridge. It looked, as it had looked before, like the most honest building on the coast — a structure whose entire purpose was to tell the truth about where the rocks were.

He knocked at the cottage door and waited.

No answer.

He knocked again, harder, and this time heard movement inside — quick feet on flags, the scrape of a chair — and then the door opened and it was not Mrs. Ashgrove at all but the girl. Wren. Eleven years old or thereabouts, dark-haired, her sleeves pushed up past elbows still damp from washing, regarding him with the frank, unhurried assessment of a customs officer.

"You're the counting man," she said.

"I'm the census taker," Edmund said. "Edmund Vane." He removed his hat, which seemed correct, though he could not have said for whom. "Is your mother at home?"

"She's oiling the lamp room. She does it mornings." The girl did not move aside. "You can't go up. You'd touch things."

"I don't intend to go up. I only need to correct an error in the household schedule."

Wren's eyes narrowed by a degree. "What error?"

"A matter of arithmetic," Edmund said. "Nothing that concerns you."

"Everything concerns me. I live here." But she stepped back, opening the door onto the low kitchen, and he understood that she was not admitting him so much as permitting him, which was a different thing entirely, and one he suspected she had learned from her mother.

* * *

The kitchen smelled of bread and lamp oil and, faintly, of bracken smoke, as though a fire had been fed with something gathered rather than bought. Edmund stood just inside the door with his hat in his hands while Wren returned to her place at the table, where a length of rope lay coiled like a sleeping animal and a knife sat beside it with the handle worn pale.

"You may sit," she said, "if you're going to be long."

"I shan't be long." He did not sit. He took out the correction form and laid it on the table's corner, well away from the rope, and uncapped his pen. "Mrs. Ashgrove stated the number of souls in this dwelling as four. On my schedule I first entered one, then amended it. I require her to confirm the figure in her own hand, so the record is consistent."

"Why'd you write one first?"

The question arrived without warning, the way gulls dropped out of sunlight. Edmund found himself looking at the girl directly for the first time. She had her mother's stillness but none of her mother's guard; the guard would come later, he supposed, the way calluses come.

"Because I miscounted," he said.

"No, you didn't." She picked up the rope and began, absently, to work a strand of it between her fingers. "Mum says you count things proper. She said you'd be back."

Something shifted in Edmund's chest — not alarm exactly, but the sensation of a stair where he had expected floor. "Your mother expects me?"

"She said the counting man would come back, because you wrote four and then couldn't leave it." Wren tied off something small and invisible in her hands, tested it, frowned. "She said people who count can't bear a wrong sum."

"And is it a wrong sum?" Edmund asked quietly. "How many souls dwell here, Miss Ashgrove? In your own estimation?"

The girl went very still. It lasted perhaps two seconds, and in those two seconds Edmund watched a door close behind her eyes — watched the guard assemble itself, piece by piece, years early — and he understood that he had just seen the exact moment a child learned what her mother already knew: which questions must be answered slowly.

"Mum counts us," Wren said. "Ask her."

* * *

But Mrs. Ashgrove did not come down. The minutes stretched; the kettle ticked on the hob; the oil clock on the mantel marked time in silence. Through the window Edmund could see the tower's gallery rail against the sky, empty. Somewhere below them all, he thought — he did not know why the thought came, or why it came shaped like certainty — somewhere below the flags of this cottage there was space enough for a man.

He tried to put the thought down. It was not evidence. It was a feeling dressed as evidence, and feelings had ruined better men's careers than his.

To occupy his hands he glanced at the rope on the table, and that was when he saw the knot.

It was a bowline on a bight — he recognized it the way he recognized his own signature — tied in a doubled line, the loop standing open and neat, the working end secured with a stopper knot of a pattern he had not seen outside naval manuals. It was not a knot a Cornish child learned from a Cornish fisherman. Cornish fishermen tied their bowlines left-handed and sloppy and proud of it. This knot was merchant service, deep-water, taught by men who had sailed out of ports whose names ended in vowels. It was precise, disciplined, and utterly foreign to this kitchen.

"That's a fine knot," Edmund said, before he could stop himself.

"Toby taught me." The words were out before she could catch them, and he saw her hear herself say them — saw the color rise and the small hands flatten against the table as though to hold the sentence down. "Uncle Toby," she said. "He's — he's not here. He's a story. Mum made him up. For bedtime."

"In your bedtime stories," Edmund said carefully, "your uncle teaches you knots?"

"He teaches me everything." Her chin came up, defiant and frightened in equal measure, and Edmund saw with terrible clarity that the girl had decided, in the last five seconds, to protect someone, and had done it badly, because she was eleven, and brave, and alone in a kitchen with a stranger who counted things. "He taught Mum too. Before. Everyone knows Toby. He's just not —"

"Wren."

The voice came from the doorway, quiet and level, and Edmund turned to find Coral Ashgrove standing there with oil rags over her arm and the sea smell of the lamp room on her. She did not look alarmed. That was the remarkable thing. She looked at her daughter, and at the census taker, and at the correction form on the table's corner, and Edmund watched her take the whole scene in the way he imagined a keeper reads weather — not the visible sky but the pressure behind it.

"Mr. Vane," she said. "You've found my daughter unsupervised. I'll thank you not to enter that on any schedule."

"I've found your daughter tying a knot I last saw in the merchant marine," Edmund said. "I hadn't planned to enter it anywhere."

The silence that followed was long enough to have weather of its own. Wren had gone scarlet. Mrs. Ashgrove set the oil rags down on the dresser, each motion deliberate, a woman putting her house in order before the storm she could see coming over the horizon.

"Uncle Toby is a story," she said.

"Yes," Edmund said. "I imagine he is. I imagine he is a story that ties bowlines on a bight, and coughs in winter, and eats from a fourth bowl."

He had not meant to say the last part aloud. It had lived in him six days, that bowl drying by the sink — a plain brown earthenware bowl among the three fine ones, dried and shelved in a position that suggested use rather than storage — and it escaped now with the rest.

Mrs. Ashgrove did not deny it. That was the second remarkable thing. She simply looked at him, level and unblinking, and Edmund felt the full weight of whatever it was she carried settle onto the kitchen like a change of air.

"What will you do?" she asked.

And there it was: the question he had walked up the cliff to avoid, arriving ahead of him, waiting at the table with the rope and the kettle. What would he do? His duty was written on the form in his pocket, in regulations older than either of them: discrepancies reported to the district registrar; anomalies investigated; the census a complete and truthful account of every soul. If there was a man hidden on this island — a man whose name might be Toby, a man who might be the fourth smuggler the sea never gave back — then accuracy required Edmund to report him, and accuracy was the whole of Edmund's religion, the only congregation he had ever belonged to since Mary died.

"I came to correct an error," he said at last. "The household figure. Four souls."

"Four souls," Mrs. Ashgrove repeated.

"Or three." He looked down at the form, at his own copperplate waiting for the final stroke. "I find I am no longer certain which number is the accurate one. And a census taker who is uncertain should record nothing rather than something false."

He capped the pen. He slid the correction form back into his breast pocket, unsent, unsigned, a document that officially did not exist.

Mrs. Ashgrove's face did not change, but something behind it changed — some door, some shutter, some long-closed window letting in a thread of light. "You're a strange man, Mr. Vane."

"I'm an accurate one," he said. "Or I was. I appear to be developing doubts."

"Mum," Wren whispered, "what's happening?"

"Nothing, love. The counting man is leaving." Mrs. Ashgrove held the door open, and the wind came in off the sea carrying salt and the far-off clang of the harbor bell. "Mr. Vane. Whatever it is you didn't write today — thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," Edmund said. "I haven't finished not writing."

* * *

He walked down the cliff path faster than he had climbed it, because if he slowed he would turn around, and if he turned around he would ask the questions whose answers he suddenly dreaded — the man's name, the wreck, the four bowls — and once asked, they could not be unasked, and once known, they would have to be written somewhere, and everything written becomes a weapon in someone's hands eventually.

Halfway down he stopped at the bend where the path crossed the old quarry track, and took out the correction form, and looked at it.

Four souls, in his careful hand. Three, perhaps, in truth. Or four, if truth included a ghost named Toby who taught children knots the Navy used and mothers did not correct their daughters' stories, only their silences.

A gull screamed somewhere over the water. Below him the harbor lay bright and innocent, the fishing boats at their moorings, the harbor office with its unlocked drawers, the flag over the custom house stirring in a wind that had not yet decided which way to blow.

Edmund Vane folded the form into quarters and did not tear it up. Tearing it would have been a decision, and decisions left marks. He simply put it back in his pocket, unreported, and walked on, and told himself the lie that meticulous men tell best: that he was merely postponing.

But at the bottom of the path, where the ground flattened toward the inn, he passed the notice board by the ferry steps and saw, pinned fresh that morning among the tide tables, a printed sheet with a revenue stamp at its head. He read it twice.

*H.M. Cutter Meridian — arriving within the week. Any resident harboring knowledge of persons unlawfully at large upon this island is instructed to make such knowledge known to the commanding officer. Reward offered. Silence itself will be construed as complicity.*

*Silence itself will be construed as complicity.* The words seemed to lift off the paper and follow him up the street.

Edmund stood a long moment in the road with his hat in his hand, a man who counted things, discovering that the largest number on the island was the one he could no longer total — and that somewhere below a lighthouse, in a cellar smelling of bracken smoke, a story called Toby was running out of darkness to hide in.
