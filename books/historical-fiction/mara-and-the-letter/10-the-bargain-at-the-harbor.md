## 10. The Bargain at the Harbor

The *Meridian* sat at her moorings in the harbor like a knife laid on a table — slim, black-hulled, her guns shrouded under canvas but her intent visible from the cliff path all the same. Coral had watched revenue cutters come and go for seven years, and she had learned to read them the way other women read weather: this one had not come for cargo. This one had come to stay.

She went down at half past eight, while Wren was still asleep and Tobias was below with his ear pressed, no doubt, to the cellar's stone and every footstep above translated into a verdict. She left the lamp trimmed and ready, the log open on its stand, everything in the lighthouse arranged so that if she did not return by noon it would look — to anyone who came looking — as though nothing at all were wrong.

The tide would turn at 11:40. She had counted it out on her fingers twice before leaving the kitchen. Whatever bargain she meant to strike, it had an hourglass built into it.

* * *

The harbor office smelled of tar, wet rope, and old paper. Captain Aldous Renwick received her standing behind the harbormaster's own desk — the harbormaster himself conspicuously absent, which told Coral more than any words could have. A man who believed himself innocent of suspicion did not clear out of his office when the cutter arrived.

"You'll be Mrs. Ashgrove." Renwick was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the flat, patient eyes of a man who had spent thirty years reading coastlines and the people who lived against them. "Keeper Ashgrove's widow."

"I keep the light now," she said. "Seven years."

"So I'm told. Sit, if you like."

"I'd rather stand." Standing kept her near the door, and near the door kept her mind working. "You've posted a notice. Any resident who fears for their safety may claim passage on the morning boat. No questions asked."

Renwick folded his hands on the desk. "That's standard procedure when there's reason to believe—"

"Believe what?" She let the silence stretch just long enough to make him uncomfortable, then finished it herself. "A fugitive. You believe there's a fugitive on my island. That's why the *Meridian* is here in October, off her usual station, that's why the harbormaster has suddenly developed business on the mainland, and that's why your notice promises mercy to anyone frightened enough to run."

Something flickered at the corner of the captain's mouth. Not quite a smile; more the look of a man finding a knot exactly where he expected one.

"You're not frightened, then?"

"I've been frightened since 1886, Captain. I've simply gotten good at carrying it." She crossed to the window. Below, the tide was already beginning its slow retreat, baring the green-slicked stones of the harbor wall. "I didn't come to claim your berth."

"No?"

"I came to sell you something better than a fugitive."

* * *

He did not answer at once, and she made herself endure the pause without filling it. Silence was a tool she had sharpened over seven years of census men and customs men and neighbors with kind, prying eyes. Most men could not bear three seconds of it. Renwick lasted five before he said, quietly:

"Go on."

"The wreck of the *Sea Lark*. Four men drowned, November '86. Your inquiry closed within six weeks. Two bodies recovered, two presumed lost. The ringleader named in the file was a dead man — my husband — because a dead man makes a tidy ending to a ledger." She turned back to face him. "But the *Sea Lark* wasn't running brandy for love of it. Someone financed the venture. Someone owned the boats outright, insured them twice over through firms in Plymouth, and stood to gain more from four drowned witnesses than from a dozen successful runs. Your inquiry never asked who that someone was, because your inquiry was steered."

"By whom?"

"By the man whose desk you're standing behind."

The clock on the office wall ticked through four full seconds. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up.

"Harbormaster Penhale," Renwick said at last, and his voice had changed — flattened, careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice and testing it with each word.

"Cornelius Penhale. Thirty-one years in the post. Godfather to half the children on this island. The man who organized the relief fund for the drowned men's widows — paid out of money he'd already taken from the same run he scuttled them on." Coral heard her own voice and marveled distantly at how steady it was. Seven years of practice saying nothing had taught her, at last, how to say something. "I can give you dates. I can give you the insurance houses. I can give you the name of the Plymouth clerk who handled the double policies, if he still lives. I cannot give you papers, because Penhale burned what could be traced — but paper isn't the only evidence a man leaves. Ask him about the winter of '85. Ask him where the second boat went that he reported lost."

"And why," Renwick said slowly, "would the widow of the man your theory implicates hand me all this?"

"Because the theory doesn't implicate him. It clears him. My husband ran contraband, Captain — I won't insult us both by pretending otherwise. But he didn't drown those men, and neither did the man you think is hiding in my house. Cornelius Penhale drowned them, and he has spent seven years being mourned as their friend, and every year that passes he stands a little straighter and the truth stands a little deeper underwater." Her hands had curled at her sides without her permission. She loosened them, finger by finger, the way she loosened knots. "I am done letting him stand."

Renwick came around the desk. Up close he was shorter than he'd seemed, broad through the shoulders, a working sailor's build beneath the uniform. He studied her the way he might study a chart with one sounding that didn't fit the rest.

"You understand what you're describing. If I act on this and you're wrong — or if you're right but the evidence won't hold — you'll have made an enemy of the most powerful man on this coast. He can have your light reassigned. He can have you removed. There are ways, Mrs. Ashgrove, to make an island unlivable for a woman alone."

"I've survived worse than unlivable."

"And the fugitive? The one we both know I'm here for?" He said it gently, which was somehow worse. "Whatever you sell me today, I still have orders. If there is a man hidden on this island, I am bound to find him. Passage on the morning boat protects whoever takes it from prosecution. Nothing else does."

"That," said Coral, "is the bargain."

* * *

She walked to the window again, mostly to put distance between her face and his, because what she was about to do required her face to be stone and stone was easier at a distance. The tide had turned. She could see it in the drift of the weed along the harbor wall — imperceptible to anyone who hadn't spent seven years watching water, unmistakable to her. The hourglass had begun to run.

"Here is what I offer," she said. "Before the morning tide, I will give you enough on Penhale to justify opening a new inquiry — enough that your superiors in Falmouth cannot bury it the way the last one was buried. Dates, names, the shape of the thing. In exchange, when your search comes to the lighthouse — and it will come, Captain, don't pretend otherwise — whatever you find or don't find, one man boards the *Meridian* at dawn under your protection. No prosecution. No inquiry file with his name circled. He sails, and after that he is yours to lose track of."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I tell you nothing about Penhale, and you spend the next month turning over rocks on this island while he sits in that chair behind you, laughing at you, and the trail goes cold the way it went cold in '86, because he has had seven years' practice at cold trails. You'll catch a smuggler's ghost, maybe. You'll never touch him." She paused. "Or you take my bargain, and you leave this island having caught the biggest fish this station has seen in twenty years. Think about how that reads in Falmouth."

Renwick looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — short, genuine, surprised out of him.

"They warned me about you. Down at the inn. Said the lighthouse keeper's widow was the quietest soul on the island." He shook his head. "Quiet as deep water, maybe."

"Deep water drowns careless men."

"Yes." The humor drained out of his face, and what remained was harder and, in its way, kinder. "Mrs. Ashgrove, I'll be plain with you, because you've been plain with me. I believe you. That's the trouble. I've read the '86 file twice since I anchored — it stinks. Closed too fast, too clean, too many convenient corpses. And I've known Penhale by reputation fifteen years, and reputations that spotless are usually bought." He moved to the window beside her, so they stood together looking down at the ebbing harbor like two people sharing a confidence rather than striking a bargain. "But believing you and proving you are different animals. I have no authority to promise immunity. What I have is discretion in how I conduct a search and discretion in what I report. I can tell you that much honestly: if a man presents himself at this vessel before the morning tide, voluntarily, I am required to detain him — and I can choose to record his surrender as cooperation rendered in the matter of the *Sea Lark* inquiry, reopened. Cooperation is treated differently than capture. It's a thin thread. But it holds weight, if nothing pulls too hard on it."

"And if something pulls?"

"Then it snaps, and I won't be able to stop it." He turned to face her fully. "You'd be gambling a great deal on a thread. Him especially. Whoever he is."

Coral held his eyes. Somewhere below, the water slid further down the harbor wall, and the smell of exposed weed rose up through the window gap, dark and mineral, the smell of things revealed when the sea withdraws.

"He's my brother-in-law," she heard herself say. "Tobias. My late husband's brother. He's been in my cellar two years, and he is the only living soul besides myself who knows what Penhale did, because he was on the water that night and saw whose signal lit the *Sea Lark* onto the rocks." It came out of her whole, like a tooth — seven years of clenching around it, and now it was gone, and her tongue kept touching the empty place. "There. Now you have the last thing I had left to hide. So you see, Captain, we are well past bargaining. We're past the point where either of us can pretend the other is honest. All that's left is whether we're useful to each other."

For the first time, Renwick looked genuinely shaken. She watched him master it — jaw set, breath drawn slow through the nose, a sailor steadying himself against a deck that had suddenly heeled.

"Two years," he said. "In your cellar. With a child in the house."

"With my child in the house. Yes." She lifted her chin. "Judge me if it helps you sleep. But judge quickly, because the tide is going out and my offer goes with it. Name, dates, testimony — for a thread of cooperation on a reopened inquiry. Before the morning tide. After that, I walk out of here, I say nothing, and you may search every cave on this island until Christmas."

* * *

He made her wait while he wrote. She stood at the window and watched the harbor empty itself, gulls picking at the wet stones, a fisherman hauling his punt higher up the sand with the unconscious competence of a man who has read tides since childhood. Wren would be awake by now. Wren would find the note — *gone to the harbor, keep the kettle warm, mind the lamp log till I'm back* — and read it correctly, because her daughter had inherited the family gift for reading between lines. Perhaps Wren would even guess. Eleven years old and already fluent in silences; Coral both blessed and cursed the inheritance daily.

Behind her, pen scratched. Once, twice, Renwick stopped and started again, crossing something out. Finally he cleared his throat.

"Here's what I can write and stand behind." His voice had gone formal, official, the voice of a report rather than a man. "Acting on information received regarding irregularities in the inquiry of 1886, I have reopened the case of the wreck of the *Sea Lark*. An informant has provided material testimony concerning parties previously unnamed. This informant is to be afforded protection and consideration proportionate to the value of that testimony, per standing instructions regarding cooperative witnesses." He signed it, blotted it, and held it out to her. "That's not immunity. Read it carefully — I won't have you thinking it is. It's a door left unlocked. Whether anyone walks through depends on what happens in the next twelve hours, and on how hard anyone pulls on that thread."

Coral took the paper. Her hands were finally trembling now — late, useless, human. She read it twice, committing it to memory the way she committed the light's characteristics, because paper could be confiscated but memory was hers alone.

"It says informant," she said. "Singular. Singular covers a lot of ground, Captain."

Renwick almost smiled. "It does, doesn't it. Funny thing about ink." He sobered. "Mrs. Ashgrove. One more thing, and then you should go, because if Penhale returns and finds you here, everything I've just written becomes worth less than the paper. You said your brother-in-law saw whose signal lit the *Sea Lark* in. Whose signal?"

Coral folded the paper into her bodice, against her heart, where the important things lived.

"You'll have my testimony before the tide," she said. "All of it. But I'll give it to you at the light, tonight, with him standing beside me — because a witness alone is a rumor, Captain, and two witnesses are a case. And because if this goes wrong tomorrow, I want the last night my family spends together to be spent telling the truth."

She was at the door before he spoke again.

"Mrs. Ashgrove." She turned. Renwick stood by the window with the light behind him, and for a moment he looked very tired, very human, a man who had wanted a quiet posting and gotten a wreck with a widow attached. "The tide turns at 11:40. I'll send a boat at eleven sharp. If your testimony is what you claim, the thread holds. If it isn't—" He didn't finish it. He didn't need to.

Coral climbed the cliff path with the paper against her heart and the wind rising off the water, and she did not let herself think about what she had done until she reached the top. Then she stopped, once, and looked back at the harbor — the cutter riding her moorings, the tide sliding away, the whole glittering machinery of law and consequence that she had just invited into her house.

Somewhere below the lighthouse, behind a stone door, a man who had chosen guilt and darkness for two years was about to learn that his sister-in-law had sold their secret to buy his life.

She walked on toward the light, faster now, because the sky in the northwest had gone the color of a bruise, and the barometer in the hall had been falling since yesterday, and there was not, she suspected, going to be time for anything gentle.
