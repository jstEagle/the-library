## 12. The Financier's Name

The walk down from the lighthouse took Coral twenty minutes in fair weather. In the grey bruising light of that October morning, with the wind already tasting of iron, it took her forty, and she arrived at the harbor office with salt crusted white along the hem of her coat and her hands so cold she had to knock twice before the sound carried.

Edmund Vane opened the door himself.

He looked as though he had not slept, and she supposed she looked the same. His collar was crooked, his ink-stained fingers curled around the door's edge as if he meant to close it against her, and for one long moment the two of them stood in the draft with nothing between them but seven years of careful silence and one night of terrible honesty.

"You came," he said.

"You asked what I would do with what I know." She lifted her chin. "I've come to answer."

He stepped back and let her in.

* * *

The harbor office smelled of tar, cold tea, and old paper. Ledgers lined the walls in ranks, their spines cracked from decades of hands, and above the hearth hung a framed portrait of a square-jawed man with a beard like a ship's figurehead — Aldous Penhale, harbormaster of Gullwrack Island for thirty-one years, savior of fishermen, founder of the Seamen's Fund, the man whose name every child on the island could spell before they could spell their own.

Coral looked at that portrait for a long time.

"He was at the funeral," she said quietly. "My husband's funeral. He stood at the head of the grave with his hat off, and he wept. Real tears, Edmund. I watched them fall into the hole. And I thought — here is a good man, grieving a good man, and I am the only soul in that churchyard who knows what my husband truly was."

Edmund had not moved to his desk. He stood with his back to the cold hearth, his census satchel still slung across his body as though he had forgotten to take it off, and he watched her the way he watched everything — as though counting.

"You don't have to do this," he said. "I told you last night. What I know, I will carry or I will bury, but I won't force it out of you."

"You asked me what I'll do with what I count." She turned from the portrait. "That was your question. And the answer is that I have spent seven years doing nothing with it, and doing nothing has a cost too. It has a cost every time Penhale shakes my hand at the harvest feast. It has a cost every time Wren says his name the way she says the name of a saint. It has a cost every night Tobias lies in that cellar growing older in the dark while the man who put him there sleeps above the harbor with the whole island's love around him like a blanket."

Edmund's breath caught. "Put him there?"

"Sit down," Coral said. "This is a long story, and I will only tell it once, and I will not be interrupted."

* * *

She told it standing, because sitting would have felt like surrender.

"My husband Joss was not a smuggler when I married him. He was a fisherman with strong arms and weak arithmetic, and when the pilchard shoals failed in '84, he fell behind on the boat payments, and a man offered him work. You will want me to say it was Penhale. It wasn't. Not then. Penhale never dirtied his hands with the cargo itself. He was cleverer than that. He was the one who made the smuggling *possible*."

She walked to the window. Below, the harbor lay low and gleaming under the scudding clouds, the boats knocking gently against their moorings, the whole scene as peaceful as a painted thing.

"Every cargo that came into this island came past the harbormaster's eyes. Every revenue schedule, every patrol rotation, every night the cutter was due or not due — all of it passed through Aldous Penhale's office. He didn't run contraband. He sold *absence*. A dark night here, a blind eye there, a patrol delayed by a day. And in exchange, a share of everything that landed, laundered clean through the Seamen's Fund and the harbor dues and a dozen little charities that made him beloved. The ring had six men on the water and one man on the shore, and the man on the shore never once touched a keg or a rope."

Edmund had lowered himself onto the bench by the door at some point without seeming to decide to. "The wreck," he said. "The *Providence*."

"The *Providence*." Coral's mouth shaped the name like something bitter. "November of '86. The biggest cargo of the year — brandy, silk, and tobacco out of Roscoff. Four boats went out to bring her in through the channel, because Penhale had told them the revenue cutter was at Falmouth and would not sail till morning. Joss was pilot on the second boat. Tobias was on the third."

Her voice did not break. She had practiced this, in a way, though she had never known it — all those years of saying *the sea took them, the sea took them* had been rehearsal for this single telling.

"But the cutter was not at Falmouth. It was ten minutes off the point, running dark, because someone had tipped them — and the only man who knew the *Providence*'s true night was Aldous Penhale. The revenue men found nothing, you understand. The *Providence* never even sighted them. But Penhale had debts. Gambling debts, from cards in Penzance, and the men he owed them to had begun to ask questions, and the simplest way to erase a debt is to erase the ledger. Four boats went out that night. The storm came early, out of the northwest, the kind of storm Penhale — who had watched these waters for thirty years — should have read an hour before anyone. He read it. I believe he read it and said nothing, because a dead ledger cannot be read by anyone else."

"Four boats," Edmund said softly. "You said four men drowned."

"Four men drowned. Joss. Tom Curnow. The Pascoe brothers. And Tobias — Tobias lived. He washed up on the far shingle with a broken arm and the knowledge of what had happened burning in him like a brand. He went to Penhale, in the first weeks. Told him he knew. And Penhale smiled and poured him brandy and told him that a man with a broken arm and no boat and no witnesses was a man who ought to think very carefully about his family — because Coral, he said, and Wren, they live on an island, don't they. Small island. Lighthouses are lonely work. Accidents happen."

The fire had gone out in the hearth at some point, or perhaps it had never been lit. The room was very cold.

"So Tobias ran," Coral said. "And I hid him. Because the law would have hanged him for the smuggling, and Penhale would have seen to the rest, and there was no one — *no one* — on this island I could tell, because half of them love Penhale and the other half owe him, and the ones who might believe me have children and boats and no wish to be widowed the way I was widowed."

* * *

Silence filled the office like water fills a hold — slowly, then all at once.

Edmund Vane sat with his hands flat on his knees, and Coral watched the meticulous machinery of his mind working behind his eyes, the man who counted things confronting the largest number he had ever been given.

"The inquiry file," he said at last. "In the records room. The names were redacted — all of them, every witness, every statement. I thought it was routine."

"It was Penhale's own hand that drew the ink through them. He conducted the inquiry into his own crime, and the Board sent down a man from London who stayed four days, dined at the Penhale house twice, and went home satisfied. That is what justice looks like on Gullwrack Island, Edmund. It looks like whatever Aldous Penhale says it looks like."

"And the men who owed him the gambling debts — they're gone?"

"Dead or paid. Penhale is thorough. It's what makes him a good harbormaster." She said it without heat, and the absence of heat was worse than rage. "That is the trap of him, you see. He *is* a good harbormaster. He has saved drowning men with his own hands. He built the lifeboat station out of his own purse. He is the best man this island has, and he is also the man who fed my husband to the sea to settle a card debt, and both of those things are true at once, and I have lived inside that contradiction for seven years and it has eaten me hollow."

She turned at last to face him fully.

"So now you have it. The whole ledger. Joss smuggled, and I knew it, and I said nothing. Tobias survived, and I hid him, and I said nothing. And Aldous Penhale — beloved Aldous Penhale, the saint of Gullwrack — murdered four men with a weather report and a lie, and I am the only living soul who can prove it, and the price of saying so is this island's heart torn out of its chest."

Edmund rose slowly. "The cutter's captain. You made a bargain with him."

"I offered him a name in exchange for Tobias's safe passage. I have two days to give it." Her jaw tightened. "I came here not for your permission, Edmund Vane. I came because you asked me your question, and because — God help me — you are the only person on this island who is not Penhale's, and I needed one pair of ears that did not love him before I said it aloud."

"And now you've said it."

"Now I've said it." She laughed, and there was no joy in it, only a strange lightness, like a rope parting. "Do you know what I thought would happen? I thought the words would choke me. Seven years I have carried them, and I thought they were lodged in my throat like a fishbone. But they came out easy. That frightens me more than anything. That it should be *easy* to destroy a man."

"It isn't destroying a man," Edmund said quietly. "It's naming what he did."

"Isn't it the same thing, here?" She gestured at the window, the harbor, the sleeping island. "Look at them down there. The Sowdens, who named their boy Aldous. Mrs. Tregenna, who has baked his birthday loaf for twenty years. The lifeboat crew who would follow him into hell and have. You don't destroy a man like that, Edmund. You destroy a *town*. You take the one good thing everyone agrees on and you break it in their hands, and nothing on this island will ever be whole again — and the worst of it, the very worst of it, is that they'll hate *me* for it. Not him. Me. The widow who spoke ill of a saint."

"Then don't speak it," Edmund said. "Take the berth on the Meridian. Take Wren. Let the sea keep its dead and let the past keep its silence, and live."

Coral looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the wind had begun to rise, worrying at the shutters of the office, and somewhere down the quay a gull screamed once and was silenced.

"If I stay silent," she said, "then Penhale wins twice. He took my husband, and he takes the truth, and Wren grows up lighting a candle to her father's murderer every Penhale Day with a garland on the statue. No." She shook her head, and something in her face settled, the way the sea settles after a long swell finally makes up its mind. "I have done my silence. I have paid its wages every day for seven years. The captain gets his name before the morning tide."

* * *

She was at the door when Edmund spoke again, and something in his voice stopped her hand on the latch.

"Coral. If you give the captain that name — Penhale will know within the hour. The Meridian's crew drinks at the Ship Inn. The captain's clerk plays cards with Penhale's nephew. You cannot say a word in that harbor that the harbor does not hear."

She stood very still.

"I know," she said. "I've known since I made the bargain. Whatever I tell that captain, I must assume Aldous Penhale is standing behind his shoulder, listening. Which means that the moment I speak his name, I had best be ready for what he does next." She pulled the hood of her coat up against the wind's rising moan. "He has drowned four men to protect himself, Edmund. I have no doubt at all what he'll try to do with one widow, one child, and one fugitive in a cellar — especially with the great storm coming, and a night coming after it when no one on this island will be able to see what happens at the lighthouse."

She opened the door into the wind.

"But he has made one mistake," she said. "He thinks I am what I have always seemed. A quiet woman. A keeper of silences." Her eyes were grey as the sea and just as unquiet. "He is about to learn what a lighthouse keeper does with the dark."

She went out, and the wind took her coat, and far down the harbor, in the window of the harbormaster's house above the quay, a light that had been burning late went suddenly out.
