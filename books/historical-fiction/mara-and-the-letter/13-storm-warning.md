## 13. Storm Warning

The barometer had been falling since Tuesday, and by Thursday morning the needle had slid into the red and stayed there like something drowning.

Wren Ashgrove knew the instrument better than she knew her own handwriting. It hung in the passage between the kitchen and the stair, a brass-cased circle of glass her father had polished every Sunday until his hands stopped being able to polish anything. Her mother checked it now with the same ritual gravity — two fingers on the frame, a tilt of the head, a small silence that meant *I am doing arithmetic I do not like.* On Thursday morning the silence lasted longer than usual.

"Fetch my oilskins down from the peg," Mama said, not turning from the glass. "And yours."

"It's going to be bad." It wasn't a question. Wren had learned that you didn't ask her mother questions about weather; you stated your conclusions and waited to be corrected.

"The worst of the year, I should think. Maybe the worst in twenty years, if Mr. Penhale's knees can be believed." Mama's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling when she was frightened — a flat press, like sealing an envelope. "The Meridian's crew is striking their awnings. Captain Rooke sent a boy up at first light. They mean to stand off in the roads rather than risk the harbor mouth."

Wren looked out the kitchen window past the rain-streaked glass. Down the cliff, the cutter sat grey on grey water, her masts swaying against a sky the color of unwashed wool. Gulls were coming inland in long ragged lines, which was another kind of barometer, the kind that never lied. The sea beyond the point was white already, and the storm proper hadn't even arrived.

"Mama." She made her voice casual the way you made bread dough — carefully, so it didn't tear. "Should I tell Uncle Toby?"

The word had been out of her mouth for three weeks now, ever since the night she'd finally said it aloud in this very kitchen, standing over the bread she'd been leaving on the cellar stairs since midsummer. Saying it had changed nothing in the house and everything in her. Her mother had gone very still, and then very quiet, and then she had cried — only a little, only for a moment, in a way that made Wren feel older than eleven and younger than six all at once.

Mama turned from the barometer. The lamplight caught the grey of her eyes.

"Yes," she said. "Go down and tell him. Tell him the glass has fallen two inches since yesterday and the wind's backing south-sou'west. Tell him if the light gallery takes damage I'll need him above, and if it doesn't, he stays below no matter what he hears — no matter what falls, no matter what breaks. Can you remember all that?"

"I remember everything."

"I know you do." Something crossed her mother's face then, quick as a tern. Pride, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Take the lamp. And Wren—" She caught her daughter's shoulder at the door, and her hand was cold from the window-glass. "Whatever he says to you, whatever he looks like down there in the dark after all this time — you come straight back up when you're done. You hear me?"

"He's not frightening, Mama."

"You don't know that."

"I do, though." Wren said it simply, because it was simply true, and watched her mother absorb it the way stone absorbs rain — slowly, and all the way through. "I've been leaving him bread since July. He folds the cloth back over the plate when he's done. Neat corners. Ghosts don't fold napkins."

* * *

The cellar door lived behind the coat pegs, and you had to know it was there. Wren had known since she was eight, when she'd noticed the floorboards by the third peg were worn paler than the rest, scuffed in a way that said *feet, often, going down.* Children notice things. Adults forget that children notice things, and that forgetting is how children survive.

She took the lamp down the steps one at a time, the way she'd done a hundred nights in imagination and never once in fact. The stairs were stone, sweating cold, and the air changed around the fourth step — warmer, closer, smelling of tallow and old woodsmoke and a man. That was the thing nobody told you about people: they left weather of their own. She'd smelled her father in this house for years after the sea took him, in his coat, in the creases of the settle. Tobias's weather was different but just as real. Tobacco he shouldn't have had and somehow did. Tar. Underneath it all, faintly, the same salt-and-soap smell that clung to Mama's collar, because blood keeps its own accounts.

He was sitting at the bottom with his back against the wall and his legs stretched out, mending a net — her father's net, she realized, the big trammel with the cork floats, the one that had hung in the loft gathering dust for seven years until sometime this autumn it began disappearing piece by piece and coming back whole. His hands went still when the lamplight found him.

"Wren." Not *who's there*, not *stay back*. Just her name, said low, like setting down something fragile.

"It's me." She came down the last steps before her courage could reconsider. Up close he was thinner than the shadow-shape she'd built out of coughs and footfalls and neat-cornered napkins. His beard was going grey at the jaw. His eyes were her mother's eyes — grey, unquiet — set in a face that was otherwise her father's, and that was the strangest thing of all, seeing the dead man's brow move on a living man, furrowing at her now with worry.

"You shouldn't be down here."

"Mama sent me." She crouched, set the lamp on the step between them, and delivered her message exactly as instructed: the glass fallen two inches, the wind backing, the gallery contingency, the stay-below-no-matter-what. He listened the way sailors listen, eyes half-shut, lips moving faintly around the numbers.

"Two inches," he said at last. He looked up at the underside of the stairs as though he could see through them to the sky. "Your mother kept a good glass. Your father's, was it?"

"Everything here was Papa's."

Something moved through his face like a current under ice. He gathered the net into his lap and began winding it again, though his hands had gone clumsy, and Wren understood suddenly that he was winding it to have somewhere to put himself.

"You're not afraid," he said. It wasn't quite a question either.

"No."

"Most folk would be. A man living under their floor two years. A man they were told was dead — well. Never told, were you. Just not told otherwise." His laugh came out wrong, more cough than laugh, and he pressed the back of his hand to his mouth. "I heard you on the stairs sometimes. Singing. You've your grandmother's ear for it."

"You heard everything?"

"Enough. Enough to know you were safe, which is all I needed." He set the net down. In the lamplight his hands were enormous and gentle, scarred across the knuckles, rope-marked. Hands that had hauled nets beside her father once, she thought. Hands that had been on the water the night four men drowned. She didn't know how she knew that, but she knew it the way she knew the barometer's needle, without being taught. "Wren. There's things you'll hear someday about what happened out there. About me and your father and the men who didn't come home. When you hear them, I want you to remember—"

"I don't need to remember anything yet." She said it firmly, the way Mama said things to Captain Rooke. "You're my uncle. Mama says family isn't what you're born to, it's what you choose twice — once when it costs you and once when it costs more." She paused. "She said that about you. Last week. She doesn't know I heard."

Tobias looked at her for a long time. The lamp flame stood very still between them, the way flames do when the air itself is holding its breath, waiting for a storm.

"She always did talk too much," he said finally, and his voice cracked clean through the middle of it, "for a woman who lives on silences." He reached out — slow, giving her all the time in the world to pull back — and touched her hair, once, the way you touch a thing you've decided to believe in. "All right, then. Niece."

The word settled over Wren like a shawl. She hadn't known she'd been cold.

* * *

They climbed the last hour of daylight together in the lantern room, because Mama broke her own rule the way she broke every rule that mattered — deliberately, and with her chin up. Tobias wouldn't go near the gallery rail where a watcher might see him, but he worked the winch room below the lens, greasing the clockwork and testing the spare mantle, and Wren ran the errands between floors: oil can, matches, the spare fog signal key. Three people moving through the lighthouse like parts of one machine. Like a family that had finally stopped pretending to be one person short.

Outside, the world was ending politely, in advance. The horizon had gone the color of pewter and the sea had begun making sounds Wren had no names for — deep, conversational groans, as if something vast were turning over in its sleep beneath the water. The first gusts came at dusk and hit the tower hard enough to make the glass sing.

By full dark the barometer needle had fallen off the edge of the scale entirely, pinned against the stop like a bird hitting a window, and Mama stood looking at it with her envelope-mouth and said, quietly, to no one, "Now we find out what we are."

At nine o'clock the wind arrived properly.

It came out of the south-sou'west exactly as forecast, and it did not blow at the lighthouse so much as besiege it — a sustained, furious weight that leaned on the walls and hunted down the chimneys and set the whole tower humming a low note Wren felt in her teeth. Rain came sideways, freighted with spray torn off the crests half a mile out. Through the lantern glass the world beyond the beam was simply gone; there was the light sweeping its twelve seconds of dark and flash, and there was nothing, and there was the light again.

Wren stayed up. Nobody told her to go to bed, which was its own kind of answer.

Near midnight, hammering rose from below — not the wind. Knuckles. Someone was beating on the lighthouse door in the middle of the worst storm of the century, and Mama went down with the lamp held high and a boat-hook in her other hand, and Wren followed on the stairs because following was what she did now.

Through the door, half-drowned in wind, a voice she knew: Edmund Vane, the census man, soaked black to the skin, hatless, specters of rain streaming off his spectacles.

"Mrs. Ashgrove — forgive the hour — the harbormaster's raised men — they're coming up tonight, Mrs. Ashgrove, tonight, in this—" He had to stop and fight for breath against the gale. "He's sworn out a search warrant. He says there's a fugitive in this house. He means to prove it before morning tide."

Behind Wren, at the top of the cellar stairs, in the dark where the lamplight couldn't reach, she heard the smallest sound in the world: a door easing shut, and a bolt drawn soft as a prayer.

Mama's hand found her daughter's shoulder and gripped it, steady as iron, steady as a lighthouse.

"Then they'd best hurry," Coral Ashgrove said to the storm. "The tide won't wait for any of us."
