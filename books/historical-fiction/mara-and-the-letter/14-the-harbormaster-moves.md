## 14. The Harbormaster Moves

Edmund Vane had counted things his whole life, and he had learned this much: a number was never only a number. It was a door, and behind it stood whatever the counter wished to keep there.

He was standing in the harbor office at half past four in the afternoon, copying figures into his field ledger by the light of a guttering oil lamp, when the door opened without a knock.

Harbormaster Josiah Penhale filled the doorway the way weather fills a window. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, silver-bearded, famous for his laugh and his generosity — he paid for the harvest festival, he'd endowed the school's coal bin, he'd once carried old Mrs. Trevanion two miles through floodwater on his back. The whole island loved him. Edmund had noted that love in his own private arithmetic, the way he noted everything: it was an asset, and assets could be spent.

"Mr. Vane." Penhale did not laugh now. "You'll walk with me."

It was not phrased as a request. Edmund capped his ink bottle, blotted the page with care, and followed him out into the wind.

The wind had changed since morning. It came off the sea now with a low, grinding note in it, like a millstone turning somewhere beneath the world, and the gulls had gone silent or inland, which was never a good sign. Down in the harbor the fishing fleet rode double-moored, and even from the top of the steps Edmund could see the water climbing the slipway in long, hungry surges.

Penhale led him not to his own office but along the quay to the cutter *Meridian*, warping at her moorings with her rails already shipping spray. A boat put off from her side as they watched — the captain's gig, pulling hard against the chop.

"You've been asking questions," Penhale said. His voice stayed pleasant, the voice of a man discussing pilchard prices, but his eyes were fixed on Edmund with something colder underneath. "At the inn. At the chandlery. About the Ashgrove woman."

"I'm a census taker. Asking questions is my trade."

"And what did your trade find?" Penhale smiled, and it was the worst thing Edmund had seen all day, because it was warm. "Four souls at the light. That's what you wrote. That's what you signed."

"That is what I wrote."

"Mm." Penhale turned to watch the gig come alongside. "Then you'll be interested to know that Mrs. Tregose at the inn remembers you counting on your fingers over your supper like a schoolboy with his sums. And she remembers you going back up to the light the next day, uninvited, alone, and coming down again looking like a man who'd seen a ghost." He paused. "Or the lack of one."

The gig bumped against the cutter's chains. A figure descended the side ladder with the stiff caution of a landsman — the revenue officer, Lieutenant Harker, come ashore ahead of the storm. He crossed the wet stones toward them, oilskin snapping.

"Harbormaster," Harker said. "You sent word you had information bearing on our inquiry."

"I have better than information. I have a falsified government document." Penhale produced a folded paper from inside his coat — Edmund recognized the cream stock of the census forms with a lurch that felt almost physical. It was a duplicate, one of the copies left with the parish clerk. Someone had copied it out in a fair hand. "Household of the lighthouse keeper, Coral Ashgrove. Four souls. But my harbor accounts show lamp oil drawn for five men's consumption, winter before last. Groceries ordered up the cliff path in quantities no widow and child eat. And seven years ago four smugglers drowned on the Gull Rock, and the inquiry closed with one man unaccounted for — Tobias Ashgrove, brother to the keeper's dead husband. Never found. Never buried." He let that sit in the salt air. "Never drowned either, I'd wager."

Edmund kept his face still. Inside, something turned over slowly, like a stone in a tide.

"You've done considerable research," Harker said.

"I keep accounts, Lieutenant. It's my trade." Penhale's smile widened a fraction. "And I'll tell you what my accounts say about Mr. Vane here: he wrote four souls when his own questions told him otherwise. Which makes him either a fool or a party to concealment. Either way, his census isn't worth the paper."

"The census is a matter for the Registrar-General," Edmund said quietly. "Not for you."

"Everything on this island is a matter for me." For the first time Penhale's voice dropped its warmth entirely, and Edmund saw the man beneath the benevolence — saw him plainly, the way you see the reef when the tide goes out. "There's a fugitive living off government lamp oil while honest fishermen pay their duties, and a government clerk helping hide him. If you won't search the light tonight, I'll swear the information before the justice in the morning and raise the island myself. Every fisherman with a boat and a grudge will climb that path, and we'll see what's found. I'd hoped to spare the lady the indignity." He shrugged, vast and terrible. "But storms don't wait, and neither do I."

* * *

They argued in the cutter's cabin for nearly an hour, while the barometer needle crept down and down and the ship began to pitch at her moorings.

Harker was younger than Edmund had expected, precise, tired around the eyes. He spread the charts on the table and weighted them with brass instruments and said, "Rising seas make a night search dangerous and a landing doubtful. My orders are discretion until the fugitive's presence is confirmed."

"It's confirmed," Penhale said. "I've confirmed it."

"With inference. Not evidence."

"Inference!" Penhale laughed then, but there was no humor in it. "Lieutenant, I've run this harbor for twenty-two years. I know how much oil a lighthouse burns. I know how many mouths a garden feeds. And I know that woman has been drawing rations for a fifth soul since the spring after the Gull Rock wreck. You want evidence? Put me ashore below the light tonight and I'll bring you a man."

"And if the storm catches your search party on those rocks?"

"Then we'll be no worse than the men who've been drowned waiting for London to finish its paperwork." Penhale leaned both hands on the chart table, and the cabin seemed to shrink around him. "Captain. Mr. Vane. Consider what happens tomorrow if you do nothing. The whole island knows by noon. The woman's dragged before the magistrate with her daughter watching. Is that the mercy you're protecting? Because I can offer you tonight instead — quiet, quick, done before the worst of it."

Edmund looked at the chart. The lighthouse stood on its black headland at the island's western tip, the cliff path a thin penciled thread between the sea and the sky. He had walked it twice. In daylight, in calm, it had been treacherous enough.

"There's another consideration," he heard himself say, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out. "If we land a search party on the headland tonight, in rising seas, and anyone is lost — the inquiry that follows will hang on every decision made in this cabin. Including yours, Harbormaster. Including your insistence. You should be certain your accounts are right, because dead men's relatives are famously poor witnesses, but surviving ones read depositions very carefully."

Penhale turned and looked at him fully for the first time. Something passed across his face — not fear exactly. Recalculation.

"Are you threatening me, Mr. Vane?"

"I'm counting," Edmund said. "It's my trade."

The silence stretched. Outside, the wind rose a full note, and the cutter rolled, and the inkwell skated an inch across the chart table before Harker's hand pinned it.

"Tomorrow at first light, before the tide turns," Harker said at last. "Weather permitting, I'll put a party ashore with a warrant. Tonight the sea decides." He looked at Penhale. "That's the best you'll get from me, Harbormaster. Take it or take your oath to the justice and explain to the Admiralty why you demanded a night landing against my objection."

For a moment Edmund believed Penhale would refuse — would bellow, would invoke names in London, would spend that enormous stored-up goodwill of his like a drunkard spending wages. Instead the harbormaster straightened, resettled his coat, and became once more the beloved pillar of the island, laughing softly at himself, at the weather, at the stubbornness of young lieutenants.

"First light, then," he said. "Though mind—" and here his eyes found Edmund's again, and held them, and there was nothing soft anywhere in them— "the tide waits for no man's paperwork. Nor do I. If anything moves on that headland tonight, Mr. Vane, anything at all, I'll consider your part in it settled."

* * *

Edmund went back up the harbor steps into the teeth of the wind.

The island had begun to shut itself. Shutters banging home, boats hauled high, the inn's windows lamplit and streaked with spray. Above the rooftops the first ragged clouds were coming in off the Atlantic in long gray banners, and beneath them the lighthouse headland stood black against the dying light — and on it, small and steady, the light itself was already lit, sweeping its white arm across the water and gathering it back again.

*One beat too long*, he thought. *Over the word "four." She hesitated, and I counted the hesitation, and I have been counting it ever since.*

He thought of Wren Ashgrove sitting cross-legged on the kitchen floor, teaching him — no, showing him, correcting him, her small fingers quick on the tarred twine — the bowline her uncle had taught her. *Uncle Toby.* She had said it the way children say things, careless and trusting, and he had walked away down the cliff path without writing a word, telling himself that duty and mercy had merely coincided for once.

They were not coinciding anymore. Tomorrow at first light the net closed from the sea; tonight it was closing from the land, in the person of a generous, beloved man who kept accounts of other people's bread.

Unless.

Edmund stopped where the cliff path forked — down to the village, up toward the light. The wind shoved at him like a hand deciding for him. Rain came in the first cold spits, tasting of the deep ocean, of the great storm the barometers had been promising for three days.

He was a meticulous man. He had built his whole gray, careful life on the principle that a thing recorded truly was a thing made safe. And yet he stood now at a fork in a path with a choice on either prong: go down and warn no one and be clean; or go up, in the dark, in the rising sea, and put himself past any cleaning.

*What will you do with what you count?*

She had asked him that. He still didn't know the answer. But he knew he could not let the answer be decided by Josiah Penhale's accounts.

Edmund pulled his hat down, turned up the cliff path, and began to climb.
