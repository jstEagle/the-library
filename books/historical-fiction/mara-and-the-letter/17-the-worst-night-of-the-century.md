## 17. The Worst Night of the Century

The barometer had fallen so far by dusk that Coral stopped pretending it would turn. The needle lay flat against the stop like a bird pressed under a stone, and the air itself felt bruised — thick, electric, wrong. She had kept the light through thirty years of gales, first as her father's daughter, then as her husband's wife, and now as no one's anything but the lamp's own servant, and she knew the difference between weather that meant to frighten her and weather that meant to kill her.

This was the second kind.

She worked the wick up to full brilliance while the glass in the lantern room trembled with the wind's first long howl off the Atlantic. Below her, the tower hummed like a struck bell. Wren had sailed on the morning tide — the image of it rose unbidden, her daughter small and straight-backed at the rail, Tobias's compass clutched in both hands — and the house below was empty of everything except Tobias, who had come up from the cellar at her word and now stood in the service stairwell with an oil can in his hand as though two years of hiding could be set aside like a coat.

"You should go back down," she said, not turning from the lens.

"And you should have four hands," he said. "We'll call it even."

He looked older than thirty-four. The cellar had bleached him — skin pale as fish-belly, hair gone grey at the temples, eyes that flinched from lamplight before remembering there was nothing left to hide from tonight. He had decided, he told her that afternoon, that if they were coming for him he would not be found crouched in the dark among the coal. She had not argued. There was no time left for arguing, only for work, and the storm gave them both something better than courage: it gave them tasks.

At half past eight the sea began to come over the island. Not spray — *sea*, whole green tons of it, torn loose and thrown against the cliff below the tower so that the rock shuddered under her feet like a deck. The rain arrived horizontally, driving past the glass in white ropes, and beyond it the world simply ended. No horizon. No water line. Only blackness and the great slow sweep of the beam going out into it, again and again, like a hand reaching into a locked room.

* * *

Edmund Vane stood at the base of the cliff path with nine men behind him and understood, with a clarity he had never once achieved over his ledgers, that he was about to do either the most honest or the most dishonest thing of his life.

The harbormaster had assembled the party himself — revenue men, two constables borrowed from the mainland packet, and Edmund, because it was his census, his arithmetic, his error that had put the idea of a fourth soul in their heads. Penhale had said the word *falsified* twice, loudly, in front of everyone, and each time his eyes had found Edmund's face with the satisfaction of a man setting a trap he believes already sprung.

"Count them out," Penhale shouted now, against the wind. "Before we climb! Nine men plus myself plus you — eleven. I want eleven coming down that path tomorrow morning, Vane. I want your numbers exact for once."

Eleven, thought Edmund. Eleven going up.

His notebook was in his breast pocket, dry inside its oilcloth, and his pencil was in his hand because his hand did not know what else to do in weather like this except count things. That was all he had ever been. A man who counted things. It had been his pride once — that the world might be chaotic everywhere else but between the covers of his book every soul sat where it belonged, one to a line, no more and no fewer.

Coral Ashgrove had asked him what he would do with what he counted. He had told her he didn't know whether accuracy was the same as truth, and he had believed the question open. Standing here, with the wind trying to peel his coat from his back and the lighthouse beam sweeping overhead like the eye of God, he found the question answered. Some truths were not sums. Some truths were a woman keeping a light burning above a brother who had watched four men drown and had carried it, alone, in a hole in the ground, for two years — while the man who had actually sent those men to their deaths slept warm in the harbor office with his name on a brass plate.

The harbormaster let the four drown, Coral had said. To erase his debts.

And Penhale had demanded this search be made *tonight*, in rising seas, when any reasonable officer would have waited for dawn — demanded it before Coral's evidence could reach the cutter's captain in writing, before the tide of truth could come in behind the tide of water.

They started up the path. The wind hit them sideways at the first turn and took the breath out of the party as one body; Edmund bent into it and climbed, boots skidding on wet granite, and somewhere below him a man cried out and was hauled back from the edge by his collar. Above, impossibly, steadily, the light turned. Through the roar he could feel it more than see it — a pulse of brightness crossing the rain every few seconds, patient as a heartbeat.

*She's up there,* he thought. *Alone with the wind, she is doing the one useful thing on this island tonight, and we are climbing to drag her family out of it.*

* * *

The lamp required her whole attention and gave her, in return, a strange mercy: there was no room in the work for fear. The clockwork rotation needed winding every forty minutes and the mechanism was fighting the vibration of the storm, gears shuddering, and twice she had to hold the winding crank with her whole weight while Tobias braced the housing. Water had found its way past the glazing bars and ran down the inside of the lantern glass in silver threads. Once, a wave struck the tower's seaward face so hard the whole structure rang and a pane high in the dome cracked from corner to corner with a sound like ice giving way — and Coral stood under it, arms moving, hands steady, trimming the wick as though it were a quiet Tuesday.

"The board will replace it," Tobias shouted, meaning the glass.

"The board can replace *me*," she shouted back, "if the light goes dark tonight. Nothing else matters."

It was true and she knew it was truer than it had ever been. Somewhere out in that blackness were ships. There were always ships in weather like this — fools and heroes running for harbor, and the harbor was a lee shore tonight, and every life aboard them hung on the rotation of this lamp and on nothing else. Let them come with their warrants and their handcuffs. Let them stand in her lantern room dripping seawater and read their papers aloud. She would keep trimming the wick while they did it.

But when the knock came — hammering on the door at the tower's base, faint beneath the storm, unmistakable all the same — her stomach dropped anyway, seven years of dread arriving all at once like water finding its level.

"They're here," Tobias said. His face in the lamplight had gone the color of the sailcloth. "Coral. Whatever happens—"

"Don't." She gripped his wrist. His pulse hammered against her fingers, fast as the clockwork. "Whatever they say, whatever they promise — you say nothing. You are my late husband's cousin from Falmouth, come to help with the storm, and you have been here since yesterday, and I will swear to it until my mouth fills with salt. Do you hear me?"

He heard her. She saw in his face that he intended to break her word the moment it became necessary — that he had spent two years in the dark deciding exactly what he would trade himself for, and that she was standing in the list. But there was no time. Boots on the stairs, many boots, and voices, and then the lantern room door burst inward on a fistful of rain and lantern-light and men.

Penhale entered the way he did everything, as though the world owed him right of way. Behind him: the revenue lieutenant, gray-faced with cold; two constables; three local men pressed into service who could not meet Coral's eye; and last, hatless, notebook clutched to his chest, Edmund Vane, looking like a man being marched to his own execution and knowing precisely which side of it he stood on.

"Mrs. Ashgrove." Penhale had to shout, and shouting gave his voice a coarse triumph it would not have had in calm air. "By authority of the Customs Board, this premises will be searched. Every room, every outbuilding, every—" his eyes glittered "—*cellar*. And every soul within will be counted. You'll oblige us by telling me how many souls dwell at the light, Mrs. Ashgrove. Take your time. I know how you enjoy taking your time."

* * *

Edmund stood at the edge of the lantern room with the rain running down his collar and watched Coral Ashgrove refuse to stop working.

That was the thing he would remember afterward, when everything else had blurred into noise: her hands. While Penhale thundered, she moved along the lamp with her polishing cloth and her scissors, trimming the wick's carbonized edge, adjusting the feed, and the great lens turned above them all, throwing its six-second flash across the party's faces — light, dark, light — so that the whole confrontation seemed to happen in a series of photographs, each one printed on Edmund's memory forever. Penhale mid-shout, mouth wide. The constables huddled. The lieutenant staring up into the rotating optic like a man hypnotized. And Coral, upright, unhurried, terrible in her calm, tending the flame as though the fate of nations were a lesser business than the length of a wick.

"Two souls dwell at the light," she said, when she chose to answer. "Myself and my husband's cousin, Mr. Tresidder of Falmouth, who came yesterday to help me through the blow. You may search the cellar to the last stone. You'll find coal, and you'll find the wine my husband laid down, and you'll find nothing else, because there is nothing else."

"Tresidder." Penhale smiled without warmth. "Convenient. And where does Mr. Tresidder sleep?"

"In the house. Where any Christian sleeps."

"Then he'll be glad to say so himself. Mr. Tresidder!" Penhale's voice filled the lantern room. "Step forward, sir, and give the officers your name!"

And Edmund saw Tobias move — saw him square his shoulders and lift his chin and prepare, with a kind of ruined dignity, to lie badly and be taken — and something in Edmund's chest went very quiet and very clear, the way it did when a column finally balanced.

Because that was when the wave struck.

It came without warning, a mountain of water hitting the tower's base fifty feet below, and the whole structure lurched — actually *moved*, stone groaning, the lamp's mantle guttering — and in the same instant every lantern the searchers carried went out, drowned by spray through the broken pane, and the room plunged into darkness broken only by the great revolving beam, sweeping round and round, six seconds of blinding white and six of absolute night.

"Count them!" Penhale roared from somewhere in the black. "Lieutenant — count your men! Count *everyone*! I'll have heads numbered before we leave this room!"

* * *

Light. Darkness. Light.

In the flashes, Edmund counted. It was the one thing he was made for, and he did it now with the whole of his soul.

Flash: Penhale, gripping the rail, mouth still moving. Flash: the lieutenant, soaked, fumbling a match. Flash: Coral at the lamp, one hand flat against the lens frame, riding the tower's sway like a sailor. Flash: Tobias Tresidder — tall, pale, standing near the service stair with nowhere left to hide and no need of hiding, because in this strobing chaos a face was only a face for one-sixth of every twelve seconds, and men in terror do not study faces, they count *heads*.

"Eight!" the lieutenant bawled, striking his match alive. "I make eight souls in this room, Captain!"

"There were eleven of us on the path!" Penhale shrieked. "Three lost on the climb, they've sheltered below — count the *room*, damn you, count it properly!"

"I've counted it twice, sir! Eight!"

And Edmund, standing in the dark between the flashes with his notebook pressed unseen against his chest, did the quiet treason of his life.

There were ten in the room. Himself, Penhale, the lieutenant, four constables and pressed men, Coral, and Tobias. Ten. He had counted them in the first three flashes with the effortless precision that had won him his commission, the gift that had made him a census man, the curse that had brought him up this cliff in the teeth of the century's worst storm.

When Penhale seized his arm and dragged him toward the stairwell, screaming that they would search the cellar floor by floor and count every living thing between the lamp and the rock, Edmund raised his pencil and his voice together and gave the number that would save them all.

"Ten souls in the tower!" he shouted into the wind. "Ten accounted for — myself included, the household's two, and the party's seven! The arithmetic closes, Captain! There is no one else in this building!"

He wrote it in his book as he said it, in his small exact hand, because a lie written down becomes a fact, and facts were the only country he had ever held citizenship in. *Ten souls.* He felt the words leave him like something warm leaving his body in the cold.

Below them, the search went down through the house — through the kitchen where the extra bowl still dried by the sink, through Wren's empty room with its bed made tight as a drum, down, down, toward the cellar door. Edmund descended with them, holding his lantern low, counting aloud at each landing in a voice like a clerk reading scripture, and behind his steady voice his heart performed the strangest arithmetic of his life: that he had spent forty years believing the world was saved by getting the numbers right, and had climbed a storm-drowned staircase tonight to learn it was sometimes saved by getting them wrong.

The cellar door stood open at the bottom of the stair. Cold air breathed up out of it, smelling of coal and salt and old wine.

"After you, Mr. Vane," said Penhale, and thrust his lantern forward into the dark.
