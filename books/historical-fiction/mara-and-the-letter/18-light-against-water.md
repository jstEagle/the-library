## 18. Light Against Water

The storm did not break so much as arrive all at once, like a door flung open in the dark.

Coral felt it before she heard it — a change in the pressure of the wind against the tower, a shudder through the iron of the gallery rail that had nothing to do with the lamp's turning. She had kept this light through seven years of gales. She knew its moods the way she had once known her husband's: the sulks, the feints, the long grinding patience of a storm that meant to stay a week. This was none of those. This was the storm the old fishermen named and crossed themselves over, the one they said came once in a man's lifetime if he was unlucky.

She clipped the safety line to her waist and went out onto the gallery to check the ventilators, because routine was the only prayer she trusted. The wind took her breath and gave it back sideways. Below, the sea had gone the color of wet slate, moving in hills rather than waves, and the spray was coming over the rock in sheets that fell as rain on the far side of the tower.

That was when she saw it.

A light where no light should be — not the cutter riding at anchor in the lee, not the harbor's fixed green, but a swinging, desperate white, low on the water, north by northeast, coming up on the Teeth.

"No," she said aloud, though there was no one to hear, and then she was running down the spiral stair two steps at a time, one hand on the rail, the lamp turning above her without her, faithful and blind.

* * *

Tobias heard her boots before he saw her face, and knew from the sound alone.

"The Teeth," she said. "There's a ship standing straight onto the Teeth."

He was already pulling his oilskins off their pegs — his oilskins, which had hung in the cellar dark for two years waiting for exactly this, and he had told himself more than once that he would die before he wore them again, because wearing them meant being seen. He shrugged into them now with hands that shook only a little.

"How many aboard?"

"I can't tell. She's small. A coaster, maybe. Thirty tons." Coral grabbed the signal lantern and the spare line box. "Tobias — if you come up those rocks tonight, every soul on this island will know you're alive by morning."

He looked at her. In the lamplight his face was older than his years, seamed with the cellar's damp and the weight of four drowned men, and for a moment she thought he would go back down the stairs and bolt the door behind him, and she would not have blamed him, God help her, she would not have blamed him at all.

"Four men drowned on these rocks because nobody came," he said. "I'm done being nobody."

They went out into the wind together.

* * *

The winch house stood in the lee of the tower's base, a low stone shed built by Coral's husband and his brother in another life, housing the breeches buoy gear and the rocket apparatus that the Board of Trade had issued and never once inspected. Coral had kept it oiled anyway. She had told herself it was habit, diligence, the discipline of a keeper who maintained everything whether it was wanted or not. She had known, in some chamber of herself she did not visit, that she was keeping it for Tobias — that the day might come when the sea put someone on the rocks and the island would need two pairs of hands, and one pair could not do it alone.

Now the day had come, and it had brought the harbormaster's son with it.

She did not know that yet. What she knew was the ship — the *Kestrel*, she could read the name now through the glass as the lightning walked across the water — driving broadside toward the black teeth of rock that guarded the northern approach, her sails in ribbons, her steering gone. The Teeth had taken four men seven years ago. They were patient. They would wait.

"Rocket first," Tobias shouted against the wind. "Line across her forefoot while there's still water under her."

Coral broke open the rocket case. Her fingers, numb with cold, remembered everything: the charge seated, the line coiled clear, the angle judged against the wind's fury. She had fired this apparatus twice in drills, alone, in daylight, with Edmund Vane's census forms folded in her pocket and her whole careful life balanced on a knife-edge of silence. She had never fired it in earnest. She had never fired it with her heart hammering anything but fear.

"Stand clear!"

The rocket went up trailing fire, leaned hard east in the gale, and dropped short, the line vanishing into the trough of a wave. Tobias was already re-coiling. "Again. Higher. She'll drift onto the outer tooth inside ten minutes."

The second rocket climbed into the black and seemed to hang there, pinned against the rain, and then the line paid out and out and caught — caught on the Kestrel's foremast shrouds, sagging, whipping, alive.

"They've got it!" Coral could barely see through the spray, but she saw movement along the ship's rail, men hauling, and then the line went taut and sang like a struck wire.

"Hawser next," Tobias said. "We'll run the buoy across. Get the whip rove."

They worked as they had worked once before, years ago, before the wreck and the lies and the grave — brother-in-law and sister-in-law, keeper and smuggler, two people who knew each other's hands better than their own faces. The hawser went out by whipsend, sagging under its own weight, and the Kestrel's crew bent it to their masthead, and slowly, agonizingly, the breeches buoy began its first crossing, a small canvas shape swinging above water that rose up to touch it and fell away again.

The first man came ashore half-drowned, coughing seawater into Coral's arms, a boy hardly older than Wren would be in ten years. She dragged him up above the surge line, cut him free, and went back for the next.

* * *

They came across one at a time, eleven of them, and Coral lost count of the crossings somewhere past the eighth because counting was a luxury and her arms were burning and the tide was making. Tobias worked the winch with a strength that frightened her, hauling against the full argument of the storm, his face set in the expression she remembered from the old photographs — the look he wore when he was doing the thing he was made for. He had been made for ropes and weather and other people's lives. The cellar had starved him. Tonight he fed.

The ninth man out of the buoy was heavier than the rest, and when Coral got the canvas open he fell into her arms shouting something she couldn't hear, and she bent close, and the lightning chose that moment to stand the whole world white.

She knew the face. Everyone on the island knew the face. It was younger than his father's, softer, but the jaw was the same jaw that had shaken her hand at her husband's funeral and spoken kind words while his father's money lay under the drowned men's silence.

Charles Penhale. The harbormaster's only son.

For one heartbeat — one turn of the lamp above them — Coral held the son of the man who had let four sailors drown to erase his debts, and thought of the berth on the morning boat, and the search party that would climb to this light within hours, and how easy it would be to let the sea have this one back.

Then the boy vomited salt water and gripped her wrist with both hands, and he was just a boy, twenty years old and blue-lipped and terrified, and she hauled him up above the tideline with the same arms she'd used for all the others.

"Two more!" Tobias bellowed. "Two still aboard!"

* * *

The last crossing nearly killed them all.

The Kestrel was breaking up by then; they could hear her timbers going between the gusts, a sound like a great animal in pain. The hawser had parted from her mainmast and hung from the fore alone, and the buoy swung wide on every transit, dipping its canvas skirt into the sea. The tenth man made it across screaming prayers. The eleventh was the captain, who refused to leave until his crew was counted, and counted them himself by lightning-flash, and only then stepped into the buoy with his ship's flag wrapped around his arm.

Halfway across, the hawser parted at the ship's end.

Coral felt it through the winch — a sudden lightness, a wrongness, the way a rope feels when the weight is gone — and Tobias threw himself onto the brake and held, held, held, the drum smoking under his gloved hands, while the buoy swung on the shore-end whip like a bell in an earthquake. The captain swung out over the rocks, over the foam, over forty feet of water that wanted him, and Tobias took up the slack hand over hand, faster than Coral had ever seen any man work, until the buoy kissed the shingle and they dragged the captain out by his collar as the sea reached for his boots.

Then it was done. Eleven men alive on the rock below the lighthouse, and the Kestrel going to pieces in the dark behind them, and the storm still roaring like something cheated.

Coral sat down in the shingle beside Tobias, quite suddenly, her legs refusing further argument. The rain ran off them both in rivers. Above them the lamp turned, and turned, and turned.

"You all right?" she managed.

He laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound she hadn't heard from him in two years. "I haven't been all right since 1886. But yes. Yes, Coral. I'm all right."

* * *

They got the survivors into the boathouse and the oil stove lit, and Coral moved among them with blankets and hot tea made on the spirit burner, working by habit because thinking was still too large an undertaking. Eleven men. A coaster out of Falmouth, bound for Padstow, blown fifty miles off her course. The boy Charles Penhale sat closest to the stove, wrapped in three blankets, watching Coral with the particular intensity of a young man who has recently understood that he owes his life to a stranger.

"You're the lighthouse keeper," he said. His voice was wrecked with salt. "Mrs. Ashgrove."

"I am."

"My father speaks of you. Of your husband." He hesitated. "I'm sorry. I know — about the wreck. About what happened here before."

"Do you." Coral handed him tea and kept her voice level, and marveled distantly at how many years of practice it took to say two flat words.

"He doesn't talk about it. None of them do. But I know my father lost friends that night." The boy looked into his cup. "He's never been the same."

Coral stood very still. Outside, the wind screamed against the tower, and eleven rescued men breathed and steamed and slept, and somewhere below the cliff the sea sorted through the bones of the Kestrel, and Tobias stood in the doorway with the lamplight behind him, listening, his face carefully empty.

Lost friends, the boy had said. That was the story the island had agreed upon — that the harbormaster had mourned the drowned men, that grief had hollowed him, that his hardness afterward was sorrow and not arithmetic. Seven years Coral had bitten her tongue bloody rather than name the truth, and here was the truth's own son, handing her the lie like a gift, believing it with his whole warm heart.

"Yes," she said gently. "It was a terrible night. We all lost things."

* * *

Dawn came gray and shaking, the storm spending itself seaward, and with the dawn came boats from the harbor — the news had traveled ahead of the light, as news does — and men scrambling up the path to the boathouse, and cries of joy, and weeping, and the clasping of hands. And among the first up the path, mud to his knees, coat flying, came Edmund Vane, who had no business being there at all and whose eyes went at once to Tobias Ashgrove standing openly in the doorway of the boathouse with a rescuer's rope still coiled over his shoulder.

Their gazes met across the shingle. Edmund did not reach for his notebook. After a moment he inclined his head, very slightly, the way one man acknowledges another across a room where certain things are understood, and went past to help carry the injured.

By midmorning the whole island knew. By noon the story had a shape, and the shape was this: the widow keeper and her unknown kinsman — for that was the word the fishermen used, *kinsman*, adopting him in a single tide — had saved eleven lives off the Teeth in the worst night in living memory, working the old rocket gear alone, unassisted, magnificent. Men clapped Tobias on the back and asked where he'd learned the apparatus, and he said, "My brother built it," which was true, and no one asked anything else, because the island had decided, and the island's decisions were load-bearing walls.

And Charles Penhale stood on the cliff path telling anyone who would listen that Mrs. Ashgrove had pulled him from the surf with her own hands, and that whatever debts his family owed that household, they were beyond reckoning now.

Beyond reckoning. Coral heard the phrase and almost laughed.

* * *

In the afternoon, with the survivors taken down to the inn and the gear stowed and the winch house locked, Coral climbed the tower one last time and found Tobias in the lantern room, standing where she stood every evening, looking out at a sea gone suddenly gentle, as if it had done nothing at all.

"They'll come tonight or tomorrow," he said quietly. "The search. Penhale won't let it go — not after the census, not after Vane. One rescue doesn't erase a fugitive."

"No," Coral said. "But it changes what they can do about it. You can't hang the man who pulled the harbormaster's son off the rocks. Not publicly. Not on this island. He knows it too — I watched him understand it, standing on the path with his boy's blanket around his shoulders." She paused. "Politics, Tobias. The ugliest lifeboat ever launched."

He smiled faintly, and then it faded. "Wren," he said. "Is she —"

"On the mainland, by now, if the tide served. With the Underhill cousins, and papers, and your compass." Coral's throat closed on the words and she forced them through. "She argued me into it. She said she could carry our story where you couldn't."

Tobias nodded slowly, and turned back to the window, and Coral saw that his eyes were bright and did not pretend otherwise. Two years in a cellar, and he had come up into the worst night of the century and saved eleven men, and the price was that he would not see his niece grow up.

Below them, on the cliff path, figures were climbing. More than two. More than visitors. Coral counted them with a keeper's eye — six, seven, eight — and saw the lanterns, and the uniformed shoulder of a revenue officer, and walking at the front of them, upright and unhappy, a thin man with a notebook in his pocket whom she had last seen turning away down this same path without writing a word.

Edmund Vane had come back. And this time he had brought the law with him.

"Tobias," Coral said. "Whatever happens in the next hour — remember that the light is lit, and the light is ours, and no one takes that from us tonight."

The knock, when it came, echoed up the whole stone spine of the tower.
