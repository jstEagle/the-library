## 19. Morning Tide

Wren had never seen the sea so quiet.

It lay flat as poured pewter, holding the last of the night's darkness in its skin, and the sky above it was the color of a wound just beginning to close — a thin line of grey-gold along the east where the storm had spent itself against the cliffs and finally given up. The gale that had tried to unmake the world was over. In its place there was only this: the hush after, the dripping of the tower stones, the smell of salt and lamp-oil and wet wool, and somewhere below the cliff path, the creak and knock of a boat being made ready.

Her mother's hand was on her shoulder. It had been there all night, it seemed — through the search party tramping up the stair, through Edmund Vane's careful voice counting heads in the dark, through her mother's voice saying words Wren half understood about names and ledgers and a man called Harrowgate who everyone on the island loved and no one would look at now. Through Tobias coming up out of the cellar for the first time in two years, blinking in lamplight like something exhumed, his beard full of grey he hadn't earned honestly.

"Your hair," her mother said now, quietly, working a comb through the tangles the wind had left. "A girl boarding a government cutter should look as though someone loves her."

"Someone does love me."

"I know it." Coral's hands did not stop moving. "That is not what I said."

Wren looked at her in the little mirror propped against the kitchen wall — a mirror with a crack down one side, so that everything reflected in it was slightly doubled, slightly broken, slightly true. Her mother stood behind her, and behind her mother, leaning in the doorway with his cap in his hands like a boy called before the schoolmaster, was Uncle Toby.

He wasn't really her uncle. She'd known that since she was nine, when she'd worked out the arithmetic of years and wrecks and silences and kept it folded away inside herself like a note passed under a desk. He was her father's brother, which made him something else — something without a clean word for it. Family by wreckage. The ghost story she'd fed bread to on the stairs for a whole winter because she'd decided, in the way children decide things, absolutely and without evidence, that ghosts were more likely to be lonely than dangerous.

"You'll want the compass," Tobias said.

He crossed the room and held it out to her on his flat palm, the way you'd hold out a bird you'd rescued — carefully, as if it might startle. It was his father's, and then her father's, and she had seen it maybe four times in her life, always briefly, always in Tobias's hand, its brass case worn bright at the hinge from years of thumbs.

"It points home," Tobias said. "Whatever they tell you a compass is for — north, true north, magnetic deviation, all of it — that's what it's for. It remembers where you started." His voice did something at the end of the sentence that he covered by clearing his throat. "You point it back at us every day, do you hear me? Every single day. Don't let the mainland make you ashamed of pointing it."

Wren took it. It was heavier than it looked, and cold, and the needle inside trembled and settled, trembled and settled, like a small animal deciding whether to trust her.

"I'm not going because I'm scared," she said. This was important to her. She needed it written into the record of the morning, into the stone of it. "I'm going because Mama asked me to carry something."

"Carry what?" her mother asked, though Wren suspected she knew; her mother always knew, which was different from admitting she knew.

"The story," Wren said. "Toby can't carry it. If he goes, they put him in a cell and the story dies in there with him. But nobody stops an eleven-year-old girl from telling a story. They think stories are what we're *for*."

For a moment neither adult spoke. Then Tobias laughed — a short, cracked sound, rusty from disuse, like a winch turning after a long winter — and Coral pulled Wren around and held her hard enough that the cracked mirror lost them both entirely.

* * *

The harbor at dawn was full of people who didn't know how to behave.

That was the thing Wren noticed most, standing on the quay with her one carpet bag — a bag that contained, by her own strict inventory: two dresses, her mother's spare shawl (her mother's own shawl having been put around her shoulders at the door despite protest), the compass, a paper packet of ship's biscuits, a slate pencil, and, hidden in the lining where only she would ever know, the pressed starflower she'd found last summer in the ruin of the old signal station, flat as a saint in a book.

People lined the quay. Mrs. Trevanion from the inn, who had once told her mother that some doors were best left unopened, now pressing a twist of barley sugar into Wren's hand and unable to meet anyone's eyes. Old Pentreath the fisherman, whom Tobias had nearly revealed himself to in the first great storm, standing with his cap off, which Wren had never once seen. The harbormaster's wife, grey-faced, holding her son's arm — the son who had been pulled alive off the rocks three nights ago by two figures working the lighthouse winches in weather that should have killed them, one of those figures a man the whole island had pretended for two years did not exist.

Nobody said *thank you* out loud. That was the Cornish way, Wren understood: gratitude lived in caps removed and eyes lowered and fish left anonymously on doorsteps. But she felt it coming off the crowd like heat off a kiln, and she felt, too, the other thing underneath it — the shame, the collective flinch of a community that had known or guessed or half-known for seven years and chosen its own comfort, and was now watching the cost of that comfort walk down the quay in a child's boots.

The harbormaster himself was not there. Someone said he'd locked himself in the harbor office. Someone else said worse than that, and got elbowed.

At the boat's rail stood Edmund Vane.

He had climbed the tower with the law behind him and then, in the dark, with the storm screaming and eight men counting heads by lantern-light, he had counted wrong. Wren knew this the way she knew most things — sideways, in fragments overheard through floorboards. He had counted three souls where there were four, and written the number down in his beautiful careful hand, and committed, in his own small ink-and-paper way, a crime larger than any Tobias had ever committed, and he had done it with a steady face while the wind tried to take the roof off the lantern room.

Now he stood at the gangway with a ledger under one arm and an expression Wren couldn't read, because grown-ups' faces in moments like this were written in a language children could see but not yet translate.

She stopped in front of him.

"You counted wrong," she said.

"Wren." Her mother's warning voice.

But Edmund crouched down — actually crouched, folding his long clerk's frame onto his heels so their eyes were level, which no grown man on this island had ever bothered to do for her — and said, "I counted exactly what I saw. Three souls. A keeper, her daughter, and no one else in the house at all." He paused. "Census work is very precise. You'd be surprised how much depends on what a man decides is visible."

"That's lying."

"It's arithmetic," Edmund Vane said, "with a conscience." And then, because he was apparently constitutionally incapable of letting anything go uncorrected, he added: "Though technically it was a miscount. There was a great deal of wind."

Wren studied him. He had sand in the creases of his coat and a cut on his cheekbone he hadn't bothered to dress, and he looked, she thought, like a man who had spent a week discovering that the world did not resolve neatly into columns, and who was going to spend the rest of his life paying for the discovery.

"Will you write to her?" she asked. "Mama. When you've finished your forms. She gets terribly few letters."

Something moved across his face like cloud-shadow over water.

"I don't imagine I'll have a post to write from," he said. "Not after this week. But yes. If you give me the address, I will write to your mother if it's the last official act of my career." He straightened up, took out his notebook, and — she watched him do it — wrote down the mainland address of the Underhill cousins in his small exact hand, tore the page free, and handed it not to her but to her mother, as though filing it properly even now. "There. Witnessed and recorded. One address, correctly entered."

* * *

The tide turned at ten minutes past six. Captain Merrick of the revenue cutter *Meridian* — a broad, weathered man with a voice like a foghorn and, Wren had already determined, eyes that missed nothing and disapproved of less than his uniform required — called the boarding in that voice, and the quay began to empty toward the gangway.

Which left only the hardest part, the part Wren had rehearsed all night in the dark of her bed and discovered, standing here in the actual grey morning, that no rehearsal survives contact.

Tobias knelt down in front of her. He smelled of lamp oil and cellar damp and, faintly, of the storm, because he'd spent the worst hours of it hauling on ropes beside her mother saving the harbormaster's son, and the whole island had watched him do it, and somehow that was the thing that had saved him more surely than any pardon could — you could not hang a man the village had watched pull a drowning boy out of the sea. Still, the papers in Captain Merrick's cabin said *conditional*, said *pending*, said *service rendered, sentence commuted*, and conditional was a word that meant the law hadn't finished deciding.

"Right," Tobias said, and his voice was almost steady. "Rules of the road. One: you eat breakfast every morning whether you want it or not. Hunger lies to children. Two: knots — you'll learn new ones over there, and you'll learn them properly, because whoever teaches them will not have my patience—"

"Nobody has your patience."

"—and three." He stopped. All the scaffolding of jokes fell away and left just him, just the face she'd only ever seen in stair-shadow and lamplight and storm, and his hands came up and framed her face the way you'd cup water. "Three: you are an Ashgrove. Whatever name the papers give you over there, whatever ward-of and dependent-of they write in their books — you are Coral Ashgrove's daughter and your father's blood and mine, and there is no tide anywhere in the world that changes it. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"Say it back."

"I am an Ashgrove."

"And?"

"And the light is ours," Wren said, "and no one takes that from us."

Tobias made a sound that was mostly a laugh and mostly not, and then her mother was kneeling too, and the three of them made a knot right there on the quay that no knife aboard any cutter could cut, until Captain Merrick, gently and with the tact of a man who had buried sailors, said, "Missus — the tide won't beg."

They walked her to the gangway. Her mother's promise — *midsummer, if not before; I will come myself; I swear it on the light* — had been given twice more in the night, and once again now, whispered into her hair at the top of the plank, and Wren held it the way she held the compass: tight enough to keep, loose enough to use.

Then she was aboard, and the plank came up, and the quay — her mother on it, Toby's hand raised, the whole shamefaced grateful weeping-is-not-the-Cornish-way crowd of it — began, slowly, to move.

* * *

The island shrinks faster than you think it will.

That was her first real lesson of the sea. Standing at the stern rail with the wind pulling her hair out of its braid, Wren watched the rock she'd been born on contract from a country to a hill to a hump to — unbelievably quickly — a tooth. The lighthouse went last. Of course it did. It was built to be seen from farther than anything else, built to stand above the drowning dark and say *here, here, here* to ships that would otherwise die; and so it stayed visible long after the cottages and the quay and the chapel and the cliff path had all melted into grey stone and then into grey nothing, a white finger against the morning, and Wren understood suddenly why her mother loved it the way she loved it, with her whole spine, and why she had given up her own berth on this boat without hesitating, without appearing even to weigh it. Some people aren't meant to leave the light. Some people *are* the light, and the rest of us are only ships, and ships need something fixed to steer by.

She took out the compass.

The deck rolled, gentle now, the storm's tantrum forgotten. Around her the cutter went about its business — sailors coiling rope, Captain Merrick at the wheel, the mainland a smudge ahead where none existed yesterday. Wren set the brass case flat on the rail and opened the lid, and the needle swung, wobbled, argued with itself, and settled.

It pointed back. Straight back, over her shoulder, at the tooth of rock already sinking into the haze, at the white finger standing on it, at the woman inside the white finger keeping the lamp lit in daylight because that is what keepers do whether anyone needs it or not.

Home, Tobias had said. It remembers where you started.

"Every day," Wren said out loud, to the needle, to the wind, to the disappearing island. "I promise."

The island went.

One moment there was grey stone and a white tower and everything she loved in the world balanced on it; the next there was only sea, the same pewter sea running to the edge of everywhere, and Wren stood at the rail with the compass open in her hands and her chin up and her face dry, because she had decided on the quay that she would not cry where anyone could see, and she was her mother's daughter, and her mother's daughters keep their lamps lit.

She cried only when the island was fully gone — when there was nothing left of it, not even the tower, not even a smudge, when the horizon showed nothing but water and weather — and then, alone at the stern rail with the whole crew politely elsewhere, she let it come. She cried for the cellar stairs and the bread and the coughing in the dark. For seven years of silence her mother had carried like ballast. For a father whose name she'd finally heard spoken aloud last night, forgiven, in a lantern room. For a man who'd taught her knots from a shadow and was now sitting below decks in irons-that-weren't-quite-irons, conditional, pending, at the mercy of men with files.

And then, because she was also eleven, and because grief in a child is a tide and not a wall, it went out again, and left other things glittering behind it.

She dried her face on her mother's shawl. She closed the compass. And she thought about the story she was carrying — the whole weight of it, smugglers and wrecks and a beloved harbormaster who had let four men drown to erase his debts, a keeper who'd lied to a census man and out-stormed a century's worst night, a clerk who'd miscounted on purpose — and she thought about how stories work, how they travel, how a girl with a slate pencil and a good memory could carry one across the sea the way her grandfather had carried the compass, and set it down in new harbors, and watch it spread.

Captain Merrick appeared beside her, offering a biscuit and, with it, gruffly: "You'll want to know what happens to them. Back home, I mean. Most passengers do."

"I know what happens," Wren said, taking the biscuit. "The truth happens. Slowly. Like a tide."

The captain gave her a long look, the kind adults gave her lately and didn't used to, and went back to his wheel.

Behind her, the sea ran smooth all the way to the mainland. Ahead of her, in the growing gold of the morning, the coast was rising out of the haze — green hills, white houses, cousins she'd never met, a life with no cellar in it.

In her pocket, wrapped in its page of a dead man's Bible, the starflower rode flat and patient, waiting for a window box, waiting for spring.

And far behind her, past the curve of the world, a lamp she could no longer see was burning anyway — burning because her mother had lit it, and would go on lighting it, every dusk of every day, until midsummer, if not before.

Wren Ashgrove turned her face to the new land, opened the compass one last time to check the bearing home, and began, silently, to count the days.
