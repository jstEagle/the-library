## 20. The Reckoning

The harbor office smelled of tar and old rain when Edmund Vane came down the cliff path for the last time.

He carried two things: the canvas folio containing the season's census schedules, and a second, thinner packet wrapped in oilcloth — the inquiry file he had taken from this very office six weeks ago without asking leave, and which had sat beneath his bed at the inn ever since like a stone under a mattress that no position of the body could ease. He had told himself at first that he was only borrowing it. He was an honest man; honesty had been his whole life, and even now, with everything about to end, he could not bear to think of himself as a thief. But he knew better. A man who takes a file in order to study it is a scholar. A man who takes it in order to act is something else entirely.

The morning was cold and rinsed clean after three days of storm. Gulls wheeled over the masts, screaming their grievances at the world, and the *Meridian* lay at her moorings with her decks scrubbed and her guns covered, waiting on the tide. On the quay, fishermen were coiling rope and saying very little to one another in the way of men who have survived a common terror and not yet decided what to make of it.

Captain Rennick's clerk let him into the office. The harbormaster's chair stood empty behind its high desk, as it had every morning that week. Josiah Pennock had not come down to the water since the night of the storm. Edmund had watched him from the inn window the evening before, standing alone at his own front gate in the failing light, looking out at the lighthouse the way a man looks at a creditor.

"Is the captain aboard?" Edmund asked.

"Ashore yet, sir. He'll be at the tide table till eight."

"Then I'll wait."

He did not wait in the office. He walked out onto the quay and stood among the fishermen, close enough to hear them, far enough that they did not lower their voices on his account — or perhaps they simply did not care anymore. There comes a point in the affairs of small places when secrecy stops being worth the labor, and he judged that point had been reached somewhere around dawn, when the harbormaster's son had been carried ashore half-drowned and set down on these same stones while his father stood back from the crowd with his hands behind his back like a man at someone else's funeral.

"—told the boy himself, that's what I heard. Told him there'd be no pilot boat out in weather like that and he'd best stay in port, and then took his fee anyway—"

"—my cousin says the ledgers don't lie. Four boats paid pilotage that month and only three recorded—"

"—seven years, mind. Seven years we said nothing, because who were we to say? And now a woman kept the light through the worst night God ever sent and saved a dozen men including his own son, and he'd have sent soldiers up that rock to drag her family out by the hair—"

They fell quiet as Edmund passed, but not guilty-quiet. It was the silence of men who have finished a calculation and are waiting to see if anyone else has done the sums.

Edmund walked to the end of the quay and looked at the lighthouse.

It stood white against the scoured sky, its glass flashing with terrible patience, and he found that he could not look at it the way he had looked at it in June, when it had been merely a structure on a schedule, a household of four souls — or was it five? — a line in a ledger awaiting his pen. He thought of Wren Ashgrove sitting on the cellar steps in the lamplight, showing him the bowline knot with her small competent hands, saying, *Uncle Toby says a knot should be able to tell you its own name.* He thought of Coral Ashgrove in the lantern room, salt-crusted and gray-lipped, working the winches beside a man whose face the whole island would know within the week, and neither of them pausing once to consider what the rescue would cost them.

*Four souls,* he thought. *Then three.*

That was the true count. That was what the forms would say when he filled them tonight: four souls dwelling at the light as of the last day of March — keeper, daughter, brother-in-law, and himself, though he had no right to the fourth place and had never claimed it. And then, come morning, when Tobias Ashgrove sailed on the *Meridian* under escort toward a hearing rather than a hanging, three souls, until the pardon came or did not.

He had spent thirty years believing that accuracy was the highest thing a man could offer the world. Count correctly, record truly, sign your name beneath the truth, and you have done your duty whatever else befalls. His father had taught him that over a shopkeeper's counter in Plymouth, tallying bolts of cloth by lamplight. *A man who fudges a figure fudges himself, Edmund, and there's no un-fudging either.*

But his father had never stood on a quay while a child's mother chose between her family members like a surgeon choosing which limb to save. His father had never miscounted heads in a dark stairwell on purpose, feeling each warm body pass him in the wind and calling out *three souls, all present* to a search party full of armed men, committing his first deliberate lie in forty years of life and finding it the easiest words he had ever spoken.

Accuracy, he understood now, was a tool. Truth was what you built with it — or failed to.

* * *

At eight o'clock Captain Rennick came ashore, and Edmund followed him into the harbor office and closed the door.

Rennick was a broad, weathered man with a face like a hatchet that had been left out in the rain too long. He looked at the two packets Edmund laid on the desk and did not touch them.

"You've made your decision, then."

"I have." Edmund's voice was steadier than he felt. "I'll ask you to look at both documents before you say anything."

Rennick opened the census folio first. He read slowly, moving his lips over the figures, and Edmund watched him arrive at the household of the light: *Coral Ashgrove, widow, 34, keeper. Wren Ashgrove, 11. Tobias Ashgrove, 29, brother-in-law.* And beneath, in Edmund's cramped upright hand, the note he had written at midnight by the inn's bad candle:

*Amended schedule. The undersigned certifies the above as the true count of souls resident at this station on the final day of March, having personally verified each entry. My earlier returns for this household were falsified — by me, deliberately, on the night of the great storm, to conceal the third party from a search party acting on the harbormaster's instruction. I make this statement freely and accept whatever consequences follow.*

Rennick looked up. "You're confessing."

"I am."

"To falsifying a government census. To obstructing a lawful search." The captain's voice was flat, giving nothing away. "You understand what that means. You'll be struck off. There may be charges. A man your age, Vane — your whole livelihood—"

"I've considered my livelihood," Edmund said. "I've considered very little else for a fortnight. Look at the other packet."

Rennick unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside lay the old inquiry file, its redactions glaring, and beside it — this was the part Edmund had worked on until four in the morning, until his hand cramped and his eyes burned — eleven pages of his own careful script. He had always been told his hand was beautiful. He hoped it would prove persuasive now, since beauty was the last argument available to him.

"Pilotage ledgers," Rennick murmured, turning pages. "Where did you get—"

"The harbormaster keeps duplicates. So does the customs house at Falmouth; I wrote to them three weeks ago, telling them I was verifying prior-year figures for the census abstract. They sent copies without question. No one questions a census man." He allowed himself the ghost of a smile that died quickly. "It's all there. For seven years, vessels running contraband through the eastern channel paid pilotage fees to this office that were never entered in the public register. The difference — the skim — went somewhere. Follow the dates of the largest discrepancies and they cluster around four shipments in the autumn of '86. Those are the four shipments whose runners drowned on the Carrick rocks in November of that year."

"And the harbormaster—"

"Let the money finish speaking." Edmund leaned forward. "After the wreck, the discrepancies stop. Not taper — stop, overnight, as if a tap had been shut. A man who loses his couriers might grieve them. A man who *needed* them gone doesn't restart the trade. And here—" he turned to the last page, where he had laid the two documents side by side in facsimile, "—is the debt schedule from the Falmouth merchant bank, obtained under the same pretext. Josiah Pennock owed nine hundred pounds to that bank in October of '86. By February of '87 the debt was cleared in full. In cash. The bank records the payment as received from 'a Cornish correspondent.' Nine hundred pounds, arriving precisely when four men who knew where his money came from were dead on the rocks below his own harbor."

The office was silent except for the gulls outside and the creak of the building settling with the tide.

"He drowned them," Rennick said quietly. "Or let them drown. Either way."

"He lured them onto the Carrick with a false signal — that's what Coral Ashgrove believes, and she has reason to know; her husband was one of the runners and confessed it to her before his death. I can't prove the false light. But I can prove the debts, the missing fees, and the sudden wealth. And I can put it in front of a magistrate along with the testimony of Mrs. Ashgrove and her brother-in-law, both of whom are willing to swear to what they know." Edmund straightened. "Which brings me to the price, Captain, so we may speak of it plainly. I want safe conduct for Tobias Ashgrove on today's tide. I want immunity offered — offered, mind, not extorted — for his testimony. And I want it understood that the Ashgroves' concealment of him was done in fear of exactly the man I'm accusing, and ought to weigh accordingly."

"And if the magistrate laughs at you?"

"Then I'll have lost my post for nothing," Edmund said, "but I'll still have filed a true census, and that will have to do."

Rennick studied him for a long moment. Something moved behind the hatchet face — not warmth exactly, but recognition, the look of one solitary man acknowledging another.

"My orders say deliver the fugitive," Rennick said. "They don't say in irons. He can sit in my cabin as a witness under protection, and I'll log it however the paperwork reads best." He gathered the papers and squared their edges with two precise raps on the desk, a gesture so like Edmund's own that it almost made him laugh. "You know what will happen to Pennock before any magistrate sees this, don't you?"

"I suspect."

"There won't be a courtroom. There won't need to be." Rennick stood, taking up his hat. "This island has been carrying him for twenty years because nobody had anything to carry *against* him. Now they'll have it, and the island will do what islands do. Go home, Vane. File your papers wherever they go. You've got maybe an hour before you're famous."

* * *

Edmund did not go home. He walked up through the town instead, past the chandlery and the Pilchard and the churchyard wall where the grass grew silver in the wind, and he saw the reckoning begin — not with officers or writs but with doors.

It began, absurdly, at the baker's. Old Mrs. Treloar stood in her doorway with her arms folded, watching Josiah Pennock cross the square toward his office, and she did not nod to him. She had known the man forty years; she had baked bread for his wedding; and she stood in her doorway and did not nod, and turned her back with a slowness that was somehow louder than shouting. Pennock stopped mid-stride. Edmund, watching from the shadow of the churchyard wall, saw the moment land — saw the harbormaster understand that the arithmetic of the town had changed overnight.

By noon it was everywhere and nowhere. The harbormaster's clerk resigned, citing his health, and carried his box of belongings down the street past a dozen men who touched their caps to *him*. The innkeeper declined, with elaborate regret, to serve Mr. Pennock's table at supper, as the kitchen was short-handed. The churchwardens called an emergency meeting about the roof fund and emerged having also resolved, by unanimous vote, that certain long-standing arrangements regarding the harbor trust deserved review. Nobody accused anybody of anything. Accusation was unnecessary. The facts walked the streets on their own legs now, going door to door faster than any summons could.

Toward evening, Edmund stood again on the quay and watched the community complete its work. Josiah Pennock came down to fetch his boat — the one indulgence, the fast little cutter he kept at the private mooring — and found three fishermen already aboard it, sitting on the thwart with their arms crossed, smoking pipes, in no hurry whatsoever.

"She's needed for harbor work," said one of them, a huge gray-bearded man named Curnow whom Edmund recognized from the rescue night. "Harbor trust property, see. Trust business. You'll want to raise it at the next meeting."

"That boat is mine—"

"Records say otherwise, I reckon," said Curnow comfortably. "And records don't lie. Isn't that right, Mr. Vane?"

Every head turned to Edmund. He had not intended to be drawn in; he had intended, all his life, to stand at the edge of such scenes with his notebook. But he heard his own voice answer, clear across the water:

"The records are being corrected as we speak."

Pennock looked at him then — really looked, the first time in the whole affair — and Edmund saw in the man's face not rage but a kind of appalled vertigo, the expression of a man discovering that the ground he had stood on for twenty years had been water all along. Then the harbormaster straightened his coat, turned on his heel, and walked back up the street alone, and the whole length of the quay watched him go, and not one person moved to walk beside him.

Edmund remained at the rail long after, watching the light come up gold on the water. His hands were trembling — delayed, he supposed, the way storms arrive late inland. Thirty years of service, ended between breakfast and supper. No pension worth the name, probably a tribunal, certainly a stain on a record that had been spotless since he was nineteen years old.

And underneath the fear, quiet as ballast, something that felt unbearably like relief.

* * *

He filed the amended census at the district office on the mainland ten days later, traveling over on the mail packet and returning the same afternoon, because he could not bear to be away from the island longer than the errand required. The clerk accepted the schedule without reading it, stamped it, and dropped it in the drawer where truth goes to sleep, and Edmund stood a moment looking at the drawer and thought: *somewhere in that box is the only honest thing I ever did with a pen, and no one will ever open it to admire it.* It seemed right that it should be so.

When he came back up the cliff path in the late light, the lighthouse lamp was already lit, steady and white above the darkening water, and a figure stood at the foot of the tower waiting for him — a small figure with a shawl over her head, holding a lantern of her own.

"We heard the packet come in," Coral Ashgrove said. "Wren's been watching the path since noon. She's set a third place at the table and she won't hear an argument about it." She paused, and the lantern threw her shadow long against the whitewashed wall. "I wanted to say it properly, before I say it in front of her. What you did in that stairwell — and what you did after. We know what it cost you. Tobias knows. He talks about you the way sailors talk about a fair wind."

Edmund found, to his horror, that his throat had closed. He managed, after a moment, "I only corrected a count."

"No," Coral said, with the mild implacability of a woman who had kept a light burning through the worst night of the century. "You counted true. There's all the difference in the world, and you're the only man on this coast who knows how much." She turned toward the door, then looked back at him, and her voice softened into something almost shy. "There's spring coming, Mr. Vane. The board will send its letters soon, good and bad. Whatever they say — there's a place at our table whenever the packet lands. Wren counts it as settled. She counts everything."

She went in. Edmund stood a while longer in the cold, looking up at the lamp, at the beam swinging out over the water where four men had drowned and one had been saved and a town had learned, at last, to add up its own accounts.

Then he climbed the stairs, and sat down at the table where a child had set a third place, and picked up his knife and fork with the strange sensation of a man beginning, at fifty-one, to learn the use of his own hands.

Above them, all night, the light turned.
