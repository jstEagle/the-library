## 1. The Summons

My master used to say that a clockmaker's opinion of history should be the same as his opinion of weather: noted from a window, endured if necessary, and never taken personally. I was twenty-three years old and had not yet learned to take even my own life personally, so you may imagine how seriously I took history — which is to say, not at all. History was something that happened to other people, in other places, at a comfortable remove. It happened in capitals, in wars, in the deaths of men whose portraits hung in schoolrooms. It did not happen to apprentices with ink-stained cuffs and a toolbox that weighed more than their ambitions.

I want that on the record, because everything that followed happened anyway.

The letter had come to the shop in March, smelling of river damp and sealed with wax the color of dried blood. *The master clock at Harrow's Bend Observatory has stopped. Come at once. Bring your tools.* No signature worth trusting, no explanation of why the town's own two repairmen — both of them living, as far as anyone knew — had been passed over for an outsider barely out of his indenture. My master read it twice, set it down, and looked at me the way he looked at a movement that had been dropped down a staircase.

"You'll go," he said.

"Me?"

"The town pays in advance." He slid an envelope across the bench, heavy with coin. "And it pays enough that someone has decided this clock matters more than pride. When money decides things before people do, boy, there is always a reason. Find out what it is, fix what needs fixing, and don't sign anything."

It was, in retrospect, the best advice anyone ever gave me, and I ignored nearly all of it.

* * *

Harrow's Bend sat where the river made up its mind to turn, and the town had arranged itself along the curve like laundry hung out to dry — practical, faded, and slightly resentful of the wind. The steamer let me off at a landing of gray planks at the end of April, though you would never have known it was spring. The air came off the water with an edge on it, and the hills above the town were still wearing their winter brown like a coat nobody had offered to take in.

The observatory stood on the rise above everything, a round stone drum crowned with its shuttered dome, and beside it the tower — square, plain, taller than any church spire in three counties — carrying the master clock that kept time for every court house, train schedule, ferry manifest, and church bell within forty miles. From the landing I could see the tower's face, white as a peeled egg, and I noticed what the whole town must have been noticing for weeks: the hands were wrong. Not stopped-wrong, exactly. They disagreed with each other, the minute hand lagging behind the hour by eleven minutes and some seconds, as though the clock had begun to lie to itself and couldn't keep the story straight.

A clockmaker notices these things the way a doctor notices a limp. I stood on the landing with my box in my hand and felt the first small hook of curiosity set itself under my skin. Curiosity, I have since learned, is the most expensive appetite a man can have. But that morning it cost nothing, and I followed it uphill.

The town watched me go. I don't mean that poetically. I mean that doors opened along the main street as I passed, and faces appeared in them, and none of those faces smiled. A woman sweeping her step swept slower as I went by, her broom pausing mid-stroke, her eyes doing the arithmetic of my face — the shape of the eyes, the color of the hair, the name she'd have heard whispered from the landing to the general store before my boot touched the plank.

Nakamura. The name had traveled ahead of me, as names do in river towns, moving faster than steamers because gossip doesn't need coal.

At the store, buying bread and a twist of tobacco I didn't use but liked to carry, the clerk counted my coins twice and gave me change in the smallest denominations available, as though I might try to counterfeit something with whatever he handed back. When I asked which road led up to the observatory, he pointed with his chin and said, "You'll want the orchard road," and then added, in the tone of a man testing a wire to see if it's live: "Past the Vane property."

"Is that significant?" I asked.

He looked at me the way clerks look at men who ask questions they shouldn't be able to afford to ask. "Everything past the orchard road is significant here," he said, and turned to serve the next customer, who had suddenly become very interested in the price of lamp oil.

So. Cold reception. I filed it away with the other facts of the day — the disagreeing clock hands, the heavy payment, the word *Vane* said like a stone dropped in a well — and climbed.

* * *

The orchard road ran between rows of bare apple trees, old ones, their trunks gnarled into shapes like arthritic hands. Even leafless they had presence, the way old men do in a room. Someone had tended these trees for generations; you could see it in the pruning scars, deliberate and patient, decades of decisions written in wood. I am ashamed to say I didn't think about whose hands had made those cuts. At twenty-three, I thought trees were scenery. It would take me a winter to learn that in this town, trees were testimony.

The observatory door was iron-banded oak, and before I could knock it opened.

The man standing there was sixty-odd, built like a stovepipe, with a beard that had once been red and was now the color of rusted iron left out too long. He wore the black coat of a keeper or a caretaker and the expression of a man who had been waiting for bad news and was disappointed only that it had taken so long.

"Nakamura," he said. Not a question.

"Isandro Nakamura. I'm sent by—"

"I know who sent you. Everyone knows who sent you." He stepped back to admit me, which struck me as gracious until I realized he simply wanted me inside and off his step, where the neighbors could count him fraternizing with me. "Grieve. Observatory keeper. You'll sleep in the east room. The clockroom is through there." He pointed at a low door. "And Nakamura — before you open it, understand what you're being paid for. The clock isn't broken the way clocks break."

"How else do clocks break?"

"They stop meaning what they say." He closed the outer door behind us and shot a bolt I hadn't noticed, thick as my thumb. "That one stopped honest. This one—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "Go and look. Then come tell me what you see. And Isandro." He caught my sleeve, and his grip was stronger than his age suggested. "Whatever you find in there, you tell me first. Not the Vanes. Not the council. Me. Do we understand each other?"

"We understand that everyone in this town wants to speak to me second," I said.

Something moved across his face that might have been approval, or indigestion. He released my sleeve and gestured at the low door again.

* * *

The clockroom smelled the way all clockrooms smell — oil, dust, cold brass, the faint animal warmth of weights and cable — but underneath it ran another smell, older, sweeter: apples. Apples, in a room with no windows that opened and no fruit in sight. I noted it and put it aside, the way you note a strange word in a foreign sentence and promise yourself you'll look it up later. Later, as usual, arrived with interest.

The master clock filled the room the way a body fills a coffin — snugly, finally, with intent. It was a regulator of serious pedigree, a deadbeat escapement behind glass, a pendulum longer than I am tall, weights on cables thick as ship's rigging. Built, according to the maker's plate, by one *E. VANE & SONS*, and finished — this was the odd part, the part that made me crouch and squint — with a second plate beneath the first, smaller, unpolished, almost apologetic, reading only: *FOR THE ORCHARD.*

"For the orchard," I said aloud, to nobody. Clocks are not for orchards. Clocks are for courts and trains and churches and men who wish to bill other men by the hour. An orchard keeps its own time; it is called spring.

Then I opened the case, and I stopped making jokes to myself for quite a while.

The movement was beautiful. That's the first thing, and I want it recorded honestly, because everything ugly that came after grew out of something beautiful. E. Vane had been a craftsman of the first order — the wheelwork was cut clean, the pivots polished to a black shine, the jewels set without a bubble in the oil. And yet the clock had stopped, and the stopping made no sense. The mainspring held tension. The escapement showed wear but not failure. The pendulum hung true. Every component I examined was capable of running, and the whole of them together had quit, the way a committee quits — mysteriously, unanimously, and against its own interest.

I worked until the light failed, then lit the lamp and worked more. Grieve brought me bread and cold ham at some hour and left without speaking, which I was beginning to understand was his version of hospitality. Around midnight I found the first thing that was genuinely wrong, and it was so small I almost laughed.

One gear — a wheel in the going train, third from the escapement — was loose on its arbor. Not worn-loose. *Made*-loose. Its boss had been bored a half-hair oversize and pinned with a taper pin seated just shallowly enough to slip under vibration. Any competent repairman would reseat it, tighten it, and call the job done. I nearly did. But my master's voice was in my ear — *when money decides things before people do, there is always a reason* — and instead of fixing it, I did something no self-respecting apprentice admits to: I took the wheel off entirely and weighed it in my palm.

It was heavier than it should have been. By grams. By very few grams, hidden in the thickness of the webbing, distributed so evenly that only a balance and a suspicious mind would catch it. I turned it under the lamp and saw, in the spoke-work, channels I had taken for decorative fluting. They weren't fluting. They were passages. Something had been built *into* this wheel, or this wheel had been built around something, and the whole gear train of the finest regulator in the county had been arranged — I began counting, heart ticking faster than any escapement — arranged so that this wheel did almost nothing. It turned. It transferred no meaningful force. It existed in the train the way a sleeper exists in a parliament: present, voting, deciding nothing, unless.

Unless.

I sat back on my heels in the lamplight, grease to the elbows, and looked at the great silent machine that had told forty miles of country what time it was for fifty years, and I understood that I had been summoned not to repair a clock but to open a question, and that everyone who had paid for my ticket already knew what the question was except me.

History, I had always maintained, happens elsewhere. To other people. In capitals.

But history, it turns out, can also happen in a gear wheel weighing four hundred grams, in a cold room that smells of apples, in a town that watches a stranger climb its hill and says nothing, because the stranger is cheaper than the truth.

I reseated the wheel — loosely, as found — closed the case, and went to find Grieve.

He was waiting in the hall with his lamp already lit, as though he'd heard my thoughts cross the floorboards. He looked at my face, and whatever he saw there made him nod slowly, once, like a man confirming a diagnosis he'd dreaded.

"Well?" he said.

"There's a wheel in the going train that does nothing," I said. "And it's doing it on purpose."

Grieve closed his eyes. For a moment he looked less like a stovepipe and more like a man carrying something heavy up a long hill for a very long time. "Fifty years," he murmured. "Fifty years it's ticked over our heads, and now it stops, and they send for a boy with a foreign name." He opened his eyes and fixed me with them. "Do you know what an orchard is worth, Nakamura?"

"Bread, cider, shade—"

"Land," he said. "Land on a river, in a valley, with a town growing fat around it. Worth enough that families tear themselves apart over it. Worth enough that a clever man might hide the proof of who owns it somewhere no thief would ever think to look." He lifted his lamp toward the clockroom door. "Somewhere that ticks."

I should have gone home then. I want that understood. There was a steamer on Thursdays; I had advance pay in my pocket; my master wanted nothing from me but a report and my skin intact. A sensible apprentice would have written *mechanical fault, repaired, invoice enclosed* and been on the Thursday boat with his opinions of history comfortably intact.

But the wheel was still turning in my mind, that useless, purposeful wheel, and I have never in my life been able to leave a mechanism alone once it has shown me that it means something.

"I'll need a week," I said. "Possibly two."

Grieve blew out his lamp, and in the dark his voice came dry as paper. "They'll give you two," he said. "The Vanes will give you anything you ask, right up until the moment you actually find something. After that, boy, they'll give you nothing at all — and take plenty."

I went up to the east room and lay down under a quilt that smelled of cedar and river, and through the wall I listened to the great clock not ticking, and the river muttering below, and somewhere between the two sounds I fell asleep without knowing that on the stone steps outside, in the last cold of an April night, a child was walking up the hill toward the only door in town that had ever been bolted against the world.

She would arrive tomorrow. Everything else arrived tomorrow, too.
