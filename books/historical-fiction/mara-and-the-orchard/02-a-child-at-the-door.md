## 2. A Child at the Door

I woke before dawn because a man who has spent his apprenticeship waking before dawn does not stop merely because he has crossed half a province to do it. The room was gray. The river was still muttering its one long complaint. And the clock through the wall was still silent, which by the second morning had begun to feel less like a mechanical failure and more like a held breath.

I dressed in the dark because I did not trust the town's coal and did not yet know where the observatory kept its own. My master, old Ferro, back in the city, used to say that a clockmaker's first duty on arriving anywhere new is to make tea, and his second is to insult the local timekeeping, and I intended to do both in reverse order.

The tower stairs were colder than the rooms below, stone drinking the heat out of my boots. I carried the winding key — a brass crank as long as my forearm, heavier than it looked, the way all important things are — up the spiral in the dark, feeling for each step with my toe like an old woman. At the top, the clockroom opened around me: three faces of the great dial looking out over the town, the river, and the orchard hills beyond; and beneath them, hanging in the gloom like the rib cage of some patient animal, the great weight-driven train of the master clock, stopped mid-sentence.

I stood there a while, I'll admit it, just looking. You can learn a mechanism's temper from how it stops. A clock that dies running forward is sick. A clock that dies deliberately, gears set down neat as cutlery after a meal — that's a clock somebody stopped. This one had died running, weights low, escapement jammed with what felt like grit and grief in equal measure. Somebody had wound it faithfully until they hadn't. There was a story in that, and stories, I reminded myself, belonged to other people. I was here for the wages and the work.

I cranked the winding drum until my shoulders burned. The weights rose. The train shivered. Nothing turned. Fine. Broken clocks are why clockmakers exist; if the thing had worked, they'd have sent me nowhere and paid me nothing, which is the arrangement most towns prefer.

* * *

By full morning I had the movement's front plates off and laid out on wool blankets across the clockroom floor, and I will not pretend I wasn't pleased with myself. There's a particular happiness in a clean disassembly, every wheel in its numbered place, the whole secret of a thing spread out where you can scowl at it. I was deep in the going train, elbow-deep in the century, when the light through the east dial began to go strange — thickening, golding, the way it does before weather or before evening — and I realized I'd missed dinner entirely and possibly lunch too.

That was when I heard the knock.

Not a knock, exactly. More a scrabbling, small and irregular, at the base of the tower door. Wind, I thought. The river ice shifting, maybe; it groans and taps against the pilings at night and carries up the hill like something asking to be let in. I went down anyway, mostly because the alternative was admitting I'd been listening for it.

The door was bolted. I remembered bolting it the night before with a certain satisfaction, the outsider locking himself into his exile. I drew the bolt and pulled, and the April cold came in like a debt collector, and there on the top step, folded up small against the doorpost, was a child.

She was asleep. That was the first absurdity of it — asleep, genuinely asleep, in the last cold of an April night, curled with her knees to her chin and her cheek against the stone as though the step were a bed she'd known for years. She couldn't have been more than six. Seven at the outside. Dark hair cut short and unevenly, the way hair gets cut by someone who cares about the child but not about hair. A coat too big for her, wool, good quality once, now with a torn pocket and a button missing, and pinned to the lapel of that coat —

Pinned to the lapel of that coat was a note.

I want to tell you I understood everything at once. I didn't. What I understood first, with a craftsman's eye, was the pin: a brass pin, the kind used for pinning paper patterns, driven cleanly through wool without tearing it, done by someone with steady hands. Then the note itself, a strip of good rag paper, written on in ink gone brownish at the edges from damp. Three words.

*Forget the face.*

That was all. No name. No instruction. No sum of money tucked beneath, no promise of return, no plea. Just the command, addressed to whoever opened the door, as though the writer knew exactly who would open it and exactly how much trouble those words would save everyone.

I crouched down on the step. The cold went through my trousers and made itself at home in both knees. Up close the child smelled of woodsmoke and horses and, underneath it, faintly, of apples — that sweet dry cellar smell of fruit stored past its season. Her hands were tucked inside the coat. One thumb had worked free and hung loose, and there was a smudge of soot along her jaw like a fingerprint left by someone bigger.

"Hello," I said, which is the traditional opening for negotiating with the impossible.

She didn't wake. Children sleep like the dead when they've decided the world is safe enough to do it, and something about that — that she could sleep here, on a stranger's steps, bolted door and all — told me more than the note did. Whoever had brought her had not dropped her and fled. Someone had sat with her until she slept. Someone had waited for her breathing to go slow and even before walking away down the hill. That takes time. That takes a kind of terrible patience, and I found I was already angry at this unknown person in a way I hadn't been angry at anything since I'd arrived, which, given the town's collective opinion of me, was saying something.

I read the note again. *Forget the face.*

Well. I am many kinds of fool, but I am not the kind who forgets instructions on principle — I follow instructions beautifully, that's practically the whole of an apprenticeship. But you cannot forget a face you haven't properly seen. So I did the only professional thing available: I looked.

Her face was thin and round at once, the way children's faces are, all cheekbone deferred to later. Chapped lips. Lashes down against cheeks. A small white scar through one eyebrow, already old, already healed silver. And under the soot-smudge on her jaw, the beginning of a stubbornness in the set of her mouth even in sleep, the mouth of a person who, at seven years old, would grow up to argue with clocks and win.

I looked, and then I understood the note completely, and I hated it. Because the note wasn't wrong. Forgetting her would be easy. Forgetting her would be the sensible act, the act the town would applaud, the act that kept the observatory quiet and the wages coming and history happening elsewhere, to other people, where it belonged. Whoever wrote those three words knew human nature the way I knew gear trains — intimately, and with contempt.

And I stood there on my own doorstep, freezing, hungry, twenty-three years old and responsible for nothing in the world but a broken clock, and I thought: *no.*

Not nobly. Let's be clear about that. It wasn't charity. It wasn't even curiosity, not yet. It was simpler and worse than either: it was the specific resentment of a craftsman being handed a job he didn't ask for, by someone who assumed he'd refuse it. Nobody tells Isandro Nakamura what to forget. My whole life people had been deciding what to remember and what to erase around my family; I'd crossed half a province to get away from that arithmetic. The note was just one more page of it, written smaller.

"Right," I said aloud, to the child, to the note, to the river. "You're coming inside."

* * *

Getting her inside was a comedy I performed alone, since she slept through all four acts. I couldn't lift her without waking her and couldn't leave her without being the villain of my own story, so I ended up carrying her the way you carry a rolled carpet, awkwardly, apologizing to her unconscious form whenever her head knocked my shoulder. The tower stairs defeated us twice. In the east room I laid her on the daybed under the cedar quilt, and she turned her face into the pillow and sighed like a small banked fire settling, and that was that.

I put water on to boil. I set out bread and the heel of hard cheese I'd carried upriver wrapped in oilcloth, food meant for one pessimist, now stretched across two. And I sat at the table with the note flat in front of me and studied it the way I study any mechanism, because paper and ink are mechanisms too, and every mechanism confesses eventually.

Good rag paper. Expensive, or at least expensive-adjacent — the kind sold to households with a stationery budget, not the kind sold in single sheets to river folk. The ink was iron-gall, common enough, but applied by a hand that wrote quickly and well, no blots, the letters slanted with impatience rather than ignorance. And the phrasing — *forget the face*, imperative, unadorned, no *please*, no explanation — that was the phrasing of someone accustomed to being obeyed. Not cruelly. Efficiently. The way you'd write instructions to a tradesman whose cooperation you'd purchased and whose feelings were not part of the transaction.

Which raised the question of whether my feelings *were* part of the transaction, and what exactly had been purchased, and by whom, and with what. I didn't like any of the answers forming themselves in the backs of my thoughts, so I drank my tea instead and let the questions sit there like tools in a drawer, waiting.

The child slept through the afternoon. I went back up to the clockroom and worked with one ear cocked down the stairwell like a dog's, pretending I wasn't doing it. I got nowhere with the train. Twice I caught myself holding a wheel up to the window and thinking, absurdly, *she should see this* — and twice I put the thought down the way you put down something hot.

Near dusk she cried out in her sleep. Just once, a short sound, the sound of someone surfacing from somewhere bad, and I was down the stairs before I'd decided to move, which told me something about myself I filed away for later examination. When I came through the door she was sitting up, awake, watching me with the flattest, oldest eyes I have ever seen in a face that young. Eyes the color of the river in November. She didn't scream. She didn't ask where she was. She looked at me, took inventory — the apron, the ink-stained fingers, the general air of a man who has recently run downstairs — and apparently found me harmless, or at least harmless-adjacent.

"You're the clock man," she said.

"I'm the clock man," I agreed.

"They said you'd come." She said it without surprise and without gratitude, a statement of logistics, and then her gaze went past me to the table, to the bread and cheese still laid out, and her stomach spoke — loudly, rudely, with a growl that would have embarrassed a bear — and her face performed the entire war between pride and hunger in the space of two seconds, and pride lost, as it always does at that age, thank God.

"Eat," I said, and pushed the plate toward her.

She ate the way she did everything, I would learn: precisely, completely, wasting nothing, watching me sidelong the whole time as if I might revoke the offer. Halfway through the cheese she paused, and I saw her look at the note, still lying on the table where I'd left it, and I watched her read it — she could read, then, or read enough — and watched her face do nothing at all. Not a flicker. Whatever those three words meant to her, she'd already made her peace with them, or had never had peace to lose. She finished the cheese, drank the tea I'd sweetened without being asked to, and said:

"You're not going to send me back down the hill?"

It wasn't a question, quite. It was a test with the answer already penciled in. Children who've been moved around like furniture develop a terrible fluency in adults; they can read our intentions off our hands. I thought about the note in my pocket. I thought about the word *forget*. I thought about how much simpler my year would be if I walked her to the door right now and pointed down the hill, and how some doors, once opened onto certain things, don't close again behind you no matter how hard you pull.

"What's your name?" I asked.

"Mara," she said. Just that. No surname offered, none asked for, and I understood that the absence was the answer to a different question, one neither of us was going to ask out loud.

"Mara," I said. "I'm Nakamura. Isandro Nakamura."

"Nakamura." She tried it out. Her mouth shaped the foreignness of it without flinching, and something in my chest did a thing I chose not to examine. "Like the ferry ledgers," she said.

I went very still. "What do you know about the ferry ledgers?"

"Nothing," she said, in the tone of a person who knows several things and is conserving them. She set down her cup, folded her hands — seven years old, folding her hands like a small magistrate — and looked at me with those November eyes. "The lady said you wouldn't forget."

The fire ticked. Down through the floorboards, the dead clock held its silence, and the river muttered on below, and I understood that the note had been a lie of omission, that *forget the face* had been written by one hand and contradicted by another, and that whatever had been left on my steps that night was not an abandoned child but a message — delivered, signed, and waiting for me to read the rest.

"The lady," I said carefully. "What lady?"

Mara looked at me the way apprentices look at masters who ask stupid questions, and pulled the cedar quilt up to her chin, and said, "Tomorrow."
