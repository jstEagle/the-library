## 3. The Orchard Above Town

The clockmaker had a face like a door that hadn't decided whether to open.

That was my first thought about Isandro Nakamura, and I stand by it. Some faces you can read right away — the baker's wife in our town had a face like an open ledger, everything written on it, even the sums that went wrong. But his face kept its accounts elsewhere. He looked at me the way men look at a coin they've found on the ground: pleased, suspicious, and wondering whose it really was.

He fed me bread and cheese on the first night, sitting across the table with his elbows on it like he was guarding them. He did not ask my name. He did not ask where I came from. He asked exactly one question, which was: "Can you eat that without making yourself sick?"

I could. I proved it.

"Good," he said, and that was the whole of our conversation for a while, which suited me fine. Talking costs something. I'd learned that much before I ever arrived at his door with a note pinned to me like a price tag.

*Forget the face*, the note said. Well. Nobody had asked the note's opinion of what I would or wouldn't do.

* * *

The tower was cold in the way that stone is cold — not sharp like river wind, but patient, like it could wait longer than you could be warm. Isandro gave me a cot in a little room off the clockroom that smelled of oil and iron filings and, underneath all that, of apples. Old apples, ghost apples, apples so far gone they were only a suggestion. I decided not to mention this, because mentioning things is how you get sent places.

I knew about being sent places. Being sent places was practically my trade.

But the apple smell — that I held onto. You can hold a smell the way you hold a rope, if your hands are small enough to grip it tight.

In the morning he showed me three things. The first was the privy, which he pointed at from a distance as though introducing a shy relative. The second was the stove, which he taught me to feed, because "a clockmaker who freezes to death keeps terrible time." And the third was the clock itself.

It filled the room. That's the only way to say it. Most things in the world are *in* rooms; the master clock was a room, and we were in *it*. Gears big as wagon wheels, weights hanging down the stairwell on cables thick as my arm, and everywhere, everywhere, the tick. Not tick-tock, actually — I learned later that the great clocks don't say tick-tock, they say *tick... tick... tick...* like someone walking who never turns around. But everyone calls it tick-tock, so I will too, and let the pedants grind their teeth.

"Mind the wheels," Isandro said. "They don't care that you're small."

"Nothing does," I said, and he gave me one of his looks, the kind that goes into a drawer somewhere instead of coming out of his mouth.

* * *

I should tell you what I remembered, because for a long time it was all I owned, and you don't leave your belongings lying about where strangers can tidy them away.

Smoke. That was the first thing, always the first thing, the way a song starts with its first note no matter how many times you sing it. Woodsmoke, sweetish, with wet leaves under it — orchard smoke, burning the prunings in autumn. I didn't know that's what it was called until later. At the time it was just *smoke*, warm-colored, and behind it —

A dog. Big, brown, ears like folded gloves. He had a name that started soft, that my mouth made without any help, a name like a sigh. I used to lie on the cot in the tower saying sounds to myself in the dark, trying to find him again. *Barlo. Bruno. Barno.* None of them fit. It was like trying a key in a lock when you know the shape but not quite — the teeth almost catch.

And a woman's voice. This was the worst and best of the three, because a voice is a thing you carry inside your own head already, so how do you know which parts are her and which parts are you pretending? Hers was low. She sang sometimes but mostly she said one sentence, over and over, in memory, like the clock said *tick*: *"Stay by the trees."*

Stay by the trees. I had stayed by trees plenty of times since, in ditches and hedgerows and once in a churchyard yew that smelled like a coffin, and no woman had come. So either the instruction was broken, or I had been doing it wrong, or — this was the possibility I liked least — she had meant a particular tree.

Trees are particular. Anyone who tells you different has never loved one.

* * *

On the third day, Isandro caught me watching him work.

I want to be clear that I wasn't *sneaking*. There is a difference between sneaking and standing very still where nobody told you not to be. He was up on his ladder inside the clock's chest, so to speak, with his sleeves rolled and a wrench between his teeth like a rose stem, and his hands moved the way water moves — like they already knew what they were going to do before he did.

"What are those?" I asked. The gears above him were small ones, bright brass, arranged in a ring.

"Escapement," he said around the wrench, then took it out. "It's the part that lets the clock breathe."

"Clocks breathe?"

"Everything breathes." He climbed down, wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being white sometime the previous century. "Tick is the heart squeezing. Tock—" he caught himself, corrected, "*tick* again is it letting go. If nothing let go, everything would seize and stop. You have to give the time out a piece at a time or it isn't time, it's just..." He waved the wrench vaguely at the universe.

"Just everything at once," I said.

He looked at me properly then, the full weight of the door-face swinging open an inch. "Yes," he said. "Exactly that."

After that he stopped shooing me back to the cot-room. He didn't invite me closer, either — inviting would have cost him something, and I understood by then that Isandro Nakamura paid for things in full, always, and expected the same. But he left a gap beside the workbench that happened to be exactly the size of a small girl who stood very still where nobody told her not to be.

* * *

The dreams started the fourth night.

Not nightmares. Nightmares have teeth in them, chase you, end with falling. These were softer and therefore worse. In them I stood in long grass between rows of trees heavy with fruit, and the light was the gold that comes sideways at the end of the day, and the dog pressed his whole warm weight against my legs, and the woman's voice came from somewhere past the third row — always the third row — singing something with no words I could keep hold of, notes sliding through my fingers like minnows.

And every night, just as I turned to run toward the voice, the dream ended. Not faded. Ended, cut clean, like a page torn out.

I woke the fifth morning furious, which is a strange thing to wake as when you're small and homeless, but there it was. I went out to the clockroom where Isandro was winding the great drum, hauling the crank in slow circles, his breath smoking.

"You're angry," he observed.

"I lost something."

"In the night?"

"How would you know that?"

"Because you're clenching your fists at a clock." He finished the winding, seated the crank in its bracket with a click that ran up the whole tower like a spine straightening. "Things lost in the night come back sometimes. Not usually the same. Usually smaller, or stranger. But back." He sat down on the workbench stool, and after a moment, patted the crate next to it. An invitation, finally, paid for in full.

I sat. My feet didn't reach the floor. They never reached the floor anywhere in that tower for years, so I might as well tell you now and spare us both the footnote.

"I had a dog," I said. "And there was smoke. And a lady sang."

Isandro didn't laugh. I want that recorded, because grown men laugh at children's ghosts all the time, gently, the way you pat a bruise, and it bruises deeper. He nodded slowly, the way he nodded at a gear whose wear-pattern he was reading.

"Do you remember anything else? A house? Roads?"

"There were trees. Lots of trees, in lines. Like soldiers but nicer."

Something crossed his face then — quick, gone before I could name it, a fish under ice. "Lines," he repeated.

"And a hill. The trees were on a hill, and you could see water from up there. Gray water." I frowned, reaching. "With a bend in it."

"The river bends below the ridge north of town," Isandro said quietly. Mostly to himself, I think, though he wasn't the sort to say things mostly to himself; he kept even his thoughts in good repair. Then he looked at me and put the door-face nearly all the way shut again. "Eat your breakfast."

There was no breakfast in front of me yet. He got up and made some appear, which I have since learned is the closest a man like that comes to saying *don't worry*.

* * *

Here is a thing nobody warns you about trust: it doesn't arrive. It accrues. Like interest. Like dust. One day you look around and realize it's everywhere, on everything, and you couldn't say when any single grain of it landed.

For me it landed in small coins. The day he started leaving the second mug out without being asked. The day he said "hand me the two-point" and I handed him the wrong one and he didn't sigh, just said, "the flat one, the one that looks like it apologizes for itself," and I found it instantly, and he almost smiled, which on his face counted as a festival. The day the stove smoked and he coughed and said, "we're both useless before noon, you and this stove," which was the first time anyone had grouped me with anything as *we*.

And then there was the afternoon the snow came early — fat, silent flakes, the first of the winter — and I stood at the tall clockroom window looking north toward the ridge, and Isandro came to stand beside me, and neither of us said anything for a long time.

"My mother used to say snow means the year is putting itself away carefully," I said. "Like linen."

"Your mother." His voice was careful, the way his hands were careful. "You remember her."

"Some." Smoke, a dog, a voice. Stay by the trees. "Mostly her voice. Faces go. Voices stay. Isn't that funny? You'd think it'd be the other way, since faces are the part you see."

"It isn't funny at all," Isandro said. "But it's true." And then, after the snow had thickened and the world outside the glass had gone soft as unbaked dough: "Mara."

I startled. I hadn't told him my name. I was sure I hadn't told him my name. The note pinned to my coat had said *forget the face*, and names had seemed, in those first days, like dangerous currency — spend them and you're known, known and therefore sendable.

"The coat," he said, seeing it land on me. "Inside the collar. Someone embroidered it. M-A-R-A, in red thread, with a little stitch pattern around it like blossom." He looked straight ahead at the snow while he said it, giving me the courtesy of not watching me flinch. "I'm sorry. I should have told you sooner. I didn't want you to think I'd gone through your things."

"You did go through my things."

"I read a label on a coat. There's a difference, and I'll defend it if I have to." Now he did look at me, and his face was open all the way, door thrown wide, and what was behind it wasn't suspicion at all. It was something more tired and more stubborn than that. "You're in my keeping now, Mara. That word means something to a clockmaker. A thing in my keeping doesn't get lost. It gets wound, oiled, mended, and kept. Do you understand me?"

I did understand him. What I said was: "Even if I'm not yours?"

"Nothing in this tower is mine," he said, gesturing at the great ticking dark around us, the wagon-wheel gears, the weights asleep on their cables. "I mind it for other people. That's the whole trade." He hesitated, and I saw the next words cost him, saw him pay. "You especially aren't mine. But you're kept."

Outside, the snow came down on the town, on the gray bent river, on the ridge to the north where — though I couldn't have known it then, though I could not possibly have known it — lines of bare black trees stood waiting in somebody's orchard, holding their dead leaves and their living secrets, and under the first tree of the row, deep in frozen ground, a small round thing of silver and enamel lay in the dark beside old bones, keeping its own appointment with spring.

I put my hand flat against the cold window glass.

"Stay by the trees," I whispered, practicing, the way you practice a key in a lock.

Behind me the clock said *tick*, and said *tick*, and said *tick* — patient as stone, patient as snowfall, patient as a promise that hasn't figured out yet how it's going to be kept.

And down in the town, in a lit window of the great house on the hill, someone was watching the observatory lights and wondering — I learned this much later, but wonder travels backward as well as forward — just what the new clockmaker was doing with the child he'd been told to forget.
