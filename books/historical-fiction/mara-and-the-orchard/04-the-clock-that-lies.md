## 4. The Clock That Lies

A clockmaker learns early that every machine tells the truth eventually. It may lie for a while — a wheel slipping, a hand drifting four minutes slow while its owner swears at the weather — but open the case, lay the train out on felt in the order God intended, and the mechanism will confess everything. Gears do not have opinions. Escapements do not hold grudges. This was the religion I had been raised in, back in my master's shop downriver: the belief that beneath any surface confusion there is an honest arithmetic, and that a man with clean hands and good light can always find it.

I want it on record that on my third morning at the observatory, I still believed that.

The town had not warmed to me. I had been there long enough to learn the geography of coldness — which shopkeepers served me in silence, which children were pulled indoors as I passed, which windows went dark at the sight of my coat. The name Nakamura did things to faces here. I hadn't yet learned what, exactly; that discovery was waiting for me like a trap set under leaves. For now I knew only that the word landed wrong, the way a coin of foreign mint lands wrong in a local till.

But the observatory itself — the observatory I loved without reservation, the way you can love a building that has never once asked you where your grandfather was born.

It sat above the town on its bald hill like a stone thought somebody had forgotten to finish. Below, the river ran gray and purposeful toward the sea, carrying whatever the autumn had left on its banks. Inside, the tower rose through three floors of scaffolding and dust-sheets, and at the top of it all hung the master clock — the regulator, the old men called it, in the tone other towns reserved for their churches.

Every clock in the valley took its time from this one. The ferry office, the bank, the schoolhouse, the two churches that agreed on nothing except the hour. When the master clock stopped — and it had stopped, some weeks before my arrival, at eleven minutes past four in the afternoon — the whole region had gone quietly out of joint. Trains guessed. Appointments dissolved. Somewhere a judge was sentencing men by a watch he no longer trusted.

And so they had sent for my master, and my master, whose knees had opinions about hills, had sent for me.

* * *

I began, as one begins, at the top.

The clockroom occupied the tower's crown, a round chamber walled in pale stone, lit by tall arched windows on every side. In the center stood the movement itself behind a glazed case — a great weight-driven regulator, the pendulum hanging below like a held breath, the dial above white as a peeled egg. Around it, the room kept its furniture like relics: a chair worn glossy by decades of the same sitter, a rack of winding keys, a ledger stand with the timekeeper's logbook still open to an entry half-finished, abandoned mid-word the day the clock died.

The last timekeeper's name was Voss. He had wound this clock for thirty-one years, according to the flyleaf of the logbook, and then one October afternoon he had written *wound, regulated, all well*, put down his pen, and — according to the landlady who told me this with relish over supper — walked out of the tower, down the hill, into the town, and dropped dead outside the baker's before his bread hit the ground.

The clock stopped eleven minutes later.

The town found this meaningful. The town, I was learning, found most things meaningful. I found it mechanical. A clock does not die of grief. A clock stops because something in its train fails, or because someone stops it, and either way the answer is sitting right there in the wheels waiting for a man with tools.

All well, Mr. Voss? We would see.

* * *

The first day was housekeeping. I swept two years of dust from the case, cleaned the glass, and let the movement tell me its biography. It was a fine piece — better than fine. The plates were hand-finished to a standard I associated with the great observatory workshops of the continent, the pivots polished to a black mirror, the teeth of the wheels cut with a precision that made my master's best work look like fence-mending. Somebody had built this clock the way cathedrals get built: by a person who expected never to be thanked and didn't care.

There was no maker's mark. That was the first oddity. Fine work always signs itself; vanity is the engine of craftsmanship. But these plates were anonymous as a grave.

The second oddity arrived when I lifted the pendulum free and weighed it in my hands. Too heavy. Not absurdly — nothing you'd notice carrying it — but a regulator pendulum is a sacred object, calculated to the grain, and this one carried perhaps four ounces more than its bob could account for. I shook it gently near my ear. Something inside shifted with a small, dense sound, like sand in a closed fist.

I set it aside and told myself it was a repair weight. Clockmakers do that. We are, as a profession, excellent at telling ourselves stories that let us keep working.

* * *

On the third day I opened the going train.

For those who have never had the pleasure: a clock's going train is a chain of gears that carries power from the falling weights up to the hands, each wheel spinning faster than the last, until the final small motion is tamed by the escapement into that tick we all pretend not to hear. It is the simplest poetry in the world. Power goes in at one end; time comes out the other. Every wheel has a job. Every tooth earns its keep.

I laid the train out across the bench on white felt, in order, left to right, the way my master taught me — *so the eye can read it like a sentence, boy; a clock is a sentence about time* — and I sat back on the stool and read the sentence.

And the sentence lied.

There were five wheels in the train where there should have been four. I counted twice, then a third time with my finger touching each arbor, because a man alone in a tower starts doubting his own counting before he doubts the clock. Five. And the fifth wheel — the extra one, seated between the second and third in the sequence — did nothing.

Let me be precise about *nothing*, because nothing turned out to be a very busy thing.

The wheel meshed perfectly. Its teeth engaged the wheels on either side with a fit so exact that if you looked at the assembled movement, you would swear it was turning, driving, doing its share. But when I traced the geometry — and I am good at tracing geometry; it is nearly the only kind of tracing I'm good at — the power did not go through it. The wheel on the far side was driven directly past it. The fifth wheel spun, yes, when the train ran — spun beautifully, balanced to a whisper — but it spun *beside* the work, not inside it. It was a passenger. A stowaway dressed as crew.

I checked for wear, thinking perhaps a wheel had been replaced badly sometime in the clock's life. No. There was no wear on it at all. Its pivots ran in jeweled bearings — jeweled, in a position that needed no jewels, where plain brass would have served — and the steel was bright, unscarred, untouched by the decades that had dulled every honest wheel around it.

Somebody had built this wheel to run forever and accomplish nothing.

I did what any sensible craftsman does upon meeting the impossible: I blamed myself. I disassembled the train again from scratch, laid it out fresh, re-traced the power path with a pointer, muttering aloud like a madman in a belfry. *Great wheel, second wheel — here the power passes — third wheel, fourth, escape wheel.* The fifth wheel sat in the middle of my sentence like a word in a language I almost recognized.

Then I took it out entirely, rigged the train to run without it, and the clock — or rather, the bare train on the bench, driven by a test weight — ran perfectly. Better than perfectly. Nothing changed. The fifth wheel was load-bearing in appearance only.

You cannot understand what that does to a clockmaker unless you have been one. It is the equivalent of opening a human body and finding a heart that pumps nothing, connected to veins that go nowhere, installed with loving skill by a surgeon who then sealed the patient up and let him live a long and public life.

* * *

I sat with it through the afternoon. The light came around the tower's windows in a slow golden rotation, and I watched it cross the bench the way I'd watched it cross a thousand benches, except that this time the moving light felt less like a day passing than like a searchlight looking for something.

Because here is the thing I had not yet said aloud, even to myself: nobody builds a wheel that does nothing. Not by accident. Not by madness. A dummy gear costs as much to make as a true one — more, given the jewels — and it must be designed with the full genius of the craft precisely so that it will fool everyone forever. You do not spend that kind of skill on nothing.

You spend it on hiding something.

I turned the fifth wheel over in my hands. On its face, unremarkable. On its back — I nearly missed it — a single letter stamped small and deep into the web of the wheel, hidden where only a man who removed it would ever see: not a maker's initial, not a number. A shape. A branch, cut in miniature, with five buds along its length.

An orchard branch.

My hands, I noticed distantly, had gone very steady — that particular steadiness that arrives when the body understands danger before the mind consents to. Because I knew what an orchard meant in this town. Even I, three weeks off the boat of this place's history, knew that. The orchard above town was not fruit; it was a war with trees growing out of it. The family that owned it had quarreled so long and so publicly that the quarrel had become civic architecture — you could not buy nails without hearing whose side the hardware merchant was on. They owned the slopes, the docks' best warehouse, half the ferry shares, and, by common report, the patience of everyone else.

And their emblem was stamped on the secret heart of the master clock.

I looked from the wheel to the pendulum lying on the bench, with its four ounces of unexplained weight shifting in its bob like a swallowed word. I looked at the empty fifth position in the train, and at the logbook standing open to Mr. Voss's last entry — *all well* — written by a man who had tended this machine for thirty-one years and must have known, must have *known*, what lived inside it.

All well. All, apparently, except the truth.

* * *

Here is what I should have done. Any prudent man would have done it. I should have wiped the wheel clean, replaced it exactly as found, finished the honest repairs — a worn pallet, a dirty bearing, ordinary sins — restarted the clock, collected my fee, and gone home downriver with a story for the shop: *the famous regulator, and the strange wheel that does nothing.* A curiosity. An anecdote. History happening to other people, which was, as I have said, my entire philosophy at the time.

Instead I sat in the gold-going light of that round stone room, holding a branch of five buds between finger and thumb, and felt the floor of my profession tilt beneath me.

Because a clock that lies is worse than a broken clock. A broken clock is merely defeated. A lying clock has been *designed* — deliberately, expensively, by someone who understood exactly what they were doing — to deceive every person who ever trusted it. And this clock was not any clock. It was the master. Its hands told the whole valley what time it was. Its honesty was infrastructure. If there was a falsehood built into the very center of it, then everything arranged around that center — every schedule, every contract, every alibi, every inheritance timed to the stroke of noon — stood on a foundation someone had hollowed out.

I am not a romantic. Let that be clear. I did not think, *a mystery!* and rub my hands. What I thought, with a rising professional nausea, was: *this is a vault.* Someone had used the finest clockwork I had ever touched as a lockbox, and had hidden the lock so well that thirty-one years of daily winding had never sprung it. Which raised the question that made my neck prickle in the cooling air:

What was in the box?

And the second question, arriving on the heels of the first like a debt collector: who besides Mr. Voss — who besides the dead man and whoever had commissioned the wheel — knew it was there?

I thought of the town's cold eyes. I thought of the orchard war. I thought, absurdly, of the child on the steps — Mara, asleep upstairs in the keeper's cot where I had, against every rule of my trade and probably of decency, let her stay these three nights because she ate her bread like someone who had learned not to count on the next slice, and because when she woke she looked at the clock's pendulum with an attention no adult in this town had shown anything.

She was tied to the orchard. I didn't know how yet — the note pinned to her coat had offered no genealogy, only that instruction I could not obey: *forget the face.* But blood leaves marks the way water leaves marks, and I had already begun to suspect that the child sleeping one floor below me was a loose thread in somebody's tapestry, and that people who build lying clocks do not leave threads loose.

Outside, the light was failing. Down the hill, the town was lighting its lamps by a clock that had been dead for weeks, and beyond the town the river moved in the dark, carrying ice down from the high country — the first reports of the winter that was coming for us all.

I wrapped the fifth wheel in flannel, put it in my tool chest, and locked the chest. Then I sat a while longer in the dark rather than light the lamp, listening to the silence where the tick should have been, and understanding for the first time that the silence was not emptiness.

The clock wasn't stopped.

The clock was *keeping* something.

And somewhere below me, in the cot by the cold stove, a child stirred in her sleep and murmured a word I couldn't make out — a woman's voice, maybe, remembered — while in my locked chest a little brass orchard waited to tell me whose.
