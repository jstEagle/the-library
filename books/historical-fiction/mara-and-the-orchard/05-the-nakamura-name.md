## 5. The Nakamura Name

The trouble with a name, I had always believed, was that it was portable. You could carry it upriver in a carpet bag along with your files and your oil can, set it down on new ground, and it would sit there quietly like any other tool until you chose to pick it up again. A man's history, I told myself for twenty-six years, lived wherever he wasn't. It happened to other people, in other towns, at distances convenient for ignoring.

I want it noted that I held this belief right up until the morning I walked into Grubbe's General Store to buy flour, tea, and a second blanket for the child, and the store went quiet around me like a clockwork with a finger laid against its escapement.

There were six people in Grubbe's that morning. I counted them later, the way you count the teeth of a wheel after something has gone wrong — obsessively, looking for the flaw. There was Grubbe himself behind the counter, a broad man with the complexion of boiled ham; two farmwives by the dry goods; an old fellow mending a fishnet by the stove who never stopped mending it the entire time I knew him; a boy of perhaps twelve stacking crates; and a woman in grey near the window whose face I would come to know far better than I wanted to, though I didn't know her then.

"Flour," I said to Grubbe. "Tea. And a wool blanket, nothing fancy."

Grubbe looked at me. He did not reach for the flour. Behind me, one of the farmwives said something in a low voice to the other, and the word I caught — or thought I caught, though I would swear to it later under less charitable weather — was *orchard*.

"You'll be the clockmaker," Grubbe said. "Up to the observatory."

"I will."

"The one they sent for." He wiped his hands on his apron in a way that suggested he found the whole arrangement distasteful, the town included, himself possibly excepted. "Nakamura, the letter said."

"Yes."

And there it was. Not my face, which was unfamiliar. Not my trade, which they needed. The *name*. It moved through the store the way cold moves through a wall — not visibly, but you could feel it arrive. The fishnet man's hands stilled. The boy stopped stacking. Even the stove seemed to burn more quietly, as if it too were listening.

"My grandfather," I said, because silence is a debt and I have never liked owing one, "worked these parts. Before the war. Isamu Nakamura. Grafting, mostly. Some pruning."

"We know," said the woman in grey by the window.

She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. Heat you can argue with. She turned back to the glass, to whatever she'd been watching — the frozen street, the smoke rising off the roofs, the observatory hill with its white dome catching the morning — and let the sentence stand there among us like a tool nobody would pick up.

Grubbe sold me the flour eventually. And the tea. The blanket he fetched down from the shelf with exaggerated care, as though handling something that might be claimed later in a dispute, and when I paid he counted my coins twice and gave me change in small denominations, coin by coin, into my palm, so that our hands nearly touched nine separate times. I understood this to be a message. Towns are fluent in such messages. I had simply never before been addressed in the language.

I carried my purchases out into the cold and heard, through the door before it shut, the fishnet man say, "His grandfather, all right. Same walk."

*Same walk.* As if the man had been waiting thirty years to deliver that observation, keeping it wrapped in oilcloth like a spare part, and had produced it the moment its fitting arrived.

* * *

You should understand what I knew of my grandfather, which is to say: almost nothing, delivered in almost no words.

My father spoke of him exactly twice that I remember. Once, when I was small, watching him file a pallet arbor, I asked where we came from — children ask, it is their one reliable vice — and my father said, "From an orchard we don't own," and went on filing. The second time was on his deathbed, when he gripped my wrist with a strength that surprised us both and said, "If anyone asks about the trees, tell them we kept them alive. Tell them that. We kept them alive."

Then he died, having explained nothing, which is the traditional inheritance of fathers, and I went on believing that history was a thing that happened in capitals and battlefields, to generals and ministers, to men whose names got printed. My history, I maintained, was gears. A gear has no ancestors. A gear has only its pitch, its count, and whether it runs true. This struck me as a mercy, and I arranged my life accordingly.

Now I stood on the frozen street of a river town holding a blanket that had been handed to me like evidence, being recognized by my surname in a tone usually reserved for debts and diseases, and the belief began — slowly, the way ice begins, from the edges inward — to crack.

*We kept them alive.*

What trees, Father? Whose orchard? Alive for whom?

* * *

The child asked about it that evening, because children hear everything and pretend they haven't.

I had rigged a second cot from the observatory's old survey bench and some sacking, and Mara — for I had learned her name from the note, the single legible word on it, everything else being either instruction or threat — sat cross-legged on it winding a scrap of brass wire between her fingers while I attempted to cook. I am a fair hand with an escapement and a criminal one with a stew, and the stew knew it.

"People talked today," she said. "In the store."

"You weren't there."

"I was outside. You left me to watch the sled." She wound the wire tighter. Her hands were remarkable, even then. Small, chapped, steady. "They said your name funny. Like it was hot."

"It's a common enough occurrence," I said, "for names to be mispronounced."

"Not mispronounced." She looked up. Children have not yet learned to look away, and so they see more than we do. "Like it was somebody's."

I stirred the stew. The stew declined to be saved.

"There was a man," I said finally, "a long time ago, who worked with trees here. He was my mother's father. That's all."

"What happened to him?"

"He left."

"Why?"

"Because leaving is what men do when staying costs too much."

Mara considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she said, "Did he leave anything?"

And I confess the question went through me like a punch through wet paper, because I had spent the day turning over exactly that — the ledger of what a man leaves: his walk, apparently, recognizable after three decades; his name, apparently, still warm enough to make a store go quiet; and somewhere, presumably, the reason itself, buried in whatever soil this town kept its reasons in.

"He left a story," I said. "Nobody's told it to me yet."

* * *

It was told to me, in fragments, over the following week, and never by anyone who wished to tell it.

Towns don't confess. Towns leak. You learn a place the way water learns stone — by returning daily with the same small pressure until something gives.

The leak came first through the boy from Grubbe's, who appeared at the observatory one afternoon ostensibly selling eggs and actually selling curiosity, and who examined the tower, the child, and me with equal frankness before saying, "Pa says you're the graft-man's grandson. The one from the bad winter."

"Which bad winter?"

"They're all bad," he said, with the wisdom of the young, and left the eggs, and left.

The second leak came from the ferry office, where I went to study the manifests — but that is another chapter of this account, and I will keep it in order. What matters here is what the ferry clerk, a narrow woman named Ostrova with spectacles like frosted glass, said when I signed my name in her book. She read it upside down, as clerks do, and her pen stopped.

"Nakamura," she said. "That name used to be in this book every week. Spring and fall, twenty years running. Orchard labor coming upriver, going down." She tapped the page. "Then one year it just — stops. Like a clock."

"When?"

"Before my time. But the old manifests are in the back if you've got the patience. Most folk haven't."

I said I had patience. It is the one virtue my trade requires and rewards, and I had believed it was the only one I possessed. I was beginning to suspect it came with obligations attached.

The third leak was the worst, because it came dressed as kindness. Old Mrs. Halloran — the fishnet man's wife, as it turned out; the net-mender mended nothing, it was his way of having somewhere to sit — stopped me on the bridge one evening as the light went copper on the ice. She pressed a jar of preserved plums into my hands and looked up at me with eyes that had clearly been doing arithmetic for thirty years.

"You favor him," she said. "Around the mouth."

"So I'm told."

"He was a good worker, your grandfather. Best grafter this valley ever saw. Could make a cutting take where God Himself had given up on the soil." She patted the jar in my arms, once, hard, like a verdict. "And they took every tree he ever touched and wrote him out of it. Every one. Ask the orchard family why. See how long they'll look at you."

"Mrs. Halloran—"

"Wrote him out," she repeated, and walked off across the bridge, small and straight-backed, leaving me holding plums I couldn't eat for days because my hands wouldn't stop remembering the weight of her pat.

*Wrote him out.* Not drove him out. Not ruined him. *Wrote* him out — as though a man could be removed the way a name is struck from a manifest, the way a wheel is taken from a train, the train closing seamlessly around the gap as though the wheel had never been pitched or counted or cut.

I stood on the bridge until dark, thinking about the dummy gears upstairs in the master clock — wheels that turned and accomplished nothing, motion without function, function without motion — and for the first time it occurred to me that a clock might lie in more ways than one.

* * *

That night Mara was asleep and I sat at the workbench with the lamp turned low, and I took out the fifth wheel again, the flannel-wrapped thing from my locked chest, and unwrapped it where no one could see.

Under lamplight I found what I had missed in the tower's gloom. Stamped into the brass hub, small enough to miss entirely, worn nearly smooth: a maker's mark. Not the mark of the firm that had built the clock — I had their plates memorized by now. This was older, cruder, cut by hand with a graver by someone working alone and in a hurry or in secret or both.

A branch. Five blossoms on it.

An orchard branch, stamped in brass, inside the heart of the town's great clock, on a wheel that did nothing but turn and keep its secret.

I sat for a long time. Below, the river groaned and settled under its ice, patient as a creditor. In her cot, the child breathed evenly, her hands curled loose at her chin the way they curled around wire when she was awake, already practicing for some work neither of us could name yet.

History, I had always believed, happened elsewhere. It happened to ministers, to armies, to families with portraits in halls. It happened to other people, and a man could carry his name in a carpet bag and set it down anywhere and it would stay quiet.

But the name hadn't stayed quiet. It had waited thirty years in a river town, folded into ledgers and preserves jars and half-sentences, and the moment I stepped off the ferry it had begun unfolding — and worse, far worse, it had been unfolding here all along, in brass, in the dark, in the belly of a machine I had been hired to repair.

*Tell them we kept them alive,* my father had said, gripping my wrist.

Kept *what* alive, Father? The trees? Or something the trees were standing guard over?

I wrapped the wheel again. But before I locked the chest, I did something I had not done since I was a boy: I took out my notebook — the apprentice's log every journeyman keeps, recording work done, parts made, hours billed — and on the first blank page I wrote, in my careful drafting hand:

*Master clock, observatory hill. Fifth wheel, dummy train, marked with five-blossom branch. Maker unknown.*

Then I crossed out *unknown*, and wrote beneath it:

*Nakamura.*

My hand shook slightly doing it. It is one thing to carry a name; it is another to write it into a record, where ink makes it true and permanent and answerable. I blew the lamp out and lay in the dark listening to the river and the child and the great silent vault above my head, and I thought: tomorrow I will go up to the great house on the hill, the orchard house, and I will knock on that door and give them my name plainly and watch their faces while I do it.

I did not know then that they were already expecting me.

I did not know that a summons was even now being written — heavy paper, heavy seal, the handwriting of a family that had spent thirty years watching that white dome on the hill the way a man watches a grave he filled himself.

The letter would arrive with the morning post.
