## 6. The Ferrymaster's Ledger

The ferry office sat at the bottom of Rope Walk like a tooth that had been left in too long — brown at the edges, loose in its socket, and stubbornly refusing to fall out. I had passed it a dozen times since arriving in this town without giving it a second glance, which I now recognize as a kind of blindness. Clockmakers are supposed to notice everything. We are trained to see the small wrongness, the tooth on a wheel that doesn't quite match its neighbors. But I had walked past that door with my collar up against the wind and my thoughts full of gears, and it took Mara — who noticed everything because she was small enough to be overlooked herself — to point at the sign in the window.

*Ferry manifests, 1860 to present. Inquire within.*

"Why would anyone keep those?" she asked. She was sitting cross-legged on my workbench at the time, which was against three separate rules I had invented for her benefit, watching me file a pivot with more attention than most journeymen give their whole apprenticeships.

"Because people are nosy," I said, "and ferrymen are paid to remember."

"Are you going to inquire within?"

"I am going to finish this pivot."

"You are going to inquire within," she said, with the serene confidence of a child who has learned exactly one thing about me and found it reliable: that I say no first and yes second, always in that order, and that the gap between them is never more than a day.

She was right. That is the trouble with children. They read you faster than you can wind yourself.

* * *

I went down to the ferry office on a Thursday afternoon, when the light through the river fog came in flat and grey as unpolished brass. The ferrymaster was a man named Odilon Breck, sixty if he was a day, with hands like tree roots and a face that suggested he had spent forty years being rained on and had decided, somewhere around year ten, not to take it personally.

He was mending a net when I came in, though God knows what he expected to catch in a river locked edge to edge with ice. Perhaps he simply liked the motion of it. Men who work water all their lives get restless when the water stops talking to them.

"I'm looking at the manifests," I said. "The old ones. The ones from before the fire in the east warehouse, if any survived."

Breck looked up. He had the particular stillness of a man deciding whether you are a customer or a problem, and I watched him decide, slowly, that I might be both.

"Clockmaker," he said. Not a question. News travels fast in a river town, especially news that nobody asked for. "Nakamura."

"That's the name on my tools."

"Mm." He set down the net. "My father kept these books before me, and his father before him. Three generations of Brecks writing down who went where." He tapped the ledger shelf with one root-like finger. "You'd be surprised what a ferry book knows about a town. More than the church register. Church tells you who's born and who's buried. Ferry tells you who *left*, and why, and what they took with them, and who waved from the bank and who didn't come down to wave at all."

"That's exactly why I want to see them."

"And why would a clockmaker care who left?"

I had prepared an answer. I want to be honest about that; I had stood on the observatory steps rehearsing something plausible and boring, something about shipping clock parts and verifying deliveries. But standing in that cold office with the smell of tar and wet rope, I heard myself say instead:

"My grandfather worked here. Or near here. And his name isn't in anything anymore."

The words came out of me with the strange weight of things you didn't know you were carrying until you set them down. Breck studied me for a long moment, and then he did something I did not expect. He nodded. Once. Slowly. The nod of a man who has been waiting years for someone to come ask the question everyone in town already knew the answer to and had agreed, by silent and cowardly consensus, never to say aloud.

"Aye," he said. "That's about the size of it, isn't it."

He pulled a key from around his neck — literally around his neck, on a string among the fishhooks, which told me how much those ledgers mattered to him — and unlocked the shelf.

* * *

There were eleven volumes. They smelled of mildew and lamp oil and, underneath it, the faint sweet rot of paper that has been handled by too many wet hands. Breck cleared a space on his desk with the economy of a man who has a system for everything, and set the earliest volume down in front of me like a priest laying out a relic.

"Read what you like," he said. "But you'll mind the rule."

"What rule?"

"Don't write in them. Don't tear out of them. Whatever you find, you find, and it stays found. I've had men come in here wanting pages gone. I tell them the same thing I'm telling you." He leaned close, and his breath smelled of black tea and tobacco. "A ferryman who lets a passenger be unwritten is no ferryman at all. My father taught me that. Said it was the only law worth keeping on the water."

"Who wanted pages gone?"

"They always do, after," he said vaguely, and went back to his net, and I understood that this was all the answer I would get for free.

So I read.

I will not pretend I started with a plan. I started with 1871 because it was the volume on top, and I read forward, and for an hour or so it was nothing but names — merchants and millhands and brides going downriver to weddings they hadn't fully agreed to, coffins going the other way, a circus in October 1873 that apparently departed in some haste with two fewer horses than it arrived with. History, I thought. This is history. A list of departures. No wonder people leave it to other people.

Then I reached the autumn of 1874.

The page began normally enough. *October 9th — J. Pellier, 2 crates dried apples, 1 crate glassware.* *October 11th — Mrs. A. Voss and infant, passage paid by her brother, going to her mother's, don't expect her back.* (Even the old Breck wrote commentary; it ran in the family like big hands.) *October 14th — T. Okafor, sawyer, seasonal labor ended.*

And then, on October the seventeenth, a line that had been written and scraped away.

Not torn out — Breck's rule held, whoever else's hadn't. But scraped. Someone had taken a knife or a razor to the paper while the ink was barely dry, and the fibers of the page were roughened there, furred pale, like skin after a bad shave. Underneath, if you tilted the page against the window light — and I did, holding it up like a man checking a coin — you could just make out the ghost of the pen strokes. Ink bites into paper deeper than any blade can lift it back out. Any clockmaker knows this; we work with engraved plates and stamped faces, and we know that a mark made with force leaves a memory in the metal that polishing cannot entirely kill.

Paper remembers too.

Three words survived the scrape well enough to read. The first was a surname, and I will write it plainly here because it deserves plainness: *Nakamura*. The second word was *and*. And the third was *wife*.

Below where the rest had been destroyed, the original entry had continued in the normal hand — old Breck's hand, the same steady script that recorded the circus and the coffin and Mrs. Voss who wasn't coming back. It read: *passage refused by order of —*

And there the scraping stopped, mid-phrase, either because the scraper lost his nerve or because the order itself was the part someone couldn't bear to have survive.

I sat in that cold office for a long time with the ledger open under the grey window light, and I felt something shift in my chest the way ice shifts on a river — not breaking, not yet, but groaning along its whole length, announcing that it intends to move eventually whether anyone consents or not.

"Passage refused," I said aloud.

Breck didn't look up from his net. "Refusal's written on the line below sometimes. When it's the ferry's doing. Weather, high water, a man too drunk to be safe aboard." His needle paused. "When it's other folks' doing, the refusal usually gets written and then... amended."

"Amended."

"Scraped," he said. "By whoever paid for the amendment. Paper's cheap. Knives are cheap. Names are dearer than either, and some men in this valley have spent thirty years making sure that stays true."

"Whose order was it? The refusal. Whose name came after 'by order of'?"

Breck finally set the net down. He came over and stood beside me, looking at the scraped line with an expression I could not read — grief, maybe, or the tired satisfaction of a debt long overdue.

"I was eleven years old the day your grandfather came to this dock," he said. "I remember him because he carried a toolbox the way other men carry children. Careful, like it was alive. Him and his wife, going downriver to take work in the city — that's what they told folk. And then a carriage came down Rope Walk, and a man got out of it, and there was words on the dock, quiet words, and the next thing anybody knew the Nakamuras' trunks were back on the cobbles and the ferry left without them." He shook his head. "Old Breck — my father — wrote the truth in the book. Passage refused by order of — and then he wrote the name. And two days later a gentleman came in, very civil, very sorry, paid for a new roof on this building, and while the roofer's ladder was against the wall somebody got at the page with a knife."

"Do you remember the name?"

"I was eleven," Breck said. "But I knew whose carriage it was. Everyone knew whose carriage it was. It was the orchard's carriage, and the man in it was the orchard's man, and the orchard's man only ever spoke for the orchard's family." He met my eyes. "Your grandfather built the master clock, didn't he. Up at the observatory."

"He did."

"Funny thing," Breck said mildly, "a man builds the finest piece of work this valley's ever seen, and the same season he's thrown off a dock by the finest family this valley's ever produced. You'd think one of those facts would cancel the other out. Never does, though. Never once does."

* * *

I stayed until dark. I read every volume, cover to cover, because once you find one erased name you cannot stop wondering what else has been unwritten, and Breck let me, feeding the stove and bringing tea without being asked, saying nothing, mending his useless net in the lamplight like a man keeping vigil.

I found two more ghosts. Smaller ones. A line from 1877 recording a delivery — *1 crate clockwork, consigned N., observatory* — where the "N." had been blotted rather than scraped, blotted so heavily the page beneath was stiff with it, as if someone had leaned on the blotter a long time, thinking. And in 1881, a departure record for a woman named Yuki Nakamura — my grandmother — traveling alone, destination left blank, a single word in the margin in old Breck's smaller hand: *weeping.*

She went downriver alone. She wept. She never came back up. My grandfather stayed, finished the clock, and died in this town a stranger, and whatever happened between him and my grandmother happened on this dock or in the silence afterward, and there is no one alive now who can tell me which.

Here is what I had believed when I came upriver with my master's tools in October: that history was a foreign country, a thing that happened to other people in other places, and that I had the good fortune to live outside its borders. I had believed it the way you believe the floor will hold you — without thinking about it, because thinking about it serves nothing.

But the floor had spoken. The floor had said *your name was here, and someone paid to remove it, and the man who removed it rode in the orchard's carriage.*

I walked home through the frozen streets with my collar up and my breath smoking, and the town looked different to me. That is the true cost of finding a name in a ledger: you can never again walk through the place where the name was taken out. Every window on Rope Walk became a window someone had watched from. The orchard house on its hill above town, dark against the stars, became not scenery but a defendant.

At the observatory door, Mara was waiting up, wrapped in a blanket she had definitely stolen from my cot, sitting exactly where she had been found four months earlier, on the third step, as if the step belonged to her now and she to it.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"You inquired within." Her eyes were bright in the lantern light, and she had that look children get when they know the grown-up has come back changed and are deciding whether to mention it. "What did the ferry book say?"

I should have lied to her. I want to be honest about that too; I considered lying, the way I had considered a plausible story about shipping parts, and rejected it for the same reason. She had been left on this exact step with a note telling a grown man to forget her face. If anyone alive understood what it means to have yourself written out of a record, it was the small person in the stolen blanket.

"It said," I told her, "that a long time ago, people with my name tried to leave this town, and someone wouldn't let them. And then someone tried to pretend it never happened."

Mara thought about this with the terrible seriousness she gave to everything.

"Did it work?" she asked. "The pretending."

I looked at her — at her face, which I had been instructed to forget and had instead memorized down to the last freckle — and I thought of scraped paper and ghost-ink and an old ferryman's law: *whatever you find, you find, and it stays found.*

"No," I said. "It didn't work."

"Good," said Mara. "Pretending never works. That's what my mother said." And then she yawned enormously, ruined the gravity of the entire conversation, and demanded to be carried inside, which I did.

But I lay awake a long while afterward in the clockroom, listening to the stopped mechanism hold its silence above me, and I understood that something in me had finished turning over, the way a heavy wheel turns over at last when the weight finally outweighs the friction.

I had come to this town believing history happened elsewhere. Now I knew better. It had happened here, on a dock, to my own blood — and it was still happening, quietly, in every scraped page and every closed face on Rope Walk. The clock above my head was not just a machine my grandfather built. It was the one thing he built that they had never managed to erase.

In the morning, I decided, I would begin taking it apart in earnest.

Whatever they hid in it — whatever they were afraid of — I intended to find it.
