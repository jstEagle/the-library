## 7. Mara Learns the Escapement

The tower had a smell, and the smell was oil and cold iron and, underneath that, a kind of patience I didn't have yet.

I'd been at the observatory eleven days when Isandro finally let me touch something. Eleven days of watching. Eleven days of being told *not yet* and *that's not for you* and — my least favorite — *you're too small to hold the wrench.* As if size were the whole of it. As if hands were just hands.

But on the twelfth morning he set a broken thing in front of me like it was a plate of bread, and everything after that was different.

"You're going to hold this," he said, "and you're not going to turn it. Just feel it."

It was a wheel about as wide as my two palms together, brass around its edge, with teeth cut into the rim so fine they looked like they'd been made by someone who hated the idea of gaps. A long arm crossed over it, weighted at one end, hooked at the other. The hook rested against one of the little teeth.

"What is it?"

"Escapement." He said it the way some people say *grace* before eating. "It's the reason clocks work and rivers don't. Everything else in this machine wants to fall all at once. The weights want to drop, the gears want to spin, the whole train of them wants to spend itself in one glorious second and be finished forever. The escapement is what says no. It lets out exactly one tooth's worth at a time. That's the tick. And then—" he nudged the wheel a hair's width, and the hook slipped off one tooth and caught the next, and the whole thing went *tock* under my fingers, a small knock traveling up through my bones— "that's the tock. It steals time from the falling and pays it back evenly. Do you understand?"

I said yes, because I was seven and proud.

I did not understand. But I understood the feeling of it, which turned out later to be most of the matter.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about living inside a clock: you stop hearing it.

The master clock lived two floors below our room, but its heart was everywhere in the tower. Every beam carried the tick up through the floorboards into the bed frame, into the pillow, into the skull. The first three nights I lay awake certain the building itself had a pulse and that it was counting down to something. By the fourth night I slept through it. By the eleventh I couldn't hear it at all unless I tried, and then it came back all at once, everywhere, like remembering a language.

Isandro heard it always. I knew because sometimes, in the middle of him talking about gear ratios or the weather or whether we could afford butter, he would go silent mid-word, tilt his head, and frown.

"The beat's dragging," he said once, apropos of nothing, mid-sentence about butter.

"It sounds fine to me."

"It's a sixteenth slow." He stood up from his bread. "Something's catching in the third wheel."

And there was — a burr of rust no bigger than a grain of rice — and he found it because the clock had lied to him by a sixteenth of a second, and he took it personally. That was the day I decided he was either very brilliant or slightly mad, and that I intended to stay near enough to find out which.

* * *

On the twelfth morning he was working at the bench with his back to me, and I was supposed to be sweeping, and instead I was watching his hands. I want to say now, honestly, that watching his hands was better than anything the town ever showed me from outside. They were long-fingered and quick and they never hesitated the way people do. When he picked up a screw he already knew which way it wanted to turn.

"Why does yours go *tock* louder than *tick*?" I asked.

He didn't turn around. "Because the left pallet catches harder than the right. In a perfect escapement they'd be equal."

"So ours is imperfect."

"Everything is imperfect. That's why we get jobs." He glanced over his shoulder. "Why? Does it bother you?"

"No," I said. "I like it. Tick-tock is boring. Ours goes tick-TOCK, tick-TOCK. Like footsteps. Like somebody walking."

He put down his file and looked at me for longer than was comfortable, the way he did when I said something I hadn't meant to be clever.

"Come here," he said.

That was how the bread arrived. The wheel, I mean. He set it on a cloth in front of me and folded my fingers onto the brass, which was cold, then less cold.

"Hold it. Don't turn it. Just feel."

I held it. He lifted the arm — the anchor, he called it, though it looked nothing like an anchor and everything like a bird's foot — and let the wheel breathe against it, tooth by tooth by tooth, and each tooth paid itself into my palm as a small knock, and I felt where the knock was strong and where it was shy, and I don't know how to explain what happened except that my hands understood it before the rest of me did. My hands said: *this tooth is proud and this tooth is worn and this one has been dropped sometime, poor thing, here's the scar.*

"There," Isandro said quietly. "Do you feel the unevenness?"

"Third tooth from the gap. And the ninth. The ninth is scared."

"The ninth is *worn*—"

"Scared," I said, "is when it jumps before you press it. The ninth jumps."

He stared at me. Then he took the loupe from around his neck — the little round magnifying eye he wore like jewelry — and bent over the ninth tooth for a long time.

"Pitted," he said finally, mostly to himself. "Fatigue crack starting. You can't see that without the glass. How did you—"

"I felt it jump."

"Mara." He straightened up. He had a particular voice for my name that I was still learning to read; this one had wonder in it, filed down small so it wouldn't show. "Nobody feels fatigue in brass. Clockmakers take fifty years to learn to guess at it."

"Well," I said, "I'm not guessing."

* * *

I should explain about my grandfather, since everyone in that town eventually did, usually behind their hands, sometimes not even bothering.

The man who built the master clock — the whole mechanism, every wheel of it — was my mother's father. Orchard family. The orchard family, the one whose name got shouted across fences and printed in the newspaper arguments and spoken in the shops the moment the shopkeeper thought I was out of earshot. I didn't remember him. I didn't remember much of anything from Before, and what I remembered wasn't useful: smoke, a dog's ear under my hand, a woman's voice saying a sentence I couldn't finish. No faces. The note pinned to my coat had said *forget the face*, and whatever had happened before the steps of the observatory seemed to have taken the instruction seriously and cleared out everything else while it was at it.

So when the townsfolk whispered *that's the old man's blood, that is*, they meant something they thought I couldn't understand. What I understood was simpler and stranger: strangers knew more about my hands than I did.

"They say your grandfather tuned that escapement by ear," Isandro told me one evening, both of us sitting on the stairs that spiraled up past the clock room, sharing the last of a loaf because neither of us wanted to walk down into town for supper and be looked at. "By ear, Mara. No instruments. He'd listen to the beat through the wall of the case and know which wheel was lying."

"Was he good at it?"

"He was the best I ever heard of." Isandro tore the loaf and handed me the bigger piece, which I noticed he always pretended was accidental. "My master in the city had a drawing of one of his trains. Kept it pinned up like a saint's picture. He used to say — what did he say—" Isandro squinted at the middle distance. "*Whoever built this learned that iron forgives you if you ask politely.* Something like that. Master talk. Half wisdom, half showing off."

"Did you ever meet him? My grandfather?"

"No." And then, because Isandro was constitutionally incapable of leaving a true answer alone until it was also a complete one: "I almost did. My grandfather worked the orchard lands, years ago. Our families — touched, somewhere back. Nobody talks about it now." He said it lightly, but he said it fast, the way you step over a hole in the dark.

I chewed my bread. Below us the clock went tick-TOCK, tick-TOCK, somebody walking, patient as winter.

"My hands are his, maybe," I said. "Even if I don't have anything else."

Isandro was quiet a moment. "Your hands are your own," he said. "But they've got good teachers in them. Dead ones count too."

That was the first warm thing anyone had said to me since the note said forget the face, and I didn't cry about it, because I was seven and proud, but I took the bigger half of the bread after that without pretending to be surprised, and he stopped pretending it was accidental, and that was a treaty between us that never got written down and never got broken.

* * *

Winter was coming on hard by then. You could hear it deciding in the river at night — a low grumble under the ice skin forming along the banks — and the light through the observatory's slit windows went thin and blue by four o'clock. We worked by lamp. Isandro had begun, cautiously, the way you feed a stray animal, letting me do actual things.

First it was holding parts. Then cleaning parts — pegwood and whiting and a patience I did not possess but was going to fake until it grew. Then, one blue afternoon, he put the anchor assembly on the bench, disassembled, all nine pieces laid out in a neat row like a procession, and said:

"Put it back together."

"All of it?"

"I'll watch."

Now. I want to be fair to myself here. I had watched him do it twice. But watching Isandro reassemble an escapement was like watching water run downhill — it gave you no information about how difficult it was, because he made difficulty invisible. I picked up the first piece and my hands went ahead of my head, the way they had with the ninth tooth, and I felt rather than reasoned: *this pivots there, this seats against this, the stones go gentle, gentle, they're the teeth of the bird's foot and birds don't clench.*

Halfway through I hit the pallet stones — little ruby slivers set in the arms — and one of them sat crooked in my tweezers and I knew, absolutely knew, without knowing how, that it wanted to be eased in at a slight angle, rolled onto its seat, not pressed.

"Stop," Isandro said.

My stomach dropped. "I broke it."

"You didn't break it. You're doing the stones wrong, though — you're pressing them square. They seat on an angle. Watch." He took the stone, showed me, and then — and this is the part I keep like a coin in my pocket even now — he stopped, and put it back in my tweezers, and said, "No. Show me how you were doing it."

I did it wrong again, on purpose, which felt like a crime.

"And what did it tell you?"

"That it doesn't want to go straight in."

"Right. So?" He spread his hands. "Fix it your way."

My way was the right way, arrived at crooked. The stone rolled onto its seat and sat there glowing like it had always lived there. Isandro looked at it, then at me, then took off his glasses and polished them on his sleeve for longer than glasses require, which I would learn was what he did instead of saying things.

"When I was apprenticed," he said at last, "my master made me assemble that same movement forty times. Forty. On the thirty-ninth I still forced the stones. You did it in one afternoon by asking the metal questions." He put his glasses back on. "Mara, I'm going to teach you properly. Not because you're a novelty. Because you'd learn it anyway, stealing it out of the air, and it might as well come with supper attached."

"Does it come with supper attached?"

"Butter," he said gravely, "if the beat allows."

* * *

We fell into a rhythm after that, the two of us and the clock, and I will tell you plainly that it was the first rhythm of my life I trusted.

Mornings: lessons. He drew the train of the clock on butcher paper — great wheel, center wheel, third wheel, fourth, escape wheel, anchor — a river of wheels all trying to fall, and the anchor standing in the current letting them down gently, one tooth at a time, forever. "Time isn't a line," he told me, tapping the paper. "It's a weight falling. Somebody just agreed to catch it often enough that it looks smooth." I copied the diagram until I could draw it blind, and then I drew it blind, and he checked it with his eyes closed, and pronounced it correct except that I'd given the anchor a smiley face, which he allowed to stand on the grounds that the anchor deserved encouragement.

Afternoons: work. Real work. I cleaned pivot holes with pegwood until my fingers smelled of whiting. I learned the names of things — collet, arbor, pallet, beat — and discovered that naming a thing made it behave better, the way a dog settles when you say its name. I learned to listen to the beat the way Isandro did, head tilted, and found that the tower spoke differently in cold weather, slower and deeper, like an old man in the morning, and Isandro wrote down my observations in the workshop ledger under a heading he created for the purpose: *Mara's Notes,* and beneath my first entry he wrote *verified,* which I looked at so many times the ink nearly wore away.

And evenings, when the lamps burned low and the river grumbled at the ice, we sat on the stairs with bread or soup and he answered my questions, or refused to answer them, which taught me almost as much.

One night I asked him why he'd stayed. Anybody could see the town didn't want him — the shopkeepers with their flat eyes, the men who spat when he passed, the word that traveled ahead of his name like a bad smell. Nakamura. The town said the name like it owed them money. He could have gone anywhere downriver. Instead he wound a clock for people who wouldn't sell him butter without counting his coins twice.

He was quiet a long time. The clock walked overhead. Somewhere below, the ice groaned and settled.

"When I took this post," he said finally, "I believed history was something that happened in cities. In books. To important people. Not here, not to clockmakers, not to me." He turned his cup in his hands. "Then a man at the ferry office looked at my papers and said my grandfather's name like he was spitting out a seed, and I understood the history had been here the whole time, waiting for me to arrive and stand in it." He shrugged, elaborately casual, which is how he did sincerity when it embarrassed him. "Besides. Somebody has to wind the clock."

"Somebody," I agreed, "and it may as well be the person the clock likes best."

"The clock doesn't *like*—"

"It goes softer when you're in the room. Tick-tock instead of tick-TOCK. Evened out. I've heard it."

He opened his mouth to argue, and then, being Isandro, actually listened to the beat for a moment, and closed his mouth again, and I counted it a victory and never let him forget it.

* * *

There was one more thing that happened that winter week, and I set it down last because it's the part I keep even now, when most of the rest has gone soft at the edges.

He'd begun letting me regulate the clock. Small corrections only — a hair of adjustment to the rating nut, raising or lowering the bob, teaching the pendulum to run true. One evening the beat had gone ragged, tick-tick-TOCK, a stutter in its walk, and he was elbow-deep in the fourth wheel with a burnished pivot and swore softly at something, and said, "Mara, listen. Tell me where it's catching."

I climbed onto the stool beside the movement and put my ear to the plates and let the sound come into me. Tick-tick-TOCK. Tick-tick-TOCK. Somebody walking with a stone in their shoe.

"Not the fourth," I said. "Lower. It skips coming *into* the tock. It's the escape wheel — a tooth is shy. Third from the gap."

Silence behind me. I kept listening. "Or ninth," I added, doubting myself. "Third or ninth. It's one of the ones that jumps."

Cloth rustled. Glass clicked — the loupe coming off his eye. When I turned around he was looking at me with an expression I hadn't seen on him before, and it took me years to name it correctly: it was grief and pride sitting in the same chair, holding hands.

"What?" I said. "Is it wrong?"

"The ninth tooth," he said slowly, "of the escape wheel. Your grandfather filed that wheel himself. There's a mark — a maker's mark — cut into the plate behind it. I found it the first week. I've been keeping it to myself because I didn't know what it would do to you."

"Show me."

He held the lamp close and turned my chin gently with one finger, and there, behind the wheel where only a dismantler would ever look, cut small and sure into the brass: a branch. Five blossoms on it, and a letter I couldn't read yet beside them.

"That's him," Isandro said. "His mark. And you just put your ear against his work and found the tooth he cut last, the one he was hurrying on. His hurry's still in it. A hundred years from now, somebody will hear it jumping, if they know how to listen." He paused. "You listen like him, Mara. Or he listens like you. I haven't worked out which, and I don't suppose it matters."

I put my finger through the wheels, careful, careful, and touched the shy tooth, and it jumped under my fingertip like a heartbeat answering, and I thought about the smoke and the dog's ear and the unfinished sentence, and for the first time the Before didn't feel like a locked door. It felt like a room in the house I was already living in. He had been here. His hands had been where mine were. The clock had been keeping him all along, the way it kept everything, one caught tooth at a time.

"Teach me the rest of it," I said. "All of it. Even the parts that aren't mine."

"Even those," Isandro said. "Especially those."

* * *

Two nights later, a carriage I did not recognize stood at the foot of the observatory hill in the blue snow-light, with the orchard family's crest on its door, and a man in a fine coat climbed our stairs two at a time and knocked on the tower door like he owned the knocking.

Isandro looked at me. I looked at Isandro. Overhead the clock went tick-TOCK, tick-TOCK, steady as a promise, and I had the sudden clear feeling — the kind my hands always knew before I did — that whatever came through that door was going to try to stop the beat.
