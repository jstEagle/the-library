## 8. The Orchard Family

The summons came on a Tuesday, which I mention only because Tuesdays in that town always seemed to arrive carrying bad news the way other days carried weather. It came by way of a boy of perhaps twelve, red-cheeked and breathless, who climbed the observatory hill as though the house below had lit a fire under him.

"You're wanted at Orchard House," he said, when he'd recovered enough to speak. "Mr. Aurelio's compliments."

"Does Mr. Aurelio know my name?"

The boy frowned. "He knows your trade. He says the clockmaker's to come down before supper, dressed decent, and mind his manners."

I looked down at my sleeves, which were spotted with clock oil and had been for some weeks, and considered the phrase *dressed decent*. "And if I decline?"

"He said you wouldn't." The boy delivered this with the flat certainty of someone repeating scripture, turned, and clattered back down the hill without waiting for a tip. Even the messenger children in that town had learned not to linger around me. Word travels fast in small places; by then I was three weeks into my tenancy of the tower, and the town's opinion of me had settled into something between wariness and superstition. The foreigner's grandson, they called me — though half of them said it wrong, and the other half said it right, which was worse.

Mara was in the clockroom, cross-legged beneath the great case, practicing her figures in the dust with one finger. She had developed a habit of narrating her sums aloud in a whisper, as though the numbers might overhear themselves and misbehave. When she saw me gathering my coat, she stood up too quickly, dusting off her skirt with both hands, watching my face the way she watched the pendulum — as though my leaving might mean my never coming back.

"I'm wanted down the hill," I told her. "The big house."

Her face did something complicated. She knew the house. I'd learned by then that there were whole geographies in the child's memory — the orchard above all — but she kept them locked away like drawers she didn't have keys for, and any mention of the place made her go quiet in a way that wasn't shyness so much as listening. Listening for something behind the words.

"Will you be long?"

"Till after dark, likely." I crouched to her level. "There's bread in the tin. Wind nothing while I'm gone — just watch the weights. If anything moves that shouldn't move, you come find me. Not before, not after. Understand?"

She nodded solemnly, the way she nodded at everything I said about the clock, with the gravity of a person being entrusted with state secrets. It occurred to me, walking down the hill afterward, that I had given a seven-year-old charge of the most important mechanism in the valley, and that I trusted her more than I trusted any adult in the town below. This should probably have alarmed me. At the time it only seemed efficient.

* * *

Orchard House sat above its own grounds the way a magistrate sits above a courtroom — you approached it already feeling accused of something. The road up from the ferry landing curved through bare apple rows, black-limbed and patient in the winter cold, and the house itself rose at the crest: gray stone, tall windows, a portico supported by columns that had been painted white sometime during the previous century and had been apologizing for it ever since.

A servant took my coat. Another servant showed me down a corridor lined with portraits of people whose faces suggested they had paid for their likenesses and regretted it. And then I was in the drawing room, standing on a carpet so thick it swallowed my footsteps, facing three people arranged around a fireplace like pieces on a board no one was winning.

I knew them by sight, of course — everyone in the valley knew them by sight, the way you know the phases of the moon — but knowing a thing by sight and meeting it are different animals entirely.

The eldest son stood with his back to the fire, which struck me immediately as the posture of a man who believed warmth was his entitlement rather than its source. Aurelio. He was perhaps thirty-five, broad through the shoulders, with his father's heavy jaw and his mother's calculating eyes, and he wore his inheritance the way some men wear medals — prominently, and slightly too high on the chest. He assessed me in one pass: hands, tools, face, name. I felt catalogued.

The widowed daughter sat nearest the window, hands folded in her lap, in mourning gray that had clearly been expensive and had clearly been worn past the point where expense mattered. Elena, they called her. She did not look at me directly. She looked at a point roughly six inches to the left of my head, and her stillness had a quality I would come to understand much later — the stillness of someone holding very carefully onto the lid of something.

And in the deep chair by the hearth sat the uncle, a dry stick of a man named Tomás with spectacles and a blanket over his knees despite the fire, watching everything with the expression of a man who had attended every family funeral for forty years and enjoyed most of them.

"The clockmaker," Aurelio said, by way of introduction, though I was plainly already present and had been for several seconds. "Nakamura's boy."

"Nakamura's grandson," I said. "The boy is a category I've outgrown."

Something flickered across his face — annoyance, or possibly amusement; with men like Aurelio the two are often indistinguishable — but he waved me toward a chair without offering it, which is a particular skill. I remained standing. I had learned early that in that house, every courtesy was a hook with bait on it.

"You've had the tower three weeks," Aurelio said. "Three weeks, and the master clock still doesn't run."

"The master clock hasn't run properly in eleven years," I said. "Your father let it decay for a decade before anyone thought to send for anyone. Three weeks is what it takes to learn what eleven years of neglect has done."

"And what has it done?"

I could have answered honestly. I had spent those weeks taking the great movement apart piece by piece, and I had found things in that gear train that no honest clock contains — wheels that drove nothing, arbors that turned in air, an entire hidden architecture threaded through the works like a secret written inside a letter. I had told no one, because I didn't yet understand what I'd found, and because a lifetime of being an outsider had taught me the value of knowing more than the room does.

"It's rusted," I said instead. "It's been patched badly by men who didn't understand it. And it was built by someone who understood it better than anyone alive today, which means it will take time."

"My grandfather built it," Aurelio said, and here his voice changed — dropped a degree, acquired weight, became the voice of a man reciting a claim of title. "Old Mateo. The finest mechanic this valley ever produced. The clock is a Vargas work, and it will be restored to a Vargas standard, or not at all."

"Then we're agreed on the standard," I said. "Though I notice the Vargas name hasn't sent anyone up the hill in eleven years either."

Tomás made a sound that might have been a cough and might have been laughter, quickly suppressed. Elena's eyes moved — briefly, involuntarily — to me, then away again. I noted it and filed it, the way I filed everything in those days, without understanding the drawer it belonged in.

"My grandfather," Aurelio continued, ignoring the interruption with the ease of long practice, "built that clock for this family. It keeps the valley's time. Every train schedule, every ferry crossing, every court date in three counties runs off that mechanism. It is, in every sense that matters, *ours* — and it has stopped, and the town whispers, and I will not have my family's property sitting dead on that hill while strangers poke at it."

"Strangers," I repeated. "You summoned one."

"To supervise him. To receive reports." He stepped away from the fire at last, and crossed to the sideboard, and poured himself a measure of something amber without offering any to anyone else, including his own family. "You'll understand that the situation requires discretion."

"The situation."

"The estate." He drank, set the glass down. "My father is four months dead. The orchard, the lands, the house, the shares in the ferry line — none of it is settled, because my father, in his last year, grew strange. Secretive. He spoke of documents. He rewrote papers. He met with lawyers and would not say why. And now the lawyers say the disposition of the estate depends upon—" he paused, choosing the word with care, "—*certain instruments*. Which cannot be located."

"Instruments," I said.

"Papers. A deed, possibly. My father hid something, clockmaker, and he was a careful man, and careful men hide things in careful places." Aurelio's eyes went to the window, and beyond it — I understood then, though I pretended otherwise — toward the dark shape of the observatory on its hill, small against the evening sky. "You are working inside the most careful place my grandfather ever built."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a door closed. And Tomás spoke for the first time, his voice dry as old paper: "The boy looks confused, Aurelio. Perhaps you should tell him what's actually being asked."

"What's being asked," Aurelio said, "is a report. Weekly. On the condition of the mechanism, on anything unusual found within it, on anything — *anything* — that isn't strictly horological. Sealed letters. Compartments. Markings. You'll bring word to me, and to me alone, and in exchange the family will fund your work properly — materials, wages, coal for the tower. Your master's shop in the city will receive the commission of its life. Everyone profits. The clock runs. The valley gets its time back."

I thought of the dummy gears turning in darkness, driving nothing, guarding whatever they guarded. I thought of a note pinned to a child's coat. I thought — absurdly, and I couldn't have told you why — of my grandfather's hands, which I barely remembered, and of a ledger page with a name scraped off it.

"You want me to open your grandfather's clock," I said, "and report to you before I even understand what I'm looking at."

"I want you to remember whose house this is," Aurelio said pleasantly, "and whose clock. And I'd think carefully, Nakamura, about how many friends a man has in a town where he has none."

That was the moment Elena finally looked at me — full in the face, for perhaps two seconds, with an expression I couldn't read and have never entirely forgotten. It wasn't warning. It wasn't pleading. It was closer to *measurement*, as though she were weighing me against something, though what scale she used I couldn't say. Then her eyes went back to their point six inches left of my head, and her hands folded tighter in her lap, and she said nothing at all.

Tomás leaned forward in his chair, blanket sliding, and fixed me with eyes sharp as awls. "Mind how you answer, young man. Families are like clocks. All the wheels look separate until you realize they're meshed." He smiled without warmth. "And every wheel turns another."

"A charming metaphor," I said, "from a man sitting in front of a fire he didn't build."

The silence that followed had texture. Aurelio's jaw worked once. But it was Tomás who laughed — genuinely, it seemed, a dry rustling sound — and settled back. "Oh, I like him. Aurelio, I like him. He'll do nicely."

"We'll see," Aurelio said. "Well? Your answer, clockmaker."

I gave him the answer I had decided on somewhere between the second portrait and the third: the answer of a man with no loyalties and fewer options. "I'll send reports," I said. "On the condition of the mechanism. As for anything else I find — I'll decide what a clockmaker's discretion covers when I know what's in it."

For a moment I thought he would refuse the terms. Then he smiled, and extended his hand, and I shook it, and his grip was exactly what I expected: firm, brief, proprietary. "Then we understand each other."

We understood nothing of the kind. We understood each other perfectly.

* * *

They walked me out — Aurelio did, personally, which I later understood was itself a message: *this man answers to me, watch him leave my house*. At the door he paused, coatless in the cold, breath steaming.

"One more thing," he said. "The child."

The word landed in my chest like a dropped wrench. I kept my face still, which cost me something. "What child?"

"Don't insult us both." His voice stayed pleasant, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "A girl was seen on the hill. Small. Dark-haired. Someone left her at the tower, and someone has been keeping her, and the town talks, and I don't care for talk I can't trace." He looked out past me, up the darkening slope toward the observatory, and his expression — for just a moment — lost its polish entirely. Something raw moved underneath it. Greed, I thought at the time. Only greed.

"If you're harboring anything of mine, clockmaker," he said, "you'll want to reconsider the arithmetic. A foundling is worth nothing. A foundling with a claim —" he shrugged, spreading his hands, letting the sentence finish itself.

"She's a child asleep on my floor," I said, "with no claim on anything except her supper. Good evening, Mr. Vargas."

I walked up the hill faster than dignity allows. Behind me the house windows burned yellow against the blue-black dusk, and ahead the observatory stood dark and patient, and I caught myself thinking — with a clarity that frightened me more than Aurelio's threats had — that I had just shaken hands with three people, and that one of them had never spoken, one of them had measured me like cloth, and one of them had looked at the tower on the hill with the expression of a man counting money that wasn't yet in his pocket.

Inside, Mara was asleep under the great case, wrapped in her coat, one hand curled near the pendulum's path — safely outside it, she'd learned the arc by heart — and the bread tin beside her, empty, a crust saved on a square of paper for my return.

She'd saved me a crust. From her own supper. In the house of strangers she couldn't remember and the family that had stopped the clock of her life without knowing it.

I sat down on the floor beside her, my back against the warm wood of the case, and listened to the silence where the tick should be. Eleven years dead, and still the whole valley bent around that stopped heart — trains, ferries, courts, and now three heirs circling it like gulls over a fish.

Tomorrow I would write my first report to Aurelio Vargas. It would be accurate, thorough, and completely useless. Because I had looked into the heart of his grandfather's clock, and I now knew something the heirs did not: the clock was not broken.

The clock was *locked*.
