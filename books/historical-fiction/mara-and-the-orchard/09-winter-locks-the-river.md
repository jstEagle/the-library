## 9. Winter Locks the River

The river began to die on a Tuesday.

I know it was a Tuesday because Isandro said so, and Isandro is a person who knows what day things happen on the way other people know their own names. He stood at the clockroom window with his chin in his hand and watched the water go strange and slow, and he said, "Tuesday. Ice always comes early on a Tuesday," as if the river had consulted him first, which knowing Isandro, it might have.

I did not understand at first that a river could die. I had only known it alive — brown and muscular under the bridge, carrying logs and gossip and once, spectacularly, an entire overturned rowboat with nobody in it, which the town discussed for a week. A dead river was flat and grey and had a skin like old milk. It made a sound I didn't like, a groaning sound, deep down under the ice, as if something enormous were turning over in its sleep and dreaming about us.

"Is it hurting?" I asked him.

"The river?" He glanced down at me. "No. It's just cold. It does this every year."

"Does it come back?"

"Every year." He said it the way he wound the great weights, steady and certain, hands that never hesitated once they'd decided. "That's the whole point of rivers, Mara. They're the most stubborn things there are. You can freeze one solid for four months and the moment you look away, off it goes again."

I decided to believe him, because Isandro was not a person who lied to make you feel better. He was, if anything, a person who told you the truth when you'd have preferred a lie, which took some getting used to but turned out to be worth it.

* * *

The last ferry of the season was scheduled for the following Saturday, though everyone in town spoke of it as though it were a funeral with a timetable. I heard them through the tower door when they came to argue with Isandro about the stopped clock — because they still came, every few days, men in heavy coats with their breath smoking, wanting to know why the master clock that ruled all the little clocks in all the shops and banks was still standing at eleven minutes past four like a sulking child.

"It's not broken," Isandro would say, for perhaps the hundredth time. "It's *stopped*. There's a difference."

"There's no difference to my wife," one man said, "who sets her bread by that clock and has eaten raw loaves for three weeks."

"Tell your wife the bread is the least of anyone's problems," Isandro said, and shut the door, and then stood with his forehead against it for a moment, which was how he apologized to people without them finding out.

I asked him once, while we sat among the gears with our sleeves rolled up, why he wouldn't just fix it. I had been learning the escapement by then — more on that later, because I intend to be smug about it properly — and it seemed to me that a clock was just a puzzle that had been taken apart by someone rude.

"Because it's not mine to fix," he said.

"Whose is it?"

"That," he said, "is an excellent question," and he looked at me in that way he had, where his eyes went somewhere else entirely, someplace with orchards in it, and then he ruffled my hair, which I pretended to hate and secretly arranged my head to make easier.

But this chapter is meant to be about the ferry, so I will get back to the ferry.

* * *

On Friday night — the night before the last crossing — I woke to a sound like a giant cracking its knuckles. I lay in my cot behind the clockroom, in the nest of blankets Isandro had acquired for me from a woman at the wool shop who asked no questions, which was my favorite kind of person, and I listened to the river talk to itself in the dark. Groan, crack. Groan, crack. Like a house settling, if the house were two miles long and made of water.

I got up and went to the window. The moon was out and hard as a coin, and the ice had come all the way across in the night — shore to shore, a white road where the brown muscle had been. And out on that white road, small and absurd, was a lantern.

A man was walking on the frozen river. Just strolling along it, testing each step, like the whole impossible thing was a pavement he owned.

"You'll want to see this," Isandro said from behind me. He had a blanket over his shoulders and his hair stuck up in six directions, and even half-asleep he moved quiet as oil. "First crossing. Everyone does it the first winter. It's a kind of madness the town is proud of."

"He'll fall through."

"Not yet. Give it a week and someone will. Every year someone does, and every year the same someone's cousin pulls him out, and every year they both drink too much cider about it. But tonight it holds." He leaned beside me at the window, our breath fogging the glass, and for a while we just watched the little lantern pick its way across the drowned world.

"Isandro," I said. "Tomorrow's the ferry, isn't it."

"Yes."

"And we're not on it."

"No."

I had known this, the way you know the sky is up — completely and without ever having decided it. But saying it out loud made it heavier somehow. "Am I supposed to be on it?"

He was quiet long enough that the river groaned twice.

"Why would you ask me that?"

"Because people put me places," I said. It came out smaller than I meant it to. "There was a door, before. Somebody put me somewhere once already."

I felt him go very still next to me. Isandro has a stillness like a held breath; everything about him stops at once, even, I think, his heart, or near enough.

"Mara," he said, "look at me."

I looked at the lantern instead, because looking at him felt dangerous, like looking directly at the sun during an eclipse, which a boy in town had told me would burn your eyes right out of your head and I believed him about nothing else.

"Mara. Look at me."

So I did.

"I don't know whose idea you were," he said, "and I don't know who wrote that note, and I'm going to find out — both of those things, I swear it, before the ice breaks in spring. But I know this." He crouched down so we were eye to eye, and his hands, which were always precise, were precise now too: they held my shoulders exactly, firmly, the way he seated a jewel bearing into its plate. "You are not cargo. Nobody is putting you anywhere. If you leave this tower, it will be because you chose to walk out that door, and it will be in daylight, and I will know where you're going, and I'll probably follow you anyway because you have the survival instincts of a moth."

"A moth?"

"You keep flying at windows."

"That was *once*."

"Twice," he said. "And once at a lamp. The point is—" and here his voice did something I hadn't heard it do before, something that cracked slightly in the middle like river ice, "—the point is that you are staying. With me. Through the winter and past it. That is not a plan I'm considering. It's a fact, like Tuesday, like the ice, like the river coming back. Do you understand?"

The window had gone almost fully white with our breath. I wiped a circle clear with my sleeve so I could see the lantern man again, and he had reached the far shore, and he raised his lantern high over his head in triumph, a tiny gold star celebrating at the edge of the dark.

"Yes," I said.

"Say it back, so I know it landed."

"I'm not cargo."

"Correct."

"I'm staying."

"With me," he said. "Past spring."

And here is the thing I have thought about many times since, in later years, when people ask me — and they do ask, visitors mostly, leaning on the clockroom rail — when I knew. When I knew that the tall, prickly, truth-telling apprentice with ink on his fingers was family. Everyone expects me to say the mechanism opening, or the deed, or the dawn the ice broke. But no. It was that night, at the window, with a madman waving a lantern on a frozen river. Because Isandro didn't promise to keep me safe, which anyone can promise and anyone can break. He promised me a *place*. He made himself into geography. And geography, unlike people, doesn't change its mind.

* * *

Saturday morning, the whole town walked down to the landing to watch the last ferry go, and we went too, because Isandro said watching was part of living in a river town whether you liked it or not, and also because he wanted to speak to the ferrymaster about the old manifests, though that thread belongs to a different chapter and I am telling this one.

The *Marta B.* — I learned her name from the painted letters, flaking gold — sat low in the black open water at the channel's edge, where the current kept a wound in the ice that steamed like a kettle. Her deck was stacked with crates and barrels and one enormous, indignant pig, and her rail was crowded with passengers wrapped in wool until they were less like people than parcels. Going downriver for the winter, away from the cold and the closed roads and the quarreling families — going anywhere, which in winter is the same as going somewhere better.

Isandro and I stood at the back of the crowd, by the coal shed. I remember the smell of it, coal dust and cold iron, and I remember that Isandro kept one hand on my shoulder the entire time, lightly, not holding me — just resting, the way you rest a hand on something that belongs where it is.

The ferrymaster rang his bell. There were speeches, briefly, from a councilman whose name I've since learned and whose speeches I've since endured, and then the lines came off, and the *Marta B.* backed her stern water into the channel wound, and the propeller churned, and she slid away down the grey throat of the valley trailing smoke and one long, mournful blast of her whistle.

The crowd waved. Some of them cried. A woman near us sang something low and cracked in the middle, and other voices joined it, and I found that my own throat had gone tight for people I'd never met, on a boat I'd never sail, leaving a town I barely knew. Grief is generous that way. It will lend itself to anybody.

"They'll be back," Isandro said. "When the river opens. First boat of spring — half the town stands on these banks to meet it. It's practically a holiday."

"When does the ice break?"

"Late March, usually. Sometimes April. It's not a thing anyone can promise." He paused. "Why?"

Because I was doing arithmetic. I was a girl who had been given a place and a promise and a pocketful of nothing else, and I had already learned — at seven, at eight, at whatever age I was that year; even I have never been sure — that promises have seasons. His promise was warm now, in November, with the wool blanket and the lantern man and the hand on my shoulder. But promises, like rivers, can freeze. And I wanted to know exactly how long the winter was, so I would know exactly how long I needed to hold my breath.

"Just wondering," I said, which was the first lie I ever told Isandro Nakamura, and I have felt guilty about it ever since, so consider this my confession, delivered decades late to a man who will read it and roll his eyes and tell me that keeping one secret for a few weeks hardly counts as a character flaw, and who will be wrong, because it counted, it counted enormously, though not in the way you'd think. It counted because it taught me what secrets cost, and that lesson — not the locket, not the deed, not any of the grand machinery of that spring — is what made me able to do what I did later. Everything important I ever learned, I learned in that tower, usually by accident.

We stayed on the bank long after the *Marta B.* had shrunk to a smudge and the smudge to nothing. The crowd drained away toward warm houses. The channel wound in the ice began, slowly, to heal itself, black water narrowing to a seam, the seam to a scar.

"She left without us," I said.

"Everyone leaves without us," Isandro said. "That's what makes staying interesting." Then he seemed to hear himself, and winced, and added: "That sounded better in my head."

"It sounded awful out loud."

"Thank you. I've raised you honest."

We walked back up the hill to the observatory as the light went blue and then violet, and the first snow of the year started coming down in fat, unhurried flakes, the kind that seem to be considering their options. Behind us the river lay sealed and silent, dreaming its groaning dreams. Ahead of us the tower rose against the last light, one lit window at the top like a lamp held up over dark water.

I did not know then what the winter would bring. I did not know about blizzards, or sabotage, or the coat I wore and who had sewn its buttons, or the word *escapement*, which would become the finest word I knew. I knew only this: the river was locked, the ferry was gone, and the man beside me had promised me a place in the world with the seriousness of a man seating a jewel bearing.

But that night, as I fell asleep to the sound of wind testing the tower's seams, I heard Isandro downstairs in the clockroom talking to himself — or not quite to himself — murmuring over paper and lamp-oil, tracing something, counting something, the way he counted when a problem had gotten its teeth into him.

And I remember thinking, in the drifting moment before sleep: whatever he's hunting, it has to do with me.

I was right. But I'll come to that.
