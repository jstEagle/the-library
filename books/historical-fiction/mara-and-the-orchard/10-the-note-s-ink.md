## 10. The Note's Ink

There is a particular kind of fool who believes that a mystery, once noticed, will simply explain itself if you look at it long enough. I had been that kind of fool for most of my apprenticeship — staring at a stopped clock as though the gears might grow ashamed and start turning again out of politeness. Master Ovidio used to say that clocks never confess; they only ever testify, and only under disassembly.

He said it about clocks. But by my third week in the observatory tower, with winter pressing its thumb against every windowpane, I had begun to suspect the principle applied more broadly.

The note was still in my coat pocket. I had told myself I kept it there for practical reasons — evidence, reference, the sort of thing a careful man preserves. This was a lie of the same quality as the ones I told Mara about how much sugar remained in the tin. The truth was simpler and less flattering: I could not put it down, because I could not stop reading it.

*Forget her face.*

Four words. Whoever wrote them had pressed hard enough to emboss the paper from behind, which told me they were angry or frightened or both, and that they had written quickly, probably standing up, possibly outdoors. The ink had feathered slightly at the terminals of each letter — bled into the fibers — which meant the paper was cheap and unsized, the kind sold by the ream at the chandler's on Front Street, the kind any household in this town might keep in a kitchen drawer.

Any household. Except that the note had been pinned to a child's coat on the steps of an observatory at night, in November, by someone who knew exactly where the new clockmaker would be winding the master clock, and when.

I laid it flat on my workbench beside the disassembled heart of the master clock, whose dummy gears sat in their oiled rows like soldiers dressed for a war that would never be declared. Two puzzles. One table. And me, a man who had come to this river town believing history happened elsewhere, now unable to walk ten paces without tripping over somebody's buried past.

"Right," I said to the empty room, because talking to oneself is either madness or method, and I preferred to think of it as method. "Let's find out what you're made of."

* * *

The paper was the easy half. Paper has a biography if you know how to read it: the watermark, the rag content, the way it yellows, the deckle edge or lack thereof. I held it against the lamplight first. No watermark — consistent with cheap stock. Then I tilted it through the light slowly, the way one checks a watch dial for hairline cracks, and there, faint as breath on glass, a chain line pattern: two parallel wires running lengthwise, spaced a finger's width apart.

Every paper mill lays its pulp on a wire screen, and every screen leaves its fingerprint. I had spent enough years repairing ledgers and mending account books for clients who cared about such things to know that the chandler's paper on Front Street carried three wires, evenly spaced. The stationer up the hill sold a heavier stock with a laid pattern so fine it was nearly wove.

Two wires, wide-spaced. I went down into the town that afternoon on the pretext of buying lamp oil — a pretext so thin that Mrs. Halloran at the general store looked at my full oil can and then at me with the expression of a woman deciding not to charge me for the entertainment — and I bought sheets of paper from four different shops, folding each into my pocket like a man collecting alibis.

Only one matched. Not the chandler's. Not the stationer's. The paper sold in bulk to the great houses along the orchard road — the stock the Vidal family bought by the crate for their correspondence, their accounts, and, apparently, their abandonments.

I stood in the shop doorway holding that knowledge the way you hold a hot coal passed hand to hand: wanting to be rid of it, unable to open my fingers.

Because here is what I had not yet let myself think, in all the weeks since that night on the steps: notes do not write themselves. A child does not walk herself up an observatory hill in the dark. Someone had carried her — or walked beside her holding her hand, or driven her in a cart and lifted her down — someone with warm hands and a cold purpose, someone who had pinned those four words to her small chest and turned around and gone home. Gone home to a house with crates of good paper in it.

Someone inside the orchard house had put Mara on my doorstep like a parcel marked *return to sender*, except there was no return address, only an instruction, and the instruction was a lie I had already refused.

*Forget her face.* As though the face were the problem. As though the child were a debt that could be discharged by misremembering.

I bought the lamp oil after all. It seemed important, somehow, to leave the shop having purchased something honest.

* * *

The ink took longer, and longer things have a way of becoming obsessions. Ink, unlike paper, is chemistry — iron gall, mostly, in these parts, sometimes logwood, sometimes aniline dyes if the writer fancied themselves modern. Iron gall ink has a virtue useful to my purposes: it ages. It oxidizes, darkens from blue-black toward brown, and it eats into paper at a rate that can be read like tree rings, roughly, if one is patient and slightly unhinged.

Mine was browned at the edges but still blue-black in the stroke centers. Fresh-ish. Months old, not years. Written sometime in the autumn — which fit, obviously, since Mara had arrived in autumn, but confirmation is its own comfort.

More interesting was what the ink did under water. I cut a corner scrap from the note's bottom margin — barely a fingernail's worth, taken with the guilt of a man defacing scripture — and floated it in a saucer. Old iron gall, properly aged, holds fast. Younger ink, ink only months in the paper, releases a faint halo of gray-blue, like a bruise surfacing.

The halo came up promptly and generously. Autumn ink. Written within weeks, perhaps days, of the night she appeared.

And there was something else, something I nearly missed because I was looking at color instead of shape. Under magnification — I own a jeweler's loupe and I am not ashamed to use it for non-jewelry purposes — the pen strokes showed a hesitation pattern. A right-handed writer, yes, but one who had paused mid-word twice, and both times the pause fell before the same letter: the *f*. *Forget. Face.* Twice the nib had lifted, and twice it had come down again with a tiny splash of excess ink, the telltale bloom of a hand pressing harder than it meant to after a moment of doubt.

Whoever wrote *forget her face* had faltered at the very word *forget*. Had stopped, and started, and pressed too hard.

You cannot unlearn a thing like that. I set the loupe down and sat for a long while in the clockroom with the weights hanging silent above me and the town's noise rising faintly through the floor — sled runners, a dog, someone beating a rug with the rhythm of a person taking out a grievance on an innocent object — and I understood that I was no longer investigating a note. I was investigating a person. A specific person, with a right hand, a conscience that interrupted her — his? its? — writing, and access to Vidals' paper.

The *her* was instinct, not evidence. I noted it anyway. Instinct is just experience wearing a disguise.

* * *

Mara found me at the bench that evening, as she always did, drawn up the tower stairs by some internal tide. She had taken to appearing at dusk with whatever she'd been doing all day folded into her manner — today it was sawdust, I noticed, in her hair and on her cuffs, though she claimed to have been "just walking."

"Just walking," I repeated. "Through the orchard. Where nothing happens."

"Nothing happens," she agreed solemnly, and climbed onto the stool she considered hers, and watched me work with those eyes that missed nothing and reported less.

I had been avoiding a question for weeks, and that evening it finally worked its way loose, the way a seized screw works loose — suddenly, with a sound you feel in your wrist.

"Mara," I said. "The note. Do you remember anything about it? Who pinned it. Whether you saw a face. Anything."

She went very still, the way children do when a grown-up asks a question that matters more than it pretends to. Her hands, which were always moving — worrying a thread, tracing gear teeth in the air — went quiet in her lap.

"It was dark," she said. "There was a coat sleeve. Wool." She frowned with the effort of retrieval, a frown I recognized from watching her puzzle over escapements. "It smelled like apples. Not eating-apples. Cellar-apples. The kind that go soft."

Wool and cellar-apples. The orchard house had cellars, I happened to know, because the whole town knew, because the whole town knew everything about the Vidals except the things that mattered. Apples stored in autumn. A wool coat worn in autumn. A child delivered in autumn.

"You did well," I said, and meant it, and she glowed in the way of children who are praised for accuracy rather than obedience, which is the only praise worth giving.

Then she said, "Did you forget my face yet?"

The question landed in the middle of the workbench between us like a dropped tool. She wasn't being arch. She genuinely wanted to know whether the instruction had been followed — whether the world had done what the note asked, whether forgetting was a thing that simply got done to you, the way weather gets done to you.

"No," I said. "I'm afraid I'm a very bad forgetter."

"Good," she said, with the finality of a judge closing a case, and asked to see the pallet arbor again.

* * *

I should say something about what the discovery did to me, because a man who narrates his own betrayals owes the listener at least an attempt at honesty. The obvious feeling would have been anger, and there was anger, banked and structural, the kind built into a wall rather than piled in a grate. But underneath it, embarrassingly, was something closer to grief — and grief on behalf of people I had never met, over a wrong done to a child I had known barely two months.

Because trace the logic through. The paper came from the orchard house. The ink was fresh. The hand hesitated at *forget*. Therefore the writer loved the child — or feared for her, which is love wearing armor — and gave her away anyway. You do not hesitate over the word *forget* unless some part of you is screaming against your own hand. Whoever left Mara on those steps had not discarded her. They had *placed* her, deliberately, with the one person in town newly arrived, unaligned, and paid by no family — placed her with the clockmaker the way you place a document in a vault, and pinned on instructions designed to keep the vault sealed.

Which meant the note was not an abandonment at all. It was a hiding.

And hidings, in my widening education in the ways of this town, had a way of connecting to other hidings. To dummy gears in a master clock. To deeds that lived where deeds ought not live. To names scraped out of ferry ledgers by hands that presumably also had access to good paper.

I thought about my grandfather then — really thought about him, for the first time since arriving, since the tavern keeper had spat the name Nakamura at me like a pit. A man who worked the orchard lands and vanished from its records. A man erased. And now a child delivered to his grandson's doorstep with orders to be forgotten. There was a symmetry there that I did not yet understand, but symmetry, in my trade, is rarely decorative. When two sides of a mechanism mirror each other, it is because power flows between them.

* * *

The next morning I did something I had been circling for days the way a fly circles a window: I walked up the orchard road and stood at the gate of the Vidal property, in the gray hour before the household stirred, and I looked.

The house sat above its terraces like a man above his accounts — broad-shouldered, shuttered, self-satisfied even in winter. Smoke rose from the kitchen chimney. Somewhere behind the bare apple rows, a dog barked twice and thought better of it. On the service side, a woman in a gray shawl was shaking crumbs for the birds, and I watched her long enough to see that she moved like someone listening for footsteps behind her.

I did not go in. I had no warrant, no invitation, and — I admitted, turning back down the road — no plan beyond the animal satisfaction of having seen the source. But as I walked, the pieces rearranged themselves in my head with the click of a detent engaging:

Paper from the house. Ink weeks old. A hand that hesitated. A child who remembered cellar-apples and wool. A hidden deed in a hidden train, built by a grandfather the town had agreed to forget. And somewhere inside that shuttered, self-satisfied house, a person who had chosen the clocktower — *my* clocktower, *me* — as the safest place in the valley for a little girl whose face must not be forgotten precisely because everyone else had been ordered to forget it.

They had used me. That stung, I'll grant. An apprentice clockmaker, hired to wind a dead clock, conscripted sight unseen into somebody else's desperate scheme — I hadn't even gotten a choice in my own mystery.

But beneath the sting, colder and more durable, was the other thing. The thing that would not let me sleep that night and would not let me stop, thereafter, until the whole mechanism lay open on the bench of the world.

If someone inside that house had hidden a child in my tower, then someone inside that house believed the child was worth hiding. Worth more than convenience. Worth more than safety, maybe — because whoever left her had also, by leaving her, surrendered any chance of seeing her grow, unless—

Unless they visited. Unless they watched. Unless, on some evening I had not thought to mark, a figure in a gray shawl or a heavy coat had stood at the foot of the observatory hill, looking up at a lit window, checking that the vault still held.

I reached the tower as the light was failing. Mara was at the bench already, sleeves pushed up, practicing the delicate business of lowering the pallet fork onto the escape wheel without letting it chatter — a task requiring exactly the patience she otherwise refused to spend on soup. She looked up when I came in, and her face did the thing it did perhaps once a week: opened, entirely, like a window unshuttered.

"You were gone long," she said.

"I went walking," I said. "Through the orchard road. Where nothing happens."

"Nothing happens," she agreed, and returned to her wheel.

I hung up my coat, and my hand brushed the pocket, and the note crackled inside it like a small dry voice. *Forget her face.* Written by a hand that couldn't. Hidden in a house that kept its own secrets in cellars and crates and locked trains of gears.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would begin asking the town's older paper-sellers and ink-grinders who bought what, and when, and in what quantity. Tomorrow I would narrow the field. Because a household contains many hands, and not all of them hesitate at the same words — and I intended to find out which member of the orchard family had stood on my steps in the dark, pinning a lie to a child's coat with fingers that shook.

I did not yet know it was a woman. I did not yet know she wept. All I knew, as I trimmed the lamps and listened to Mara's small tongue of concentration press between her lips, was that the trail ran uphill, through frozen ground, straight to the door of the great house —

— and that someone, somewhere in that house, had recently learned that the clockmaker was asking questions. Because the next morning, pinned to the observatory door with the same neat, furious stitches I had seen on a child's coat, was a second note.

It said only: *Stop.*
