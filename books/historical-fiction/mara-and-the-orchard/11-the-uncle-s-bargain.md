## 11. The Uncle's Bargain

The note said *Stop*, and I did what any sensible craftsman does with an anonymous instruction: I read it twice, admired the stitching, and kept working.

Not out of bravery. Bravery is a word people use afterward, when they want their fear to sound like virtue. No — I kept working because the alternative was to stop, and if I stopped, I would have to think about what stopping meant: that someone up the hill had looked at a clockmaker with dirty fingernails and decided he was worth threatening. That flattered me more than it frightened me, which tells you something about my judgment at twenty-three.

Mara read it too. She stood on tiptoe at the door, her breath fogging the iron hinge, and studied the single word the way she studied everything — completely, without blinking, as though the world owed her an explanation and she intended to collect.

"Someone doesn't like your questions," she said.

"Someone doesn't like *anyone's* questions. It's a whole region."

"You asked about the paper." She said it flatly, without accusation, which was somehow worse. She'd been there when I'd spread the abandonment note under the lamp beside the stationer's stock list from the ferry office, comparing watermarks, holding both to the flame to watch the fibers curl the same direction. She was six years old and she already understood evidence better than the constable did.

"I asked about the paper," I agreed.

"And now somebody stitched you a warning."

"Now somebody stitched me a warning."

She considered this. Then she said, with the terrible practicality of children, "You should ask louder ones, then. If you're going to be punished either way."

I have thought about that sentence many times since. It may be the only genuinely good advice anyone gave me that winter.

* * *

The summons came two days later, and it came properly — not stitched to a door this time but delivered by a boy with a sledge and chilblains, on card stock heavy enough to stun a rabbit. *Mr. Tomás Vidal requests the pleasure of Mr. Isandro Nakamura's company at four o'clock,* and beneath that, in smaller script, as if the house itself were embarrassed by its own grandeur: *the Orchard House.*

Tomás Vidal. The uncle. I knew him the way the town knew him: by reputation and by silhouette. He walked the river road every morning at seven regardless of weather, a broad man gone soft at the edges, wearing a fur collar that made him look like something respectable stuffed by a taxidermist who had run out of patience. He tipped his hat to ferrymen. He paid for the church roof. He was, everyone agreed, the reasonable one — which in a family like his was less a compliment than a diagnosis.

I left Mara with Mrs. Ferreol downstairs, along with strict instructions involving bread, the alphabet, and absolutely no touching of the gear train. Mrs. Ferreol, our landlady by way of the observatory keeper's charity, had taken to the girl with the fierce efficiency of a woman whose own children were grown and scattered, and she looked at my instructions and said, "Boy, I raised six. Go be threatened by your betters."

The climb to the Orchard House took forty minutes through snow that squeaked like new leather. The orchard rose around me as I went — bare trees in their winter rows, black and patient, pruned so precisely they looked less grown than engineered. I tried to see them as Mara must have seen them in whatever fragmentary smoke-and-dog memory she carried, and failed. To me they were just wood waiting for spring. History again, happening to other trees.

The house itself sat at the crest like a man leaning back in a chair he owned. Two stories of grey stone, glassed porch, a weathervane in the shape of — I am not inventing this — an apple. Inside, a housekeeper took my coat and my dignity in the same motion and showed me into a study that smelled of pipe smoke, leather, and money that had been old long enough to stop trying.

Tomás Vidal sat behind a desk the size of a coffin lid. He rose, extended a hand warm and dry as a stove brick, and said, "Nakamura," with a pause before the name just long enough to let me know he'd noticed it.

"Vidal," I returned. Fair's fair.

"Young Nakamura. Your grandfather worked these orchards, I'm told. Before my time, you understand. Before most everyone's time, it seems. Names have a way of coming back." He smiled the smile of a man who has practiced looking harmless in mirrors. "Sit. Drink?"

It was not yet five o'clock anywhere civilized, but this was the river country, where the sun went down at half past three in January and drinking began whenever the dark did. I accepted a small glass of something amber and warming, mostly because refusing seemed like information, and I was trying, that afternoon, to give away as little as possible.

He talked first. That was the tell, though I didn't know it yet. A man who wants nothing talks last; he makes you spend yourself before he spends anything. Tomás Vidal talked about the weather, the ice, the price of apples, the lamentable state of the ferry company's boilers. He talked for ten minutes without once saying why I was there, and I sat in a chair too deep for my back and drank his brandy and waited, the way I'd learned to wait over a movement that wouldn't come right until it was ready.

At last he set down his glass and became, visibly, a different man — or rather, he let the first one step aside.

"The clock is stopped," he said.

"The clock is being repaired. There's a difference."

"My nephew believes it should have been repaired weeks ago. My niece believes—" he waved a hand, delicately, the way you wave off smoke "—my niece believes many things. The town believes the master clock is the town's pulse, and a town without a pulse starts asking questions about whether it's alive. And I believe—" here he leaned forward, fur collar whispering against the desk edge "—that a young man of your evident talents should not have to work in a freezing tower on a stipend that wouldn't keep a cat."

"I manage."

"You manage. Yes. I've heard you manage remarkably. New mainspring, re-cut escape teeth, the whole train cleaned — alone, in winter, with a child underfoot." He said *a child* lightly, but not so lightly that I missed it. Neither of us mentioned whose child. That silence sat between us on the desk like a third glass neither of us poured.

"What are you offering?" I said, because I have never seen the sense in making men like this build their own scaffolds.

"Funding." He opened a drawer and laid out papers — real papers, drawn up by a solicitor whose name I actually recognized, which frightened me more than the stitching on the door ever had. "Materials at cost, doubled labor for the winter months, a workshop of your own in the spring. Stone floor, north light, room for lathes. Everything your master's shop never had room for." He watched my face while he said it, and I'll grant him this: he wasn't wrong about what he saw. A workshop. North light. Room for lathes. A man can be offered a kingdom and stay calm; offer him a bench by a window and watch his hands.

"In exchange?" I said.

"Simplicity itself. Reports. As you open the mechanism — and I understand from the trade journals that the great clock's train contains certain... irregularities — you write to me. Monthly. What you find. What you remove. What you leave in place. Drawings if necessary. Nothing more."

"Nothing more."

"Nothing more." He refilled my glass unasked, which is a technique older than either of us. "You understand, Nakamura, that I am not my nephew. Aurelio wants the clock opened because he believes it holds his birthright, and he wants it opened loudly, with lawyers, with the whole town watching him win. Elena wants it sealed forever, for reasons she keeps to herself with impressive stubbornness. And I—" he spread his hands, a man presenting an empty tray "—I only want to know what's inside before other people decide it belongs to them. Knowledge first. Is that so corrupt a position? You're a tradesman. Would you let a rival open your client's case before you'd looked yourself?"

"It's not my case," I said. "That's rather the point of a case."

He laughed — a genuine laugh, which unsettled me worse than the brandy. "Oh, I like you. Your grandfather, they say, was the same. Straight answers. Hands that didn't shake." He paused. "They also say the ledgers lost him. Did you know that? Somewhere between one year's manifest and the next, the Nakamura name simply stops appearing, as though the man had never picked fruit on this hill at all. Curious thing, ink." He looked at me with mild, terrible kindness. "Names come back, Nakamura. But they come back to people who are useful. Think about it. The offer stands until the thaw."

* * *

I walked down through the orchard in the blue dark, and I will be honest with you, because a narrator who lies about his own temptations is worthless: I wanted it.

I wanted the workshop the way a drowning man wants a rope and resents himself for wanting it. I lay awake that night in the tower room above the clockroom doing arithmetic — materials, coal, the cost of a lathe, the cost of a life — and every sum came out the same: one letter a month, a few drawings, and in exchange a future with a floor that didn't tilt and a window that faced the light. Who would know? The mechanism belonged, by every law I'd been taught, to whoever held the deed to it, and the deed to the clock was — well. That was the question, wasn't it. Perhaps it belonged to the Vidals after all. Perhaps reporting to Tomás was simple diligence, properly addressed.

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter and colder, another voice: *they wrote forget her face on a child's coat, and one of them stitched Stop to your door, and this man knows about the ledgers.* He hadn't learned it from me. Someone in that house had told him a clockmaker was asking about erased names, which meant the family discussed me at their dinner table, which meant I was already a piece on their board whether I signed anything or not. Refusing the offer wouldn't take me off the board. It would only leave me unpaid for being on it.

I got up twice to look at Mara sleeping in the alcove behind the pendulum wall, wrapped in two blankets and Mrs. Ferreol's spare shawl, one fist curled under her chin. The second time, she opened her eyes — she always knew, even asleep, when someone stood over her; a habit, I understood later, of children who have been left once — and said, "Did the rich man give you money?"

"No."

"Was he nice?"

"He was very nice."

"That's how they get you," she said, and rolled over, and was asleep again before I could decide whether to laugh or write that down somewhere permanent.

In the morning I wrote the refusal. Not on his fine card stock — on my own cheapest paper, the kind that drinks ink and blots. I kept it short, because everything else I might say would be ammunition:

*Mr. Vidal — I thank you for your offer and decline it. The mechanism will be opened in full, and what is found will be reported in full, publicly, to those with lawful claim. I do not sell reports. I repair clocks.*

Then, because I was twenty-three and couldn't help myself, I added a postscript:

*I am given to understand my grandfather's name was removed from your ledgers. When the clock runs again, I intend to put it back.*

I sent it up the hill with the chilblained boy, and then I stood in the clockroom with the lamps lit and the dummy gears gleaming in their secret cradle, and I felt — not fear exactly. Resolution's cheaper cousin, the feeling you get the moment after you've done the brave stupid thing and before the consequences arrive.

Mara appeared at my elbow with her sleeves pushed up, ready for the morning's lesson on the escapement. She looked at my face, then at the papers still lying on the bench — his solicitor's draft, which I'd kept, God knows why, perhaps as evidence, perhaps as temptation to be stared down daily.

"You said no," she said.

"Yes."

"To the money and the workshop and all of it."

"All of it."

She nodded slowly, absorbing this, and then delivered her verdict with the gravity of a magistrate: "Good. Because you'd have needed a bigger coat pocket for all that money, and yours is full of springs."

It was true. My coat pocket was full of springs. It has been, in one way or another, ever since.

We were halfway through the lesson — her small fingers finding the pallet faces, learning the tick's grammar, tick and *tock*, the heart's two syllables — when I noticed the light through the west shutter going strange, thickening, the way lamplight looks through smoke. I climbed to the gallery window and looked north, toward the ridge, and saw the sky there had turned the color of wet slate, low and boiling, moving toward us faster than weather has any right to move.

By noon the wind had begun to sing in the tower louvers. By two, the world outside the glass had stopped existing altogether, and the first flakes were finding their way in through gaps no clockmaker had ever gotten round to sealing —

— and the storm, which would hold us prisoner for three days, had Mara begin, in the small hours of the first night, to talk about her mother's voice, and a promise that had been made to her under the trees.
