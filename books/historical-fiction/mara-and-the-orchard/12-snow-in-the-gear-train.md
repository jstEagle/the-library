## 12. Snow in the Gear Train

The snow came in sideways on Tuesday, like it had somewhere to be and no patience for scenery.

I knew it was Tuesday because Isandro kept a calendar nailed to the clockroom wall, one page for every month, and he crossed off each day at dusk with a pencil he sharpened on the stone windowsill until the sill looked like it had been chewed by a very particular mouse. He crossed off Tuesday, squinted out the east window, and said a word I was not supposed to know yet.

"What does that mean?" I asked.

"It means," he said, "that you should fetch the second blanket from my room before the wind changes its mind about being polite."

The wind did not change its mind. The wind doubled down. By the time I came back with the blanket — the good one, wool, smelling faintly of lamp oil and of him — the world outside the glass had gone white in every direction at once, as if someone had shaken the town like a snow globe and forgotten to set it down again. The river was gone. The orchard was gone. The rooftops below us were suggestions. Even the observatory's own dome had disappeared into the gray, so that we seemed to float inside a cloud that had decided, for its own reasons, to keep two people company.

"Three days, if it's a proper one," Isandro said, in the tone other men used for announcing deaths or tax assessments. "Maybe four."

"We have food?"

"We have bread, cheese, dried apples, and a jar of something your predecessor left that might be honey or might be a punishment." He held the jar up to the lamp. Whatever lived inside it shifted, slowly, like it resented being observed. "We'll ration the apples."

I want to say I was afraid. People expect a girl of eight to be afraid of a blizzard, and mostly I was — but underneath the fear there was another thing, small and warm and shameful: I was glad. The tower was the only home I had ever been given on purpose, and a storm that sealed its door was a storm that said *stay*. Nobody had ever told me to stay before. They had pinned instructions to my coat instead.

*Forget the face*, the note had said. Whoever wrote it had not reckoned on Isandro Nakamura, who could forget a face the way a clock can forget to tick — which is to say, not at all, and not without breaking something essential.

* * *

The first day, we worked.

That is his answer to everything — cold, hunger, grief, me asking questions — work. He showed me how to pack the pendulum case with cloth so the cold wouldn't make the steel shrink and lie to us about the time. He taught me the word *contraction*, which I liked immediately because it sounded like a small animal. We wrapped the weight cables where they passed near the drafty window, and he let me hold the lamp while he climbed the ladder to the gear train, and from below I watched him move among the wheels with his sleeves rolled up, patient as a priest, touching each gear the way you'd touch a sleeping dog you didn't entirely trust.

"The snow gets in here," he called down. "Through the louvers. It melts, then it freezes, then it's ice in the teeth of the escapement, and then the whole town wakes up believing it's noon when it's midnight, which is the sort of lie a clock should never tell."

"Why not?"

"Because everything else lies," he said, "on purpose. The clock only has to do one thing. If it can't do that one thing, there's nothing left to trust."

I thought about that all through supper, which was bread and cheese and one dried apple each, and the honey-that-might-be-a-punishment, which turned out to be honey after all, just honey that had been alone too long and grown opinions.

That night the wind found the tower's seams and played them like a fiddle. I lay in my cot in the little room under the clockroom — my room, the first room in my life with my name chalked on the door in Isandro's careful hand — and listened to the building groan and settle around me like an enormous animal turning over in its sleep. Once, the whole tower shuddered, and dust sifted down through the lamplight, and I heard Isandro's boots cross the floor above me, slow, checking, checking, and then return. He did not come down. He did not need to. Knowing he was up there walking the gears was enough to make the groaning sound less like dying and more like breathing.

* * *

The second day, the drifts won.

They sealed the lower door completely — we knew because Isandro opened the trapdoor above the stairwell and a fist of snow fell through and landed on the landing with a sound like a dropped mattress. He stared down at it for a long moment.

"Well," he said. "That's that."

"That's what?"

"We're not going anywhere until the thaw does some of our digging for us." He closed the trapdoor, brushed the snow off his hands, and looked at me with an expression I had learned to read by then: the look of a man recalculating. "Three days of food, four days of storm. We'll eat half rations tomorrow. Today—" he glanced around the clockroom, at the tools laid out in their felt rolls, at the great dark shape of the master clock with its hidden train of useless gears, at the calendar with its chewed-pencil days, "—today we'll do the escapement properly. You've been watching long enough. Time you learned it."

So he sat me down on the low stool beside the movement, and he taught me the tick and the tock.

Not the words. The *thing itself*. He put my fingers — my cold fingers, which he warmed first between his palms without making any fuss about it, the way you'd warm a tool you needed — against the anchor bracket while the escape wheel turned above it.

"Feel that? Tick. That's the wheel being caught. Tock. That's it being let go. Catch and release, catch and release. Every clock in the world is just that, over and over. A wheel that wants to fall, and a hand that says *not yet*."

"Not yet," I repeated.

"Not yet," he agreed. "And then — *now*. That's all time is, Mara. Somebody holding something back, and then letting go, exactly once per swing, forever." He tapped the pendulum rod gently with one knuckle. It swayed, ticked, caught. "Your job, someday, is to be the hand. You don't push. You don't hurry. You hold, and you release, and you never, ever lie about when."

I was good at it. I don't say that proudly; I say it the way you'd report weather. My hands understood the mechanism almost before my head did. When he let me try setting the pallet stones, my fingers found the right pressure on the second attempt, and Isandro went very quiet, and when I looked up he was looking at my hands with an expression I couldn't name — something old and startled moving behind his eyes, like a fish under ice.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing." He cleared his throat. "You have — steady hands."

"My grandfather was a clockmaker," I said, because he'd told me that, because it was one of the few true things anyone had ever given me about myself. "Maybe he's in them."

"Maybe he is," Isandro said softly, and went to fuss with the weights, and did not talk for a while, and I let him have his quiet because even at eight I understood that some silences are doors you shouldn't knock on.

But the storm kept us shut in, and shut-in people run out of doors to leave closed.

* * *

It happened on the third night, though I suppose it started earlier, with the lamp and the chessboard he'd carved for me out of scrap brass and boxwood, with the pieces that were clock parts pretending to be armies — a bob for a king, pallet stones for bishops. We'd played three games. I'd lost all three, but in the third I took his queen with a pawn, and he made such a ceremony of mourning her — laid her down flat, folded his hands, bowed his head — that I laughed so hard the cocoa nearly went up my nose.

Then the wind screamed particularly hard against the north shutter, and the lamp guttered, and in the sudden dark I saw it: smoke. Not real smoke. The kind that lives behind your eyes. Smoke rolling across a green place, and a dog barking, and a woman's voice saying words I couldn't catch, and my chest went tight and hot all at once, and when the lamp steadied I was crying without having decided to.

Isandro didn't panic. I'll give him that forever. Other people, when a child cries, either freeze or descend on you like weather. He simply set down the chess pieces, moved his stool next to mine, and waited, the way you wait for a wheel that wants to fall.

"I remembered something," I managed. "From before."

"Do you want to tell it?"

"I don't know how to tell it. It isn't a story. It's just — pieces. Like the gears that don't do anything." I wiped my nose on my sleeve, which he permitted without comment, though I saw him notice. "There was a dog. Big, brown, one ear that stood up and one that didn't. She slept under my cot. And there was a woman who sang when she thought I was asleep, and her voice was low like the river under ice, and I used to pretend to sleep just to hear it longer." My throat closed. I pushed through it, because once you start telling a thing you have to get to the far side of it or it owns you twice. "And there were trees. Rows and rows of them. And she held me up once — I remember her arms shaking, she wasn't strong, but she held me all the way up so my hand could touch a branch, and there was a blossom on it, one blossom, out of season, stupid early blossom, and she said—"

I stopped. Because this was the part. This was the piece of gear that connected to everything else, and I had never said it aloud, and saying it would make it either more real or less, and I couldn't bear either one.

Isandro waited. Not-yet, not-yet. Then: *now*.

"She said, 'Whatever they take, they can't take the trees. As long as one tree stands, you belong somewhere.'" My voice cracked clean through on *somewhere*, like a bad gear tooth. "And then the smoke came. I don't remember after the smoke. Just the coat. The coat I was wearing when—" I gestured at nothing, at the note, at the whole machinery of my abandonment, which I still only understood in fragments, like a movement seen through frosted glass.

The clockroom was very quiet except for the tick, the tock, the catch-and-release, forever.

"They can't take the trees," Isandro said at last. He said it carefully, tasting it, the way he tasted a strange gear when he wanted to know what it meshed with. "She said that."

"As long as one stands."

He nodded slowly. He got up, crossed to the window, and stood looking out into the white blindness where the orchard would be, if anything could be, if the world hadn't been erased. And I watched his back and saw him thinking, really thinking, the way his shoulders changed when his mind was working a problem bigger than clocks.

"Trees outlive men," he said, mostly to the glass. "That's the whole trick of an orchard. A man plants what he'll never sit under. It's the most hopeful thing a person can do with dirt." He turned around. His face had settled into something I'd learn later was resolve, but that night just looked like a man deciding to carry something heavy. "Mara. Listen to me. Your mother gave you a promise, and promises are like deeds — worthless unless somebody honors them. So we're going to honor it. When the snow goes, I'm going to find those trees. All of them. And as long as one stands, you belong somewhere — and where it stands happens to be a tower full of clocks, with a clockmaker in it who has gotten extremely fond of a girl who takes his queen with a pawn."

"You let me take the queen."

"I absolutely did not," he said, with dignity. "You took her fair. I mourned her fair."

I laughed, wet and hiccupping, and he came back and sat down, and neither of us said anything for a while, but it was the good kind of silence now, the silence of two gears that have finally found the same tooth.

Before he tucked me in, he paused at my door with the lamp.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I could never forget your face. I want that noted somewhere official."

"It's noted," I said. "I'm noting it right now."

"Good." He snuffed the lamp. "Sleep. The clock will hold."

And it did. Catch and release, catch and release, all through the last night of the storm, while the wind spent itself against the stone and lost.

* * *

On the fourth morning the light through the east window came back gold instead of gray, and Isandro stood at the glass for a long time without speaking, and when I joined him I saw why.

The world had been remade overnight into something enormous and silent. Drifts buried the lane to the level of the fence posts. The river below wore a lid of ice and new snow, smooth as unmarked paper. And up the hill, half-buried, black branches stark against the blue-white morning, stood the orchard — the real one, the one from behind my eyes, close enough now that I could count the trees.

Except.

"Isandro," I said. "Count them."

He counted. I watched his lips move. I counted too, and got the same wrong number he did, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the rock, all the way down to the frozen river.

Rows don't end early. Rows don't have gaps with raw stumps in them, stumps pale as bone where the saw had bitten. Someone had been cutting trees all winter, up there, while the town argued about inheritances and nobody watched the orchard.

"There are fewer than there should be," I whispered.

Isandro's jaw tightened. He reached for his coat, already calculating drifts and distances and whether the path could bear a man's weight yet — and stopped, because below us, at the edge of the whited-out lane, a figure had appeared out of the morning, small and hooded, picking a careful line uphill toward the observatory steps.

A woman. Alone. In a storm's aftermath, with no servant and no sleigh and no business that honest people announce.

Isandro's hand found my shoulder and drew me back from the window, into the shadow of the great clock, and we watched her climb.
