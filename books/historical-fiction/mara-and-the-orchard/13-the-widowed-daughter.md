## 13. The Widowed Daughter

I want to say I knew something was wrong the moment the bell rang, but the truth is less flattering: I was elbow-deep in the going train with a screwdriver between my teeth, and when the knock came at the tower door after dark, my first thought was that Aurelio's men had run out of patience and had come to break my fingers alphabetically.

The second thought — arriving as I wiped my hands and descended the spiral stair — was that nobody knocks politely at an observatory door in February unless they don't want to be seen knocking.

Mara was already awake. She slept in the old astronomer's alcove now, a nest of blankets she had arranged with an architectural ambition that would have impressed her grandfather, and she stood at the top of the stair with her hair down and one hand on the rail, listening the way she listened to clocks — entirely, without blinking.

"Stay up here," I said.

She didn't answer, which from Mara meant *I heard you and I will decide for myself*, but she didn't come down either, so I counted it a partial victory and unbolted the door.

The woman on the steps wore a gray traveling coat buttoned to the throat and a veil that made her face a rumor. She held a lantern low, so its light pooled around her boots instead of climbing toward her chin. Everything about her said *I am not here*. It was well done. I'd have believed it myself if I hadn't spent three months learning to read the small dishonesties of mechanisms.

"You're the clockmaker," she said. Not a question. The voice under the veil was low and even, the kind of voice trained by expensive rooms to never rise above the level of the chandelier.

"I'm the apprentice," I said, because precision matters, "and it's past ten."

"I know what time it is." Something flickered through the evenness — not quite humor, more like exhaustion wearing humor's coat. "You keep the only honest clock in the valley. That's rather the point of you, isn't it?"

Behind me, faintly, the master clock ticked on with its borrowed heart, the patched train I'd rebuilt holding time the way a man holds a door shut against weather — with his shoulder, and not forever.

"Madam," I said, "if you've business with the observatory, bring it at business hours. If your business is with me—" I looked at the lantern, the veil, the hour. "—then you'd best say it fast, because I'm expected to wind at eleven, and I don't like witnesses who arrive veiled."

The veil tilted. She was looking past me, up into the dark of the stairwell, and though I couldn't see her eyes I felt them land somewhere above my shoulder and stop there like birds coming to roost.

"The child," she said. "Is she well?"

Every hair on my arms stood up under my shirt.

* *

Let me back up, because I have learned — from clocks, mostly — that a story told out of order tells lies.

Mara had been with me since autumn. The note pinned to her coat that first night said four words: *Forget this child's face.* No name, no signature, no instruction beyond forgetting, which is a strange thing to ask of anyone and a stranger thing to ask of a clockmaker, whose entire trade is the refusal to forget — we remember every tooth of every wheel, every repair, every failure, all of it written down in ledgers that outlive us. Forgetting is the one thing we are professionally incapable of.

I had not forgotten her face. I had fed her, taught her the escapement, watched her hands do things with tiny brass parts that took me years, and somewhere along the way stopped thinking of the note as anything but the opening line of a mystery I intended to solve.

I had suspects. I'll admit that freely. A town like this one runs on suspicion the way the master clock runs on weights — constantly, silently, downhill. Aurelio Vidal, the eldest son, wanted everything and trusted no one; his uncle Tomás wanted everything and smiled while wanting it; and there was a widowed daughter mentioned in town gossip with the frequency people mention weather — often, and with nothing useful attached. Elena. Married two years, widowered — widowed — before the marriage had time to become a story. Lived at the orchard house. Rarely seen.

So when the veiled woman on my steps asked after Mara's health with a crack running straight through the middle of her careful voice, I did what any sensible apprentice does when the mechanism finally turns over.

I stepped aside and let her in.

* *

She climbed the stair slowly, lifting her skirts clear of the treads with one gloved hand, and when she reached the clockroom she stopped in the doorway as if she'd walked into a wall made of memory.

Mara had not gone back to bed. Of course she hadn't. She sat on the workbench with her feet dangling, wrapped in her blanket like a small disapproving magistrate, and she looked at the visitor with those grave dark eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

The woman's lantern hand shook.

It was the coat that did it, I think. Mara's coat hung on its peg by the stove — a child's coat of good wool, chestnut brown, with bone buttons and a lining that had once been silk and had been mended, badly, by someone who understood fabric but not thread. The woman crossed the room without seeming to decide to, put two fingers to the collar, and turned it back the way you'd turn the page of a book you'd read a hundred times.

There, inside the collar, where a maker stamps his name: a monogram. *EV.*

"Elena," Mara said.

Not a question. A seven-year-old doesn't ask questions of ghosts. She simply names them and waits to see if they hold their shape.

The veil came off then. Elena Vidal — I knew her face from the market square, from church processions, from the particular way grief arranges a woman's features into something the town can look at without discomfort — stood in my clockroom with her brother's daughter's coat in her hands and cried the way I imagine dams fail: quietly at first, structurally, and then all at once.

Mara slid off the bench. She didn't go to her. She stood a pace away, arms folded inside the blanket, watching the tears with the expression of an engineer inspecting a leak — concerned, cataloguing, unconvinced.

"You're the one who wrote the note," Mara said.

Elena went still. Even her weeping paused, the way rain pauses, not finished but gathering.

"Yes," she said.

"You told him to forget my face."

"Yes."

"Why?"

And here is where I should report that the woman broke down and confessed everything in a rush, and here is where the truth refuses to cooperate, because Elena Vidal dried her eyes with the heel of her glove, set the coat back on its peg with the care of someone returning a borrowed tool, and said:

"Because faces get remembered, and remembered things get used. Sit down, Mr. Nakamura. This will take longer than your winding."

* *

She wouldn't sit. She stood at the window instead, looking out at the black river, and spoke with her back to both of us, which I recognized as the posture of someone who cannot lie to a child's face and has arranged her life accordingly.

"Aurelio watches this tower," she said. "You know that. His men count your candles. They know when you eat, when you sleep, when the girl goes out to fetch water. Do you know why?"

"I have theories."

"Then let me give you the true one." Her breath fogged the glass. "Our father — Mara's grandfather — built this clock. Everyone says so. What nobody says, because saying it would require admitting what he was capable of, is that he built it *twice*. Once for the town, to tell the hours. And once inside that, for himself."

"The dummy gears," I said. "I found them in November. Wheels that drive nothing. A train that moves and accomplishes nothing — except that it isn't nothing, is it. It's concealment. It's a lock wearing a gear's clothes."

Elena turned. In the lamplight her face had lost the arrangement grief gives it and shown something rawer underneath. "He told me the night before he died. He was very ill and very clear, the way dying men are permitted to be. He said the orchard would be sold within five years of his funeral. He said his sons would tear each other and the land apart, and the town with them, and that the only way to stop it was to make the deed impossible to find until the fighting burned itself out." Her mouth twisted. "He was right about all of it, which is the worst inheritance he left any of us."

"And the deed is in the clock."

"I believe so. He never said so plainly. He said, 'When the mechanism opens, the orchard will speak.' And he gave me—" she pressed her lips together, "—he gave me a task. Not the deed. A key. And instructions about a child, if a child ever needed hiding."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below us the river ice groaned, long and low, the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Mara's mother was my sister-in-law," Elena said, softer now. "She married into the family and was never forgiven for it — wrong side of the river, wrong kind of name. When she died, and Mara was left, Aurelio saw exactly one thing when he looked at that little girl: leverage. A ward he controls is a claim he controls. Whoever holds the child holds sympathy, holds the courts' attention, holds—" she gestured vaguely at the whole clockroom, the whole tower, the whole winter, "—holds everything. My uncle wants her raised gentle and grateful in his household. My brother wants her raised as a bargaining chip. I wanted her raised."

"So you carried her down the hill at night," I said. "Pinned a note to her coat. Left her on the steps of the one building in town nobody in your family can enter without being seen."

"My grandfather designed this tower," Elena said. "Aurelio believes the deed is here, and so he must pretend, publicly, that he believes nothing is here. If he were caught sneaking into the observatory, he'd confirm the rumor he's spent a year denying. So the tower is the one place in this valley his greed cannot follow her — not openly. It was the safest door I could find." She looked at me, finally, fully. "And behind that door was you. An outsider. A Nakamura. A man with no stake in the quarrel and, I hoped, a spine."

"That's a great deal of hope to hang on a stranger's spine."

"It was a greater deal of fear to hang anywhere else." Her composure cracked again, briefly. "Mr. Nakamura, I have lain awake every night since October imagining her cold, or hungry, or crying for me in the dark. Every night. And every morning I have gotten up and worn my widow's gray and eaten breakfast across from my brother and said nothing, because saying anything — letting my face soften, letting my eyes go to the hills where this tower stands — would hand her to him. Do you understand? I could not even grieve where anyone could see it. Grief leaves traces. Traces lead Aurelio here."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight I followed one of his men's rotation schedules. There's a gap between the midnight and one o'clock rounds. Forty minutes." She almost smiled. "I planned it like an escape. I've gotten good at planning like an escape. It's the only skill my family has left me."

Mara had not moved from her spot by the bench. But during all of this she had been doing something I recognized, because I'd done it myself for months: she was watching Elena's hands. Hands tell truths mouths won't. And whatever Mara read there — the shaking, the care with the coat, the way they kept returning to the buttons, touching the bone like rosary beads — it moved something in her, because she unfolded one arm from the blanket and said:

"You mended my lining. Badly. You used the wrong stitch."

Elena made a sound that was half laugh and half wound. "Silk lining needs a fell stitch. I know. Mother taught me properly and I rushed it, because it was the last night I had and I wanted—" She stopped. Started again. "I wanted her to be warm, and I was crying too hard to sew straight."

"You were crying," Mara repeated, testing it.

"All night."

Mara considered this with the gravity she brought to escapements and other delicate mechanisms. Then she crossed the space between them — three steps, no more — and did not hug her, exactly. She stood close enough that the blanket touched the gray wool, and she leaned, slightly, the way ice leans on a riverbank before deciding, and Elena's arms came around her, and that decided it.

I looked at my tools. I looked at the clock. I became, with tremendous professional discipline, extremely interested in neither.

* *

Later — much later; the winding came and went unremarked, because some nights the clock can mind its own hours — Elena sat at my workbench with tea she didn't drink, and Mara lay asleep in her alcove, and the tower held the three of us the way it held everything: patiently, at a slight chill, waiting to be understood.

"She can't stay hidden forever," Elena said. "That was always the flaw in my plan. Hiding is a loan, not a gift. Spring comes. The river opens. Aurelio will move — he's already bought ferry berths, did you know that? Three of them, before the ice even breaks. He's preparing to leave with the deed the moment he has it, and take the child with him as proof of claim."

"He'll need the deed first."

"He'll need *you* first. You're the only man alive who understands that mechanism, and he knows it, and it is eating him alive." She turned her teacup a quarter-turn, a habit, a fidget, a woman managing her own hands. "Which brings me to what I came to beg, and I will beg, because pride is a luxury I surrendered on a doorstep in October."

"Keep her hidden," I said. "Yes. You said that on the stairs."

"Not only that." She looked up, and her eyes were dry now and terrible in their steadiness. "Keep her hidden *from me*. If I come again — if I weaken, and I will, I have already broken every rule I set myself just by climbing these stairs — then send me away. Don't let me near her. Because every visit makes her traceable, and every trace makes her valuable, and the day Aurelio learns his lost niece sleeps thirty feet beneath the master clock is the day she stops being a child and starts being currency." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "I left her once to save her. I can bear to leave her a hundred times, if leaving is what saves her. What I cannot bear—" and here the steadiness failed at last, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth until she could finish, "—what I cannot bear is that I have done it so long I've grown *good* at it."

The fire settled. Below us, patient as scripture, the clock ticked.

I thought of my grandfather's name scraped off a ferry ledger. I thought of a note with four words and no signature. I thought of a girl who had learned to sleep in a clocktower without once asking when she'd go home, because asking was the same as hoping, and hoping was the same as bleeding.

"Mrs. Vidal," I said. "Elena. I'll tell you something, and then I'm going to say no to you, and I want you to hear both halves in order."

She braced herself. Widows are good at bracing.

"I'll keep her safe. That part you have for free; you've had it since October, whether you knew it or not. But I won't hide her from you, and I won't send you away, and here's why: because a child raised on secrets learns she *is* a secret, and there is no colder bed than that. I know. I grew up a name nobody would say. Your father hid a deed inside a clock to protect a town, and the cost of that protection was twenty years of silence, and look what the silence grew — your brother, your uncle, a valley full of people sharpening knives over fruit trees. Secrecy is how this family loves. It's also how it destroys. Someone in this story has to try the other way."

"You don't know Aurelio."

"No," I agreed. "But I know what he fears, because everyone fears it: being seen clearly, in daylight, by a crowd. Whatever comes in spring, it will come in the open or it won't come at all. And Mara—" I glanced at the alcove, at the small mound of blankets rising and falling with the even rhythm of a well-regulated escapement, "—Mara will not spend another season as a note pinned to a coat. Four words, Mrs. Vidal. *Forget this child's face.* Well. Nobody's forgotten it. Not me, not you, not this tower. Maybe it's time somebody wrote her a better sentence."

Elena looked at me for a long moment. Outside, far off downriver, the ice groaned again — longer this time, deeper, a note changing underneath, the way a mechanism changes when a hidden train finally engages.

"When spring comes," she said slowly, "when the ferry berths are called and Aurelio makes his demand — because he will, he's already circling — you'll have to choose, Mr. Nakamura. He'll see to it. He'll arrange the whole board so that someone must be left behind, and he'll make sure the someone is never him." She rose, drew her veil back down, became once more a rumor in gray. "My father hid the truth in a clock because he believed time would sort the righteous from the greedy. I've never had his faith. But standing here, watching a Nakamura defend a Vidal child in a tower full of stolen hours—"

She didn't finish it. Some sentences are better left as load-bearing walls than finished into floors.

At the door she paused, one gloved hand on the bolt, and looked back once at the alcove where her brother's daughter slept.

"She has your stubbornness," Elena said quietly. "Grandfather's, I mean. She fixed the third-wheel pivot last month, didn't she. I watched through the glass, one afternoon, from the tree line. Her hands. Exactly his hands."

And then she was gone, down the stairs, into the dark, forty minutes of gap closing behind her like water.

I bolted the door and stood a while in the cold entry, listening. The clock ticked. The ice groaned. And from the alcove, in a voice thick with the edge of sleep, Mara said:

"She's coming back, isn't she."

It wasn't a question. Seven-year-olds don't ask questions of ghosts, or of promises.

"Yes," I said.

"Good," said Mara, and rolled over, and was asleep before I reached the stairs — leaving me alone with the lamp, the ticking, and the sudden, heavy knowledge that spring was closer than the calendar admitted, and that when the river broke, it would not be the only thing coming apart.

Above me, in the dark heart of the clock, the dummy gears turned their empty circles, keeping a secret they had kept for twenty years — and I fell asleep that night certain of only one thing: the secret was running out of time faster than we were.
