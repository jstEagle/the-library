## 15. The Eldest Son

The summons came the way bad news always comes in a river town: by hand, on good paper, with too much wax.

A boy of perhaps ten stood at the observatory door holding an envelope as if it might bite him. He wouldn't come inside — I offered, because Mara was napping and it seemed the civilized thing to do — but he shook his head and looked past my shoulder into the dark of the tower like something in there owed him money.

"Mr. Aurelio says come at four," he said. "He says don't be late."

"He says a great deal for a man who sent a child to do his talking."

The boy's eyes went wide, and he left at a pace that suggested I'd confirmed every rumor about the clockmaker being touched in the head. I couldn't blame him. In the three weeks since I'd arrived, I had become, in the town's estimation, a foreigner with a foreign name who lived alone in a tower with a stolen child and a broken clock, and no amount of accurate repair work was going to dislodge that story. Stories in this town were load-bearing. You didn't pull one out without the whole wall coming down on you.

I read the note twice. It was brief, expensive, and unsigned except by a seal — an orchard branch pressed into red wax, which told me everything before I'd parsed a single word. *Mr. Nakamura will attend Mr. Aurelio Vidal at the house above the orchard at four o'clock. Matters concerning the clock.*

*Matters concerning the clock.* As though the clock were a horse that had gone lame and the family had retained me out of civic duty rather than because their grandfather had built a vault into its heart and everyone with a drop of orchard blood could smell what was inside it.

Mara woke while I was dressing for the cold. She sat up in her cot beneath the great gear that did nothing, hair wild, watching me button my coat with the flat, unblinking attention she gave to anything she intended to understand.

"You're going to the big house," she said.

"I am."

"Aurelio's house."

"You know his name."

"Everybody knows his name." She said it the way children say things that are simply true: the river is wet, winter is coming, Aurelio is the name people lower their voices around. "Elena's brother. He has a dog that bit the postman. He has a gun room." A pause, and then, with the terrible precision of the very young: "Is he going to take me?"

I crouched so we were eye to eye. This was the part of guardianship nobody warns you about — that you must lie fluently or not at all, and I had discovered I was constitutionally incapable of the first and increasingly attached to the second.

"No," I said. "Nobody is taking you anywhere. You are the keeper of the escapement until I return, and if the tick goes wrong, you put the brass wedge back where I showed you and you wait. Can you do that?"

She nodded solemnly, and I felt the lie settle somewhere behind my ribs like a swallowed stone. Because the truth was that a man with a gun room had summoned me, and men with gun rooms do not summon clockmakers to discuss pendulums.

* * *

The house above the orchard was the kind of building that announces itself a quarter mile early. It sat on the rise with its back to the trees, all pale stone and dark shutters, and even stripped bare by winter it managed to look like it owned the view rather than merely enjoyed it. The orchard itself stretched down the slope toward town — hundreds of black, pruned trees standing in rows like an audience waiting for someone to say the wrong thing.

Two men were shoveling the drive. They stopped when I passed, and they did not resume until I'd reached the door, which is a particular species of courtesy that means *we are watching you* dressed up as diligence.

Aurelio Vidal received me in a study lined with books that had clearly never been opened, spines uncracked, dust in the gilt. He was perhaps forty, broad through the shoulders, with his father's heavy jaw and his mother's quick eyes, and he wore the expression of a man who has spent his whole life being told he is the heir and has recently begun to wonder whether anyone intends to honor the paperwork.

"Nakamura." He pronounced it carefully, correctly, which was somehow worse than getting it wrong. "Sit."

I sat. The chair was designed to make sitting feel like a concession.

"You've been in the tower three weeks," he said. "The clock still doesn't run."

"The clock doesn't run because your grandfather built it not to run. It would take a lesser craftsman a week to make it go. It will take me considerably longer to make it go *honestly*."

Something flickered across his face — I want to say respect, but respect in a man like Aurelio arrives pre-corrupted, already calculating what it can be traded for. He leaned back and laced his fingers over his waistcoat.

"My grandfather," he said, "was a sentimental old fool who liked mechanisms more than people. Everyone says so. What nobody says — what everyone thinks but nobody says — is that he spent the last ten years of his life up in that tower more often than any sane man visits a clock. And that when he died, half the papers in his desk were missing, and the other half were worthless."

"And you believe—"

"I believe nothing. Belief is for priests." He smiled, and it was a good smile, the kind that has opened many doors and closed a few permanently. "I *suspect*. I suspect that a clock that cannot run is not a clock. I suspect that a man who hides papers does so because the papers are worth hiding. And I suspect that you, Mr. Nakamura, know exactly what I'm talking about, because your family has been in the business of knowing things about this valley longer than mine has been in the business of owning it."

There it was. The Nakamura name, laid on the table between us like a card he'd been waiting to play. My grandfather had worked these orchards — picked fruit, fixed fences, and according to the ferry ledger with its scraped-away line, once mattered enough to be erased. I kept my hands still and my face blank, which is the only inheritance my master ever gave me that I've fully honored.

"I fix clocks," I said.

"You fix clocks." He nodded slowly. "Then here is what fixing clocks pays. Restore it. Make it run. And whatever you find in there — and we both know there is something in there, don't insult us both — you bring to me. First. Before anyone else. There is a position for a man like you at this house. A workshop of your own. A future."

"And if I find nothing?"

"Then you'll have made the clock run, and I'll have paid you well for a job the town already thinks you can't do." He rose, ending the audience the way kings end audiences, and walked me to the door himself, which was its own message: *I am not afraid of you; I am investing in you.* At the threshold he paused.

"One more thing. The girl."

My blood went very quiet.

"She's not your concern," he said mildly. "Whatever arrangement you've made, it's temporary. Children drift. Rivers carry them off. If she turns up missing one day, no one will trouble you about it." A beat. "But if she's in that tower when you open the mechanism, Mr. Nakamura — if she sees what's in there before I do — then our arrangement changes shape entirely. Do we understand each other?"

"We understand each other perfectly," I said, "in the sense that I now understand exactly what sort of man you are."

He laughed — genuinely, warmly, as though I'd paid him a compliment. Perhaps I had. Men like Aurelio collect contempt the way other men collect medals.

* * *

I noticed the watchers before I was halfway down the hill.

They were not subtle, because they did not need to be. One stood at the corner by the cooper's yard, ostensibly examining a barrel stave with tremendous interest. Another leaned against the ferry office, hat low, doing nothing with such commitment that he had to be paid for it. By the time I reached the river road I counted four, positioned with a geometry that could not be accidental: two with sightlines to the tower door, one covering the path up from the orchard, one idling near the ice-house where anyone leaving the tower after dark would have to pass.

A siege, then. Not of soldiers — of boredom. Aurelio had understood something essential about the tower: he didn't need to break in. He needed only to make sure that whatever came out of it came out through his hands. Watch the door long enough and the door tells you everything.

I stopped in front of the man by the ferry office. Up close he was young, chapped-lipped, wearing a coat too good for his posture. Hired muscle, freshly hired.

"Cold day to admire the architecture," I said.

"Evening, sir." He touched his hat brim. Perfectly polite. Perfectly immovable.

"You'll be wanting relief shifts. Standing in river wind rots the lungs, and dead watchers tell their employer nothing."

Something flickered in his jaw — surprise, maybe, that I'd named the game aloud. I let him sit with it and walked on.

At the tower steps, Mara was waiting in the doorway with her arms crossed, having decided sometime during my absence that doors were for keeping and she was the keeping kind.

"There's a man by the cooper's," she reported. "And one by the ice-house. And one who walks the orchard fence every twenty minutes. I counted."

"Did you."

"He drops his cigarette ends. Every twenty minutes, same spot." Her chin lifted. "People think children don't see things. People are wrong about most things."

"People are," I agreed, and held the door for her, and locked it behind us with the heavy iron key that suddenly weighed a great deal more than iron.

* * *

That night I worked by lamplight with the shutters drawn, and I confess I worked differently than I had before. For three weeks I had approached the mechanism as a puzzle — beautiful, strange, mine to solve at my own pace. Now every dummy gear I lifted felt like evidence, every hidden channel a secret with a price on it. The tower had stopped being a workplace and become a vault under watch, and I was the only man alive who knew the combination, which sounds flattering until you notice that the men outside the door have guns and patience, and you have neither.

Near midnight, Mara padded down the stairs in her blanket. She did this sometimes — slept badly, came down, watched my hands move among the gears until her eyes closed again. That night she didn't sleep. She climbed onto the stool beside me and studied the mechanism with her chin in her fists.

"They want what's inside," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

"Grandfather put it there so they *couldn't* have it." Also not a question. She had heard Elena's name spoken softly enough times, and pieced together enough of the town's whispers, to assemble a picture no adult had given her. Children build cathedrals out of scraps.

"That seems to be the shape of it."

"Then why are you opening it?"

I set down my tweezers. Somewhere below us the river groaned against its forming ice, a long animal sound, patient and enormous.

"Because a secret that only dies with me isn't a secret anymore," I said. "It's just a grave. Your grandfather didn't hide the truth to bury it, Mara. He hid it to keep it safe until someone came along who could carry it out into the light. I intend to be that someone. But I intend to do it on our terms — not his." I tipped my head toward the window, toward the watcher at the cooper's yard, invisible now in the dark but present as a toothache.

Mara considered this with the gravity she brought to everything, then reached out and, with one small finger, tapped the escapement — gently, precisely, the way I'd taught her, the way her hands seemed to have known before I ever taught them.

"Tick," she said, "and tock. We have time. They don't know that. They think time belongs to them because they own the clock."

I looked at her — this scrap of a girl, abandoned on my steps with a note instructing the world to forget her face — and I thought: *they have no idea what they're besieging.*

I was right. But I was also wrong, in ways I would learn within the week, when I came home one evening to find the tower door ajar and Mara alone with a broken escapement and a look on her face I had never seen before — the look of a Nakamura, though she didn't bear the name, who had just done something impossible with her hands and knew it.
