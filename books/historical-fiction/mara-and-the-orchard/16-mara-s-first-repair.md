## 16. Mara's First Repair

I want to say, before anything else, that I did not mean to break it.

That is the truth, and I will hold to it even though nobody asked me for a defense and even though Isandro, when he heard the story later, only raised one eyebrow in a way that suggested he believed the opposite. But he was not there. He was down the hill being held prisoner by two men with bad coats and worse manners, which is the whole reason any of this happened, so he does not get an eyebrow in the matter.

Here is what happened.

Isandro had gone into town that morning — the fourteenth of some gray month whose name I never learned because winter in that town was less a season than a long argument — to buy lamp oil and, if the shopkeeper would sell to him, a new file. Aurelio's men had been watching the tower for six days by then. They stood at the bottom of the hill like fence posts that had learned to smoke. Isandro called them *the posts* and made a joke about them needing painting, and I laughed, because with him I was learning how laughing worked when you were not just copying other people.

"Stay inside," he said at the door, the way he said it every time. "Wind nothing. Touch nothing. Read the ledger if you're bored."

"I'm never bored," I said, which was a lie we both allowed.

He went out. I watched him from the clockroom window go down the hill with his collar up and his toolbox under his arm, and I watched the two posts fall in behind him at a distance, the way they always did, herding him gently toward town as though a man carrying tools needed escorting. He turned once at the bend and looked up. I ducked. It seemed polite.

* * *

The tower without Isandro is a different building. I have thought about this a lot since. When he is there, the tower is full of ticking and muttering and the smell of oil and the small sounds of him being wrong about things and then right about things, usually in that order. When he is gone, the tower is just tall. The stairs go up farther than stairs should. The wind finds every seam. And the master clock — our clock, the great dead-hearted thing in its case of black walnut — stands at the center of everything like a patient animal that has decided not to die yet but is keeping its options open.

I had been reading the ledger for exactly as long as I could stand it, which was not long, when I went up to look at the escapement.

I should explain what I knew by then, which was more than anyone guessed and less than I pretended. Isandro had been teaching me since autumn. He taught me the names of the wheels first — the great wheel, the second wheel, the escape wheel, all the little brass society of them, each with its job and none of them able to do another's. He taught me that the escapement was the heart's own valve: the piece that let the power out one tooth at a time, tick, tock, tick, tock, so that all the weight and fury stored in the driving weights came out as something a person could live inside. "Anyone can store force," he said. "The whole trick of a clock is spending it slowly." I think he was talking about himself a little, though I did not know enough then to say so.

I had cleaned pallets under his eye. I had held things. I had, twice, been permitted to lower the crutch off its arbor while he caught the pendulum, and both times my hands had done what he asked before he finished asking, and both times he had looked at them — my hands — with an expression I could not read then and can read now, because it is the expression people wear when they are looking at a ghost doing housework.

But he never left me alone with the clock open. That was the rule. Rules are made to be obeyed until the day they are made to be broken, and I knew which kind of day this was going to be approximately forty minutes after he left, when I heard boots on the stair.

Not his boots. His boots had a drag on the left heel from years of standing at benches. These were even, deliberate, heavy — the boots of men who wanted you to hear them coming. There were two sets.

I did what any sensible person does when strangers climb toward them in a tower: I blew out the lamp and hid behind the clock.

It is, I will admit, a large clock. Hiding behind it was not difficult. What was difficult was staying quiet while the door opened below and the boots came up, and while a voice I already knew — flat, unhurried, the voice of a man who has never once been surprised — said, "Check the room. Careful. Nothing broken."

Aurelio's voice. Not Aurelio himself; he did not climb his own stairs. But his voice travels with certain men the way a smell travels with a coat.

They searched the clockroom badly, which told me they were hired for their loyalty rather than their eyes. They found the ledger, leafed through it, put it back crooked. They found Isandro's bench and touched everything on it, which would have offended him more than theft. Then one of them said, "It's not here," and the other said, "It has to be here. Old man built the whole works around it. You don't build a vault into a clock and then keep the deed in your sock drawer."

And then they did the thing.

I heard it before I understood it — a sound like a knuckle cracking, but bigger, brassier — and then the sound of metal being forced, and then a short ugly snap, and then silence, the specific silence of two men listening to see if the world noticed what they'd done.

The world noticed. I noticed. Behind the clock case, with my hand pressed over my mouth, I felt the silence come into the tower like cold air under a door. Because the tower had not been silent since the day I arrived on these steps. Even stopped, even dead, the master clock had kept a kind of breathing — the creak of the case settling, the faint swing of a pendulum Isandro kept alive by hand each evening, winding and nudging, nursemaid to a heartbeat that belonged to no one. That breathing was gone now. Whatever they had snapped, they had snapped the last thread between the clock and its own pulse.

One of them laughed softly. "Let the clockmaker explain that to the town. Master clock dies on his watch. See how long they let the Nakamura boy keep the keys."

Then the boots went back down, and the door closed, and I counted to three hundred before I moved, because Isandro had taught me that counting was free and courage cost extra.

* * *

The escapement was ruined.

I came out from behind the case and looked, and I will not pretend my stomach did not drop, because it dropped like the weights themselves, all that stored force let out at once instead of tooth by tooth. The anchor — the pallet-arm, the little brass bridge that rocks over the escape wheel's teeth and spends the power tick by tick — had been bent hard sideways and then snapped at the left arbor. Not torn. Snapped, clean, by someone who knew exactly where brass gives up. This was not vandalism. Vandalism breaks things anywhere. This was surgery by an enemy: they had cut the one piece that could not be replaced from any shop in town, because it had been cut by hand, sixty years ago, by a man who measured in his head and signed nothing.

I sat down on the floor and considered crying, briefly, the way you consider a coat you have outgrown.

Then I got up, because sitting on floors is for people with time, and I did not know how much time I had. The posts might come back. Aurelio might come himself. And when Isandro came home and saw what had been done to his clock — *our* clock, though I had no word yet for what it was to me — he would wear a look I had seen once, on the night he found me on the steps: the look of a man being handed a responsibility he did not ask for and cannot put down. I did not want to be that again. I had been that once. Once was plenty.

So I did the arithmetic of repair, out loud, because talking to myself in that tower had become a habit and I refuse to apologize for it.

"The arbor is snapped. The arbor is a pin. Pins are steel. Steel is in the toolbox." I went to the toolbox. Isandro's toolbox has a smell — oil and iron and something like rain on hot stone — and opening it without permission felt like reading someone's letters. I opened it anyway. "The pallets are fine. Only the arm is bent and the pin is broken. So I need to straighten the arm and make a new pin." I looked at the files. There were many. I had watched him choose among them the way other men choose horses. "Which file," I said to the toolbox, "is the question, isn't it."

The toolbox declined to answer. I chose by feel, the way I chose everything, and later Isandro would tell me I had chosen correctly, and I would decline to tell him it was by feel, and he would continue believing I had some method, and that lie has kept us both humble in different ways.

* * *

Here is what fixing it actually required, so that no one thinks this was magic. Magic is what the town called it afterward. It was not magic. It was eight hours, two burned fingers, one small fire risk, and the willingness to fail eleven times where anyone sensible would have failed twice and quit.

First: the arm. Brass forgives if you coax it and hates you if you hurry. I clamped it — badly, re-clamped it — between two blocks of Isandro's hardwood and eased it straight with the wooden handle of a brush, a little at a time, feeling for the moment the metal stopped arguing and started agreeing. My mother's hands, I thought, without knowing why. The thought arrived the way thoughts arrive in that tower, uninvited and true. Somebody in my blood had worked with material that argues. Smoke, a dog, a woman's voice saying *gently, gently* — I had carried those fragments since the orchard, and suddenly *gently, gently* was not a memory of apples. It was a memory of someone bending something back into shape, saying it to the metal or to me or to herself.

Second: the pin. This was the impossible part, the part the town would later call impossible, and I want it recorded that I did not know it was impossible while I was doing it, which is the only reason I could. A clockmaker makes such a pin on a lathe, with gauges, with ten years of apprenticeship. I had a file, a vise, a scrap of steel rod from the bottom drawer, and the memory of watching Isandro turn pins — maybe nine times, maybe twelve, always the same slow rotation, always checking against the hole with the piece held up to the window light.

I filed. The first pin was too fat. The second too thin, and thin is worse than fat, because fat can be corrected and thin is a confession. The third I burned my thumb on, holding it against the lantern glass to check the color of the steel, because Isandro had once murmured, half asleep at the bench, that you temper by color and cherry-red lies. The fourth through the eleventh are not worth describing except to say that each one failed differently, and failure, it turns out, teaches faster than success, the way a scolding teaches faster than a compliment.

On the twelfth attempt I stopped thinking like a girl imitating a man and started thinking like the clock. The pin did not need to be perfect. It needed to fit the hole the way the tick fits the tock — snug, but with play, because a clock that binds is a clock that stops. I filed, tested, filed, tested. The rod went into the arbor hole with the smallest possible resistance, the resistance of a secret sliding home.

Then I had to rivet it — flatten one end so the pin could not retreat — and I had no hammer small enough, so I used the blunt nose of a wrench wrapped in cloth, tapping, tapping, the way you tap a sleeping dog you do not wish to wake. *Gently, gently.* My hands shook for that part, not from fear but from the effort of being careful, which is heavier than most fear.

Last: assembly, upside-down logic, and prayer. The anchor goes back over the escape wheel with its pallets engaging teeth that do not care about your feelings. One tooth too deep and the clock locks. One too shallow and it skips and runs mad. I lowered it in blind, by feel, both hands, breath held like a coin in a fist — and it seated, and the pallets took their teeth, and for one long moment nothing happened at all, which was the worst moment of the entire day.

Then I reached up — I had to stand on Isandro's stool and stretch, I remember the ache in my shoulder still — and I gave the pendulum the smallest push.

Tick.

I heard it in my chest before I heard it in the room. Tock. Tick. Tock. Steady, slightly uneven — the left pallet ran a hair proud, I would learn, a flaw Isandro refused for weeks to correct, saying the clock now limped with character and he wasn't touching it — but steady. Alive. The great dead animal at the center of the tower had taken a breath and decided, after all, to keep living.

I sat down on the floor again, this time because my legs had resigned, and I laughed until I cried, which is a thing I had heard described and never understood. The clock ticked above me like a chaperone who pretends not to see.

* * *

Isandro came home after dark, escorted by the posts to the foot of the hill, and I heard his boots — the dragging left heel — start up the stairs, and slow, and slow further, and stop.

He had heard it from outside, he told me later. Halfway up the hill he had heard the tick coming through the tower's stone like a pulse through a wrist, and he had stood in the snow telling himself he was imagining it, because the alternative was impossible.

He came into the clockroom and looked at the clock, running, swinging, spending its power tooth by tooth. Then he looked at the escapement — at the straightened arm, the new pin, the burn blisters on my thumb. Then he looked at me.

"What happened," he said. Not a question. A door held open.

I told him all of it: the boots, the hiding, the snap, the arithmetic, the twelve pins, the wrench-wrapped hammer. He listened the way he listened, which is completely, the way clocks listen to their own trains. When I finished he was quiet for a long time, and I began, stupidly, to prepare my defense about the rule, about touching nothing —

"You broke my rule," he said.

"Yes."

"And you fixed the escapement."

"Yes."

"With a wrench and a kitchen stool."

"There was no kitchen. There was a stool."

He turned away from me then, quickly, toward the window, and I saw his shoulders move once, and I understood — not fully, but enough — that grown men also laugh until something leaks, and that he was letting me not see, because that was a kindness, and kindness was a language we were both only beginning to speak.

"My grandfather," he said finally, still facing the glass, "made that pallet-arm by hand. There is no drawing of it. There is no spare. Every clockmaker who has ever looked at this train has told me that piece, if it broke, was the end of the clock. The end of everything it holds." He turned around. His eyes were wet and furious and lit up like the lantern glass. "You repaired it with a wrench."

"It was the only hammer-shaped object," I said, "that respected the work."

And then he did the thing that mattered more than any praise. He crossed the room, took the burnt thumb — gently, gently — and turned my hand over, and looked at it the way he looked at mechanisms, and said: "Tomorrow I'll teach you to file properly. Tonight, you eat first. Apprentices eat first on the day of their first repair. It's a rule."

"It's not a rule," I said.

"It is now," said Isandro Nakamura. "I'm making it up this instant. In my family, that's how rules are made — someone decides, and everyone else pretends it was always so."

We ate. The clock ticked overhead the whole meal, slightly uneven, limping with character, keeping time like a promise.

It was much later — after the dishes, after the lamp was trimmed low — that we heard them on the stairs again. Two sets of boots, even and heavy, wanting to be heard. They climbed. They stopped outside the clockroom door. And through the wood, in that flat unhurried voice that wears a coat like a smell, the man who was not Aurelio said:

"We know the clock's running, clockmaker. Our employer wants to know what else the girl's hands can do."
