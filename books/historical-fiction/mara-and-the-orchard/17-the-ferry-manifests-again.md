## 17. The Ferrymaster's Ledger

I had been in Vellwater long enough by then that the ferry office no longer felt like an intrusion — which is to say I had learned the trick of walking into a room as though I had always belonged there and was merely returning from somewhere dull. It is a clockmaker's skill, if you want the truth of it. We spend our lives opening cases that were never meant for our fingers, and we learn early that the difference between trespass and inspection is nothing but the confidence of your posture.

The ferry office sat at the foot of Rope Street, wedged between a chandler's that smelled of tar and a boarding house that smelled of regret. It was a narrow room with a counter scarred by fifty years of crates, a potbellied stove that gave heat grudgingly, like a man asked to share his dinner, and behind the counter a ledger shelf that sagged under the weight of forty years of river crossings.

The ferrymaster himself was a broad, slow-moving man named Odo Brask, who had hands like mooring posts and a face that suggested he had once considered thinking about something and decided against it. He was not unkind. He was not anything, particularly. He was the kind of man the river produces when it wants someone to take tickets without asking questions.

"Master Nakamura," he said, in the way people said my name now — carefully, the way you set down a tool you suspect might be sharper than it looks. "Come about a crossing?"

"Come about paper," I said. "The old manifests."

His eyebrows performed a small negotiation with each other. "Whatever for?"

"Habit," I said. "A clockmaker reads everything with numbers in it."

That was true enough, and it had the advantage of being boring, and boring is the finest disguise a dangerous question can wear. Odo grunted, wiped his palms on his apron out of what I can only assume was ritual rather than necessity, and led me to the ledger shelf.

* * *

Let me be plain about what I was looking for, because I was not entirely plain about it at the time even to myself.

Elena had told me about the deed hidden in the clock — told me in the dark of my own workroom, with her voice shaking and her grief leaking through her composure like water through a bad gasket. And since that night I had been turning over one fact the way you turn over a coin whose edge feels wrong: the ice would break in spring, and when it broke, the ferry would run.

I knew what that meant for the town. The spring crossing was the artery of the year — goods upriver, wool and fruit downriver, letters, debts, brides, coffins. But I also knew, in the way you know a thing before you admit you know it, that the first boat after the break did not carry the town. It carried whoever could pay for a berth, and there were never many berths, because the ice-out channel was narrow and temperamental and the ferry company loaded only what it could guarantee against the floes.

A handful of places. A handful of names.

And a family at war over an orchard, all of whom understood perfectly well that possession in this country was a matter of being present when the papers changed hands.

So I stood at the counter while Odo hauled down the manifests — great bound volumes with spines cracked like knuckles — and I began with the oldest, because if I have learned one thing in that tower, it is that every lie has an ancestry.

* * *

The manifests themselves were beautiful in the way that only working documents are beautiful. No ornament, no vanity. Just columns ruled in faded red ink: date, vessel, cargo, passengers, berths. Decades of the river's traffic recorded in the handwriting of men who mostly couldn't be bothered to lift their nibs.

I read them the way I read gear trains — looking for the tooth that doesn't mesh.

And there they were. Spring crossings, year after year. Berths: six. Sometimes five, in lean ice years. Once, memorably, three — 1871, the year the break came late and violent, and the ferry lost part of its rail to a floe the size of a chapel. Three berths that year, and the manifest recorded who took them, and I found myself reading those three names slowly, imagining the crowd on the bank watching that boat pull away, everyone doing the arithmetic of who mattered and who stayed.

Six berths. That was the number that mattered. Six, most years, before the channel settled and regular service resumed. Six seats on the first boat of the year, when the whole town stood on the banks and watched the ice go out like a held breath finally released.

Six berths, and an orchard family of more than six claimants, counting spouses and grudges.

"You'll want the current book," Odo said, appearing beside me with the particular silence of large men. He set a newer ledger on the counter and opened it to a page already ruled for the coming season. "Spring manifest. Not filled yet."

"Not filled yet," I repeated.

"No." He scratched his jaw. "Well."

"Well?"

He looked uncomfortable, which for Odo Brask was roughly the emotional range of a full opera. He turned the page toward me fully and let me look.

The berths were listed. First crossing after the break — provisionally dated, in pencil, for whenever the river decided — and beside each berth, in confident ink, a name.

*Aurelio Vasch.* Berth one.

*Uncle Teodor Vasch.* Berth two.

*Mr. Halloran, solicitor, for the estate.* Berth three.

Berth four: blank. Berth five: blank. Berth six: blank.

But beside each of the blank ones, smaller, in the same confident hand, a note: *held.*

"Held," I said.

"Reserved," Odo said, as though the word were softer. It wasn't.

"On whose instruction?"

"The family's. Through Mr. Halloran." Odo shrugged, a landslide in miniature. "Deposit's paid. Company takes deposits. That's the whole of the law so far as I'm concerned."

"And the other three? The ones marked held — held for whom?"

Odo looked at me with mild surprise, as though the answer were written on my forehead where everyone but me could read it. "For whoever the family says. That's how holds work, Master Nakamura. You don't put a name till you know which way the wind sits."

Which way the wind sits. I felt the phrase settle into me coldly. Three open berths, held in suspension, waiting on the outcome of a war I had been watching from a clocktower like a man watching weather approach across a flat field. Aurelio would take his. Teodor would take his. The solicitor would carry whatever instrument of ownership the season produced. And the remaining three — Elena, perhaps, if she groveled; Mara, if anyone remembered she existed; or whoever else proved useful enough to save.

Or nobody. Three empty seats going downriver past a town full of people who had assumed, the way towns always assume, that the boat would somehow include them.

"Has anyone else asked about these?" I asked.

"Asked about what?"

"The berths."

Odo considered. "The widow Vasch came round in the fall. Before the river shut." His face did its best with something that might have been sympathy. "There weren't any holds then. She'd have had a berth same as anyone. But she didn't leave a deposit."

Of course she hadn't. Elena, who had given away a child to keep her safe, who had wept over a small coat in my workroom and begged me to keep her secret — Elena would no sooner have laid claim to a berth than she'd have laid claim to the orchard itself. That was precisely the shape of her. She asked for nothing. It was her whole character, and it was going to kill her.

"And the child?" I heard myself ask, before I could stop my mouth. "Anyone come asking for a berth for a child?"

The silence that followed lasted exactly as long as it takes a man to decide whether he knows what you're talking about. Odo decided he didn't. "No children booked," he said. "Company doesn't discount them, mind. Same fare, same hold."

Same fare, same hold. I copied the page into my notebook — names, dates, holds, deposits — in the small precise hand my master had drilled into me, the hand that matched the way I cut wheels, and thanked Odo, and walked back up Rope Street with the wind coming off the river hard enough to make my eyes water, which was convenient, because I didn't care to examine why they were watering.

* * *

The tower was warm that evening — warm for Vellwater in February, which means the water in the wash basin had a skin of ice thin enough to see your reflection die in. Mara had taken over the little table by the stove for her lessons, and I found her bent over a practice escapement I'd made her from soft brass, her tongue between her teeth, her small fingers moving with a steadiness that still unsettled me. Children are supposed to be all thumbs and appetite. Mara's hands were quiet. When she worked, she reminded me of photographs I'd seen of dead men.

"How many teeth?" I asked, nodding at the wheel she was counting.

"Forty-two," she said, not looking up. "It's missing one. Number thirty-one is short."

I checked. Number thirty-one was short. I had put the flaw there deliberately, weeks ago, and she had found it in four minutes, and I had pretended to be unsurprised both times, which was getting harder.

"Good," I said. "What does a short tooth do?"

"Makes the pallet drop early. Makes the clock fast." She frowned at the wheel. "Like the big clock used to be. Before you fixed it. It ran fast for years and nobody noticed because everybody just believed it."

I sat down across from her. The stove ticked. Somewhere below us the frozen river made its long grinding sound, the sound of a thing too heavy to move deciding, very slowly, to move anyway.

"Mara," I said. "Do you remember the ferry?"

She looked up. Her face did what it always did when memory came near — went careful, guarded, a door pulled almost to. "The big boat?"

"The spring boat. Do you remember riding it?"

"I think—" She turned the brass wheel over in her hands, considering, and I realized she wasn't stalling; she was using the object the way other people use a rosary. "Smoke. There was smoke from a stack and it smelled like the orchard burning but it wasn't bad smoke. And a woman held my hand so tight it hurt, and she kept saying *hold on, hold on,* and I thought she meant the railing." Her eyes came up to mine. "She didn't mean the railing, did she."

"No," I said quietly. "I expect she didn't."

"She was telling me to hold on to her."

"Yes."

Mara nodded slowly and set the wheel down, aligning its edge with the table edge, the way she aligned everything. Then, in the flat practical voice she used for facts rather than feelings: "Is the ferry coming soon?"

"When the ice breaks. Weeks, maybe. The river will tell us before the calendar does."

"Will we go on it?"

The question landed in the middle of the table between us like a dropped wrench.

"I haven't bought berths," I said.

"Can you buy berths?"

"They're mostly spoken for. Held." I watched her absorb the word without understanding it, and I hated the word for existing. "There are three left unclaimed. Sort of."

"Sort of?"

"It's complicated."

"You say that a lot," Mara observed. "About things that aren't actually complicated. They're just bad."

I laughed despite myself, and she permitted herself the ghost of a smile, and then she went back to her wheel, because that was Mara — she could stand in the middle of a question that would flatten an adult, note it in her ledger, and pick up her tools. I envied her that more than I could say. My own questions did not sit politely on tables. They paced.

Three berths. I turned it over while the stove ticked and the child counted teeth. Aurelio had claimed his passage and his uncle's and his lawyer's, and held three more like a card player holding trumps, waiting to see what the thaw brought up. Elena had asked for nothing and therefore had nothing. Mara was not on any manifest at all. As far as the ferry company knew, the child of the clock did not exist, which was the exact condition her mother had engineered for her safety and which now looked, from the cold side of winter, like erasure.

And me? Where was my name in the ledger? An apprentice clockmaker, a foreigner's grandson, a man the town still measured for his surname before they measured him for his work. I hadn't thought about a berth for myself. That was the strange part, the part I kept circling back to like a tongue to a broken tooth. Somewhere between the night I found her on the steps and tonight, the arithmetic had changed without my permission. It was no longer *can I get away before the river opens.* It was *who goes, and who stays, and who decides.*

Whoever held the last berth held the season's whole meaning. I could feel that shaping itself around me, the way pressure shapes before a storm. Aurelio would want his birthright carried downriver to whatever court or buyer waited beyond the ice. Teodor would want his cut. Elena wanted — what? To be sent away from her own daughter forever, if it kept the girl breathing? And Mara wanted the only thing no manifest had a column for: to stay.

* * *

After she was asleep — curled in the alcove off the clockroom, under the good quilt, with the brass wheel tucked beside her pillow like a doll — I climbed the tower stairs to the clockroom and stood a while among the great silent weights.

The master clock hung stopped, as it had hung since before I came. I had repaired half its train by then; the rest waited on the mechanism, on the key, on the locket none of us had found yet. In the dark the case rose up like a headstone, and I put my palm flat against the wood, an old habit, feeling for a pulse that wasn't there.

Six berths, I thought. Every spring of Vellwater's history, six berths, and every spring somebody standing on the bank learning what their name was worth.

My grandfather had stood on such a bank once. I was sure of it now — surer than I'd been in the ferry office, surer than I'd been reading the gap in the old manifests where a name should have been. The Nakamura name had ridden that river, worked that orchard, and been struck out of the record so cleanly that even the ink seemed embarrassed. Whatever happened this spring, whatever choice the thaw forced on me, I was beginning to understand that it wouldn't be only about Mara, or Elena, or the deed sleeping in the clock's heart. It would be about every name the ledger had ever swallowed, and whether this year, at least, the swallowing stopped.

Below me, faint through the floorboards, I heard Mara turn in her sleep, and murmur something, and go quiet again.

Three berths held. Two people I would not abandon. One river getting ready to crack.

I took out my notebook by lamplight and wrote, beneath the copied manifest, a single line:

*Find out what a berth costs — and what it's worth.*

Then I blew out the lamp, and in the dark the stopped clock and I kept our silence together, listening to the ice.
