## 18. A Clockmaker's History

The thing nobody tells you about living in a clocktower is that the clock becomes a kind of weather. You stop hearing it after a while, the way you stop hearing rain, and then one night it stops raining and you wake up wondering why the world has gone deaf.

That was how I learned to tell when Isandro was worried. Not by his face — his face was built for keeping secrets, all corners and closed doors — but by the clock. When something gnawed at him, he wound it late, or early, or twice in one evening, as if the extra turns could wind down whatever was coiled inside him too.

It was the third week of February, and he had wound it twice.

I was sitting on the workbench with my legs swinging, mending a coat sleeve with thread I'd stolen fair and square from his sewing tin, watching him pretend to polish a gear wheel he had already polished four times.

"You're going to wear a hole in it," I said.

"It's brass. Brass doesn't wear holes."

"Everything wears holes. That's what Grandfather's clock is for. Counting them."

He set the wheel down. He looked at me for a long moment in that way he had, like he was measuring me against some standard only he knew, and then he did something he almost never did. He sat down across from me instead of standing over me.

"Mara," he said. "How would you like to hear a story?"

I put down the coat. Stories from Isandro were rarer than warm days in February. "Is it true?"

"Why does that matter?"

"Because if it's not true, it's just a story, and I can get those out of books. If it's true, it's yours." I said this with the confidence of a nine-year-old who had recently discovered she was clever, which is to say, insufferably.

He almost smiled. Almost. His smiles were like the hidden gears we'd found in the clock — everyone knew they must exist somewhere, but no one had actually seen one turn.

"It's true," he said. "Every word of it. And some of it is mine, so listen well, because I'm only going to say it once."

* * *

He started, as he started everything, with tools.

"When I came upriver in October," he said, "I told you I believed history happened to other people. In other towns. To men with statues. I thought a clockmaker's job was to stand outside of time and fix it when it broke, not to be caught up in it." He turned a screwdriver over in his fingers without looking at it. "My master in the city used to say I had the coldest hands and the coldest heart of any apprentice he'd trained. He meant it as a compliment."

"I don't think your heart is cold," I said. "I think it's like the strike train. It works, but only on the hour."

For a moment he looked genuinely wounded, and then he laughed — a short, surprised bark, like the sound the ice makes when it shifts. "On the hour," he agreed. "Well. Here is what I never told you. My family name isn't just Nakamura of the city. It's Nakamura of here. Of this valley. My grandfather — my father's father — worked these lands before I was born. Before my father was born."

I stopped swinging my legs.

"The orchard?" I asked. Our orchard. The one above town that belonged to the family whose quarrels were splitting the town down its seams like a bad weld.

"The orchard," he confirmed. "He was a field hand, then an overseer of the lower terraces. Twenty-two years. He planted rows of trees that are still standing. He knew every graft on every trunk, they say, the way I know every tooth in the escapement. And then one winter — the winter my father was seven years old — he was gone. Not dead. Erased."

"Erased," I repeated. It was a word I liked the shape of. It sounded like something done with an eraser, which made it sound gentle, which made it worse.

"The ferry manifests list every worker who ever crossed to the orchard side. Every name, every season, every wage paid. Except one page. One page where the ink has been scraped away so carefully you can still feel the ghost of the letters under your thumb, if you know to look. I found it in November. I've been carrying it around inside me ever since like a splinter too deep to draw."

"Who scraped it?"

"That," he said, "is the question I have been asking the dark for three months." He set the screwdriver down with a small precise click. "What I know is this. The same year my grandfather's name vanished from the ledger, the old man who built this clock — the grandfather of the people now fighting over the orchard like dogs over a bone — took over the lower terraces himself. And the same year, he began building a clock with gears that do nothing. A vault disguised as a mechanism. A secret buried inside a machine whose whole purpose is to keep honest time."

I felt something cold walk up my spine on little feet.

"You think he erased your grandfather."

"I think someone did, and I think it was him or done at his order, and I think the reason is sitting in the middle of that clock like a stone in a peach." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. He looked suddenly very tired, and very young, and I understood for the first time that being grown-up was mostly being tired in places children hadn't grown tired yet. "And here is the part that shames me, Mara. All winter I have been telling myself it doesn't matter. That the deed matters, and your safety matters, and the truth about the note matters, but my grandfather is dust and the man who erased him is dust, and dust doesn't inherit anything. I told myself history happened to him, and I was only the clockmaker come to fix the clock."

"But?"

"But then there was you." He said it simply, the way you'd say *and then it rained*. "A child left on my steps with a note telling me to forget her face. A child tied by blood to the same house that scraped my grandfather out of a ledger. Do you understand what I'm saying? Two names, two erasures, one family holding the pen both times. Your mother's face was supposed to be forgotten. My grandfather's name was supposed to be forgotten. And I stood in the middle of it believing history was something that happened to other people, when all along it was happening to us — to you and me — quietly, patiently, waiting for us to walk into it."

The fire ticked in the little stove. Below us, through the floorboards, the great clock breathed its slow breath, tick and tock, tick and tock, the sound I had learned to love before I learned to trust any person in this town.

"What was his name?" I asked. "Your grandfather."

"Kenji," he said. "Kenji Nakamura. My father wouldn't speak of him. Said it hurt too much, like pressing on a bruise. So I grew up with a hole in my family shaped exactly like this valley, and I filled it with gears." He looked at his hands — those quick, sure, cold-handed hands that could mend anything except, apparently, themselves. "You asked me once why I stayed. Why I didn't leave when the town froze me out, when Aurelio's men spat at my door, when everyone with the sense God gave a goose would have taken the next ferry downriver. This is why. Not the clock. Not even you, though God knows you're reason enough. I stayed because somebody wrote my grandfather out of this story, and I am constitutionally incapable — it's a professional failing — of leaving a mechanism broken when I can see exactly which gear is missing."

* * *

We sat with that for a while. I want to say I said something wise, but what I actually said was:

"So we're both orphans of the same orchard."

He blinked. "In a manner of speaking."

"Not in a manner. Exactly. Somebody took things from both of us and then swept up the evidence, and now the sweeping-up is the only crime anybody can prove." I had been thinking about evidence a lot lately, since the night we matched the note's ink to the orchard house. Thinking about evidence makes you older, I find. "Isandro. If your grandfather planted trees up there, do you think he knew? About the deed in the clock?"

The silence went on long enough that the stove needed another log.

"He must have," Isandro said slowly. "Twenty-two years. He was the overseer of the lower terraces. If the old man hid a paper worth more than the whole valley inside a clock, he needed hands he trusted absolutely. Hands that could build a false gear train and never breathe a word." His voice changed — dropped, went careful, the way it did when he was handling something fragile. "Mara. What if the missing page in the manifest isn't a record of a dismissal? What if it's a record of a payment?"

"A payment for what?"

"For silence. For service. For building half a vault and taking the secret into the ground with him." He laughed, but there was no humor in it, only wonder wearing humor's coat. "Or — worse, better, I can't decide — what if the scrape isn't hiding his name at all? What if it's hiding what he was paid *in*? Not coin. Something else. Something that couldn't be entered in a ledger because it wasn't the ferry company's business."

"Like what?"

"Like a promise. Like a claim. Like—" He stopped himself, both hands flat on the bench, the way he did when a mechanism tried to run ahead of its mainspring. "No. I'm getting ahead of myself. That's how clocks break and how men break worse."

But I had seen his eyes while he talked, and I knew the shape of what lived in them, because I had seen the same shape in the mirror of the clockroom window on nights when I dreamed about smoke and a dog and a woman's voice promising me the trees. Hope is a dangerous thing to hold in a tower full of people who want what you have. But it is also, I have since decided, the only tool that fits every lock.

"Say his name again," I said.

"Kenji."

"Kenji Nakamura." I tried it out loud, feeling how it fit together, two words like two wheels in a train. "If we find the chamber — when we find it — and there's papers inside, maybe there's more than the deed. Maybe there's letters. Old men who hide things always write letters. It's in all the books."

"And if there are letters?"

"Then maybe one of them says your grandfather's name." I hopped off the bench, because sitting still had become impossible; the story had gotten into my blood like spring water under ice. "Maybe it says Kenji Nakamura built this. Maybe it says he kept the secret. Maybe it says he was owed something, and debts don't die when people do — you told me that yourself, about the ferry company and the widow Kessler."

"I did say that." He was watching me with an expression I couldn't read, and then I realized I could, and it frightened me a little, because it was the expression of a man looking at something he was afraid to hope for. "Mara. There's something else. The part I said I'd only say once."

I went still.

"When I found the scraped page in November, I wasn't looking for my grandfather. I was looking for cargo records — weights, freight, anything that might tell me how heavy the hidden chamber's door might be, what kind of counterweight would be needed to move it. Clockmaker's habit. Everything is weight and balance." He reached into his waistcoat and drew out a folded paper, soft as cloth from handling, and unfolded it on the bench between us with the reverence other men showed for holy books. "But the page before the scraped one — the intact one — lists the last crossing my grandfather ever made. And beside his name, in the cargo column, there's an entry."

He turned the paper toward me. The handwriting was old-fashioned, flourished, brown with age. I read it twice, because the first time my eyes refused to believe my own reading.

*One crate, marked FRAGILE — TOOLS OF THE TRADE.*

"Tools," I whispered.

"An overseer doesn't ship his tools home separately, marked fragile, on his final crossing. An overseer's tools are hoes and pruning knives, and they travel with the man. But a *clockmaker's* tools—" His finger hovered over the entry without touching it, the way he touched nothing he feared to damage. "My grandfather shipped a crate of tools home the last time he ever crossed to the orchard. Tools I have never seen. Tools my father never mentioned, because my father never spoke of him at all. Tools that may or may not still exist, in a house in the city, in a crate nobody ever opened because opening it meant talking about the man who sent it."

The clock below us ticked. Tocked. Somewhere far beneath the frozen river, the water was moving the way it always moved, patient as a debt.

"Do you think," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted, "that the key could be in the crate?"

"I think a man who helped build a lock that wants a key shaped like an orchard branch might have kept the tools that shaped it." He refolded the paper along its worn creases. "And I think that if such a crate exists, it's four hundred miles downriver in my father's cellar, behind twenty years of things my father refused to look at. And I think the river will open in six weeks, and the ferries will run, and—" He stopped. His jaw did the thing it did when he was deciding whether to say a hard thing, and then he said it. "And the berths on the first ferry of the season are already spoken for. I checked the manifests yesterday. The family has claimed them. All of them."

All of them. Elena, who wept over my coat and begged me to stay hidden. Aurelio, who watched our tower with hired eyes. The uncle, who bought honesty the way he bought coal, by the sack. Three berths, three claimants, and none of them named Nakamura, and none of them named Mara.

But that wasn't the hook that caught me. What caught me was simpler and colder.

"If the tools are in the city," I said slowly, working it through the way I worked through the gear train, drawing each wheel with my finger in the air, "then somebody has to go and get them. Downriver. Before the thaw closes the water again, or after it opens. Either way — a berth. A place on a boat. And there aren't any places, and the ones there are belong to people who'd burn this tower down before they'd let us open that chamber."

Isandro looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the February wind came down off the ridge and leaned against the tower, and high above us the vane creaked through a quarter-turn, and below us the great clock went on counting, faithful and blind, guarding its stone in the peach.

"Yes," he said finally. "That is exactly the problem. And I've been turning it over all week, and every solution I find has teeth on one end." He stood, and began gathering his tools back into their roll with sharp, economical movements — the movements of a man who has decided something and hasn't yet admitted it aloud. "Tomorrow I wind the clock at dusk instead of midnight. Don't ask me why. And Mara — tomorrow night, whatever you hear from the clockroom, whatever sounds come up the stair — you stay in your room with the bar across it until I call you. Promise me."

I promised. Children promise easily; it costs nothing until it doesn't.

But lying awake later in my narrow bed under the eaves, listening to the wind worry the tower and the clock count out the dark hours below, I found myself thinking not about keys or crates or berths, but about a name.

*Kenji Nakamura,* I whispered to the rafters, practicing it, keeping it safe. *Kenji Nakamura, who planted trees.*

Somewhere down the black river, four hundred miles south, a crate waited in a cellar with his name going soft on its lid. And somewhere closer than that — close enough to hear, if you knew how to listen — something in the clockroom shifted in the dark with a sound like a gear turning that should not have been turning at all.
