## 19. The Winding Night

The sound I'd heard in the dark — that gear turning where no gear should turn — was gone by morning, and I did myself the discourtesy of believing it had never been there at all.

This is a fault of mine, and I'll own it plainly: when the world offers me a choice between an explanation and an excuse, I reach for whichever one lets me get back to work. A clockmaker's trade is mostly patience punctuated by small disasters, and if you stopped to interrogate every creak and whisper in an old tower you'd never finish a single repair. So when the wind rattled the louvres at midnight, I called it wind. When something ticked against my ear through three floors of stone, I called it settlement in the timbers. And when, two nights later, I stood in the clockroom with my hand on the winding crank and felt it give a quarter-turn too easily, like a tooth missing from a ratchet — I called that fatigue. Mine, not the clock's.

I want it noted, for the record I am apparently keeping in my head these days, that Mara noticed first.

She had taken to sitting on the stairs during the winding now, wrapped in a blanket Elena had left folded on a landing rail weeks ago without ever quite admitting she'd left it. She didn't talk much while I worked. She watched. There's a difference between those things that most adults never learn, and this child had learned it before she lost her milk teeth.

"It sounds wrong," she said, as the crank came around.

"Everything sounds wrong. It's been stopped for months."

"Not *that* kind of wrong." Her brow furrowed, that particular gravity she could summon out of nowhere, like weather. "It sounds *thin*."

I should have put down the crank then. Instead I said, "Thin isn't a thing a clock can be," and kept winding, because the weights needed raising and because I wanted very badly for the evening to be ordinary. The town had been watching the tower all week — Aurelio's men had taken to standing across the lane in pairs, breathing steam, doing a poor job of pretending to discuss the price of nails — and I had decided, with the wisdom of a man walking backward into traffic, that routine was armor. Wind the clock. Feed the child. Lock the door. Let them see nothing worth reporting.

The crank gave again. That soft, hollow slip.

And then the weight above me moved.

Now, understand what a winding weight is: a box of iron the size of a coffin lid, hung on a drum by a steel cable, raised and lowered twice a week by a man with a crank and a healthy respect for gravity. Understand also that it hangs directly over the head of that man, and that the only reason he doesn't lie awake thinking about this is that the pawl — a small iron tooth, humble as a fence post — catches the drum and holds everything still.

The pawl was not holding.

I heard it go. That's the part I keep returning to, the part that wakes me even now. Not a bang, not a snap — a *tick*. The same tick the clock made ten thousand times a day, so familiar my ear filed it away as nothing, and by the time my brain caught up and understood that the ticking was coming from *behind* me, that the pawl was clicking past its teeth one at a time like a man descending stairs he meant to jump off of—

I threw myself sideways.

The weight came down through the space where I had been standing and hit the floorboards with a sound I felt in my teeth before I heard it in my ears. Dust leapt up white in the lamplight. The whole tower shuddered, and somewhere below us the stopped clock groaned in its case like an old man struck in his sleep.

Then silence. The bad kind. The kind with dust settling in it.

Mara was screaming. I want to say that gently, because she would hate it written plain — but she was, high and thin and furious, and she was already on the stairs coming down two at a time, which was worse than the screaming. I lay on my back among the wreckage with the lamplight wobbling and took inventory: hands, yes; feet, yes; ribs — sore, but mine; head — ringing but attached. The weight had clipped the edge of the winding platform as it fell. Six inches. Perhaps eight.

Six inches between Isandro Nakamura continuing and Isandro Nakamura becoming a stain on the floor of a room only three people were supposed to know about.

"*Don't move!*" I shouted up at her, and she stopped, frozen on the stair, blanket trailing, her face white as the dust. "I'm fine. I'm fine. Don't come down until I say."

"I heard it fall—"

"I know what you heard." I sat up slowly, testing each joint like a stranger's machinery. "I need you to do something for me, and I need you to do it exactly right. Can you do something exactly right?"

She nodded. Her chin was trembling. She locked it down anyway, which told me more about her than any test of skill ever had.

"The lamp on the third landing. Bring it down slow, hold it away from your body, set it on the bottom step. Then go back up and stay there."

She did it perfectly. Of course she did. And while she fetched the light I looked at the wreck of the winding gear and began, with the cold clarity that arrives after fear instead of before it, to read the scene the way you read a movement.

Because here is the thing. Weights fail. Cables fray. Pawls wear smooth. I have seen every accident a clockroom can produce, and they all look like accidents — worn metal, split wood, honest entropy. This did not. The cable was frayed, yes, but frayed *clean*, a dozen strands cut through in the same inch, and cut from one side. And the pawl — I crawled over and lifted it, and my stomach turned over with something colder than fear. The tip had been ground down. Deliberately. Filed round so it would ride the teeth instead of catching them, so gradually that a man cranking the drum would feel only a slight slip, a small wrongness, easy to blame on his own tired arms — until the night the weight came down.

Someone had done this work standing in this room, with tools, taking their time. And they had done it knowing that the winding happened on Thursdays, at dusk, by the clockmaker, alone.

Mara's voice floated down the stairwell, small and controlled. "Isandro? Are you still there?"

"I'm still here."

"You're being quiet."

"Yes."

"That's how you are when you're counting," she said. "You count when you're frightened or angry. You're doing both."

I looked at the filed pawl in my palm and found, sitting underneath the fear where I hadn't invited it, a hard little coal of anger. I blew on it.

* * *

We did not sleep well, either of us, and we did not pretend otherwise. I barred the clockroom door — the first time I had ever done so — and Mara dragged her blanket down and slept on the landing outside it rather than return to her room under the eaves, and I let her, because arguing with her about safety that night would have been like arguing with the river about ice.

In the morning I went out into the town for lamp oil and did not go near the orchard road, and Aurelio's men watched me from their post across the lane with expressions of magnificent innocence, and I watched them back, and nobody said anything, and the whole street performed a play called Nothing Has Happened Here with tremendous commitment and no audience.

Elena came at noon, through the kitchen door of the sexton's cottage and up the back way, hood up, moving like a woman who had learned the geography of concealment the hard way. She took one look at my face and one look at the splinted railing where the weight had torn free, and went pale in the specific way of someone doing arithmetic she doesn't want finished.

"He could have killed you."

"He nearly did." I showed her the pawl. She turned it over once and handed it back as though it had burned her. "That's not wear. That's intention. Someone spent an hour or more in this room making sure Thursday ended badly."

"And you think—" She stopped. Started again. "Aurelio wouldn't. He wants the mechanism intact. Killing the clockmaker before it opens gets him nothing."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't get him anything. Which is why I don't think it was him." I let that sit between us for a moment. "But it was someone who knows this family's quarrels from the inside, and someone who'd profit just fine from the clock staying dead forever. A dead clock keeps no secrets. A dead clock means the deed stays buried, and whoever inherits by the old papers — the papers everyone believes in — keeps everything, uncontested, permanent."

Elena sat down slowly on the winding platform's broken edge, which I suppose tells you something about how the week had gone for all of us.

"You think it was the uncle."

"I think your uncle made me a bargain in autumn and I refused it, and men who offer bribes and get refusals sometimes send other kinds of messages." I hesitated. "I also think that if I'm wrong about him, then it hardly matters, because either way the lesson is the same. Do you want to hear the lesson?"

She closed her eyes. "Say it."

"Your family would let the clock die." My voice came out flatter than I intended, and I let it. "Not just the clock. What's inside it. Your father built a vault into this tower to hold the truth safe, and the people who share his blood — his son, his brother, whatever portion of the family the sabotage traces back to — would rather see the whole mechanism smashed and silent than see it open and speak. They'd let Mara's inheritance rot in the dark. They'd let *Mara* — " I caught myself, and steadied. "They've already let Mara. She slept on a doorstep in November with a note telling me to forget her face. The only difference between that doorstep and that falling weight is that one of them missed."

The silence afterward had weight of its own. Elena pressed both hands flat against her knees.

"If I thought my brother did this," she said quietly, "or my uncle, or anyone carrying our name into that room with a file in his pocket — Isandro, I would walk into the town square and tell the whole town what's in that clock myself. I swear it on my mother's grave."

"I believe you." And I did, which surprised me a little. "But belief isn't a pawl. I need new ones installed, and a chain in place of the cable, and a lock on that door that wasn't bought in this town. And I need you to not come here for a while. If they're watching, you're safer far from this tower."

"They're watching *you*," she said, standing, pulling her hood up. There was something fierce in her face now, grief and guilt transmuting into the same coal I'd found under my own fear. "And you're going to keep winding it anyway, aren't you. You're going to fix it and climb up there every Thursday with your back to an open stairwell."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was a fair question and deserved better than wit. I thought about the six inches. I thought about Mara asleep on the landing with her blanket pulled to her chin, having decided, at seven years old, that the safest place in the world was outside a locked door guarding a man who wasn't her father.

"Because a clock that stops stays stopped," I said finally. "And because somebody in this family hid the truth in a machine that requires tending, betting that someone would tend it. Maybe he bet wrong. But I'm not going to lose the wager for him."

Elena looked at me a long moment. "My father would have liked you," she said, which from her was practically a eulogy, and slipped out the kitchen way.

* * *

The repairs took three days. New pawl, forged from stock I'd brought upriver in my own crate precisely because I trusted nothing local anymore; new cable, chain actually, ugly and loud and utterly uncuttable with a pocket file; and a lock ordered through the ferry agent four hundred miles south, which would arrive whenever the river decided to allow such things, meaning spring, meaning possibly too late, meaning I slept poorly regardless.

Mara helped. Of course she helped. She held parts and passed files and asked questions with the unnerving precision of someone assembling a picture out of fragments, and on the second day, while I fitted the new pawl, she sat cross-legged on the floor amid the wreckage of the old cable and said, without looking up:

"Whoever did it stood there." She pointed to a scuff in the dust near the great wheel. "For a long time. He shifted his weight. See? Two marks, left foot. He favors his left leg."

I stared at her. She stared back, entirely calm, holding a file the way her grandfather must have held one sixty years before either of us existed.

"There'll be a man in town," she continued, "who walks like that."

"There will," I said. My voice sounded strange to me. "Mara — after the thaw, when the ferries run and all this is settled — remind me to teach you everything I know. All of it. Every trick."

"You already are," she said, and went back to the cable.

Thursday came again, as Thursdays are contractually obligated to do. Dusk. The new pawl seated true, the chain running clean, the weights rising smooth as water. I wound the drum and listened to the clockroom the way you listen to a house at night — for the tick behind the tick, the gear that turns where no gear should turn.

Everything was quiet. Everything was correct. And somehow that was worse.

Because I understood, climbing down past the barred door with the lamp steady in my hand, that the sabotage hadn't really been aimed at me. Oh, my body had been the convenient obstacle, but the true target was the mechanism itself — its keeper, its tending, its future. Kill the winder, stop the winding, let the great clock sit dead in its tower until the deed inside meant nothing and the family divided the orchard by the old lies and no one alive remembered otherwise. They weren't trying to frighten me off. They were trying to erase the last pair of hands that cared whether it lived.

Well. They had miscounted. There were two pairs of hands now, and one of them belonged to a child who read footprints in dust and slept outside locked doors out of loyalty, and neither of us was going anywhere.

I banked the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the river muttering under its ice, and I made the sort of promise you make alone in towers, the kind with no witnesses except the machinery.

*You will run,* I told the clock. *Before the ice breaks, you will strike, and the whole town will hear it, and whatever is hidden in you will come out into the light where it belongs.*

And then, from the landing above, in the dark, Mara's voice — awake after all, she had heard every word:

"What if they come back?"

I climbed the stairs and sat beside her blanket in the cold and said the only true thing available to me.

"Then we'll be here," I said. "That's the whole plan, Mara. We'll be here."

She considered this, seven years old and gravely judicial, and nodded once, satisfied, and closed her eyes.

I stayed awake long after, listening. And it was near dawn, half-dreaming, that I remembered the scuff-marks she'd shown me — left-footed, favoring a bad leg — and placed them, suddenly and completely, against a memory from my first week in town: a heavyset man ascending the observatory steps ahead of me on market day, hitching slightly, apologizing for the slowness, smiling.

The uncle's steward. The one who carried his ledgers.

The thaw was weeks off. The berths were already sold. And now there was a name to go with the file-mark — and only one place in this town where a ledger-keeper's errands might be written down in his own hand.
