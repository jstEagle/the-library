## 20. Elena's Confession

I have told lies of every size, and I can rank them the way a jeweler ranks stones.

There are the pebbles — *yes, Aunt Vesta, your preserves were delicious* — which cost nothing and buy nothing. There are the semiprecious ones: *I am quite well, thank you*, delivered at my husband's funeral with a face like a locked drawer. And then there is the stone I have carried for three years now, the one I sleep beside as other women sleep beside a husband: *the child is gone, and it is better so.*

Tonight I mean to set that stone down. God help me, I don't know where.

The walk up to the observatory takes twenty minutes in daylight. In the dark, with the wind coming off the ice in long white breaths, it takes an hour and costs me a slip on the frozen ruts that nearly puts my ankle out of joint — which would have been fitting, I suppose. Limping up to confess one's sins has a certain theatrical honesty to it. But I arrive whole, merely bruised in dignity, and stand a while below the tower looking up at the lit window.

He's there. The clockmaker boy — not a boy, he must be five-and-twenty, but everyone under forty is a boy when you are thirty-one and tired. Isandro Nakamura. The name did its work on me before I ever saw his face; names do that in this town, where half the quarrels are conducted entirely through surnames. A Nakamura back at the observatory, winding the great clock our grandfather built, keeping time for the family that erased his family from the ferry ledgers. There is a joke in there somewhere, but it is the kind of joke you'd have to be dead to laugh at.

I knock. My knuckles barely make a sound against the oak, and yet he hears me — or perhaps he was expecting no one and therefore everyone. The door opens on lamplight and the smell of oil and cold iron, and there he is, spectacles pushed up into his hair, a wrench in his hand like a scepter he's not sure he wants.

"Madam," he says, careful as a man stepping onto river ice.

"Mrs. Ferrante," I say, because my married name buys me nothing here either, and then, because honesty is supposed to be the evening's project, "Elena. Elena Aldous-Ferrante. Aldous being the name that matters, though you'd never know it from the will."

He steps aside. I climb the tower stairs behind him, counting them the way one counts anything when one's mouth has gone dry — ninety-one, if you're wondering, and I have counted them before, in another life, when they led down instead of up.

* * *

Mara is asleep.

I had prepared myself for many things on that stair — for her awake, for her crying, for her looking at me with those gray eyes that are my mother's eyes and asking *why* — but I had not prepared myself for the simple violence of her sleeping. She is curled in a chair by the stove with a blanket over her and a book sliding off her knee, and she is missing a tooth, the front one, and her hair has escaped its braid entirely, and she is so exactly herself, so stubbornly, improbably alive, that I have to put my hand flat against the stone wall to remember how standing works.

"You've seen her before," Nakamura says quietly. It is not a question. He is holding the door of the inner room half-shut behind him, giving me the sight of her and blocking the rest of the world from it, and I understand in that moment that whatever else this man is — an outsider, a Nakamura, a hired pair of hands — he has made himself a wall around my daughter, and he does not even know she is my daughter.

Or perhaps he knows. Perhaps he has known since the night he found her, and has been waiting to see what I would do about it. Clockmakers are patient people. It's the trade.

"I changed her diapers," I say, which is not the sentence I rehearsed on the walk up. Rehearsed sentences never survive contact. "That coat she sleeps in — I sewed the blue lining. She chewed the left cuff when she was teething and my mother said I should let her, that it builds character. It built a hole."

He looks at me for a long moment. Then he pulls a second stool over to the stove, sets the kettle on, and says, "Tea's weak. I'm told I make it like a man who thinks of tea as water that's been shouted at."

"That's accurate," I hear myself say, absurdly, and something in my chest cracks along a seam I didn't know was there, because this is the first conversation I've had in months where nobody wants anything from me except the truth.

* * *

Here is what I came to say, and I will say it badly, because the true things always come out of me sideways, like furniture going down a narrow stair.

When Father died, the will was read in the east parlor with all the windows shut against November, and Aurelio sat at the head of the table like a man warming himself at a fire only he could see. Uncle Tomas picked at his cufflinks. And I sat with my hands folded, three years widowed, holding my daughter on my knee, watching my brother's lawyer slide papers across the wood, and I understood — not gradually, not with dawning horror, but all at once, the way ice understands it is no longer water — that Mara was not in the will as a granddaughter.

She was in it as a clause.

Aurelio's claim to the orchard ran through the male line, through Father, through Grandfather Aldous and his famous deed — except the deed was missing, had been missing for years, and everyone pretended otherwise, and everyone knew otherwise. Without the paper, the inheritance hung on bloodlines and witnesses and the town's habit of doing whatever the Aldouses had always done. And a child — a legitimate granddaughter, the last child born under that roof — was not a person in that arithmetic. She was a signature waiting to happen. Whoever held her custody held a living seal. Marry her off at eighteen to whoever suited the estate, or better, use her existence now, as a lever, as a hostage of blood, to force the town's elders to accept any version of the inheritance Aurelius cared to dictate.

He said it without saying it, which is his gift. He stood by the window after the reading, looking down at the orchard bare as a ribcage, and he said, "It's good the girl will grow up knowing what she's worth to this family." And he smiled at me. Aurelio smiles the way a bill collector smiles. You feel the arithmetic of it before you hear the words.

I went home that night and I wrote the note.

* * *

The kettle screams and Nakamura moves to take it off, and I watch his hands — steady, scarred at the knuckles, sure — and think: she watches hands like that. She already sits like him when she's working. Whatever else I have done, I left her somewhere her own cleverness would be fed instead of spent.

"You could have taken her yourself," he says. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — genuinely asking, the way a craftsman asks about a joinery he doesn't recognize. "Run. Downriver. Women disappear with children every year; the manifests are full of them."

"Yes," I say. "And every one of those manifests is copied, indexed, and filed by men my brother pays. Do you know what Aurelio does with money? He doesn't spend it. He spends *people*. A widow running downriver with a child isn't a tragedy to him, it's a receipt. Two days' wire, and we'd be found, and found we would come home — or worse, we'd come home as proof of how far a mother will run, which makes her unreliable, unstable, unfit, and the courts eat mothers like that for breakfast. I've watched him do it to tenants. I've watched him do it to Uncle Tomas." I wrap my hands around the weak tea because they need a job. "You cannot outrun a man whose grandfather taught this town to tell time."

"So you made the town lose her instead."

"So I made *everyone* lose her." My voice comes out wrong, too loud in the round stone room, and Mara stirs, and we both freeze like burglars until she sighs and turns toward the warmth. When I speak again it's a whisper, and the whisper is somehow worse. "If she vanished, truly vanished — abandoned, unclaimed, no name worth pursuing — then she was worthless. That was the whole design. Strip her of value and Aurelio's lever breaks. A foundling is no one's leverage. A foundling is charity, and charity is beneath him." I laugh, and it comes out like a cough. "Do you see? My grand plan was to make my daughter poor enough to be safe."

"And the note," he says. "*Forget the face.*"

"The note was cowardice wearing armor." I look at the fire because I cannot look at him. "I told myself it was protection — that if no one knew whose she was, no one could sell the knowledge. But I'll tell you the truth of it, clockmaker, since truth is apparently tonight's currency: I couldn't bear for her to remember me. If she remembered, she'd wait. Children wait. They'll wait on a doorstep until frost takes them, waiting for a face. So I asked the world to take my face away from her, because I hadn't the spine to stay gone clean. I wanted her forgotten and I wanted her found and I wanted both at once, and that is not a plan, that is just love and fear fighting in a small room."

The clock ticks above us. Our grandfather's clock, though I suppose by every law but blood it is hers more than mine. Ninety-one stairs, and the whole way up them my confession sat in my chest like a swallowed stone, and now it is out and the room does not end. The room simply continues. That's the thing no one tells you about confession — the world declines to react. The fire keeps burning. The tea stays weak.

"She remembers smoke," Nakamura says at last, gently, the way you'd handle a gear with a cracked tooth. "And a dog. And a voice singing something about trees. She gave them to me the first week, like coins she wasn't sure were still legal tender."

"Juniper sang to the trees." My mother. Even her name in my mouth is an act of treason against Aurelio's tidy story. "She used to say the orchard kept accounts too — that every tree remembered who planted it. Aurelio thought it was nonsense. Aurelio thought everything Mother loved was nonsense, right up until he started searching her rooms for the deed she wouldn't give up." I stop. I have said more than I meant to. The deed is the one subject in this family that functions as a loaded weapon.

But Nakamura only nods slowly, and I realize — with the strange falling sensation of a floorboard giving way — that he already knows. Of course he does. He has been inside the clock for weeks. He has probably mapped more of my grandfather's secrets in two months than the family managed in thirty years, and he has kept Mara fed and warm and unreported the entire time.

"Why?" I ask him. It comes out raw. "Why keep her? Why risk Aurelio's men, the magistrates, the whole rotten apparatus — for a child that isn't yours, in a town that's been nothing but cold to you?"

He takes off his spectacles and polishes them on his sleeve, buying the moment the way careful men do, and when he answers he doesn't dress it.

"Because someone pinned a note to her coat telling the world to forget her face," he says, "and I looked at her face, and I found that I couldn't. It seemed like poor workmanship, obeying an instruction that contradicts the evidence." He sets the spectacles back on. "Also, she's better with an escapement than I am, and I refuse to send the only competent colleague I've ever had downriver to strangers."

I laugh again — really laugh, this time, horribly, wetly, into my sleeve, because grief and gratitude use the same exit and they will insist on leaving together.

* * *

She wakes near midnight. I hear the small sounds of her surfacing — the rustle, the blink, the little frown she inherited from Juniper — and I should go. Every prudent bone in my body votes to go, to slip down the ninety-one stairs with my secret intact, because a confession given is not the same as a confession received, and what if she hates me, what if she looks at me the way Aurelio looks at the ledger—

"Mara," I say.

She sees me. In the stove light, in the ticking dark, my daughter looks at me across three years of engineered forgetting, and I watch her do the arithmetic children do — faster than adults, crueler, truer. The blue lining. The chewed cuff. Something moving behind her eyes like ice shifting on a river.

"You sing about trees," she says.

"When I'm not afraid," I say. "Lately I've been afraid a great deal."

She considers this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she scoots over on the blanket and lifts one corner, an offer, imperious and four years old and utterly unbearably generous, and I cross the room and sit beside my daughter for the first time since I nailed a note to her coat, and she leans against my arm as if no court in the world could rule otherwise.

Nakamura stands by the door with his back politely turned, pretending tremendous interest in the clock's case, and I mouth *thank you* at him, and he pretends not to see that either.

I should go before dawn. I know the cost of staying past dawn; Aurelio's watchers count the tower's lights the way misers count coins. But my daughter's weight is warm against my side, and outside, faint under the tick of the master clock, I can hear it — a long, groaning sigh rolling up from the river, the ice beginning to talk in its sleep.

Spring is coming. The berths on the ferry are already spoken for, every one of them bought by my brother, and I have just handed the clockmaker the last piece of my heart to hold.

Whatever happens when the ice breaks — I am done letting other people write my notes.
