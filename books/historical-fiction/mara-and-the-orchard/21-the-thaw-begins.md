## 21. The Thaw Begins

The ice spoke first.

I had been living in the clocktower long enough by then to know its moods the way you know a dog's — the particular creak of the stairs at midnight, the way Isandro hummed when a repair was going well and went silent as a held breath when it wasn't. But this sound came from outside, from below, from the river itself, and it woke me in the grey hour before dawn with my heart going like a dropped marble down a staircase.

*Groooooan.*

Not a word, exactly. More like the sound a giant door makes when someone who has no business opening it puts their shoulder to it anyway. I lay in my cot under the eaves of the clockroom and listened to the river talk in its sleep, and I thought: *it's waking up.*

I didn't tell anyone. I got up instead, wrapped myself in the coat that had once carried a note pinned to it — the note long since burned, though the pinholes stayed like small scars if you held the wool up to the lamp — and climbed the inside stair to the clock face, where the glass looked out over the town and the water.

The river was still white. That was the thing about it. From up here it looked like nothing had changed, a great frozen road laid flat across the world, smooth as a swept floor. But underneath that floor, something enormous had turned over in its bed. The whole sheet of ice shivered. A crack ran out from the far bank like a lightning bolt drawn slow, with a sound like God tearing linen, and then the river went quiet again, smug about what it had done.

I pressed my nose to the cold glass and grinned at it.

"About time," I told the river.

Because here is what nobody says about winter in a river town: it is beautiful for exactly one week, and after that it is a wall. The ice had closed the water back in November, and the last ferry of the season had gone without us — without me, without Isandro, without anyone who hadn't bought their place months ahead. All winter the town had been a cup with a lid on it. And now the lid was lifting, and everything that had been waiting underneath — the quarrels, the bargains, the berths on the spring ferry, all of it — was going to come up for air at the same time.

I was nine years old that spring, or thereabouts. Nobody had ever told me my birthday, so I had decided it was the day the ice broke. It seemed fair. If the river got to be reborn every year, so did I.

* * *

By midmorning the whole town knew what I had known at dawn, because towns are like that — they are one body with a thousand mouths, and news travels through them faster than through any wire. Isandro heard it from the baker's boy, who heard it from his mother, who had heard the ice herself and run out into her yard in her nightclothes to shout at her neighbors like a woman possessed.

"The thaw," Isandro said, coming up the tower stairs two at a time with flour still on his sleeve from shaking the boy's hand. "Mara. Did you hear it?"

"I heard it before anybody." I was sitting cross-legged on the floor beside the open clock case, oiling a pivot with a cloth smaller than my hand. "I always hear things first. You should make a rule about asking me."

"You're right, I should." He said it lightly, but he stopped at the top of the stairs and stood there a moment, looking out the tall window toward the river, and the lightness drained off him like water off a slate. "Three weeks," he said. "Maybe less. Once the ice starts talking like that, it goes fast."

"Fast until what?"

He didn't answer right away. He took off his spectacles and cleaned them on his sleeve, which is what he did instead of answering questions he didn't like. I had catalogued his habits by then the way other children catalogue birds. Spectacle-cleaning meant evasion. Tapping the second finger against his thumb meant arithmetic. Humming meant happiness. Silence meant fear.

"Ferry comes up from the delta when the channel clears," he said finally. "Takes on passengers. Only a few berths this year — the manifests said six, and the family's already claimed most of them."

"The family," I said. I had learned to say those two words flat, without the question mark, because the question behind them was too big to ask every time. *Which family member. Going where. Taking what.*

"Aurelio. His uncle. Two of the uncle's men." He put his spectacles back on. "That leaves very little room on that boat, Mara."

"For whom?"

"For whoever isn't them."

I went back to my pivot. My hands were steadier than my chest was. This is a thing I learned early in the tower: your hands can be brave even when the rest of you is negotiating terms. Isandro taught me that without meaning to. He'd hold a gear up to the lamp, some tiny thing worth less than a button, and his hands would never shake, not even the night after the sabotage, not even with the smell of burnt rope still in the clockroom. *The work doesn't know you're afraid,* he told me once. *That's why we do the work.*

So I oiled the pivot, and the escapement ticked above us, steady as a heart that had made up its mind.

"When it breaks," I said, "the whole town will go down to the banks."

"Yes."

"To watch?"

"To watch. And to count." He looked at me then, really looked, the way he did when he'd decided I was old enough for whatever came next. "People will be counting berths, Mara. Counting who gets on that boat and who stays."

"And I stay."

It wasn't a question either. He opened his mouth — I watched him do it, watched the whole machinery of comfort begin to turn, gears engaging somewhere behind his face — and I held up one oily finger.

"Don't clean your spectacles," I said. "Just say it true."

He shut his mouth. Then, quietly, like a man setting down something heavy: "You stay. Whatever happens at that bank, you stay with me. That's the true version."

"All right," I said, and went back to the pivot, and both of us pretended my hands weren't trembling now. The work didn't know I was afraid. But I did.

* * *

The next morning the town went down to the water.

Isandro tried to leave me in the tower. I want that recorded fairly — he asked, politely, whether I might prefer to stay warm and mind the clock, and I looked at him with the expression I had practiced in the mirror of the clockroom's little washstand, the one that says *I am a reasonable person who will absolutely do the opposite of what you just suggested,* and he sighed the sigh of a man who has lost an argument before it started, and we went together.

The banks were black with people. That's not poetry; that's inventory. Every soul in town seemed to have found a reason to be standing in the mud staring at the river, shopkeepers with their aprons still on, children perched on fences, old men who had clearly been arguing about the exact day of the break since February and were now positioning themselves to win. Somebody had brought a stove out onto the dock and was selling roasted chestnuts to a crowd standing in freezing wind, which struck me as the most human thing I had ever seen.

And the river — the river was putting on a show.

The ice had gone rotten overnight. That's the word the ferrymen used, *rotten*, and it was the right one. What had been a white road was now a broken mosaic, plates and slabs of it grinding against each other with a sound like crockery in a giant's sink. Out in the channel the current showed through in long black veins, widening even as we watched. Chunks the size of wagons tilted and sank and rose again downstream. The groaning hadn't stopped since dawn; it came up through the ground and into your boots, so that you felt the breaking in your teeth.

"It sounds like the tower," I said.

Isandro glanced down at me. "How so?"

"Like something big deciding to move." I hugged my arms around my knees. We had claimed a spot on a stack of empty barrels near the ferry landing, high enough to see over the crowd. "Everything big does that first. Makes noise. Argues with itself. And then it goes."

He was quiet a moment. "Where did you learn that?"

"Living with you."

That earned me half a smile, the kind he rationed. But then his eyes went past me, along the bank, and the smile folded itself away, and I followed his look to see why.

They had come down to watch too. Of course they had. The whole town was here, and the orchard family was the town's sore tooth — everybody's tongue went to it eventually.

Aurelio stood apart from the crowd the way a fencepost stands apart from grass. He had two of his men with him, and he wasn't watching the river. He was watching the landing, walking its length slowly, counting — I saw him counting, lips moving — the bollards, the planks, the space where the ferry would tie up. Measuring how many people fit on a boat by measuring the boat with his eyes. When his gaze crossed ours on the barrel stack, it paused. Not long. Long enough.

"He's counting," I whispered.

"They're all counting," Isandro said. "Look."

And they were. Up and down the bank, in twos and threes, people were doing the same arithmetic with their faces. The widow Voss, whose son had gone downriver two winters ago and never written. The schoolmaster, whose lungs the doctors said wouldn't survive another winter here. Families with bundles already packed in their houses, I realized — everyone in this town had someone or something waiting on the other side of the ice, and everyone had spent the winter telling themselves *next boat, next boat*, and now the next boat was three weeks away and had six berths, and four of them belonged to Aurelio's family.

A town is a kind of clock, I decided, watching them. Everyone wound tight. Everyone pointed at the same hour. And nobody could stop the mechanism, because nobody owned it — except somebody always thought they did.

"There," Isandro said softly.

At the far end of the bank, half-hidden behind the fish sheds, stood a figure in a dark green coat. Elena. She came down alone, keeping to the shadows of the sheds the way she always did, and she stood at the water's edge with her hands clasped in front of her like a woman at a graveside, watching the ice break up. She didn't look at the crowd. She didn't look at the landing. She looked at the water, at the black veins widening in it, and even from the barrels I could see what her face was doing. It was the face of a person counting too — but counting days, not berths. Counting how much time was left to do whatever she was going to do, or say whatever she was going to say, or ask whatever she was going to ask.

She had come to our door twice that winter, always at night, always leaving before full dark. She had wept over my coat and begged Isandro to keep me hidden, and then she had gone away again, and none of us had said out loud what all of us knew: that spring was coming, and spring would force every hidden thing into the open, the way the current was forcing itself up through the ice.

"She'll ask soon," I said.

Isandro didn't pretend not to know who I meant. "Ask what?"

"You know what." I picked at the splintery edge of the barrel. "The boat only has so many seats. And she's going to ask you for one. For her, or—" I stopped. Saying *for me* out loud made it real in a way I wasn't ready for, so I finished it differently: "For whichever of us you think needs it more."

"Mara—"

"I do sums," I said. "You taught me. Six berths, four taken. Two left. Three people who need them. That's not a hard sum, Isandro. Even I can see it doesn't come out."

He looked at me for a long moment. Down on the water, a slab of ice the size of the chapel roof cracked clean in half and slid under, and the crowd cheered like it was a festival, and the sound of the cheering mixed with the groaning of the river until you couldn't tell celebration from warning.

"That sum isn't finished," he said at last. "There's a term missing."

"What term?"

"The one I haven't added yet." He stood up from the barrels and offered me his hand, formal as a dance. "Come on. Cold's getting into you, and the clock needs winding before dark."

* * *

We climbed the hill back to the tower as the light went long and orange, and behind us the river kept up its argument with itself, cracking and groaning and shifting, getting ready. In the clockroom Isandro lit the lamps and began the evening winding, hauling the weights up hand over hand, and I sat beneath the great train of gears with my chin on my knees and listened to the two clocks — the one in the case and the one in the river — ticking toward the same moment.

Three weeks. Maybe less.

I thought about the note that had been pinned to my coat, the one that said *forget the face.* Whoever wrote it had wanted me erased, tidy as a name scraped off a ledger page. But faces don't work that way, and neither do towns, and neither, it turned out, do rivers. Everything frozen comes up again. Everything hidden thaws. The ice was proof, groaning on the water all night long, and the clock was proof, ticking above my head, and somewhere down at the fish sheds a woman in a green coat was proof too, gathering her courage the way the current gathered under the ice — silently, patiently, and with irresistible force.

The question was only ever going to be what came up first when the break finally came.

I fell asleep to the tick and the groan, and dreamed of blossoms, and woke before dawn to shouting in the street below — because during the night, while the town slept, the river had pushed a floe against the ferry landing and torn loose the first three pilings, and the ferrymen were standing in the wreckage announcing that the spring boat would arrive sooner than anyone expected.

Days, they said. Not weeks.

Days.
