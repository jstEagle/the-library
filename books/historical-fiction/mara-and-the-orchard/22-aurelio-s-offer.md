## 22. Aurelio's Offer

The shouting woke the whole town, and by the time I reached the window the street below had filled with people in nightclothes, standing in the slush like a congregation that had forgotten what it was praying for. Down at the landing the ferrymen were hauling planking out of the ice, and someone was already saying — I could hear it rising up between the rooftops — *days, not weeks.*

Mara appeared beside me before I'd finished buttoning my coat. She didn't ask what was happening. She'd heard it too. Children hear everything; they just aren't consulted about any of it.

"The boat," she said.

"Sooner than we thought."

She looked down at the crowd for a while. Then she said, "Are you going to have to choose now?"

I hadn't told her about the berths. I want that on the record. Whatever else I did wrong that spring, I never once discussed ferry manifests with a nine-year-old girl. But she'd been living in a clocktower with a man who talked to himself over ledgers, and children assemble the truth out of scraps the way I assembled clocks — patiently, from parts nobody thought were important.

"Nobody has to choose yet," I said.

"That means yes."

"It means not yet."

She nodded slowly, accepting this with the terrible gravity children reserve for adult lies they've decided to permit. Then she went back inside and wound her little practice movement, tick and tock, tick and tock, and I stood at the window watching the river tear itself open a week ahead of schedule and thought: *well, that's convenient, isn't it.*

Because here is the thing I understood only later. The floe hadn't come early by accident. Ice does what ice does. But men do what men pay them to do, and there had been Aurelio Vasseur's men loitering at the landing since February, ostensibly guarding against nothing, and one of them had been seen talking to the ferrymaster more than once. Whether the pilings broke on their own or whether someone helped them along — that question kept me up nights afterward, and I never got an answer I liked. What I knew then, standing at the window, was simpler: the timetable had moved, and when a timetable moves suddenly, somebody wanted it moved.

I should have guessed who would come calling first.

* * *

He came at midmorning, alone, which surprised me more than anything else that day. No watchers on the steps. No escort idling by the door with their hands in their pockets trying to look casual. Just Aurelio Vasseur climbing the observatory hill in a good coat, breathing hard from the climb, hat held in both hands like a man arriving at a funeral he wasn't sure he was welcome at.

I met him at the door because I refused to let him past it.

"Master Nakamura." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "You'll forgive the hour."

"You'll forgive me if I don't invite you in."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Calculation, maybe, recalculating. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with his father's heavy jaw and none of his father's patience, and I had spent the winter learning his habits secondhand through Elena's confessions and Mara's frightened reports. I expected bluster. What I got instead was a man who had clearly rehearsed this on the walk uphill and was determined to stick to his script even as the script died in front of him.

"I won't waste your time," he said. "I know you know what's in your clock."

"Do you."

"My grandfather built half of it. Don't look at me like that — you of all people know what it is to be written out of a family's story. The Nakamuras worked our land sixty years. Your grandfather mended the press house clock, the cider mill, the orchard gates. My father erased him off the manifests after the quarrel, and everyone pretended it never happened, and your family went downriver with nothing." He paused, and I will grant him this much: the pause looked genuine. "I'm offering to undo that."

I leaned against the doorframe. Below us the town sprawled gray and dripping, and somewhere down there the ferrymen were counting berths.

"How," I said.

"A workshop. A real one — not a tower room with a cot in it. Stone floor, north light, room for three apprentices if you want them. The Vasseur name on the sign, and the Nakamura name under it, restored, in the ledger, in the church record, everywhere it was cut away. I'll have it done by summer." He straightened his coat, a small vain gesture that told me how much this cost him. "All I want is the mechanism opened and the deed handed to me. Unread. Sealed. You don't even need to know what's in it."

"And then?"

"Then nothing. Then the orchard goes where it was always going to go, and the town stops tearing itself apart, and you get your name back and your shop and your life." He spread his hands. "Everyone walks away richer than they came."

"Except Elena."

The name landed the way I'd intended it to. His jaw tightened.

"Except my sister," he said evenly, "who has spent two years selling pieces of what's left to keep herself fed, and who would be ruined inside a month by the debts anyway. You think you're protecting her. She's drowning, Master Nakamura. This is a rope."

"She asked me for a berth on the ferry. Not a rope. A berth."

"For herself or the child, whichever I judge kinder — yes, I heard. Very moving. Did she also tell you she wrote the note? Pinned it to the girl's coat and walked away before dawn?" He shook his head, almost sadly. "You're being asked to choose between the sister who abandoned a child on a doorstep and the brother who is offering you your own history back. Forgive me if I don't weep over the difficulty."

For a moment neither of us spoke. Upstairs, faintly, I could hear the tick of the master clock, steady as a pulse through the stone. And beneath it — so faint anyone else would have missed it — the other sound, the dry whisper of gears that turned nothing, doing whatever it was they had been built to do for thirty years. Waiting.

"Your grandfather helped build the hiding place," I said. "Is that true?"

A flicker of surprise. He hadn't expected me to know, and I watched him decide, quickly, whether admitting it helped him or hurt him.

"He was the better mechanic," Aurelio said finally. "Old Nakamura. My grandfather drew the pretty parts. Yours made them work." A thin smile. "So you see, it's practically yours. Family property, coming home. All I'm asking is that you finish what your grandfather started."

That was the cleverest thing he said all morning, and it nearly worked. Because it was true — or true-shaped, which is more dangerous — and for one long breath I stood there feeling the shape of it fit into my hands: the workshop, the name, the ledger line with NAKAMURA written back into it in a fair hand. I am not going to pretend I felt nothing. I had spent the whole winter learning that history had happened to me, to my family, right here in this town where I'd believed it happened to other people, and every inch of me wanted the erasure undone.

But that was exactly the trick of it, and I saw it a beat too late and a beat soon enough. He wasn't offering me my name back. He was offering to sell it to me, priced at one deed, one child's inheritance, one widow's last chance. A name bought that way wouldn't be restored. It would just be re-inked in his hand instead of his father's, and I'd spend the rest of my life signing documents that belonged to him.

"No," I said.

He blinked. I think he had genuinely budgeted no time in his rehearsal for the word.

"Master Nakamura—"

"You came up this hill and offered me my grandfather back if I'd help you rob a nine-year-old. So let me tell you what my grandfather would say, since apparently you knew him." I stepped forward, off the doorframe, and he actually took a half-step back, which I confess I enjoyed. "He'd say a mechanism built to protect something doesn't get opened for the man who wants to take it. He'd say a name isn't given, it's earned, and yours has been spending itself badly for years. And he'd say—" I pointed past him, down the hill, toward the river where the ice groaned and split and the whole town listened, "—that the water doesn't care whose name is on the manifest."

"This isn't finished," Aurelio said. His voice had dropped all its polish now, and underneath it was just a man, angry and afraid. "You understand that. There are ways to make a man leave a tower. There are ways to make a tower uninhabitable."

"There are," I agreed. "There already were. Ask whoever sabotaged my winding chain in January — oh, wait. You'd be asking yourself."

He went very still, and I knew I'd guessed right, and he knew I knew, and for a moment we stood there in the cold with the whole rotten arrangement laid out between us like a disassembled movement, every part visible.

Then he put his hat back on, and the polish came up over his face again like a case closing over a clockwork.

"When the boat comes," he said, "there will be three berths, and they'll be full, and you'll stand in that window and watch us go. And the girl will still be here, and the town will still know what she is, and no deed in any clock changes what happens to unclaimed children, Master Nakamura. Think about that while you mend your gears."

He went down the hill without looking back. I watched him until the fog took him, and I noticed — this is the part that matters — that he did not go toward the great house. He went along the river path, toward the landing. Toward the ferrymen.

* * *

Mara was sitting on the winding stool when I came back up, legs swinging, pretending very hard to be absorbed in a gear she was cleaning with a rag gone gray from use. She had gotten good at pretending lately. It worried me more than tears would have.

"Was that the tall one?" she asked. "Elena's brother."

"It was."

"What did he want?"

I considered lying. I had gotten good at it myself. But there was a limit, I'd decided, and the limit was this room.

"He offered me a workshop and our name back," I said. "If I gave him the deed."

Her feet stopped swinging. "And you said—"

"No."

"Why?"

It deserved better than the answer I almost gave — some tidy thing about honor, suitable for a child. Instead I sat down on the step across from her, knees creaking, and told the truth.

"Because he wanted me to buy something that shouldn't be for sale. And because—" I rubbed my face. "Because when he offered it, for about ten seconds, I wanted to say yes. That's why, really. That's the whole reason. A man who wants a thing that badly for ten seconds ought not be trusted with the decision."

Mara considered this with enormous seriousness.

"Papa used to say that about cards," she offered.

"Your papa sounds like he was smarter than me."

"He cheated," she said, and then, catching my expression, added hastily, "at cards. Only cards."

We sat with that a moment, the two of us, and the clock ticked above us, and somewhere below, the river kept up its low grinding argument with the shore.

"Mara," I said. "When the boat comes. Whatever happens with the berths. You're not going anywhere unless you choose to. I want you to hear it plain, because adults say things sideways and children are left doing arithmetic."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she nodded once, the way she did when she'd decided to believe something — the same nod she'd given me at the window that morning, granting permission to a lie. Except this one wasn't a lie, and I meant to die before it became one.

"Good," she said, and went back to her gear.

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I told myself it was the wind off the breaking ice, but that was a lie, and I've stopped lying to this account even where it makes me look foolish. The truth was that Aurelio's arithmetic kept running in my head. Three berths. Elena asking. Aurelio threatening wards and orphanages. A boat arriving in days. And underneath it all, the mechanism in the core of my clock, sealed behind dummy gears and a lock shaped like an orchard branch, holding the only document in this valley that could end the war — if I could reach it.

Because that was the thing Aurelio's visit had clarified, the way a hammer clarifies. He'd come to buy the outcome because he couldn't seize it. Which meant the deed was still beyond him. Which meant the lock was still locked. Which meant the race wasn't for the ferry at all.

The race was for the chamber. Whoever opened it first owned the ending. He had watchers on the orchard and friends at the landing and a keyhole somewhere in that machine that needed a branch of blossoms to turn it.

And I had a nine-year-old with a memory of a dog buried under the first tree.

I got up in the dark, lit the lamp, and pulled the clockroom drawings out from under the cot where I kept them flat. The hidden train ran down through the great wheel and vanished into the pillar on the east side — I'd traced it to within a hand's width of the core chamber in February, working by lamplight while Mara slept, before the sabotage and the siege and the thaw had eaten my nights. One more session. Maybe two. If I mapped the final linkage, I'd know exactly where the key went and exactly what turning it released.

Behind me, the door to the stair creaked, and Mara stood there in her blanket, hair wild, watching me hunched over the drawings like a thief over a map.

"You're going to open it," she said. Not a question.

"Tonight, maybe. Tomorrow."

"Then I'm staying up."

"Mara—"

"You said I could learn the whole train." Her chin came up, and I recognized the expression, because I'd seen it that afternoon on a bigger face coming up the hill: a person who had rehearsed and meant to stick to the script. "You promised. Tick and tock. All of it."

I looked at her — small, stubborn, wrapped in a blanket, standing guard over a secret she didn't yet know she was the key to — and I thought about Aurelio's offer, and the price of belonging, and how cheap he thought belonging sold for.

"All right," I said. "Fetch the lamp oil. We'll trace it together."

She grinned, and ran for the oil, and upstairs the master clock ticked on over both our heads — patient, faithful, and full, at its heart, of everything we were about to wake.
