## 24. The Key in the Orchard

The trouble with remembering, Isandro had told me once, is that memory is like a mainspring. Wind it too hard and it snaps. Let it run down and you lose the time entirely.

I was seven years old and I did not know what a mainspring was yet, not really. I knew it was the coiled-up heart of every clock, and I knew that when he said this he was looking at me the way he looked at gears that wouldn't seat — patient, and a little sorry, and waiting for me to do something clever on my own schedule and not his.

So when the dream came back — the smoke, the dog, my mother's voice saying *under the first tree* — I did what any sensible apprentice would do. I got up before dawn, put my boots on the wrong feet twice because the tower room was dark as a pocket, and went down to wake him.

He was already awake. He was always already awake. I have decided since that grown men simply pretend to sleep so children feel they've accomplished something by waking them.

"You're up early," he said, which was his way of asking why without wasting words.

"I remembered," I said.

He set down the lamp he'd been trimming. "Remembered what?"

"The tree."

* * *

Here is what I could tell him, sitting on the cold flagstones of the clockroom with my knees pulled up and my hands tucked under them, because my hands were always cold then no matter how many pairs of socks Isandro knitted for them:

I remembered an orchard. Not the orchard above town — or maybe it was, but seen from very low down, from the height of a small person walking between rows of trees that went up forever. The grass had been long. There had been apples fallen in it, brown-spotted ones the pickers missed, and I remembered stepping on one and it bursting under my heel with a smell like cider gone to vinegar.

I remembered a dog. A big shaggy dog, some kind of herding thing, with one ear that stood up and one that didn't bother. He had followed me everywhere. His name — I closed my eyes and reached for it the way you reach for a gear tooth in the dark — his name had been Barley. Barley. I was almost sure. It might have been Barley because of the field, or because of a biscuit, but it was Barley.

And I remembered the last day. Smoke, though not fire exactly — woodsmoke, hearthsmoke, the kind that means someone is home — and my mother's voice coming through a window, and she wasn't calling me in. She was singing, low, the kind of song you sing when you think nobody's listening. And then there was a different day after that, the bad day, and the bad day had rain in it, and men's voices arguing, and my mother's voice going tight and quick, saying things like *not while I'm alive* and *he won't find it* — and then she had crouched down in front of me with her coat on and her hair pinned up wrong, like she'd dressed in a hurry, and she had held my face in both her hands the way you hold something breakable.

"Under the first tree," she said. "With Barley. Remember the first tree, little bird. If you ever need the key, remember where we buried Barley."

And then the note, later, and the steps, and the tower, and forget-the-face, and none of that needs telling again.

Isandro listened to all of it without moving except to feed the stove once. When I finished he was quiet for a long moment, and then he said, "The locket. Your mother's locket. Elena wore one like it — a branch, with blossoms. Silver."

"She had one," I said. "Hers had a chain. Mine — Mama's — hers was bigger. It opened. There was a picture inside, of Papa." My chest did the thing it does when I talk about Papa, the squeezing thing. "But that's not the point."

"No?"

"The point," I said, in the voice I used when grown people were being slow, "is that a key can be anything if you're clever enough. You told me that. A key is just a shape that fits a lock. And Mama said *remember where we buried Barley*, and she said it like it mattered more than goodbye, and Mama never wasted words that mattered." I looked at him. "Barley's buried under the first tree. In the orchard. With the locket. Because Mama hid the deed in the clock, and you can't hide a deed without a key, and she wouldn't have left the key lying around for Aurelio to pocket."

Isandro stared at me. Then he laughed — a short, startled laugh, like a clock striking an hour it hadn't been wound to strike.

"You've been thinking about this."

"All night," I said honestly. "My feet are frozen. Can we go now?"

* * *

We couldn't go now. That is one of the great unfairnesses of being seven: you can solve everything in your head by lamplight and still be made to wait for daylight, and breakfast, and grown-people-caution. Isandro wanted to send word to Elena, and I said absolutely not, because Elena tells Aurelio things even when she doesn't mean to, she has a face like a window with no curtains, and besides, watchers had been seen near the orchard wall since the thaw started — two of them, loafing by the gate with the studied boredom of men who are paid to be bored.

"We'll go tonight," Isandro said finally. "After full dark. And Mara —"

"I know. Boots. Coat. Don't touch the spade until you say."

"And stay behind me."

I agreed to all of it, the way you agree to rules you intend to renegotiate later.

* * *

The waiting was the worst part. All day the clock ticked overhead and I could hardly stand it, because now I knew — or nearly knew — that inside its brass belly was a chamber built by my own grandfather, holding a piece of paper that named me, or named somebody, keeper of the only place I had ever been happy. The whole town walked under that clock every day and heard it lie to them, tick-tock, all is well, and I wanted to shout down the stairwell: it isn't just a clock, it's a promise, and the promise is buried under a dog.

At dusk it began to snow again — fine dry snow, the kind that squeaks. Isandro frowned at it out of the window and said snow was good, actually; snow covers tracks and keeps honest folk indoors, which meant the watchers would either leave or get careless. I chose to believe the second one.

We went out the lower door, past the dead garden, along the lane where the ice on the ditches had gone grey and rotten-sounding. The river below town was making noises I felt in my teeth — long groans and cracks, like a giant turning over in bed. Spring was coming whether anybody liked it or not, and the ferry berths were bought and sold, and none of that seemed real compared to the small iron weight of the folding spade under Isandro's coat.

The orchard wall was taller than me but not taller than Isandro, and there was a place where a branch leaned close, and he lifted me over like a parcel and came after.

Then we were inside, and it was dark among the trees, and the snow was coming down soft through the bare branches, and I stopped breathing for a second because I knew this place. Not remembered it — knew it, in my legs, in the turn of the paths. Left at the big tree. Straight past the well. The rows were older now, the trees gnarled and tall, but the shape of the ground was the same, the slight downhill toward the creek, the way the third row bent around a rock too big to dig out.

"This way," I whispered, and Isandro, who was holding the lantern unlit so its light wouldn't show, hesitated — I felt him hesitate, a grown man deciding whether to trust a child's sense of direction — and then he took my hand instead of arguing, which was better than arguing.

* * *

The first tree stands alone at the top corner of the orchard, apart from the rows, with a stone bench beside it that nobody had sat on in years. Under the snow I found the bench by shins, painfully, and decided not to mention it. The tree itself was enormous, an old apple gone silver-barked and hollow-limbed, the kind of tree that looks like it remembers things and finds most of them disappointing.

"Here," I said.

"Mara." Isandro crouched to my level. In the dark I could just see his face, and it was doing the careful thing it does. "Are you certain? Because if your mother said *the first tree*, she may have meant the first of the planted rows, down the hill—"

"It's here." How did I know? I don't know how to explain knowing. But I remembered Barley's weight against my leg as we walked, and I remembered being small and looking up at this particular trunk, and there had been a knot in it shaped like a screaming mouth, and I turned and put my hand into the dark where the knot should be and touched bark, old and smooth-worn, shaped exactly like a screaming mouth.

Isandro made a sound low in his throat.

"Under the roots on the bench side," I said. "Dogs get buried where people sit. So they can keep you company."

"That is either very wise or very grim," Isandro said, "and in my experience those are usually the same thing."

He lit the lantern then, hooding it with his coat, and set to work with the folding spade. The ground was frozen on top but the thaw had gotten underneath, and the digging went in slow wet bites. Snow kept falling into the hole and onto his collar and he swore under his breath in two languages, which I wrote down mentally for later use.

I stood watch. That was my job and I took it seriously, listening for the watchers at the gate, for boots on the path, for anything. What I heard instead was the tree. Wind moved through the bare branches and made a sound like the river under ice, like the clock, like all the ticking things in the world agreeing about something.

"Deeper than I thought," Isandro muttered. "She packed it down. She wanted it to hold." Another bite of the spade. "Or someone else did it for—"

His blade struck something that was not root.

* * *

It was a flat stone, laid deliberately, and under the stone a small grave that time and frost had mostly given back to the earth: bones, light as twigs and honeycombed with age, arranged with care, and — I knew before he lifted it, my hands were out before my head agreed — wrapped in oilcloth against the bones' side, a bundle the size of two fists.

A collar tag came up with the first handful of earth. Brass, green with rot, stamped with letters Isandro rubbed clean with his thumb.

BARLEY.

"Oh," I said. Just that. Everything else got stuck somewhere behind my ribs. Seven years is a long time to carry a dog whose name you weren't sure of, and finding out you were right turns out to hurt worse than finding out you were wrong.

Isandro didn't say anything sensible, because there isn't any. He just put his hand on my head, heavy and warm, and let me cry into his coat sleeve while the snow came down on the open grave, and after a while he said quietly, "He waited well. Dogs are better at it than clocks."

Which is a strange thing for a clockmaker to say, and exactly right.

The oilcloth bundle was stiff with cold. We carried it to the lee side of the trunk, out of the falling snow, and Isandro unwrapped the folds with the same care he gave a broken escapement, and there it was.

The locket was larger than Elena's, silver gone dark, and it was shaped — of course it was shaped — like an orchard branch, with five blossoms worked in raised relief, each petal a separate little plate of metal. It hung from a broken chain. Inside, when Isandro thumbed the catch, was a curl of hair tied with thread, and a picture I didn't look at longer than a breath, because Papa's face is a door I'm not ready to walk through in a freezing orchard at midnight.

But the back. The back was what mattered. Isandro turned it over and angled the lantern, and his breath went out of him slowly.

Stamped into the silver, tiny as watchmaker's marks, was a pattern of lines. Not decoration. I've spent enough evenings hunched over his drawings to know a mechanism diagram when I see one — wheels and arbors and a dotted line running through the train to a chamber drawn in cross-section, and numbers, small and exact.

"It's not just the key," Isandro said. His voice had gone quiet the way it goes when he's working, when the world narrows to brass and problem. "It's the instructions. She engraved the whole hidden train. Mara—" He looked up at me, and the lantern-light made his eyes very bright. "Your mother drew the map herself."

"I know," I said, though I didn't, quite. "Mama was like that."

Down the hill, toward the gate, a lantern flared. Then another. Voices, low and male, and the crunch of boots on crusted snow, coming up the row where the third row bends around the rock.

Isandro closed his fist around the locket and snuffed the lantern with his thumb, and the dark came down over us both like a lid.

"Mara," he breathed. "Take the spade. Take my hand. And whatever happens —"

"I know," I whispered. "Don't forget my face."

Somewhere below us, a man said, clearly, in the cold air: *"They went up toward the old tree. I saw the light."*

* * *

What happened next belongs partly to Chapter 25, but I will say this much, because it was mine: I led us out. Through the hollow trunk — the screaming-mouth knot opens, it always opened, that's why Barley liked sleeping against it, draft-warm — and down the far slope through the dead brambles, over the wall by the leaning branch, along the ditch with the grey ice that groaned but held. Isandro carried the locket against his chest with one hand and my wrist with the other, and neither of us spoke until the observatory door was shut and barred behind us and we were standing in the stairwell gasping like landed fish.

Then he knelt down on the flags, right there in the cold, and held out the locket on his open palm between us.

"Tomorrow," he said, "at sunup, when the light strikes the tower — we open the clock."

Above us, faint through three floors of stone and timber, the master clock ticked on, tick-tock, all is well, all is well — and for the first time since I'd come to the tower, I believed it was telling the truth.

Then somebody knocked at the lower door. Three times. Softly.

Elena's knock.
