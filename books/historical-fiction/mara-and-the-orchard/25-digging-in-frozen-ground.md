## 25. Digging in Frozen Ground

The moon did us a discourtesy that night. It hung low and fat over the orchard like an unpaid bill, lighting the rows of bare trees so plainly that I half expected to see one of Aurelio's watchers step out from behind a trunk and clear his throat. Mara, beside me at the orchard gate, looked up at it without fear.

"It's a good digging moon," she said.

"There is no such thing."

"There is. Grandmother's dog knew which nights to bury bones. She watched him." She said this with the flat certainty of a child stating physics. "He liked the moon when it was fat."

I had my master's tools in a canvas roll across my back — the small spade-shaped picks used for cleaning pivot holes, two iron bars, a lantern with its shutter closed to a coin's width of light. A clockmaker's kit is a poor thing for grave-robbing, but it is honest work with frozen earth if you are patient, and patience is the whole trade. You cannot hurry a mainspring and you cannot hurry ground that has been freezing since November. You can only persuade it.

"First tree," Mara said. "Past the wall, past the well, the first tree on the left as you come up from the river path. Under the roots where the dog is."

"You're certain?"

She gave me a look I was coming to know well, the look of a seven-year-old being asked whether water is wet. "The locket went in the box with him. Mama wrapped it in her handkerchief first. The blue one with the strawberries. So the silver wouldn't get cold." Children preserve such details the way amber preserves flies: perfectly, and without any idea of their worth.

We went over the wall rather than through the gate, because the gate hinge had sung for thirty years — every soul in town knew the orchard gate's voice, and I wanted no witnesses, not even a hinge. On the other side the snow lay ankle-deep and unbroken except for rabbit stitchings, and the trees stood over us in their long winter sleep, black arms full of stars.

I have dug graves in fiction only, before that night. Let me tell you what no novel bothers with: frozen ground fights back. The first six inches came away in slabs that rang like crockery when the bar struck them. Beneath that, the earth was merely hard, the kind of hard that takes the skin off your knuckles and laughs about it. We worked at the base of the first tree, on the side facing the river, where the roots made a natural shelter for the hole and our backs made a natural target for anyone coming down the row.

Mara knelt in the snow and moved earth with her bare hands once I'd loosened it, refusing gloves on the grounds that she needed to feel for the box. Her fingers went red, then purple, then a color I didn't care for at all.

"Gloves," I said.

"I'll feel better without them."

"You'll feel nothing at all if they freeze off. Gloves."

She put them on with the martyred sigh of her entire sex and age, and I went back to swinging the bar, and the night went back to its two sounds: the crack and scrape of iron, and somewhere below the hill, the long groan of the river ice shifting in its sleep. That groan had been getting louder all week. The whole valley sounded like a great door being forced. In three days, maybe four, the ferry would take its berths, and everything we were doing here would be either too late or just barely in time.

* * *

We had been at it perhaps forty minutes when the lantern caught it — not the box, but a change in the soil. The earth around the roots turned darker, richer, worked soil, old composted leaves, the deliberate filling-in of a grave rather than the accident of one. And then the bar struck something that was not root and not stone, something that gave back a wooden knock, soft and hollow, and both of us went still as though the tree itself had spoken.

"The dog," Mara whispered.

"Yes."

"Careful," she said, and there was such weight in it, such ceremony in the small hunched shoulders, that I set the bar down and took up the spade instead, and we finished with hands more than tools, brushing the dirt away the way you'd uncover a sleeping face.

The box was cedar, small, the size of a bread loaf, and the wood had gone silver-grey but held. Inside, curled as dogs curl, was a shape the frost had kept whole these eleven years. Mara looked at it for a long time. She did not cry. She put her mitten out and rested it, very lightly, on the small skull, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you loved.

"His name was Brioche," she said. "Because he was the color of bread. Mama said he was the only sweet thing Grandfather ever baked."

And beneath the dog, wrapped in cloth gone stiff with cold and years, was the second bundle. Smaller. I lifted it out and my heart was going like a train. Through the rotting cloth I could feel the shape of it — oval, thin, a chain coiled inside like a sleeping snake.

"Mara. This is it. Do you understand? Everything — the orchard, the clock, your mother's whole plan—"

"I know," she said. "Open it after. Not here."

"Why not here?"

"Because," she said, with terrible practicality, "you'll drop it if you're surprised, and you're always surprised."

* * *

It was on the walk back — we were at the wall, I had Mara boosted up and was reaching for the stones myself — that the light found us.

Not the moon. A lantern, down by the well, swinging in a man's hand, and under it a second man, and behind them both, at the gate we had declined to use, the sound of the hinge singing its thirty-year song.

"Someone's been at the tree," one voice said, low. "Look at the snow. Look at it — all torn up—"

I pulled Mara off the wall into the shadow of it and we stood pressed against the cold stone while the lights bobbed closer along the row. My mind did what a clockmaker's mind does: counted. Two men at the well, one or more at the gate, and the wall between us and the tower path, and the snow on our side of the wall printed with our coming and going like a confession. There was no clever way out of this. There was only the dumb animal way, which is to say, running, and running makes noise, and noise brings the whole watch down on a girl they had already tried once to make disappear.

"Isandro." Mara's whisper against my ear. "The drain. By the well corner. The big one. It goes under the wall to the river path. I go through it when I want to see the boats."

"You go through a drain."

"When I *want* to see the boats," she repeated, as though the sentence had been insufficiently appreciated.

The lanterns were close now. I could hear breathing, boots crushing snow, a man saying "get the other light, check the far rows" — Aurelio had posted his watchers thick tonight, then, because he too could hear the ice breaking up and knew his hour was coming. I took Mara's hand. Her glove was frozen stiff and her grip underneath it was not.

We ran crouched along the wall, four trees' lengths, five, while behind us the voices rose — "*here*, the ground's been opened—" — and reached the well corner, and there it was: a dark mouth in the wall's footing, half-choked with dead leaves, big enough for a child and, God forgive me, just barely big enough for a grown man who had spent his life folding himself into clock cases.

"In," I breathed. "Quickly."

She went in like an otter. I went in like a piano. There is no dignity in a drain; there is only forward. The stone scraped my shoulders, the locket bundle jammed against my ribs inside my coat, and behind me, muffled by the wall, I heard a watcher's shout: "Tracks! Tracks by the wall! *They're here!*"

Then we were through, out onto the river path, and the night opened around us, wide and dark and loud with the ice. We ran — I carried her the last stretch, her arms around my neck, the bundle safe between us — until the observatory's black bulk rose ahead and we stumbled in the door and I threw the bolt and stood in the dark of the clockroom, gasping, laughing, half weeping, holding a seven-year-old and a cedar-scented ghost of a dog between my hands.

"They followed us to the wall," I said. "They know someone dug. By morning Aurelio will know it was us."

Mara wriggled down. She stood there in the lamplight, snow melting in her hair, entirely unafraid, and held out both mittens.

"Now," she said. "You can open it now. You weren't surprised."

* * *

So I unwrapped it there on the winding table, among the oil cans and the gear wheels, with the stopped clock hanging silent above us like a held breath. The cloth fell away and there it was: a silver locket on a fine chain, oval, and on its face — worked in enamel, white on white, so that you only saw it when the light crossed it right — a branch of blossoms.

An orchard branch. The key to the hidden train, shaped like the thing it guarded.

My hands, which do not shake on delicate work, shook now. I turned it over. The back was plain, hinged, and I understood suddenly why the mechanism had never opened for any heir who came hunting with crowbars and greed: it did not want force. It wanted this. It wanted the branch laid into the lock the way a blossom is laid into a book — gently, in season, by someone with a right.

"We should wait," I heard myself say. "Daylight. Witnesses. If I open it alone and Aurelio claims I stole what was inside—"

"He'll say that anyway," Mara said. "He says things anyway."

She was right, of course. She usually is, which I am learning to find less charming than I pretend.

And then, through the floor, up the tower stair, faint but unmistakable: boots. More than one pair. Climbing.

Mara looked at me. I looked at Mara. The bolt was thrown — but bolts are wood and honesty, and men with crowbars are neither, and I had perhaps a minute, maybe two, and in my pocket a dead woman's locket and in the machine above us a chamber that had waited eleven years for exactly this light, this hour, this child standing in her grandfather's clockroom with snow still in her hair.

The boots reached the landing.

The knocking began — heavy, deliberate, a fist with authority behind it — and a voice I knew as well as my own heartbeat, because I had heard it making offers across a desk not two weeks past:

"Nakamura. Open the door. I know what you've got, and I've brought men to make sure you give it to me."

Mara's hand found mine in the dark. Below us the river groaned, long and low, like a thing turning over in its sleep before waking — and in my other hand, hidden, the little silver branch lay cold as the ground we'd stolen it from, waiting to be planted.
