## 26. The Berths Are Sold

The notice went up on the ferry office door on a Tuesday, in the particular handwriting that officialdom reserves for bad news — copperplate, unhurried, faintly pleased with itself.

*SPRING MANIFEST FINAL. ICE-BREAKING FERRY *MERIDIAN*. THREE (3) PASSENGER BERTHS. NO ADDITIONAL PASSAGE WILL BE SOLD UNDER ANY CIRCUMSTANCES.*

I stood in the slush outside the office with my collar up and my hands in my pockets, reading it the way you read a diagnosis. Not because the words were complicated. Because they were so simple.

Three berths.

Aurelio Voss had one. His uncle, old Severin, had the second — bought, I'd learn later, less out of any desire to leave than out of the principle of not letting his nephew have anything unopposed, even an escape. And the third berth belonged to a man named Corben Halsey, who was not family at all but a land agent from downriver with ink-stained cuffs and a smile like a ledger line. I knew what he was there to buy before I knew his name: Aurelio had sold him an option on the orchard, sight unseen, deed or no deed. The berth wasn't passage. It was collateral. A man doesn't buy a piece of property in winter unless he intends to be standing on it when the ice goes out.

So there it was. Three berths, all spoken for, every one of them claimed by the machinery of the inheritance war — the heir, the obstacle, the buyer. The ferry would take on its passengers at dawn on the day of the break, whenever the river chose that day to be, and it would carry them downriver ahead of the cracking ice, and it would take no one else.

Not Elena.

Not Mara.

* * *

"You're staring at it as though staring might add berths," said the ferry clerk, a narrow woman named Mrs. Ostrey who had watched me through the glass with the patience of someone who charges by the question.

"Could a man buy a berth privately? Off the manifest?"

"Off the manifest." She said it the way you'd repeat a curse word to be sure you'd heard it right. "Mr. Nakamura. That manifest has been finalized, witnessed, stamped, and posted. The company's charter says three berths for the spring run, no more, because three berths is what the boat can hold above the weight of the ice tackle. You could offer me your grandfather's ghost and my answer would still be no."

"What about crew?"

"Crew is crew. Signed articles, inspected." Her eyes narrowed, just slightly, and something moved behind them — not sympathy exactly, more like the memory of sympathy, filed somewhere she couldn't easily reach. "You're asking for the girl."

"I'm asking generally."

"You're asking for the girl." She folded her hands. "Here is what I can tell you, generally. Berths one and two were claimed in December, paid in full, gold, before the channel closed. Berth three was claimed eleven days ago by Mr. Halsey, also gold. The company does not care who rides as long as the gold is real. But the company also does not refund, and it does not reshuffle, and if you come to this window again I will charge you for the conversation."

She slid the wicket shut. Then, after a moment, it opened a finger's width.

"The daughter tried," Mrs. Ostrey said quietly. "Miss Elena. Three weeks ago, before the freeze took the landing. She offered everything she had, which was not much, and then she offered her mother's ring, which was." The wicket closed again. "I told her no. I've regretted it since, if that's worth anything to anyone. It isn't. Good day, Mr. Nakamura."

* * *

I walked back up through town with the notice burning behind my eyes like a negative image. Three berths. The whole moral architecture of the thing had been assembled while we were digging in frozen ground, while Mara was learning escapements, while I was tracing dummy gears by lamplight. They hadn't needed to scheme about the ferry at all. They had only needed to buy it early, the way you buy anything — quietly, in gold, before the people who'd need it understood they were drowning.

That was the trap, and I could finally see all of it, the way you see a gear train whole once the plates are off. Aurelio didn't need the deed anymore, not urgently, not the way he had in January. He needed only to be gone before the mechanism opened. Let Isandro Nakamura open his little vault in spring. Let the paper say whatever the dead man wanted it to say. Paper could be fought for years — lawyers, courts, claims, countersuits — but a man standing on downriver soil with gold in hand and an agent beside him was already past the reach of paper. The orchard's future could rot in a clocktower for all he cared, so long as his own future was on the water.

And the third berth — Halsey's berth — meant the sale was already half-drafted. Whatever the deed said, Aurelio intended there to be a buyer present, ready, holding an option signed in winter against a signature extorted in spring. He didn't want to steal the orchard. He wanted to sell it, cleanly, before anyone could read the fine print of its heart.

Which left Elena with nothing, and Mara with less, and me standing in the middle holding a locket shaped like an apple branch and calling it a key.

* * *

Mara met me at the tower door, as she always did now, with snow to her knees and her breath hanging around her head like a small weather system. She was nine years old and she had learned to read my face faster than I could arrange it.

"They've finished the list," I said. There was no point pretending; she'd have pried it out of me by supper anyway, and lying to her had become a thing I found myself physically unable to do, like whistling.

"How many?"

"Three."

"Who?"

"Aurelio. His uncle. And a buyer."

Her face did the arithmetic without flinching. Children are better at sums like this than adults, because adults have learned to look away from totals. "So there isn't one for Elena."

"No."

"And there isn't one for me."

"Mara—"

"It's all right." She said it in the tone of a person comforting someone at a funeral, which is a tone no nine-year-old should own, and she came inside and stamped the snow off her boots with terrible thoroughness, and hung her coat on its peg — the coat, the one with the note still pinned inside the lining where I'd never removed it — and went to light the lamp by the workbench as though the world were a reasonable place.

It was not a reasonable place. I stood in the doorway hating it with a completeness I hadn't felt since my grandfather's name came off a page in front of me.

* * *

That night I did what I always did when the town handed me a wall: I sat down with the mechanism and looked for a seam.

The master clock ticked overhead, patient, indifferent, keeping a time that had nothing to do with ferries. Below it, spread across the bench, lay the hidden train — the dummy gears, the locked chamber, the whole beautiful stubborn vault that old Voss had built into his masterpiece so that his orchard could not be sold out from under the town that worked it. I had spent months learning its grammar. I knew now that it wanted a key shaped like a branch, that the key was a locket, that the locket had been buried with a dog under the first tree, that a nine-year-old had led two adults to it across frozen ground while Aurelio's watchers closed in like weather.

We had the key. We had, as of last week, the chamber located, the tumblers mapped, the sequence reasoned out. What we did not have was time, and now I understood that the time had been taken deliberately, purchased in gold in December while the rest of us were watching the ice.

I turned the locket over in my fingers. Blossoms in silver, worn nearly smooth. Somewhere under the first tree, a dog's grave with a hole in it now, refilled badly by lamplight, marked by nothing.

"Isandro." Mara's voice from the stair, small and awake when she should have been asleep. She came down in her blanket with her hair loose and stood looking at the bench, at the open works, at me. "Are you going to give Aurelio the berth?"

"I don't have a berth to give."

"You know what I mean." She sat on the stool across from me, knees together, hands folded — her thinking posture, the one that made her look like a clockmaker's apprentice instead of a child, which is what she was becoming whether anyone authorized it or not. "Everyone wants something from you. Uncle Aurelio wants the deed. Aunt Elena won't ask for herself but she'll ask for me, I've seen her practicing. And you're going to stand between them and pick, and whoever you don't pick will hate you, and maybe you'll hate yourself, and the clock will keep ticking either way." She said it flatly, the way she recited escapement theory back to me, having understood the mechanism completely and finding it ugly. "Why does it have to be one of theirs?"

"Because those are the berths that exist."

"Then why does it have to be one of theirs?" she repeated, with the mild infuriating patience of a child who has caught an adult in an error and will hold the position until corrected. "Make a different one exist."

I laughed — actually laughed, once, short and surprised, into the lamplit dark of a clocktower in the last week of a hard winter. Make a different one exist. As if a manifest were a gear I could cut. As if the company's charter were a plate I could drill and repin.

But I am a clockmaker's apprentice, or I was, and there is a disease we all carry, which is that when someone tells us a thing is impossible, our hands start moving before our pride can stop them.

"Go to bed," I said.

She went. At the top of the stair she paused. "Elena gave away everything she had," Mara said, not turning around. "Mrs. Ostrey told me. Even the ring. She didn't even try to keep the ring secret, that's how you know it was really everything." Then she was gone, and I sat alone with the tick of the master clock and the silver branch in my palm, and the thought arrived the way thoughts do when they've been waiting a long time downstairs: the manifest was final, witnessed, stamped, posted.

Posted.

Public. Read by everyone. Which meant everyone in town knew there were three berths and three claimants, and everyone in town had also, for five months, watched a clockmaker and a child keep a dead man's promise alive in a tower while the family that owned the paper warred over it like gulls over a fish. Towns remember things like that. Towns keep accounts of their own, unofficial ones, kept in talk rather than ink.

I had spent the whole winter believing history happened elsewhere. Then I had learned it happened to my grandfather on a page in a ferry ledger, and to my own name, and now it was happening to a nine-year-old on a posted manifest, in copperplate, in broad daylight.

Well. I knew something about pages.

I got out paper of my own.
