## 27. Elena Asks

There is a particular humiliation in climbing a clocktower to beg, and I want it noted that I did not beg. I climbed. That was already more than my brother had done in twenty years of wanting things.

The stairs were narrow and smelled of oil and cold iron, and by the third flight I had stopped pretending I wasn't counting them as a way of not thinking about what I would say at the top. Forty-one steps, a landing, thirty-eight more. The orchard house had a staircase twice that length and I had descended it every morning of my marriage like a queen reviewing troops, and now here I was, winded, gloved in my second-best wool, hauling myself up toward a boy who wound clocks for a living.

The door at the top stood open. Of course it did. He knew I was coming; half the town had known since the manifests went up on the ferry office board with three names inked where four should have been.

He was at his bench with his back to me, sleeves rolled, a lamp burning against the grey afternoon, and he did not turn around right away. I understood that too. It's what you do when you know the visitor has come to take something and you'd rather have ten more seconds of being a person than a problem.

"Mrs. Vane," he said finally. "Or should I say — no, I don't actually know your maiden name."

"Elena does fine," I said. "Mrs. Vane died with my husband, thank God, and everyone else in this town insists on resurrecting her daily."

That got the ghost of a smile out of him. He turned then, and I saw what the town had been muttering about — that he was young, younger than his hands suggested, with the careful stillness of someone who had learned early that being watched was the same as being hunted. My brother's men had seen to that.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. "Aurelio posts watchers on the lane after dark."

"It isn't dark yet, and one of your watchers owes me money from a card table in '94, so we came to an arrangement involving his dignity and my silence." I set down my satchel. "Also I brought bread. Bribery is a language this family speaks fluently; I thought I'd use it for good."

The girl was not in the room. I looked anyway — I couldn't help it, my eyes went to every corner, every shadow behind the great brass shapes of the clock — and Isandro watched me look, and something in his face closed a degree.

"She's asleep," he said. "She sleeps badly when strangers come."

"I'm not a stranger."

"No," he agreed, and there was an edge under the mildness. "You're the woman who recognized her coat and wept and asked me to keep her hidden, which I have done, at considerable risk, without ever once hearing a reason that survived daylight."

I deserved that. I had carried the deserving of it up forty-one steps and thirty-eight more, and it sat in my chest like a stone I had swallowed whole back in November.

"Then let me pay part of the debt," I said. "Sit down, clockmaker. This will take a while, and I'd rather do it sitting, because if I do it standing I'll do it proudly, and pride has cost this family everything it owns."

* * *

I told him about my father first, because everything else hangs off him like coats on a nail.

Old Tomás Ferro — Ferro, yes, iron, my grandfather had a sense of humor that skipped two generations — built the master clock and most of its lies, and loved the orchard the way other men love God, which is to say loudly in public and desperately in private. He had three children: Aurelio, who loved the orchard the way a wolf loves a sheep; my brother Tomás the younger, dead of fever before any of this mattered; and me, who loved the trees themselves, which made me, according to Aurelio, sentimental, and according to our father, the only one of us paying attention.

"He drew up the true deed himself," I said. "Not the one the lawyers hold. The lawyers' copy says the orchard descends to heirs. The real one — the one in your clock, if my father told the truth, and he lied beautifully about everything else but never about the trees — says otherwise."

Isandro set down his file. "You've never read it."

"No one has. That was the point. A deed in a hand is a thing that gets stolen, forged, burned. A deed inside a mechanism only a clockmaker can open — that's a deed that waits." I laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "He trusted a stranger's profession more than his own children. I used to think that was the cruelest thing about him. Then my daughter was born and I learned there were crueller arithmetic available."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below us the town was lighting its own lamps, window by window, and the river under all that ice was making the sound it makes in late winter — a long groaning, like a giant turning over in a bed too small for him, dreaming of moving.

"I left Mara at your door," I said.

"I know."

"You don't know *why*, though. You've guessed, and your guesses are unkind, and they're allowed to be, because from where you stand there is no kind version." I took off my gloves, finger by finger. My hands are my father's hands, broad and scarred from grafting knives, and they shook, and I hated them for it. "Aurelio wanted her. Not wanted — *needed*. A child of the blood is leverage. If the deed ever surfaced, whoever holds the child holds the claim to guardianship, and guardianship of an heir means administration of the estate, and administration means Aurelio signs and everyone else applauds. He said it plainly at Christmas, over the ham, in front of the servants. 'The girl is worth more than the trees.' I heard my father die a little at that table, and he'd been in the ground six years."

"So you gave her away."

"So I gave her away," I said. "To a note pinned to a coat, at the one door in this town my brother doesn't watch, to a stranger whose name meant nothing to anyone — or so I believed. I chose the observatory because it was neutral ground. Because the clock belonged to the town, not the family. Because a foundling left at a public institution becomes the town's, and the town cannot be bullied the way a widow can." I looked at him. "And then it turned out the clockmaker was a Nakamura, and history, which I thought I had outrun, was standing at the door holding a lantern."

He was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its edge and gained something worse — pity.

"My grandfather worked your orchard," he said. "Picked fruit, mended fence lines, and for one winter, they say, helped your father carry timbers up this hill. And when the quarrels started, his name came off the ferry manifest like it had never been written. Erased. I grew up on stories about a town that happened to other people, Mrs.— Elena. I came here believing history was a country I didn't hold a passport for." He gestured at the great silent gears above us, the stopped heart of the place. "Then I climbed these stairs and found your father's vault and your daughter asleep on the step, and now I live inside history the way you live inside a coat you can't take off. So no. My guesses weren't kind. But they were informed."

"I'm not asking for forgiveness."

"What are you asking for?"

And there it was. The question I had climbed sixty-nine steps to answer, and now that it hung in the lamplight between us, my mouth went dry as August.

* * *

"The ferry takes three berths before the ice breaks," I said. "Aurelio has claimed them. His name, his factor's name, his lawyer's name. Three berths, three men, and the moment the river opens they'll ride down to the city and start selling an inheritance they may not even legally possess, because none of them have read the real deed and none of them want to." I folded my gloves in my lap. Folded them again. "You know how these berths work. The ferryman honors claims, but a berth can be *transferred*. Signed over. It's done every spring — a man buys his neighbor's passage when the neighbor decides to stay. The paper just needs the holder's mark and the ferryman's stamp."

Isandro had gone very still. Clockmakers go still the way water goes still — you sense the depth underneath.

"Aurelio would never sign one over to you."

"Aurelio doesn't need to." I made myself look at him straight. "One of those three names is a formality. His lawyer has a bad hip and a wife in the city and no love for my brother — I've known the man since he was courting my sister-in-law, and I know what he wants, which is to be elsewhere. He'll sell his berth. To me. For money I have, from a marriage settlement Aurelio has spent two years trying to touch and hasn't managed, because dead husbands occasionally prove useful. So the last berth of the season, the one berth that isn't nailed down—" my voice cracked and I hauled it level like a load that had shifted, "—is yours to give. Or to keep. That's why I'm here. That's the whole of why I'm here."

"And you want it for yourself."

The wry narrator in my head — the one that had gotten me through the funeral, through the Christmas table, through the night I laid a sleeping child on stone steps and walked away without looking back because looking back would have killed us both — offered up several elegant deflections. I declined them all.

"I want it for whichever of us you judge should have it," I said. "Me or Mara. I will accept your judgment either way, Isandro, and I mean that as a binding oath, not a performance. That's the ask. That's the humiliating, unforgivable, entirely earned ask." I stood, because I could not say the rest sitting down. "If you give it to me, I go downriver, and I come back in summer with a solicitor from the capital and enough legal weight to crack Aurelio's claim like a walnut, and I fight for the orchard and for her from outside the walls, because inside the walls I'm just the widow who cries at card tables. If you give it to her—"

I stopped. There was no elegant way to finish that sentence, so I finished it honestly.

"If you give it to her, she goes downriver alone, or with whatever escort the town provides, into a world I abandoned her into once already, and she grows up wherever orphans grow up, and maybe someday she comes back and finds her mother's grave, because I will not survive another winter in this town without her, and we both know grief keeps excellent books."

The clock ticked. No — it didn't; the master clock had been stopped since autumn, and yet the room ticked anyway, small mechanisms on his bench, the pocket watches, time itself refusing to grant anyone an intermission.

"You'd let a nineteen-year-old apprentice decide," he said. "Between a mother and her child."

"You're not nineteen. You're the man my father's ghost hired." I smiled, and my face felt like it was cracking along old seams. "And I'd let you decide because you're the only person in this valley who has held both of us — her at the door, me in my worst hour — and not flinched. Aurelio flinches at anything that can't be inventoried. The uncle flinches at weather. I flinch at mirrors." I picked up my satchel. "My father built his masterpiece so that no single heir could steal the orchard. He just never imagined the deciding would fall to an outsider. Though knowing him—" the laugh escaped before I could stop it, wet and startled, "—knowing him, he'd call it the best joke he ever wrote."

I put the bread on his bench. My hands were steady now; it figures, steadiness always arrives after it's useful.

"There's no answer needed tonight," I said. "The ice won't break for days yet. But I'll tell you one thing more, free of charge, and then I'll climb down these stairs and walk past your watcher and owe him nothing further." I looked at the stopped clock, at the dummy gears he'd shown me drawings of, at the locked chamber at its heart where a dead man's truth waited behind a lock shaped like a blossom. "Whatever you choose, choose it as the man who opened the door in November. Not as the man the town has been trying to make you since — theirs, Aurelio's, mine. We are all of us trying to spend you, Isandro Nakamura. Don't let a single one of us buy you cheap."

At the landing halfway down, her voice reached me — small, sleep-thick, calling his name, and his answering, low and certain, *I'm here, little bird, I'm here* — and I stood in the dark between floors with my hand pressed flat against the cold stone, listening to a child trust a stranger completely, and I understood, with the terrible clarity the dying are supposed to get and mothers get instead at random hours, exactly what my answer would be if the choice were mine.

But it wasn't mine. That was the whole point of asking.

Above me, the tower creaked against the wind off the river, and beneath everything, faint as a pulse under floorboards, the ice began to talk.
