## 28. Aurelio Asks

The knock came at half past nine in the morning, which told me everything before I opened the door. Aurelio Vasseur was a man who believed that important business was conducted before noon and that anyone who disagreed was a servant.

I had spent the previous evening with Elena's request sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone. She had asked for the last berth — for herself or for Mara, whichever my judgment favored, she'd said, which was either the most generous thing I had ever heard or the cleverest, depending on how cynical I allowed myself to be by lamplight. And cynicism, I'll admit, is a clockmaker's occupational disease. We spend our lives assuming every beautiful movement has a hidden spring.

So when Aurelio's fist hit the tower door for the third time, I opened it with the particular calm of a man who has been expecting one wolf and is not entirely surprised to find two at the same threshold.

"Master Nakamura." He said my name the way he said all names — as if granting them a loan he expected repaid with interest. He stood on my steps in a coat worth more than the observatory's annual coal allowance, breath steaming, two of his men arranged behind him on the frozen path like furniture that could bite.

"Signor Vasseur."

"I'll come in." He didn't ask it, and he didn't wait. He brushed past me into the clockroom, filling the space the way cold air fills a room with a broken window — completely and without apology. His eyes went around the tower: the weights, the pendulum hanging still, the great motionless face of the master clock above town. He looked at it the way a man looks at a safe he's been told contains his money.

"You've found it," he said.

"Found what?"

"Don't." The word came out flat and quick, a trapdoor dropping. "My sister came here Tuesday night. My sister, who hasn't set foot on this hill since our father's funeral, climbed your stairs in the dark like a girl sneaking out to meet a sweetheart. And yesterday morning you were seen digging in the orchard. At night. With a lantern and a child holding the shovel." He smiled, and it did nothing to warm his face. "I own men who can count, Nakamura. It's the one skill I never had to buy."

I said nothing. There are moments when silence is cowardice, and there are moments when it is simply the only tool that fits the screw. This felt like the second kind, mostly because my heart was hammering hard enough to be heard across the room, and I needed a moment to get it under control before I trusted my voice.

Mara was upstairs. I knew this the way I now knew things about her without looking — by the small creak of the third step, the almost-silent pad of her feet along the gallery. She had heard him come in. She would stay where she was, because we had an arrangement: when the family came, she went up, and she listened, and she learned. It was not, perhaps, the education other children received. But then, other children weren't deeds made flesh, fought over like a parcel at a widow's auction.

"The deed," Aurelio said again, slower, savoring it. "My grandfather built it into this clock rather than leave it in a lawyer's drawer. Sentimental old fool. He hid it from *me* — from his own heir — because he thought I'd sell." He walked to the stopped clock and laid his gloved hand against the case, almost tenderly, which was somehow worse than if he'd kicked it. "He was right, of course. I would have sold. Land is land. It wants to be money. That's not greed, Nakamura, that's just translation."

"And yet you've spent a winter trying to translate it back."

His head turned. For a moment something honest moved behind his eyes — surprise, maybe, that I had teeth. Then the smile returned, thinner. "You have a mouth on you. Good. I respect a craftsman who knows his own price."

Here it was, then. I had known since the ferry manifests that this conversation was coming; I had rehearsed it in the dark while winding weights, running through refusals the way a boy runs through conjugations. But knowing a storm is coming and standing in it are different trades entirely.

"I already refused your uncle's version of this offer," I said. "In December. You may compare notes."

"My uncle offered you money." Aurelio removed a glove, finger by finger, and I understood that the gesture was theater, that every part of this man was theater, but that the play always ended in the same act. "I'm offering you something better than money. A workshop. Stone floors, north light, room for six apprentices. Your name on the door. And more than that—" he let the glove drop onto the clock case, deliberately, an insult so small only a clockmaker would feel its full weight, "—your family's name. Restored. The ledgers corrected. Nakamura back in the orchard's story where it should have been all along. Your grandfather worked those rows, didn't he? Until someone decided he hadn't. I can un-decide it. I'm the last person alive who can."

And God forgive me, for three full seconds, I wanted it.

You have to understand what that name means to a man who grew up watching his mother's face go carefully blank whenever the orchard was mentioned. Erasure isn't a single wound. It's a weather. It gets into everything. Every form filled out, every introduction at every fair, every time some clerk squinted at our surname and asked, *and your people are from...?* My grandfather had pruned those trees for eleven years, and then a page in a ferry ledger went blank and he became nobody from nowhere, and my father inherited that nowhere like a coat too heavy ever to take off.

Aurelio watched me wanting it. He was good enough at reading faces to see exactly what he was looking at.

"There it is," he said softly. "Yes. That's the look. That's the price, isn't it?" He picked up his glove. "Say yes, and by summer no one in this town will remember there was ever a question about your people. Open the mechanism, hand me the deed, and I'll write your name back into this valley with my own pen. The orchard keeps a clockmaker. The clockmaker keeps his honor. Everyone walks away richer."

"And Mara?"

The name landed between us like a dropped wrench.

"What about her?"

"She's the child of the clock. The deed says so — you know that much, at least, or you wouldn't be standing in my tower offering me your pen." I kept my voice level, though it cost something. "What happens to her in your version of everyone walking away richer?"

Aurelio shrugged, and it was the shrug that did it — that small, economical lifting of shoulders, the gesture of a man declining the last of the wine. "She goes where foundlings go. There's a process. An orphanage downriver, a family upriver, paperwork. It's not cruelty, Nakamura, it's administration. She's seven years old and legally nobody. In a year she won't remember this tower."

"In a year," I said, "she'll remember everything. I've watched her memory work. It's the best mechanism in this building."

"Then she'll remember a warm orphanage and a decent family, which is more than the river would have given her." He stepped closer, and now the theater fell away, and what stood in front of me was simply a man who had never once been told no by anyone whose opinion mattered to him. "Let me be plain, since you're fond of plainness. The deed is mine. It was always going to be mine — the only question was whether it came to me cleanly or through lawyers, and whether you were standing beside me when it did or standing in front of me. Choose the first, and you get everything I described. Choose the second—"

"You'll take Mara as a ward of the state." I said it before he could, because I wanted him to know I'd already done the arithmetic. "You'll file papers. You'll claim concern for her welfare. You'll have a magistrate here within the week, and she'll be gone from this tower before the ice finishes breaking."

Something flickered in his face — not shame, he wasn't built with any, but the brief irritation of a negotiator whose leverage has been priced correctly. "You make it sound sinister. It's the law. A child living in a clocktower with an unmarried foreign apprentice, no guardian, no papers, no school — do you imagine that stands? One letter from me and she's gone. And here's the thing—" he leaned in, and his voice dropped to the register men use for confidences and threats, having discovered long ago they're the same instrument, "—you couldn't even stop it by giving the deed away afterward. The moment she's in the system, she's in it. No deed brings her back. No locket. Nothing."

*Nothing.* The word hung in the cold air of the clockroom, and I thought: he doesn't know about the locket. He knows I dug. He doesn't know what I found. Elena hadn't told him — of course she hadn't; she'd spent a winter learning to keep secrets from her brother the way other people learn to keep warm. And Mara, bless her silent feet, had not breathed a word through the floorboards.

Which meant I still held one card he couldn't see, and the only thing more dangerous than a desperate man is a desperate man who thinks he's already counted your hand.

"Is that your final offer?" I asked.

"That depends on your answer."

"Then let me give you a different answer to a different question." I moved to the window — partly for the light, partly because I wanted my back to stone and my face toward the river. Below, the ice lay white and ridged all the way to the far bank, groaning sometimes in the sun like an old man turning over in bed. Two days, maybe three, and it would go. "Why do you want the deed so badly, signor? Not the orchard — you said yourself you'd sell it inside the year. The deed itself. What does a piece of paper mean to a man who deals in translations?"

For the first time, Aurelio Vasseur had no reply prepared. He stood there with his gloves in his hand and his men waiting outside in the cold, and something crossed his face that I recognized, because I'd seen it in mirrors after my grandfather's funeral: the look of a man who has just realized he wants a thing not because it will make him happy but because not having it has become unbearable. His grandfather had built the vault to spite him specifically — a dead man reaching through forty years of gears to say *not you*. Aurelio wasn't fighting for land. He was fighting to win an argument with a corpse.

"It's mine," he said finally, and there was nothing smooth left in it. "By law, by blood, by every custom this valley has ever kept. It's *mine*, and I am tired of dead men and children and clockmakers telling me otherwise."

"Then we understand each other," I said. "Because I'm tired of them too. Dead men erased my grandfather. Living ones are trying to erase a child. And I have decided — recently, and with considerable pleasure — to be finished being polite about it."

He stared at me. Behind us the stopped clock ticked its single ghost-tick, the one loose tooth in the escape wheel that still caught and released each hour, a heartbeat in a corpse. Upstairs, very faintly, a floorboard settled under small careful weight.

"You're refusing," Aurelio said. Not asking. Confirming, the way a man confirms a diagnosis.

"I'm refusing."

"You understand that I keep my promises as well as my threats."

"I'm counting on it. It's the one thing I trust about you." I walked to the door and opened it, letting the knife-cold of the morning pour in. "Good day, signor. Mind the steps — the thaw's made them treacherous."

He put his glove back on, finger by finger, unhurried, making a ceremony of it, and walked past me to the door. On the threshold he paused. When he spoke, he didn't turn around.

"When the ferry loads Thursday, there will be three berths taken by my family. If you change your mind before then, send word. If you don't—" now he turned, and his eyes went up, deliberately, toward the gallery where a child was holding herself perfectly still, "—then enjoy your last week with her. Magistrates travel fast once the roads clear."

Then he was gone, down the path, his men falling in behind him like punctuation, and the door swung shut, and the silence that followed was the heavy kind, full of everything unsaid.

I stood with my hand on the latch until his footsteps faded on the frozen road below.

Then the third step creaked, and small feet came down the stair, and Mara appeared in the doorway of the clockroom with her arms wrapped around herself and her chin up in the way she'd learned from watching me lie to officials, which I resolved on the spot to stop modeling.

"He's going to take me away," she said. Not a question. She had listened to the whole thing, and she was seven, and she had understood every word, because children always understand the words adults wish they wouldn't.

"No," I said.

"You said so. Out loud. But saying isn't gears." She came closer, and her voice stayed steady in the way that frightened me far more than crying would have. "Saying can be broken. Like the clock was broken. What's going to hold?"

I knelt down to her height, and I thought about what holds. Weights hold. Escapements hold. Promises, sometimes, if you build them well enough and service them regularly.

"Mara," I said, "do you remember what I told you about the hidden train? How the whole secret of this clock rests on parts nobody can see?"

She nodded.

"Well. From where that man is standing, we're the parts nobody can see." I took both her cold hands in mine. "And tomorrow night, before the ice breaks, we're going to open the mechanism in front of the entire town — and read what's written inside it out loud, where no magistrate and no signature and no Aurelio Vasseur can ever file it away. Whatever happens with the ferry. Whatever happens with the berths. That happens first. I promise you on my grandfather's name, which I intend to make worth promising on."

She studied my face with those grave dark eyes, doing what she always did — checking the mechanism of me, tooth by tooth, for anything loose.

"All right," she said at last. "But I'm packing my bundle anyway."

And upstairs, I knew, the little bundle sat ready by her bed, just as it had since the night Elena first asked for a berth — packed and repacked a dozen times by small hands, waiting for a world that kept teaching her she might have to run.

Outside, far below, the river gave a long, low groan, and somewhere in its depths a crack began, slow and patient, working its way toward morning.
