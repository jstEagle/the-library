## 29. The Night Before the Break

I had learned to count in ticks before I learned my letters, so I will tell you exactly how long a night can be: eleven thousand four hundred and forty ticks, if you are waiting for morning, and twice that if you are waiting for something else.

I was waiting for something else.

The river had been talking for three days now — not cracking yet, not breaking, just muttering under its ice like an old man who wants everyone to know he is awake and displeased. Isandro said that was normal for this time of year. He said it in his clockmaker voice, which is the one he uses when he is telling you a true thing and hoping you won't ask what it costs.

Everyone in town had been walking down to the banks to look at the water. Even Aurelio's men, the ones who were supposed to be watching the tower, spent more time at the river than at their post, because there is nothing interesting about watching a door that doesn't open when the whole world is about to crack open instead.

But I knew where the real breaking would happen. It wouldn't happen on the water. It would happen in the clockroom, tomorrow, when Isandro stood between two people who each wanted a berth on the ferry, and one of them wanted me too.

* * *

Let me explain how I figured it out, because nobody told me. Nobody ever tells children things; we have to do our own arithmetic.

Elena came Tuesday, in her gray coat with the hood up, and she sat on the third step of the tower stairs where she always sits because she thinks sitting low makes her smaller, as if being small were a thing you could choose. She asked me how I liked the tower. I said I liked the tick best, and the way the light comes through the east window in the afternoon and makes the dust look like it has somewhere important to go. She laughed, but it was the kind of laugh that has a stone in it.

Then she asked Isandro, quietly, out on the landing where they think I can't hear, whether he had decided.

And Thursday, Aurelio himself came up the hill — which he had never done, he always sent men, because I don't think he likes climbing — and he stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands like a man come to church, and he looked at me the way you look at a horse you are thinking of buying. Not unkindly. That was the worst part. He looked at me the way people look at things that could be theirs.

So I did the arithmetic. One berth left. Two people asking. And me, standing in the middle like the pin in a hinge, the thing that lets the whole door swing.

Children are not supposed to know they are prizes. But we know. We always know. We just aren't supposed to say it out loud, because then the adults have to look at us while they decide, and looking is hard for them.

* * *

I packed on Friday night, after Isandro went down to check the weights.

I want to be honest about this, since this is my story and honesty is the only inheritance anyone actually gave me: packing took longer than it should have, because I kept picking things up and putting them back.

The bundle started sensible. My other dress, the brown one, folded small the way Elena taught me. The wool stockings. The comb with three teeth missing that Isandro had whittled smooth for me when I first came, back when my hair fought him every morning like a cat being washed. Then it got less sensible. A gear tooth, brass, from the first escapement I ever fixed myself — the one where Isandro was gone all day and I did it alone, and when he came home and saw it running I thought he might cry, though he'd deny it. I couldn't leave that. It was proof. Proof that I could make something work that had been broken, which seemed like the most useful thing a person could be.

Then the note. My note. *Forget the face.* I had kept it pinned inside my coat lining all winter, which Isandro knows about and has never once mentioned, which is its own kind of kindness. I took it out and held it up to the lamp. The ink had gone brown at the folds. Whoever wrote it had pressed hard — I had learned enough from watching Isandro trace papers to know that much — had written it fast or angry or both, and then had spent a whole winter weeping on the third stair.

People are very strange. They write *forget the face* and then they climb forty steps to remember it.

I put the note in the bundle. Then I took it out. Then I put it back. Then, finally, I did neither — I left it on the table, weighted under the whetstone, where Isandro would find it, because I had decided something and I wanted the decision to be clean.

Here is what I decided. You can judge me for it later. Everyone else in this story gets judged; why should I be different?

* * *

If I left before dawn, nobody would have to choose.

That was the whole plan, and I want you to appreciate its elegance. Elena asks for the berth. Aurelio demands the berth. Isandro stands between them like a man on ice that is already groaning. Whatever he does, he loses something — Elena, or Aurelio's mercy, or the town's respect, or me, depending. I had watched adults long enough to know they cannot bear losing things. They would rather lose sleep, lose years, lose each other, than lose a thing they were promised.

But if I was gone? Gone downriver on my own feet, gone over the orchard wall, gone into the town that barely knew my name — then there was no prize. Two people still wanted a berth and only one could have it, yes, but that was their quarrel, an ordinary quarrel, the kind grown folk settle with lawyers and cold nods. Nobody would be standing in the middle of it wearing my face.

*Forget the face.* Well. I could help with that.

I am not saying it didn't hurt. I am saying I was nine years old and I had been given away once already, and I had learned from the best that the kindest thing a child can do is become easy to lose. Small footprint. Light bundle. No fuss. There is a version of love that looks exactly like leaving, and I had studied it the way Isandro studied gears, until I could build one myself.

* * *

I waited in the clockroom.

Not the little room where we slept — I made my bed look slept-in, lumped the blanket into a Mara-shaped hill, which felt dishonest and necessary at once. I went up, past the great drum of the pendulum, past the weights hanging in their shaft like slow bronze rain, up to the room where the dial lives, where the numbers are painted backwards on our side so the town can read them forwards. The moon came through the glass and laid a pale stripe across the floor, and the tick was loudest here, right against your chest, as if the clock weren't around you but inside you, some enormous patient heart deciding whether to keep beating.

I sat against the wall under the twelve and I waited.

I had it all worked out. At half past five, before the gray came, I would take the back stairs, the ones Isandro used for hauling oil, and slip out by the coal door. I would cut through the orchard — my orchard, though I suppose I should stop calling it that, though I never intend to stop — and down the ferry path, and I would watch the break from the crowd like any child, and no one would miss me until the sun was up and it was too late to matter.

I even knew what I'd say if someone caught me. I'd practiced it. *I'm going to see the river.* Children are allowed to want to see rivers. It's the perfect crime, wanting a small true thing.

The moon moved. The stripe crossed the floor and touched the pendulum shaft, and the weights caught the light, and I thought about how Isandro had taught me that a clock never hurries and never rests, it just spends itself evenly, minute by minute, until someone comes to wind it again.

Nobody had wound me in a while. I could feel it.

* * *

Sometime after midnight I heard the stairs.

I knew his step — everyone has a step, that's another thing nobody tells you; you learn it living in a tower where sound comes up the stairwell like water finding its level. Isandro climbed slowly these days, favoring the leg that got bruised in the sabotage, and he paused on the landings the way he always did, listening to the train above him the way a doctor listens to a chest.

He was coming up to wind the strike train early. He'd been doing that all week — checking, re-checking, as if the clock might feel the tension in the town and seize up out of sympathy.

I pressed myself flatter against the wall under the twelve, which was foolish, because there is nowhere to hide in a clockroom, that's rather the point of them. Everything in a clockroom is exactly where it must be or the whole thing stops.

His head came through the hatch, and his eyes found me in about a second and a half, which tells you everything about how well hiding works when the person looking knows you.

"Mara."

"It's my turn to listen to the tick," I said. Which was even true. I had been listening to the tick all night. It was the truest sentence available to me and I reached for it the way you grab the nearest solid thing when the floor tilts.

"At midnight."

"Clocks don't have a midnight," I said. "That's for people."

He came the rest of the way up and stood there with the winding crank in his hand and the moon behind him, and he looked at me for a long moment, and then he looked at the floor beside me, at the place where I had been sitting, where I had — without noticing, without meaning to — arranged my things. Not the escape bundle. That was hidden below. But the little collection I carry in my pockets without thinking: the brass gear tooth, a dried apple ring from autumn, a scrap of blue thread off Elena's coat, a pebble shaped like a heart that I found in the orchard mud and kept because it was funny, a rock pretending to be something soft.

All of it laid out neat as a shop display, because apparently when I sit still too long, I inventory my life.

Isandro crouched down. He didn't touch anything. He looked at the little row of treasures the way he looked at a mechanism he was diagnosing — patiently, thoroughly, with that terrible gentleness he has that makes you want to confess things you haven't done yet.

"Heart pebble," he said. "Blue thread. Apple ring." His finger hovered over the gear tooth without landing. "First repair."

"You're not supposed to know which one that was," I said.

"I know everything you've fixed in this tower. It's the only ledger I keep properly." He sat down fully then, his back against the wall next to mine, not touching, but close enough that I could feel the warmth coming off his sleeve, which in a tower in March is practically an embrace. For a while neither of us said anything. Below us the tick went on, spending itself evenly.

"The bed upstairs is lumpy," he said eventually. "In the shape of somebody."

"I move around when I sleep."

"You snore like a bellows."

"I do not."

"Elena says so. When she visits. She says the tower snores." He turned his head, and in the moonlight his face was doing the thing it does where the wry part stays on top and something else moves underneath, like fish under ice. "Where were you going, Mara?"

"I wasn't—"

"The coal door was oiled last week. It hasn't squeaked since. So whoever leaves by it will leave quietly, and whoever oiled it will know exactly when." He let that sit. "I oil things when I'm worried. It's a fault of mine."

My throat went tight. I looked up at the numbers on our side of the dial, the backwards ones, and I said the true thing, because he had earned it, because he'd climbed forty steps at midnight and sat down in the cold next to a liar instead of scolding her.

"If I'm gone, nobody has to choose. That's all. That's the whole thing. Elena gets the berth, or Aurelio does, and it's just — a ferry problem. Ferry problems get solved every spring. People get on boats. Nobody has to stand in a clockroom feeling like a purse someone's fighting over." My voice cracked on *purse*, which annoyed me enormously. "You shouldn't have to lose something tomorrow because of me. You've lost enough. Your grandfather, your name, your — " I gestured at the whole tower, the whole town, the whole erased ledger of him. "I'm the cheapest thing here to lose. I take up almost no room. I checked."

The tick filled the silence. Eleven thousand four hundred ticks, give or take, and every one of them landing on my chest.

Then Isandro laughed. Not a big laugh — a short, broken sort of huff, the kind that means *you have just said the stupidest wise thing I have ever heard.*

"The cheapest thing," he repeated. He shook his head. "Do you know what Aurelio offered me? A workshop. My family's name back. Do you know what your mother's note cost? Nothing. Ink and paper. Do you know what Elena gave up?" He held up his hand and began counting on his fingers, and I realized he was doing my trick, the arithmetic, but turning it the other direction. "A daughter. A family. A whole life, to keep one child from being used as leverage. And you sit here calculating yourself as ballast." He lowered his hand. "You're not the cheapest thing in this story, Mara. You're the only thing in it that everybody agrees on. That's rare. That's worth more than an orchard, and I've read the deed, so I know what an orchard goes for."

"I don't want anybody agreeing on me," I whispered. "It's worse than being forgotten. Being agreed upon."

Something moved across his face at that, quick and pained, and I understood — not all at once, but the way understanding comes in a tower, tick by tick — that he knew exactly what I meant. He'd been agreed upon his whole life, or refused outright; either way, decided about, never asked.

"So don't be agreed upon," he said. "Be consulted. Starting now." He nodded toward the little row of treasures. "Tomorrow, when the ice breaks, I have to stand between two people who both want the same boat. I can't get out of it. It's mine to do — I'm the one holding the locket, I'm the one they'll both look at. But you don't have to be the prize, and you don't have to be the sacrifice, and you absolutely do not have to be the coal-door ghost." He picked up the heart pebble and turned it over once in his fingers, then set it back exactly where it had been, in line with the rest, careful as a man zeroing a balance. "What you can do is stay here, in the warmest room in town, and let one adult do one difficult thing correctly for once. Can you grant me that?"

I looked at him for a long time. The moon stripe had crawled to the far wall. Somewhere below us the river muttered and shifted, testing its ice, and the whole tower carried the sound up to us like a rumor.

"And if you choose wrong?" I asked. Because someone had to ask it, and I was evidently the only person in this family willing to say things out loud.

"Then you may pack again," Isandro said. "And this time I'll oil the coal door myself and hold it open."

* * *

We stayed in the clockroom until the sky went from black to iron to the color of a fresh-cut apple. He showed me, one more time, how the strike train counted the hours — how the pins on the drum catch the lever, one, two, three, patient as footsteps — and I put my hands on the brass and let it talk to me. It was going to be a loud morning. The whole valley would hear it.

Below us, faint and far away, came a sound like a door opening in the earth — long, low, splitting — and then another, closer, and the tower shivered on its stones.

The river was waking up.

Isandro stood and went to the window, and I stood beside him, and together we watched the first gray light find the ice, which had begun, at last, to move. Down in the town, bells were starting. Doors were opening. Somewhere on the stairs below, boots — more than one pair — were beginning to climb.

"They're early," Isandro murmured. "They're both early."

I slipped my hand into my pocket and closed it around the heart pebble, and I did not reach for the coal door, and I did not reach for the stairs, and when the knock came at the clockroom hatch — heavy, then heavier — I stood at Isandro's shoulder, where I had decided to stand, and we answered it together.
