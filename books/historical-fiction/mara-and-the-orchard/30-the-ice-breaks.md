## 30. The Ice Breaks

I have never trusted rivers that keep quiet. A river that murmurs is thinking; a river that goes silent has decided something. All through the last week of March the river below the town had been silent as a held breath, and every soul in it had been walking on eggshells of their own, waiting for the water to make up its mind.

The clock knew first. Of course it did. At four minutes past five in the morning, with Mara asleep in her corner of the clockroom and me halfway up the weight shaft with a lantern and an oil can, I felt the tower shudder — not the tick-shudder I had lived with for a year, but a long, low tremble that came up through the stone from somewhere far below, like a giant turning over in bed.

The master clock stopped.

Not wound-down stopped. Not broken stopped. Stopped the way a man stops talking when he hears a noise downstairs. The pendulum hung mid-swing, and the whole tower seemed to lean its head, listening.

Then the river spoke.

It was not a crack so much as a tearing — a sound I can only describe as the world's largest piece of cloth being ripped along a seam someone had been sewing all winter. It rolled up the valley and rattled the observatory windows, and dust sifted down from the rafters onto my lantern glass, and below us, under three feet of ice and forty feet of dark water, the river shrugged off its winter coat and began to move.

"Mara," I said, too loud, then again, softer. "Mara, wake up. It's happening."

She came up out of sleep the way she always did — instantly, completely, no fog about her — and was standing before I'd finished the second sentence, hair wild, eyes enormous. "Is it time?"

"It's time."

"Did you bring the bundle?"

"I brought the bundle." I had packed it twice, actually — once the night before with bread, cheese, the deed papers wrapped in oilcloth, and once more at three in the morning when I couldn't sleep and repacked it to make sure the bread wasn't stale. This is the sort of thing nobody tells you about history: it is mostly packing and unpacking.

* * *

By the time we got down the two hundred steps and out the observatory door, half the town was already in the street in nightclothes and coats thrown over them, moving toward the banks with the strange collective purpose of people who have been waiting for one thing for five months and have just heard it knock. Nobody ran exactly. They moved quickly, the way you move quickly toward a sickroom or a wedding.

The cold hit like a slap. Dawn was only a gray suggestion behind the eastern hills, and our breath went up in columns, hundreds of them, converging on the water.

Mara took my hand without asking, which she hadn't done since she was small enough that asking would have been reasonable, and we walked together down Ferry Street, past the shuttered chandlery, past the church whose bell-ringer was already hauling on his rope with more enthusiasm than rhythm, down to the frozen mud of the landing where the *Persephone* sat in her winter cradle of timber, steam already coughing from her stack.

And there they were. All of them.

Aurelio Voss stood at the gangplank like a man who owned it, which legally he very nearly did — the ferry company having accepted his family's money for three berths back in February with the cheerful amorality of any business that has survived two fires and a flood. He wore a good coat. He always wore a good coat. His men flanked him, two of them, big as furniture.

His uncle stood apart, near the pilings, hands clasped behind his back, watching the ice with an expression I might have called scholarly if I hadn't spent a winter learning how much calculation could hide behind a scholar's face.

And Elena — Elena stood furthest out, alone at the edge of the crowd where the mud began, in a gray traveling dress that had clearly been put on in the dark, looking not at the ferry but upriver, at the broken ice grinding its way down the channel, plates the size of barn floors tilting and groaning and going under with a sound like a door closing.

Nobody looked at me. Everybody looked at me. That is a trick crowds manage better than you'd think.

"Clockmaker," Aurelio said, when I had come close enough. He said it the way he always did, making the word do double duty as both a greeting and an insult. "You're up early."

"The clock woke me."

"Sentimental of it." He glanced at Mara, and I watched his gaze flick over her — cataloguing, pricing — before returning to me. "You have it, then. What's in your pocket."

It was not a question. That was the trouble with Aurelio: he asked questions the way a bailiff posts notices, already assuming the outcome.

"What makes you think I have anything in my pocket?"

"Because you've been walking around for a week like a man with a hot coal sewn into his lining," he said. "And because my uncle told me what you were doing in the orchard the other night. Digging. In March. In frozen ground." He smiled, and it was almost a good smile, which was the worst thing about it. "You found it. And this morning, clockmaker, there are exactly three berths on that boat, and I hold all three, and you are standing between me and the water holding whatever it is you're holding. So let's not perform. Let's transact."

The ice chose that moment to speak again — a long grinding roar out in the channel, and the whole crowd made a sound, a single involuntary syllable, the way congregations do.

* * *

Here is what nobody had told me about the last berth on the spring ferry, and what I had learned only by reading eleven years of manifests in the ferry office with a lamp and a bad attitude: the *Persephone* could run the channel only during the break-up, in the narrow window between when the ice began to move and when it moved too much to be safe. Three days, maybe four. She carried passengers downriver ahead of the flood — always had, since the town's founding; it was practically a sacrament — and the berths were few because the channel was narrow and the hull was old and the ferrymaster was a cautious man named Odile Brac who had buried two husbands and did not intend to be buried by a river.

Three berths. Claimed in February, paid in full, by the orchard family. Aurelio. The uncle. And the third — this was the detail I had turned over all winter like a stone looking for what lived under it — the third was registered simply as *Voss party, third berth, name to be confirmed.*

Name to be confirmed. As if even the paper didn't believe them.

Elena's name appeared nowhere. Neither did Mara's. When I had pointed this out to Ferrymaster Brac — a woman who looked at you the way a scale looks at weights — she had tapped the manifest with one finger and said, "Money talks, clockmaker. If somebody else wants to talk louder before the boat sails, my window doesn't close till the lines are cast off." Then she had gone back to pretending I was furniture.

Somebody else wanting to talk louder. That was me. That was, apparently, the entire shape of my life now: a man standing on a mudbank between a fortune and a child, with a brass locket shaped like an apple blossom branch in his pocket and the whole town pretending not to watch.

"You're not listening," Aurelio said.

"I'm listening," I said. "I'm also watching the ice, which is more interesting."

* * *

Let me tell you what the ice was doing, because I have seen floods and fires and one memorable bank failure, and nothing prepared me for the break-up. The river had been a road all winter — carts had crossed it, children had skated it, the town had walked all over it the way you walk over any fact you've grown used to. Now that same surface was breaking into continents. Great white plates tilted against each other and reared up, edges grinding, throwing off spray that froze again as it flew. Downstream of the bend the jam was still holding, a rampart of shattered ice piled fifteen feet high, and above the bend the whole accumulated pressure of a hundred miles of thawing river was leaning on it.

Everyone knew what the jam meant. Everyone had known for generations. When it let go — and it always let go — the water would come down the channel like a train, and anything low along the banks would be taken, and the *Persephone* would ride out ahead of it or not at all.

Which meant the berths weren't a luxury. They were arithmetic. Stay, and you spent the flood on high ground with everyone else — or, if you were Elena, whose house sat low by the water, you spent it in a cellar with the river coming down the stairs. Go, and you started over downriver, where the Voss money had already bought a house and a future with no orchard in it at all.

That was the cruelty of it, if you wanted to be precise about the cruelty. Nobody on that mudbank needed the ferry to survive. They needed it to win. And the only person for whom the arithmetic was honest was Elena, whose home would be under six feet of brown water by noon tomorrow, and whose name was on none of the papers.

"Third berth," Aurelio was saying. "Yours. Name on the manifest, passage downriver, and my workshop in the city — the real one, not the shed — and the Nakamura name in the ledger of the company my grandfather kept, restored, in ink, witnessed. You want your grandfather back? I'll give him back to you. All you have to do is hand me what your pocket is hiding and get on the boat, and take the girl with you, because frankly—" and here he lowered his voice, which somehow made it worse— "frankly, clockmaker, she's a complication you can't afford, and I'm offering to solve it for you."

Behind him, his uncle had drifted closer, and I saw the old man's face do something complicated — not objection, exactly. Regret wearing objection's coat.

"And if I don't?" I said.

"Then you stand here," Aurelio said, "with your coal in your pocket, until the boat leaves, and then we discuss what the courts think of an unmarried apprentice keeping another man's ward without papers, because I've spoken to Judge Ferro, and he takes an interest in unregistered minors. Especially ones connected to contested estates."

There it was. He'd said it plainly at last, the thing he'd been circling all winter. Not the deed. Not really. The girl. Because a child tied by blood to the orchard was a claim with a heartbeat, and claims with heartbeats could be kept, and kept things could be traded.

Mara's hand tightened in mine. She hadn't said a word. She had stood through all of it the way she stood through blizzards — absolutely still, absolutely awake, watching everything.

"I'm not a parcel," she said.

It was the first thing she'd said aloud on the mudbank, and it came out level and clear, and several people in the crowd heard it, and several people in the crowd would remember it later, though none of us knew that yet.

Aurelio looked at her for a long moment. "No," he said finally, and something crossed his face that was almost human. "No, you're not. That's rather the problem, isn't it."

* * *

Odile Brac came down the gangplank then, keys in her fist, and clapped her hands twice, and the crowd's murmur dropped away like water finding a drain.

"Lines off in twenty minutes," she announced. "Berth-holders aboard in ten, or your money stays with the river like everybody else's does." Her eye found me across the crowd, and held, and one finger tapped the manifest rolled in her belt. Once. The window is open, the tap said. It will not stay open.

Ten minutes.

I looked at the locket in my mind's eye — I didn't dare look at my actual pocket — and thought about the hour I'd spent on my knees in frozen orchard mud with a broken spade while Mara dug with her bare hands beside me, faster than I did, always faster, until the tin box came up and the locket inside it opened with a click I will hear until I die, and inside the locket, coiled small as a snail, the key: a branch of blossoms cast in brass, sized exactly, precisely, for a lock hidden in the heart of a clock built by a grandfather who had understood, better than anyone in this town, that some doors should only open for the people willing to dig.

The mechanism was ready. The chamber was oiled, the train was true, the sun would strike the tower within the hour. One turn of the branch and the truth would come out of that clock like a bird out of a cage, in front of whoever was standing in the clockroom when it happened.

All I had to decide was who stood there. And who got on the boat.

Elena was still at the edge of the crowd. She had not approached me. She had not asked — not here, not in front of them all, though I knew what she'd said in the tower in the blue dark of February: *Ask me for nothing, Mr. Nakamura, and I will ask you for less.* She was watching the ice come down the channel with her hands folded like a woman at a graveside, and I understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that she had already lost. She knew it. She had known it since the manifests were posted. Her house would flood, her daughter was claimed by a man with lawyers, and the woman who had written *forget the face* on a scrap of orchard paper was going to stand on this mudbank and watch both of those facts become permanent, and she had not asked anyone for so much as a rope.

The uncle caught my eye across the space between us. He inclined his head, slightly — the smallest possible bow — and mouthed something I couldn't hear over the river. But I knew what it was. I had heard it in November over brandy I hadn't drunk: *Whatever you find, boy, weigh it. Weigh everything.*

I weighed it.

Eight minutes.

The ice groaned upstream, a long rising note, and the whole jam shifted, and a cheer went up from the boys on the seawall who had no sense whatsoever, and Odile Brac shouted *five minutes* in a voice that carried over everything, and Mara pulled my sleeve and leaned up to my ear.

"Isandro," she said. "The clock's stopped. Somebody has to wind it."

And I looked down at her — this child who had arrived on my steps with instructions to be forgotten, who had learned the escapement in a winter, who had fixed a broken anchor single-handed while I sat detained in Aurelio's parlor being offered my grandfather's name back like a bone thrown to a dog — and I understood that she had just solved the entire question for me, the way she solved most things: by looking at it sideways, and seeing the part that mattered.

The clock had to be wound. The mechanism had to be opened. And the sun would be striking the tower within the hour.

I put my hand flat on my breast pocket, over the branch of blossoms, and turned to face the water, and the boat, and the family, and the town, and the whole assembled weight of everything that had happened to me since a note on a child's coat taught me that history had been waiting outside my door the entire time.

"Ferrymaster," I called out, loud enough for the crowd, loud enough for the record, "I'd like to talk about that third berth."

Odile Brac rolled the manifest off her belt and licked her thumb, and every face on that mudbank turned toward me like flowers toward a very dangerous sun.

"One name," she said. "One berth. Clockmaker — who is it?"

The river roared. The jam upstream lifted, groaning, a wall of white tilting toward its own collapse. And I opened my mouth to answer.
