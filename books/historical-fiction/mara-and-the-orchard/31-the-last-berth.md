## 31. The Last Berth

The river cracked at dawn the way a liar cracks — all at once, and too late to be useful.

I was in the clockroom when it happened, though I heard it through my boots before I heard it with my ears: a long groan under the town, deep as the master clock's lowest weight, and then a sound like God snapping kindling. The whole tower shivered. Dust came down off the beams in a slow gray snowfall, and Mara — who had slept curled on the settle by the window for three nights running, waiting for exactly this moment — sat up with her hair full of cobwebs and her eyes wide as coins.

"That's it," she said. "That's the sound."

"That's the sound," I agreed.

I had imagined this morning a hundred times during the winter and had never once gotten past it. In my imaginings I was always calm. In my imaginings I said clever things at the correct volume. In my imaginings, the choice had already made itself, quietly, the way good mechanisms make their decisions — one tooth engaging the next, no drama, no splinters.

The river did not care for my imaginings. The river had spent four months pressing its shoulder against the ice and now intended to have done with it before breakfast.

Below us, the town woke like an anthill kicked. Bells that weren't ours began ringing. Dogs took up the announcement. And down at the landing, I knew without looking, the *Persephone* — the ice-breaking ferry, squat and black-hulled and ugly as a stove — would be taking on steam for the season's first run, with three berths aboard and every one of them claimed by the orchard family.

Three berths. One family. Two people who needed them most and held no claim at all.

"Isandro," Mara said. She had come to stand beside me at the window, small hands flat on the sill, watching the smoke rise from the landing. "Which one are you?"

"Which one am I what?"

"Going to pick." She said it simply, the way children say terrible things. "Aurelio says you'll give him the berth because he can hurt you. Elena says she doesn't want it if it costs anything. They both asked me what you'd do, which is strange, because I'm the only one who knows you."

I looked down at her. Eight years old, or thereabouts — we'd settled on eight, since no document in the town could agree — wearing the same coat someone had pinned a note to in November. *Forget the face.* I had failed at the note's single instruction within the hour, and had been failing at it comprehensively ever since.

"What do I do?" I asked.

"You stay," she said, with the confidence of someone reciting arithmetic. "You're a clockmaker. Clockmakers don't leave clocks."

* * *

Getting down to the landing took longer than it should have, because half the town was climbing up while the other half climbed down, everyone wanting either to see the ice break or to be seen having seen it. Old Mrs. Calloway caught my sleeve on the stairs and pressed a boiled sweet into my hand as if I were departing for the war. Perhaps I was. The sweets were terrible; I put it in my pocket anyway, next to the locket.

The locket. I should say about the locket what I could not yet say aloud: that it hung against my ribs all morning like a second heartbeat, a small brass branch of blossoms dug out of frozen ground two weeks before, warm from my body and cold with everything it meant. Aurelio knew I had it. Elena suspected. The whole orchard inheritance — the true deed, the grandfather's hidden train, thirty years of quarrel — turned on a key shaped like a flowering limb, and I carried it to the water's edge the way you carry a lit lamp through a powder store.

The *Persephone* lay against the dock with her bow iron plating gleaming wet, steam standing off her stacks in white columns. The ice below her was already moving — great plates of it tilting, grinding, opening seams of black water that breathed fog into the cold air. The whole bank of the town stood along the railings, breath steaming, hats gripped against the wind off the water.

And at the gangplank, arranged like a tableau nobody had thought to paint: Aurelio Voss in his good coat, his wife beside him, his men behind him; Uncle Teodor a little apart, holding his hat, wearing the expression of a man who has bet on every horse in the race and is therefore certain to lose; and Elena, alone, at the very edge of the light, her hands folded, her face doing the work of being calm.

The purser stood at the plank with his ledger open. Three berths. He checked names off with the serene indifference of a man who had been paid not to think about any of it.

"Nakamura!" Aurelio saw me and his voice carried beautifully across the crowd; he had a voice built for auction houses and courtrooms. "There he is. The clockmaker comes to see justice done."

"I come to see the ice break," I said. "It seems to manage that on its own."

He laughed, and some of the crowd laughed with him, because crowds will laugh at anything said loudly enough near water. Then he came toward me along the dock, and the crowd made a lane, because crowds also do that, and I understood that whatever happened in the next ten minutes was going to happen in front of every soul in this town who had ever whispered about my grandfather's name or Mara's coat or the stopped clock on the hill.

Good, some cold clear part of me said. Let them watch. A mechanism works best in daylight.

* * *

Aurelio stopped close enough that I could smell the bay rum on him. Up close his certainty had hairline fractures in it, the way porcelain does before it goes. He had spent a winter posting watchers on my tower and buying my silence and threatening my ward, and none of it had produced the deed, and this morning — with the ice going out and the berths sailing and his birthright locked in a tower he couldn't crack — this morning was the last lever he had.

"The berth," he said, quieter now, for me alone, though everyone strained to hear anyway. "You give me the berth, and we conclude our business before witnesses. You hand over what you found in the orchard ground, I sign the paper restoring your family's name — the full restoration, workshop, standing, the lot — and Nakamuras walk this town with their heads up again. Your grandfather's debt paid in one signature. Isn't that what you wanted? Isn't that why you came upriver?"

It is a strange thing, being offered exactly what you came for. It's like a lock presenting you with the wrong key cut perfectly — beautiful, convincing, and it won't turn, because the mechanism inside knows its own truth.

"My grandfather," I said, "worked your father's orchard for nineteen years and was struck from the ferry manifests like a smudge of ink. My grandmother died believing he'd abandoned her, because a page was torn out. You're offering to write his name back in exchange for a piece of paper that lets you sell the trees over the heads of everyone standing on this dock."

"I'm offering you a place in the world."

"You're offering me a place in *your* world." I took the locket out of my pocket and let it sit in my open palm between us, brass catching the early light, the little blossoms sharp and bright. The crowd went quiet in that way crowds do when something valuable appears — the hush of a hundred people deciding simultaneously whether to be thieves. "This isn't yours, Aurelio. It was never going to be yours. It was buried under a dog's grave by a woman who trusted her son with it, and you've spent a winter proving her right."

His face did something complicated. For one unguarded instant I saw the boy he must have been, standing at his grandfather's knee in that same orchard, and then the ledger closed over it, the way ledgers do.

"You'll lose everything," he said. "The workshop offer dies today. The ward — the girl — I'll have the courts place her by Friday. She'll be gone from that tower before the month is out, and you'll be alone in it with a broken clock and a name nobody wants."

"There," I said, "you're mistaken on nearly every count."

I turned from him before he could answer, because a man arguing with a wall should stop while the wall still respects him, and I raised my voice so it carried the way his had — not with money behind it but with something better, something free.

"Miss Voss," I called, and the crowd turned as one animal, and Elena lifted her head. "Elena. Will you come here, please?"

She crossed the dock slowly. She had dressed plainly, I noticed — traveling clothes, nothing of the great house about her — as though some part of her had decided long ago how this morning would end and had dressed for the version where she lost. That is the thing about Elena Voss that I understood finally, fully, standing there with the river roaring and the whole town watching: she had given away a daughter, a name, a home, and a claim, and had asked for nothing back except that the child live. Even now, even at the gangplank, she came toward me with her hands empty.

"Elena," I said, when she reached me, and then found I had prepared no speech, which was unlike me and probably for the best. "The purser's ledger shows three berths claimed by your family. Yours was sold by your brother in December, along with your daughter's."

"I know what my brother sold."

"I've bought it back." I said it plainly, and heard the murmur go through the crowd like wind through wheat. "Not with money. With the only coin I had, which was stubbornness. The berth is yours. Not as a favor. Not as charity. As payment — owed to you since November, by me, for a note I should have burned and a door I should have knocked on sooner. You wrote *forget the face.* I'm telling you before the whole town: it cannot be done. I've tried since winter. So instead, take the face with you. Take her mother's story downriver. Take the locket—" and here I closed her fingers around it, and felt her whole arm tremble, "—and tell it properly, someday, to the person it belongs to. When she's grown. If she asks. Not before."

Elena looked down at the locket in her fist. When she looked up her composure had gone the way of the ice — not gradually, but all at once, in great groaning sheets.

"She stays with you," she said. It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway.

"She stays with me. I'm apprenticed to her, actually. It's a long arrangement. Decades, likely."

Someone in the crowd laughed — Mrs. Calloway, I'd swear it — and the laugh broke something open in the morning, and then the purser was calling berths, and Aurelio was shouting something about courts and wards and theft, and the *Persephone*'s whistle tore the sky open like a kettle answering all of it at once.

* * *

What happened next happened quickly and is preserved in the town's memory in about nine different versions, none accurate, all flattering to somebody.

Aurelio tried to physically bar the gangplank. His men did not help him; I watched them find sudden fascination with the rigging. Teodor, of all people, took his nephew by the elbow and spoke low and urgent, and whatever he said — I have always wanted to know — made Aurelio step back with his jaw working like a man chewing glass. The purser checked his ledger, found Elena's name restored in ink that was definitely not the ink of December, and waved her aboard with the magnificent blindness of officialdom everywhere.

Elena paused at the top of the plank. She didn't look at Aurelio. She looked up the hill, at the tower, where a small figure stood in the clockroom window — Mara had refused to come down; she said goodbyes were for people leaving, and she wasn't the one leaving — and Elena lifted the locket high, brass blazing, once, twice, like a signal lamp.

Then she was aboard, and the lines came off, and the *Persephone* backed water into the black seam the river had opened for her.

Aurelio stood on the dock and watched his sister diminish. I stood beside him, because someone ought to, and because cruelty is cheapest when practiced at a distance.

"You understand what you've cost yourself," he said at last. He didn't look at me. "The workshop. The name. Everything I offered."

"I understand what I've kept," I said. "It's a shorter list. I find I prefer it."

"They'll remember this," he said, and there was something almost like wonder in it, the wonder of a man discovering the floor was never where he'd built it. "The whole town. They'll remember you chose her over me."

"No," I said. "They'll remember you offered to sell the orchard and I wouldn't hold the ladder. Those are different memories. Only one of them survives telling."

The ferry cleared the point. The ice groaned, split, and ran.

And that is when Mara came down the hill.

* * *

She came at a dead run, coat flying, bundle — her little packed bundle, the one I'd pretended not to see for three days — still tied and slung on her back, because apparently she had planned to present herself at the gangplank as a solution to everyone's problem, the way she presented herself as a solution to most problems, and thank every god watching that she'd arrived four minutes too late. She skidded to a stop at the dock's edge beside me, chest heaving, staring at the wake.

"I missed it," she said.

"You missed a great deal of shouting and one very ugly coat."

"Did you do it? Did you choose?"

"I gave the berth to your mother."

Mara was quiet for a moment. The river filled the silence, running free now, chunks of ice riding the current like white boats with no passengers.

"Good," she said finally. "That's the right answer. I worked it out last night." She glanced up at me, sideways, sly. "I stayed in the clockroom ready to leave so nobody would have to choose. But then I thought — if Isandro gives the berth to Mama, then he's already chosen *me*, haven't you? Because you could have gone. Any of the three berths. And you stayed before anyone asked you to."

The child had unpicked my entire moral architecture in one night, the way she unpicked escapements, without breaking anything, and laid the parts out on the bench in order. I had no reply. The crowd was still watching us — the clockmaker and the clock's child — and I found I didn't mind. Let them look. History, I had come to believe, was not something that happened elsewhere; it was something that happened in front of people, and was remembered correctly only if somebody had the nerve to do it in daylight.

"Aurelio," I said then, turning, raising my voice one more time, because there was one more thing and I meant to say it before witnesses, before the water, before whatever gods kept ledgers of their own. "You offered me my family's name for the price of a town. Here is my answer, and let everyone hear it: the Nakamura name was never yours to sell. It was torn out of your father's books by men afraid of what my grandfather knew, and it will be written back by the clock, not by you. As for the deed in the tower—" I touched my breast pocket, where the locket had been, and found it gone, given away, and smiled at the lightness. "As for the deed, you may come and watch it open with everyone else. Tomorrow, when the sun strikes the tower at mid-morning. Bring your lawyer. Bring your grief. Admission is free."

The crowd made a sound I hadn't heard before — not a cheer, not a gasp, but a low collective intake, the sound of a town inhaling a new story it intended to keep.

Aurelio stared at me for a long moment. Whatever moved behind his face, he kept it there, behind the porcelain. Then he straightened his coat, gathered his wife on his arm as though she were a document, and walked up the dock without a word, his men trailing after him like the tail of a kite coming down.

Mara slipped her hand into mine. Her fingers were cold and certain.

"The sun strikes the tower at quarter past ten tomorrow," she said. "I checked with the clock."

"Of course you did."

"Isandro?" She was looking at the river, where the ferry was a dark comma on all that bright moving water, carrying her mother and her mother's story down toward the sea. "When the chamber opens tomorrow. What if the deed says something we don't expect?"

I thought about her grandfather, the builder, who had hidden a town's future inside a machine designed to tell the truth twice a day, every day, forever.

"Deeds are like clocks," I said. "However strange the mechanism looks from outside, what it was built to do is always written somewhere plain, for whoever's patient enough to read it. Come on. We've a lock to oil and a keyhole to clean, and I want every gear in that tower bright as the morning when the sun finds us."

Above the town, on the hill, the observatory stood waiting with its stopped face turned east — and inside it, behind the dummy gears and the lying train, something old shifted in its sleep, as if it had heard its name called at last.

We started up the hill together, into the light.
