## 32. The Mechanism Opens

*Isandro*

I did not sleep the night before the ice broke. This will surprise no one who has ever stood in a clockroom at three in the morning holding a locket shaped like an orchard branch and waiting for a sun that refuses to hurry.

The tower was cold in that particular way towers are cold — not merely cold but *architecturally* cold, cold with intent, as though the building itself had opinions about what we were about to do. I had lit every lamp I owned and two I'd borrowed from the ferry office, and still the great dark bulk of the master clock sat above me like a patient animal, all its gears stilled, its hidden train waiting behind a lock that had not turned in thirty years.

Mara sat on the winding stool with her legs crossed, the locket in her lap, turning it over and over. She had refused to sleep too. We had made a treaty of our sleeplessness somewhere around midnight, an unspoken agreement that if neither of us mentioned how tired we were, neither of us would have to admit to being afraid.

"Isandro," she said, around four o'clock, "what if it doesn't open?"

"Then I will take it apart piece by piece until it does."

"What if it opens wrong?"

I considered this. "Clocks don't open wrong. They open or they don't. It's people who do things wrong." I wiped my hands on my apron for the fortieth time. "Your grandfather built honest machinery. Whatever else he hid in it, he didn't hide dishonesty."

She nodded, satisfied by this in the way children are satisfied by answers that sound like laws of physics. Then, after a while: "What if Aurelio comes up here before the sun does?"

"He won't. The town is on the banks watching the ice. Even Aurelio can't be in two places at once, and his whole claim depends on being seen to own the orchard when the deed surfaces. He'll be down there, front row, hat off, looking magnanimous."

"And Elena?"

"Elena will be where she always is," I said, and did not finish the sentence, because finishing it meant admitting that by sundown she would either be on the last ferry going downriver or standing beside us in this room, and both possibilities hurt in different places.

The lamps burned low. Somewhere below us the river groaned — a long, tectonic complaint, the sound of winter giving up its argument. I had heard it all night through the stone, that slow grinding, like a giant shifting in bed. The old ferrymen said you could set your watch by the break once the groaning started, and they were nearly right: the ice would go at first light, when the sun came over the eastern ridge and struck the river full length.

Which meant the sun would strike the tower at nearly the same moment.

I had worked this out weeks ago, back when the mechanism was still a mystery instead of a locked door with one key. There was a reason the grandfather had built the chamber where he had built it, high in the clock's core, behind the dummy train — and there was a reason, I was now almost certain, that the keyhole was not a keyhole at all but a socket, angled precisely toward the east window. The branch-shaped locket was not merely a key. It was a *gnomon*. At the right hour, on the right day, with the spring sun falling through the glass, the shadow of the mechanism's own gnomon-pin would fall across the socket and show the angle of insertion. Any other day, any other light, and the key went in crooked and the lock stayed shut forever.

He hadn't just hidden the deed. He had hidden it inside the town's own calendar, inside the turning year itself. You could not steal the orchard without stealing the seasons.

"I wish my mother were here," Mara said quietly, near dawn. Not crying. Just saying it, the way you set a heavy thing down on a table to rest your arms.

"She is here," I said, which was sentimental nonsense, and also true, and I let it stand.

* * *

*Mara*

The sun came into the room like it had been invited.

That is what I remember most — not the sound the lock made, not Isandro's hands shaking (though they shook; I saw), but the way the light crossed the floorboards all at once, gold and hard, the way it does on the first warm morning when winter has finally been beaten. It came through the east window and climbed the side of the clock, and Isandro said, "Now," in a voice like someone stepping off a ledge, and I climbed up onto the platform beside him with the locket held out flat on my palm.

The pin above the socket cast its little shadow across the brass, exactly the way Isandro had promised it would, and the shadow pointed to the notch on the branch, and Isandro looked at me.

"You found her," he said. "You dug her out of frozen ground. It should be your hand."

My hand was very steady. That surprised me. Inside I was all birds and thunder, but my hand was steady, and later Isandro told me that was the Nakamura in me, or maybe the orchard blood, or maybe just a girl who had learned early that trembling wastes time.

I fitted the locket-branch into the socket the way the shadow showed.

It did not turn.

For one horrible breath nothing happened at all, and I felt the whole morning tilt under me — Aurelio's men on the banks, the ferry loading, Elena waiting — everything balanced on a small brass branch that would not move.

"It needs weight," Isandro said suddenly. "It's a lever. It needs—" He pressed his thumb on top of mine, gently, adding only a few ounces, and said, "Together, then," and we pushed.

Something deep inside the clock went *clunk* — a sound I felt in my teeth, in the floor, in the soles of my feet, a sound like a chest being unlocked from within. And then the gears began.

Not the big public gears. The hidden ones, the ones Isandro had spent a winter calling useless, the ones that had done nothing but sit in the train like stones in bread. They woke all at once, and the wall of the case swung outward on a hinge I had never seen because it had never existed until now, and there was a chamber no bigger than a bread box, lined in green felt gone grey with years, and in it a tin box, and in the box —

Papers. A folded deed with a wax seal like a dropped plum. And beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, a photograph of a woman holding a baby under a blossoming tree, and a dog sitting at her feet with his head tipped, waiting for something wonderful.

"That's her," I said. My voice came out very small. "That's my mother. And that's—" I couldn't say the dog's name because I didn't know it, but I knew him anyway, the way you know a thing out of a dream.

Isandro put his hand on my shoulder and said nothing, which was the right thing to say.

Down below, faint through the stone, the river gave a crack like the sky breaking, and then a long rolling roar, and Isandro said, "The ice is going. They'll be coming to the tower now. All of them." He lifted the tin box. "So we had better read it aloud before they get here, and read it *properly*, where everyone can hear."

* * *

*Isandro*

There is a bell deck above the clockroom, and a balustrade, and from the balustrade you can see the whole bend of the river and half the town besides. By seven o'clock the banks were black with people — I could see them from the stairwell, a crowd pulled tight along the waterline where the ferry sat with steam rising off her boiler, and beyond them the ice was going in earnest now, great plates tilting and shattering, the river shrugging off winter like a coat worn too long.

Aurelio was down there. I could pick out his grey coat even at that distance, planted at the foot of the gangplank like a man who owns the plank and intends for everyone to know it. Beside him his men. And apart from the family cluster, standing alone with her back to the wind, Elena — who had come to the banks after all, because she had promised Mara she would see the break, and because she had asked me yesterday for a berth she believed she would not receive, and had asked it so plainly that it had rearranged something in my chest and left it rearranged.

I carried the tin box up the outside stairs to the bell deck. Mara carried the photograph. I had offered to carry both; she had declined with a look that ended the discussion.

The crowd heard us before it saw us. A clocktower has a voice even when its bells are silent — footsteps on iron stairs carry — and heads began to turn upward, and a murmur ran along the bank like wind through wheat. By the time we reached the rail, several hundred faces were aimed at us, and among them I found the ones I needed: Aurelio, pale with fury beneath his composure; the widowed daughter's brother-in-law squinting; old Mrs. Voss from the dry goods store, who had sold my grandfather flour forty years ago and pretended since then not to know his name.

I am not a public man. Clockmakers are hired precisely because they say little and mean it. But I had spent a winter learning what silence costs when it belongs to other people's stories, and I had resolved, somewhere between the sabotage and the digging, that this morning there would be no more quiet erasures.

"Ladies and gentlemen!" My voice cracked on the first word and steadied on the second. "You know me as the apprentice who came to fix your clock. Some of you know me as a Nakamura, and have known that longer than you've admitted." A ripple. Old Mrs. Voss's chin came up. "This morning your clock opened. What came out of it belongs to all of you, so I'm going to read it where everyone can hear, and then no one — not the family, not the town, not me — gets to pretend it says anything other than what it says."

I unfolded the deed. My hands were steady. I thought of Mara and was glad of where she had learned it from.

"The instrument reads: 'I, Tomás Herrera, orchardist of this town, being sound of mind and tired of quarrels, do hereby convey the whole of the hillside orchard, its trees, its land, and its water rights, not to my son, nor to my daughter, nor to any heir of my line —'" I paused, because Aurelio's face at that moment was worth pausing over, "'— but to the town entire, in perpetual trust, that no child born under these trees shall ever be turned from them. The orchard is to be kept by a keeper appointed by the townsfolk, and the first such keeper I name is the child of my daughter, should she live, or failing her, the child of the clock — whoever keeps the master clock wound and true.'"

The murmur became a roar. Down on the bank I saw men turning to look at one another, at the family, at the ferry, at the tower. I saw the eldest son's mouth moving, arguing with a world that had stopped listening.

And I read the last line, the one that mattered most to two people in particular:

"'And let it be recorded that the name Nakamura, which worked these lands and was struck from their story, is restored to the roll of keepers, first among them, for the debt of the erased is owed before all others.'"

Mrs. Voss was crying. So, to my lasting astonishment, was I.

* * *

*Mara*

While everyone was shouting, I held up the photograph.

I don't know why I did it. Maybe because a deed is words and my mother was a face, and I wanted them to know the words had a face. I held it high over my head the way Isandro holds a lamp up into the clockworks, and the spring sun caught it, and the crowd got quiet again, all those faces tipping back, and somebody near the front called out, "Who is she?" and I said, "My mother," and my voice carried better than I expected, the way voices do off stone and water.

"The keeper," Isandro said beside me, leaning to the rail, "is named in the deed. The child of the clock. The townsfolk appoint her." He looked at me. "Mara. Do you accept?"

I looked down at all of them — the whole town, and the broken river running free behind them for the first time in months, brown and fast and loud, carrying the winter away in pieces. I looked at Elena, whose face was doing several things at once. I looked at Aurelio, who had stopped arguing and gone very still, the way a man goes still when he finally hears the exact size of what he has lost.

"I accept," I said.

And then, because I had earned the right to one more thing that morning, I took the locket-branch from my pocket and held it up too, next to the photograph, branch and mother together in the sun.

"This is hers," I said. "It opened the clock. It stays with the clock. That's the rule now. I just made it up, but rules have to start somewhere."

Somebody laughed — Mrs. Voss, I think, bless her — and then everybody laughed, the good kind, the kind that means a thing has become true by agreement, and the bells above us caught the wind and hummed a note they hadn't sung all winter, and below us the last great slab of ice went under the bridge with a sound like applause.

* * *

*Isandro*

Afterward — afterward being perhaps ten minutes later, when the crowd had begun to sort itself into committees, because nothing makes a town form committees faster than sudden ownership of an orchard — the moment arrived that I had been dreading since the berths were sold.

Aurelio climbed the tower stairs.

He came up slowly, hat in hand now, the magnanimity abandoned somewhere on the landing. Behind him, uninvited, came Elena, who had started up the moment he did, as though she intended to be present for whatever happened next whether anyone wished it or not.

"So," Aurelio said. "Read to the whole town. Every word. You understand what you've done."

"I understand it completely," I said. "It's the first thing I've understood all winter."

"The orchard was ours—"

"It was never yours. It was your father's, and he chose otherwise, deliberately, with a seal and a witness, and he chose *against* you specifically. You may hate the wording. You may not rewrite it. I've had enough rewriting done to names I love."

His jaw worked. Below us, the ferry whistle blew — the boarding call, short and final, the last berth of the season waiting on the last person entitled to it. He glanced at the sound, and I watched him make his arithmetic: the deed lost, the town against him, and one thing left in his grasp, one card still in his hand.

"The berth," he said. "It's mine. I paid for it. And I'll tell you now, clockmaker, if that child sets foot off this hill, my lawyers—"

"She isn't going anywhere," said Elena.

She had come forward to the rail, and the wind took her hair sideways, and she looked at her brother with an expression I had seen once before — on the night she confessed to writing the note, when guilt and love finished their long war and love won. She reached past him, without touching him, and took Mara's hand.

"You're not taking anyone," she said. "Not today. Not ever. Go catch your boat, Aurelio. It's leaving, and it isn't waiting, and neither am I anymore."

For a long moment nobody moved except the river. Then Aurelio put his hat on — a small defeated gesture, straightening the brim twice — and went down the stairs without another word, and we listened to his boots fade, and then, minutes later, we watched from the rail as he boarded the ferry alone, first aboard, last ashore, a man claiming the one thing left that he had actually paid for.

Elena stood between us, Mara's hand in hers, watching the gangplank swing free.

"I have until the whistle blows twice," she said quietly. Her voice was steady. Her eyes were not. "Isandro. You know what I asked you."

"I know," I said. "And I've made my answer. But not here, and not shouted over a crowd. Come inside. Both of you." I looked at the clockroom door, at the open chamber where the green felt lay bare, at the whole winter waiting to be set in motion again. "There's a clock in there that hasn't run since November, and a girl who can fix an escapement better than I can, and a town downstairs that's about to discover it owns four hundred apple trees. Before anyone boards anything, I intend to start the master clock — with both of you standing beside me."

Below, the whistle blew a second time, long and low, and the crowd on the banks surged toward the gangplank, and Elena drew one breath and held it, and I put my hand on the clockroom door.

"Come," I said. "We're going to wake it up."
