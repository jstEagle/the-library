## 33. The Deed's True Wording

There is a particular silence that falls over a crowd when it realizes the paper being read aloud concerns every one of them. I had heard it once before, years ago, when my master read the terms of an estate will to three squabbling cousins in a parlor that smelled of camphor and grievance. But that silence had been small — four walls' worth of silence. This one was the size of a town.

I stood at the clockroom window with the deed unrolled before me on the sill, weighted at both corners by two brass gear-cases, because the wind off the thawing river was determined to have its say first. Below me, the banks were black with people. The ice was going out of the river in groaning white plates, and nobody was watching the ice anymore. They were watching me. They were watching, most especially, the small girl standing beside me on a stool so she could see over the sill.

Mara's hands were folded in front of her like a woman twice her age attending a funeral. She had insisted on wearing her coat — the same coat she'd been abandoned in, the one Elena had wept over — and she had pinned the note to its lapel, the note that said *forget the face*, turned now so the words faced inward against her chest. Hidden. She hadn't explained why she'd done it. She didn't have to. Some documents you carry so you can remember what they got wrong.

"Read it," someone called from below. It might have been the baker. It might have been God. In this town those voices had begun to sound alike by late winter.

"I'm reading it," I said, which was true, in the sense that I had been staring at the same line for the better part of a minute without letting any of it pass through me into speech.

The trouble was that I already knew what the paper said. I had known since the chamber opened — since Mara's hand, steady as tide-work, had fitted the locket-branch into the lock while the spring sun came through the east window exactly the way her grandfather must have planned it forty years ago, because clockmakers are the only men alive who genuinely believe the future can be scheduled. The chamber had breathed out air older than I was, smelling of oil and cedar, and there inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the deed. I had read it by lamplight the night before, twice, then a third time with Mara asleep against my shoulder, checking each clause the way you check the beat of a watch you suspect of lying.

But knowing a thing privately and saying it to a river town from a tower window are different trades entirely. One is craft. The other is confession.

* * *

Let me go back an hour, because the hour matters.

The mechanism had opened at seven minutes past eight in the morning, and by half past, the news had gone through the town the way cold goes through a wet boot. By nine, the observatory steps held more people than they had ever been built for, and Aurelio Vasseur stood at the bottom of them with his hat crushed in one fist and his face arranged into something he probably believed looked like dignity.

He had come up the hill fast. I'll grant him that. When word reached him that the deed was found — *found*, not surrendered — he had abandoned his watchers at their posts, abandoned his berth on the ferry, abandoned whatever strategy he'd spent the winter constructing, and climbed the hill in a coat done up wrong, one button off, so that the whole garment sat twisted on him like his argument with the world. That image has stayed with me longer than I expected. There is no portrait of ruin quite so honest as a rich man dressed crooked.

"Isandro." He said my name the way a creditor says it. "That document belongs to my family."

"That," I said, "is the second thing you've been wrong about all year, and the morning is young."

"You're an apprentice. A foreigner's grandson playing with gears above your station—"

"Aurelio." I came down two steps so we stood level, or nearly; he had the height on me but I had the high ground, which in a town built on a hill counts for more. "In about ten minutes, everyone here is going to hear what that paper says, read aloud, in full daylight, with the ferry captain and the magistrate and your own sister standing witness. You can be standing here when it happens, or you can be elsewhere. Those are the options. There is no third door."

His jaw worked. Behind him, Elena stood very still near the orchard gate, her hands white around a handkerchief she wasn't using. She had asked me yesterday for the last berth — for herself or Mara, whichever I judged — and I had given it to her at dawn, and she had boarded the ferry, and then the ice had cracked early, and then the mechanism had opened, and the whole tidy arrangement of departures had dissolved like the river itself. The ferry was holding at its mooring. The captain had agreed to wait until noon and not one minute past, because ice waits for no man's family drama, and captains are the second-greatest skeptics alive after clockmakers.

Aurelio chose to stay. Of course he did. Men like him cannot walk away from a room where something is being counted, in case the counting comes out in their favor.

* * *

So: the window, the sill, the weights on the corners, the crowd hushed as a held breath. Mara beside me on her stool. And me, beginning at the top, in the voice I use for difficult customers, which is to say clearly and without mercy.

"'This instrument,'" I read, "'made under the hand of Tomás Vasseur, master of the orchard lands and builder of the great clock, being the true and final instrument concerning the said lands, supersedes and makes void all instruments signed before or after it.'"

Below me, a murmur went through the crowd like wind through wheat. *Supersedes.* Somewhere in that murmur was every lawyer in three counties recalculating his fees.

I read on. "'The orchard, its trees, its ground, its water rights, and its harvests, are given not to any one heir of my blood, nor divided among them, but held entire, in trust, for the town that stands below it — for every soul who has picked its fruit, milled its cider, walked its rows, and every soul yet to come.'"

The silence deepened. Not the shocked silence — that had already happened, back when I read the word *supersedes*. This was the slower silence of people realizing that a thing they had fought over, gossiped over, taken sides over all winter, had never been theirs to fight over at all. The inheritance war, revealed as a war over an empty chest. I watched the realization move down the hill from face to face, and I confess — wry narrator, dispassionate observer, all that — I felt something warm settle in my chest that had nothing to do with triumph and everything to do with justice arriving on schedule, which is the only kind of arrival a clockmaker truly respects.

Beside me, Mara tugged my sleeve. "Keep reading," she whispered. "The part next."

She knew which part was next. Of course she did. She'd made me read it to her four times the night before, and each time she had put her finger on the same passage and waited, the way she waited beside the escapement when she wanted to hear the tick and the tock separately, before they became time.

I put my finger where hers had been.

"'And the keeping of this trust,'" I read, and my voice did something I didn't authorize it to do — it roughened, just at the edges, and I let it, because some things should be heard to cost the speaker something — "'shall belong to the child of the clock. Whoever winds it, whoever tends it, whoever keeps the hours honest above the town: that one is keeper of the orchard, though they hold no blood-right to it, for I have learned that blood is a poor clerk and keeps bad books. Let the orchard be kept instead by the ones who keep faith with the work.'"

I stopped. The crowd had gone absolutely still. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"And there's more," I said. "One line more. The last line. Written in a smaller hand, added later — I'd stake my tools it was added the night before he died, because the ink is younger than the rest and the penmanship is worse, and dying men write in a hurry."

From below, Elena's voice, thin across the distance: "Read it, Isandro. Please."

"'And to the apprentice who finds this — for it will take an apprentice, no master would stoop to look where I have hidden it —'" a ragged little laugh moved through the crowd at that, and I had to press my lips together, because Tomás Vasseur had been dead eleven years and had still managed to insult me accurately — "'let it be written that the name Nakamura worked these orchard lands in the years before the fire, and worked them well, and was struck from the ledgers by men ashamed of what they'd done.
