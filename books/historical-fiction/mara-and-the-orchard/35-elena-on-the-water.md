## 35. Elena on the Water

The river does not care what you leave behind. That is its whole character, really — a long gray indifference running north to south, carrying whatever it pleases and dropping the rest on the mud. I had lived beside it my whole life and never once considered that one day it would be asked to carry me away from everything I had chosen poorly.

The ferry was smaller than I remembered. Everything from childhood is smaller when you return to it, but the *Marta B.* had shrunk without my permission, her deck gone soft underfoot, her rail painted so many times the paint had become its own geological record. Three berths, the manifests said. Room for three souls before the ice went out, and no more, because the ferryman was a careful man with a careful boat and the river between its banks was still half knife.

I was one of the three. I had not asked for it. That is the part I keep turning over like a stone in my pocket — I did not ask, and Isandro Nakamura gave it to me anyway, and I have spent the entire morning trying to decide whether that was mercy or judgment or some third thing I do not have a name for.

* * *

We cast off at first light, when the ice was still holding in the middle channel and the ferryman swore he could thread the open lead along the eastern shore if God was willing and the current obliging. The whole town came down to watch. Half of them to wave, I suspect, and half to confirm with their own eyes that the widow Vasseur was actually leaving, actually giving up the orchard question, actually going quietly after all those years of going loudly.

I stood at the stern rail because I could not bear to stand anywhere else.

The tower caught the early sun first, the way it always did. It stood up over the rooftops pale and patient, and below it the town lay in its winter grime with thaw-water running silver down every gutter, and above the town, terraced up the hillside like seats in an empty theater, the orchard — bare now, black-limbed, waiting, ours and not ours, which is to say everyone's, which is what my father wanted and what none of us children understood until it was too late to matter.

And in the clockroom window, small enough to cover with my thumbnail, a figure.

She had climbed up there before dawn. I know because Isandro told me she would, told me the night before in the clockroom while the deed lay open on the workbench between us like a wound finally allowed to air. *She'll want to see you off,* he said. *She'll be at the window. Don't make it harder by pretending you won't look.*

He was twenty-two years old and he had already learned to talk to grieving women as though we were escapements that only needed handling correctly. I resented it for exactly four seconds and then I was grateful, because he was right. She was at the window. And I looked.

She raised one hand. Not waving, exactly. More like showing me something — an open palm, fingers spread, the way you'd show an adult that your hand was empty. *Look, nothing hidden.* Or maybe the opposite: *look what I'm holding, even though you can't see it.*

I raised mine the same way, and we stayed like that across forty yards of cold air, two open hands, until the current took us around the bend and the tower slid behind the bluff and I let myself cry in the way I have practiced since I was nine years old, silently, facing forward, so that no one would think to comfort me.

* * *

You will want to know about my mother's locket. Everyone downriver will want to know about it, eventually — the story has already grown a second head in the telling, and by the time the ferry reaches Marlowe's Landing they'll have me smuggling the deed out in my bodice and dueling Aurelio on the ice. So let me set it down properly, here, where the only witnesses are the river and a ferryman pretending not to listen.

The locket was brass, worn to the color of old honey. On its face, a branch of blossoms — five flowers, my mother said, one for each row her father planted before she was born, though I counted six once and she corrected me without looking up from her mending, which tells you how much of our family history was arithmetic performed under duress. Inside were two portraits too faded to identify and, folded into the backing, a slip of paper my mother called *the promise* and never showed anyone, not even Father, especially not Father.

She wore it every day of the picking seasons. She wore it the autumn she died, walking the rows in the first frost with her coat unbuttoned because she said the trees liked to see her face. When she was buried we buried the dog with her — the old shepherd, Hansel, who had refused all week to eat and who lay down on her grave the day they finished it and had to be carried home — and Aurelio, who was fourteen and already keeping accounts of everything, complained that burying a good collar and chain was wasteful.

The locket was not buried. The locket went into a drawer. Drawers are where our family keeps the things it cannot look at, and we have excellent drawers.

It was Mara who remembered. Of course it was. A child of six summers who had been abandoned on an observatory step with instructions to forget her own face, and she was the one who walked us through the frozen orchard by moonlight to the grave beneath the first tree and said, in the flat certain voice she uses when she is frightened and refusing to show it: *Mother said the dog keeps it. Mother said dogs keep things better than people.* She dug with a broken oar handle. I dug with my hands. Isandro dug with his grandfather's spade, which he had brought upriver in his tool roll like a relic, and perhaps it was one.

We found the locket at moonset. We found the branch-key inside it. And three days later — I will get to the three days later, the berth, the choice, the thing I owe that clockmaker that I cannot repay in this life — three days later the sun struck the tower and the locket turned in the lock and the clock gave up the secret it had been keeping for thirty years, which was my father's true handwriting, which said:

*To the town, in trust, kept by the child of the clock.*

Not to Aurelio. Not to me. Not to the uncle with his soft loans and softer threats. To everyone. To the pickers and the millers and the ferrymen and the families who had bought our apples at fair prices for sixty years while my brother priced them out of their own valley.

My father hid the deed in a clock because he knew his children. That is the terrible joke of it. He loved us, I believe he loved us, but he knew us — knew Aurelio would sell the rows for timber and me for respectability and the uncle for anything with interest attached — and so he built a vault out of gears and patience and put the key where only someone who loved the trees more than the trees' fruit would ever think to dig.

A child. He trusted a child he never met. He trusted the granddaughter of a man whose name the family scratched out of the ferry ledgers, and I did not learn until the reading that the girl sleeping on the observatory steps was bound by blood to the very name our family erased.

There is a symmetry there that I refuse to find beautiful. I find it just.

* * *

The ferryman's name is Odo Brandt and he is sixty if he is a day and he has been making conversation at me since the second bend.

"You're the one," he says. Not a question. "The sister."

"I'm the one."

"The one that left the child on the steps." He says it mildly, steering with one hand, watching the ice shelf on the western bank the way other men watch weather. "Whole town's talking about it. How you wrote the note. *Forget the face.*" He snorts. "Cold words. Cold as anything."

"Yes."

"And then the same woman gives up the last warm berth on this tub so the child could—" He stops himself, which is more courtesy than I expected, and tries again. "So the child stays."

"She was never going on this boat." I have thought this through so many times the thought is smooth as river stone. "If I took her downriver, I'd be doing exactly what my brother wanted — taking her somewhere she could be made useful, made leverage, made *his*. Here she has a clock, a craft, a town that owes her an orchard, and a man who winds things." I look at Odo. "Would you put a plant back in a pot it had already outgrown?"

Odo considers this with the gravity of a man considering cargo weights. "Depends who broke the first pot," he says, and lets it lie.

But the truth, the truth I will tell the river and eventually, God willing, tell her face to face when she is grown and can weigh it herself: I did not leave her to protect the orchard. I have heard the town's version — noble Elena, sacrificing the girl to save the land — and it flatters me and it is false. I left her because Aurelio told me over supper, in front of the uncle, in that reasonable voice he uses for cruelty, that a child with a claim was worth more than a child with a home. That a ward could be kept. That wards could be married off, or signed away, or simply *held* until the deed surfaced and then traded like any other asset.

He was talking about his own niece the way he talked about apple futures. And I sat there and ate the supper and said nothing, because saying something meant the fight started that night instead of the night I was ready for, and I chose ready over righteous. I have chosen ready over righteous my whole life. It is why I am a widow at thirty-one and why my husband's people speak of me as *practical*, which is what they call a woman when they mean she has stopped surprising them.

The note was written on orchard stationery because I was shaking too hard to walk to the village shop. The ink was the ink on my father's desk because it was the nearest ink. I pinned it to her coat — her coat, Hansel's old blanket sewn into the lining, the one warm thing I could give her that Aurelio didn't know to count — and I carried her up the hill in the dark and I laid her on the steps of the only building in town with a light burning, and I knocked, and I ran.

Forget the face. I wrote it because I could not write *forgive me*, and because I believed — I still believe — that a child who was forgotten cleanly might grow up free, whereas a child who was missed might grow up hunted. I was wrong about almost every part of it except the hunting.

And then a stranger from upriver, a boy with his grandfather's tools and his grandfather's grudge, picked her up off the stones and did not forget the face either. Did not report her, did not sell her, did not send her to the parish. Just — kept her. Fed her, taught her the tick and the tock, let her sleep in the warm room among the gear-oil smell and the ticking, and when the town asked questions he answered them the way a clock answers: accurately, and only as much as required.

I went to him in February to beg him to hide her and found them at the workbench, her small hands in the works up to the wrist, him talking about pallets and locks like she was an apprentice of ten years. She looked up at me and did not know me. Why would she? I was a shape in a doorway that had once, briefly, been warm.

She knows me now. That is the miracle I am carrying downriver along with everything else. In March, after the blizzard, after the digging, after the reading — she took my hand in front of the whole town, in front of Aurelio with his face going the color of the ice, and she did not let go. She is six years old and she decided, with the absolute authority of a six-year-old, that I am hers, and she informed me I would write to her, and that I would come back in blossom time, and that these were not requests.

Children forgive the way rivers flood: entirely, and leaving everything rearranged.

* * *

Past noon the channel widened and the last of the shore ice went by us in great grinding plates, tilting, blue-shadowed underneath, and the sound they made against the hull was the sound the whole valley had been listening for since December — the groan and shift of a thing that seemed permanent admitting it was not. Odo crossed himself without breaking stride at the tiller.

"That'll be the back of winter," he said. "You'll see blossom downriver a month early."

"I know. I've seen it."

"No," he said, with the mildness of a man correcting a manifest error. "You've seen *our* blossom. Hill fruit. Late, stingy, half of it frost-bit. Downriver it comes fat and early and smells so sweet it makes strong men stupid." He nodded at my bag. "You'll want to bring some back. For the child. First blossoms keep pressed in a book well enough."

I had not thought of it. That is the shame of me — I think of everything, I plan everything, I hold the whole board in my head like Aurelio taught me, and it had not once occurred to me to bring her flowers, because flowers are the currency of people who visit, and I had not let myself be a visitor. Visitors come back.

"I'll press them in the ledger," I said, and Odo laughed like I'd told a joke, and maybe I had.

The tower was long gone behind us by then. But I kept standing at the stern rail, because there is a kind of looking that isn't about seeing. Somewhere back there, up that hill, in a room full of ticking, a small figure was learning the trade of keeping time, and a young man who arrived believing history happened to other people was writing both our families' names back into a ledger with his own hand, and an orchard full of black winter branches was, at that very moment, underground, deciding.

That is the thing about trees nobody tells you until you lose one. The decision was made months ago. All winter, while we fought over berths and deeds and who deserved the future, the buds were already formed, wrapped tight, counting the warm hours in the dark. The bloom was never in question. Only whether anyone would be there, unhurried, to see it.

I will be there. Blossom time. I have a daughter to embarrass with pressed flowers and a brother's ruin to outlive graciously and a mother's locket — no. The locket stayed. That was Mara's ruling, delivered at the graveside with her hand still dirty from the digging: *it belongs to the tree now. You can visit it.* She is her grandfather's blood, truly. She understands vaults.

What I carry instead is lighter and harder to pack: the story. My mother's five-blossom branch, the dog who kept things, the note I wrote and the note my father hid, the boy who chose the town over his own restored name, the girl at the window with her palm open. Downriver they will want it simple — villain, saint, treasure. I will give it to them complicated, the way it happened, the way my father would have wanted it written in a margin somewhere in his small exact hand: *the orchard was saved by the two people it had wronged most, and neither of them asked for anything.*

Except — that is not quite true either, and here, alone at the stern rail with the water running clean at last, I can afford the truth. She asked me for one thing. The night before I sailed, in the clockroom, with the weights swaying overhead like slow bronze pendulums, Mara took both my hands and said:

"When I'm keeper — the real keeper, when I'm grown — I want the sign at the gate to have two names on it. The ones everybody forgot." She counted them off on her fingers, gravely, the way she counts gear teeth. "Grandfather Nakamura. And you. Because you dug."

I told her the gate belonged to the town now and the town would decide such things, which was cowardice wearing an official's coat, and she saw straight through it, because children always do, and she said only: *"Then decide fast. Trees don't wait."*

The river bends ahead. Odo shouts something about the shallows at Tern Point and the deck tilts and I catch the rail and laugh — actually laugh, first time since the thaw — because the river is running free, the ice is broken, and I am a practical woman at last doing something impractical: going home to a house I sold, to a valley I betrayed, to a child I abandoned, carrying nothing but a story and a promise to argue with a town council about a sign.

Sorrow braided through it all like wire through cable, yes. But cable holds.

I turn my face downriver. Behind me, faint on the wind, I would swear — I will always swear — I hear a clock striking the hour, and under it, thinner, a child laughing in the trees.

Blossom time. I had better hurry.
