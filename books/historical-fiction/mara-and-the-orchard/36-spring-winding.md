## 36. Spring Winding

The master clock had been dead for one hundred and eleven days, and I knew this because Isandro counted, and because once a person teaches you to count days, you can never stop. Before him, time was something that happened to other people — grown-ups mostly, and the river, and winter. Now time was mine too, or half mine, which was more than anyone had ever given me of anything.

One hundred and eleven days since the mechanism seized, since the sabotage, since the night I'd packed my bundle and waited in the clockroom to be taken away so nobody would have to choose me. One hundred and eleven days since the ice broke and the ferry left without us, carrying Elena downriver with her hand pressed flat against the rail like she was waving at a window she couldn't see.

And now it was spring winding day.

Isandro had talked about nothing else for a week. He talked about it while he oiled the great bearings, talking more to the clock than to me, which was normal. He talked about it while he checked every tooth on every wheel twice, because he was Isandro and checking things twice was how he prayed. He talked about it over supper, which was bread and cheese and the last jar of the orchard's preserved plums, eaten at the workbench because neither of us saw any reason tables should get in the way.

"Tomorrow," he said, "at nine o'clock exactly."

"Exactly," I said, because I knew my part.

"Not approximately. Not 'sometime in the morning.' Nine o'clock." He pointed his knife at the dead dial above us, its hands frozen where they'd stopped in January, pointing at a moment that hadn't existed in months. "The whole town is going to be listening for it. Half of them will pretend they aren't. Old Serafina will stand in her doorway with her arms crossed and say *hmph* whether it works or not, because that's what she does, but she'll be listening."

I believed him about Serafina. I'd met her twice now, once when she brought us a pie she claimed was for "the tower, not the people in it," and once when she caught me buying thread in the market and inspected my stitching through my sleeve until I showed her. The town had decided about me the way towns decide things — slowly, sideways, by way of pies and thread — and I was learning that being decided about was not the same as being wanted, but it was a door that could open onto wanted if you were patient.

"And if it doesn't start?" I asked.

He looked at me over his spectacles, which he wore now even though his eyes were fine, because he said a clockmaker of standing ought to have spectacles, and I said that was the silliest reason to wear glass I'd ever heard, and he said yes, probably, and kept wearing them.

"Then we'll find out why," he said. "That's the whole trade, Mara. Things don't start until you find out why they stopped."

* * *

We climbed before dawn anyway. Neither of us admitted we meant to; we just both appeared in the clockroom early, wrapped in blankets, and looked at each other, and pretended it was an accident.

The clockroom in spring light was a different country from the clockroom in winter. In January the windows had worn sleeves of frost and our breath hung around us like company we hadn't invited. Now the light came up gold and long across the floorboards, catching the dust we'd never quite beaten, and the weights hung in their shaft like sleeping animals, patient, enormous, waiting to be told it was morning again.

I put my hands on the escapement first. I always did. It was the part Isandro taught me — the tick and the tock, the little anchor that catches the wheel and lets it go, catches it and lets it go, the whole trick of time in one small rocking motion. *Everything else is muscle,* he'd said, back when I was small enough that the escapement seemed tall. *This is the heart. Guard the heart.*

My hands were bigger now. Spring had made me taller — Elena would not know me, and that thought arrived and sat down heavily on my chest, so I made myself look at the escapement instead, at the pallets I had polished, at the tiny scar on the anchor arm where I'd slipped with a file when I was eight and Isandro hadn't scolded me, only said, *good, now it's yours.*

"It wants oil," I said. "Third pivot. It's been dry all winter."

"It has been dry all winter," he agreed. "You've been saying so for two weeks."

"Somebody has to say it. You're busy having feelings about the weights."

"I am not—" He stopped. He adjusted his pointless spectacles. "I am having appropriate professional anticipation about the weights."

"You named them."

"They needed names. You can't hoist four hundred pounds of cast iron and call it nothing."

"The big one is Gertrude," I said, "which remains ridiculous."

"Gertrude is a dignified name for a dignified weight," said Isandro, and began to rig the windlass, and I let him win, because today of all days he could have whatever he liked.

* * *

Winding a clock that size is not turning a key. It is arguing with gravity and winning slowly. The weights come up on a drum turned by a crank through gearing, and each turn takes both hands and most of your opinion of yourself, and Isandro did the crank while I paid out and guided the cable, because that was our division, established over three winters: his shoulders, my eyes. His grandfather had wound this clock once — I knew the story now, all of it, the name scraped off the ferry ledger, the man who'd worked the orchard lands and been written out of them — and sometimes when Isandro hauled and swore under his breath in the old language his grandfather had left him, I thought the tower held three of us, not two.

"Gertrude's home," he grunted, and the great weight settled into its running position with a groan of iron that went down through the floors and out into the stone of the tower like a word spoken into water.

Then the second weight, and the third, and the strike train last, because the strike train was temperamental and Isandro treated it the way you treat a cat that has been wronged.

I knelt back at the escapement. Oiled, pivots clean, pallets seated. My hands were not shaking. I want that recorded somewhere: my hands were not shaking, although everything under them felt like it was.

"Mara."

"Hm."

"When it starts, the hands are going to move." He said it carefully, the way he explained things he thought might frighten me, which was a habit I tolerated because he usually wasn't wrong. "From where they stopped. January to April. They'll sweep through a hundred and eleven days in about a minute, if the governor does its work. It looks strange the first time."

"I've seen it in books."

"Books lie about clocks. Books lie about everything; that's why I became a clockmaker."

I grinned at the escapement so he wouldn't see it fully. "What time will it say?"

"Whatever time it truly is, when I release the train. That's rather the point of the exercise."

"No." I turned around. "I mean — the hands stop where they stopped because of what happened. Aurelio's men, the file in the works, the night I almost—"

"You didn't almost anything. You fixed an escapement alone under worse conditions than any apprentice in the history of the trade, and I will die saying so."

"—because of what happened," I went on, because I was not going to let him reroute me, he did it constantly, "and then the hands will just go on from there, like none of it happened."

Isandro came and sat down on the floor beside me, which he did when a thing mattered, because standing was for ordinary remarks. The dawn had gone from gold to white. Below us the river ran loud and free, the ice long broken, the water brown and muscular with snowmelt, hauling the winter off to the sea.

"It isn't like none of it happened," he said. "It's like this: a clock doesn't remember by stopping. Anyone can stop. Stopping is easy — ask the family that used to own your orchard." He tapped the floorboards between us. "A clock remembers by continuing. Through everything. That's the whole dignity of the thing. It goes through the fire, through the winter, through the worst night anybody ever spent in this room, and afterward it shows the true time. Not the time it wishes it were. The true one. Continuing is the memory."

I looked at my hands. They were steady after all. Eight years old and steady, which would have frightened the girl who'd been left on these steps with a note pinned to her coat, except that girl had become this one, gear tooth by gear tooth.

"Catches it and lets it go," I said.

"Catches it and lets it go," he agreed. "Now. Third pivot's oiled?"

"Oiled and verified."

"Governor?"

"Cleaned, balanced, and insulted daily."

"Pallets?"

"Seated." I set my fingers lightly against the anchor, feeling for its rest position, the little notch of stillness where it waited. "Ready."

He stood. He took hold of the maintaining lever — the small brass handle that held tension while the weights slept, that had been holding, patiently, all this time, so the clock would not forget itself entirely — and he looked at the pocket watch he did not need, because the sun told him better, but he consulted it anyway, for ceremony.

"Nine o'clock," he said, "exactly," and released the lever.

* * *

Here is what happens, and no book gets it right.

There is a click, very small, close to my hand — the escapement taking up its duty, catching the wheel, letting it go, catching, letting go — and the sound runs up through the metal into my fingerbones before it ever reaches the air. Then the gears wake below us, all four hundred pounds of Gertrude and her sisters giving their stored winter to the train, and the noise builds the way weather builds, wheel answering wheel answering wheel, until the whole tower is a body remembering it has a pulse.

And the hands move.

They moved the way Isandro promised — fast, smooth, terrible and wonderful, sweeping through the dead months. I watched the minute hand cross the hours of the blizzard, three days of snow in perhaps four seconds; I watched it pass through the night of the sabotage, through the thaw, through the morning the ferry pulled away, through all of it, unhurried in its hurry, refusing to linger anywhere, and I understood what he'd meant about continuing, understood it in my teeth, and something in my chest that had been clenched since the note, since the smoke and the dog and the woman's voice saying *wait here*, unclenched all at once, and I laughed, or cried, or both, which is a thing I have learned people do and pretend is one thing.

The chime struck nine. All of it. Every bell, the full peal, rolling out over the roofs and down to the river, and the sound went on and on because the strike train had a hundred and eleven days of opinion saved up.

Below, faintly, the town answered. Doors. Voices. Someone — I would have bet the orchard itself — said *hmph*, and someone else laughed at her.

Isandro had gone very still by the weights, one hand flat on the case the way you touch a horse's neck, and when I looked at him his face was doing something complicated behind the useless spectacles, so I did what he always did for me and handed him a job.

"Check the beat," I said. "Tick-tock's got a lean. Left side heavy."

"There is no lean, I regulated it Tuesday—"

"Check it."

He listened. His head tilted. A long pause.

"...Slight lean," he admitted.

"Left side heavy."

"*Slight.*" He fetched the regulating key, and I watched him work, and I thought about how a year ago I'd have called him my teacher and meant it and it would have been enough. It wasn't enough anymore, and the new word was sitting in me like a seed under snow, waiting for its season, and I didn't say it yet. Some words you plant. You don't dig them up to check.

Instead I said, "Pendulum length's a hair long. Shorten it and it'll keep true through summer."

"You shorten it. Your repair."

So I did. My hands, my file, my clock — ours, ours, the word *ours* fitting the tower the way the locket-branch fit the lock, which is to say perfectly, as if it had been cut for that purpose by somebody who loved us before we ever met.

When the beat ran even — catch and release, catch and release, the true time showing through — Isandro sat back down beside me on the floor, and we stayed there through two full strikes of the quarter-hour, saying nothing, because some machinery doesn't need winding, only witnessing.

Downriver, I thought, Elena was somewhere on the water, telling someone the story of a branch of blossoms and a dog's grave. Up here, the clock remembered by continuing.

"Orchard tomorrow?" Isandro asked finally. "The ground's soft enough now. If we want your mother's tree in before the blossoms finish, it wants planting soon."

I had known it was coming, and it still landed in me like the weights landing home. A tree. For her. Beside the dog, under the first tree, where the locket had waited in the dark all those winters for a child with steady hands.

"Yes," I said. "Tomorrow."

The clock ticked. The river ran. And far below, small in the spring light, the whole town was already moving toward the orchard road, and I did not yet know why — only that the bells had barely finished ringing when Isandro leaned to the window and went quiet in the particular way he went quiet before telling me something enormous.

"Mara," he said. "You should come see who's walking up the hill."
