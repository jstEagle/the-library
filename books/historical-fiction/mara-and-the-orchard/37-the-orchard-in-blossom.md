## 37. The Orchard in Blossom

The town reopened the orchard on a Saturday in late April, which was the sort of thing the town did without asking anybody's permission, least of all mine. I had learned by then that towns are like clocks that way. They keep their own time and expect everyone else to synchronize to it.

I woke before dawn anyway. Not because anyone asked me to. Because my body had been trained by a master clock for three years now, and it had opinions about mornings, and those opinions were not negotiable.

From the clockroom window I could see the orchard laid out on its hill like a table set for guests who were very late. The trees had come into bloom overnight, it seemed — though of course it hadn't been overnight, it had been weeks of patient work by roots nobody watched — and the whole hillside wore that pale pink-white haze that made it look less like an orchard and more like a cloud that had gotten tired of the sky and decided to settle.

Three years ago I had stood on those observatory steps with a note pinned to my coat telling the world to forget my face. Now the whole town was walking up that hill to look at me. Or at the orchard. The distinction felt important and also impossible to draw.

"You're up early," Isandro said from behind me, carrying two cups of tea, which was how he said good morning, since actual words appeared to cost him something.

"I'm always up early."

"You're always up early when something's going to happen." He handed me a cup. Steam rose between us. "You were up early the day the deed was read. You were up early the day the ferry left."

"The ferry leaves at dawn. Everyone's up early for the ferry."

"Not everyone stands at the window watching it go with a face like a closed pocket watch."

I drank my tea instead of answering. That was another thing I'd learned from him — you didn't have to fill every silence, no matter what the town gossips believed. Some silences were load-bearing.

Below us, people were already gathering at the orchard gate. I could pick out faces even at this distance: Mrs. Aldous from the bakery with her arms full of something; old Tomas who ran the ferry dock, standing a little apart from everyone the way he always did since the thaw, as if the water still owed him an apology; children running ahead of their mothers through the wet grass. Aurelio wasn't there. He had taken the road south in March and hadn't sent word, and I had decided — after some deliberation — not to spend any more of my feelings on him than he'd spent on me, which was to say none, which was to say he could keep his feelings and I would keep mine and we would call the accounts settled.

Elena's berth on the ferry had carried her downriver to her aunt's house in the city, where there were doctors and quiet streets and no one named Aurelio. She wrote every month. Her letters smelled faintly of ink and came with pressed flowers sometimes, and each one ended with the same line: *Tell me everything about the blossoms. Spare nothing.* As if she thought I might ration them out, hoard spring like a miser.

Today I intended to write back with an inventory.

"Are you nervous?" Isandro asked.

"No," I said, which was true in the way that a stopped clock is technically showing a time.

He looked at me over his cup. Isandro had this way of looking at things — straight on, unblinking, like a man reading small print — that made lying feel like a waste of both our time.

"A little," I admitted. "What if I don't remember right? What if I plant it in the wrong place?"

"There's no wrong place."

"There's absolutely a wrong place. There's a place where the drainage is bad and a place where the frost pools and a place where the dog used to—" I stopped.

The dog's grave. Under the first tree. Where my mother's locket had spent two winters in the dark, waiting for a child too small to remember where she'd put it.

"That's the right place," Isandro said quietly, because of course he knew what I'd remembered. He always knew. It was the most annoying and most necessary thing about him.

* * *

The town had voted in February — after the deed was read aloud from the clockroom window, after my name went into the ledger as keeper, after all of it — to open the orchard gates to everyone. No fences. No family quarrels. No heir standing at the gate deciding who was worthy of fruit.

The deed's wording had done most of the work, I'll grant it that. *In trust, for the town, kept by the child of the clock.* Grandfather — my grandfather, the one I never met, the one whose hands apparently lived on in mine the way a song lives on in someone who only ever heard it once — had written us all out of the inheritance and into something better. A shared thing. A held thing.

But deeds don't prune trees, and laws don't clear deadwood, and so for six weeks the town had been coming up the hill with saws and ladders and baskets, working off the neglect of the quarrel years. Mrs. Aldous organized everyone with the efficiency of a woman who had fed a town for thirty years and did not intend to stop now. Old Tomas repaired the gate hinge. Children hauled water in buckets too big for them, spilling half of it and being praised extravagantly for the other half.

And me? I walked the rows.

That was my job now, or part of it. To walk the rows and know them. Which tree was the oldest — a gnarled apple near the crest of the hill that the older townsfolk called the Grandmother, though nobody could say why, and I suspected the name predated everyone currently alive, which seemed fitting for this town. Which tree bore early and which bore late. Which tree had been struck by lightning sometime before my mother was born and healed around the wound, growing a seam of dark wood down its trunk like a scar it had decided to be proud of.

Isandro said I had a head for it. Elena, in her letters, said it was in the blood. I thought it was neither, exactly. I thought it was that I had lost everything once — smoke, a dog, a woman's voice — and that some part of me had made a private vow never to lose track of anything again. So I memorized trees the way other people memorized prayers.

This morning, though, I wasn't here to walk the rows.

I had brought a sapling.

It had arrived two weeks ago from a nursery downriver, ordered by Mrs. Aldous on my behalf after I'd made the mistake of mentioning the idea out loud in the bakery. A young apple tree, barely taller than me, its branches still bare-boned and waiting. I had kept it behind the observatory in a barrel of good soil, watering it daily, talking to it occasionally — not because I believed plants listened, but because Isandro talked to clocks and claimed they ran truer for it, and I figured the principle probably transferred.

Now the sapling stood at the orchard gate beside me, wrapped in burlap around its roots, while half the town pretended not to watch.

"They're staring," I muttered to Isandro.

"Of course they're staring. You're about to plant a tree in front of the entire municipality. Staring is the municipal pastime."

"It's just a tree."

"It is not," he said, "just a tree, and you know it, and if you say 'it's just a tree' one more time I will tell everyone present about the escapement incident of last November."

"You promised never to bring that up."

"And you promised never to call the master clock 'grumpy,' and yet here we are, both of us living in a world of broken promises."

Mrs. Aldous rescued me then, sweeping up in a cloud of flour dust and authority. "The ground's turned where you wanted it," she said, jerking her chin toward the first tree. "Tomas did it himself, wouldn't let the young ones near it. Said some holes deserve respect." She looked at the sapling, then at me, and her voice softened in a way that would have embarrassed us both if either of us had been the sort to show it. "It's a good thing you're doing, child."

"It's a tree, Mrs. Aldous."

"Mm. And the deed was just paper." She patted my shoulder with a hand like a bread paddle and moved off to bully somebody else into usefulness.

* * *

The first tree stood alone at the top of the rise, older than everything, its blossoms so thick the branches looked snowbound. Beneath it, the ground was unmarked — no stone, no cross, nothing. The dog had been buried there long before I could remember clearly, in the winter of the smoke, in the last days before everything else happened. My memory of that time was still mostly fragments: a bark, a warmth against my side, a woman's voice saying *stay with him, stay with the dog, I'll come back.*

She hadn't come back. But the dog had stayed with me, in whatever way dogs stay, and when the dog was buried under this tree, something of my mother must have gone into the ground with him — the locket, yes, the silver branch of blossoms that had opened the clock's hidden heart — but something else too. Something that had waited three winters for me to grow tall enough to carry it back.

The crowd gathered at a distance. They were good about that. The town had learned, slowly and imperfectly, when to be a crowd and when to be witnesses, and this morning they chose witnesses.

I knelt at the edge of the turned earth with the sapling across my knees.

The hole was deep enough. Tomas had seen to it. I unwound the burlap with fingers that shook slightly, which annoyed me, and I told them so, and they ignored me, which was typical of hands. Hands do what they've decided to do regardless of editorial input. Isandro says that's why he trusts hands more than heads.

The roots came free pale and tangled, smelling of dark earth and river water. I lowered the sapling in. It went crooked. I straightened it. It went crooked again, leaning east toward the river, and finally I stopped fighting it.

"Fine," I told it. "Lean, then. See if I care."

It leaned. I packed the earth around it with my palms, then with the flat of a board Mrs. Aldous pressed into service, then with my palms again, pressing down until the ground was firm and the little tree stood at its chosen angle, bare branches raised against the blossom-heavy boughs of the first tree above it like a child lifting its arms.

I had planned to say something. I had drafted remarks. In my head there had been a speech about remembrance and roots and the way grief grows greener than anything else, and I had rehearsed parts of it while winding the clock.

What came out instead was:

"Her name was Sable."

A ripple went through the crowd — not shock exactly, but the sound of a town receiving a piece of information it had wondered about for years. Nobody had known my mother's name. I hadn't known it myself until Elena told me, the week before she left, holding my hands across the clockroom workbench, crying and smiling at the same time in a way I have since learned is simply what love looks like when it arrives too late to be simple.

"Sable Thackeray," I said again, louder, so it would reach the back row. "She grew up in this orchard. She sang while she worked — that's what Elena says. She had a dog who loved her more than he loved food, which for a dog means something. And she loved this town, even when the town was busy tearing itself apart, and she hid the truth inside a clock so the town couldn't destroy it." My voice wobbled on the last part. I let it. Isandro had taught me that too — that a mechanism works better when you don't force the tension. "So this is her tree. It'll bear fruit for whoever wants it. Same as she tried to."

I put my hand flat on the fresh-turned earth, and for a moment — just a moment, just long enough — I heard the smoke and the dog and the voice, all three of them at once, layered like rings in old wood. Then it passed, the way weather passes, leaving the sky clearer than before.

Behind me, quietly, Mrs. Aldous began to sing.

It was an orchard song, an old one, the kind the grandmothers knew — about blossom and bee and the patience of fruit — and one by one the voices joined hers until the whole hillside was humming it, off-key and uneven and entirely sincere. Isandro appeared at my elbow. He did not sing. Isandro did not sing for the same reason he didn't dance: some instruments refuse to be played. But he stood close enough that our shoulders touched, and he took off his hat, and that was his verse.

The sapling leaned east. Above it, petals from the first tree came loose in the breeze and drifted down onto the new-turned ground, settling over the grave that had never had a marker, as if the old tree were tucking something in.

* * *

Afterward, when the crowd had thinned and Mrs. Aldous had deployed cake with tactical precision, I stayed on the hill. Isandro sat nearby on the low wall that bordered the orchard's oldest terrace, ostensibly supervising the repair of a trellis and actually doing nothing whatsoever, which was his second-favorite activity after winding clocks.

I watered the sapling from a bucket. Twice. Three times. On the fourth trip Isandro said, "It's planted, not shipwrecked," and I set the bucket down with dignity.

We sat in the blossom-light for a while. Bees were already investigating the new tree as if they'd received formal notice. Somewhere down the slope, children were arguing about whose turn it was on the rope swing Tomas had hung from the Grandmother's lowest branch.

"Can I ask you something?" I said eventually.

"You can ask. Whether I answer depends on the question."

"What happens now? To the orchard, I mean. Everybody keeps looking at me like I'm supposed to know. Mrs. Aldous asks me about grafting stock. Tomas asked me yesterday whether we should replant the south row. Me! I'm eleven. Eleven-year-olds don't have opinions about rootstock."

"Some eleven-year-olds," Isandro said, "have already opened a clock vault, survived a blizzard, dug up a locket in frozen ground at midnight while armed men closed in, and addressed a municipality. Those eleven-year-olds may have opinions about rootstock."

"That's different. That was emergencies. Emergencies are easy. It's Tuesdays I don't know how to do."

He considered that with the seriousness he gave everything — gear trains, ferry manifests, the correct amount of honey in tea. "My grandfather," he said at last, "kept a ledger."

I looked up. This was rare territory. In three years Isandro had told me about his grandfather exactly twice: once the night of the sabotage, when fear had loosened him like a stuck screw, and once in the tower during the siege, when he'd explained that his grandfather's name had been cut out of the orchard's story — erased from the ferry ledgers, struck from the payrolls, gone — because of some old grievance between the families, a debt of silence that had passed down to Isandro like an inherited coat two sizes too big.

"He wrote everything down," Isandro went on, watching the river. "Every repair. Every season. What bloomed when, what froze, what thrived. He believed a thing that isn't written down can be erased. He was right, as it turns out. They erased him precisely because the ledgers were burned." His jaw tightened, just slightly, the only tell he ever allowed himself. "The Nakamura name has been out of this valley's books for fifty years. Out of the orchard's books longer."

"But the deed put it back," I said. "*With the Nakamura name restored among its keepers.* I've read it a hundred times. Your grandfather's name is in the wording. Yours too, someday."

"Yes." He said it the way he said *yes* when yes cost him something. "Which means someone ought to start writing again. Before the next erasure. Someone with steady hands and a suspicious memory."

I understood then — the slow way understanding comes, like dawn rather than like a lamp — that he wasn't talking about himself. Or not only about himself.

"Isandro. Are you asking me to keep a ledger?"

"I'm asking you to think about it. Keepers keep. That's the whole job description, as far as I can tell. Trees, names, dates, grievances, debts, songs. All of it goes in the book, and the book goes somewhere safe, and in fifty years nobody gets to pretend any of this didn't happen." He glanced at me sideways. "You're better at remembering than anyone I've ever met. You memorized forty apple trees by heart in a month. You remember a dog's bark from when you were four. Frankly, you're wasted on the trees alone."

Down the slope, the blossom-light shifted as clouds crossed the sun, and the whole orchard went silver and then gold again, breathing. The new sapling stood at its stubborn eastern lean, catching light on its bare twigs, already — impossibly, wonderfully — showing the first green points of leaf along its branches, as if it had decided that waiting was for other trees.

A ledger. A book with my mother's name in it, and the dog's, and Isandro's grandfather's, and the escapement incident of last November, which I was now contractually forbidden from forgetting.

"I'll need a good pen," I said. "And paper that doesn't fall apart. And you have to teach me the shorthand clockmakers use for repairs, because I want the clock's whole history in it too, and your handwriting is a disgrace."

"My handwriting is *efficient*."

"Your handwriting is a spider's funeral."

For a moment he looked like he might argue. Then he laughed — short, surprised, real, one of perhaps nine laughs I'd witnessed in three years, each one catalogued and treasured — and stood, and offered me his hand, and pulled me up off the grass into the falling petals.

"Tomorrow, then," he said. "First entry."

"What will it be?"

He looked out over the orchard — the Grandmother, the lightning-scarred tree, the rows and rows of blossom, the small leaning sapling at the top of the hill with its brand-new leaves — and something in his face went quiet and settled, like a pendulum finding its arc.

"Start with today," he said. "Start with her name."
