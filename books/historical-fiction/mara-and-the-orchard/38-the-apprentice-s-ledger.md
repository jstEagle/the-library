## 38. The Apprentice's Ledger

There is a particular kind of quiet that lives in a clockroom after the clock has been running for a full week. It is not silence — a running clock never permits silence; it forbids it, politely, every second of every hour. It is more like the quiet of a house where someone is humming in another room. You stop listening for danger and start listening for the tune.

I had been listening to that tune for seven days when I finally sat down to do the thing I had been putting off since the ice broke.

The ledger was on the desk in the corner of the clockroom, under the window that looked down over the orchard. Not the old ferry manifests — those had gone to the town clerk, along with everything else we'd pulled out of the hidden chamber, because a deed read aloud from a clocktower window has a way of making every scrap of paper near it suddenly important. This was a new book. Blank pages. Good paper, bought from the stationer's on Ferry Street with money I earned honestly, which was still a novel enough sensation that I'd stood at his counter for a moment like a man who'd forgotten what coins were for.

A ledger. Because that was how this whole miserable, miraculous year had started — with names written down and names scratched out.

My grandfather had been a ledger man. Kenji Nakamura, orchard hand, ferryman's assistant, and — though no page in this town had admitted it in forty years — the man who built half the mechanism above my head. He kept accounts. That was what he did, according to the one letter of his that had survived the crossing and the decades and my mother's careful drawer: tallies of hours worked, wages owed, trees grafted. *The orchard owes me eleven days of spring labor,* he had written once, in that patient hand, *and I owe it three hundred trees. We are even, or nearly.* Nearly. My grandfather was the sort of man who believed you could be nearly even with an orchard.

The town had disagreed. The town had decided he was not even nearly anything, and had taken his name off a manifest with a line of ink, and forty years later a boy with his face and his surname had walked up a hill to wind a clock nobody trusted, believing — sincerely, stupidly, comfortably — that history was something that happened to other people.

Well. History had happened to me. It had happened to me in the shape of a small girl asleep on cold stone steps with a note pinned to her coat, and it had happened in the shape of gears that did nothing, and it had happened in the shape of a locket buried under a tree beside a dog whose name I would never learn. And now history wanted something back from me, which was the part nobody warns you about. History is not a debt collector. It is worse. It is a bookkeeper, and it keeps its own ledger, and it waits.

I opened the new book to the first page.

* * *

You should understand that I sat there for a long while before writing anything.

The lamplight was doing its evening work, going gold on the brass of the escapement, and below me the river ran the way rivers run in late April — fast, brown, full of itself, carrying chunks of the last rotten ice downstream like a crowd dispersing after an argument. The orchard was greening. From the tower you could see the rows clearly now, the bare black arms of the trees beginning to fuzz with leaf, and the fresh-turned earth of one new grave among the old ones, small, marked by a sapling not much taller than the child who had planted it.

Mara had planted that tree nine days ago. She had watered it from a bucket she could barely carry, twice a day, without fail, and she had informed me — in the tone she used when she had decided a thing and my agreement was merely a courtesy — that it was a plum, because her mother had liked plums, and that it would bloom white, and that when it bloomed she would know her mother had seen it.

I am a clockmaker. I deal in mechanisms. Cause, effect, tooth, pawl. But I have learned this year that some mechanisms run on nothing you can weigh, and they keep better time than anything with brass in it.

So: the blank page. What does a man write on a blank page when the page is meant to correct forty years of erasure?

I tried several openings in my head and discarded them all. *Kenji Nakamura, wrongly struck* — too much like a grievance, and grievances are a poor foundation. *Kenji Nakamura, restored* — restoration implies the restorers' generosity, and I felt no generosity toward them just then, whoever *them* was. The town? The family? Time itself? All of them had held the pen at one moment or another, and all of them had let the line stand.

In the end I wrote what was simply true, the way my grandfather would have written it, tallying hours.

*Kenneth — no. Kenji Nakamura. Orchard hand, 1859 to 1861. Ferryman's assistant, 1861 to 1864. Builder of the master clock of the observatory at [the town's name], together with its hidden works, which were built to protect the orchard and the people who depended upon it. Struck from the ferry manifests in the winter of 1864 through no fault of his own. His work kept time in this valley for thirty-four years whether anyone knew his name or not.*

I stopped. Looked at it. The ink dried the way ink does, slowly, then all at once, and there it was: a name on a page in this town, in this century, in a hand that trembled only slightly, and the trembling was mine and I let it stand too.

It seemed a small thing. It was not a small thing. Ask any erased man's grandson what a line of ink weighs. It weighs exactly as much as a deed. It weighs exactly as much as a child.

* * *

Footsteps on the stair — the small, quick ones, two at a time, because Mara had never once used these stairs like a person with joints — and then she was in the doorway with her sleeves rolled up and ink on her fingers already, which meant she'd been at the desk downstairs copying figures for the town clerk, a job she had appointed herself to and which everyone had been too wise to question.

"You're writing it," she said.

"I'm writing it."

"Can I watch?"

"You're watching."

She came and stood at my shoulder, close enough that I could feel the warmth of her, and read the page upside down with the fluency of a child who had spent a winter being read to by lamplight and had no intention of ever again being on the wrong side of a sentence. She was seven. She read like a customs inspector.

"It should say he built the *whole* clock," she said.

"He didn't build the whole clock. Hargreaves built the frame and the great wheel. My grandfather built the train and the escapement and—" I caught myself. She was looking at me with that expression, the one that said she understood perfectly well what I had just done, which was defend the accuracy of a record against the generosity of a child, and she was letting me see that she saw it, and she was not impressed. "He built," I amended, "most of the clock. The parts that matter."

"The parts that matter," she repeated solemnly, as though entering it into evidence.

"And the hidden works," I said. "Which matter most."

She considered the page a while longer. Then, in the voice she saved for the observations that went through me like a splinter: "Your grandfather's name looks nice."

"It's a good name."

"It's your name too."

"Not yet," I said. "That's rather the point of the exercise."

She frowned at me, unconvinced, and I understood — the way you understand things slowly, the way ice understands spring — that she was right and I was wrong, and that she had been right for some time, probably since the night on the steps, possibly since before that, since whatever smoke-and-dog-and-voice fragment she carried of a world that had ended for her. Names are not granted. They are claimed. A name left unclaimed is just letters waiting.

But I get ahead of myself, and a clockmaker knows better than anyone that you cannot skip the intervening teeth.

* * *

Three days later, the town council met in the observatory's lower room, because the observatory had abruptly become the place where important things happened, which amused me in a tired sort of way. For a year the town had treated this building as a cold appendage, a place where a foreign-sounding apprentice wound a suspicious clock, and now the councilmen climbed my stairs twice a month and wiped their boots and spoke to me as though we had always been on good terms. Men are adaptable. That is the charitable word for it.

The business that day was the orchard trust. The deed's wording — *to the town, in trust, kept by the child of the clock* — required an administration, and administrations require committees, and committees require minutes, and minutes require a ledger. The town clerk produced one, a fine leather-bound thing with the town's arms embossed on the cover, and began to read out the proposed keepers and trustees in order of dignity: the mayor, the council, the churchwarden, a representative of the ferry company —

"Stop," I said.

I had not planned to speak. I want that on the record, in whatever invisible ledger records such things. I had planned to stand at the back with my arms crossed and my opinion behind my teeth, as I had for a year, because a foreigner learns early that opinions are a currency he does not hold.

But the clerk had read four names and none of them was Nakamura, and the deed itself — read aloud from that very window, before the whole town, with the spring sun coming through the glass like a benediction — had named my grandfather's craft and my grandfather's works as the instrument of everything they were now dividing up among themselves. They were standing in his clock. They were keeping their minutes under his roof-beam. And his name was not on their list.

"The deed," I said, "names the mechanism. The mechanism has a maker. If the town means to keep faith with the document, it might begin by keeping faith with the man who wrote it into iron."

Silence. The particular silence of respectable men recalculating. Mayor Aldous — a decent man, which is different from a brave one — cleared his throat. "The Nakamura matter is... historical, Mr. Thackeray."

"All matters are historical, Mr. Mayor. That is what makes them matters."

Someone coughed. Someone else studied the ceiling beams, which my grandfather's era had helped raise, though no one present remarked upon it.

And then a small voice from the doorway said: "Read the deed again."

They turned. Mara stood there in her darned coat — Elena's mending, neat stitches, a mother's care applied at one remove and too late and not too late at all — with her hands folded, because she had learned that folded hands make adults listen longer than pointed fingers do.

"The deed says," she recited — and she had it by heart, of course she did; she had been present at its reading and she forgot nothing, this child, she kept time in her head the way her grandfather had kept it in brass — "*kept in trust by the town, its officers, and the lawful keepers of the clock, whose names shall be entered in the observatory ledger, in perpetuity, beginning with the maker.*"

"Beginning with the maker," I said quietly. "Yes. I had noticed."

The mayor looked at the clerk. The clerk looked at the deed. The deed, being ink, said nothing further, having said quite enough already.

"Well," said the mayor, in the tone of a man stepping off a ledge and finding the ground closer than feared, "then it seems the ledger must begin correctly. Clerk — the maker's name, if you please."

And so it was entered, in the town's own fine hand, in the town's own fine book: *Kenji Nakamura, master of the works.* Witnessed by six councilmen and one child, who watched every stroke of the pen as though supervising.

Afterward, on the stairs, she took my sleeve. "Now it's true everywhere," she said. "Not just in our book."

"Our book?"

"Yours. The new one." A pause, and the slyness she deployed only when certain of victory: "You haven't signed it yet."

"No," I said. "I haven't."

"Why not?"

Because signing beneath his name makes it real, and real things can be taken away — I did not say that. I had spent a lifetime keeping my name small enough to hide, and habits of concealment die hard, and some of them die kicking.

"Because a signature wants witnesses," I said instead, "and the right ones weren't there yet."

She accepted this with the gravity of a magistrate, and went downstairs to her figures, and I climbed back up to my page.

* * *

That evening I prepared properly, the way one prepares a movement for assembly. Cleaned the nib. Fresh ink. Set the lamp so the light fell full across the page, no shadows to write around. Below, the river ran loud with meltwater; above, the clock turned through its rounds, tick and tock, tick and tock, the sound I had once found merely mechanical and now understood to be the closest thing this valley had to a heartbeat, and I mean that without sentiment, which for me is saying a great deal.

Beneath my account of my grandfather, I wrote:

*Apprenticed to the works of the master clock of the observatory, being the last apprentice of its maker's line and school. Keeper of the clock from the winter of 1898. Present at the opening of the hidden chamber, the reading of the deed, and the planting of the plum tree.*

Then I paused, pen lifted, and made the choice that the whole year had been walking me toward the way a gear train walks a wheel toward its final tooth.

I did not sign *Isandro Thackeray, journeyman.* I did not sign *apprentice*, though my papers still said so, though I had never sat the examination, though by every rule of the guilds I remained what I had always been: a borrowed name attached to borrowed tools, waiting for a master somewhere to declare me finished.

There was no master somewhere. There was a master here, dead thirty-four years, whose clock I had rebuilt, whose hidden works I had opened, whose last secret I had carried up a hill in a child's coat. The guild could send its examiners when it pleased. Let them climb the stairs.

I signed:

*Isandro Kenjiro Nakamura-Thackeray, clockmaker.*

Not apprentice. Clockmaker. The compound name looked strange on the page, long as a barge, awkward as a first attempt at anything — and then it did not. It looked like a bridge between two banks. It looked like a name that had walked a long way to arrive at itself.

Below it, because the ledger deserved it and because I had learned this year that omissions are also entries: *Keeper, with Mara, of the orchard's memory.*

I set down the pen. Blew on the ink. Watched it dry the way ink does — slowly, then all at once — and felt something in my chest dry the same way, some tightness I had carried so long I had mistaken it for my skeleton.

The clock ticked. The river ran. Somewhere below, in the town, lamps were being lit one by one, and in the orchard the plum sapling stood in the dark doing the slow brave work of becoming a tree.

"You took your time," said a voice from the doorway, and there was Mara in her nightgown, having escaped Mrs. Ferreira's supervision with the ease of long practice, come to check my work as she checked everything now, clocks and ledgers and grown men alike.

"Yes," I said. "I took my time. It's what we do here."

She climbed onto the stool beside me and read the signature upside down, all of it, the long impossible length of it, and her face did something complicated and young and glad.

"Nakamura," she said, trying the shape of it. "That's mine now too. Ours." Then, practical as ever: "Does it fit in the register at school? It's very long."

"It'll fit. Important names usually are."

"Is it important?"

"It's ours," I said. "That's the definition."

She leaned against my shoulder, heavy with sleep, and for a while neither of us said anything, and the room filled up with tick and tock and river-sound, and outside, far off down the valley, the last light left the orchard rows.

Then she lifted her head, the way she did when a thought arrived that she intended to act upon immediately, and said:

"When the plum tree blooms, will you tell me about him? Your grandfather? All of it — the orchard, the boat, the name they took off?" Her eyes were very serious in the lamplight. "Because I'm going to be asked. Everyone comes to the clock now, and they ask me things, and someday someone will ask about *him*, and I want to say it right. I want to remember the face."

I looked at her — this child who had been delivered to my door with instructions to forget her, whom I had refused to forget, who had repaid me by remembering everything, her mother, the dog, the trees, and now, generously, absurdly, my grandfather too — and I understood that the ledger was not finished, and would never finish, and that this was the whole point of ledgers.

"Yes," I said. "When it blooms. I'll tell you everything."

She nodded, satisfied, contract concluded, and slid off the stool toward bed. At the door she paused.

"It'll bloom soon," she said. "I checked the buds today. They're getting fat."

And she was gone down the stairs, and I sat alone with the open book and the drying ink, and above us both the clock carried on, faithful, unhurried, keeping the time that belonged, at last, to all of us.
