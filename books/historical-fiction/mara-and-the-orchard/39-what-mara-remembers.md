## 39. What Mara Remembers

The visitors always ask the same three questions in the same order, and I have long ago stopped minding, because a keeper learns that people are clocks too — they run on their little trains of habit, and you do not scold them for ticking.

First: *How old is the clock?* Older than the town's manners, I tell them. Older than the quarrel that split the orchard family down its seams like a bad apple. Old enough to have been built by a man who wanted it to lie about something important.

Second: *Did it really stop?* It did. For one whole winter it stood still at eleven minutes past four, and the whole town went slightly mad without knowing why, the way a body goes restless when its heart forgets its rhythm. My father — no, I will come back to what Isandro is; he deserves better than a quick answer — my Isandro fixed it, with help from a child who was better with an escapement than she had any right to be.

Third, and this is where they lean in, because they have heard something, someone's cousin told them, there is a story that goes around the county like a coin worn smooth from handling: *Is it true there was a baby left on the steps?*

I am fifty-one years old this spring. The baby is standing right in front of them, holding the winding key.

* * *

They come up the hill most days now, once the blossoms open and the river runs green-brown and easy instead of grinding itself to pieces against the shore every winter. They come for the orchard, mostly — the town keeps it beautifully, we planted new rows after the old ones finally gave out, and there is nothing so persuasive as a hillside full of white flowers to make people believe a place is worth defending. Then someone points up the slope at the tower with its weathered copper dome, and says, *and the observatory? Can you go up?*

You can go up. That is rather the point of me.

I take them through the door that Isandro re-hung twice in his life because the frame settled, and up the stairs that corkscrew the way they have always done, forty-one steps and then the landing and then thirty more, and I let them arrive in the clockroom out of breath so they will stand quietly for a moment and simply listen. That is the trick of the room. You cannot talk over it. Even now, even after all these years, when the great pendulum swings its slow patient arc and the escapement lets the wheel go tick and then tock with the deliberation of something that has made peace with eternity, people hush. They came up here expecting machinery. They find a heartbeat instead.

And then I show them the mechanism.

I show them the honest train first — the going train, the striking work, all the gears doing exactly what gears should do, and the visitors nod along politely because gears are gears. But then I take down the lamp — yes, we still keep a lamp in the clockroom, some habits are not superstitions but inheritances — and I hold it low against the back plate, and I say, *now look again.*

There is a moment I never tire of. Their faces change. The light catches the dummy train, the beautiful useless wheels old Tomás Herrera cut into the plate with such loving precision that they would fool God Himself if He were in a hurry, and the visitors realize they are looking at a lock wearing a disguise. Gears that move nothing. Teeth that mesh with air. A mechanism built inside a mechanism, the way a lie is built inside a polite conversation.

*What does it do?* they ask, and I tell them: it did nothing. That was its genius. For sixty years those wheels turned and accomplished precisely nothing, while the thing they guarded sat behind them in a chamber no wider than a hymnal, waiting for a key shaped like an apple branch in blossom.

Somebody always asks where the deed is now, and I tell them the truth: framed in the town hall, behind glass, where schoolchildren file past it and mostly look at the wax seal. Documents belong to towns. Stories belong to keepers. I know which one I got.

* * *

Here is what the story-worn coin version leaves out, the part I add when the lamplight has softened them and they are leaning in like children, which is what they are, which is what we all are in front of a locked thing:

The note pinned to my coat did not say *take care of her.* It said *forget her face.* And the man who read it — a young clockmaker's apprentice, straight-backed and suspicious, with his grandfather's tools in a canvas roll and his grandfather's surname sitting on him like a debt — looked down at a sleeping girl of four or five on the frozen observatory steps, read the instruction, and failed at it immediately, completely, permanently.

He told me once, years later, when he was old enough to say such things without his voice cracking, that forgetting her face had been impossible from the first second because her face was not the kind you forget. It was the kind you *keep*. There is a difference, and it took me half a lifetime to understand it, so let me save you the trouble: a face you forget is scenery. A face you keep becomes furniture of the soul. You arrange your life around it. You dust around it. You could not remove it without tearing out the wall.

So he carried me upstairs, and he fed me bread and honey from his own supper, and he broke approximately seventeen rules of apprenticeship and probably six of decency toward his own future, and in the morning he did not report me to anyone. He wound the clock. He taught me the names of the tools. And when the town came sniffing — because a town always comes sniffing, a town is nothing but a nose with gossip attached — he said, *she is my sister,* with such flat conviction that even Aurelio Vidal, who suspected everything about everyone, wrote it down in whatever ledger he kept behind his eyes and moved on to larger suspicions.

Sister. Yes. Let us use the word plainly, since I have earned the right to plainness: Isandro Nakamura was not my brother by blood, and he became my family anyway, which is the only kind of family worth anything. Blood tells you where you started. It says nothing whatsoever about where you end up. Ask the orchard. The Vidals owned it by blood for three generations and nearly lost it to avarice and ice. The town holds it now, and the trees have never bloomed better.

* * *

The children in the tour groups are the ones I watch, because children can hear a mechanism working under a story the way I could hear, at seven years old, the wrongness in a gear that turned to no purpose. Children always find the dummy train. One of them will tug my sleeve while the adults are admiring the pendulum and ask, in the piercing whisper of the young, *but who left the little girl on the steps?*

And I kneel down, because my knees permit it these days only with negotiation, and I say: her mother did. Her mother loved her so much and was so frightened that leaving her was the bravest cowardly thing anyone ever did, and both halves of that sentence are true, and when you are grown you will understand that most brave things are also cowardly and vice versa, and that understanding is called growing up, and it is overrated except that it is also how you forgive people.

Then I tell them about Elena.

I tell them how she came to the tower in secret, in the gray hour before dawn, and saw my coat — her stitching, her mother's stitching before her, the little embroidered branch on the collar — and wept as though something in her had cracked open after a long freeze. How she begged Isandro to hide me, not from cruelty but from Aurelio, who had already begun speaking of the child as though I were a document, leverage, a clause. How she was the one who wrote the note, whose hand shook so badly the ink feathered, who chose the observatory because her father had trusted the clockmaker's line and she had nowhere else in the world to put her trust that wasn't already bought.

And I tell them the ending, because children cannot bear an unfinished sentence: she got the last berth. On the ferry. The morning the ice broke, when everyone else was demanding passage — the heir with his threats, the uncle with his money, the whole quarreling tree of them shaking its branches at the river — Isandro gave the berth away. To her. To the woman who had asked for nothing and given up everything, who had written *forget her face* with a breaking heart and then spent a winter dying slowly of having written it.

He gave it to her, and then he stayed. With me. In a tower surrounded by Aurelio's hired men, holding a locket shaped like an apple branch, waiting for the spring sun to strike the dome at just the right angle so the metal of the key would warm and turn in a lock that had waited sixty years for exactly that warmth.

I was nine years old. I held the lamp. My hands did not shake, and Isandro said afterward that mine were steadier than his, and I have decided to believe him forever.

* * *

The mechanism opened on a morning I can still smell — river water and cut grass and the particular iron scent of a thaw. The chamber behind the dummy train held a tin box, and the box held the deed, and the deed held words that rearranged a town: the orchard to the people of the town, in perpetuity, kept in trust by *the child of the clock* — and there, written into the list of keepers, restored as though it had never been scraped off any manifest, the name Nakamura.

His grandfather's name. The man who worked the orchard rows and was erased from the ledgers when it suited the family, whose ferry passage appeared on no surviving page, whose grandson climbed a tower believing history happened to other people and discovered it had been happening to him the entire time, patiently, in the dark, waiting for him to wind the clock.

People ask me sometimes — usually the older visitors, the ones with their own erasures folded away somewhere — whether it was hard, afterward. Whether the town changed. I give them the honest answer, which is: towns do not change; they accumulate. Aurelio lost his claim and his berth and his certainty, and watched the ferry carry his sister away downriver, and what he understood in that hour I will not pretend to know, because I was not standing inside him and would not wish to be. But I will say this: he never once set foot in the orchard again, and the orchard flourished, and perhaps absence is also a kind of tending.

Elena wrote. Every year, a letter, until her hands grew too unsteady and then her daughter wrote for her. She never asked to come back. She asked, every single letter, whether I remembered the dog. His name was Bravo and he is buried under the first tree in the second row, and yes, Elena, I remember the dog. I remember the smoke and the woman's voice and the promise about the trees — *the trees will keep you* — and I have spent my life discovering that the promise was not sentimental. It was practical. Trees keep you. They feed you, shade you, employ you, employ the town, hold the hillside against the rain. My mother knew what she was saying. Mothers usually do; it is the rest of us who need sixty years and a lamplight to catch up.

* * *

This morning's group has gone, waving from the gate, and the tower is quiet in the way I love best — not silent, never silent, the clock sees to that, but quiet the way a house with a heartbeat is quiet. I make my afternoon rounds. I oil the pivot on the third wheel of the striking train, because it sings a hair sharp when dry and Isandro trained me that a clockmaker's ear forgives nothing. I check the weight lines. I wind.

Winding takes two hands and one memory. The weights rise through the case, the cable hums, and I think, as I think every afternoon, of a younger pair of hands on this crank beside mine — his on the weights, mine on the escapement, the day we restarted time together after the ice broke. He was twenty-three and pretending not to be afraid. I was nine and not pretending anything, because nobody had yet taught me that fear was supposed to be hidden, and thank goodness, because he needed someone in that room who was simply, openly glad to be alive next to him.

He lived thirty more years in this tower. He died in the orchard, in October, in a chair under the first tree of the second row, with his ledger closed on his knee and his hat over his eyes as though napping, which is how I choose to report it, and the town, bless its accumulating heart, let me choose.

Before that, though — before the chair and the October — he did the thing that brings me to what I actually want to tell you, the thing I save for the end of the tour, the inheritance proper.

He wrote the ledger. A new one. On the first page, in his careful apprentice's hand, he wrote the name that had been scraped from the manifests and the deeds and the memory of the orchard: *Tomás Nakamura, orchard hand, keeper of the east rows.* And beneath it he signed his own — *Isandro Nakamura, clockmaker, no longer apprentice* — and beneath that, when I was nineteen and my hands had proven themselves on every gear in this tower, he handed me the pen and said, *your line, Mara.*

I wrote: *Mara Nakamura, keeper.*

Not Nakamura-Vidal, though Elena's letters gave me leave. Not Vidal-Nakamura, though the lawyers suggested it. Just Nakamura, chosen, claimed, the name of a man erased and a man restored and a man who forgot nothing he was told to forget. Names, I have learned, are deeds you carry on your face.

* * *

Now the light is going gold across the clockroom floor, and tomorrow's tour is tomorrow, and there is one more thing I do every evening that I have never put in any tour. I climb to the window — the clockroom window, south-facing, the one that looks down the hill over the orchard rows to the river — and I stand there while the sun goes down, and I look at the face I was told to forget.

Not mine. His.

Because that is the last thing I tell the visitors, or almost the last, the thing they repeat to their cousins so the coin gets another layer of wear: that the note said *forget her face*, and history — which I once believed happened elsewhere, until it happened to me, thoroughly, at age four, on a frozen step — history got the instruction backwards. It was never my face that mattered. It was his. The face of the man who disobeyed a note, defied a family, refused a fortune, gave away a berth, and stayed. If the town remembers one face from this whole story, let it be that one, tired and stubborn and lit by lamplight behind the dummy gears.

I keep it. Furniture of the soul, dusted daily.

Below me the river runs free, the way it has every spring since, and the trees are in blossom, white as paper waiting for writing, and the clock behind me ticks its patient tick and its faithful tock. Tomorrow I will open the doors and tell the whole story again, start to finish, and some child will find the dummy train and ask the piercing question, and I will kneel and begin: *her mother did, and her mother loved her—*

But tonight there is only the window, and the light, and a sound from the path below — footsteps on the gravel, two sets, unhurried, familiar — and I smile before I even turn, because I know that walk. I have known it for forty-seven years.

It seems my visitors for the evening have arrived early.
