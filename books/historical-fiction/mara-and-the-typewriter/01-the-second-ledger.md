## 1. The Second Ledger

The harbor smelled of tar and rotting kelp, and beneath both, faintly, of the war itself—a sweetness gone wrong that clung to the bandages, the floors, the hair of everyone who worked in the requisitioned customs house they called a hospital now. Bram Joubert had stopped noticing it months ago. You could stop noticing almost anything if you did it long enough.

She moved down the row of cots with her basin of salt water, working by the grey light that came through the tall windows facing the water. The wounded from the northern front had arrived on Tuesday—forty-one men packed into a space meant for twenty—and already three of them had gone into the ground behind the chapel without their names being written anywhere except on the slips she kept folded in her sleeve.

"Water," said the man in the fourth cot. He had no left ear anymore and spoke as though apologizing for it.

"You'll have water." She lifted his head and held the tin cup to his lips, counting his swallows the way she counted everything now—in sevens, because seven was a number the officers never questioned when it appeared on supply forms.

His file lay at the foot of the cot. *Unknown, rank unknown, regiment unconfirmed.* The clerk who processed the dead had taken to writing *unconfirmed* the way other men took to drink: automatically, gratefully, as a way of making the day shorter. Unconfirmed men did not need letters sent home. Unconfirmed men did not appear in the weekly returns. Unconfirmed men were, in the language of the victors' bureaucracy, a rounding error.

Bram set the cup down and pressed two fingers to his wrist. His pulse was steady enough. She memorized what she could see of him—the gap in the teeth on his right side, the faded anchor tattooed blue above his knuckle, the way he said *water* like a man who'd grown up somewhere riverine, maybe the delta towns—and carried him, nameless, toward the evening's work.

* * *

Night in the hospital belonged to the dying and to Bram. The night orderly was a boy of fifteen named Pell who slept sitting up in the dispensary and could be trusted to wake only for screaming. She waited until his breathing went long and even, then crossed the ward with the oil lamp turned so low it was more memory than light.

The typewriter lived inside the false back of the linen cabinet, wrapped in an oilcloth that had once covered instruments. It had come to her three years ago, in the second month of the war, smuggled in a crate of surgical thread by a quartermaster who asked no questions because she paid him in cigarettes and in the kind of silence that is its own currency. A portable thing, half its paint chipped, the letter E worn nearly smooth. It had typed through everything since.

She knelt beside the third window from the end, where the floorboard ran beneath the wall's shadow, and lifted it with the flat iron she kept for the purpose. Inside the cavity, wrapped in sacking, lay the ledger—not a bound book, which would have invited suspicion, but pages. Hundreds of them. Typed sheets in careful columns:

*Delatour, Anselm, private, delta regiment. Died of wounds, 14 March. Buried north fence, marker 31.*
*The woman called Maren, midwife, executed at Vasse Bridge. No record exists.*

Page after page. The men the clerks marked unconfirmed. The civilians the reports called *collateral losses, number indeterminate.* The ones whose names she pried out of the dying in the last hour, when a man will trade anything for one person in the world to know he existed.

Bram fed a fresh sheet into the carriage and began to type. She typed softly, muffling the keys with a strip of blanket, though she knew the sound would carry no farther than Pell's dreams. Still, three years of habit had made caution into something like prayer—performed whether or not anyone listened.

*Hollis, T., gunner. Died 9 June, gangrene. Wife's name Odile, town of Saint-Aubin. No official record.*

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the window. Outside, the harbor lay black and patient, masts knocking together like people murmuring in a queue. Somewhere out there a ship would sail soon—she'd heard the dockhands talking about it, the historians' transport, crates of documents going north to the capital where the official history was being assembled by men who had never seen a wound that didn't fit on a form.

She pushed the thought away and kept typing. There were four names tonight. Four names meant four hours of sleep, and sleep was a debt she'd learned to carry the way soldiers carried lice.

When she finished, she read the page back once, folded it into the sacking, lowered the board over it, and scattered a pinch of dust across the seam. Then she sat back on her heels in the dark and allowed herself, briefly, to be afraid—not of discovery, exactly, but of the sheer weight of what she was keeping. Three years of names. Enough paper to hang her twice over.

Then she banked the lamp, returned the typewriter to its cabinet, and went back to the ward, where the man without the ear had begun to moan in a dialect she half recognized, calling for someone named Lise.

* * *

The census taker came on Thursday, in the hour between morning dressings and the surgeons' rounds, when the hospital smelled worst and tempers ran thinnest.

Her name was Ilse Vann, according to the card she presented at the door, and she wore the grey coat of the Registry Office with the collar turned up against the harbor wind. She was younger than Bram expected—thirty perhaps, with ink-stained fingers and eyes that moved over the ward the way a clerk's eyes move over columns, quick and totalizing.

"Routine enumeration," Vann said. "Every institution in the port district. I won't take much of your time."

"Take what you need."

They walked the rows together. Bram answered the easy questions in the tone she used for officers: staffing numbers, supply consumption, the ratio of beds to patients. Vann wrote in a small neat hand, nodding, and Bram let herself believe, for almost ten minutes, that this was what it appeared to be—paperwork, the endless paperwork of a regime that mistook counting for governing.

Then, at the fifth cot, Vann paused.

"You're listed here as matron," she said, tapping the duty roster pinned to the post. "But the registry shows no surname attached to your authorization. Just *Joubert, B.* No place of registration. No kin declared." She looked up, and her voice stayed mild, administrative, almost apologetic. "How does a woman with no registered surname hold medical authority?"

Bram had prepared for this question the way she prepared for hemorrhage—with a procedure rehearsed until it was reflex.

"The war burned the parish records in my district," she said. "I registered under the matron's name when she died, with the garrison commander's approval. Ask Colonel Ferreol; he countersigned it himself."

"I have asked. Colonel Ferreol's office confirms the countersignature but has no record of the original registration." Vann closed her notebook, unhurried. "It's nothing. Irregularities are common after a war. But the new directives require me to note them all the same."

"Of course."

Vann lingered a moment longer, looking at the man in the fifth cot—at the *unconfirmed* stamped across his file—and something passed over her face too quickly for Bram to read. Pity, or recognition, or simply the fatigue of a woman who read lists of the dead for a living.

"The port records," Vann said, gathering her coat, "will be sealed within the fortnight. Everything ships for the capital. After that, corrections become very difficult." She said it lightly, as small talk, as weather. Then she was gone, her footsteps fading down the corridor toward the next institution on her list.

Bram stood among the cots with her hands still and her heart beating hard against her ribs. *Sealed.* Shipped. Crated into the official version, where the unconfirmed men dissolved into rounding errors and the woman at Vasse Bridge became nobody at all.

That night, before she touched the typewriter, Bram knelt at the loose board and did not open it. She only laid her palm flat against the wood, feeling through the grain the weight of three years of names sleeping below.

The ship, she thought. The historians' ship.

There might still be time.
