## 2. One Question Too Many

The census taker came back on a Thursday, when the rain had turned the hospital's gravel courtyard to soup and the wounded smelled of iodine and wet wool.

Bram saw her through the ward window first—a grey figure crossing the yard under a leaking umbrella, notebook clamped beneath one arm, and Bram's hands went still over the bandage she was winding. She had told herself the woman would not return. Census takers collected their counts and moved on; that was the whole architecture of the thing. They were not meant to look twice.

Ilse Vann looked twice.

"Matron Joubert." The name landed wrong in Bram's ears—*Joubert*, the surname she had borrowed from a dead woman in her second month at Harrow Point, lifted from a casualty list because it was common enough to be invisible. "I've come about the discrepancies."

"Discrepancies." Bram kept winding the bandage. A boy of maybe nineteen lay under her hands with a shattered forearm, feverish, his eyes tracking the ceiling tiles. "We're a hospital. Everything here is discrepancy."

"The death registers." Ilse set her notebook on the foot of an empty cot, opened it with a small precise gesture, as though laying out surgical instruments. "For the autumn offensive. Your facility reported eleven dead. The quartermaster's returns for supplies consumed suggest thirty-one."

Bram tied off the bandage, tucked the end flat, and only then looked up. "Supplies go missing. Cotton walks out of hospitals the way silverware walks out of hotels."

"Twenty men," Ilse said quietly, "do not walk out on stumps."

Somewhere down the ward a man began to cough—the deep wet cough that meant the sea air was already winning—and a nurse two cots away rose to sit with him. Rain ticked against the window glass. Bram became aware of how loud her own breathing sounded.

"The autumn numbers were certified by Captain Delacroix's office," Bram said. "If you have questions about certification—"

"I don't have questions for Captain Delacroix." For the first time, Ilse Vann looked directly at her, and Bram saw something in that pale, methodical face that she had not seen the first day: not suspicion exactly. Recognition. The look of one careful person measuring another. "I have questions for whoever wrote the reports. They're very good reports, Matron. Economical. The kind that say nothing in a way that says everything."

The boy on the cot moaned. Bram pressed two fingers to his wrist—pulse quick but steady—and used the motion to buy herself three seconds.

"You should speak with the chaplain about the dead," she said. "He keeps the spiritual records."

"The chaplain drowned in November." Ilse did not open her notebook. She didn't need to; Bram understood that everything worth knowing was already written somewhere behind those grey eyes. "With nine other men whose names appear on no list I can find."

The words went into Bram like a splinter driven deep. *The chapel barge.* She had typed those nine names herself, at three in the morning, her fingertips blue with cold, the smuggled typewriter muffled under three blankets while sentries walked the seawall above her. Nine men loaded onto a barge for transport to the prison hospital and never registered again. She had watched from the dispensary window as the barge sat too low in the water going out, and had not come back.

"They were transferred north," Bram said.

"To where?"

"To where transfers go. You'd know better than I. You're the one keeping count of everyone."

Something flickered across Ilse Vann's face—not quite a smile, but its shadow. She closed her notebook. "Thank you for your time, Matron. I'll be in the port district another week. The record-keeping there is, I'm told, extensive."

She left the way she had come, under the leaking umbrella, unhurried, and Bram stood in the ward with the boy's pulse still beating under her fingers and understood, with a clarity like cold water, that she had been weighed. And that the scale had not yet tipped either way.

* * *

Ilse Vann walked back along the harbor road and permitted herself, for the first time all day, to think without transcribing.

Three years she had counted the living for a government that could not be bothered to count its dead. She had been meticulous about it, too—that was the trap of being meticulous; they gave you more to count. She knew the birth registers of four parishes by heart. She could tell you how many shoemakers' widows drew rations in the eastern districts. She was, in the language of her superiors, a reliable instrument.

And instruments were not supposed to notice that a hospital matron with no registered surname held authority no matron should hold. Instruments were not supposed to pull supply manifests against death certificates and find twenty men unaccounted for. Instruments were certainly not supposed to lie awake doing arithmetic on the chapel barge.

But Ilse had grown up in a house where her father, a customs clerk, kept two sets of books—one for the revenue office, one in pencil, hidden in the lining of his coat, recording what the revenue office took from families who could not object. She had found the pencil ledger the week after he died, read every page, and then burned it, because some truths were more dangerous kept than known. She had regretted the burning ever since.

Now there was a matron at Harrow Point who wrote reports like a customs clerk's daughter—economical, exact, each omission placed with the care other people spent on facts. And somewhere in that hospital, or near it, Ilse would have wagered her ration book, there was a second accounting.

Her orders were simple: identify irregularities, report them to the Office of Vital Statistics, which forwarded them to Captain Delacroix's administration. Her orders assumed she wanted the irregularities corrected. But Ilse had learned what correction meant in this country. Correction meant the twenty missing men stayed missing, officially, forever. Correction meant the pencil ledgers burned.

She reached the census office, hung her dripping umbrella on its hook, and sat down before the blank reporting form. At the top: *Facility inspected. Findings.*

Her pen hovered.

Outside, a staff car ground through the flooded street, and a lieutenant she recognized from the port commandant's office was nailing a printed notice to the customs-house door. Ilse watched him through the window, and read the notice upside down from fifty feet away, because she had long practice reading official documents from angles their authors never intended:

*BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL AUTHORITY: ALL PORT DISTRICT RECORDS, CIVIL AND MILITARY, TO BE SEALED AND CONVEYED ABOARD TRANSPORT PER THE OFFICIAL HISTORY COMMISSION. NO FURTHER ENTRIES WILL BE ACCEPTED AFTER THE MORNING TIDE, [date].*

Five days. The tide table hung by the door; she checked it out of habit. Five days, and then every register, every manifest, every death certificate in the port district would be crated, sealed, and shipped to the capital—where the Commission would decide which pages history got to see.

Ilse looked down at her form. In the space for findings, she wrote: *Harrow Point facility examined. Records consistent within acceptable variance.* Then she signed it, blotted it, and sat for a long moment listening to the rain, feeling the shape of what she had just done—or declined to do—settle around her like a coat that fit badly.

*Consistent within acceptable variance.* Twenty men. Her father would have entered them in pencil.

* * *

Bram heard the news from Corporal Reyes, who brought it down from the port commandant's office along with a crate of dressings and a face like a funeral.

"Sealed," he said, setting the crate down. "Everything. Shipments leave with the historians' transport on the morning tide. After that, if a record isn't in the Commission's crates, it doesn't exist. Officially." He lowered his voice. "They're saying the Commission's writing the war into a single volume, Matron. One version. Authorized."

Bram thanked him and did not let her hands shake until he had gone.

Then she stood in the dispensary, among the amber bottles and the smell of carbolic, and made herself do the arithmetic. Her true ledger lay beneath a floorboard beneath the linen stores—three years of typed names, hundreds of them, the men the barge took, the men the firing squads took, the men who simply stopped appearing. None of those names existed anywhere else. When the port records were sealed, the victors' lists would become the only lists. The dead would die a second time, permanently, and this time there would be no one left to disagree.

Five days. Four, now.

She thought of Ilse Vann's grey eyes and the sentence *consistent within acceptable variance* that she could not have heard but somehow felt arriving anyway. The census taker suspected her—that was certain. What Ilse intended to do with the suspicion was not, and Bram had survived three years by refusing to bet her life on things that were not certain.

Which meant silence was finished. Silence had been a strategy once, a shell she built the typewriter inside of, and it had worked right up until a woman with a notebook started pulling threads. Now silence was just the slowest way to lose.

That night, after lights-out, Bram knelt on the cold stone of the linen stores, worked up the floorboard with a knife blade, and lifted out the ledger—oilcloth-wrapped, heavier than she remembered. She carried it to the dispensary and laid it on the table beside the typewriter, and did not yet open either. Instead she took out the tide table Reyes had left with the dressings, smoothed it flat under the lamp, and read the column of times the way other people read scripture.

Morning tide, five days hence. A transport ship. Historians' crates.

Somewhere between this floorboard and that deck, she thought, the truth would have to travel. And she had four days to find out whether the woman with the notebook was a wall in her path—

—or the one person in the port district who might hold a door open.
