## 3. The Morning Tide

The floorboard came up easier than it had gone down, three years ago, when her hands shook so badly she'd split a nail to the quick prying at the seam. Now they were steady. Steady hands had been the war's one gift to her—hands that could hold a man's jaw closed while the light left him, and not tremble until afterward.

Bram knelt in the dark of the linen store, beneath the shelf where the bandages hung in their neat, rationed rows, and lifted out the oilcloth bundle. It was lighter than she remembered. Paper always seemed heavier at night, when you imagined its weight in someone else's hands.

She unwrapped one corner just enough to see the first page. *Corporal Edda Voss, 4th Regiment, died 11 March. Not fever.* Her own typing, three years old now, the keys striking faintly crooked because she'd learned the machine by feel, in the dark, with a coat thrown over her head like a girl playing at séance. Below Voss: two hundred and eleven more names, each one pulled from a ward, a morgue list, a dying man's mouth. Each one struck from the official record by an officer's pen before the ink was dry.

And at the center of it, threaded through every omission like wire through meat: **Colonel Aurel Sann**. The man who signed the sanitizations. The man whose signature she had copied, name by name, into the margins beside the dead he'd erased.

She wrapped the ledger again and pressed it flat against her chest, under her apron, where it made her look thick-bodied, matronly, invisible. Then she stood in the doorway of the linen store and listened to the hospital breathe—the wet rattle from the second ward, the boots of the night orderly on the stairs, the harbor foghorn sounding once across the water like something clearing its throat.

The ship sailed on the morning tide. Six hours.

* * *

The curfew bell rang at eleven, and Bram went out at half past.

She knew the patrol route better than the men who walked it—she'd typed their rotations herself, in the victors' ledger, in her neat administrative hand, because nobody suspected a nurse of reading anything but charts. The sentries crossed the customs square on the quarter hour, paused fourteen minutes at the wine merchant's where the sergeant kept a mistress, then doubled back along the quay. Fourteen minutes. She'd counted them for a month, counting being the other thing she did instead of sleeping.

The city after curfew was a city of held breath. Shuttered windows. A dog trotting ahead of her with the confidence of a resident. Moonlight lay on the cobbles like spilled milk, and her shadow moved long and strange beside her, carrying its secret against its ribs.

At the mouth of Tanner's Row a lantern swung up out of the dark and she flattened into a doorway, heart going hard against the oilcloth, against the paper, as if the names themselves could feel her fear. The patrol passed close enough that she could smell the lantern oil, hear one soldier complaining about his feet. Then dark again, and she moved.

Ilse Vann was waiting at the corner by the chandlery, exactly where she'd said she would be, standing in the lee of the wall with her census satchel still slung over her shoulder as though she'd come straight from work, which perhaps she had.

"You came," Ilse said.

"You told me the historians' crates go aboard tonight," Bram said. "You told me there would be no manifest check after midnight."

"I told you a great many things this week." Ilse's face was pale in the moonlight, unreadable, the way it had been since the day she'd sat across from Bram in the requisitioned ward and asked, too gently, why a woman with no registered surname held medical authority. "I could have reported you after the first interview. I've had three days to do it."

"Why haven't you?"

"Because I keep records too." Something moved behind Ilse's eyes—not warmth, nothing so simple; recognition, maybe. Two women who counted things. "My brother is on your list. I checked. I went home the night we spoke and I checked the name against what my office holds, and your version has a date of death and his has a blank. Your version says he existed."

Bram said nothing. There was nothing to say that the ledger hadn't already said better.

"So." Ilse stepped aside, gesturing down toward the quay, where the transport *Meridian* rode at anchor with her running lights burning low and yellow. On the dock, a crane stood idle over a stack of crates stenciled with the crest of the Commission of Historical Record. "The hold is open until the tide. The crate with the broken lid is third from the rail. And Bram—" Ilse caught her arm, grip fierce through the sleeve, "—if they take you, I never spoke to you. My report will say I found nothing. Can you live with that? Being disowned by the only witness you have?"

"I've been invisible for three years," Bram said. "One more person failing to see me won't kill me."

It wasn't entirely a lie. She let Ilse believe it was true, and Ilse let herself believe it, and that was the last mercy either of them had time for.

* * *

The gangway watch was a boy of maybe nineteen, yawning over a brazier, and Bram went past him in the gray uniform smock of a laundry contractor, head down, arms full of a linen sack with the ledger sewn into its false bottom. He looked at her the way people had looked at her for three years: without looking at all. Invisibility was a craft. She was its master, and this was her final exhibition.

The hold smelled of pitch and cold iron. Crates rose around her in the dark like furniture in a dead man's house, and she found the third one from the rail by running her fingers along the stenciling—Commission crest, catalog number, the word *ANNEXED CORRESPONDENCE*—until she found the crack where the lid had sprung. Inside: folders, bundles, ribbon-tied correspondence, the victors' history packed for shipment like so much cargo.

Bram worked fast. She unsewed the false bottom, drew out the ledger, and slid it down between two bundles of dispatches, face down, its crooked little letters hidden against the wood. Two hundred and twelve names, settling into the official record like sediment into a riverbed. She had imagined this moment a hundred times and it had always felt like triumph. It felt like burial. It felt like laying flowers on a grave she couldn't visit.

Then the hatch above her opened and lamplight fell down the ladder, and Colonel Aurel Sann began to descend.

She knew his voice before she saw his face—three years of hearing it across wards and offices, giving orders for beds to be cleared, lists to be revised, numbers to be corrected downward. He came down the rungs slowly, a lamp in one hand, an aide behind him, inspecting his cargo the way a man inspects a coffin he isn't sure is sealed.

Bram pressed herself into the gap between the third crate and the hull, pulling the empty linen sack against her, making herself small, making herself *nothing*. The lamp swept past. Swept back. Stopped.

"What's that?" Sann said.

"A sack, sir," the aide said. "Laundry contractor must have left it."

There was a silence in which the whole world narrowed to the width of a canvas bag. Bram watched Sann's boots turn toward her hiding place, watched the lamplight crawl up the crate beside her cheek—and in that crawling light she felt the fear transmute, the way ore goes in dull and comes out shining. Let him find her. Let him open the crate. Two hundred and twelve names were already inside it, and names, once written, were a kind of fact, and facts were the one thing Sann's whole apparatus could not unmake. He could shoot her. He could not shoot the truth twice.

"It's nothing," Sann said at last. "Check the manifests are dry and come up. The tide won't wait."

His boots receded. The ladder creaked. The hatch swallowed the light, and Bram stood alone in the dark with her hammering heart and her empty hands, and found that she was smiling—smiling the way you smile at a funeral, grief and defiance in the same expression.

Above her, through the deck planking, she heard the mooring lines begin to come loose. The *Meridian* shivered like a waking animal. The morning tide was moving, and somewhere below the waterline, wedged among the victors' papers, the dead were going to sea.

Bram climbed the ladder into the gray beginning of dawn—and saw, at the foot of the gangway, waiting with her census satchel and her patient, terrible eyes, Ilse Vann.
