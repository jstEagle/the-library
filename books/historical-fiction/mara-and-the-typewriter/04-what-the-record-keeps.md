## 4. What the Record Keeps

Ilse Vann stood at the harbor rail long after the transport had cleared the breakwater, watching its smoke thin into a sky the color of unwashed linen. The ship carried crates of official records—census rolls, hospital inventories, casualty reports bound in government twine—and somewhere among them, folded into a historian's crate of correspondence, pages she had never read but knew existed. She had seen Bram Joubert's hands shake as she'd handed over the last bundle. She had seen her eyes when she said *they're all in there*. Not the eyes of a woman committing a crime. The eyes of a woman setting down a weight she had carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy.

The tide took the ship south. Ilse turned back toward the customs house to write her report.

The form was standard: *Report of Irregular Personnel, Port District.* Name unknown; female; serving as nurse of unregistered status at Requisitioned Hospital No. 3 for a period exceeding three years; no surname on record; answers to "Bram." She wrote it in her careful census hand, each letter upright and blameless. A woman like that would be found, eventually. Registered, reassigned, or worse. Ilse could not prevent that. But the second question on the form asked whether the subject was suspected of document falsification or theft of state records.

She held her pen above the line for a long moment. Outside, gulls argued over the fish market refuse, and a soldier laughed at something down by the cranes, and the ordinary morning went on as if history were a clean thing.

*No,* she wrote. *Subject's duties appear confined to medical care.*

It was not loyalty to Bram Joubert. She barely knew her. It was loyalty to something older than the regime and younger than grief: the idea that a name written down is a candle that cannot be unlit. Ilse had spent eleven years counting people for governments that preferred them uncounted. She knew exactly what a ledger was worth, and what it cost, and she would not be the one who priced it out of existence.

She sealed the report, filed it with the port clerk—a dull man who stamped things without reading them—and walked out into the noise of the harbor knowing she had just committed the smallest treason of her life, and that no one would ever thank her for it, and that this was how most decent acts were shaped: small, unsigned, and gone by evening.

* * *

Bram burned the spare pages at dawn, two miles inland, in a ditch behind a burned-out granary.

She had meant to do it at the hospital, but there had been no time—the curfew patrol, the crates, the officer whose voice she still heard in her sleep, saying *papers* in that flat, bored tone while she stood in the dark hold with a historian's blanket over her shoulders and forty-one typed names against her ribs. She had escaped the ship at the pilot station before it made open water, dropped into a supply skiff, and rowed ashore with hands that did not stop shaking until the sun came up.

Now the spare pages—the carbons, the drafts, the practice sheets where she'd learned the typewriter's temper—curled and blackened in a small fire fed with straw. She watched the ink lift off the paper in blisters. Each page held names. Not the true ledger's names—those were aboard the transport now, beyond anyone's reach, riding south inside a crate marked *CORRESPONDENCE, HISTORICAL COMMISSION*—but echoes of them. Duplicates. Evidence. Things that could hang her twice over.

The fire ate them without ceremony. That was the thing about burning, Bram thought. History got ceremonies; memory got ditches.

When the last corner flaked to ash she kicked dirt over it, shouldered her bag, and walked the rest of the way to the inland registry office at Vellacourt, arriving with the morning queue of displaced persons: farmers with burned deeds, widows with children asleep against their hips, men missing arms who stood very straight anyway. The clerk behind the desk had a face like wet cardboard and did not look up.

"Name?"

Bram had rehearsed this. Three years of invisibility had taught her that a lie holds best when it sits close to the truth.

"Mara Joubert," she said. Her mother's name. Let the dead vouch for the living.

"Surname registered?"

"Yes."

"Trade?"

"Nurse."

The clerk stamped a card and slid it across. Just like that. Eleven seconds to become someone else. She picked up the card—*MARA JOUBERT, MEDICAL PERSONNEL, ASSIGNMENT: DISTRICT INFIRMARY, HARROW FEN*—and felt nothing she had expected to feel. No relief. No fear. Only a strange lightness, like a coat taken off after a long winter, and the cold underneath it that was also freedom.

Harrow Fen was four days' walk and a day by cart, a marsh country of peat cutters and fever, where no one would ask about the war because everyone there had lost it too. The infirmary needed hands. Hands were the one credential no regime could revoke from her.

On the cart north, wedged between a seed merchant and a woman selling salt fish, Bram let herself think about the ship. By now it would be past the capes. By tomorrow, in some city she would never see, a historian would open a crate and find pages typed in the small even hand of a woman who did not officially exist, and he would read names that appeared nowhere else in the world: Aldous Renk, ferryman. The Pell twins, aged nine. Sergeant Ivo Marsh, who died carrying water. Forty-one the first night, hundreds since. All of them real again. All of them *kept*.

She had imagined this moment for three years—imagined triumph, imagined weeping. Instead she felt the particular ache of a thing finished: the emptiness after a letter is sent, after a grave is filled, after you say goodbye to someone through glass. The names were safe, and they were gone, and she would never know what became of them. Whether they'd be printed or shelved or lost in some archive flood fifty years hence. She had done everything a single person could do, and the rest belonged to strangers, to luck, to time.

The cart jolted through a village where children ran alongside begging the seed merchant for sweets, and their laughter was so ordinary it hurt.

"You going far?" the fish woman asked.

"Harrow Fen."

"Work?"

"Nursing."

The woman nodded and looked back at her fish. In three years of war, Bram thought, that was the longest honest conversation she'd had.

That night, at a waystation, she sat by the common stove while others slept, and on the back of her new registration card she wrote, in pencil, in a hand smaller than her typing, a single line:

*M. Joubert keeps no ledgers.*

Then, beneath it, because silence had been her whole life and she was done pretending it hadn't cost anything:

*But someone, somewhere, is reading our names tonight.*

She looked at the words a long time. Then she rubbed them out with her thumb—not entirely; the ghost of the letters stayed, pressed into the card, legible only if you knew to look—and lay down on the bench with her bag under her head, and slept without dreaming for the first time since the war began.

In the morning she walked north into the fens, a woman with a new name and no past, carrying nothing but her hands.

And far to the south, riding at anchor in a foreign harbor, a crate waited in a customs shed to be opened—patient as a seed, holding every name the victors had tried to bury, and one more besides, penciled on the last page in a tired, steady hand, the only signature she would ever allow herself:

*Recorded by a nurse. She was here.*
