## 1. The Ledger of Small Names

The cold came through the canvas walls like a debt collector, patient and indifferent, and Cyrus Pemberton had learned to work inside it the way a fish works inside ice.

She moved down the row of cots with her basin and her rag, wringing out water that steamed faintly even as it chilled. The hospital at Verrenholt had been a granary once, then a chapel, then nothing at all until the army remembered it existed and hung its lanterns among the rafters. Now it held forty beds, thirty-one occupied, and the smell of carbolic fighting a losing war against gangrene and woodsmoke.

"Turn your arm, Halloran," she said to the boy in the third cot. He was nineteen if he was anything, his shoulder wrapped so thick he looked like he was wearing a second man under his shirt.

He turned it. He flinched. She cleaned the wound without apology, because apology would have cost them both something they didn't have.

"You've got gentle hands, Pemberton," Halloran said, the way they all said it eventually. "For a fella."

"I wash them often." She wrung the rag. "Gentle costs nothing."

It was a good answer. It was one of perhaps forty good answers she kept ready, worn smooth as river stones from use. Her voice sat low where she'd trained it to sit; her hands were large for a woman's but not impossibly so, and the binding beneath her uniform had become such a fact of her body that some mornings she forgot it was there until the ache reminded her — a dull, faithful pain, like a dog sleeping against her ribs.

Three years since she'd taken her dead brother's name. Thomas Pemberton had died at Harrow Ford with his boots on and his papers clean, and Mara had buried him in everything except the uniform, which fit her better than grief did. The regiment had accepted "Cyrus Pemberton," orderly and nurse's assistant, without a second glance, because armies do not look closely at what they already believe they see.

The officers' ledger lay open on the surgeon's table across the ward: a fat, official book with a brass clasp, recording the day's dead in the handwriting of Lieutenant Sorrel, who wrote numbers the way other men signed checks — quickly, and without looking back.

*Eleven deceased this date,* Sorrel had written. *Causes various.*

Eleven. Cyrus had counted thirteen herself. Two men had died in the night whose names would never travel further than this room — a Welshman called Prosser who'd bled out before dawn, and an old sutler's boy no one could even name, brought in off the road frozen harder than the ground would take him. The officers' ledger did not record men without units. It did not record deserters, or prisoners, or boys too young to have been issued papers. It recorded, in short, only the deaths that were convenient to count.

That night, after the last lamp was trimmed and Sorrel's snores rattled the partition, Cyrus knelt by the iron stove with a candle stub cupped in her palm and opened her own ledger — a slim book bound in grey cloth, small enough to hide in a boot, hidden now beneath a loose floorboard with her brother's Bible and the letter from their mother she had never answered.

She wrote in a hand much smaller than her own:

*Josiah Prosser, 2nd Rifles, Welsh by speech, died 14th of the frost month, wounds to the belly. He spoke of a farm near Brecon and asked twice for water I did not have.*

*Unnamed boy, sutler's follower, approx. twelve years. Frozen. No one claimed him. I gave him Prosser's blanket, as Prosser had no more use for it.*

The candle guttered. She shielded the flame with her hand and watched the wax pool in the creases of her glove, and she thought, as she thought every night, that this was either the most important thing she did or the most useless — that somewhere, someday, a historian might read these pages and know that two extra men had died on the fourteenth of the frost month, and that their deaths had mattered to exactly one person, who had written them down by stove-light while the war went on winning without them.

Her hands were steady. They always were, at this hour. It was the daytime that shook her.

* * *

Elias Vane came into the ward at six, as he always did, bringing the smell of snow and spirits of ammonia with him.

He was not a handsome man in the way that made women in towns turn their heads; he was handsome in the way that made wounded men trust him — level-eyed, deliberate, with a scar splitting one eyebrow and hands that never hurried. He was the only surgeon left at Verrenholt who still took the time to learn names, and he used them constantly, deliberately, the way other men used brandy.

"Pemberton." His gaze found her across the ward the way it always did — first, and a half-second too long. "How is our Halloran?"

"Fever's down half a degree. He'll keep the arm."

"He'll keep the arm because you found the pocketing when Sorrel missed it." Elias set down his bag and began unwinding his scarf. "I heard."

"Sorrel has a great many ledgers to keep."

"Yes," Elias said, and looked at her again — that look, that particular look, that had been arriving more frequently these past months and staying longer each time. It rested on her face as though reading fine print. "Yes, he does."

She turned away to fold bandages, which was safe, because folding bandages required her eyes and gave her an excuse not to return his. This was the arithmetic of her life: every glance she permitted was a risk compounded; every glance she refused was a small cruelty she paid for later, alone, in the dark, with interest.

Because the truth — the truth she kept below the floorboard with everything else she could not afford — was that Elias Vane saw her. Not Cyrus Pemberton, nurse's assistant; not the flat-voiced, careful-walking fiction she had built plank by plank over three winters. *Her.* Whatever it was that lived underneath, he saw it, the way a dog scents smoke long before any human nose knows the house is burning. And she could not decide whether that was the most frightening thing in the war or the only thing keeping her warm.

"Vane." She kept her voice level, professional, pitched low. "The boy in the last cot — the sutler's boy. He'll need burying today. There's no unit to claim him."

Elias was quiet a moment. When she glanced over, he was watching her with the ledger-clasp expression he wore during hard operations — total attention, nothing wasted.

"There won't be a record," he said. "You know how it goes."

"I know how it goes." She smoothed a fold that needed no smoothing. "Someone should know anyway."

"Someone should," he agreed softly. "Someone usually does. I've wondered, sometimes, who that someone is here."

The words landed in the ward like dropped glass. Cyrus kept folding. Fold, tuck, smooth. Her heartbeat was a drum in her throat.

"I'm sure I couldn't say, sir."

"No," he said. "Of course not." But he smiled then, faintly, at the bandages rather than at her, and it was not a smile that believed her, and it was not a smile that pressed her either. It was a smile that simply waited, patient as the cold, for whatever she might one day choose to tell him.

They worked through the morning side by side, amputating Halloran's arm after all — the fever turning, turning — and Cyrus held the boy's shoulders while Elias worked, and afterward Halloran wept and thanked them both, and Cyrus stepped outside into the yard to be sick behind the woodpile, quietly, the way she did everything.

When she came back, Elias was waiting by the door. Snow in his dark hair. Something folded in his hand.

"You dropped this," he said, "in the ward. By Halloran's cot."

It was a scrap of paper. Her stomach dropped through three floors of her body before she saw what it was — only a bandage inventory, her own cramped writing, harmless. Harmless. She took it from him and their fingers touched through the wool of her gloves, and neither of them pulled away fast enough, and both of them noticed.

"Thank you, sir."

"Cyrus," he said — his first use of the Christian name in eleven months, delivered quietly, without rank, without witnesses. "Whatever it is you carry in that trunk of yours — whatever it is you write at night when you think the ward's asleep—" He stopped himself. Breathed out white air. "Be careful. That's all. Be careful."

He walked off toward surgery before she could answer, and she stood in the doorway of the freezing granary with the harmless scrap of paper burning in her glove, understanding two things at once and unable to reconcile them: that her secret was no longer entirely hers, and that the man who knew of it had chosen, this morning, to protect it rather than report it.

That night she wrote no new names. There were none to write — the guns had gone quiet, a rare mercy — and instead she sat by the stove with her ledger closed on her knees and listened to the wind worry the canvas, and told herself the quiet was rest.

It was not rest. Quiet, in her experience, was only the war drawing breath.

Near midnight, hoofbeats came up the frozen road — too fast for a supply wagon, too reckless for an officer riding sober. A courier, she thought, listening. Some poor devil with dispatches, riding through the dark to keep someone's paperwork honest.

The hoofbeats stopped at the gate. There was shouting, then no shouting, then footsteps in the ward — uneven, dragging, desperate — and a voice, cracked nearly beyond recognition, gasping for the surgeon.

And a fist, hammering weakly against the doorframe, holding something sealed in wax.
