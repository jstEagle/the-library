## 2. A Letter Under Seal

The courier came in on a stretcher at dusk, when the lanterns were being lit and the frost was already climbing the canvas walls like a slow white tide.

Cyrus had learned to read men by the way they were carried. The ones who screamed were often the ones who lived; the quiet ones worried her more. This man said nothing at all. His eyes moved across the ward's low ceiling as though memorizing it, and his hands—cracked, black with road dirt, a soldier's hands but not this army's uniform—lay folded over his stomach as if guarding something there.

She cut away his coat and understood why.

Three wounds. A musket ball through the meat of his shoulder, a saber gash along the ribs that some field surgeon had sewn crooked and hastily, and a third hole, low in the belly, that told her everything she needed to know about how the night would end. Belly wounds did their arithmetic slowly. She had watched that sum worked out enough times to know the total before the last figure was entered.

"Water," he said. His voice was gravel and distance. "Please."

She gave him water and laudanum and did not lie to him about either. He drank greedily, then caught her wrist with a grip that had no business being so strong in a man so far gone.

"Sister." His eyes found hers, and in them was a desperation she had seen only twice before—in men who knew they were dying and had one thing left unfinished. "You're Pemberton. They said—the orderly said—Pemberton keeps the records."

"I keep the records," she said carefully.

"Not theirs." His grip tightened. "Yours. I was told. Find Pemberton. *Find* Pemberton." He laughed then, a wet, terrible sound. "I've ridden four days looking for a name."

Behind her, the ward went on breathing—men coughing, moaning, calling out for mothers in three languages. Elias Vane was forty feet away at the surgery table, his sleeves rolled to the elbow, his back to her, bent over an amputation with the steady economy of motion she had watched for two years without ever letting herself watch too long. If he turned now—

"What is your name?" she asked the courier.

"Hale. Josiah Hale." He pulled her closer, and the smell of him rose up: blood, horse, woodsmoke, something burned underneath the rest that made the hair on her arms lift. "I carried dispatches for Colonel Marrow's staff. Until Marrow stopped needing couriers."

"Stop talking. Save your strength."

"No." He said it flatly, and something in his face reminded her of her brother—not in feature but in certainty, that particular stubbornness of a person who has decided how a thing will be and will not be argued from it. "Listen to me, Sister, because I haven't got the strength to say it twice. There's a letter. Sealed. In my coat lining—I paid a woman in Verren to stitch it there. You'll feel the wax through the cloth."

Her hands went still over his wound.

"A village called Sainte-Aubin surrendered in October. They rang the church bell. They put out white linen on every door. And Ashe—" his breath hitched, and she pressed down harder on the dressing, working, keeping her face the smooth blank mask the war had taught her, "—Ashe burned it anyway. Every soul. The officers wrote it up as an engagement. Enemy action. The historians are already—" Another hitch. "There's a man writing the official history. Ashe's cousin or his patron's creature, I don't know which. He's been buying testimony. Burning letters. The dead can't argue, Sister, but paper can."

"Paper hangs people too," she heard herself say.

"Yes," Hale said, and smiled with bloody teeth. "That's rather the point."

* * *

He died an hour past midnight, and she took the letter from his coat before the burial detail came.

She did it in the supply tent, alone, by the light of a single candle shielded behind a tin plate. Her fingers found the seam of the lining where a woman in Verren had stitched well—small, even stitches, the work of someone who had done such things before and charged accordingly. Inside lay a packet wrapped in oilcloth, and inside that, a letter under a colonel's seal, and beneath the seal, in a hand Cyrus recognized from a thousand requisitions and casualty returns as Colonel Ashe's own:

*...the village having surrendered its arms, no further resistance being anticipated, the example to be made complete...*

*Complete.* The word sat on the page like a fly on milk.

Below the order itself were appended depositions—three signatures, sworn statements, men who had stood at the tree line and watched the flames take the church with the villagers locked inside, because locking them inside had also been ordered. Two of the three signatures belonged to men whose names she knew. One of them had died in her ward in November, delirious, weeping about bells.

Cyrus sat very still in the cold tent and listened to the wind worry the guy-ropes, and felt the ground shift beneath the whole architecture of her life.

She had spent years building herself into a shadow. Mara Pemberton had died with her brother; Cyrus Pemberton was a function, a pair of competent hands, a name on a duty roster that no one looked at twice. Invisibility was armor. It was the entire strategy of her survival: be useful, be unremarkable, be no one. And here was a sealed letter in her hands that could pull down a colonel—and the only way to keep it safe was to become, overnight, the most dangerous person in the regiment.

If Ashe learned the courier had reached the hospital—if anyone searched her trunk—they would find the letter. And beside it, wrapped in the same oilcloth, her ledger: four hundred names in her careful hand, including men recorded as deserters who had died loyal, including the two orderlies who had noticed her too closely and whom she had written into the book anyway because the dead deserved their true names even when the truth endangered the living. Four hundred lines of evidence that Cyrus Pemberton was exactly what the official history could not afford to contain.

They would not court-martial her. Court-martials left records. They would simply find her gone one morning, another name the ledger of small names would never receive.

* * *

Elias found her there. Of course he did—he always knew where she was, which was the trouble with Elias Vane, the beautiful insoluble problem of him. He ducked through the tent flap with a cup of coffee he'd clearly brought for her and stopped when he saw her face.

"You look," he said slowly, "as though you've been reading your own death warrant."

The candle guttered between them. She weighed lying against him and found, not for the first time, that she didn't have the strength.

"The courier," she said. "Josiah Hale. He brought me something tonight."

"His papers?"

"His evidence." She turned the packet in her hands once, like a card player showing and concealing. "Elias, if you walk out of this tent, you can still not know. That's worth something in the next month. Not knowing might save your life."

He set the coffee down on the supply crate. He crossed the small space between them, and instead of taking the letter, he took her hands—cold fingers around colder ones—and she let him, and hated how much she let him.

"Cyrus." Her false name in his mouth, worn soft as river stone from two years of saying it. "I have spent this whole war pretending not to see what I see. It's the worst discipline I know. Don't ask me to practice it now."

So she showed him. The order. The depositions. The word *complete*. He read by candlelight with the stillness of a man holding a live shell, and when he finished, his jaw was working and his eyes were bright and terrible.

"They'll come for it," he said. "Not may. Will. Ashe can't leave a loose courier alive and a loose letter unfound—it's already the only thread that can unravel him. When they trace Hale's route, it ends here. At our hospital. At your trunk."

"I know."

"And what's in the trunk besides that—"

"My ledger," she said quietly. "Every name they've erased. Including mine, at the end of it, if I'm ever able to write it honestly."

He was silent a long moment. Outside, somewhere toward the officers' quarters, a door slammed, and both of them flinched like startled birds.

"At dawn," Elias said at last, "Major Corven arrives with orders to inspect the hospital. All stores, all correspondence, all personnel effects." He met her eyes. "Corven reports directly to Ashe."

The candle flame leaned hard sideways, as though something had opened a door somewhere in the dark.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

Elias looked at the letter, then at her, and she saw the exact moment the decision arrived behind his eyes—saw duty and love weigh each other in his face and felt, absurdly, guiltily, glad.

"Until dawn," he said.
