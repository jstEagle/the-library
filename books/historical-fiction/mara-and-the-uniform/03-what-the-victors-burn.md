## 3. What the Victors Burn

The armistice came to them first as a sound: the guns falling silent at dawn, one by one, like a choir forgetting its song.

Cyrus stood in the surgery doorway with a basin of bloody water in her hands and listened to the quiet spread across the valley. Four winters of thunder, and now this — a silence so complete she could hear the frost creaking on the canvas. Behind her, Elias looked up from the table where he'd been stitching a boy's shoulder, his needle hovering, as if the silence itself required suturing.

"It's over," someone said in the ward.

It was not over. Cyrus knew that with a certainty that sat cold in her stomach. Wars ended for soldiers; for paper they had only just begun. Somewhere in the officers' tents, historians were already sharpening their pens, and somewhere in her trunk beneath three folded shifts and a spare bandage roll lay a ledger with two hundred and eleven names that no official record would ever carry.

She went back to work. There was always work. But all morning her ears strained past the moans of the wounded for the sound she dreaded most: hooves on frozen mud, coming up the hospital road from the direction of Colonel Ashe's headquarters.

They came at dusk.

* * *

Elias heard them too — six riders, then the jingle of a dozen more dismounting beyond the lantern-light. He was elbow-deep in a thoracic dressing when Captain Rourke of Ashe's staff pushed through the tent flap without saluting, bringing the cold in with him.

"Surgeon Vane." Rourke's breath fogged. "Colonel's compliments. We're conducting an inventory of regimental property before demobilization. All correspondence, all documents, all personal papers of the deceased are to be catalogued."

"The deceased," Elias said carefully, "belong to their families."

"The dead belong to the Crown." Rourke smiled without warmth. "You'll find we're very thorough. Begin tonight if you can manage it. The Colonel dislikes loose ends."

He let the flap fall. Through the gap, Elias watched the soldiers fan out toward the wards, toward the stores, toward the row of officer trunks stacked by the mess — and toward the small lean-to where the nurses kept their belongings, because nurses, in the arithmetic of Colonel Ashe, were barely persons at all, and barely-persons' trunks could be opened without ceremony.

Cyrus was across the compound. And her trunk held more than shifts.

* * *

Cyrus felt them coming the way animals feel weather. She had the ledger out of the trunk and against her body before the first boot crunched the frost outside the lean-to — sewn now into the lining of her coat, a lumpy seam along her ribs that she prayed read as mending and not as contraband. On the trunk's bottom, where the ledger had lain for two years, she left a hymnal and a bundle of letters from a mother who was probably dead; every soldier carried such things, and even thorough men grew bored of grief.

The soldier who searched her space was nineteen and apologetic about it. He turned over her spare boots, sniffed at her tin of carbolic, and blushed when he touched the folded shifts.

"Beg pardon, Nurse."

"You're only doing what you're ordered," she said, and watched him flinch, because kindness from the searched is worse than anger. He moved on to the next cot. She buttoned her coat to the throat and walked, slowly, deliberately, toward the surgery, where the lantern still burned, where Elias would be waiting, where the second copy — the one they'd begun two nights ago in his careful surgeon's hand — lay hidden inside the binding of Gray's *Anatomy* on the reference shelf.

Her ribs ached where the ledger pressed. Two hundred and eleven names. Tonight she would make it two hundred and twelve copies' worth, or die trying, because the war was ending, and what the victors burned, no one would ever miss — except the ones whose names were written down.

* * *

Elias met her eyes across the surgery and understood everything in a single glance, which was the strange fluency they'd built without ever speaking of it. He sent the orderly to the far ward with an invented complaint about a man's breathing. He closed the flap. Then he took Gray's *Anatomy* down from the shelf, slit the binding with a scalpel, and drew out forty pages of copied names, each attested in the margin with the date, the place, the witness — because a list was gossip, but an attested list was evidence.

"How much remains?" he asked.

"Sixty-three names. The winter entries." Her voice was steady; her hands were not. "And the letter must be copied too. The original stays sewn in my coat — if they take me, they take the proof with me, and I'll burn it before I surrender it."

"You will not burn anything." He said it quietly, the way he said the worst things. "If they take you, you give them nothing to find and nothing to fear. The copies leave this hospital tomorrow whether you do or not."

"With whom? Every road out of here has a checkpoint."

"With me." He set the pages on the table, weighted them with a mortar, and lit a second lantern. "Sit. Write. I'll stand watch and forge your travel papers while you work. You can pass as nobody's wife going home to nobody's farm. I've written enough death notices to know how officials phrase things."

She almost laughed — a cracked, exhausted sound. "You've thought about this."

"I've thought about little else since the courier died in your arms." He pulled a blank requisition form from his desk drawer, held it to the light. "Sit down, Mara."

The name landed between them like a dropped instrument. She hadn't heard it aloud in four years. She looked at him, and he looked back, and neither of them apologized, and after a moment she sat and uncapped the ink.

* * *

They worked until the candles guttered — she copying, he forging, the scratch of two pens the only sound in the lantern-lit surgery. It was nearly midnight when Major Halloway found them.

Elias heard the horses late; the snow had muffled everything. The tent flap lifted and there stood the divisional medical director himself, unescorted, unpowdered, his face gray with something that wasn't quite illness.

"Vane." Halloway glanced at Cyrus, dismissed her as furniture, and produced a sealed envelope. "Congratulations are in order. Chief Surgeon of the garrison hospital at Verrenholt, effective upon demobilization. A considerable practice. A considerable future." He laid the envelope on the table — directly atop the copied ledger pages, and did not look down at them, which told Elias everything. "There is one condition. Colonel Ashe requires assistance identifying a member of this hospital's staff. A person of interest in the matter of certain missing correspondence."

The silence stretched. In the lantern light Elias could see the sweat on Halloway's upper lip, the way his hand trembled slightly on the table's edge. The major was not threatening him. The major was begging him, in the language of rank, to make this easy for everyone.

"And if I cannot assist?" Elias asked.

"Then I'm afraid the appointment goes elsewhere, and the inquiry into this establishment's irregularities proceeds without a friend at its head." Halloway's gaze flicked — once, briefly — to Cyrus, and away. "Think on it until dawn. That's all the time the war leaves any of us."

He left. The flap settled. Cyrus had not stopped writing; her pen had not paused; but her shoulders had gone rigid as splinted bone.

"Elias." She didn't look up. "Don't answer him. Don't even think—"

"Cyrus." He said her false name gently, and then, because the night had already taken everything else, her true one: "Mara." Her pen finally stilled. "I have spent four years learning to save lives by the book. Every book I was ever given says duty first. Every one of them is wrong about what duty is." He drew the blank commission paper toward him and began, with a surgeon's precise hand, to write her a new name onto a new life. "Verrenholt is east. The neutral city is north, past the river, three days' ride. At dawn you'll be a widow named Mrs. Mara Pemberton, traveling to her sister's. At dawn I'll be a fool."

"You'll be ruined."

"I'll be free." He signed the forged pass, blotted it, and looked up at her across the lantern light — at the woman the war had hidden inside a dead brother's uniform, holding sixty-three names that history wanted dead. "Now listen carefully, because we have perhaps five hours, and Ashe's men search again at first light. Here is how you get out."

Outside, the snow began to fall, soft and enormous, erasing the road north even as he described it — and somewhere down the valley, in the direction of headquarters, a single window still burned, where Colonel Ashe sat awake, waiting for dawn, and for a name.
