## 4. Mara Pemberton, Attested

The city of Verenholm did not know there was a war on. That was the first thing Mara noticed, stepping down from the mail coach in a widow's black that smelled of another woman's grief — lavender and mothballs and the faint ghost of someone's kitchen. The cobblestones were dry. The shop windows were full. A boy sold roasted chestnuts on the corner as though boys had nothing better to do than sell chestnuts, and for a moment she stood very still in the crowd and let herself be ordinary.

In her satchel, wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, lay the ledger. Two hundred and eleven names in her small, careful hand — names the officers' reports had folded away like inconvenient letters. Beneath it, sealed twice over with Elias's wax and her own thumbprint pressed into the cooling tallow, the courier's letter: Colonel Ashe, Third Brigade, ordering the village of Saint-Aubin burned with its surrendered civilians inside, and the order countersigned by men whose signatures would hang them all.

She had worn black because widows were invisible. She had learned invisibility from masters — from three years of binding her chest and lowering her voice and answering to a dead brother's name. Cyrus Pemberton had been a good nurse, a quiet one, the kind officers forgot existed until they needed him. Cyrus Pemberton had kept his head down and his ledgers hidden and had loved a surgeon he could never let himself love properly, not while the disguise held.

Cyrus Pemberton was about to die, and Mara Pemberton was going to kill him.

* * *

The press office of *The Verenholm Dispatch* occupied the third floor of a building that smelled of ink and cold tea. She climbed the stairs slowly, letting the widow's weeds do their work; two clerks glanced up, marked her as no threat, and looked back at their columns of figures.

The editor was a heavy man named Corrigan with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that missed nothing, which was precisely why she had chosen him. She set the oilcloth bundle on his desk without ceremony.

"My husband was a correspondent," she said. "He died at the front. Before he died, he asked me to bring you this."

Corrigan unwrapped the ledger the way a man handles something that might be armed. He read for a long time. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, a press began to turn, printing tomorrow's lies or tomorrow's truths, whichever sold better.

"Where did your husband get this?" he said at last, not unkindly.

"He wrote it himself." Her voice did not waver. She had practiced this in coach yards and inn corridors for nine days. "Every name. He was meticulous about the ones the army left out."

"And the letter?"

"His last acquisition. He paid for it with his life."

It was a clean lie, well-constructed, and it cost her almost nothing to tell, which frightened her more than any search party ever had. She watched Corrigan weigh it — the widow, the bundle, the timing. Tomorrow morning the victors' historians would publish their account of the war's final winter: honorable engagements, regrettable accidents, no massacres, no omissions, no small names. She had seen the advance sheets smuggled out in a diplomat's trunk. She knew exactly how many hours remained.

"If I print this," Corrigan said, "there will be an inquiry. There may be a trial. And whoever gave you these papers will be hunted by every intelligence officer on the continent." He looked up. "You understand what you're carrying, madam."

"I understand what I'm carrying better than anyone alive," Mara said.

Something shifted in his face — recognition, perhaps, of the thing under the thing. He pulled a fresh sheet toward himself and dipped his pen.

"The massacre makes the record tonight," he said. "Full text of the letter. Facsimiles of the ledger pages. I'll hold the correspondent's name out of it if you like."

"No." She heard her own voice come out steady, and marveled at it. "Print the name. Print it exactly as written."

* * *

They walked out of the city before dawn, when the gates opened for the milk carts and the market wagons, and the guards were too cold and too bored to study faces. Mara wore the widow's weeds still — old habits, and besides, the road north was full of refugees and half of them were grieving. Elias walked beside her in a civilian coat that hung wrong on his shoulders, a surgeon's hands gone idle in a surgeon's pockets, stripped of rank by a court-martial that had lasted forty minutes and accomplished nothing except making him free.

Neither of them spoke until the walls were behind them and the road opened into white fields under a graying sky.

"It'll be on every corner in Verenholm by noon," Elias said. "*The Dispatch* prints fast when Corrigan smells blood."

"I know."

"Ashe will deny everything. He'll call it forgery. He may call *you* a spy — the real kind, the hanged kind."

"I know that too."

He stopped then, in the middle of the frozen road, and turned to look at her, and she saw that he was smiling — a tired, ruined, unmistakable smile. "Say it once," he said. "I've been calling you Cyrus for three years. I'd like to get it right before we reach anywhere with people in it."

The wind came off the fields and moved through her, cold and clean. She thought of the field hospital, the candle guttering over the ledger, the two hundred and eleven names she had carried like stones in her chest. She thought of Cyrus Pemberton, who had been brave and careful and alone, and who had done what needed doing, and who could rest now.

"Mara," she said. "Mara Pemberton."

"Mara Pemberton." He tried it the way a man tries a key in a lock he's been afraid of breaking. Then he took her hand — bare now, ungloved, no longer a soldier's hand — and held it as though testing whether it would stay.

Behind them, faint across the fields, the bells of Verenholm began to ring the hour, and somewhere beneath the ringing, if either of them had cared to listen, the presses were already turning. The truth was loose in the world now, wearing her name like a borrowed coat it intended to keep.

Ahead, the road ran north through the snow toward nothing in particular — no rank, no regiment, no uniform, no history but the one they would make themselves.

"Well, Miss Pemberton," Elias said. "Which way?"
