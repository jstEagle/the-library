## 1. The Question Too Many

The fog came off the Saône that morning like a held breath finally released, and Elias Vane walked into it with his ledger under one arm and the particular weariness of a man who had counted four hundred souls since dawn. Census work was not glorious work. It was ink-stained, boot-sodden, and thanked by no one — but it was honest, or so he told himself each morning as he tied his cravat, and honesty was a currency he could still afford in the autumn of 1789, when so many men in Lyon were trading in less reputable stock.

The Thackeray & Sons Print Works stood on the rue des Capucins like a fat black beetle of a building, all soot-dark stone and windows fogged with the breath of molten lead. Elias knew it well. He had counted its occupants two years running: old Thackeray himself, widower; his daughter Paloma, entered in the column marked *dependents* because the census form offered no better word for a woman who set type better than any journeyman in the city; three compositors, an apprentice or two, and a boy who swept the floors. The numbers had never once disagreed with themselves.

That should have pleased him. Numbers, when they agreed, were like well-set type — reassuring in their order.

He knocked, and the door was opened before his knuckles fell a second time, as though someone had been waiting behind it.

"Master Vane." Old Thackeray's voice was warm enough, but his eyes went past Elias's shoulder to the fog-filled street before they settled on his face. "Come in out of the wet. You'll want the front room, I expect. Same as last year."

"The same," Elias said, shaking the damp from his coat. "I shan't take above a quarter hour."

The front room smelled of ink and lamp oil and, beneath it, something faintly sweet — the beeswax Paloma used to polish the brass of the hand-press. She was there already, standing at the compositor's frame with a composing stick in her hand, and she did not look up when he entered, though her shoulders stiffened in a way he noticed and then, deliberately, chose not to notice. A census taker learns to see everything and record almost none of it.

"Mademoiselle Thackeray." He inclined his head. She gave him a nod that was half greeting, half dismissal, and returned to her letters.

Elias opened the ledger to the page for the rue des Capucins and dipped his pen. The ritual of it steadied him: name, age, trade, years in household. He worked down the list aloud, and Thackeray answered from his chair by the cold hearth, and the answers came smooth as oiled paper.

"Elias Vane," the old man said suddenly, when the last name was written. "You've a fine memory for faces, I'd wager. In your trade you'd need one."

"A fair one."

"Then tell me — do you remember every soul you've ever set down in that book of yours?"

It was an odd question. Elias looked up. The old man was smiling, but the smile sat on his face the way frost sits on glass — present, decorative, and holding nothing behind it. And his hands. His hands, folded in his lap, were trembling. Not the palsy of age, which shakes evenly, like water; this was a finer tremor, a thread of fear pulled tight beneath the skin.

"I remember what's written," Elias said carefully. "Which is sometimes more than I remember seeing."

"Mm." Thackeray's gaze drifted toward his daughter. "Paloma, my dear, isn't there coal to be fetched?"

"We've coal," she said without turning around. Her composing stick clicked against the frame, setting a line with quick, precise strikes. "We've coal enough for winter."

A silence followed that had weight to it, the silence of a house where something has been moved hastily and hidden badly. Elias bent back over his ledger and let the moment pass, because letting moments pass was most of his profession. He checked his figures. Three compositors, one sweeper, the master, the daughter.

Except.

His eye caught on the margin of last year's entry, where he had made a small notation in his own hand — a habit, noting apprentices separately so the guild rolls could be reconciled. There, in faded iron-gall ink: *apprentice boy, apprenticed at Michaelmas, name unrecorded at time of visit, to be confirmed.*

To be confirmed. He had never confirmed it. The year had swallowed the errand whole, the way years did.

"Master Thackeray," he said, and heard his own voice change key, becoming the mild, relentless instrument of the state, "your apprentice. The lad taken on at Michaelmas last — I have him here as unrecorded. I'll need his name now, for the rolls."

The click of the composing stick stopped.

The silence that followed lasted three full seconds, and Elias had counted enough seconds in his life to know that three was a very long time for a man whose conscience was clean.

"There's been no apprentice," Thackeray said.

"Last October. Your own word, sir — I wrote it down myself."

"Then your book is wrong." The old man rose, and his knees cracked, and his hands would not stop their fine trembling even as he gripped the chair arm. "My memory is worse than my book, Master Vane, but I tell you plainly: no such boy was ever hired. We took no apprentice at Michaelmas. We took none since. The shop is small. My eyes are bad. That is the whole of it."

*No one remembers hiring him.* That was the phrase that lodged in Elias's mind afterward, though no one had quite said it — it assembled itself later, from the pieces: the too-smooth denial, the daughter's stopped hands, the way the fire had gone cold in a house of printers, who were never short of something to burn.

"Curious," Elias said mildly. "That a household forgets a member of it."

"People forget things," Paloma said.

She had turned at last. She was perhaps twenty, dark-haired, with ink on her fingers and a smudge of it along her jaw where she pushed the hair from her face, and her eyes were the level, assessing eyes of someone who sorted letters for a living — small letters from capital letters, truth from near-truth. She did not look frightened. She looked *careful*, which was worse, because careful was what fear looked like after it had learned manners.

"Do people forget things, Mademoiselle Thackeray?" Elias asked. "Or do households?"

"Both." She set down the composing stick with a small deliberate click. "My father misremembers. I wasn't here last October — I was at our cousin's in Villeurbanne, nursing her through a fever. Whatever happened in this shop while I was away, I can't speak to. Perhaps the boy came and went inside a fortnight. Boys do. Apprentices run off constantly — ask any master printer in Lyon."

It was a good answer. It was, in fact, an excellent answer, delivered without haste and without heat, and if Elias had been any other man on any other day he would have written *no apprentice, matter closed* and gone on to the next door in the fog.

But he had spent six years asking strangers to account for themselves, and he had learned that lies come dressed in their best clothes precisely when the thing beneath them is naked.

"Then you won't object," he said, turning to a fresh page, "to my noting the discrepancy. Unrecorded apprentice, engaged Michaelmas 1788, departed — unknown date, unknown name. The guild clerk will want it reconciled."

Something flickered across old Thackeray's face — not anger, not guilt, but a kind of bleak relief, as if a blow had landed somewhere he had braced for it months ago. "Write what you like," he said. "It's only ink."

"It's only ink," Elias agreed, and wrote:

*Thackeray & Sons, rue des Capucins — discrepancy noted. Apprentice engaged Oct. 1788, never named, never seen since by this recorder. Household denies. Daughter absent at time of engagement. To be pursued.*

He blotted the page, closed the ledger, and stood. The visit was over. It should have been over.

At the door, Paloma passed close beside him — too close for accident, he thought later, though at the time he registered nothing but the smell of ink and cold air moving with her. Her voice dropped to nearly nothing, pitched under the sound of the street.

"Master Vane. Some questions are doors."

He paused, hand on the latch.

"And doors," she said, looking at him with those sorting eyes, "once opened, let in the weather."

Then she stepped back, and she was only a printer's daughter again, and the fog was coming through the doorway, and old Thackeray was saying something courteous about next year's census, God willing, and Elias found himself out on the rue des Capucins with the wet pressing at his collar and his heart beating faster than a quarter hour of clerical work had any right to make it beat.

He did not go home by the direct road. He walked the long way along the river, and twice he stopped under a lamp and opened the ledger and read his own notation, as if the words might rearrange themselves into innocence if given enough light.

They did not.

*A boy taken on at Michaelmas whom no one will name. A father who fears his own census. A daughter who warns him off with proverbs.*

Lyon was full of strange weather that autumn — bread riots, pamphlets, talk from Paris that made magistrates sweat. A missing apprentice was a grain of sand on that beach. Any sensible man would leave it be.

But Elias Vane had built six years of honest work on the principle that every soul entered in a ledger deserved to remain entered — that to strike a name was a small murder, done in ink instead of blood. Someone had struck this boy from the world's book before Elias had ever finished writing him in. And the trembling of old Thackeray's hands said the striking had not been the old man's choice.

He stopped at the last lamp before his lodgings, took out his pen knife and his travel ink, and in the margin beside the entry, in letters small and firm, he added a single line — not a record, for records required facts, and facts he had none of. Just a name-shaped space. A placeholder. A debt.

*Who was he?*

He closed the book. Somewhere across the rooftops, a press began to turn in the dark — he could hear it, the rhythmic iron heartbeat of it, printing tomorrow's lies or tomorrow's truths, he could not tell which — and Elias Vane stood in the fog with the certain, cold knowledge that he had asked one question too many, and that the door it had opened would not be shut again by anything so simple as his choosing to walk away.
