## 2. Marguerite Cut Small

The type had a name before it ever had a purpose.

Paloma Thackeray was nine years old the winter she cut it — small letters from soft end-grain boxwood, her father's spare graver held in both fists like something that might bite. She had cut them badly at first, then better, then with a strange fluency that made her father go quiet when he saw them. The serifs were too fine. The bowls of the *a* and the *o* carried a slight asymmetry, a lean to the left, like handwriting pretending to be print. He held a proof sheet up to the lamp for a long time.

"It has a face," he said finally. "It looks like someone."

She named it Marguerite, after the daisy her mother had pressed between the pages of a prayer book, the one flower that survived every winter in the garden by looking like nothing at all. Her father laughed then — a real laugh, unguarded — and locked the type in the cedar box with the brass fittings, and for six years the box sat on the high shelf above the composing stone, gathering dust and candle smoke, a child's toy grown into an heirloom.

Paloma thought about that shelf now as she swept the shop floor at dusk, listening to the presses tick as they cooled. October had come down hard on Lyon; the Saône breathed fog through the open loading doors, and the smell of ink and cold iron hung in the room like weather. Her father worked late more often these past months. He said it was the almanac orders. He said it was the diocese wanting breviaries before Advent. He said many things in a voice that had learned, somewhere over the summer, to flatten itself out like paper under a weight.

* * *

She found the certificates on a Thursday, because Thursdays were when she oiled the second press, and the second press stood against the wall behind the drying racks where her father believed no one looked.

They were stacked beneath a false bottom in the paper cabinet — twenty sheets, maybe thirty, each one bearing the seal of the Hôtel-Dieu and the careful, official hand of a physician Paloma did not know. *Died of fever. Died of consumption. Died of a rupture of the heart.* Names she half recognized from the parish rolls, men who had come into the shop once or twice, quiet men with quiet hands, men who bought pamphlets and paid in coin and never gave their surnames.

And set into each certificate, so fine she had to hold it to the window to be sure, the letterforms leaned left. The serifs were too fine. The *a* and the *o* carried their small, stubborn asymmetry.

Marguerite.

Her own hand, nine years old, staring back at her from the face of a lie.

She stood very still. Outside, a cart rattled over the cobbles and the sound seemed to pass through her chest. She read one certificate twice, three times, matching the names against her memory of the shop's visitors, and the arithmetic assembled itself with terrible patience: these men were not dead. These men were alive, and alive was precisely what these papers denied them. Somewhere a ledger said they had died; here was the proof, printed in her father's shop, in her father's lockup, in her own childhood alphabet.

She heard his step on the stair and did not have time to decide anything. She only had time to close the cabinet and turn, oil rag still in her hand, wearing what she hoped was the face of a daughter thinking about nothing at all.

"Late again," her father said. His coat was wet at the shoulders. He crossed to the stove without meeting her eyes, and when he passed the paper cabinet his hand brushed its top edge — lightly, the way a man touches a door he has locked a thousand times, checking.

"Papa." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "Who is Aurélien Marchand?"

He stopped. For a moment the shop was so quiet she could hear the fog pressing against the windows. Then he said, "A name off a jobbing list," and began unbuckling his apron, and his hands — his steady, sure, compositor's hands — trembled so badly the buckle clicked against itself three times before it caught.

Paloma looked at those hands and understood that whatever she said next would either open something or seal it shut forever. She was nineteen years old. She loved him more than she feared almost anything.

"Goodnight, Papa," she said, and sealed it.

* * *

The clerk came on Saturday morning, and he came with soldiers.

There were two of them, posted at the loading doors with the bored posture of men guarding a warehouse, while the clerk himself stepped inside shaking rain from a gray coat and announcing, in a voice like a filed document, that he represented Magistrate Delorme's office and required to inspect the works for unlicensed printing. He did not wait for permission. He had a way of moving through the shop as though he had already memorized it — touching the type cases, lifting the lids of the ink stones, running one finger along the drying rack and examining the fingertip afterward as if the shop might confess to him directly.

Her father answered questions. Paloma watched him do it and marveled at how well he lied: the almanacs, the diocese work, the breviaries, all of it delivered in the flat voice he had practiced all autumn. But the clerk was not really listening. The clerk was reading the walls. And when his eye passed over the framed proofs hanging by the office door — specimens, keepsakes, her father's little museum of forty years' work — it stopped.

It stopped on a sheet Paloma knew intimately. A specimen of Marguerite, cut small, mounted under glass, labeled in her father's hand: *P.T., aged 9.*

"What is this face?" the clerk asked.

"A fancy," her father said. "A child's exercise. Cut for play, kept for sentiment."

The clerk leaned close. He did not touch the glass. He looked at the leaning letters the way a man looks at a face he has seen on a wanted bill, and something moved behind his eyes that Paloma could not name and did not want to.

"I'll need to see your stockroom," he said.

* * *

Later — much later, years later, in whatever life waited for her after this one — Paloma would try to remember whether she had decided or simply moved. There was no gap in which a decision lived. The clerk went toward the stockroom with her father trailing him, reciting inventory, and Paloma was alone for perhaps ninety seconds with the cedar box on the high shelf, and her body simply understood what her mind had been refusing to say aloud: *if he finds Marguerite, he finds everything.*

The box was heavier than memory. She came down from the stool with it against her chest and crossed the floor to the great folio standing open on the sorting table — the census atlas, the enormous bound register of households that the census man Elias Vane had left for correction and collection, its pages thick as boards, its spine wide enough to swallow a psalter whole. She pried the hollow where the binding gaped between gatherings, deep along the spine where no page lay flat, and wedged the cedar box into the dark, and closed the covers over it, and weighted the atlas with a stone as if it had always lain there, patient and official and blind.

Her heart was a fist knocking on a door no one would answer. She picked up her oil rag. She resumed oiling the second press with hands that, she noted distantly, did not tremble at all — hands steadier than her father's, steadier than they had any right to be, as though complicity were a kind of craft and her body had learned it in a single morning.

The clerk emerged from the stockroom dissatisfied. He paused at the door, coat already buttoned, and let his gaze travel the room one last time — the presses, the racks, the atlas under its stone.

"The magistrate thanks you for your cooperation," he said. "He is compiling a record of the city's printers. Names, faces, hands." A thin smile. "Everyone leaves a mark, Monsieur Thackeray. Even children."

Then he was gone, and the fog took him, and Paloma stood in the cooling shop beside the atlas with the stolen alphabet sleeping inside it, understanding for the first time in her life that she was no longer a girl who had cut pretty letters. She was a keeper of evidence. She had hidden a living thing inside a book of the dead.

That night she wrote a note in her neatest hand — not in Marguerite, never in Marguerite — folded it small, and sent the errand boy running toward the lodgings of the census taker named Vane, who had asked one question too many and whose margins, she suspected, were already inked with the beginning of the end.
