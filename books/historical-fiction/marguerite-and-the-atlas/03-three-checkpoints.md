## 3. Three Checkpoints

The atlas rode against his chest like a second heart.

Elias had sewn it into the lining of his greatcoat himself, working by candlelight with a sailmaker's needle borrowed from the landlady, and even so it made the coat hang wrong — a stiffness on the left side, a weight that pulled when he raised his arm. He kept catching himself touching it. A man who pats his own chest every quarter hour might as well carry a sign: *Something valuable here. Rob me. Arrest me.*

He left Lyon before dawn on the fourth day after Paloma's note arrived, folded small as a moth. The duplicated pages were inside the atlas now, interleaved among its columns of the counted dead — her work, cut in that secret alphabet she called Marguerite, letterforms like frost ferns, delicate and unrepeatable. Beside them lay his own margins, inked over weeks of census rounds: names cross-referenced against burial registers, dates that refused to agree, and at the center of it all the list of condemned men whose trial had never been held by a magistrate who did not officially exist. *A book of the dead,* she had written him, *with living things hidden in it.* He understood now that he was not carrying evidence so much as carrying people — eleven men breathing somewhere in France under forged certificates, alive only as long as no one looked too closely at the paper that said they weren't.

The Saône was up with winter rain, brown and muscular, dragging ice floes from the north. At the first toll below Vaise the bargemen were arguing with a collector whose breath fogged blue in the cold, and Elias joined the queue of travelers shuffling across the plank gangway with their bundles and grievances.

"Papers."

He handed over his census commission — genuine, stamped, signed by men whose signatures he had verified twice — and watched the tollkeeper read it. The man's eyes went from the document to Elias's face and back, and something shifted there, a flicker Elias had learned to dread: recognition of the office without respect for it. In 1789, commissions were becoming things people read closely.

"Coming from the print works quarter?" the tollkeeper asked.

"Taking the census," Elias said. "I go where the households are."

"Households." The man snorted and stamped the paper. "Half of them don't exist, if you believe the magistrate's lists. And half the ones that do exist aren't on any list." He waved Elias through, then added, almost as an afterthought, almost kindly: "There's a clerk asking after a census man on the Paris road. Gray gloves. Ask questions before you answer any."

Elias walked the towpath with the river's cold climbing through his boots and did not look back. *Gray gloves.* He knew those gloves. They had rested on the counter of Thackeray & Sons while their owner smiled and asked about typefaces.

* * *

The second checkpoint was a garrison gate at Villefranche, where the road narrowed between barracks walls and soldiers checked carts with the bored thoroughness of men paid by the search rather than the finding. Here the queue was longer and colder, and here — pressed among wine merchants and a family of wool factors — Elias felt the dread change texture. It stopped being an animal feeling in the gut and became arithmetic. If the clerk had reached the garrison first, there would be a portrait. If there was a portrait, then the only question was whether Elias's face matched it well enough for a tired soldier to care.

It was while he was doing this sum that the stranger spoke.

"You're holding your arm wrong," the man said quietly. He was older, gray-stubbled, dressed as a dealer in haberdashery — buttons and thread samples visible in his open satchel — but his hands were ink-stained at the cuticles, and no soap fully removes that particular stain. "Left side. Like something's sewn in."

Elias did not answer. Around them, a soldier barked at the wool factors to open their bales.

"I'm not threatening you," the man said. "I'm telling you because I've carried things myself. You hold the left arm stiff, you favor it, you touch it. Soldiers see that all day. They don't think *book.* They think *weapon* or *coin*, and either way they'll want to open the coat." He paused. "Carry it like it's nothing. Carry it like a loaf of bread you've already eaten half of. Nobody searches a man for half a loaf."

"And how would you know what I'm carrying?"

"Because I set type for thirty years in Lyon before they closed my shop," the man said, "and because three days ago a girl came to my cousin's house with a page I'd know anywhere. Letters cut by hand, every one different, like snowflakes. My cousin asked me what such a face would be called, if anyone ever cast it." His eyes were steady and very tired. "I told him I didn't know its name. But I knew its maker. She used to bring her father his dinner when she was nine years old and beg him to let her cut a lowercase g. Best g I ever saw, and I told her so."

Elias's mouth had gone dry. "You've seen the pages."

"I've seen one. Enough." The man glanced toward the gate, where the soldier was now waving them forward in pairs. "Listen to me, census man. There's a clerk ahead of you. Gray gloves, traveling under a name that isn't his, carrying portraits drawn from a description that fits maybe forty men in France. You're one of the forty. But so is every thin dark-haired traveler on this road, and he can't stop them all — unless someone helps him. So." He pressed a thread sample card into Elias's hand, ordinary as anything, a dozen colored strands waxed to cardboard. "Show this at the gate. Say you're a factor's agent buying for spring. Haberdashery men get waved through; census men get read. Your commission is a target. This is camouflage."

"Why?" Elias asked. "Why help me? You don't know me."

The old typesetter looked out at the gate, at the flag hanging limp above it, and his voice dropped to almost nothing.

"Because a law that kills men without trying them isn't a law. It's just a hand over a mouth. Somebody has to be the breath underneath." He stepped forward into the line. "Justice isn't granted, friend. It gets carried. By hand. Through gates like this one. Usually by somebody terrified."

At the barrier, Elias showed the thread card and mumbled his cover, and the soldier glanced at the satchel-less, commissionless traveler with mild contempt and waved him past into the town beyond. Only when the barracks were behind him did he allow himself to breathe, the atlas riding warm against his ribs like a sleeping animal he could not put down.

* * *

The third checkpoint was not announced as a checkpoint at all, which was how he should have known.

The road to Paris passed through a cathedral town — Mâcon, gray and golden on its hill — and travelers always stopped there, because the cathedral stood beside the only inn, and the inn's fire was famous, and God and comfort shared a doorstep. Elias told himself he would walk straight through. Then the rain came down sideways, and his resolve drowned with everything else, and he climbed the steps under the great west door merely to stand out of the weather until it passed.

The porch smelled of stone and candle smoke. A few beggars slept in niches. And near the font, studying a notice board with the patience of a man reading scripture, stood a figure in gray gloves.

Elias did not turn around. Turning around was confirmation. He moved instead toward the notice board itself, slowly, letting his eye travel over proclamations about grain prices and a lost dog, and in the polished bronze of the board's frame he saw the reflection of the porch behind him: the clerk, still reading, and beside the clerk — leaning against the pillar where any traveler might see it — a sheet of paper weighted with two stones.

Drawn in charcoal, skillfully, was a thin man with dark hair going early gray at the temples.

The portrait was good. It was, Elias thought with a strange, floating calm, better than most likenesses managed. It had caught something true — not the face exactly, but the *looking*. The look of a man who counts things.

The rain eased. Bells began somewhere above, calling the hour, and pilgrims stirred in the porch, and the clerk pushed off from the pillar at last and began, unhurriedly, to walk the length of the porch — past each traveler in turn, pausing, comparing, moving on. Gray gloves flexed once at his side.

Elias turned to the notice board again and, with hands that surprised him by being steady, unpinned a grain proclamation, folded it, and tucked it into his coat — the right side, the empty side — as though he had come for the notices all along. Then he walked out into the wet evening, down steps worn hollow by centuries of feet, toward the road and the dark and the last hundred miles, while behind him, faint beneath the bells, he heard the clerk ask the sacristan a question in a voice like dry paper:

Whether any census men had passed this way.

And whether, by chance, they had been carrying a book.
