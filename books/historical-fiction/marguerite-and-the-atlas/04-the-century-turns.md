## 4. The Century Turns

**Elias**

Paris smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone, and beneath both, faintly, of ink — the whole city steeped in it like a page left too long in the bath. Elias came down the rue Saint-Jacques at dusk on the last day of the decade with the atlas strapped against his chest beneath his coat, its canvas cover warm from his body, as though the book itself had a pulse. Above him, on the third floor of the printing house of Delorme & Fils, lamplight bloomed gold in the frosted windows. The compositors were already at work on the morning edition. By dawn it would be set. By dawn, if he did everything correctly, forty-one names would be set among the advertisements and shipping notices, tucked into the paper like seeds pressed into winter soil.

He climbed the outside stair because the inside stair was watched. He knew this the way one knows weather: by the ache in old wounds. The clerk had been behind him since Lyon — since before Lyon, perhaps, since the moment Elias had asked for the name of an apprentice no one remembered hiring — and tonight, of all nights, Aurélien Marchand would not be drinking wine somewhere with his boots up. Tonight Marchand would be working.

The stair groaned under him. At the top, the door to the composing room stood ajar, spilling light across the landing like spilled milk.

Elias stepped through it and stopped.

Marchand sat at the stone table in the center of the room, alone amid the sleeping presses, his clerk's coat folded neatly over a chair back, his hands laced before him on the wood. He looked less like a hunter than a man waiting for a train that had been delayed many years. A single candle burned between them. On the table beside the candle lay Elias's portrait — the small miniature taken from him at the cathedral checkpoint, or rather its twin; they had made copies, of course they had made copies — propped against a chaser of type like a saint's icon in a poor man's shrine.

"Master Vane," Marchand said. "You're late. The bells will ring in ninety minutes, and I have been here since four."

"Then you've had time to read the atlas."

"I've had time to read *everything*." Marchand's voice was soft, unhurried, the voice of a man reading aloud a ledger of debts. "Forty-one men certified dead in the parish registers of Lyon between 1787 and this autumn. Forty-one men whose bodies no gravedigger buried, whose families no priest consoled, whose property passed — every last sou of it — through the offices of Magistrate Corvel. Do you know what Corvel says when I bring him these discrepancies? He says, *Aurélien, you are a diligent man, and diligence is a disease.*" He smiled without warmth. "I have decided to let the disease progress."

Elias kept his hand on the strap of the atlas. "Then step aside."

"I intend to do nothing of the kind." Marchand rose. He was not a large man, but the candlelight made him tall, threw his shadow up the wall and over the racks of type until he seemed made of the same metal. "Corvel owns three of the five magistrates who could hear this evidence. He owns the censors' office. He owns, as of Tuesday, the man who owns this press. If that book goes out tomorrow, it goes out as ash — pulped by noon, its printers jailed by evening, and you, Master Vane, dead by Friday in a cell officially recorded as a suicide. I have written enough of those records to know how they read."

The clock in the corner ticked. Somewhere below, a compositor laughed at some private joke, the sound muffled by floors and decades.

"And yet," Elias said slowly, "you are telling me all this instead of taking the book."

Marchand's smile flickered, guttered, went out. "Because I have spent eleven years writing lies in a beautiful hand, and my hand has begun to shake. Because there is a woman coming up those stairs right now whom I could not stop if I wanted to, and I find — God forgive me — that I do not want to."

The door swung wide behind Elias, letting in the cold.

**Paloma**

Paloma Thackeray came up the outside stair with the Marguerite box strapped to her back the way other women carried infants, close and curved and unbearably precious, and she came up it fast, because her father was in Lyon under the eye of Corvel's men and every hour she spent in Paris was an hour he spent as collateral.

She had cut Marguerite when she was nine years old, in her father's shop, from scrap brass, each letter small enough to hide under a thumbnail. She had named the typeface for the flower her mother grew in a cracked pot on the windowsill — the only thing in the house her father never touched, never sold, never explained. Her mother had been dead six years by then. Paloma had understood even at nine that some names are prayers said sideways.

For eleven years her father had used Marguerite for the certificates. Forged deaths, printed in a daughter's letterforms: her own small hands signing away forty-one lives so that her father's hands would stay steady, would stay fed, would stay out of the Bastille. She had learned of it only in October, when she found the proofs hidden in the binding of her childhood catechism. She had not wept. She had sharpened her graver instead.

Now she stood in the doorway of the composing room, snow melting on her shoulders, and saw two men turn toward her: the census taker, gray-faced, gripping a canvas-wrapped book like a shield; and the clerk — Marchand, it must be Marchand, she knew his handwriting better than her own psalter — standing very still beside a candle.

"You're the daughter," Marchand said.

"I'm the typefounder." She unslung the box and set it on the stone table with a sound like a coffin lid settling. "And you're going to help me make a trade."

"There is no trade Corvel will honor."

"The trade isn't with Corvel." She opened the box. Candlelight fell into it and the letters caught the light — dozens of them, hundreds, brass and steel, each one cut by a child's patience, gleaming like the inside of a geode. "The trade is with history. Here is how it reads. The atlas prints tomorrow, set in Marguerite, in full, with the certificate numbers and the property transfers and the magistrate's name spelled correctly. In exchange, my father's name appears nowhere. Not as printer. Not as accomplice. As far as your beautiful lying records are concerned, Aldous Thackeray hired no apprentice, forged no document, touched nothing but his own legitimate work. You will write it that way, because you are the only man alive whose false entries Corvel believes."

Marchand looked at her for a long time. Outside, the bells of Saint-Séverin began to stir, testing their voices.

"And what do you get," he asked quietly, "besides your father?"

Paloma thought of the boy no one remembered hiring — the forty-second name, the one Elias had inked into his margins a year ago, the thread that had unraveled everything. She thought of her mother's flower dying in its cracked pot while the house filled with paper ghosts.

"Witness," she said. "That's the whole price. That someone, someday, opening this newspaper, knows it happened. Memory that's been printed can't be erased. Corvel can burn every copy in Paris and the setting still exists — *this* setting—" she laid her palm flat on the brass, cold and true, "—in letters no one else can duplicate, cut by a girl whose name you'll also leave out of it. Let them puzzle over that in fifty years. Let them wonder who held the graver."

Marchand exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a weight carried across a country. Then he took off his coat entirely, rolled his sleeves, and pulled the first case of type toward him with the ease of thirty years' habit.

"Bells in eighty minutes," he said. "We'll want the obituaries column. No one reads the obituaries — which means everyone archives them. Master Vane, the atlas. Madame Thackeray, your flowers. We are going to bury forty-one living men in print so thoroughly they can never die again."

* * *

**Elias**

They worked as the century died.

Elias read the names aloud from the duplicated pages, his voice growing hoarse, and Marchand set them line by line in Marguerite's small bright alphabet while Paloma stood at his shoulder correcting his spacing with a touch light as frost. The composing room filled with the music of it — the click and slide of brass, the rasp of the imposing stone, the low murmured recitation of the dead-who-were-not-dead: *Renard, a cooper. Aubry, a weaver. The boy — the boy's name was Lucien — Lucien, apprenticed, vanished, certified, gone.*

At half past midnight the forme was locked. Below them the presses waited like patient animals.

It was Paloma who heard the bells first — not Saint-Séverin now but all of them, Notre-Dame and the rest, rolling across the frozen city in waves, 1789 drowning in bronze thunder as 1790 rose gasping out of the Seine. Through the frosted windows Paris lit itself candle by candle, window by window, a constellation deciding to burn.

Marchand sent the forme down the hoist himself. They heard the press take it — the great sighing stamp of it, once, twice, a hundred times, the sound of memory being hammered into permanence.

Elias stood at the window with the empty space where the atlas had been against his chest, and found he was not afraid. Whatever Corvel did next — and he would do much, and quickly — the morning edition was already folding itself into ten thousand hands, sliding into pockets, lining birdcages, wrapping bread, crossing rivers in peddlers' packs. You could burn paper. You could not unprint it.

Beside him, Paloma watched the snow come down over the roofs, and said, softly, almost to herself:

"Now it's history happening to us."

Below, in the street, a carriage drew up that bore neither a printer's mark nor any honest livery — and from it, wrapped in furs, Magistrate Corvel himself stepped out, looking up at the lit windows with an expression Elias could read even at that distance, because he had spent a year learning to read the faces of men consulting ledgers.

Corvel had arrived to see the fire lit.

Instead he had arrived in time to watch the smoke rise.
