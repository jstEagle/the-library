## 2. One Question Too Many

The morning the census man came, August Villalobos was polishing the brass on the motorcar's headlamps when he heard the gate latch click — a sound he knew the way other men knew their own heartbeat. He had catalogued every voice that latch produced: the postman's heavy tread followed by a whistle; the grocer's boy dragging his feet because Mrs. Ashcombe's drive was the longest on his route; the vicar, who always paused to admire the wisteria and pretend he hadn't come about the subscription. This tread was none of these. It was measured, deliberate, and it stopped twice — once at the gravel's edge to scrape his boots, once halfway up to look at the house.

August set down his cloth. Twelve years had taught him that a visitor who admired the house before knocking wanted something from it.

The man at the door was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, dressed with the careful drabness of a government clerk trying not to look like one. He carried a leather satchel and an umbrella despite the cloudless sky, and when August opened the door he presented a card as though it were a small gift he expected to be declined.

"Mr. Pell," the card said, beneath an embossed crest August did not recognize. "Census of 1899. I'm collecting schedules for this district."

"Mrs. Ashcombe is engaged until noon." The lie was smooth as the polish on the lamps. Marguerite was at her desk upstairs, visible through the study window if one knew where to look, writing letters that would be steamed open and resealed by no one now, because August had retired that habit years ago — or told himself he had.

"I can wait," Mr. Pell said pleasantly. He had pale eyes the colour of watered milk, and they moved past August into the hall with the unhurried appetite of a man reading a room the way August read letters. "It's a fine morning for waiting. Lovely house. Georgian?"

"Tudor foundations, Georgian face. The family liked to appear newer than they were."

Something flickered across Pell's face — appreciation, August thought, or its counterfeit. "And how long have you been in service here, Mr.—"

"Villalobos."

"Villalobos." Pell said the name as though tasting it. "Twelve years, I'd guess. You have the look of a man who arrived with the last century and means to see out this one."

August felt the words settle somewhere beneath his ribs like a stone dropped into still water. "You've a good eye for faces, sir."

"For records, rather." Pell smiled. "Faces are only records that walk about."

* * *

Marguerite heard them from the landing.

She had not meant to listen. She had come down for her second cup of coffee and stopped on the third stair when the unfamiliar voice reached her — a voice like a filing cabinet opening, courteous and bottomless. She stood in the half-dark of the staircase, teacup in hand, and watched through the banister spindles as August stood in the doorway below, blocking most of the light and all of the view, his shoulders squared in that particular way she had learned to read over twelve years the way sailors read weather.

She knew that stance. It was the one he took when there was something between the house and the world that must not pass.

"—the schedule requires household members as they were twelve years past, you understand," Pell was saying. "Not merely present occupants. The Act asks after persons who slept here on census night, eighteen eighty-seven. Deaths, departures, births. The statisticians want a complete picture of the household across time."

"Then your statisticians will be disappointed," August said mildly. "I keep the garden, the car, and the accounts. History is above my station."

"Of course." Pell's voice did not rise, but it acquired a new precision, like a blade being turned to catch light. "Still — the late Mr. Ashcombe. Edmund Ashcombe. He would have been resident in 'eighty-seven. The schedule will want dates. Departure, I mean. And cause."

From the stairs, Marguerite saw it happen — saw the thing she had seen perhaps four times in twelve years and never wished to see again. August hesitated. Not long. Not even a full breath. But a hesitation in August was like a hesitation in a clock: the mechanism went on regardless, yet something in the tick had gone wrong, and anyone listening closely would hear it forever after.

"He died in London," August said. "April of 'eighty-seven. I drove his body back myself. The dates are in the parish register if the Crown wants them."

"The Crown usually does want them." Pell made a note — Marguerite could hear the scratch of pencil against paper, absurdly loud in the quiet hall. "And you, Mr. Villalobos. Were you in service here on census night, eighteen eighty-seven?"

"I was."

"Then you'll be entered. Name, age, place of birth, occupation — and relationship to the head of household." A pause, delicate as a scalpel being laid on velvet. "What would you say that relationship was?"

Marguerite came down the last three stairs.

She did it deliberately, letting her heel ring on the oak so that both men heard her before either could answer, and she watched the question die in the air between them — watched Pell turn with a bow calibrated to the inch, and August's face assemble itself into the smooth blankness she had relied upon for twelve years and had never, until this moment, thought of as a mask.

"Mrs. Ashcombe." Pell advanced with his card extended anew, as though the first presentation had been lost in transit. "An honour, ma'am. Your name carries further than you might wish."

"Further than I wish," Marguerite agreed, taking the card, "and rarely as far as I deserve. You're collecting the past, Mr. Pell."

"Every ten years, ma'am. It's the law's way of remembering." His milk-pale eyes rested on her with what might have been sympathy. "Some households remember more easily than others."

"We remember perfectly well. We simply prefer to do it privately." She handed the card back. "My husband died in April, eighteen eighty-seven. My driver has served me since the autumn of that year — he was not resident on census night, whatever he may have told you out of modesty. You'll find him in the schedule under his previous situation, the Harrowgate household of Sir Charles Denby. As for the rest—" she gestured at the hall, at the portraits, at the twelve years standing in the air like dust in sunlight, "—you may write that the house holds one widow, one servant, and no ghosts. Ghosts are not enumerated."

"No, ma'am." Pell closed his satchel with a soft, final snap. "Though I confess ghosts are my favourite part of the work. Every district has them. Houses that held someone in 'eighty-seven who isn't spoken of now." He smiled, and the smile was genuinely warm, which made it worse. "You'd be astonished, ma'am, what a census turns up when a man reads the schedules against each other. Year against year. Household against household. Names that appear and then... don't."

"Names do that," Marguerite said. "It's called dying."

"It's sometimes called other things." He bowed again, deeper this time. "I'll leave the schedule for completion. There's no hurry — the enumerator calls back within the fortnight. I do hope you'll both be feeling more... forthcoming." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at August with an expression of almost tender curiosity. "Twelve years is a long time to stand at a gate, Mr. Villalobos. You must see everyone worth seeing."

He let himself out. They listened to his boots cross the gravel, unhurried, stopping once — at the carriage house, Marguerite noted, though neither said it — before the gate clicked shut behind him.

* * *

In the silence that followed, August returned to the headlamps. It was reflex, not industry; his hands needed occupation or they would betray him. He worked the cloth in slow circles and listened to Marguerite's footsteps on the gravel behind him, unhurried, coming to stand in the smell of metal polish and cut grass.

"You lied to him," she said.

"I misspoke. You corrected the record."

"I corrected it *for* you." She came around to the front of the car, into his sightline, and he set down the cloth because she hated speaking to the top of his head. "August. On census night, eighteen eighty-seven — where were you?"

The question landed harder than Pell's had, because Pell had asked it as a stranger, and she asked it as the woman whose correspondence he had read for twelve years, whose secrets he had carried like stones in both shoes, and whose trust he had never once tested — until now, apparently, by lying to a clerk with a leather bag.

"At the Harrowgate station," he said quietly. "Waiting for the train that brought me here. You know that. You met it yourself."

"And yet you told him you were resident."

"Because a man who says he arrived in October raises fewer questions than a man who says he arrived in April — the month the master died, the month the house was sealed, the month every servant in England knows meant something happened that the family didn't want counted." He met her eyes. "You taught me that, Mrs. Ashcombe. Or I learned it watching you teach everyone else."

The wind moved the wisteria along the south wall, and somewhere down the lane a cart rattled past, ordinary as bread. For a moment neither spoke, and August understood that they were standing on either side of a line that had existed for twelve years without ever being drawn — the line between the things he kept for her and the things he kept *from* her — and that Mr. Pell, with one pencil and one pleasant voice, had just walked up and put his boot on it.

"Did he trouble you?" she asked at last, and her voice was the public voice, the platform voice, warm and slightly amused and giving away nothing. "Census men can be persistent. If he returns, I'll deal with him."

"He won't return," August said.

She raised an eyebrow. "You sound very certain."

"I am certain of very few things, ma'am. That's one." He picked up the cloth again. "Men like that aren't collecting. They're comparing. He'll go to the register office, then to the parish, then wherever registers lead, and he'll find that everything we've written matches everything we've said, because it does — because you've kept it true for twelve years down to the day. He'll find nothing."

"Then why," Marguerite said, and here her voice changed — dropped its warmth, dropped its amusement, became for one unguarded second the voice she used at three in the morning over a cold hearth, the voice he had heard perhaps twice and never forgot, "did you hesitate?"

The cloth stopped moving.

"When he asked where you slept in 'eighty-seven," she said. "Half a breath, August. I've watched you lie to bishops, journalists, and two Home Secretaries without blinking. Today you blinked."

"Ma'am—"

"Don't *ma'am* me. Not today." She stepped closer, and he caught the scent of ink and lavender that had been the atmosphere of his working life. "I have spent twelve years asking you to carry things so I wouldn't have to look at them. I have earned, I think, one honest answer. What did that man's question touch?"

And August — who had answers ready for every question in England except the ones she asked plainly — found himself looking at her across the polished bonnet of the car and understanding that the silence he had built, brick by brick, letter by letter, for twelve years, had developed its first crack, and that through the crack he could hear something moving on the other side. Something old. Something with Edmund Ashcombe's name on it.

"He touched nothing," August said. "But I'll tell you what troubled me, since you ask for honesty." He folded the cloth with great care, as though the fold mattered. "He didn't ask about you. He didn't ask about the household. He asked about *him*. By name. Census men count sleepers, Mrs. Ashcombe. They don't count the dead by name — not unless someone has already given them the name to look for."

Marguerite went very still. Above them a jackdaw crossed the pale sky, and the clock in the hall — the mantel clock, Edmund's clock, the one that had stopped on the morning of his death and that she had never permitted anyone to repair — began, as it did every quarter hour, its dry and faithful ticking, marking out the time as though the last twelve years were merely a pause between strokes.

"Someone gave him the name," she repeated softly.

"That's what I'd want to know," August said. "Who."

She turned toward the house. At the terrace steps she paused, not looking back, her hand resting on the stone balustrade the way she rested her hand on lecterns before beginning a speech — gathering herself, arming herself.

"August," she said. "When he comes back — and he will come back — you will not lie for me. Do you understand? Whatever he asks, however you think you're protecting me. Those days are done."

"Yes, ma'am," August said.

And because he had spent twelve years learning to mean precisely what his words required and nothing more, she believed him.

She should have known better. So should he.

Upstairs, alone, he took from the locked drawer of his own bureau — the drawer no one knew of, the twin to hers — a single item he had kept since April of 1887: not a letter, but the *ash* of one, folded into a twist of paper, because August Villalobos had burned many things at Marguerite's instruction and had discovered early that some things refuse to stop existing simply because they've stopped existing. He looked at it for a long time.

Then the gate latch clicked again, far off down the drive, and he was moving before the sound finished — out the door, down the path, into the light — knowing even as he went that no visitor returning that same afternoon could mean anything innocent, and that the wall of silence, cracked at dawn, was about to learn what it was made of.
