## 3. Marguerite's Ledger

The speech had been written four times before breakfast.

Marguerite Ashcombe sat at the long window of her study with the fourth draft in her lap and the fifth forming itself somewhere behind her eyes, and she understood — as she had understood each morning for twelve years — that the words were not yet true enough to be spoken aloud. Truth in a tribute was not the same as truth on a page. A page could be corrected. A voice, once released into a hall full of listening women, became permanent, like a name carved in wet stone.

She read the draft again anyway, because reading it was easier than admitting what she was actually doing, which was stalling.

*"We gather,"* the page said, *"not to mourn Edmund Ashcombe but to thank him."*

That was good. That was very nearly perfect. Thank him. Not mourn — mourning was private, and this occasion was anything but; the Albert Hall committee expected six hundred chairs filled and a resolution passed and photographs taken for the papers that would run beneath headlines about the movement's fallen knight. Twelve years since the fever took him, twelve years since the movement had needed its martyr more than its husband, and every year the martyr grew taller while the man grew fainter, until Marguerite sometimes caught herself unable to recall the exact weight of his hand on her shoulder and able instead to recite, flawlessly, the obituary language: *a pillar of the cause; a gentleman who gave his fortune and finally his life to the enfranchisement of women.*

She had written that obituary herself. She supposed that was what troubled her about it now — not that it was false, exactly, but that she had been its author, and an author is responsible for everything her sentences leave out.

Outside, October was doing its work on the garden. The beech hedge had gone the color of old brass, and the gardener's boy was raking leaves into piles that the wind immediately redistributed, so that he seemed to be conducting a labor with no possible conclusion. Marguerite watched him for a while with the peculiar gratitude one feels toward any task visibly more futile than one's own.

"Mrs. Ashcombe."

August stood in the doorway. He always announced himself rather than simply appearing, though he was entirely capable of appearing; she had never once heard his footsteps on the stairs, and had concluded years ago that this was not natural grace but discipline. He held the morning's correspondence in both hands, squared, the way he always held things — as though paper were something that might startle.

"Thank you, August. Anything that can't wait?"

"Two invitations for Saturday. A note from Mrs. Pankhurst's office asking whether you'll share the platform. And this." He crossed the room and set a single envelope apart from the others, on the corner of her desk, the way he did when a letter required her attention before her attention was ready to give it. "From the Hall committee. Final arrangements."

"Final arrangements." She smiled at the phrase. "As though I were the deceased."

August did not smile. He almost never did, and she had stopped expecting it, and yet some part of her registered each absence the way one registers a clock that has stopped — not with alarm, precisely, but with the sense that something in the house had gone quiet that ought to be ticking. He inclined his head and withdrew, and she heard, a moment later, the front door, and then the motor starting in the yard, taking him off on whatever errands the day required. He would read nothing today that she hadn't already seen. She knew this because she had never asked him to do otherwise, and because she had never once caught him doing otherwise, and because — she admitted this only in the privacy of her own skull, where even she spoke carefully — she preferred not to test it.

Some trusts are load-bearing walls. You do not knock on them to see if they're sound.

She turned back to the speech.

*We gather not to mourn Edmund Ashcombe but to thank him.* And then the paragraph unspooled in its familiar way — his generosity, his arrest outside the Home Office in '89, the illness contracted in custody that his constitution could not survive, the deathbed scene that three separate newspapers had embroidered into three separate legends and Marguerite had allowed all three, because legends, unlike wives, cannot be contradicted without cost.

There it was again. *Cost.* Her mind kept circling back to the word the way her tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

She set the draft down, went to the window, opened it, and let the cold in for a count of thirty, because cold was honest and the study was not.

Then she unlocked the drawer.

* * *

It was the bottom drawer of the writing desk Edmund had used, oak, German-made, with a lock whose key she kept on the same ring as the house keys, so that opening it required no decision — only habit, which is a far more dangerous thing. The key turned with the small dry click she had heard perhaps a dozen times in twelve years, and each time it sounded to her less like a lock yielding than like a door being knocked upon from the inside.

His papers lay where they had always lain. She had organized them herself in the first year of her widowhood, when grief had made her industrious and the movement had made her thorough: correspondence by correspondent, alphabetical; speeches by date; financial papers bound in green ribbon. A scholar's arrangement. A widow's fortification. Everything in its place, and nothing — this was the point, she saw now, though she had not seen it then — nothing requiring her to *read* anything.

She lifted out the first folder. Correspondence, A through C.

The letters were innocuous. That was the horror of them, if horror was the word — she still hadn't decided — that after twelve years the drawer contained no smoking pistol, no confession, no single damning line she could point to. What it contained was pattern. A letter here from a man named Corrigan thanking Edmund for "the information regarding Thursday's meeting, which proved most useful." A note there, unsigned, arranging a meeting at a hotel near Charing Cross. A receipt for sums paid into an account she had never known he kept. Individually, each document had an innocent explanation, and over the years she had supplied every one of them with the diligence of a woman proofing her own eulogy. Corrigan had been a journalist; journalists exchanged information. Hotel meetings were how men of affairs conducted business. The account —

The account she had never managed to explain. It sat in the bottom of the drawer like ballast.

She had never fully faced these papers. She told herself, as she told everyone including August, that she kept them locked out of reverence — a widow preserving her husband's effects. And this was true, in the way that a locked door is true: accurate in its description, silent about what stands behind it. The fuller truth was that she had read perhaps a third of the contents, in the first terrible months, and had understood perhaps half of what she read, and had chosen — chosen, deliberately, with the door of her own mind standing open and the wind blowing through it — to stop. To arrange instead of read. To curate instead of confront. She had built an archive around the parts she couldn't bear and called the whole of it love.

Now she sat with Corrigan's letter in her lap and the cold October air still moving through the open window behind her, and she read it a fourth time, a fifth, hunting for the innocent explanation the way one hunts for a mislaid key: certain it must be somewhere, increasingly afraid it never was.

*"The information regarding Thursday's meeting, which proved most useful to our mutual friends."*

Mutual friends. In the cant of the time, in the letters she had seen from other hands across other decades of her life, *mutual friends* meant the police. It meant the Home Office. It meant the men whose names the movement spoke in lowered voices, the ones who took minutes at their own meetings by other means.

But Corrigan might have meant the Liberal club. He might have meant — 

"Might," she said aloud, to the empty room. "Might."

The word was becoming the motto of her interior life, and she despised it.

She worked through the folders methodically, because method was the only courage available to her before ten in the morning. Speeches, 1887 through 1891 — she skimmed these, and found them good, better than her memory of them; Edmund had been a genuinely fine speaker, warm and funny in a way the martyr-legend had ironed flat. Financial papers, green ribbon. She untied the knot and spread the documents across the blotter and there it was, the account, or rather the evidence of it: deposit slips, a passbook copy, entries in Edmund's own upright hand. Sums arriving quarterly. Sums that did not correspond to any income she had ever known him to have, in years when she had personally signed the household accounts down to the coal bill and knew to the shilling what money entered and left this house.

Money from where?

She laid the slips side by side, dates uppermost, and looked at them the way one looks at a family portrait searching for a resemblance no one will name. 1888. 1889. The spring of 1890, when Ada Merrick had been arrested at the Paddington depot and held eleven days without charge, and come out of it changed, quieter, and within two years dead of the decline everyone agreed the prison had given her. The autumn of 1890, when the Kennington branch had been raided on the eve of its largest action, names taken, organizers scattered, and the movement had called it betrayal and hunted the informer for a season and found no one, and Edmund had stood at the funeral meeting and spoken so beautifully about endurance that several women wept.

Marguerite had wept. She remembered weeping. She remembered leaning against her husband's arm afterward in the cab home, and his hand on her shoulder — there it was, the weight of it, recovered at last, retrieved from wherever grief had mislaid it — and saying, *They will never break us, Edmund,* and his answer.

What had his answer been?

She closed her eyes and reached for it and found, with a sensation like stepping off a stair that isn't there, that she could not remember. Twelve years, and the sentence was gone. Only the hand remained, and the warmth of the cab, and her own certainty, radiant and total, that the man beside her was the best man she had ever known.

Certainty, she thought, opening her eyes, is a form of sleep.

* * *

She worked until the light went thin and gray, and then she worked past that, lighting the lamp without noticing she had done it, the way she had once written through whole nights for the cause and emerged at dawn surprised by her own reflection. By four o'clock the desk was covered in documents arranged not alphabetically now but chronologically, and beside them, on a fresh sheet of her own cream-laid paper, she had begun — without deciding to, the hand simply moving, the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth — a second list.

Not a speech. A ledger.

*Corrigan, letter undated, reference to "Thursday's meeting." Which Thursday?*

*Deposit, March 1889, £40. Deposit, June 1889, £40. Source unstated.*

*Paddington raid, September 1890. Who else knew the depot plan? Five people. Edmund was one.*

She wrote the last line and looked at it for a long time. Five people knew. Four of them were dead or scattered beyond finding. The arithmetic was simple and she hated it with a hatred that frightened her because it had nowhere to go — not at the government, which merely paid; not at fate, which merely killed; but at a dead man who could not be confronted, tried, forgiven, or even properly accused, because accusation requires a living face and she had spent twelve years polishing his into marble.

And beneath the marble, all this time, the drawer. Beneath the martyr, the man. Beneath the tribute, the ledger.

The bell rang downstairs — the front bell, deep and domestic — and she heard August's footsteps at last, audible now because he chose to make them so, crossing the hall, opening the door. Voices, low. Then his tread on the stairs, and the knock.

"Come in."

He entered with a card on a small salver, which was absurd and ceremonial and exactly like him. "A Mr. Pell, madam. From the census office. He says he wrote ahead — I don't find a letter." A pause, fractional, that another person would have missed and Marguerite did not, because she had spent twelve years learning to read August the way she was now learning to read a dead man's papers: by what was absent. "I explained you were not receiving. He was... persistent. He asked, in particular, whether the household composition had changed since the last enumeration. Whether anyone had left us."

"Since the last enumeration." She repeated it slowly, tasting the official blandness of it. "Twelve years ago."

"Yes, madam."

Twelve years. The number landed strangely, the way numbers do when they've been waiting all day to mean something. Twelve years since Edmund died. Twelve years since the household had lost — she almost smiled — *anyone*. Unless one counted the driver who had resigned the week after the funeral, a quiet Welshman whose name she'd already forgotten, replaced within a fortnight by a young man with careful hands and silent feet who had been reading her post ever since, presumably, and whom she had never once tested, and whom she trusted more than she had ever trusted her husband, and what did *that* fact belong in — the speech, or the ledger?

"Show him in," she said. "In the drawing room. I'll receive him there."

August hesitated. It was the first hesitation she had ever seen him commit, and it lasted less than a second, and it told her more than the card had. "Shall I stay nearby?"

"No," said Marguerite Ashcombe, and heard, in her own voice, a new register — not the widow, not the chairman, something older and colder that had apparently been waiting in the drawer along with the papers. "Close the door behind you."

He went. She gathered the ledger pages into a folio and locked the drawer, and stood a moment with her hand flat on the oak, feeling through her palm the small dead weight of the lock, and thought: *I am about to lie to a stranger about my household, and I have just lied to myself for twelve years about my husband, and the second lie is the reason the first one comes so easily.*

Then she lifted her chin, arranged her face into the expression the newspapers called *serene*, and went down to meet Mr. Pell.
