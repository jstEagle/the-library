## 4. Pell Returns

The rain came at four o'clock and did not stop, and with it came the sense — old, animal, reliable as the ache in August's knees before frost — that something was wrong.

He had learned long ago to trust that sense more than any letter he had ever steamed open. Letters lied; weather told the truth. And this weather, this low grey pressing on the glass of the carriage house like a palm laid flat against a window, said *someone is thinking about this house tonight.*

He was in the tack room when he heard the knock at the service door. Three knocks, evenly spaced. Not the hurried rattle of a tradesman, not the double rap of the postman who had been coming twelve years and knew better than to stand in the rain. A patient knocking. The kind of knocking a man does when he has all night and knows you know it.

August set down the harness brush. He did not go to the door at once. He stood in the lamplight and let himself feel it plainly: the cold slide of recognition along his spine, the way the body keeps its own ledger even when the mind refuses to write in it. Then he crossed the yard under the rain, hatless because he would not give the visitor the satisfaction of seeing him hurry for a hat, and opened the door.

Mr. Pell stood on the step with an umbrella he had not bothered to open, holding a leather case against his chest the way a clergyman holds a prayer book. Rain ran off the brim of his bowler and dripped from his chin. He looked, if anything, tidier than he had in the morning — as though bad weather were simply a form he was required to complete and had dressed accordingly.

"Mr. Villalobos," he said. "I hope I'm not intruding."

"You are," August said.

Pell smiled. It was a small smile, economical, the smile of a man who budgeted his expressions. "Yes. I rather hoped I was." He tilted his head toward the house, where one window on the ground floor still burned yellow behind the rain-streaked glass. "Is Mrs. Ashcombe at home?"

"She's retired for the evening."

"Ah." Pell let the word sit there between them, dripping. "Then perhaps I might impose on you instead. You being, I imagine, the person in this household who knows where everything is kept."

*Everything,* August thought. Not *everyone.* Everything. As though Marguerite were a house too, and he the man who held her keys.

"There's a porch at the front door," August said. "You can leave whatever it is with the maid tomorrow."

"It isn't a parcel." Pell shifted the leather case under his arm. "It's a conversation. And conversations, Mr. Villalobos, keep badly. Like fish." He glanced past August into the warm dimness of the tack room, at the lamps, the smell of leather and linseed, the orderly dark of the stalls beyond. "Might I come out of the rain? I promise to be brief. Or failing that, honest. One of the two."

Every instinct August possessed — and he possessed the instincts of a man who had spent twelve years deciding, letter by letter, what other people should be allowed to know — told him to close the door. But he had also learned that a thing refused at the doorstep comes back through the window, and windows are harder to watch. Better to let the past knock politely, seat it by the fire, and count exactly what it carried in its pockets.

He stepped aside. "Five minutes."

"I'll need ten." Pell came in, closed his umbrella against the doorframe with a precise shake, and looked around the tack room with the unhurried attention of a man taking inventory. His gaze moved over the harness rack, the labeled tins of brass polish, the ledger open on the workbench in August's careful hand — feed, coal, lamp oil, all of it entered nightly in a script so regular it might have been printed. "You keep good books," Pell said.

"I keep my books."

"Yes," Pell said softly. "I can see that you do."

They sat on either side of the pot-bellied stove, and Pell declined tea with a small movement of his hand that managed to suggest he had refused better offers in grander rooms, and then he sat for a moment saying nothing at all, letting the silence do what silences do — which, August knew better than any man alive, is make people reach into themselves and hand things over.

August did not reach. He waited. It was, he reflected, rather like watching another man attempt to pick a lock that August himself had installed.

"You were in the hall yesterday," Pell said at last. "At the memorial lecture. Standing at the back."

"I drive Mrs. Ashcombe. I wait where I can see her."

"Mm. And did you see me?"

"I saw a man taking notes during a speech about a dead man's virtue."

"And what did you think of that?"

"I thought either he was writing a very admiring obituary or a very unadmiring biography, and I've known both kinds to be written about the same man."

Pell laughed — a short, genuine bark of laughter that seemed to surprise even him. He recovered it quickly, pressed it flat, but for one instant the official face slipped and August glimpsed underneath something tired and quick and hungry. It lasted less than a second. August filed it. He filed everything.

"Mrs. Ashcombe is a considerable woman," Pell said. "Twelve years a widow, and the movement never once faltered under her hand. That takes — discipline."

"It takes her."

"Yes. Well." Pell opened the leather case. He did not take anything out; he merely rested his hand on it, the way a card player rests his hand on his cards to say *I have them.* "I'll be plain with you, Mr. Villalobos, since plainness seems to be the currency here. I wasn't always a census man. The census is — how shall I put it — a useful vantage. People tell census men things they'd never tell a policeman, because a census man only wants numbers. Numbers feel harmless." He turned his hand over, examined the palm as though checking it for ink. "Before this, I was at the Home Office. Eleven years. Special Branch, latterly."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the stable a horse shifted its weight and sighed. August kept his face in the configuration it had held for twelve years — attentive, blank, a servant's face, the face of a man thinking about oats.

"The Home Office," August said. "That explains the questions about twelve years past."

"Does it?"

"Census men ask who lives in a house now. You asked who lived here then."

"A habit of thoroughness." Pell leaned forward slightly. The firelight found the lines around his eyes and deepened them. "Do you know what I did at the Office, Mr. Villalobos? Files. Years and years of files. Names, addresses, subscriptions, meetings attended, speeches given. The suffrage agitation especially — the Office kept a very thick drawer for the suffrage agitation, thicker every year, and every year some new lady gave us fresh paper for it." He paused. "And every drawer, sooner or later, acquires a source. Someone inside who feeds it."

August felt the cold arrive in his hands first, then his chest. He did not move. Twelve years of practice held his body still while everything behind it went white and ringing, the way a struck bell goes silent at the center and loud everywhere else.

"That's a great deal of confidence," August said, "to place in a driver you met yesterday."

"Oh, I haven't placed any confidence in you. Confidence is for friends. What I've placed in you is arithmetic." Pell's voice remained mild, conversational, almost kind. "A movement of hundreds. A betrayal of — well. We'll come to that. The point is that the source was close. Close enough that when the arrests came in 'eighty-seven, they came to the right houses, at the right hours, missing the wrong ones entirely. Someone with access. Someone trusted." He let that hang. "And then Edmund Ashcombe dies — falls, they said, from the Embankment steps, drunk or despairing, poor gentleman — and within a year the arrests stop, the leaks dry up, the drawer goes quiet." He spread his hands, an apology for the obvious. "Arithmetic, Mr. Villalobos."

"Or coincidence."

"Do you believe in coincidence?"

"I believe in minding my own affairs."

"Yes." Pell smiled again, and this time the smile stayed a moment longer, and there was nothing warm in it at all. "So do I. It's why we get on."

He took his hand off the case at last and opened it, and drew out a single sheet of paper, folded once, and laid it on the workbench between them without unfolding it. He did not push it across. He simply made it present, the way one sets a dish on a table.

"Names," Pell said. "Old names. Men and women who passed through this house in the years before Mr. Ashcombe died. Some of them are dead now. Some of them are grandmothers. Some of them, I am told, will be quoted extensively in the centenary histories currently being planned by people who think history is a monument rather than a minefield." He tapped the folded sheet once with one finger. "There's a name on that list I've been trying to place for eleven years. A name that appeared in our files three times in connection with this address, and then vanished — no further record, no trail, nothing. As though someone had taken an eraser to it." He looked up. "You'll forgive me, but erasers are rarely accidents. Someone removed that name. Someone who had access to the files, or to the man whose name it was."

The stove ticked. The rain worked at the roof. August sat absolutely still and understood, with the clarity that comes too late and always precisely on time, that the past had not come knocking politely after all. It had come the way debt collectors come — courteously, with hats, having already established what you owe.

"I couldn't speak to that," August said.

"No. I didn't suppose you could. You strike me as a man who couldn't speak to a great many things." Pell rose, buttoned his coat, collected the case but left the folded paper where it lay. "Keep it. Consider it a calling card. There's a name on it I want — a full name, an account, an explanation of what became of him. In exchange—" He paused at the door, umbrella in hand, and for a moment the official manner dropped entirely, and what looked out through the rain-grey light was something older and wearier, a man who had spent eleven years reading other people's ruin and had begun, somewhere along the way, to want his own piece of the telling. "In exchange, the census error disappears. The household of 'eighty-seven stands as recorded. No historical review. No follow-up correspondence. This house, this lady, this whole careful arrangement — forgotten, officially, forever. You understand what I'm offering. I've seen what happens when the Office decides a household is historically interesting. It doesn't stop being interesting."

"And if I don't have your name?"

Pell settled his hat. "Then I'll find it myself, Mr. Villalobos. I'm patient — it's the whole of my trade. But finding it myself means opening files, and opening files means other eyes, and other eyes mean the centenary historians, and journalists, and God knows what else, all of it arriving on this doorstep in weather considerably worse than this." He opened the door onto the dark and the rain and stood half in, half out, a silhouette with a courteous tilt of the head. "Think of me as the past, if it helps. I always knock politely. But I do keep knocking."

The door closed. The footsteps went away down the gravel, unhurried, measured, a man with all night.

August sat alone by the stove for a long while. Then he reached out and picked up the folded sheet, and held it unopened in both hands, feeling through the paper the faint ghost of its contents — the way, twelve years ago, he had learned to feel letters through their envelopes, secrets through steam, other people's lives through the thin membranes that were supposed to keep them sealed.

He did not unfold it. Not yet. First he carried it to the lamp and held it closer, and read the outside, where in a clerk's precise hand a reference number had been written, and beneath the number two words in the same hand:

*Ashcombe file.*

He stood very still in the tack room, with the rain coming down on the roof like a slow applause, and understood that somewhere in a government drawer lay eleven years of paper with this house's name on it — and that the man who wanted a name from him would settle for nothing less than the whole story, and that Marguerite, upstairs, serene behind her yellow window, did not yet know that the past had found its way back over the threshold.

He folded the list away inside his coat, next to his heart, where he kept everything he had never been meant to hold — and began, in the dark, to plan what he would have to do before morning.
