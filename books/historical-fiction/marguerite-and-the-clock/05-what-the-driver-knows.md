## 5. What the Driver Knows

There were two kinds of nights in the Ashcombe house, and August Villalobos had learned to tell them apart by the sound of the clocks.

On ordinary nights the mantel clock in the drawing room ticked like any other clock — patient, indifferent, a small brass heart keeping time for no one in particular. But on nights when something was wrong, when grief or fury moved through the rooms like weather, the clock seemed to tick louder. Not faster. Louder. As though the house itself had begun listening.

The night he burned Edmund Ashcombe's last letter was a loud-clock night.

August had been in service eleven months then. Eleven months of learning the geography of the house from the outside in: which floorboards spoke, which doors wanted lifting before they would close, which windows caught the wind and sang at three in the morning like widows. He knew the house better than Marguerite did by then. She lived inside her work the way other people lived inside their skin, and she noticed nothing that did not concern the vote, the movement, the great slow machinery of persuasion she had given herself to body and soul.

He had been hired as a driver and had made himself something else without anyone quite authorizing it. He read the post first. Not because anyone asked him to — because he could not help it, because the letters came addressed to *Mrs. E. Ashcombe* in hands that trembled with hope or threat, and because somewhere in his second month he had understood that the woman who signed those replies with such ferocious clarity was also a woman who left the kitchen door unlatched at night, who walked home from meetings alone through streets where men spat the word *suffragist* like a stone from the teeth, who trusted the world because she could not afford to distrust it and keep working.

So August steamed the envelopes open over the kettle, read what needed reading, memorized what needed memorizing, and delivered the rest untouched. He kept a ledger in his head — no paper, never paper — of names and threats and promises, of the correspondent in Leeds who wrote love letters under a false name hoping to compromise her, of the gentleman in Whitehall whose polite inquiries about her travel schedule made August's spine go cold. He learned the art of protective silence the way a surgeon learns anatomy: thoroughly, privately, and with a certain horror at how much there was to know.

He told himself it was service. He told himself it was devotion, though he was careful not to examine too closely what kind. He was twenty-six years old and the daughter of a Cornish fisherman and he had no business feeling what he felt when Mrs. Ashcombe laughed at her own jokes over breakfast, so he folded the feeling small and put it away in the same drawer where he kept everything else that did not concern the driving of the motor car.

And then, on a wet October night in 1887, Edmund Ashcombe came home.

*

August heard the carriage before he saw it — wheels on gravel at half past eleven, far later than the London train could account for. He went out with the lamp because that was his place, and found Mr. Ashcombe standing in the drive in the rain without an umbrella, looking up at the lit window of the drawing room the way a man looks at a country he has been exiled from.

"Evening, Villalobos." Edmund's voice was pleasant as ever. It was always pleasant. That was the thing about Edmund Ashcombe that August had never managed to explain to himself: the man was pleasant the way still water is still, and you only learned about the depth when something went down into it and did not come back up.

"Sir. Shall I take your bag?"

"There is no bag." A pause, rain ticking on the lamp glass between them. "I've come to say goodnight to my wife."

August led him in, hung his coat, announced him — though the announcement was a formality; the whole street had probably heard the gravel. And then August withdrew to the hall below, because withdrawing was also his art, and stood in the dark with his back against the newel post while above him two voices spoke in a register too low for words.

He did not eavesdrop. He wants that recorded, wherever such things are recorded: he did not listen for words. But you cannot stand beneath a room where a marriage is ending and hear nothing. The sounds rose and fell for the better part of an hour — Marguerite's voice sharp once, twice, cracking on the third edge like ice underfoot — and then silence, long enough that August began to think they had simply gone to bed in separate silences, which happened more often than the household acknowledged.

Then Edmund came down alone.

He stopped in the hall. He looked at August standing by the stairs with the lamp turned low, and for a moment — one moment that August would spend the rest of his life turning over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit — Edmund Ashcombe looked frightened.

"You're the new man," Edmund said. "Villalobos."

"Yes, sir."

"My wife will want to burn something tonight." He said it mildly, almost kindly, the way he might have said *she'll want the doctor*. "When she does — don't watch. Don't ask. Don't remember." He put his hand briefly on August's shoulder, and his grip was light and cold. "You seem like a young man who knows how not to remember."

Then he went back out into the rain and August drove him to the station himself, because who else would, and the whole way Edmund sat with his hat off and his eyes closed, and August watched him in the mirror and thought: *he looks like a man going to pay a debt.*

Edmund Ashcombe died four days later. Heart failure, said the doctors, brought on by overwork. The movement buried him as a martyr within the month.

Marguerite burned the letter the night after the funeral.

*

She came down to the kitchen herself. That was the first wrongness — Mrs. Ashcombe did not come to kitchens; kitchens came to her, in the person of cooks and maids who had mostly been let go since the funeral, the house running thinner every week as money went to the movement instead. So it was nearly midnight and the kitchen held only August and the banked stove, and then the door opened and there she stood in her black mourning dress with her hair coming loose from its pins and a letter in her hand.

Her face, he thought afterward for years, her face was the strangest thing he had ever seen, because it was composed and destroyed at the same time. Composed around the destruction. A woman holding a broken vase together with her bare hands and smiling so no one would see the blood.

"Villalobos," she said. "Are you awake?"

"No, ma'am."

Something moved through her eyes — not quite amusement; recognition, maybe, of a fellow practitioner. Of the trade they both plied without yet admitting they shared it.

"I need the fire," she said.

He fed the stove. He knelt at the iron door and coaxed the coals with the poker until they glowed, and all the while she stood behind him with the letter, and the kitchen was very quiet except for the fire finding its appetite.

"He wrote this before he died," she said. Her voice was level. Too level, the way ice is level right before it takes your weight. "It arrived today. From his solicitor. He — left instructions. That I should receive it after—" She stopped. Started again. "It doesn't matter. What matters is that it must be burned, and I find that my hands are—" She looked down at her hands as if they belonged to a stranger. They were shaking. Fine, fast, continuous, like the hands of a woman holding a bird she means to release but cannot.

August rose and turned and held out his palm. He did not say anything. Saying anything would have required knowing what to say, and he knew nothing, then or ever, about the interior country where Marguerite Ashcombe lived. He only knew that a lady does not burn her husband's last letter with trembling hands if the letter contains anything ordinary.

She looked at his open hand for a long moment.

"You haven't read it?" she said.

"No, ma'am."

"It came through the post. Through you." There was a question in it, thin as wire. Twelve years later, standing in the same kitchen grown older around him, August would still feel that wire against his skin.

"The solicitor sent it special courier," he said, which was true, "and it was marked private, and some things—" He stopped himself before the sentence became a confession of his entire method. "Some things I don't read."

It was the first lie he ever told her. It was not even a lie, exactly. It was a truth wearing another truth's coat, which he would learn was the most durable kind.

She put the letter in his hand.

It was heavier than paper should be. Two pages, folded once, sealed — no, the seal had been broken and refastened, clumsily, by someone who had wanted to know what it said and had wanted it known afterward that they didn't. The solicitor, presumably. Or someone else. August registered this in the part of his mind that registered everything, filed it in the drawer, and did not look at the writing, did not let his eyes fall to the salutation, kept his gaze fixed on Marguerite's face instead, which was watching him with an expression he could not name and would spend a decade trying to.

"Burn it," she said. "Don't read it. Whatever he says in it, whatever he—" Her voice did something then, dropped through the composed surface into the wreck beneath, one syllable of raw sound before she sealed it again. "I am asking you, Villalobos. As one keeper of things. Burn it unread."

*As one keeper of things.* He carried that phrase the way other men carry wounds. She had seen him. In the middle of the worst hour of her life she had looked at the driver kneeling at her stove and seen exactly what he was, and rather than being horrified she had conscripted him.

"As you wish, ma'am."

He fed the letter to the fire unopened. He held it with the tongs and pushed it deep into the coals and watched the paper catch — the edges curling brown, then black, then gone — and he kept his eyes on the burning the whole time because looking away would have been worse than reading. Somewhere in the middle pages the fire reached something dense, wax perhaps, a seal or a wafer, and there was a small green flame, brief and chemical, and August thought: *there. That's the secret leaving.* Superstition, of course. Paper burns to ash regardless of what it carried. But the green flame stayed with him.

Marguerite watched beside him. Their shoulders were nearly touching at the stove's iron lip. Neither moved away.

"He was a good man," she said to the fire. It was not a statement seeking agreement. It was a statement daring the fire to contradict it.

"Yes, ma'am," said August, who had watched Edmund Ashcombe leave the house looking like a man going to pay a debt, and who understood in that moment — fully, terribly, for the first time — that he had just become complicit in whatever that debt had been. He had burned the receipt. Whatever Edmund had confessed in those two pages, whatever crime or betrayal or simple human rot he had set down in his careful pleasant hand, it was ash now, and August had fed it to the coals without reading it, because a woman with shaking hands had asked him to, and because asking was the one currency he could never refuse her.

Guilt, he learned that night, is loyalty's tax. You do not get to choose whether to pay it. You only get to choose what you buy with the loyalty, and whether the purchase was worth the levy, and you spend years doing the arithmetic and it never comes out clean.

"There," Marguerite said softly, when the last corner whitened and crumbled. "There."

And then she did something he never expected and never forgot. She reached out and laid her hand flat on the warm iron above the stove door — not touching him, touching the metal where the heat of the burning had gathered — and left it there until it must have hurt, until August saw her fingers whiten with pressure, and then she took it away and straightened and said, in the full clear voice of the platform:

"I shall want the motor car at nine. The committee meets at ten, and we have a petition to deliver, and I will not be late for it."

"Yes, ma'am."

She went to the door and paused there, her hand on the frame, her back to him. For a moment August thought she would say something else — something true, something that would have changed everything, spared everything. The clock in the hall ticked like a held breath.

"Goodnight, Villalobos," she said. "Thank you for not remembering."

And she climbed the stairs out of the kitchen and out of the moment, and August stood alone with the dying fire and understood that he had just agreed to carry something he could not see, for a woman he could not refuse, into a future neither of them could imagine — and that the weight of it, unseen, would prove heavier than anything he might have read.

He raked the ashes at dawn. Among them he found one fragment the fire had spared, a scrap no bigger than a stamp, and on it, in ink gone brown at the scorched edge, three words in a hand he recognized as Edmund Ashcombe's pleasant, careful script:

*forgive me — M—*

Not *forgive me, Marguerite.* The dash after the M. An initial. Someone else's initial. Someone Edmund had wronged and named only by a letter, and August stood in the grey dawn kitchen with the scrap in his glove and the choice in his chest, and he burned it a second time, separately, in the kettle flame, and ground the ash fine between his fingers, and told no one, and did not sleep, and went at nine o'clock to bring the motor car around as instructed.

That was twelve years ago. He had done the arithmetic ten thousand times since and it had never once come out clean.

*

Now it was 1899, and the arithmetic had developed complications.

August stood in the stable yard the morning after Pell's visit, working a brush along the motor car's flank harder than the paint deserved, and turned the old coin over yet again. *Forgive me — M—.* He had built twelve years of protective silence on top of that scrap of ash, and now a soft-voiced man with a census portfolio and Home Office eyes had come to the door asking questions about the household as it stood twelve years past, and August had lied to him fluently, expertly, and had watched Marguerite watching him lie, and had seen in her face something he had never seen there before.

Not suspicion. Worse. Recognition.

She had looked at him deflecting Pell the way she had once looked at him across the kitchen stove — *as one keeper of things* — but older now, and colder, and with a question in it that the younger look had never contained. Twelve years ago she had asked him to burn a letter and he had obeyed. Yesterday she had watched him bury a question and she had said nothing at all, and the nothing had lasted too long, and August knew — with the certainty of a man who has spent his life reading what people do not write — that the silence between them had begun to change composition.

He set down the brush. His hands smelled of petrol and horse and, faintly, absurdly, of ash — memory playing its tricks.

From the open window of the morning room above, he could hear Marguerite at her desk, dictating to herself the opening lines of the anniversary speech, Edmund's name rising and falling in it like a bell rung over water. *A good man,* the speech would say. *A martyr. A loss the movement has never repaired.* And August would stand at the back of the hall next week and listen to the myth pronounced over the man, and keep his face arranged, and pay the tax again.

Unless.

He looked toward the lane, where the gravel curved out of sight behind the hedge, and thought about Mr. Pell walking away down it yesterday with his notebook closed and his patience open, a man who collected names the way August once collected secrets, and who had asked one question too many and then, politely, terribly, promised to call again.

The clock in the hall struck nine. Marguerite's voice stopped mid-sentence.

In the quiet that followed, August heard her chair push back, and her steps cross to the window, and pause — directly above him, he somehow knew, looking down at the yard, at the car, at the man she had conscripted twelve years ago and never released.

He raised his head. He could not see her through the morning glare, but he lifted his hand anyway — a driver's small salute, duty acknowledged — and waited to see whether she would return it, or turn away, or come down the stairs and ask him, at last, the question that had been smouldering between them since a wet October night in 1887.

Her silhouette shifted at the glass. Then the curtain moved, and she was gone, and her footsteps started down the corridor toward the stairs.
