## 8. The Locked Drawer

Marguerite did not go straight home after the anniversary meeting. She told the cabman to take the long way, along the Embankment, where the gas lamps came on one by one in the blue dusk like a procession of small reluctant witnesses, and she sat with her gloved hands folded in her lap and watched the black water slide past and tried to remember what she had said from the podium.

She remembered the hall — that much. The heat of it, the smell of wet wool and camphor and the tea urn's steam, three hundred women and a scattering of patient husbands packed into the Mechanics' Institute because the larger halls had grown too dear or too cautious. She remembered Mrs. Fenwick in the second row with her hands folded over her stick like a woman at her own funeral, determined to be punctual for it. She remembered the moment her eyes found the man at the back, the one who was not a member of anything, who had come through the doors without paying his penny and stood against the wall with a little notebook open in his palm.

Mr. Pell. Census taker. Or whatever else he was.

And then — here was the terrible thing, the thing she turned over and over as the river slid by — she could not remember a single sentence she had spoken after seeing him. She had given the Edmund Ashcombe memorial address. She had spoken of his courage, his gentleness, the letter he had written her the week before his death which she kept framed above her desk. The words had come out of her in their accustomed order, worn smooth by eleven years of annual delivery, and the hall had wept in its accustomed places, and all the while some cold clerkish part of her mind had stood aside and observed: *you are lying to three hundred people, and you cannot remember doing it.*

Not lying, she corrected herself. Believing. There was a difference. There had to be a difference, or everything she had built for twelve years was founded on a word she no longer trusted.

The cab turned into Cavendish Row. The house stood waiting with its lit study window, and behind that window, she knew without looking, August would be at the desk he kept in the corner, sorting the day's correspondence into piles whose logic only he understood. Twelve years of piles. Twelve years of a man who handed her the world each morning already edited, already safe.

*As they were twelve years past,* Mr. Pell had asked, pen poised over the census schedule, smiling his small dry smile. And August had answered so smoothly, so instantly, that Marguerite — watching him do it from the doorway — had felt something turn over in her chest that she could not name. Not suspicion. Not quite. It was closer to vertigo: the sudden knowledge that the floor one has walked upon all one's life is also a ceiling, and there are rooms beneath it.

She paid the cabman and went up the steps slowly, letting the cold climb her legs like a tide.

* * *

August met her in the hall with her wrap already off his arm, as though he had heard the wheels stop.

"The fire's laid in your study," he said. "There's a letter from Manchester — I've put it on top. Nothing urgent."

"Was Mr. Pell here again?"

"No." A pause so brief it barely existed. "No one."

She looked at him. He looked back with that composure she had relied upon for twelve years the way one relies on a wall — not thinking about it, simply leaning. He was fifty-one years old and moved like a man carrying something breakable in both hands, always, everywhere, even empty-handed. She had never once asked him what it was.

"Thank you, August."

"Shall I bring tea?"

"In half an hour. I have reading to do first."

Something passed across his face — not fear, nothing so legible — a tightening, like water skinning over. "Very good, ma'am."

*Ma'am.* After twelve years. She climbed the stairs and hated herself for noticing how carefully he did not ask what reading.

* * *

The study smelled of coal smoke and old paper. On the desk, exactly where he said, lay the Manchester letter, and beneath it four others squared and aligned, and she understood suddenly, with the clarity that comes too late in the evening, that she did not know what he had read of them before they reached her. Of course she didn't. That was the arrangement; that had been the arrangement since 1887, since the first year, when she had been a widow of twenty-six drowning in correspondence from strangers who wanted pieces of her husband — his story, his legend, his death — and August had appeared at her door with the mail sorted and said, in his flat careful voice, *I took the liberty, ma'am, of setting aside the ones that seemed designed to wound you.*

*I read them first.* He had told her plainly, that first week, standing with his cap in his hands. *If you'd rather I didn't—*

*I'd rather you did,* she had said, and meant: *I cannot carry all of this myself.*

And so for twelve years he had carried it. And she had leaned on the wall.

The drawer was in the bottom of the writing cabinet, left-hand side, and it locked, and the key lived in the second compartment of her jewellery case behind the garnet brooch where no thief would look and where she herself had looked perhaps six times in eleven years.

Edmund's papers.

She knelt on the rug before the cabinet with the key in her fingers and did not move for a long time. The clock on the mantel ticked with the patience of something that has outlasted every argument ever conducted beneath it. Downstairs a door closed softly; August moving through the house, closing up the day.

She had opened this drawer twice since his death. Once, in the first raw month, when she had taken out only his last letter — the beautiful one, the framed one — and shut the drawer again as one shuts a coffin. Once, three years ago, when Miss Hartley of the movement had wanted the text of a speech Edmund gave in '85, and Marguerite had found it, copied it, and relocked the rest unread.

Because the rest was not speeches. The rest was letters she had never had the courage to be done with — neither to read nor to burn — and she had let them sit in the dark these eleven years like a body under floorboards, telling herself: *later, when I am stronger; later, when the movement is secure; later* — and now a man with a notebook and a Home Office past had stood at the back of a hall full of women and written things down while she lied about her dead husband, and *later* had run out.

The key turned. The lock gave with a sound like a small bone breaking.

* * *

The papers were bundled in ribbon that had once been white. She carried them to the desk, spread them under the lamp, and made herself look at them in order, because disorder was a form of cowardice.

Letters. Perhaps forty of them, tied in three bundles. She untied the first bundle and saw Edmund's hand — she knew it as she knew her own heartbeat, the strong forward slant of it, the way his *g*s descended like men leaning into wind — and relief went through her like warm water. His letters. To colleagues, mostly. To Mrs. Fawcett's circle. To a printer in Manchester. To—

To a man named Corrigan.

She had never heard Marguerite say the name aloud, but her eye snagged on it the way a skirt snags on a nail. R. J. Corrigan. Four letters in the bundle, all from Edmund, all short, all in a tone she had never heard him use with comrades. Careful. Guarded. Almost — she turned one to the lamp and read it twice — almost furtive.

*...you will understand that I can say nothing in an open letter. If the third name is required, send word by the usual means and I will contrive to be at the station Thursday. I will not put more in writing than I must. You know my situation — a man in mine cannot choose whom he serves, only how honestly he serves them...*

She read it a fourth time. A fifth. The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the house a pipe gurgled as the water cooled.

*A man in mine cannot choose whom he serves.*

It meant nothing. It could mean nothing. Correspondence was coded in those years — everyone wrote in shadows, everyone used the language of commerce and family to move information past the censors. *Served* might mean the cause. *Corrigan* might be a printer, a financier, a cousin. She set the letter down with hands that were not entirely steady and untied the second bundle, telling herself she was looking for the '85 speech, only the speech, and knowing perfectly well she was not.

The second bundle was not in Edmund's hand.

It was letters *to* him — and the handwriting on the first stopped her breath, because she knew it. Everyone in the movement had known it. Alice Pell— no. Alice *Wray*, who had been arrested at Bow Street in '86, who had gone to Holloway for five months, who had come out thin and bright-eyed and sworn on the steps of the prison that prison would never make her quiet, who had died — how? When? Marguerite had lost track of Alice Wray years ago; the movement consumed its own and scattered them like chaff.

Alice Wray's handwriting, dated eight days before Edmund died:

*Mr. Ashcombe — I write against my better judgment and because Mrs. Fenwick says you are the only one with the ear of the right people. Someone has been passing names. Three of us were followed home Tuesday by a man who knew our faces without being shown them. If it is one of us, God forgive us, but if it is one of us we must know. You are respected. You could ask questions none of the rest of us can. Do it, and do it quietly, and tell me only if the answer is clean...*

Marguerite set the page down.

The room had gone very far away. The lamp burned steadily, absurdly, illuminating a small circle of paper while the rest of the world receded like a tide going out.

Someone has been passing names.

Three of us were followed home.

She picked the letter up again and forced herself through it line by line, hunting for the sentence that would resolve it — *and Edmund replied, and the answer was clean, and Alice was satisfied* — but there was no such sentence, because this was Alice's letter, and the reply, if Edmund had written one, would be in the third bundle, in his hand, and her fingers were shaking so badly she tore the ribbon untying it.

The third bundle held his replies. She found Alice's name. One letter, dated two days after hers.

*Dear Miss Wray — I have made inquiries as you asked. I will say only what I can swear to: the information does not come from within our number. Set your mind at rest, and tell Mrs. Fenwick likewise. Some matters are best allowed to settle of themselves. Yours in confidence, E. Ashcombe.*

*The information does not come from within our number.*

He had lied. Sitting at this desk, in this room, with this lamp — or one very like it — he had written to a frightened young woman who had spent five months in Holloway and told her the answer was clean, and it had not been clean, and Alice Wray had vanished from the movement's records within the year, and Edmund had died — how had Edmund died?

A carriage accident. A wet road outside Bath. Eleven years of memorial addresses, and Marguerite realized she had never once asked herself whether the road was wet enough to explain the finality of it, because grief did not require explanations and myth preferred to travel light.

She stood up. Her knees objected; she ignored them. She walked to the window because walking was possible and sitting was not, and looked out at the dark garden where the greenhouse glass caught the streetlight in a long pale smudge.

*He informed.* The thought arrived whole, unbidden, in someone else's voice, and she struck it down. *No. There is another explanation. There must be. Corrigan was a printer. Serves means—*

But the letters kept arranging themselves in her mind the way evidence arranges itself, inexorably, each one clicking into the next. A man who met a stranger at stations. A man who refused to put things in writing and yet put *this* in writing, to Alice, the reassurance — why reassure her falsely unless silence itself was the service? A man whose widow had become, without ever choosing it, the most protected woman in England, whose mail was read before it reached her, whose enemies had somehow never materialized, whose movement had prospered while other movements bled—

The thought came again and this time she could not strike it down, and it was not about Edmund at all.

*Who reads your letters first, Marguerite?*

She pressed her hand flat against the cold glass. Below, the garden lay in darkness. The carriage house was dark too. August would be in the kitchen still, banked the stove, locking the side door, performing the small nightly liturgy of protection he had performed ten thousand times.

Twelve years, Pell had asked. As they were twelve years past.

Edmund had died twelve years ago. August had come to them eleven years ago — hired the spring after, a widower's driver recommended by a friend of Corrigan's, she suddenly could not remember, recommended by whom, she had never asked, she had been drowning and a steady pair of hands had appeared at the rail—

No. No, she was exhausted and grieving in advance and seeing conspiracies in coincidence, this was what fear did, it was a fabricator, it invented patterns the way spiders invented webs, out of their own bodies, endlessly—

But the letters lay on the desk behind her, forty of them, and one of them was a lie in her dead husband's hand addressed to a woman who had then disappeared.

* * *

She did not know how long she stood at the window. Long enough for the lamp oil to gutter once. Long enough for the clock below the mantel shelf to lose count of something.

When she turned back, she did not rebundle the papers properly. She gathered them against her chest the way one gathers fallen washing before rain — quickly, protectively, in no particular order — and she looked at the drawer, and she did not put them back.

That was the decision, though she did not know it yet, though it would take her days to understand what she had done: she did not put them back in the drawer. She crossed to the bookshelf instead and slid the bundles flat behind the row of blue-bound parliamentary reports where the spines hid them completely, and she returned the empty drawer to its lock, and she put the key back behind the garnet brooch, and she stood a moment in the middle of her own study feeling the strange lightness and strangeness of a room in which something has been moved.

Then she bathed her face, pinned her hair afresh, and rang for tea.

August brought it. He always did. He set the tray on the table beside her chair, aligned the cup's handle to the right, and paused — she saw him pause, a fraction, his eyes going from her face to the cabinet and back, quick as a fish turning — before saying:

"You've been reading."

"I have."

"Will there be anything else, ma'am?"

She looked up at him. His face was its usual settled grey calm, weathered and reliable as slate, and she thought: *he has read ten thousand letters and handed me the world already edited, and I have thanked him for it every morning for eleven years, and tonight I do not know whether I am looking at a wall or a door.*

"No, August," she said. "Nothing else. Good night."

"Good night, ma'am." At the door he stopped, half-turned, and for one unguarded instant he looked older than his years and tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "The fire will want banking before you retire."

"Yes."

He went. She heard his tread on the stairs, unhurried, even, the tread of a man with nothing hidden, and she sat alone with her cooling tea and the ticking clock and the letters flat behind the parliamentary reports, and downstairs, faintly, she heard the side door lock, and understood that whatever she had found in the drawer tonight, she had already decided — without deciding, the way one decides everything that matters — to tell no one.

Not the movement. Not Fenwick. Not Okafor, whoever she was, whose letter waited upstairs in the pile August had sorted.

And not August.

Especially not August.

Outside, the last lamplighter was making his way down Cavendish Row, and the flames bloomed behind him one by one, and Marguerite sat in the light of her own lamp with her husband's lie warm in her memory and her protector's footsteps fading overhead, and she thought, with a clarity that felt less like understanding than like falling: *twelve years. Everyone keeps asking about twelve years.*

The clock on the mantel ticked toward midnight, indifferent, exact, keeping an appointment no one had made with it yet.
