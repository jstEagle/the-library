## 9. Loyalty of Twelve Years

### August

He knew her the way a lighthouse keeper knows weather — not by instruments but by the quality of the air itself, by some pressure behind the fog that arrived before the fog did. For twelve years August Villalobos had read that pressure in Marguerite Ashcombe's step on the gravel, in the angle of her hat pins, in whether she took sugar in her morning tea. He had catalogued her silences the way other men collected stamps: the silence of fatigue, the silence of strategy, the silence of grief worn smooth by use.

The silence she had carried for six days now was none of these. It was new. And new things, in that house, were dangerous.

He noticed it first at breakfast. She sat at the long table in the morning room with the paper unopened before her, and she was not reading it — she was looking through it, through the wallpaper, through the brick of the house, at something he could not see and had not been shown. Her hands were still. That was the tell. Marguerite Ashcombe's hands never rested; they smoothed napkins, turned cups, punctuated arguments no one else could hear. Still hands meant a mind gripped around something too tightly to let go of, or too heavy to lift.

"More tea?" he asked from his post by the sideboard, where he stood each morning like furniture that had learned to speak.

"No. Thank you." A pause, precisely one beat too long. "August."

"Yes, madam."

Nothing followed. She picked up the paper after all, unfolded it with unnecessary force, and disappeared behind it as though it were a door.

He went out to the Daimler and worked a rag over the mudguards for an hour without accomplishing anything. The gravel drive held the morning sun like a held breath. From here he could see her window, third from the left above the portico, where the curtain moved once and did not move again.

Six days since Pell had come back off-hours. Six days since August had refused him at the door with the flat courtesy of a man who has decided exactly how much trouble he is willing to make, and then — this was the part that sat wrong in his stomach, that woke him at three in the morning staring at the ceiling of the carriage house — had not reported him. Not to the police. Not to Marguerite. A man claiming Home Office credentials had come sniffing at Edmund Ashcombe's name, at the census, at *her*, and August had closed the door on him and said nothing, because saying something would mean explaining what Pell had wanted, and what Pell had wanted would mean opening the drawer that had been locked for twelve years.

Complicity begins with a single unreported kindness. He'd heard that somewhere, or invented it; lately he could no longer tell which of his thoughts were his own and which were the sediment of all the letters he had read.

On the seventh day, he found her in the study.

She was standing at the window with her back to the door, and on the desk before her lay a sheet of foolscap covered in her handwriting — but crossed out, line after line struck through with such violence the pen had torn the page in two places. She didn't hear him come in. Or she heard him and permitted it, which amounted to the same thing between them now.

"Madam." He stopped three feet inside the door, hands clasped behind him, the posture of a servant who has knocked invisibly for so long the knocking has become part of his spine. "You've taken nothing since yesterday noon."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're writing a speech you keep destroying."

That brought her around. Her eyes had the brightness of a woman running a fever of thought, and beneath the brightness, something he had never seen in them before: fear. Not the ordinary fear of hecklers and constables and hostile editors, which she wore like a familiar coat. This was the other kind. The private kind. The kind he had spent twelve years making sure she would never need to feel.

"Have you ever," she said slowly, "written a eulogy for a man you cannot decide how to mourn?"

*There it is.* His heart performed a single hard knock against his ribs and went quiet again, trained quiet, professional quiet. "I should think," he said carefully, "that any honest person would struggle with that."

She looked at him for a long moment. Looking for what? For the crack in him. For evidence that he knew more than he said — though God knew there was enough of that to furnish a cathedral. Whatever she found, or failed to find, she turned back to the window.

"The anniversary dinner is Friday," she said. "Three hundred people. The movement's whole history will be in that room wearing its best clothes. And I am expected to stand up and call Edmund Ashcombe a martyr."

"And are you not going to?"

"I don't know what I am going to do." It came out of her like something wrenched rather than spoken. Then, immediately, the armor came down over it — he watched it happen, watched her shoulders square and her chin set and her voice flatten into the register she used with journalists. "Thank you, August. Send Cook up some broth. I'll eat it if it kills me."

He withdrew. In the corridor he stopped, one hand against the wall, and made himself face the arithmetic he had been refusing all week.

She knew something. Something about Edmund. He did not know what, but he knew the shape of knowing, and hers matched it exactly. And if she knew, then the papers in the locked drawer were no longer sleeping; they were awake in her mind, doing whatever damage papers do when they finally get read.

Which left him one move. It was the same move he had always had. The only move he had ever been permitted.

He waited until dusk, when the lamps came on in the study and he could see her silhouette bent over the desk. Then he climbed the stairs he had climbed ten thousand times as a servant, carrying things, removing things, and knocked — actually knocked, for the first time in years, because what he was about to offer deserved a door.

"Come in, August," she said, before his knuckles finished falling. Of course she knew his tread. She always had.

He entered and stood before the desk, and the lamplight lay across the ruined foolscap between them like a border neither dared cross.

"Twelve years ago," he said, "you asked me to burn a letter. I burned it without reading it. It was the hardest thing I have ever done, and I have driven you through mobs."

Marguerite went very still. "August—"

"Let me finish, because if I don't say it tonight I'll find a way never to say it." His voice stayed level; he had practiced levelness the way she practiced speeches. "Whatever is in that drawer — whatever you found, or suspect, or are afraid of — I can burn all of it. Tonight, tomorrow, whenever you choose. No one will know it existed except us, and we can arrange even that. Pell gets nothing. The historians get nothing. The myth stays whole and you stay free, and Friday you give your speech, and it will be true in every way that matters."

"In every way that—" She laughed, and it was the worst sound he had ever heard from her, thin and cracked as ice on a March pond. "In every way that matters. Do you hear yourself?"

"I hear myself clearly, madam. I've had twelve years of practice."

"And what does it cost, this burning? Have you priced it?" She came around the desk toward him, and for the first time in their entire acquaintance she was angry *at* him — not near him, not past him, at him, and he found he could not breathe properly under the weight of it. "You offer me fire the way Cook offers bread. As if it costs nothing. As if twelve years ago you didn't stand in the yard holding that letter while I begged you, and burn it, and become — oh, what did you become, August? My accomplice? My priest? My grave-digger? You've buried so many things for me I've lost count of the graves."

"I kept you safe."

"*Did* you?" Her voice rose and broke and rose again. "Or did you build me a beautiful little cell with bars made of my own secrets and call it safety? Every time I handed you something to destroy, I handed you a piece of myself, and you — you *kept* them, didn't you? You remember everything. I've seen you remember. You walk through this house carrying all my dead letters in your head like a man carrying coals, and you never once dropped one, and I never once thanked you, because thanking you would mean admitting what we were doing."

"We were surviving."

"We were *hiding*." She was close to him now, close enough that he could see her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "And now something has come out of the ground that fire won't fix, and you stand there offering me matches because it's the only language you know."

The word *something* hung in the room. Neither of them touched it. Both of them knew it was the drawer, the papers, the thing she would not name — and both understood, in the same terrible instant, that the other understood.

Her hesitation lasted perhaps four seconds. Four seconds in which her hand actually rose — he saw it rise, saw her fingers half-curl as if reaching for the matches he had not yet produced — and then stopped in the air, trembling, and lowered.

Four seconds. He would live inside those four seconds for the rest of his life.

"You hesitate," he said quietly.

"I am thinking."

"No." He shook his head, slow, sorrowful, and felt something in his chest tear loose that had held him upright for twelve years. "Thinking looks different. I have watched you think your whole life. This is doubt. You doubt whether the lie can be saved. And madam—" his voice caught, and he let it, for once, for once he let it show — "if you doubt it, then I have failed. Because keeping you safe was supposed to mean keeping you certain."

"Your job was never to keep me certain. Your job was the horses and the door and the discretion." But there was no iron left in it. She sat down heavily in the chair behind her, all the fury gone out of her at once, leaving something smaller and more frightened in its place. "Oh, August. Look at us."

He looked. Two people in a lamplit room, divided by a desk and a decade of unspoken bargains, each waiting for the other to say the unsayable thing first.

"Burn it or keep it," she said at last, wearily, waving at the drawer without looking at it. "Decide nothing tonight. I can't — I need to think about Friday. About what I say to three hundred people about a man I may have married a fiction."

"May have?"

"*Leave it.*"

He left it. He bowed — the old bow, the servant's bow, and hated himself for the smoothness of it — and went out, and closed the door softly, and stood in the dark corridor listening to the clock on the landing tick through sixty seconds in which neither of them called the other back.

### Marguerite

When he was gone she put her face in her hands and allowed herself, briefly, the luxury of despair.

It was a luxury. She had earned that much. Thirty years of public life bought a woman five private minutes of collapse per crisis, provided she scheduled them.

But despair was not what kept surfacing through the wreckage of the argument. What surfaced was shame, and worse than shame, recognition. Everything she had flung at August had been true, and every true thing had been a stone thrown to keep him from throwing his own. *You hesitated,* he had said, and the words had gone into her like a splinter, because he was right, and because his being right meant he could see her — really see her — and being seen was the thing she had spent a lifetime arranging her life to avoid.

She rose and went to the drawer.

The key lived in her reticule now, had lived there for six days, riding against her hip through meals and meetings and sleepless nights like a small cold tumor. She unlocked the drawer and looked down at the contents without touching them: the correspondence, the bank drafts with their government stamps, the letter in a clerk's precise hand listing names — names she knew, names whose widows she had embraced at funerals, names she had toasted as martyrs at twenty dinners — and beside them the draft of Friday's speech, martyrdom polished into marble.

She had told no one. Not Fenwick, though Fenwick had sent that strange card last month with its hint of old suspicions. Not Okafor, whoever she was, the young academic whose letter sat unanswered upstairs. And not August — most certainly not August, because August offered fire, and she was beginning to understand, in the deep place where understanding arrives unwelcome and stays, that fire was the family inheritance. Her husband had sold truth for safety. She had bought safety with silence. The whole house, the whole movement, the whole shining legend was built on the same transaction, generation upon generation, and here was her driver, her dear impossible loyal driver, holding out the matches for the next round.

*I built him,* she thought. *No. I hired a man and taught him, year by year, that love meant destruction of evidence. And then I wonder that he knows no other language.*

She closed the drawer and locked it and stood with her forehead against the cool wood.

Friday. Three hundred people. Somewhere in the city, Pell was taking notes in crowds, and somewhere beyond him were men who had paid Edmund's price and would very much like to know whether the widow intended to honor the contract or break it. And somewhere — this thought arrived last and settled deepest — somewhere there might be a way through that required neither the lie nor the bonfire, some third thing, some terrible honest thing, that she could not yet imagine because she had never once, in fifty-one years, been permitted to practice imagining it.

She blew out the lamp. In the darkness the mantel clock counted on, indifferent, keeping the appointment it had kept through her marriage and her widowhood and her twelve careful years.

Down in the carriage house, she knew, August was awake. She could feel it the way he felt her moods — some wire strung between them that no argument had ever managed to cut.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow I will decide whether I am my husband's widow or my own woman.

She did not know that downstairs, in the hall rack, a letter had arrived with the evening post — a young, unfamiliar hand, a foreign-sounding name, Miss Adaeze Okafor — requesting access, on behalf of a centenary committee, to the complete papers of Edmund Ashcombe.

*Complete*. The word lay on the envelope like a lit fuse.
