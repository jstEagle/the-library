## 10. The Historian's Letter

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing at all, except that Tuesdays were the days the second post came late and August always brought it up himself rather than letting the maid carry it, and so when Marguerite saw his footsteps pause outside her study door — that fractional hesitation she had learned to read through oak and plaster as though it were a knock — she knew before the envelope touched her desk that this one was different.

He set it down among the others without comment. He was very good at without comment; it was practically a profession. But he set it down with its face toward her, squared to the edge of the blotter like a chess piece moved deliberately into play, and then he withdrew, closing the door with the particular softness he reserved for letters he had already read.

She looked at it for a long moment before touching it.

The envelope was good stock but not fashionable — the paper of someone who spent money on books rather than cards. The hand that had addressed it was small and upright and unhesitating, every letter formed as though it expected to be read by committee. *Mrs. E. Ashcombe, Ashcombe House, Harbury Green.* No honorific beyond the widow's courtesy title, no flattery of *Madam* or *Dear Celebrated*, no appeal to sentiment. Already she liked it, in the way one likes weather that promises honesty even if the honest thing is rain.

She opened it with the bone-handled knife and read:

*Dear Mrs. Ashcombe,*

*I write to you as a historian, and I will try not to write as a supplicant, though I fear I am both.*

*My name is Adaeze Okafor. I have been engaged by the Centenary Committee to prepare the official history of the movement for publication in 1903 — the hundredth anniversary, as the committee reckons such things, of the first petition. You will know better than most that official histories are written by committees, and that committees are written by whoever survives them. I say this not cynically but precisely: my commission is real, my access is contested, and my intention is to write something worth the name "history" rather than the name "commemoration." Whether I shall be permitted to do the first instead of the second remains, at present, an open question.*

*I request access to the papers of Edmund Ashcombe.*

*I am aware of what I am asking. I am aware that you have declined similar requests from three previous biographers — I have their published apologies, and they are all, in their way, elegies rather than histories. I am aware that the martyrdom of your husband is the load-bearing wall of the movement's memory, and that no committee will thank me for testing it.*

*But I have read the trial records. I have read the Home Office memoranda that survived the weeding clerks. And I have found — forgive me if this is indelicate — gaps. Dates where nothing happened and should have happened something. Names absent from lists where names were certainly present. A silence in the record shaped exactly like your husband's final year.*

*I do not know what fills that silence. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps grief and illness and the ordinary chaos of any man's last months. Perhaps something else. I would like to look. That is all I can honestly promise: that I will look, and that I will write down what I find, whether it crowns him or costs him — whether it costs you.*

*You may refuse me. Most people in your position would, and I would understand, and some other historian with less patience would eventually be admitted after me, and the truth would come out crooked instead of straight. That is the other thing I want you to consider: refusal does not protect the record. It only decides who gets to break it open.*

*I am at the Mitre Hotel, Bloomsbury, until the end of the month. I will call upon you if you permit it. I will not call upon you if you prefer I do not, though I confess I hope you are the sort of woman who prefers to see a person's face before trusting her with a century.*

*Yours faithfully,*
*A. Okafor*

Marguerite read it twice. Then she sat very still, the way she did when the floor of a room shifted a quarter-inch beneath her and she needed to confirm the walls were still where she'd left them.

The clock on the mantel — the tall case clock, Edmund's grandfather's, the one object in the house that had outlasted every argument ever conducted beneath it — ticked with its patient, slightly arrhythmic heartbeat. Eleven minutes past four.

She did not look at the locked drawer. She made a point of not looking at it, which was, she understood even as she did it, itself a kind of looking.

* * *

August found her an hour later still in the study, the letter lying open, the light gone amber and low across the carpet. She heard him come in because she had trained herself years ago to hear him coming in — a courtesy, she sometimes thought, a way of granting him the fiction that he could surprise her if he wished, when in fact neither of them had surprised the other since roughly 1891.

"You've read it," she said. Not a question.

"The outside," he said. "The postmark is London. The hand is a woman's, young, educated abroad somewhere by the ink — Continental blue, not English." He paused. "And the weight of it suggested more than a subscription renewal."

"August."

"I read it." He stood with his hands behind his back, driver's posture even in a drawing room, as though at any moment a carriage might require him. "I'm sorry. It's habit. It's — "

"I know what it is." She turned the letter toward him. "Tell me what you see."

He crossed the room and read standing, quickly, the way he read everything — she had watched him devour a page in the time it took her to pour tea, and retain it, too, retain it the way sand retains water, invisibly and completely. When he finished he was quiet long enough that the clock filled the room twice over.

"She knows," he said finally. "Or she suspects. Either way, she's already said it aloud in writing to you, which is more than anyone else has dared in twelve years."

"That isn't what I asked."

"It's what matters." He set the letter down. Carefully. He always handled paper carefully; it was one of the things about him that had first unsettled her, a driver who treated correspondence like relics. "You asked once why I read your letters before you did. Do you remember?"

"To keep the wolves from the door."

"To decide who gets in," he corrected gently. "It's the same work this woman is asking permission to do, madam. Only she wants to do it to you, in public, in print, permanently. And she's telling you so in advance, which is either the mark of an honest scholar or a very sophisticated thief. In my experience those two categories overlap more than either would admit."

Marguerite almost smiled. Almost. "Which do you think she is?"

"I think," August said, "that she writes like someone who has already been lied to by an archive and didn't enjoy it. That's rarer than honesty. Honesty is cheap. Accuracy is expensive." He straightened. "Shall I wire a refusal?"

"I haven't decided to refuse."

"No, madam." Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the ghost of one, the place where a smile had been evicted. "That's rather the trouble, isn't it."

He left her with the letter and the light and the ticking, and Marguerite Ashcombe, who had addressed four thousand people without notes, sat alone with her hands folded like a schoolgirl awaiting a scolding, and thought about drawers.

* * *

After dinner — she ate alone these evenings more often than not; August took his plate in the carriage house by long mutual pretense that this was his preference — she climbed to the library, took the small brass key from inside the lining of her writing-case where it had lived for twelve years, and unlocked the bottom drawer of Edmund's desk.

Not opened it. Unlocked it. There was a difference. Opening was a commitment; unlocking was merely a conversation with possibility. She had unlocked this drawer perhaps forty times in twelve years and fully opened it perhaps six, and each time the papers lay there in their careful order — Edmund had been orderly even in betrayal, if betrayal it was, order being the one virtue available to cowards and saints alike — and each time she had read a little further into the stack, like a wader going deeper into cold water, and each time she had retreated before the water reached anything vital.

Tonight she lifted the top folder and carried it to the reading lamp.

Her own handwriting greeted her. That was always the shock of it — that the earliest documents in the drawer were hers, letters she'd written him in '85 and '86, full of movement business, full of names. *Fenwick has the Bristol subscriptions.* *Miss Hartley will speak Thursday, tell her the hall is secured.* *Ruth Ellery sends her love and asks about the Manchester fund.* Her younger self, generous and unguarded, mailing her trust across London to a husband whose desk, she now understood, had been a sorting office.

She had gone through these before, three winters ago, cataloguing innocently, looking for nothing. Tonight she read them differently. Tonight she read them the way Miss Okafor proposed to read everything: looking for gaps.

*Ruth Ellery sends her love.*

Ruth Ellery had been arrested in March of 1888. Ruth Ellery had been tried, convicted of conspiracy under the Act, and sentenced to eighteen months in Holloway, where her health, never robust, had broken so thoroughly that she had died the following spring of what the certificate called pulmonary consumption and what the women of the movement called, privately and furiously, *the price of the cause*. Marguerite had spoken at her memorial. She had been eloquent. She remembered being eloquent the way one remembers a fever.

Had Edmund known? Had the information that put Ruth Ellery in Holloway passed through the hands that held this letter?

She put it down. Her fingers were cold. The fire in the library grate had burned to a red arithmetic of embers, and she did not ring for more because ringing required explaining herself to a servant, and she could not currently construct a face that would survive explanation.

There was a memorandum beneath the letters, in Edmund's hand — she had seen it before, had noted it before, had filed it under *committee accounts* in her own private index of avoidance. She drew it out now and forced herself to actually read it. Payments. Sums. Small ones, bookkeeping-sized, annotated with initials. Three of the initials she recognized instantly as members of the inner circle. One was *RE*.

Ellery.

The room tilted — not dramatically, not like a stage effect, but with the slow sickening tilt of a ship that has begun, quietly and irreversibly, to take water. Marguerite gripped the arm of the chair and made herself breathe, and above her breath she could hear the clock, faithful and terrible, keeping time in a house that might have been built entirely out of lies and still stood up straight because nobody had leaned on it properly until now.

Twelve years she had kept this drawer. Twelve years she had told herself that whatever was in it belonged to the dead, that the dead were entitled to their privacy, that a widow's duty ran to protection and not prosecution. She had built a whole theology around that lock and key. And here was Miss Okafor, twenty-six years old maybe, sitting in the Mitre Hotel over a supper she probably ate too fast while reading, proposing with a fountain pen that privacy was not the same thing as truth, and that the century was coming regardless, and that archives left closed got broken open by people with less care.

What archive would she leave behind?

She thought of the three biographers she'd refused — decent men, ambitious men, each of whom had wanted the martyr and settled for the elegy. Each of whom had walked away and written around the hole in the record, the way water writes around a stone. And she thought of Pell, the census man with his soft questions and his off-hours visits, who wanted something and hadn't yet said plainly what, and who had looked at her husband's name in a household register twelve years stale as though it were a door he recognized.

Everyone, it seemed, was arriving at Edmund's desk at once. The living and the dead and the not-yet-born readers of 1950. History was not a monument, she understood suddenly; it was a queue.

She locked the drawer. She put the key back in the lining of the writing-case. Then she sat down at her own desk, pulled a fresh sheet toward her, and began three separate replies to Miss Okafor, none of which she finished.

The first refused her graciously. The second granted everything. The third said only: *You may come. I warn you that what you find may cost you your commission, and cost me considerably more. If you are prepared for that arithmetic, come.*

She fell asleep at the desk with the third draft under her hand, and dreamed of Ruth Ellery's memorial, and woke with the dawn to the sound of August's boots on gravel below the window, and the knowledge, arriving with the light like an unwelcome guest who had waited politely all night on the step, that whatever she wrote next would be the truest or falsehest sentence of her life, and that there was no longer any third category.

* * *

She sent for Miss Okafor by return of post — the third letter, with one amendment: *Come Thursday. My household keeps Thursdays quiet, which is to say my household keeps secrets badly enough on ordinary days.*

Then she spent Wednesday preparing the house for inspection, and discovered, in the doing, that she did not know how.

She had received royalty duchesses and cabinet wives and once, memorably, a princess who had held her hand a half-second too long at the garden gate and said, *They tell me you're the brave one.* She knew how to stage a house for power. She did not know how to stage it for scrutiny. Every arrangement she attempted felt like either a confession or a concealment. Should the library be open — the library where Edmund's desk stood? Closed, and thus interesting? Opened and dusted, and thus rehearsed?

In the end she did nothing, which she suspected was the only honest staging available, and on Thursday morning she dressed in the grey wool she wore for funerals and difficult meetings — the wardrobe offered considerable overlap — and waited.

August brought the hansom around at eleven. At five past, he appeared in the study doorway, and she saw at once that something had arrived ahead of the guest.

"There's a telegram," he said. "From London. It came to the village office this morning and young Simms ran it up because of the marking."

"What marking?"

"*Urgent — Home Office,*" August said, and laid it on her desk with two fingers, the way one handles something that might be hot.

Marguerite read it standing. It was brief. It was courteous. It was, in its brevity and courtesy, the most frightening document she had held in twelve years, including the ones in the drawer.

*HM Office regrets inconvenience. Records pertaining to historical review of persons connected to recent centenary preparations may require temporary retention for verification. Cooperation appreciated. P.*

Pell. Of course it was Pell. The census man with the Home Office past, reaching ahead of Miss Okafor like a hand laid flat upon a table before the cards could be dealt — claiming jurisdiction over paper that had not yet been shown to anyone.

"He knows about her," August said quietly. It wasn't speculation. He had that stillness he got when a deduction had hardened into certainty somewhere behind his eyes. "He's been watching the post."

"Then he'll watch her arrive."

"Yes, madam." A pause. The clock ticked into the pause and filled it. "Shall I meet her at the station as arranged — or shall we send Miss Okafor home before she becomes evidence?"

Marguerite looked at the telegram, and at the locked drawer, and at the window where October was doing its grey, patient work on the garden, and she heard herself answer in a voice she barely recognized — steady, and new, and already committed.

"Neither," she said. "Meet her at the station, August. And bring her in through the kitchen door."
