## 11. Night Drive

The rain had stopped by seven, leaving the gravel dark as iron filings and the air washed clean enough to taste. August stood at the carriage house door with his collar turned up and watched the light in Marguerite's study burn behind its curtain — that particular amber he had learned to read the way other men read weather. It burned late when she was troubled. It burned late and steady, unmoving, when she was troubled past troubling.

She had not come down to supper. Cook had sent up a tray and it had come down again untouched except for the bread torn once, precisely, and left. He knew what sat upstairs with her because he carried everything else in that house; he did not need to open the drawer himself to know it was open tonight, that Edmund's papers lay spread across her knees like a hand of losing cards, that she was reading her husband's handwriting and finding in it a stranger.

He should have knocked. Twelve years of knocking on doors and never once on hers — that was the arrangement they had never spoken aloud. He drove where she told him to drive, read what she permitted him to read, kept what she asked him to keep, and in exchange he was allowed to be near her, which was more than most men got from the people they loved and less than he wanted every single day.

The study light went out. A minute later the side door opened and she came across the wet gravel without a coat, without a hat, in the grey dress she wore when she meant to work all night and had decided instead not to work at all.

"The car," she said. Not a question. She had learned, somewhere in twelve years, that he understood requests before she finished making them, and she had stopped pretending otherwise. "I want to go to the sea."

"At this hour?"

"At this hour especially." She stopped beside him, close enough that he could smell the lamp oil on her fingers. "I can't think in that house tonight, August. Every room has someone in it who isn't there anymore."

He went for the car without answering, because there was no answer that wasn't either a lie or an offer, and he wasn't ready for either.

* * *

The motorcar was the Darracq, the heavy one, black as a hearse and nearly as fast if you trusted it, which August did and Marguerite didn't, which was why she loved being driven in it. He cranked it by feel, wiped his hands on the rag he kept folded under the seat, and held the door for her while the engine ticked and coughed itself awake.

They went out through the village with the lamps unlit, because she said no lights, and he understood: no lights meant not wanting to be seen, and not wanting to be seen meant Pell, though she had not said Pell's name since the man had come back off-hours three weeks ago with his soft questions and his eyes like ledger lines. The whole country between the house and the coast went by in the smell of wet hedgerow and cold engine oil, hedges streaming past like ink spilled along a rule.

Marguerite sat very straight beside him, as she always did, hands folded in her lap even alone in the dark where no one could see the posture. That was the thing about her that strangers mistook for coldness: she performed composure even for herself. He had watched her do it through funerals and arrests and the slow public flaying of the movement year after year, and he had never once seen her hands shake except twice — once at Edmund's funeral, once last month when Pell had asked his question about the household twelve years past and August had answered it wrong on purpose, smoothly, terribly, and she had watched him do it and known exactly what he was doing and said nothing then either.

Neither of them said anything now. The road unspooled. The moon came and went behind shredded cloud, lighting the fields in silver and dropping them back into black, and August drove the way he did everything, watching more than speaking, listening to the engine and to the small sounds of her breathing and cataloguing both against some future need he could not yet imagine.

It was eleven miles of this before she spoke.

"You've been driving me for twelve years," she said. "Do you ever wonder where you'd go, if it were you choosing?"

"Every time I drive anywhere," he said.

That surprised her. He heard it in the pause — the small recalibration of a woman who believed she had mapped him completely and found an unmapped province. "Where?"

"Nowhere. That's the point of wondering about it. Nowhere is wherever isn't here." He took the bend above the millpond wide and slow, the tyres hissing on wet stone. "You choose destinations. I choose roads. It works better than it sounds."

"And if you chose the destination?"

"I'd have to want something first." He said it lightly, and immediately wished he hadn't, because lightness between them had begun lately to feel like a kind of lying, and lying to her — even the protective kind, even the kind that was really kindness wearing a coat — had started to sit in his chest like swallowed stones. There were too many stones already. He could feel them shift when the car went over bumps.

Marguerite turned her face toward him. In the dashboard's dim glow he saw her properly for the first time since the house: the lines around her eyes deeper than the photographs allowed, the mouth set in the expression she used on hostile committees. But underneath it, something else. Something frightened, which he had almost never seen and which frightened him in turn, because if Marguerite Ashcombe was afraid, then the world had come loose from its moorings and he had missed the moment of it happening.

"August," she said. "If you found out something — something true — about a person everyone you loved had built their lives around. Something that would hurt every one of them to know. And keeping it hurt only you." Her voice stayed level. Only her hands, unfolding and refolding in her lap, betrayed the effort of the levelness. "What would you do with it?"

He kept his eyes on the road. Eleven years of practice kept them there, but the practice had never cost so much.

"That depends," he said, "on whether the hurting was the price of the truth or just the noise it makes coming out."

"Don't answer like a lawyer."

"I'm not a lawyer. I'm a driver who reads." He changed gear. The engine dropped into a lower complaint. "But I'll tell you what I know, which is that silence is never free. You pay for it slowly, in small coin, over years, and you convince yourself you're paying for someone else's sake. And then one day you look up and the debt's enormous and you can't remember whose name it's in."

The words came out of him before he could weigh them, and once out, they could not be taken back, and they were truer than anything he had intended to say, and he felt her go still beside him the way water goes still before ice.

"Whose name is yours in?" she asked quietly.

"No one's yet." A lie, smooth as any he'd told Pell. "I keep my accounts simple."

* * *

They came over the last rise and the sea was suddenly there, a vast darker darkness below, moving. The moon had committed itself at last and laid a long trembling road of silver straight out from the shingle toward France, and Marguerite said, "Stop here," and he stopped, nose of the car toward the water, engine ticking itself cool.

She did not get out at once. Neither of them moved. The sea made its enormous indifferent sound, wave after wave after wave, the sound that made human urgency seem like a child's argument, and August sat with his hands on the wheel and understood that whatever she had found in that locked drawer was sitting in the car with them like a third passenger, taking up the whole bench between their bodies, and that she was deciding, right now, in the space of one breath and another, whether to let him see it.

"Edmund—" she began.

His heart went still. Twelve years, and she had never spoken that name to him in the dark, in the car, in any register but the public one.

"Edmund used to bring me here," she finished, and the sentence changed horses mid-gallop, and August felt the truth she'd been about to hand him get pulled back behind a fence. "Before we married. He'd drive me down in the trap and we'd eat sandwiches out of paper and he'd talk about the century ahead like a man reading a menu. Everything the movement would win. Everything *he* would win." Her voice did something complicated on that last word. "I was twenty-three and I thought ambition and principle were the same animal. I married them."

"He loved you," August said. He said it because it was true, because he had been there, because whatever Edmund Ashcombe had been to history he had been a man who looked at his wife sometimes with an expression August had recognized and envied and never once mentioned. "Whatever else is in that drawer — and I don't know what's in that drawer, Marguerite, I've never—" and there the stone caught in his throat, because that was a lie of omission so old it had grown roots, "—whatever else is true about him, that was true."

"You say you don't know what's in the drawer." She turned to look at him fully. "You know everything else in this house. You've known it longer than I have, most days. Why should I believe the drawer is different?"

Because it isn't, he thought. Because I stood in a stable doorway nineteen years old and watched your husband hand papers to a government man and I chose you over the truth before I even knew I was choosing, and I have been choosing it every day since, and I am so tired, Marguerite, I am tired down to the marrow of pretending the choice costs me nothing.

"Because you haven't asked me directly," he said, "and I have never lied to you when you asked me directly."

Her breath came out slowly. "Haven't I asked you directly? Tonight? Just now? What would you do with a truth that hurts everyone you love?"

And there it was, laid on the bench between them, wrapped in the question like a knife in a napkin: she had asked, directly, and they both knew it, and his own rule stood between them like a drawn line. Tell the truth when asked directly. He had built that rule himself, brick by brick, as the floor of whatever integrity remained to a man whose profession was concealment. And now she stood on the far side of it waiting to see if the floor would hold.

The sea filled the silence. Wave. Wave. Wave.

"What would I do with it," he said finally, and his voice came out rougher than he meant, "is carry it. For years. Until carrying it became who I am, until I couldn't tell anymore which parts of me are mine and which parts are the weight. And I'd tell myself every day that I was doing it for love. And some days that would even be true." He looked at her then, and let her see whatever was in his face, because he had nothing left to hide it with. "Is that an answer, Mrs. Ashcombe, or is it a confession?"

For a long moment she said nothing at all. Then she reached out and put her hand over his where it gripped the wheel — the first time in twelve years she had touched him without a reason, without a coat to pass him or a letter to sign, just her hand, cold from the sea air, covering his knuckles.

"It's both," she said. "God help us, August. It's both."

She got out of the car then and walked down onto the shingle alone, and he sat in the ticking dark and watched her silhouette stand against the silver road of water, a small upright figure holding herself the way she held herself, and he understood that she had come within a hair of telling him everything and had turned aside at the last moment, and that the hair's-breadth turning had not been distrust. It had been mercy. She was sparing him the knowledge because she believed — God help them both — that the knowledge would break him, or that breaking him would break the last thing in her life that had never lied to her.

He got out and followed her down the shingle, keeping a courteous distance, the way he always had, and stood beside her while the century's last autumn worked its wind through their clothes.

"I won't ask you what you found," he said.

"I know."

"But Marguerite." He waited until she turned her head. "Whatever you decide to do with it — burn it, publish it, bury it in the garden at midnight — you won't decide it alone. That's not a servant talking. That's the only condition I have ever set you, and I'm setting it now, twelve years late."

She looked at him for a long time. Out at the edge of the dark, far down the coast road, a pair of lamps appeared — a carriage or a motorcar, miles off, moving away — and neither of them gave it a second glance, which later, both of them, would remember not doing.

"All right," Marguerite said at last. "Not alone." And then, almost smiling, almost her old self: "You realize you've just given yourself permission to disobey me. You've spent twelve years obeying me into the ground."

"Somebody had to model it badly enough that you'd notice."

The laugh escaped her — short, surprised, real — and it broke something open in the night, some pressure that had been building since Pell's soft voice first asked his terrible arithmetic question, and for a few minutes they simply stood on the cold shingle in the sound of the sea like two people who had set down loads too heavy to name and not yet picked them back up.

It was past two when he drove her home. She slept — actually slept, head tipped against the window, breath fogging the glass — for the last four miles, and he drove slower than he needed to so the sleeping would last.

* * *

He put the car away and banked the boiler and was crossing the yard when he saw the figure by the gate.

Small, stooped, muffled in an ancient ulster despite the hour, standing at his gate as if she had been standing there for some time. He knew the silhouette before he knew the name: Mrs. Fenwick, eighty-one years old, veteran of every platform and every prison cell the movement had produced, three miles from her cottage at three in the morning in the wet cold.

"Mrs. Fenwick," he said. "It's the middle of the night."

"I know what time it is, young man. I was keeping vigil before your father was born." Her voice, thin as paper, carried the flat authority of a woman who had been shouted down by better men than him and buried most of them. "I've come about Miss Marguerite. There's a man going round asking questions. About Edmund. About the old days." She gripped her stick with both hands. "He came to me Tuesday. Polite as a vicar. Writing things down. And I've lived long enough, Mr. Villalobos, to know what it means when polite men start writing things down."

August felt the night tilt slightly, the way the ground tilts when a floorboard gives.

"What kind of questions?" he asked.

Mrs. Fenwick looked up at him, and in the dark her old eyes were bright and hard and afraid.

"The kind," she said, "that have answers somebody doesn't want written down."
