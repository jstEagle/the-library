## 12. Pell Escalates

The rain had followed Mr. Pell all the way from the city, and he did not mind it. Rain made old women hospitable; it gave a caller an excuse to be invited in, to sit close to the fire, to stay until the kettle had been filled twice and the conversation had wandered past the point of caution. He carried his notebook inside his coat to keep it dry, and he carried his questions inside a manner so mild that they did not feel like questions at all until they were already answered.

The house outside Guildford belonged to Mrs. Harriet Loxley, who in another lifetime — in 1887, in a courtroom in Manchester — had been known as Harriet Loxley the Unbowed, because she had refused to give her name when arrested and had spent nine days on bread and water rather than let the magistrate write it down for her. She was seventy-one now, and her hands shook when she poured the tea, and she was flattered beyond all sense that anyone still wanted her memories.

"Twelve years past," Pell said gently, settling into the chair nearest the fire as though it had been kept for him. "The census will want the household as it stood in 'eighty-seven, you understand. Names, occupations. Who lived here then."

"No one but me and my sister, and she's dead these six years." Mrs. Loxley laughed. "You've come a long way for very little."

"Oh, I always ask about the wider circle, too. Neighbours, visitors. It helps me correct errors elsewhere." He opened the notebook on his knee, unhurried, letting her see that it was only a small book, a civil servant's book, nothing alarming. "You knew the Ashcombes, I believe."

Everyone had known the Ashcombes. That was rather the point of them.

"I marched with Marguerite," Mrs. Loxley said, and her chin came up, and for a moment she was thirty years old again and standing in the rain outside a locked hall. "Before her husband died. After, too."

"A remarkable woman." Pell nodded slowly, as though weighing the remark and finding it true. "And Mr. Edmund Ashcombe himself. You would have known him well, in those years."

"Well enough." A pause, the teacup trembling faintly at its saucer. "He was very good to us. He funded the printing press when no one else would. He drove up to Manchester himself, twice, in the winter, with money in his coat."

"So I have heard." Pell turned a page. He did not look at her while he did it; he looked at the fire, at the photographs on the mantel, at anything but her face, because faces closed when watched and opened when ignored. "It is strange, though. The Home Office records from those years — I see them all day, you understand, it is dull work — show payments authorised to an informant in the north-west. Manchester district. Winter of 'eighty-six through 'eighty-eight." He said it lightly, a clerk complaining of tedium. "Someone was selling names to the government while your press was being funded. Isn't that an extraordinary coincidence?"

Mrs. Loxley set down her cup. The china clicked, once.

"That is a wicked thing to say about a dead man."

"It is not a thing I said at all, madam." Pell spread his hands, all apology. "I said there are records. Records are not accusations. But you were there, in those years, and I am only a man with a dull job and a poor memory, and I thought — perhaps you remember something that would help me rule it out. Perhaps you remember Mr. Ashcombe meeting someone official. A man from London. In the autumn of 'eighty-seven?"

And there it was — Pell saw it land, saw the flicker move behind her eyes like a fish turning under ice. Because she *did* remember something. They all remembered something, he had learned this over four weeks and eleven interviews: a name spoken too quietly, a meeting unexplained, a friend arrested suspiciously soon after telling Edmund Ashcombe her plans. Each of them had filed the memory away in some drawer marked *impossible* and had not opened it in twelve years, because opening it meant the martyr was a traitor and their whole beautiful grief had been spent on a lie.

"I don't recall any such man," Mrs. Loxley said.

"Of course not." Pell closed the notebook. "Nor do I. I only have the records, and records are so often wrong." He rose, thanked her for the tea, praised the rain as a mercy for travellers, and at the door he paused as though struck by an afterthought.

"You'll be at the centenary events, I expect. There is talk of a history being written — an official one, for the hundred years. It would rest on testimony like yours." He smiled, mild as milk. "Memories can be such fragile things, Mrs. Loxley. Best to settle them while we still can. Settle them the way they ought to be settled."

He was gone into the rain before she could ask what he meant by *ought*.

* * *

August found the pattern before he understood it, which was the way most dangerous things announced themselves to him. It began small: a note from Miss Prudence Calloway in Leeds cancelling her subscription to *The Suffragist Record*, giving no reason. Then a letter from a Manchester correspondent who had heard, second-hand, that "questions were being asked in certain quarters about the provenance of certain funds." Then Mrs. Danvers, who cleaned the front rooms on Thursdays, mentioning that a gentleman had called at the cottage of old Nell Barrow down the lane — a census gentleman, she'd heard, though the census was long done — and stayed two hours.

August sat with these fragments at the kitchen table after midnight and fitted them together the way he fitted together everything: patiently, without hurry, reading the letters between the lines. Someone was moving through the surviving women of the movement like a man walking down a row of lit windows, looking in at each one. Not accusing. Asking. And asking, August knew better than anyone alive, was only accusation wearing its coat and hat.

He did not tell Marguerite. That was the old reflex, twelve years deep, and he obeyed it before he even noticed he was obeying it — and then sat in the dark kitchen feeling the reflex like a stone in his shoe, because lately every silence felt less like protection and more like theft.

It was Mrs. Fenwick who broke the thing open.

* * *

She arrived on a Tuesday morning in a hansom cab she had paid for herself, which told August everything before she said a word. Constance Fenwick did not pay for cabs; Constance Fenwick walked three miles in bad boots and considered it a principle. She was eighty now and nearly blind in one eye, and she stood in the hall gripping her umbrella with both hands like a halberd, and she said to the footman — there had not been a footman in years, but she addressed August as one regardless, having never once in two decades registered what he actually was —

"I must see Mrs. Ashcombe. Alone. Tell her it is about the man going round."

"The man going round," August repeated.

"You know perfectly well who I mean. The one with the little book." Her good eye fixed on him with terrible precision. "You're the driver. I know a servant's face when I see one, however they dress you nowadays. Fetch her. And then fetch yourself a chair, because I think you had better hear this too, whatever she says about it."

Marguerite received her in the small parlour, the one with the clock, and against all her instincts — August watched her make the decision and admired it even as it frightened him — she did not send him away. She rang for tea and then, when Fenwick's eye slid sideways toward the door where August stood, she said, "Mr. Villalobos stays. Whatever you say to me may safely be said before him. That has been true for twelve years."

"Has it," said Fenwick, in a voice that suggested she had just been handed a very interesting piece of evidence.

Then she sat, and folded her hands atop the umbrella, and began.

"There is a man calling on the widows," she said. "Pell, he gives as his name. He came to me in Dorking a fortnight since. Census corrections, he said, and I am old, Mr. Villalobos, but I am not stupid, and the census does not care whom Edmund Ashcombe met in the autumn of 'eighty-seven." She drew breath. "He asked me — oh, beautifully, beautifully done, butter wouldn't melt — whether I remembered a gentleman from London visiting our meetings that autumn. Whether I remembered names being given to the authorities. Whether I remembered, Mrs. Ashcombe, *payments*."

The clock ticked into the silence. Marguerite's face did nothing at all, which was itself an event; August had spent twelve years learning the grammar of her face, and total blankness was a sentence he had rarely seen her use, and never at home.

"And what did you tell him?" Marguerite asked.

"I told him my memory was failing, God forgive me." Fenwick's mouth twisted. "I told him what I have told myself for twelve years, which is that I remember nothing worth remembering. And he smiled at me and wrote it down, and then he said—" the old voice dropped, went flat and hard as slate— "he said, *memories are fragile things, best to settle them while we still can.*"

"*Settle* them," Marguerite repeated.

"As one settles accounts. As one settles a grave." Fenwick leaned forward. "I have been sitting with this for a fortnight, girl, and I am too old to sit with things anymore, so I paid for a cab, which tells you how serious I am, and I have come to ask you straight out, and I want you to look me in the eye when you answer, because I shall know if you lie, I have watched liars all my life and I married one."

"Constance—"

"Was Edmund selling us?"

August, by the window, did not move. Later — in the years after, in the life that neither of them could yet imagine — he would remember this moment as the hinge of everything: the rain against the glass, the clock's small industry, the old woman upright as a pikestaff with her question held out in both hands like a live coal, and Marguerite across from her with the whole buried architecture of her life visible for one instant in her face before the masonry came down.

"I don't know what you mean," Marguerite said.

Fenwick sighed. It was not a triumphant sigh. It was the sigh of a woman who has confirmed a thing she had prayed was wrong.

"That," she said quietly, "was the longest pause I have ever heard in my life. Longer than the war. Longer than my marriage." She rose, stiffly, using the umbrella as a cane. "I suspected him, you know. Before he died. Little things. Ada Whitmore arrested eleven days after she told him where she'd hide. The money that came and came and no one ever asked from whence. We all saw the little things, and we agreed, all of us, without ever saying a word to each other, to put them in a drawer." She paused at the door. "That man Pell is going round opening drawers, Mrs. Ashcombe. And he is not doing it for the census. He asked me, at the end, whether I would be giving testimony to the official history. He wanted to know what I intended to *settle*. Whoever sent him wants our memories arranged before they are written down — wants us tidy, wants the story clean, wants the martyr martyred properly and no questions after."

"Who would send him?" Marguerite asked. Her voice was steady. Her hands, folded in her lap, were not.

"The same sort of people who always write history, dear. The ones who were watching from the other side of the room." Fenwick settled her shawl. "There were men in the Home Office who spent fifteen years trying to break us, and they failed, and men do not forgive the people who make them fail. If someone over there knows what Edmund did — or suspects it, or has papers — then the centenary is their chance. Why strike us when we're strong? Strike us when we're busy building monuments. Strike us while everyone's eyes are on the pretty statues."

At the door she stopped again, and her good eye travelled from Marguerite to August and back, slow as a searchlight.

"He asked about your household, too," she said. "About the staff. About who reads your post." A dry, terrible smile. "I told him I hadn't the faintest idea. Which was the first lie I've told in service of the movement in forty years, and I found I'd rather lost the knack of it. He didn't believe me either."

Then she was gone, and the cab rattled away, and the rain resumed its patient work on the windows.

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. The clock wound its small eternal argument forward, click by click.

"He interviewed eleven women before Fenwick," August said finally. "Loxley in Guildford. The Marsh sisters. Nell Barrow, down the lane — that's why Danvers mentioned the visit. He started in the north and moved south. Toward you." He heard his own voice doing what it always did: arranging facts into order, building the wall brick by brick. "He's constructing a record. Testimony, collected and settled, that establishes the martyrdom beyond challenge — or establishes the doubt. Whichever the file he's writing requires. He isn't asking what happened. He's asking what people will *say* happened, and correcting them toward it."

"You've seen his notes, have you?" Marguerite said. It was almost gentle. Almost. "You've read his interviews?"

"No. I've read men like him. For twelve years I've read the correspondence of people who wanted things from you, and every one of them sounded exactly like that: reasonable, patient, concerned for accuracy." He stopped. The next sentence was there, waiting, and he understood that saying it would cost something, and said it anyway, because Fenwick's eye was still on him from across the room though she was half a mile gone. "Marguerite. What did he ask you to settle? What is in the drawer that Constance Fenwick saw the shape of through a locked door?"

She looked at him then — a long look, level and unreadable, the look of a woman performing an inventory of everything she had never told him, and finding the list longer than she had believed.

"You said it yourself," she said. "People who want things always sound reasonable." She rose, smoothed her skirt, became the public Marguerite in the space of a breath, and it was like watching a door close in an empty house. "Thank you, August. Have the carriage ready Thursday; I've a committee in town. And send flowers to Mrs. Fenwick — white ones, she'll understand the reference."

He stood in the parlour after she left, alone with the clock, and did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do for weeks. Pell was walking backward through time, door to door, settling memories — and he had begun, August was nearly sure of it, in Manchester, in the places where the arrests had come quickest and the money had moved fastest. He was retracing the exact geography of something. And Marguerite had just looked at her oldest servant the way one looks at a lock one is deciding whether to trust.

That night, August took out the one letter he had ever burned unread, twelve years ago, at her instruction, and stood holding the empty place in his memory where its contents should have been, and understood at last that the fire had never gone out. It had only been banked.

Thursday's committee could wait. Some things could not. On Wednesday morning, before the mist had lifted off the fields, August harnessed the horse without being asked, and when Marguerite came down the steps in her grey travelling coat and stopped, questioning, he said:

"You'll want the coast road, ma'am. It's where people go to decide things."

And because she was who she was, and he was who he was, she got in.
