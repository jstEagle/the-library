## 13. Mrs. Fenwick's Testimony

The note came by hand on a Tuesday, delivered by a girl of no more than fourteen who would not come past the doorstep and would not say who had sent her. The paper inside was heavy, cream-colored, and written in a spidery hand that Marguerite recognized before she read the name beneath it.

*Come to me alone. Come soon. Come without telling the driver.*

That last instruction she held for a long moment in the gray light of the morning room, the way one holds a stone found in a loaf of bread — turning it over, testing its weight, wondering what else might be hidden in the same batch.

*Without telling the driver.*

Fenwick knew. Or Fenwick guessed. Either was dangerous now, in this season when every silence in the house had begun to feel like a door left ajar.

She told August anyway. Not all of it — never yet all of it — but enough. "Mrs. Fenwick wishes to see me Thursday afternoon. I'll take the hansom rather than the motor."

He stood by the sideboard with the post sorted in his hands, and he did not steam open anything in front of her anymore; she had noticed that, the small recalibrations of his ritual, the way a man adjusts his gait when he knows he is watched. "Albany Street," he said. It was not a question. He knew where Fenwick lived the way he knew where everyone lived, filed away in that interior ledger of his.

"Yes."

"And shall I wait outside?"

"You shall not come at all," Marguerite said, and heard how it sounded — not like an instruction but like a test — and hated herself a little for it.

August set down the letters. For a moment he looked at her across the room with that expression she had spent twelve years learning to read and still could not fully translate: patience stretched over something else, something that might have been hurt or might have been fear or might have been only the weather of his face. "Then I'll expect you back by five."

"By five."

She took the hansom. She did not look back at the house as it receded behind her, because looking back had become a habit lately, and habits of looking back were how women like her ended up living entirely in the past tense.

* * *

Albany Street was a street of narrow brick houses that had once been fashionable and had since settled into the particular dignity of the respectable poor — brass knockers kept polished, curtains kept drawn, geraniums in the windows kept alive through sheer stubbornness. Mrs. Fenwick's house was the middle of three identical ones, distinguished only by a black ribbon still knotted around the door handle from some death or other, months old now, faded to the color of charcoal.

The maid who admitted her — the same girl, presumably, or another just as young — led her up two flights to a sitting room that smelled of camphor and cold tea and the particular staleness of rooms kept warm for invalids. And there in the window chair, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, sat Winifred Fenwick, who had been chaining herself to railings while Marguerite was still writing pamphlets, who had been force-fed in Holloway twice while Marguerite gave speeches from platforms, whose body was a map of everything the movement had cost and whose face, even now, even shrunken, retained the fierce architecture of a woman who had never once asked permission.

"Marguerite Ashcombe." Her voice was dry as old paper but it carried. "You came alone. I half expected you'd bring him."

"Him?"

"The driver." Fenwick's eyes were pale blue and unblinking. "Don't play innocent with me, girl. I haven't the strength for it and you haven't the talent. Sit down."

Marguerite sat. The fire was small and made more smoke than heat. On the table beside the chair lay a Bible, a bottle of laudanum with perhaps a week's worth remaining, and a photograph of four young women in white, arms linked, taken — Marguerite judged from the dresses and the defiant tilt of their chins — around 1887. She knew three of the faces. The fourth she did not know at all.

"Do you remember Ada Prentice?" Fenwick said.

The name landed somewhere under Marguerite's ribs. "Of course. She died — the winter of '88. Pneumonia, after the—"

"After the hunger strike. Yes. After they released her too late and let her cough her lungs out in a lodging house in Camden because none of us could afford a doctor and the movement's funds had gone—" Fenwick stopped. She turned her head toward the window with deliberate slowness, the way very old or very ill people move, as though each motion must be authorized in advance. "You were going to say *after the hunger strike*. That is what we all say. That is what is written on her stone. *Died of pneumonia following imprisonment.* A clean sentence. A tidy lie."

Marguerite felt the first cold touch of what this visit was going to be. "Winifred—"

"There was money," Fenwick said. "Ada should have had a doctor. There was money for a doctor. There was money for her lodging and her recovery and her passage to France if it came to that, and the committee voted it, I voted it, and the money vanished between the vote and the need. Three days. In three days it vanished." Her hand crept out from the shawl and closed over the photograph, drawing it onto her lap. "And Edmund Ashcombe was treasurer."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, a kettle began its slow approach to boiling.

"I know about the drawer," Fenwick said.

Marguerite's breath caught. "I've told no one—"

"No. But your face has been telling everyone for a month. I saw you at the anniversary speech. I saw you find that little man in the crowd taking notes, and I saw what crossed your face, and I thought: *she's found it at last, or she's about to.* And then last week Mr. Pell came to see *me*." A sound escaped her that might once have been a laugh. "Census follow-up, he called it. Household records. Twelve years past. He sat exactly where you're sitting and asked me questions about Edmund Ashcombe with the delicacy of a man defusing a bomb, and when he'd finished he looked at me with those soft gray eyes and said, *'It must be difficult, Mrs. Fenwick, holding history together with such fragile materials.'*" Her grip tightened on the photograph until the cardboard bowed. "He wanted to know whether I believed the official account. He wanted to know what I remembered about the treasury accounts of 1888. He wanted, I think, to know whether I was going to be a problem."

"What did you tell him?"

"That my memory is failing." Fenwick smiled, and it was terrible. "Which is true. My memory is failing magnificently, selectively, in whatever direction suits me. It is the one power age has granted me and I intend to spend it."

"Then why am I here?"

"Because you don't have failing memory. You have a failing nerve, which is different, and more dangerous, and curable." Fenwick pushed herself forward in the chair. Up close, Marguerite could see how thin she had become, how the illness had been working at her for months or years with the patience of water on stone. "I suspected him, Marguerite. From the beginning. Before he died. Do you understand what I'm saying? While you were standing beside him at rallies, while you were signing statements with his name above yours, while the whole world was building him a marble saint — I was watching him count."

* * *

"He was charming," Fenwick said. The kettle had boiled and been ignored; the tea sat untouched. Outside, the light had begun to fail, October pressing early against the glass. "That's the thing no one ever says about traitors, because it's inconvenient. They are nearly always charming. Brutish men betray for brutish reasons and get caught by breakfast. But a man like Edmund — a man who believes in the cause, who marches and speaks and gives money, a man everyone *likes* — a man like that can sell you out for years and be mourned by his victims."

Marguerite's hands were folded in her lap. She had arranged them there deliberately, the way she arranged her face on platforms. "What did you see?"

"Inconsistencies. Small ones. A payment recorded twice. A receipt that didn't match a date. Money pledged at a meeting that never appeared in the ledger. I raised it once — once, mind you, gently, over the minutes in his own study — and he laughed and said arithmetic was a woman's virtue and he'd try to cultivate it." Her mouth twisted. "And I let it go. God forgive me, I let it go. Because he was Edmund, and because you loved him, and because the movement could not afford a scandal and neither could I afford being the woman who accused the martyr."

"And after?" Marguerite heard her own voice as though from a distance. "After he died?"

"After he died, I did what we all did. I helped build the tomb." Fenwick's eyes closed briefly. "I wrote the tribute for *The Suffragist*. I called him faithful unto death. I stood at the graveside in the rain and I listened to you speak, and you spoke so beautifully, Marguerite, you spoke him into sainthood right there over the hole in the ground, and every word you said was a nail in the coffin of the truth, and I let you do it. We all let you do it. That is what I have to answer for. Not suspecting him — *letting you enshrine him*, because it was easier than watching you break."

The fire had gone down to embers. Marguerite became aware of the cold gradually, the way one becomes aware of grief — not arriving but deepening.

"There were others," Fenwick said. "Others who knew, or half-knew. Do you want their names?"

"Yes."

"Clara Voss. She was our secretary through '87 and '88; she handled the correspondence and she saw what moved through the office. She stopped attending meetings in '89 and would never say why. Died in '94 — her heart, they said, though Clara was fifty and strong as a dockworker." Fenwick recited it like liturgy, like a list of stations. "Ruth Ellery, who audited the books in '89 and found the gap in the year of Ada's death, and brought her findings to the executive, and was persuaded — *persuaded*, that is the word we used — that the matter was closed and best forgotten. She emigrated to Canada the following spring. Dead eleven years now. And there was a fourth."

Fenwick opened her eyes and looked at Marguerite with a directness that seemed to cost her physically.

"Alice Barrow."

The name meant nothing and then, horribly, everything. Alice Barrow. The youngest of them. The girl in the photograph whose face Marguerite did not know — except that she did know it, she realized, from a memorial card, from a subscription list in an old annual report, *In Memoriam, Alice Barrow, 1871–1890.*

"Nineteen years old," Fenwick said quietly. "She discovered the correspondence — actual correspondence, letters between Edmund and a man at the Home Office — while filing papers in the spring of 1890. She came to me, shaking, wanting to know what to do. And I told her—" Fenwick's voice failed for the first time, and she pressed her lips together and waited it out. "I told her to put the letters back. I told her the cause could not survive it. I told her that truth was a luxury for people whose freedom wasn't on the line. She was nineteen. What was she to do against all of us, against me, against the whole weight of the thing she'd given her life to? She put them back. And six weeks later she was dead — opium, they said, an accident, a tragic accident, a girl who miscalculated her dose. And I have spent ten years knowing, *knowing*, that either the despair killed her or Edmund's friends did, and either way it was my sentence that signed her warrant. Put them back, Alice. Keep the faith, Alice. Be silent, Alice."

The clock on the mantel ticked with the fat, indifferent regularity of all clocks.

"I have carried this since 1890," Fenwick said. "Thirty-one years of silence. And do you know what I learned, lying here these last months with nothing to do but take stock? Silence does not protect anyone. It does not protect the dead, whom it buries deeper. It does not protect the living, whom it poisons slowly. It protects nothing but itself. It reproduces. You inherit it like a debt — my silence became Alice's, and Alice's death became mine, and now I'm handing the whole cursed estate to you."

Marguerite found she was angry. It arrived not gradually but all at once, hot and total, and she was on her feet before she knew it, standing over the frail old woman in the window chair with her fists clenched at her sides.

"How dare you," she said. "How *dare* you sit there and tell me this now. Ten years, Winifred. Ten years you could have come to me. When I was writing the memorial edition. When I was establishing the scholarship in his name. When I stood in front of three thousand people last month and called him the conscience of the movement — you sat in that hall and you *knew*, and you said nothing, and now you send a girl with a note and pour it all out over cold tea as if the timing were mine to bear."

"Yes," Fenwick said.

"*Yes?*"

"Yes. How dare I. I dare because I am dying, and dying simplifies things. I dare because that little gray man came to my door and reminded me that silence has an expiry date — that if I don't speak while I can, men like Pell will speak *for* us, and they will write the version that flatters the government and hangs the rest of us out as fools or liars. I dare because you are stronger than I was at nineteen, and richer, and freer, and if you break under this then Alice broke for nothing and I broke for nothing and thirty-one years of swallowing this poison will have been for nothing." She drew a breath that whistled faintly. "And I dare most of all because you are angry at the wrong dead man's widow-in-law, Marguerite. Be angry at *him*. Be angry at the man who took Ada's medicine money and Alice's peace and your good faith and sold the lot. Anger at me is just anger at yourself wearing my face — and you've worn it long enough."

Marguerite stood there trembling. The fury did not leave her; it only changed address, moving through her like a search party, checking every room, finding the traitor everywhere — in the study where he had worked, in the bed where she had slept beside him, in her own mouth which had praised him, in her own hands which had built the shrine board by polished board. Twelve years of widowhood. Twelve years of being *the widow of Edmund Ashcombe*, defined by a loss, honored for a loyalty, and the loyalty had been owed to a man who had sold her sisters' names for thirty pieces of government silver.

"I found the letters," she said at last. Her voice came out low and strange. "Three weeks ago. In his drawer. Correspondence. Payments. Names." She laughed, and it was an ugly sound she didn't recognize as her own. "Names I knew. Women whose funerals I attended."

"I know."

"You don't know. You suspect. I have *read* them." She turned away, to the window, to the darkening street where a lamplighter was making his rounds with his long brass pole, lighting the lamps one by one against the coming night. "Do you know what the worst of it is? Not that he betrayed us. Men betray; history is a catalogue of men betraying. The worst of it is that I built him. Every tribute, every anniversary, every time I said *my husband believed* — I manufactured the myth with my own hands and then I lived inside it like a prisoner in a house I'd built for my jailer. And now I cannot tear it down without the whole edifice coming down on the movement, on the women, on the dead—"

"On Alice," Fenwick said. "On Ada. On Ruth and Clara. Yes. Their names are mortared into that wall, and that was done *deliberately*, Marguerite — don't you see? He hid behind the movement's martyrs. The myth isn't a monument to him. It's a shield made of our dead."

Marguerite pressed her forehead briefly against the cold glass. Below, the lamplighter moved on. The gaslight bloomed yellow in the dusk, and she thought of all the houses on all the streets where women were keeping silences right now, inherited silences, ancestral silences, passed down hand to hand like a family Bible nobody actually believed in anymore.

"Silence wasn't protection," she said. It was not a question, and Fenwick did not treat it as one.

"It was inheritance," Fenwick agreed. "The one thing our mothers' mothers reliably passed down. Along with the needlework."

A long moment passed. When Marguerite turned back, Fenwick was fumbling beneath the shawl, beneath the cushion of the chair, with the slow industry of the determined ill. She produced an envelope — old, softened at the corners, tied with a ribbon gone gray — and held it out.

"Alice's," she said. "The night before she died she came here. She'd copied out what she'd found — her own hand, her own summary, dates and initials and the Home Office man's name. She left it with me. She said, *'Keep this until someone asks.'*" Fenwick's hand shook slightly, and Marguerite stepped forward and took the envelope, and it was heavier than paper had any right to be. "No one has asked in thirty-one years. You're asking. Take it."

"Why give it to me and not Okafor — the historian? Why not the archive?"

"Because archives can be raided, and historians can be bought, and Miss Whoever-She-Is can be delayed until the centenary comes out the government's way. Because you are the one person in England whose word about Edmund carries the weight of having loved him." Fenwick settled back into the chair, exhausted now, the effort of the evening visible on her like tide marks. "And because, my dear, I may not last until the historians arrive. Paper survives. Witnesses don't. I have learned the difference at considerable expense."

Marguerite put the envelope inside her coat, against her breast, where she could feel it with every heartbeat.

"Thank you," she said, and meant it, and also did not mean it, both at once, which she was learning was simply what truth felt like.

"One more thing." Fenwick's eyes had drifted half-closed, but her voice sharpened. "Pell will come back to me. He'll come with softer questions, or harder ones, or with a document for me to sign confirming my *recollection*. If I refuse to see him, he'll conclude what I know. Don't send anyone to check on me — no drivers, no maids, nothing that leaves a trail. And Marguerite—" She caught her hand. The grip was astonishingly strong, the grip of a woman who had been dragged to a feeding tube by four warders and had fought every inch. "Whatever you decide to do with all this — do it *soon*. Men like Pell don't circle forever. At some point they land."

* * *

The hansom took her home through streets gone blue with evening. Marguerite sat with the envelope in her lap and watched London slide past — the public houses lighting up, the shops pulling in their awnings, the ordinary machinery of an ordinary Tuesday — and understood that she had crossed some line from which there was no return crossing. She had gone out as the keeper of a myth and was coming back as its executioner, and the weapon in her coat had been bought with a nineteen-year-old girl's despair.

At the house, August was waiting in the drive as the hansom slowed, holding the motor lamp, and his face in its light was the face of a man who had spent four hours imagining accidents and arrests and had prepared himself for worse than what arrived. His eyes went at once to her coat, to the shape of it, to the way her arm curved protectively over her chest.

"You're late," he said. "It's nearly six."

"I know."

"Are you all right?"

She looked at him — her reader of letters, her steamer of envelopes, her twelve-year archipelago of protective silences — and felt the full weight of Fenwick's benediction settle on her: *do it soon.*

"No," she said honestly. "But I will be. August — tomorrow, I need you to drive me to the bank. There's a box of mine I want moved to the house. Tonight, I need the study to myself, and I need you not to read anything that comes or goes."

Something moved behind his eyes — hurt, or fear, or the weather of his face — and he inclined his head the smallest degree. "As you wish."

"And August." She paused on the step, the envelope burning against her ribs like a coal. "Has anyone called today? Anyone asking after the household? A census man, a clerk, anyone?"

He hesitated. It was a very small hesitation, but she had known him twelve years, and she had spent this whole terrible autumn learning to hear the sound of his silences.

"Someone left a card," he said. "Mr. Pell. He asked me to tell you he looks forward to continuing our conversation. He said—" and here August's voice went carefully, professionally flat, "—he said he hoped the household's records would prove *complete* this time. Historical review, he called it. He left a card with the word underlined."

Marguerite stood on her own doorstep, in the yellow spill of lamplight, with a dead girl's testimony against her heart and a live man's threat in her hall, and felt the two halves of her inheritance meet at last.

"Then we haven't much time," she said, and went inside to open the envelope.
