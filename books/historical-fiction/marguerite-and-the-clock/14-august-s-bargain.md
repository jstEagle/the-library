## 14. August's Bargain

The letter August chose to give away was one he had read eleven years ago and had never been able to forget, which was precisely why it would serve.

It had come in the spring of 1888, addressed to Edmund Ashcombe in a hand that trembled at the ascenders — a woman's hand, a frightened woman's hand. August had steamed it open at the kitchen table at half past six in the morning, while the house above him still slept, and had found inside three paragraphs of nothing: an inquiry about a lecture ticket, a complaint about the price of railway travel from Norwich, a closing wish for Mr. Ashcombe's continued good health. It was the dullest letter he had ever lifted apart with steam and patience, and he had remembered it anyway, because dullness was its own kind of signature. Frightened people wrote dull letters. He had learned that early. A frightened correspondent filled the page with train fares and ticket prices so that anyone reading over her shoulder — a husband, a postmistress, a driver with a kettle and a griddle — would find nothing worth the reading.

He had resealed it, delivered it, and filed the memory of it away in the crowded drawer behind his eyes where he kept everything Marguerite did not know he knew.

Now, on a grey Thursday in late October, he took it down from the tin box at the back of his wardrobe — he kept every letter he had ever intercepted, God help him, twelve years of other people's secrets in a biscuit tin like a miser's hoard of farthings — and read it again by lamplight. Still nothing. Still perfect. A letter with no name on the signature but a Christian name, *Edith*, no address beyond Norwich, no content that could hang anyone or save anyone or interest anyone except a man who wanted very much to believe he was being handed something real.

Pell would want it to be real. That was the whole architecture of the thing.

August sat for a long while after he had decided, the letter flat on his knee, his hands quiet on top of it. He thought about what he was doing and gave it its honest name, because he had promised himself — in the wrecked days since the greenhouse, since Marguerite had looked at him across the ferns and asked how long, how long, August — that he would stop lying to himself even if he could not yet stop lying to everyone else. What he was doing was deception. He, who had built a life on the careful reading of other men's deceptions, was going to sit down and compose one. The reader of letters, becoming a writer of them. He almost laughed. It would have been a bitter sound, and the carriage house walls were thin, and Mrs. Grady the cook slept light.

There was a craft to it. There was a craft to everything, if you paid attention long enough, and August had paid attention for twelve years the way other men paid attention to horses or drink or God. A deception, like a letter, needed a true thing at its center or it rang false. His true thing was this: he was afraid. Not of Pell, particularly — though Pell was a man you were right to fear, the way you were right to fear ice on a pond that had looked solid all your life — but afraid of what Pell represented, which was the machinery, the slow patient grinding machinery of the state that had eaten Edmund Ashcombe whole and called the meal history. If you built your lie on your own genuine fear, it would hold weight. Men could smell a lie built on confidence. They trusted a lie built on fear, because they recognized the material.

* * *

He wrote to Pell first — not a note, a letter, proper, sealed, delivered by hand to the lodgings over the ironmonger's on Chandler Street where August had established the census taker kept rooms. The letter said, in August's careful, unremarkable hand, that Mr. Villalobos had given further thought to their conversation; that he was a servant, not a scholar, and had no head for papers; but that there was among Mr. Edmund's effects a correspondence he did not understand and did not wish to keep, and if Mr. Pell could call at the mews entrance on Saturday morning, before the household woke, he might have a look at it. No promises beyond that. A look. Servants did not promise their employers' property; servants only opened doors a crack and let the weather decide.

He posted it Friday evening and then spent a bad night doing what he always did when he had set something in motion: rehearsing failure. He saw Pell refusing the bait. He saw Pell accepting it and then, over the letter from Norwich, going very still and saying, *This is eleven years old, Mr. Villalobos, and I asked for recent correspondence,* and August having no answer prepared because he had not thought of that, and waking at four in the morning realizing he still hadn't. So he got up and amended the plan: when Pell noticed the date, August would shrug. Shrugging was underrated. A man who explained too much was a man lying; a man who shrugged was a man who genuinely didn't understand why any of this mattered, because to a servant nothing about paper mattered, paper was what happened upstairs. Let Pell supply his own reasons for why a frightened woman in Norwich writing about railway fares in 1888 should interest the Home Office. Given enough rope, interested men braided it themselves.

Saturday came up cold and clear, frost silvering the mews cobbles like sugar sifted over a cake. August was up at five, sweeping a path that did not need sweeping, warming the carriage house as though warmth were a plausible reason to be awake. At ten past seven — early, eager, or performing eagerness, and August noted that he could not tell the difference anymore, which was perhaps the most instructive discovery of his week — footsteps on the frost-stiff gravel.

Pell wore a different coat than before, better, dark wool with a velvet collar gone shiny at the edges, and carried a leather satchel that had once been good and had been repaired often. He looked, as always, like a clerk who had been promoted slightly past his comfort and intended to grow into the position by sheer application. His breath smoked. His eyes went everywhere — the carriage house, the locked motor car under its canvas, the kitchen door, the high windows of the main house where no curtain moved.

"Mr. Villalobos." He did not offer his hand. "You've reconsidered."

"I've considered," August said. "Reconsidering implies I'd decided something before." He let that sit, then turned and led the way into the carriage house, where he had laid out on the tack room table — deliberately, insultingly, the table where saddles were oiled — a single letter and nothing else.

Pell looked at the table. Then at August. Then at the letter. August watched him do the arithmetic: one letter, offered by a man who had refused everything for weeks, was either contempt or bait, and Pell was not a man who liked to think he was being baited, and so he would choose contempt, because contempt flattered him. August had counted on exactly this. Pride was a lever, and you did not need to be strong to work a lever; you only needed to find the place where it fit.

"What is it?" Pell asked.

"Norwich, 1888. Woman named Edith — no surname given, she signs with the Christian name only, which tells you she didn't trust the post. Tickets, train prices, wishes for his health." August recited it flatly, the way a man recites an inventory of tools. "I read it when it came. I read most things that come to this house. Someone has to know what's in the house."

"You admit that freely now."

"You asked me before whether I read the post and I told you it wasn't my business. That was me being loyal. Loyalty's been" — he searched, found the word, and used it because it was true and truth was the best mortar for a lie — "expensive lately."

Pell put on a pair of spectacles August had not seen before, small oval ones that made him look briefly like a different, softer man, and picked up the letter without asking, which August had also predicted. He unfolded it. He read it twice. August stood by the door with his arms folded and watched the disappointment arrive on Pell's face like weather crossing a field — first the stillness, then the small tightening at the mouth, then the effortful blankness as the clerk in him took over from the man.

"This is nothing," Pell said.

"It's what I have that isn't hers." He meant Marguerite. He let Pell hear that he meant her.

Pell set the letter down and removed the spectacles and polished them on his cuff, a little ritual, buying time the way August bought time with silence. "You understand my position, Mr. Villalobos. I'm compiling a record. Historical review is delicate work. Names surface. Documents surface. And when they don't surface—" he smiled, thin and reasonable, "—one wonders why."

"Wonder away," August said. "I've wondered myself, these twelve years. Wondering's free."

"And the rest of the correspondence? The papers in the drawer? You'll say there are none, I expect."

"I'll say what's true, which is worse for you: I don't know. She keeps that drawer locked and I keep my hands off it, mostly because she'd dismiss me, partly because—" he paused, and made the pause honest, because it was, "—because some of what Edmund left behind frightens me, and I'm not a brave man about paper. I'm a driver."

Something moved behind Pell's eyes at that — recognition, maybe, or envy, or the particular loneliness of a man who has just realized the person opposite him is more dangerous than he looks and cannot decide whether to be alarmed or impressed. "Frightens you," Pell repeated.

"There are letters in this house," August said quietly, "that I burned on instruction, and I've never slept properly since, and I don't know what was in them. That's the whole of my confession, Mr. Pell. Take the Norwich letter. It's yours. It proves I'm cooperating, which is what you actually want — proof, not paper. Paper you can get from her drawer yourself, if you're the sort of man who gets warrants. Are you?"

"I'm the sort of man who avoids needing them," Pell said. "Warrants make records. Records make history." He said it lightly, but August heard the second meaning land, heard it the way you hear a floorboard settle: *records make history*, and whoever held the records held the history, and that was the entire war, fought with fountain pens instead of rifles.

Pell took the letter. He produced a document wallet from the satchel — brown, government-issue, string-tied — and slid the Norwich letter inside with a care that told August it would be catalogued, cross-referenced, entered into whatever shadow archive the Home Office kept for the movement's dead and living. Good. Let it be catalogued. A file containing one harmless letter was a file that looked worked-on, and a worked-on file bought months.

Then Pell did the thing August had come for, though he had not known until that moment how it would present itself. He needed to write a receipt. It was, August understood, less a courtesy than a compulsion — clerks receipted things the way priests absolved them, it was how they touched the world safely — and he spread a sheet of official paper on the tack room table, weighted it with the letter's envelope, and uncapped a pen.

"Name?" Pell asked, already writing.

"August R. Villalobos."

"Employed by Mrs. Ashcombe since—"

"1887. March."

Pell wrote. And at the foot of the receipt, beneath his signature, he stamped — because of course he stamped, the man stamped the way other men breathed — a small purple impression from a rubber stamp he drew from his waistcoat pocket like a watch. August, standing close, angling to return the pen, read it upside down and sideways and did not move his eyes more than a flicker:

**H.M. HOME OFFICE — CRIMINAL INVESTIGATION DEPT.**
**Reg. No. C.I.D./1899/4471 — PELL, J., Insp. (2nd cl.)**
**AUTH. REF: HO/88/C-12**

Three lines. Twelve words and two numbers. August copied them into memory the way he copied everything, instantly and permanently, the letters photographing themselves onto the plate behind his eyes: the registry number, the rank, the authorization reference — and that last one sang loudest, because *HO/88* meant 1888, meant the reference predated Edmund's death, meant Pell's authority to dig into the Ashcombe circle descended directly from whatever operation had consumed Edmund Ashcombe in the first place. Pell wasn't investigating history. Pell was tidying up an old case. Someone above him wanted the file closed clean before the century turned, and had sent a second-class inspector with a rubber stamp to do the closing.

"Sign," Pell said, offering the pen.

August signed. His hand did not shake. He noted that it did not shake and understood that this was the day's true transaction: not the letter, not the numbers, but the proof, to himself, that he could stand in front of the machinery and lie to its face with a steady hand. Twelve years of reading deceptions had finally become tuition paid in full.

Pell tied the wallet, tucked the receipt copy into his coat, and paused at the carriage house door, breath smoking, frost melting under his boots. "Mrs. Ashcombe is a great lady," he said, unexpectedly. "Whatever else happens. I'd hate to see her name end up in a file next to his."

"So would I," August said, and meant it so completely that Pell nodded, satisfied, believing he had finally found the lever that would move the driver — fear for her, the oldest lever there was. Let him believe it. Belief was a rope, and interested men braided it themselves.

* * *

That afternoon August sat in the carriage house with the motor car's bonnet open, doing nothing to the engine, copying the stamp's contents onto the flyleaf of his service Bible in pencil, in his smallest hand, between the columns of Numbers where no one would ever look. *C.I.D./1899/4471. Pell, J., Insp. 2nd cl. HO/88/C-12.* He wrote it twice, checked it against memory, found the memory exact, and closed the book.

A registry number was a key of sorts. Not to a door — to a filing system. Somewhere in London there was a cabinet, literal or figurative, in which a folder marked 4471 contained everything Pell had gathered on the Ashcombe house: the census annotation, the interviews, the names of the women he had been visiting, the shape of the operation, and — crucially — the name of whoever above him had authorized it in 1888 and was authorizing it still. Miss Okafor wanted documents. Marguerite wanted truth. August wanted time, and time, he was beginning to understand, was held by the same people who held the files.

He would need help to use it. There was exactly one person he could imagine asking, and she had dismissed him nine days ago and he had stayed anyway, sleeping in the carriage house, oiling hinges, sweeping frost, visible at every window in the hope of being invisible nowhere. She had not spoken to him except through Mrs. Grady. But yesterday, passing the scullery, she had stopped — just stopped, mid-stride, in the doorway, looking at him kneeling over the boot scraper as though trying to remember a word — and then gone on. Nine days of silence ending in a pause. In August's experience, pauses were how that woman began all her most consequential sentences.

He was still holding the Bible, thinking about pauses, when he heard the crunch of boots on gravel — not Pell's measured tread but two pairs, quick, and then a third, and through the carriage house window he saw them coming up the drive toward the front of the house: young men, collarless, workingmen's jackets, moving with the loose urgency of messengers. One carried a printed broadsheet, a bundle of them, and as they passed close August caught the headline ink-black against cheap paper, caught the first line of it, and felt the floor of the week tilt under him.

Because the broadsheet bore the Ashcombe name. Because it bore the word *betrayer*. Because someone — someone other than him, other than Marguerite, other than the locked drawer — had begun telling the story first, and telling it wrong, and the wrong version was already in the hands of three running men and would be in a thousand hands by nightfall.

August set down the Bible, wiped the pencil dust from his fingers, and went to tell her.
