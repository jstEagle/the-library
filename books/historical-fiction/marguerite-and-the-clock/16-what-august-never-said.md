## 16. What August Never Said

The year was 1887, and August Villalobos had been in the Ashcombe house for eleven days when he learned that the dead do not always stay where they are put.

He had come to the position by way of a reference from Mrs. Halloway at the employment office, who had looked at him a long moment before writing his name on her card. *You're a quiet one,* she'd said. *The Ashcombe house wants quiet.* He hadn't understood what she meant then. Eleven days later he was beginning to.

The house itself taught him first. It was a tall grey house on a street of tall grey houses, but it lived differently than its neighbours. Carriages came at strange hours. Women arrived veiled and left unveiled, or the reverse, and August learned to hand them down from the step without letting his eyes ask questions. Letters came by the dozen, some franked openly, some slipped through the slot with no mark upon them at all, and each morning there were more of them than any single household could honestly generate. Marguerite Ashcombe received correspondence the way other houses received weather.

His duties were simple on their face. Drive Mrs. Ashcombe. Keep the carriage. See to the horses — two of them, a bay named Solomon and a chestnut named Ruth, both older than his position and both, he suspected, wiser. The rest of the work was unwritten. He was to be present and unremarked. He was to open doors and close them. He was to stand in hallways at the hours when hallways needed standing in.

And he was to say nothing. Not because anyone had instructed him to say nothing, precisely. Because the whole house was built of saying nothing, the way a cathedral is built of stone, and a man who wished to remain inside it simply became part of the architecture.

Marguerite herself he saw chiefly at a distance those first weeks. She was thirty-four then, and she moved through her own home like a woman moving through a train station — always about to depart, never quite belonging to the waiting room. Her husband, Edmund Ashcombe, was already famous. His name was in the newspapers twice that first week August worked there: once above an account of his speech in Manchester, and once above the word *martyr*.

That second appearance was eleven days into August's employment. He remembered because of the steam.

* * *

The ritual had begun almost immediately, though he could not have said when he invented it or whether it had been invented for him. There was a copper kettle in the scullery kept always near boiling, and there was a small back room off the kitchen with a table and a chair and one high window, and every morning August carried the letters there before they went upstairs. He held each envelope over the kettle's spout until the steam softened the glue, and he lifted the flap without tearing it, and he read.

He told himself, in those early days, that he read to protect her. That was true. It was also true that he read because the letters were extraordinary and he was twenty-three years old and hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food. He read threats — there were many, written in hands that pressed too hard, promising ruin to the movement and worse to its women. He read pleas from wives who signed themselves only with initials. He read money enclosed in tissue paper, sovereigns wrapped like sweets. He read a letter once from a girl of fifteen in a mill town asking, in spelling that wandered, whether it was true that a woman might speak in public and not be struck by God.

August answered none of these letters. He decided which of them Marguerite should see.

It was a power he did not know he possessed until the morning he found himself exercising it. A note had come, unsigned, warning that a certain meeting in Camden would be watched by men taking notes, and that the names of attendees would find their way to employers. August read it three times. Then he set it in the fire rather than the tray, watched the paper curl and go to ash, and carried the remaining letters upstairs with his face arranged into the blankness he would wear, in one form or another, for the next twelve years.

Marguerite took the tray from him that morning and thanked him. She thanked him the way she thanked everyone, courteously and without looking closely, and he understood that this was the arrangement: she would trust, and he would curate what her trust contained. He was nineteen days past his twenty-third birthday. He felt ancient.

On the eleventh day — the day of the word *martyr* in the paper — the letters included something different. An envelope with no return direction, addressed in a hand August did not recognize, posted from within London itself. He steamed it open in the back room and read:

*The arrangement stands. The second payment falls due at quarter-day. You will understand that continued discretion requires continued compensation, and that our mutual friends grow impatient for the remainder of the list.*

There was no signature. There was no address. But the paper was good paper, government paper, the kind August had seen only once before, on a summons delivered to his father regarding a fine owed on a cart licence. And beneath the printed watermark, faint as breath on glass, was an emblem August would spend the rest of his life being able to draw from memory.

He sat in the back room for a long while with the letter in his hands. Outside, Solomon stamped in his stall, impatient for the morning drive. Upstairs, somewhere, a widow was drinking tea beside an urn of her husband's ashes.

Because that was the thing. That was the whole of the thing, laid out flat in eleven lines of black type. Edmund Ashcombe was dead. The papers said so. The movement said so. There had been an accident on the northern line of the railway — a bridge, a night fog, a fall that the coroner had called misadventure — and there had been a funeral with six hundred mourners, and there was an urn in the drawing room of the house August now served, and the movement had taken its grief and forged it into a weapon, which was the best use anyone had ever found for grief. *They killed him because he spoke,* the pamphlets said. *What will you dare?*

And here was a letter, posted four days ago from a London post office, speaking of arrangements and payments and lists, in the language of a living man's obligations.

August did not yet know what the letter meant. He knew only that it must go upstairs, because everything went upstairs, because that was the covenant of his position — and also that it must not go upstairs, because he had read it, and reading it had made him complicit in a knowledge that could crack the house in half.

He put it in the tray.

* * *

Marguerite read it standing at the window of her study, and August, delivering a second cup of coffee he had invented as a pretext, watched her over the rim of the tray.

She read it twice. Her face did nothing. That was the marvel of it — he had seen faces all his life, his mother's face, the faces of men paid to lose fights, and he had never seen a face do nothing the way hers did nothing. Whatever moved behind it moved behind glass, deep water under ice. When she finished she folded the letter along its existing creases, slid it into her writing desk, locked the drawer with a small key from her chatelaine, and turned to him.

"You read this," she said. It was not a question.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Of course you did." She looked at him then, fully, for the first time since his hiring, and what was in her eyes was not anger. It was appraisal, cold and quick, a horse-trader's arithmetic. "You will bring me everything. Everything that comes to this house. Do you understand? Even — *especially* — what frightens you."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you will tell no one what you read. No one. If a priest asked you under threat of hell, if a magistrate asked you under threat of prison, if I asked you—" and here something crossed her face, some small weather front, gone as fast as it came, "—you will say you cannot read."

"I can read, ma'am."

"I know it." She almost smiled. It was the nearest thing to warmth the house had yet produced, and it frightened him more than the letter had. "That is why you are here, Mr. Villalobos. Now take the tray."

He took the tray. At the door he stopped, because he was young and the question was already burning its way up through his discipline, and he heard himself ask it.

"Ma'am — the gentleman who wrote it. Is he—"

"He is dead, Mr. Villalobos." Her voice was level as a carpenter's line. "He has been dead for five weeks. Whatever else arrives in his name is the business of ghosts, and the business of ghosts belongs to me. Good morning."

He went. But he carried the sentence out with him like a coal in his hat, and it burned there all through the morning drive, all through the afternoon errands, all through the evening when he rubbed down Solomon and Ruth and told them, because they were the only creatures in London he could safely talk to, that something in that house did not add up.

The letter had been written by a ghost. Very well. But ghosts did not collect payments at quarter-day, and ghosts did not have lists, and the phrase *our mutual friends* had a living warmth to it, a smell of tobacco and committee rooms. Somebody was alive. Somebody was being paid. And the woman upstairs knew it, and had chosen the urn over the truth, and had hired a boy of twenty-three to keep the door between the two.

* * *

He saw him in November.

The house had settled into its winter rhythms by then. Marguerite went out three, sometimes four evenings a week, and August drove her, and learned the geography of the movement the way a cabman learns the city — by rote, by lamplight, by the names shouted from doorsteps in the rain. He learned which houses had back stairs. He learned which meetings were real and which were decoys, because Marguerite began sending him ahead of the carriages sometimes, alone, to stand outside a hall and count the men loitering who were not loitering. He got good at counting them. He got good at knowing them. There was a kind of man who watched meetings, and once you had seen ten of that kind you could pick the eleventh out of a crowd by the way he stood with his weight too evenly balanced, a man pretending to wait for someone.

On the third of November he drove Marguerite to a lecture in Islington and waited, as instructed, at the corner by the closed public house. It was a wet night, the kind where the gas lamps wore haloes and the mud had opinions. And across the street, under the awning of a shuttered chandler's shop, stood a man whose weight was perfectly balanced, whose collar was better than the neighbourhood deserved, and who was not looking at the lecture hall at all.

He was looking at the alley beside the chandler's shop.

August had been waiting perhaps forty minutes when the alley produced a figure. The figure came out sideways, keeping close to the brick, hunched into an overcoat with the collar up, and even before the man turned toward the lamp August knew the shape of him. He had spent five weeks in the Ashcombe house. He had eaten breakfast beneath the portrait of Edmund Ashcombe that hung in the stairwell — the strong jaw, the poet's forehead, the eyes that the engraver had given an upward, sacrificial cast. He had dusted that portrait himself, twice, on instructions from the housekeeper, and had felt foolish both times, talking to it under his breath the way he talked to the horses.

Edmund Ashcombe crossed the street with his shoulders around his ears and shook hands with the watcher under the awning.

August did not decide to follow them. Later, examining the memory the way one examines a scar, trying to find the moment of cutting, he could never locate any decision at all. His feet simply went. He tied Solomon loosely to the rail, pulled his cap down, and drifted across the road in the wake of the two men, close enough to hear the hiss of the rain on their umbrellas, far enough to be furniture.

They walked to the end of the terrace and stopped under the gas lamp outside a chapel that had been deconsecrated and let as offices. And there, in the smeared yellow light, August saw the dead man's face entire.

Older than the portrait. Greyer at the temples. The jaw softer, the poet's forehead lined. But it was him — unmistakably, appallingly him — and he was alive, and he was laughing at something the other man said, a low confidential laugh, the laugh of colleagues.

The other man August placed within seconds. Not by face — the face was forgettable by design, middle-aged, moustached, a face that existed to be described badly afterward. By manner. The man had the weight-balanced stance, yes, but more than that: he had the stillness of a man who is paid to remember. He held an umbrella he did not open. When he spoke he did so with his lips barely moving, and Edmund Ashcombe — the martyr, the voice of the movement, the man whose speeches were printed and sold for a penny — leaned in to catch the words like a schoolboy afraid of the master.

August could not hear them. The rain ate everything below a certain volume. But he did not need to hear. He watched the government man produce a slim envelope, watched Edmund take it with a hand that was steady in the way of hands that have done this before, watched the dead man slide it inside his coat without looking down at it. Payment at quarter-day. The arrangement stands.

Then the government man said one thing clearly, pitched just above the rain, and August heard every word.

"The last of it by Christmas. She suspects nothing?"

And Edmund Ashcombe smiled — smiled, with a tired fondness that August would spend decades failing to describe — and said, "Marguerite suspects nothing. Marguerite buries what she cannot bear. It is her genius and my salvation. Good night, Mr. Collingwood."

They parted. The government man walked east. Edmund Ashcombe stood a moment under the lamp, alone, and the look that came over his face then was the one August never managed to forget — not guilt, exactly, nor fear, but a kind of homesickness, a man standing outside a lit window looking in at a family that believed him elsewhere. Then he turned back toward the alley and was gone between the bricks, and August stood in the rain becoming, minute by minute, a different man.

* * *

Here is what he did not do, in the days and weeks that followed.

He did not confront Edmund. He did not write to anyone. He did not leave the letter from October where Marguerite might see it again, nor hint, nor test, nor arrange any of the small accidents by which truths get told. He did not even, when the chance offered itself cleanly — Edmund coming out of a chandler's shop in December, thirty feet away, unprotected — allow his face to change. He looked through the dead man as through glass, and Edmund looked through him, and neither of them existed to the other, and that was the contract, unsigned, unwitnessed, binding forever.

He did not tell Marguerite. That was the heart of it, and the shame of it, and — he came to believe, much later, when shame and love had grown together like the roots of a hedgerow, inseparable — the proof of it.

Because he understood, standing in that wet street in November, something that would take most men years to learn: that Marguerite already knew. *Marguerite buries what she cannot bear. It is her genius and my salvation.* Edmund had said it lightly, carelessly, a husband's contempt worn smooth by repetition — and August had heard in it not new information but confirmation. The locked drawer. The letter read at the window with a face that did nothing. *Whatever else arrives in his name is the business of ghosts, and the business of ghosts belongs to me.*

She knew. Perhaps not everything — perhaps not the lists, not the names sold, not the full accounting of what the payments bought. But she knew the shape of it, and she had chosen the myth over the man, the urn over the truth, because the movement needed a martyr the way a body needs a spine, and because bearing it alone was, for Marguerite Ashcombe, a form of prayer. To tell her what he had seen would not inform her. It would only prove that he had watched her carrying it, and had made her carry it in front of a witness. It would strip from her the last private thing she owned.

So he said nothing. He filed the face of Mr. Collingwood in the archive behind his eyes. He memorized the date, the corner, the chapel with the deconsecrated windows. He learned, over the following months, to map the edges of the arrangement — the nights Edmund appeared, the routes he favoured, the quarter-days when a certain grey man passed through certain streets — the way a lighthouse keeper learns a coastline: not to sail it, but to warn against it. And when, in February, a letter arrived marked with the emblem and speaking of a final settlement, August steamed it, read it, and burned it, and told Marguerite the morning's post had been light.

That was the first letter he ever stole from her. He thought of it as a mercy. Mercy has a way of becoming method.

* * *

In March, Edmund Ashcombe died.

Actually died — there was no mistaking this one, because August saw the body. He was sent before dawn to a house in Kentish Town that he had never driven to before, guided by a note in Marguerite's hand that gave an address and nothing else, and he carried a stretcher from a cab into a back bedroom where a man lay who had the jaw and the forehead of the portrait and none of the portrait's certainty left in him. The landlady stood weeping in the doorway. There was a doctor's certificate already drafted on the nightstand, cause of death entered in a fair round hand: *cardiac failure, of long standing.* Dated correctly. Signed by a doctor whose name August would one day find again in very different company.

He did not ask how Marguerite had known to send him. He did not ask what Edmund had been doing in Kentish Town, or whether the envelope under the coat had been collected, or whether the last payment had been the last. He folded the dead man's hands, covered his face, and did his work, and when Marguerite met the cab at the house in town she looked at the stretcher for a long moment and then at August, and what passed between them in that look was the whole treaty of the next twelve years, ratified in silence without a word of negotiation.

*Thank you*, her eyes said. Or possibly: *Now you know, and now you are mine.*

Or possibly, in a language neither of them would admit to for years: *We are the same, you and I. We bury what we cannot bear.*

The movement got its martyr twice over. The papers wrote kindly of the long illness. The pamphlets gained a second printing. And in the back room off the kitchen, over the following spring, August perfected his ritual — the kettle, the steam, the lifted flap — and told himself, every morning, that he read in order to protect her.

Twelve years later, a census taker named Pell would knock on the door of the Ashcombe house and ask his careful questions, and August would deflect him with the ease of long practice, and would believe, in that moment, that the wall still held.

But walls are built by men, and men are built by their silences, and August's had been built on a wet November night outside a deconsecrated chapel, by a young man who chose love and called it loyalty and never once, in twelve years, asked himself what Marguerite might have chosen, if anyone had ever let her choose at all.

The answer to that question was already walking toward the house, carrying a census book and a past of his own.
