## 17. Confrontation in the Greenhouse

The greenhouse stood at the end of the walled garden like a glass lung, breathing the last warm breath of October. Marguerite had come here for thirty years — first with Edmund, who had liked to show her his orchids and explain their care as though explaining himself: fed on darkness, blooming only under exact conditions. Later she came alone, when the orchids were dead and the tomatoes gone over, because it was the one room in her life where nothing was expected of her except that she keep it alive.

She heard August's boots on the gravel before she saw him. Of course she did. Twelve years of hearing those boots.

"You didn't sleep," she said, without turning from the bench where she was cutting back the geraniums. The shears made a small wet sound. "I saw your lamp in the carriage house at three."

"I slept enough, ma'am."

"Don't." The word came out harder than she intended, and she let it stand there anyway, hard, between them. "Don't *ma'am* me this morning, August. Not today."

He stopped a few feet away. She could see him reflected dimly in the glass panes, a tall dark shape against the grey morning, hands folded behind his back the way he always stood when he was being told something difficult — by a magistrate, by a doctor, by her. Patient. Contained. A man built out of waiting.

"Mrs. Ashcombe," he said carefully, "if you'd rather I not be here—"

"When?" She turned then, and the shears hung from her hand like an accusation she hadn't decided to make yet. "When did you know?"

The question filled the greenhouse the way steam fills a kettle — invisible, pressurized, everywhere at once. August did not move. She watched him decide not to move, watched the small muscles around his eyes do their work, and understood suddenly that she had spent twelve years reading a man's face the way he read her letters, and had still missed everything.

"August." Her voice cracked on the second syllable and she hated it, hated the crack, hated him for hearing it. "*When.*"

"Twelve years ago," he said quietly. "Autumn of '87. I'd been in your service four months."

The geranium stem she'd been holding dropped from her fingers onto the bench. Four months. Four months after Edmund's death — no. Before it. During it. While she had been sitting up nights writing eulogies in her head, while the whole country had been learning to say *martyr* with its hat off, August had already—

"What did you know," she said, "exactly."

"Edmund wasn't dead yet. He was meeting a man from the Home Office. In London. I saw them. I heard part of what they said." His voice stayed level, which was somehow worse than if it had broken. "I didn't understand all of it. But I understood enough."

"Enough to know my husband was—"

"Yes."

"*Yes?*" The word tore out of her. "That's all you have to say? Yes?"

"No, ma'am." A pause. "But I don't know what else is true."

* * *

She had imagined this conversation, in the two days since Mrs. Fenwick's warning had sent her down into the locked drawer and out again changed — imagined it as a kind of surgery, clean and necessary. She would ask; he would answer; the wound would be opened and drained and begin, at least, to close. She had rehearsed her fury. What she had not rehearsed was this: the way he simply stood there and took it, offering nothing, defending nothing, a man presenting his throat.

"Say it plainly," she demanded. "You knew my husband was selling us. Selling *me*. You knew while I was signing petitions beside women who went to prison for it. You knew while I wrote his memorial address. You knew when they put that plaque up in Manchester — you drove me there, you sat outside in the motor while I wept in front of three hundred people—" Her voice climbed and she let it climb; the greenhouse walls would hold it; the glass had held worse. "You knew, and you handed me my gloves every morning for twelve years and said nothing."

"Yes."

"Stop saying yes like it's an answer!"

"It's the only true thing I have, ma'am." And now something moved in his face at last, some tectonic shift far below the surface, so that his voice when it came was rougher. "You want me to tell you why. I can't. Not in a way that wouldn't be a defense, and you haven't asked for a defense. You've asked what I knew, and I've told you, and I'll tell you anything else you want — every day, every hour, till the day I die or the day you turn me out, whichever comes first. But I won't dress it up. I read your letters because you asked me to protect you and I decided protecting you meant knowing things before you did. I kept this silence because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because I loved what you were building. Because I thought the truth would kill it. Because I was a coward. All three, in different years, in different weights. That's the whole of it. There isn't a version where I come out clean, and I'm not going to pretend there is."

Marguerite pressed her palm flat against the bench, against the cool damp wood, because the room seemed to tilt slightly and she needed an anchor.

"You decided," she repeated. "*You* decided. What I could bear. What would kill me. For twelve years, August, you sat across from me at breakfast and chose my ignorance for me like choosing my tea."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And the letter." It surfaced now, the oldest wound, the one she had almost forgotten she carried. "The night I gave you Edmund's last letter and told you to burn it unread. Did you read it?"

The pause lasted three seconds. She counted them against her own heartbeat.

"No," he said. "That one I burned sealed. It's the only one."

"The only one." She laughed, and the laugh was terrible even to her own ears. "You make it sound like a virtue. *I only betrayed you once, madam, and briefly.*"

"I never betrayed you."

"Then what would you call it!"

"Loyalty," he said. "Misused. Misdirected. Given to the wrong thing — given to keeping you safe instead of keeping you free. But it was loyalty, Mrs. Ashcombe. Every day of it. I'd swear that on any book you put in front of me."

And that — God help her — that was the worst of it, because she believed him. She believed him utterly, and belief changed nothing. Fenwick had died carrying her suspicion silently, and Marguerite had raged at the dead woman for it, had written in her notebook with a shaking hand: *silence was not protection, it was inheritance.* And here was the inheritor standing in front of her, holding out the poisoned estate with both hands, saying *it was always yours, I only kept it from rusting.*

"You should have told me," she said, and her voice had gone quiet now, quieter than the shears, quieter than the wind moving the bare plum branches against the glass. "Not for the movement. Not for the plaque in Manchester. For me. Because I am fifty-four years old and I have just learned that the two men I trusted most in this world were liars, and one of them is dead and beyond my anger, and the other one is standing in my greenhouse refusing even to apologize properly."

"I'm sorry."

"That's not an apology. That's a punctuation mark."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't one. An apology would be *I was wrong and I will never do it again*, and I can't say the second half honestly, because if you asked me tomorrow whether I'd do it again to spare you that plaque in Manchester — I don't know. I don't know what I'd do. That's the truth of me, Marguerite." He used her name for the first time, and it landed like a stone in still water. "You deserve better than a man whose truth has to be pried out of him. You've deserved better for twelve years."

* * *

The silence that followed was long enough for the light to change. Clouds crossed the sun and the greenhouse dimmed, all that glass going grey, and in the half-dark Marguerite looked at August Villalobos — really looked, perhaps for the first time since the night twelve years ago when a young driver with careful hands had taken a sealed letter from her and walked out into the rain to burn it — and saw not the servant, not the gatekeeper, not the wall she had built her life against, but a man. Tired. Frightened, maybe, though he'd never once shown it. Standing in the wreckage of the only structure he had ever built, which was his usefulness to her.

"Give me your keys," she said.

His head came up slightly. "Ma'am."

"The house keys. The motor. The correspondence box." She held out her hand, steady, she was glad to note, steady as a surgeon's. "You're dismissed, August. Two weeks' wages and a reference — a good one, I'll write it myself tonight. You can take the carriage house until you find a position. After that—" She swallowed. "After that I don't know. But not here."

He didn't move. Didn't reach for his pockets. Just looked at her with those patient dark eyes, and she realized with a fresh wave of fury that he was deciding again — deciding even now, even dismissed, what happened next.

"Marguerite." Her name again, and softer this time, and she wanted to strike him for it. "I'll give you the keys if you order me three times. I'll go if you mean it three times. But I won't pretend you mean it yet, because I've watched you too long, and I know the difference between what you say in anger and what you decide in the night."

"How dare you—"

"You asked me to stop lying to you," he said. "This is what it looks like. This is the first hour of it. It's ugly. I warned you it would be."

Her hand was still out. Trembling now. She closed it slowly, drew it back, and turned away to the window, to the garden going gold and bare beyond the glass, because she could not look at him and speak at the same time.

"Get out of the greenhouse," she said.

She heard him go. Boots on gravel, unhurried, each step deliberate — and she understood, listening, that he was leaving the greenhouse exactly as ordered and nothing further, that he was parsing her sentence like a lawyer, like a man who had spent twelve years learning precisely how much obedience cost and paying it to the penny.

* * *

He did not leave the house.

She discovered this at dusk, coming down the corridor past the study with a candle, and finding the front hall swept — swept, at seven in the evening, by no hand she had hired — and the lamps trimmed and lit, and through the kitchen window the sight of August Villalobos standing at the range making soup, sleeves rolled, moving in that kitchen as though the kitchen were his native country and he had merely been away at war.

She stood in the doorway with her candle and her fury and found, to her horror, that the fury had nowhere to land. He had obeyed her. He had left the greenhouse. He had not left her employment, because she had dismissed him in anger and he had called it, correctly, and she could not — she stood there searching herself the way she'd searched the locked drawer, turning over paper after paper — she could not find in herself the third telling. The one that would mean it.

"You can't stay," she said.

"I can sleep in the carriage house," he said, not turning from the stove. "There's a bed there. There's been a bed there eleven years; you never asked why I didn't take rooms in the village."

"Why *didn't* you?"

"Because someone should be near you at night." Now he turned, wooden spoon in hand, steam rising around him, and his face in the lamplight was open in a way she had never seen it — stripped, exhausted, forty-eight years old and done with hiding. "Not to watch you. To be near. In case. I don't fully understand it myself, ma'am, and I've had twelve years to try. But I'm not going to stand in your kitchen and lie to you about it, so there it is."

"The soup smells like onions and insubordination."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile; the ghost of one, the memory of one. "It's leek. And yes, ma'am."

"Eat it in the carriage house, then."

"Yes, ma'am."

She turned to go, and stopped, and stood a moment with her back to him, candle trembling very slightly in her hand.

"August. If Pell comes back — if anyone comes — you will wake me. Whatever hour. Whatever the cost to my beauty sleep or your sense of my fragility. Do you understand? No more decisions made on my behalf while I sleep. That arrangement is finished."

"Yes, ma'am." A pause. "Thank you, ma'am."

"Don't thank me. I haven't forgiven you. I may never forgive you."

"I know." And then, quietly, as she reached the door: "Miss Okafor's letter came this afternoon. Second one. I put it on your desk unopened."

Unopened. She caught the phrase like a thrown ball, held it, examined it. Twelve years of steaming envelopes, and now — *unopened*, offered up like a first installment on a debt he could never repay.

"See that it stays that way," she said, and went up the stairs to her cold bedroom, where she sat by the window a long time watching the single lit window of the carriage house burn steady against the dark, hating him, and hating the myth, and hating most of all the treacherous, stubborn, unmistakable relief of knowing that he was there — that whatever came with the morning, someone who knew the whole of it was near enough to hear her if she called out.

On the desk downstairs, Miss Okafor's second letter waited, seal intact, asking — though Marguerite did not yet know it — for access to the papers, and for an answer within the fortnight.

The clock in the hall struck ten. Neither of them wound it that night.
