## 18. Miss Okafor Arrives

The train from London was late by forty minutes, which gave Marguerite Ashcombe time to rehearse three separate versions of her welcome and distrust all of them.

She stood at the drawing room window in her second-best grey — the one that said dignity without saying mourning — and watched the lane for a carriage that did not come. October had stripped the chestnuts along the drive to their last copper coins, and the wind kept trying them against the gravel as if deciding whether they were worth keeping. Behind her, the house did what it always did on mornings of consequence: it held its breath. Twelve years of widowhood had taught Marguerite that houses could do that, if you lived in them long enough. The portraits of Edmund watched from the staircase wall with their painted patience, and she had not looked at them directly since the night she opened the locked drawer.

August came into the hall without announcing himself, the way he did everything. He carried the good tea service on a tray he had no business carrying himself — there were maids for trays — but she had learned years ago that when August decided a task mattered, he performed it personally, and arguing with him about it wasted breath better spent elsewhere.

"The four o'clock is delayed at Reading," he said. "Signal failure."

"You've been to the station?"

"Telegraphed." He set the tray down on the sideboard and aligned the cups with a precision that was either habit or nerves. With August it was impossible to tell, which Marguerite suspected was the point of him. "I asked the porter to watch for her. She travels alone, apparently. No companion."

"A historian who travels alone." Marguerite turned from the window. "That will tell us something already."

"It might tell us she's poor," August said. "Or careful. They look the same from a distance."

Marguerite almost smiled. "And which are you this morning?"

"Careful," he said, and then, because he was honest in his own narrow currency: "Poor."

She let the silence sit between them the way one lets soup cool. Since the drawer, since the argument about burning, since his confession and hers — neither forgiveness nor absolution, only the plain architecture of what each had done — they had developed this new manner of speaking: brief, unadorned, each sentence load-bearing. It was exhausting and it was the closest thing to rest she had known in twelve years.

"She'll ask about the gaps," August said quietly.

"Of course she'll ask about the gaps."

"And we'll say—"

"We'll say nothing," Marguerite said. "We'll let her find them herself. If she is worth anything, she'll find them herself, and then we'll know what we have. If she isn't—" She looked at the tea tray, at the cups aligned like soldiers awaiting orders. "If she isn't worth anything, it won't matter what we say."

* * *

Adaeze Okafor arrived at half past four in a hired trap driven by a boy who clearly wanted his fare and his supper in that order. She stepped down before the wheels had fully stopped, which told Marguerite something too — a woman who trusted momentum more than caution — and stood a moment in the drive looking up at the house with an expression Marguerite recognized because she had worn it herself at other people's doors: the expression of someone taking inventory and refusing to be impressed.

She was younger than her letters suggested. Late twenties, perhaps thirty. Dark-skinned, Nigerian-born — the name had said so, though the letters had been scrupulously English in every other particular — dressed in a travelling suit of severe black that had been mended well enough to pass at a distance and not at all up close. Her hair was pinned with mathematical severity. Under one arm she carried a leather portfolio scarred at the corners like a much-used suitcase, and in her free hand, a notebook already open, pencil already moving.

"Mrs. Ashcombe." She pronounced the name carefully, getting it exactly right on the first attempt, which was its own small courtesy. "Miss Okafor. Thank you for receiving me. I know your reply said Thursday next, but I took the liberty of coming early, as my funds would only stretch to one journey and I preferred to spend it in person rather than in correspondence."

It was the most disarming possible way to confess to having ignored instructions, and Marguerite felt the trap of it even as she stepped forward with her hand extended. This woman had made rudeness into charm and poverty into authority, and she had done it in under ten seconds.

"Miss Okafor. You'll take tea before you see anything else."

"I'd rather see things first, if it's all the same to you." Okafor smiled, showing that she knew precisely how this sounded. "Tea makes me agreeable, Mrs. Ashcombe, and agreeable historians are worse than useless. I'm told I'm insufferable on an empty stomach, which I've decided to consider a professional qualification."

From the corner of her eye Marguerite saw August, stationed by the door with the luggage, go very slightly still. He had spent twelve years learning to read people through paper; here was a woman who announced herself whole, unwrapped, asking nothing but to be believed as presented. It was the opposite of everything August understood about correspondence, and Marguerite wondered — with a pang she did not examine — whether it frightened him or relieved him.

"The study, then," Marguerite said. "You may be insufferable among my husband's papers."

* * *

Edmund's study had been kept as a shrine for eleven years and as a battlefield for six weeks, though only two people alive knew about the second part.

Marguerite had restored it to order after the night of the drawer — papers squared, blotter straightened, the locked drawer itself emptied and its contents moved somewhere August did not know about and had not asked after, another silence between them now, this one hers instead of his. What remained was the public archive: Edmund's published speeches, his correspondence with the movement's great names, the commemorative volumes, the framed photograph of him shaking hands with Asquith, the memorial edition of his collected essays that sold four hundred copies a year and kept the myth comfortably fed.

Okafor walked the room once, slowly, without touching anything, and Marguerite watched her the way a card player watches an opponent's hands.

"You keep it very faithfully," Okafor said at last.

"He died in this room."

"Yes." Okafor did not say *I'm sorry*, which Marguerite noted with approval; the condolence had been offered so many thousands of times it had worn smooth as a pebble and meant roughly as much. Instead Okafor set her portfolio on the desk, unstrapped it, and began laying out her instruments with the economy of a surgeon: magnifying glass, rule, two pencils, a tin of pins, a notebook whose pages were tabbed in five colors.

"I'll be candid, since candor is cheaper now than it will be in an hour." She looked up. "I've read everything published about your husband. All of it. The memorial edition twice, including the appendix of letters, including Mrs. Hartley's memoir, including the centenary prospectus that the committee sent me when they engaged me. And I've spent the last three days in the British Museum reading room with the movement's periodicals from 1880 to 1887, because the official histories begin where the newspapers end, and newspapers don't lie, Mrs. Ashcombe — they merely omit, which is lying with better manners."

"Go on."

"There are eleven months," Okafor said, "between March 1886 and February 1887, in which Edmund Ashcombe appears in no publication whatsoever. Not the *Suffragist*. Not the *Clarion*. Not a single regional paper covering the northern campaigns. A man at the center of the movement, at the height of his influence, vanishes from print for nearly a year and then reappears as if nothing happened, and every history written since has treated those eleven months as if they were a corridor everyone simply walked through quickly." She tapped the desk with one finger. "Corridors aren't empty because nothing happened in them. They're empty because nobody wants to be seen standing in them."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a door settled in its frame.

"You've been busy for a young woman," Marguerite said.

"I've been busy for any woman. Youth is only an insult when it's attached to ignorance." Okafor opened her tabbed notebook to a page dense with pencil. "May I ask you questions now? You may decline to answer any of them. But declined answers are also data, and I'll record them as such, and I'd rather you knew that going in than discovered it halfway through."

Marguerite sat. Across the room, August stood by the window with the teapot he had brought and not yet poured, and their eyes met for half a second — a whole parliament convened and adjourned in that half second.

"Ask."

"Where was your husband between March 1886 and February 1887?"

"In the north. Convalescing. His health had failed."

"That's what Mrs. Hartley says."

"Then Mrs. Hartley and I agree."

"Mm." Okafor wrote something — Marguerite could not tell whether it was an answer or a notation — and turned a page. "His letters resume in February '87 with a reference to 'the matter being settled to everyone's satisfaction.' Settled how? Satisfied whom?"

"An illness settles itself or it doesn't. My husband was not given to explaining his body in correspondence."

"No," Okafor agreed mildly. "He wasn't given to explaining anything in correspondence. Which brings me to my third question, and I'll thank you in advance for your patience with it." She laid down the pencil and folded her hands, and for the first time since entering the room she looked not like a scholar but like something older and harder — a customs officer, perhaps, or a woman who had grown up counting what authorities counted. "Where are the letters he received during those eleven months? Every archive keeps both sides of a correspondence, Mrs. Ashcombe. I can count his outgoing letters — they survive in copies and references, forty-one of them across the gap year. Incoming, I can document nine. Forty-one out, nine in. Either the man wrote forty-one letters into a void, or thirty-two incoming letters existed once and exist no longer."

The clock on the mantel — the clock, the old brass thing that had survived fires of every kind — chose that moment to chime the quarter hour, and Marguerite let it finish before she answered, buying herself fifteen seconds of bells.

"Papers are lost," she said. "Houses are closed up. Servants burn things."

"Servants burn things," Okafor repeated. She wrote it down. Then she looked up, and her eyes were not unkind, which made them worse. "Yes, ma'am. They do."

* * *

They broke for tea in the drawing room at six, and Marguerite used the interval for what she had privately decided over lunch she must do.

It was not pride, or not only pride. It was method. A woman who noticed eleven missing months and thirty-two missing letters was dangerous — dangerous to the myth, dangerous to Pell's project, dangerous most of all to whatever fragile thing Marguerite was building toward in the dark. Before she handed such a woman the truth, or any portion of it, she needed to know what kind of instrument truth was in her hands. Whether she wielded it or merely displayed it. Whether accuracy was her master or her tool.

So, over the second pot, Marguerite committed her small sin.

"You'll want the personal material eventually," she said, stirring tea she did not intend to drink. "The biographical texture. Historians always do. Edmund proposed to me in the orchard at Kew, in April of 1879. Under the cherry trees. There was a gardener nearby who pretended not to watch, and Edmund, who was shy in all matters except the cause, waited until the man left before he spoke. He'd written the proposal out beforehand and forgotten the paper in his coat, and I found it months later and never told him. I have it still."

Every word of it was invented. The orchard, the shy proposal, the gardener, the lost-and-kept paper — a fiction assembled from the raw stock of a hundred widows' anecdotes, plausible as rain. If Okafor wrote it down, Marguerite would know her: a historian who took comfort at face value, who would take the larger truths the same way, who would polish whatever she was given regardless of its provenance. Useful, perhaps, in the way Pell was useful. Manageable.

Adaeze Okafor set down her cup.

She did not write it in the notebook. That was the first sign. She looked at Marguerite for a long moment with an expression of genuine, almost pained attention, and then she said, quietly:

"Kew's cherry collection wasn't planted until 1891."

The fire ticked. In the hall, faintly, August's footsteps paused and resumed.

"I beg your pardon?"

"The ornamental cherries at Kew. The Prunus collections came in the nineties, mostly Japanese introductions. In 1879 the orchard at Kew was fruit trees — apples, pears, plums. Lovely in blossom, I'm sure, but not cherries." Okafor's voice held no triumph; that was the remarkable thing about it. It held only a kind of sorrowful precision, the tone of a woman reporting a death. "Also — and please believe I take no pleasure in this — the visitor registers for Kew Gardens are preserved, and I checked the spring of 1879 three weeks ago while preparing for this visit, because courtship stories are where widows embroider, and embroidered courtships are where archives begin to rot. There's no entry for Edmund Ashcombe in April of '79. There is one for June. The twelfth of June, party of two." She opened the tabbed notebook at last and turned it around so Marguerite could see a page of small, exact pencil entries. "June the twelfth, Mrs. Ashcombe. If you'd like to tell me about the twelfth of June, I will write it down and it will be true forever. If you'd rather I didn't know, that's your right, and I'll note only that the question was asked and declined. But I won't write down the orchard. Because if I put a lie in my notes, even a kindly lie, even a lie you gave me, then every true thing I ever write afterward sits downstream of it, and none of it can be trusted, including by me."

Silence. The clock marked its time. Marguerite became aware of her own hands in her lap, perfectly still, and of the fact that she had just been tested in return — caught, measured, and offered terms, all inside a minute.

"You checked the registers," she said, "before you ever wrote to me."

"I check everything," Okafor said. "It's why the committee finds me difficult, and why I'll never be rich, and why, if you decide to trust me, you can actually do it — not the way one trusts a friend, but the way one trusts a scale." She closed the notebook. "I came here prepared to admire your husband, Mrs. Ashcombe. I still am prepared to admire him. But I came to write what happened, not what comforts, and I want you to know that before you decide what to show me. Whatever you show me, I will verify. And whatever I cannot verify, I will say so in print. Those are my conditions. They're not negotiable, and I've learned they make a better foundation for friendship than flattery does."

Marguerite looked at her for a long time. Outside, the last light was going copper on the wet lawn, and somewhere down the lane the hired trap's boy was surely wondering about his supper.

"The twelfth of June," Marguerite said at last, and her voice did something she had not authorized — it softened. "There was no orchard. There was a bench by the palm house, and it rained, and he hadn't written anything out at all. He stammered. He'd faced down magistrates without stammering. I said yes before he finished the sentence, because I couldn't bear to watch him struggle." She met Okafor's eyes. "Why should I give you the true version now?"

"Because you just did," Okafor said gently, "and because a woman who tests a historian with a lie before trusting her with the truth has usually got a great deal of truth she's deciding about. I'd like to be here when you decide." She gathered her pencils with unhurried movements. "I'll need several days among the papers. I'll work alone, I'll take no copies without leave, and I'll show you my notes each evening — every line of them, unedited. Fair terms?"

"Fair terms," Marguerite said.

* * *

August found her in the hall at nine, after Okafor had been installed in the blue room and the house had gone quiet in the particular way it went quiet when it contained one more awake person than usual.

He did not ask how it went. He stood beside her in the dark under the staircase, where the portraits of Edmund hung above them in their gilt frames, invisible now except as paler rectangles against darker wallpaper.

"She caught it," Marguerite said. "In under a minute. She'd checked the Kew registers three weeks ago. Three weeks, August — before she ever wrote her first letter. She came here already knowing where the bodies of our anecdotes were buried."

"She's thorough."

"She's something else entirely." Marguerite pressed her fingertips against the banister, feeling the grain of wood that had been polished by forty years of hands. "She counted the letters. Forty-one out, nine in. Do you understand what that means? She found the shape of the hole without knowing what was in it. She's mapped the absence." She laughed, briefly, without much humor. "Pell wants the papers to destroy me. She wants them to correct the record. And I've invited one of them to stay in my guest room."

"They're not the same," August said.

"No. They're not." She turned to look up at where the portrait must be, though she could not see it. "But August — she'll find the drawer's contents within three days. She'll find the gap in my husband's year and she'll walk straight into it, and then everything we've buried will be on a young woman's table in pencil." Her voice dropped. "And Pell will learn she's here. He watches this house. You know he watches this house."

Down the lane, far off, a carriage lamp moved — slow, unhurried, passing the gate without turning in. August tracked it until the dark swallowed it.

"I know," he said.

He did not sleep that night. At two in the morning, by the light of a shuttered lantern in the carriage house, he copied out from memory everything he knew of Pell's schedule, his lodgings on Mill Street, the hours his landlady kept, and the evening — Thursdays, always Thursdays — when the census man walked to the King's Arms and drank exactly two whiskies and walked home again past the unlit mill.

Thursday was tomorrow.
