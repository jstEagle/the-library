## 22. Fenwick Dies

The letter came on a Tuesday, in a hand August did not recognize, addressed in ink gone brownish at the edges as though the writer had paused too long between strokes. He steamed it open over the kitchen kettle out of habit — twelve years of habit — and read it standing in the grey light before dawn, and then he sat down on the cook's stool without meaning to.

*Dear Mrs. Ashcombe,* it began. *It is my sad duty to inform you that my mother, Mrs. Harriet Fenwick, passed away in her sleep on Sunday night last. She had spoken of you often. The funeral will be held Thursday at eleven o'clock, St. Saviour's, Camden. She would have wanted you there.*

He read it twice. Then he carried it upstairs, knocked once, and stood in the doorway holding it out like something that might bite.

Marguerite was at her writing desk, the true draft of her memoir open beneath her hands, three weeks of honest sentences stacked around her like fortifications. She looked up, saw his face, and knew before she took the page.

"No," she said. It was not a question. It was a refusal of the fact itself.

"Sunday night," August said quietly. "In her sleep. They're saying her heart."

"They're saying." Marguerite took the letter. Her eyes went to those two words and stayed there, and August watched her think exactly what he was thinking, which was that Mrs. Fenwick's heart had been a stubborn, contrary organ for eighty-one years, a heart that had survived prison hunger strikes in 1872 and a husband's death and forty years of being called a lunatic by men in Parliament, and that hearts like that did not simply stop because they were tired.

But people did die. That was the terrible arithmetic of their present circumstances. People died, and some of them died conveniently, and one could never again be certain which kind of dying one was looking at.

"She gave testimony only last month," Marguerite said. Her voice had gone very level, very careful, the voice she used when the alternative was shaking. "Names. Dates. What she remembered of Edmund's letters to the Home Office man — she remembered the man's name, August. She was the only one who remembered it."

"I know."

"And now it lives—" Marguerite touched her own breastbone, where the notebook rode in her bag, where it always rode now, close as a second heart. "Here. In pencil. In my handwriting."

August said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't either a comfort or a lie, and they had spent the winter learning to tell those apart.

* * *

St. Saviour's, Camden, was full in the way churches are full when the dead woman mattered more than the living congregation could politely admit. The pews held old women in black gloves and younger ones who had come from offices and meeting halls, suffragists of every generation since the movement's first hungry years, and among them, near the back where the light came down dusty through the high windows, sat August Villalobos in his best dark coat, watching.

Marguerite sat in the third row with Miss Okafor beside her. Okafor had insisted on coming — "I am a historian, Mrs. Ashcombe, and funerals are archives" — and she sat now with her gloved hands folded and her sharp eyes moving over the congregation like a hand over braille, reading it.

August counted mourners because someone had to, and because counting kept him from thinking. Forty-one. Forty-two. The organist played something restrained. A curate with a cold spoke briefly about a long life of service, using the phrase *her cause* in the vague way clergymen used such phrases, as though the cause were gardening or parish finances rather than the emancipation of half of England.

And then August saw Mr. Pell.

Pell sat in the last pew but one, on the aisle, wearing a good coat slightly too heavy for the season and an expression of suitable gravity. He did not fidget. He did not take notes — not openly. But his head moved, slowly, deliberately, from face to face down the rows, and August understood with a chill that settled somewhere behind his sternum that the man was doing precisely what August himself was doing.

Counting. Cataloguing. Weighing each mourner for what she might know, what she might say, what she might be worth.

Pell's gaze crossed August's and stopped. For a moment neither man moved. Then Pell inclined his head a half-inch — courteous, almost warm, the acknowledgment of one professional to another across the crowded room — and returned to his survey.

August found his hands had closed into fists on his knees. He opened them. There would be time enough later for what his hands wanted.

The curate finished. The coffin went past them down the aisle, plain oak, borne by six men including two former prisoners of Holloway who walked with the particular straightness of women who had learned to carry things. Marguerite rose with the rest. Her face was composed. Her eyes were not dry, but she let no tear fall while anyone could see, and August thought: *there she is. Twelve years of practice at grieving in public without giving anything away.* And then he thought, less kindly: *and I taught her none of it. The world taught her. Edmund taught her. Every silence taught her.*

Outside, the February wind cut along the churchyard wall and snapped the wreaths' black ribbon. Mourners clustered and scattered. And Pell, patient as frost, made his way through them with his hat in his hand, offering condolences, asking small questions — *were you at the anniversary speech? how well did you know Mrs. Ashcombe? what a loss, so sudden* — collecting answers the way a man collects receipts.

Marguerite watched him do it from the steps. So did Okafor.

"That man," Okafor said quietly, "has been at three movement funerals this year. I checked. Mrs. Prynne's in October. Miss Aldous in November. Now Fenwick." She turned her clear, unblinking gaze on Marguerite. "Do you know what a historian calls a pattern, Mrs. Ashcombe?"

"Evidence," Marguerite said.

"Precisely."

* * *

They did not speak freely until the hansom was moving, and even then Marguerite waited until they had left Camden behind, until the streets narrowed and the horse's rhythm covered their voices.

"He'll come to the others next," she said. "The women who were close to Fenwick. He'll sit in their parlours with his sympathetic face and ask them what Harriet told them at the end, and they will tell him, because they are grieving and lonely and he is the only visitor asking after the dead." Her hands were tight in her lap. "Grief makes people generous with secrets, August. I've seen it. You want to give the dead away — hand them out piece by piece so they keep existing in other mouths."

"You won't tell them what she told you."

"No." Marguerite looked out the window. The city slid past, brick and smoke and bare trees. "Not yet. Not until I understand why she waited forty years to say it aloud. She told me she'd suspected Edmund since 'sixty-one. Forty years, August. She carried it through prison and hunger strike and every march we ever walked together, and she never once said a word to me until I came to her door with my notebook shaking in my hand." Her laugh had no humour in it. "She apologized. Do you know that? She apologized to me for suspecting my husband. As if the suspicion were her crime and not his."

The carriage turned onto the bridge road. Below, the river moved grey and indifferent under the February sky.

"What did she give you?" August asked. "Exactly. If we're to protect it, I should know its shape."

Marguerite was silent a moment. Then she reached into her bag and brought out the notebook — not opening it, just holding it, feeling its weight. "A name," she said. "The Home Office man Edmund wrote to, before Pell's time. A superior. Fenwick saw him once, outside our house, in 'eighty-seven. She remembered his face and his name because she'd seen him earlier, at a trial, sitting with the prosecution's counsel. She drew me his face, August. In pencil, in this book, better than any photograph. And she told me where he kept his papers — a club off Pall Mall, a private room — because her brother had been a member and pointed him out there once." Her jaw tightened. "That's what died on Sunday night and didn't get buried. That's what lives in six ounces of paper in my lap."

"And Pell knows she talked to you."

"Pell counts everything. Yes. He knows."

August watched the river go by and did his arithmetic, the grim kind. Fenwick's testimony, unrecorded anywhere but Marguerite's notebook. Pell's agents already searching houses — they had taken the false memoir draft in January, believing it real, but Pell had learned since, August was certain of it, learned that the draft he stole was the comfortable one, which meant he knew a truer one existed, which meant the notebook and the true draft were now the only copies of the truth in England, and both of them lived within reach of one house on one street.

"We should copy it," he said. "Today. Two copies, separate places. Paper survives; people don't. That's the whole lesson of today."

"Yes." Marguerite's thumb moved along the notebook's edge. "Yes, you're right. Tonight, after dark. I'll write, you'll—"

"I'll steam nothing open," August said, "and lock nothing away. Three copies. One here, one with Okafor, one—" He hesitated.

"One where?"

"Somewhere Pell will never look because it's beneath his notice." August met her eyes. "With the coach harness. In oilcloth. Men like Pell search desks and drawers and strongboxes. No man from the Home Office has ever searched a tack room."

Something moved through Marguerite's expression — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first since the letter came. "Twelve years of reading other people's mail," she said, "and it comes to hiding state evidence in the horse blankets."

"It comes to that more often than you'd think, ma'am."

* * *

They copied the testimony that night at the dining table, Marguerite writing in her upright, unornamented hand while August read aloud in a low, steady voice — Fenwick's words about the man outside the house, the trial, the club off Pall Mall, the dates, the faces. It took two hours. By the end Marguerite's pen-hand ached and the candle had burned down three inches and neither of them had spoken of anything but the text, because the text was safer than everything around it.

Okafor took her copy at the door, wrapping it in a shawl like a child. "I'll deposit it with my solicitor tomorrow," she said. "Under seal, with instructions. And Mrs. Ashcombe—" She paused on the step, snow beginning to sift past the lamplight. "Fenwick's death may be nothing. Hearts stop; I know the statistics better than most. But if it isn't nothing, then you should understand what you're carrying. You are, at this moment, the entire historical record. One notebook and one conscience. Historians have built less on more, but we shouldn't make a habit of it."

She went out into the snow. August bolted the door behind her and stood a moment with his hand on the wood.

"August." Marguerite was still at the table, the original notebook flat under her palm. The candlelight made her look older and younger at once. "Say what you're thinking. We agreed — plainly, whatever it costs."

He came and sat across from her. Plainness still cost him; he paid anyway.

"Fenwick was the last witness who could confirm the name from memory," he said. "Everyone else who saw that man's face is dead or scattered or too old to trust. Which means Pell doesn't need to find your notebook anymore. He needs only to make sure nothing you have can be corroborated — or, failing that, that you're discredited before you use it. A widow's grief-mad manuscript. Easy enough to arrange." He laid his own hands flat on the table, mirroring hers, so that the notebook lay framed between their four hands like something besieged. "He'll move soon. Men like Pell move fastest when the record narrows to one source. I've read enough government correspondence to know the shape of it."

"Then we move faster."

"We move differently." August held her gaze. "Speed gets people caught. We need the thing published, whole, everywhere, before it can be stolen or suppressed or laughed out of existence. Not hidden in tack rooms and solicitors' seals — those buy us months, not victory. We need printers, Marguerite. Many of them, working the same night, so that taking one proof destroys nothing. We need to stop protecting the truth and start distributing it."

Marguerite looked down at the notebook between their hands. Outside, the snow thickened against the windows, erasing the street, the lamps, the ordinary world, until the house seemed to float alone in the dark with its dangerous papers and its two keepers.

"There's a printer in Southwark," she said at last, slowly, the way one steps onto ice. "A woman. Ada Quill. She printed our pamphlets in the nineties when no one else would touch them, and she owes the movement her livelihood, and she has presses that run all night." Her fingers tightened on the notebook's edge. "If we do this — if we set a date and commit — there's no second version. No safe draft, no retreat. Pell will know exactly what we are the morning it lands on every desk in Westminster."

August thought of the burned letter twelve years ago, of the smoke rising off the garden, of every silence since, each one a stone in a wall he'd believed was shelter and had begun to understand was a cell.

"Then we choose the night carefully," he said. "And we tell no one the date. Not Okafor. Not Quill herself until the day before. Not even the housekeeper." He paused, and heard himself say the rest from somewhere deeper than caution. "Because I have spent twelve years keeping your secrets, and I am very good at it, and so are you. Let's spend it all in one place. One night. Everything."

Marguerite lifted the notebook and held it against her chest, over the heart Fenwick's death had made feel suddenly temporary.

"The century turns in five weeks," she said. "Let it turn on the truth."

Above them, faint through the ceiling, the mantel clock ticked on toward midnight, indifferent and exact, keeping the appointment it had been keeping since before any of them were born — and downstairs, folded into August's coat pocket where he had put it that afternoon without quite admitting why, sat a note delivered by hand to the servants' entrance, unsigned, in a small precise hand he recognized:

*You count mourners. So do I. We should talk before the will is read. — P.*

August decided, for one more night, not to show it to her.
