## 23. The Stolen Draft

The house had a way of announcing strangers before August ever saw them. It was an old house, the Ashcombe house, built in the years when carpenters believed wood should speak — floors that groaned under weight, doors that sighed on their hinges, stair treads that gave up their secrets with a soft complaint. He had lived inside its vocabulary for twelve years. He knew which boards announced Marguerite's step, which announced the postman, which announced nothing at all but the weather turning cold.

So when he stood in the kitchen on the last Tuesday of November, drying a cup that was already dry, and heard the third stair from the bottom creak in a rhythm no member of the household used, he did not startle. He set down the cup. He untied his apron with the unhurried motions of a man who intended to be surprised by whatever came next.

There were three of them. He counted them by their sounds before he saw them: two heavy, one light; boots and boots and something softer, leather soles worn smooth. They moved through the hall with the false confidence of men who had been told the house would be empty and were annoyed to find it otherwise.

Marguerite had gone to Brighton that morning. Mrs. Fenwick's grave needed tending now that frost had come, and Marguerite had insisted on going herself, refusing the companion she was usually glad to have, saying she wanted to sit with the old woman without anyone arranging her face for company. August had driven her to the station. She had gripped his hand a moment longer than farewell required — he had thought about that grip all the way home, and was thinking of it still when the strangers' boots found his hallway.

He met them in the entrance hall, wiping his hands on his trousers like any servant interrupted at his work.

"Morning," said the heavy one. He had the look of a man who did paperwork for a living and enjoyed it too much — spectacles, a ledger under his arm, a smile calibrated to be disarming and missing by several degrees. "Mr. Villalobos, isn't it? We're from the census office. Follow-up inquiries."

"Follow-up," August repeated. In his experience, follow-up inquiries arrived by post. They did not arrive as three men with soft-soled shoes and a fourth man's absence where a warrant should have been.

"Routine," the man said, and produced papers that were genuine enough to pass a glance and folded enough to survive one. "Mrs. Ashcombe's household records require verification. There's some question about the enumeration of 'ninety-one. You'll want to cooperate, of course."

"Of course," August said. "You'll want to wait until Mrs. Ashcombe returns, then. She keeps her own papers."

"We're not after her papers." The man's smile stayed fixed while his eyes went past August's shoulder, up the stairs, down the corridor toward the study. "Just the household enumeration. A few minutes and we're gone."

August considered the geometry of it. The study was locked — he held the key, and had held it since the night he'd changed what the drawer contained. The servants' quarters were above; the girl, Ada, was out marketing. The library held Marguerite's correspondence, sorted and harmless. And upstairs, in the writing room, in the second drawer of the desk beneath a folio of press cuttings from 1887, lay the draft memoir — the false one, the safe one, the myth set down in Marguerite's own careful hand: *Edmund Ashcombe, martyr, taken from us at the height of his devotion.*

The true draft did not live in this house anymore. It had not since Thursday.

*Let them take it,* August thought, and the thought arrived with a calm that frightened him more than the men did, because it meant he had been waiting for them. Some part of him, some part that had spent twelve years reading envelopes over steam and memorizing the shapes of other people's secrets, had known this visit was coming the way one knows rain by the smell of the air.

"The study's locked," he said. "Household rule."

"Then we'll do the rest of the house." The man with the ledger was already moving, and behind him the two others split without being told, one to the library, one up the stairs, their soft shoes eating the floorboards' warnings whole.

* * *

They searched the way government men search — thoroughly, joylessly, and with an air of apology that made it worse. They lifted the cushions in the drawing room and ran fingers along book spines without pulling a single volume, because they had been told what to look for and it was paper, loose paper, a manuscript. They opened the piano bench. They went through the linen cupboard with the delicacy of men who had once been embarrassed by such work and had since stopped being anything at all.

August followed them at a polite distance, hands clasped behind his back, and narrated nothing, helped nothing, hindered nothing. This was its own discipline. To protest was to confirm value. To assist was to confess knowledge. He had learned long ago that the most impenetrable surface a man could present was mild cooperation, and he wore it now like a livery.

The one upstairs — the soft-soled one, quick and quiet — took eleven minutes in the writing room. August timed him against the mantel clock in the hall below, watching its brass pendulum swing through six hundred and sixty seconds of his own heartbeat. When the man came down, he carried the folio of press cuttings loosely, carelessly, and the manuscript pages were gone from it, folded into an oilskin wallet inside his coat.

"That'll be all we need," the ledger man said. He left a printed card on the hall table — *Inquiries may be directed to the office of Mr. E. Pell* — and the three of them went out into the grey morning like smoke drawn back up a chimney.

August stood in the hall a long while. The house ticked and settled around him. On the table, Pell's card sat where a calling card might sit, white and formal and obscene.

Then he went to the writing room, knelt at the desk, and checked — though he already knew — that the second drawer held only the folio, empty now of everything but clippings, and beneath the clippings, exactly where he had left it, the small square of paper he had placed there on Thursday night:

*If you are reading this, they have taken what I wanted them to take.*

* * *

He had done it four days earlier, on the Thursday, and even now he could reconstruct the evening hour by hour, the way he reconstructed everything, because reconstruction was the only prayer he trusted.

Marguerite had been at Fenwick's bedside until late — the old woman failing by inches that week, though none of them had said so aloud. August had eaten alone in the kitchen, and afterward he had gone to the writing room with a lamp, meaning only to bank the fire, and had stood instead before the desk with his hand on the drawer pull for the better part of ten minutes.

The problem had come to him that afternoon, fully formed, the way solutions sometimes arrive uninvited and unwelcome: Pell knew there was a draft. Okafor knew there was a draft. The household knew there was a draft. A thing known by five people was a thing that could be demanded, seized, subpoenaed, stolen. And Marguerite, in her present state — grief for Fenwick sharpening into fury, fury hardening into purpose — would not hide it. Hiding was August's art, not hers. She would leave it in the desk like a gauntlet thrown down, because Marguerite Ashcombe did not conceal herself from anyone, not anymore, not since the drawer had been opened and the dead husband unmasked.

But August had concealed himself his whole life, and he knew the shape of concealment the way a blind man knows a room.

So he had copied her. That was the ugly heart of it, the part he had not yet told her and did not know how to tell her: he had sat at her own desk, with her own ink, in a hand he had practiced for twenty years of forging signatures on delivery receipts, and written out the false memoir — the martyr version, the safe version, Edmund the devoted, Edmund the betrayed — in her voice. He had studied her script the way he studied everything: the slight forward lean of her letters, the way her *g* dropped below the line like a woman curtseying, the ink pressure lighter on the second half of a page when her hand tired. He had written forty pages of lie in her hand, and when he finished, near midnight, he had read it back aloud in a whisper and heard, to his horror, that it sounded like her. Not the words — those were his, assembled from her published speeches, her letters to the newspapers, every public utterance she had ever given about her husband. But the *voice*, the cadence, the woman herself, pressed flat onto paper like a flower kept for mourning.

He had swapped it into the drawer and carried the true draft out to the carriage house, where it now lay inside the hollow axle housing of the old brougham, wrapped in oilcloth — the one place in his domain no searcher would think to look, because no searcher thinks a carriage is a document.

And he had not told her.

That was the arithmetic he turned over all day Wednesday, all day Thursday, standing in the hall with Pell's card in his hand: he had protected her again. Without asking. Without telling. After everything — after the greenhouse, after the confrontation, after the plain speaking of that terrible evening when he had confessed every silence and she had confessed every silence she had asked of him, and they had agreed, had they not agreed, that the era of protection was over?

He had done it anyway. Because he loved her. Because protecting her was the only language his hands knew fluently.

Because he was afraid that if he asked her permission, she might say no — and if she said no, and the true draft were seized, everything would end on Pell's terms.

Or because he did not trust her judgment. He turned that possibility over too, examining it like a coin of doubtful metal, and found it had some weight. That was the worst of it. Twelve years of gatekeeping had taught him that people in the grip of great purposes became careless with themselves. He had watched Marguerite walk into crowds that hated her, sign her name to things that ruined friendships, spend her health like a woman spending someone else's money. His silences had always been, at bottom, a correction for her courage — a belief that she was brave in the wrong proportion, that she needed him to be cowardly on her behalf, that between them they could average out into one sensible person.

But she had asked him to stop. She had asked him to stand beside her, not in front of her.

And he had forged her handwriting instead.

* * *

She came back on the evening train, and he met her at the station as he always did, and she knew within thirty seconds. That was the thing about Marguerite that had undone him from the first year: she missed nothing. Twelve years of his careful face, and she read it now like large print.

"What happened?" she said, settling into the motor car — they had bought it the year before, a Darracq she drove herself on open roads, another piece of the new century arriving whether the old one liked it or not.

"Pell sent men. Three. Census pretext." He turned the crank, climbed in beside her, and took the reins of the wheel. "They searched the house."

Her breath went very still beside him. "What did they take?"

"The draft."

The silence lasted a full minute. He drove. The lamps of the town thinned behind them; the hedges came black against the last grey light. He could feel her thinking beside him the way one feels a fire thinking in a grate.

"August." Her voice was level, and level in that particular way he had learned meant the opposite. "Which draft?"

And there it was. The question he had dreaded and deserved, delivered without anger, which was worse than anger, because it meant she had already worked out the answer and was giving him one chance to say it himself.

"The safe one," he said. "The martyr version."

"You wrote it."

"In your hand. Yes."

Another silence. The wheels sang on the wet road. When she spoke again, each word came separately, like stones laid across a stream.

"I signed my name to a bargain with Miss Okafor. I agreed to publish the truth under my own name and let the myth die publicly. I have spent three weeks becoming the woman who does that. And you decided — without telling me — to keep a forgery of my cowardice in my own desk, so that when Pell's men came, they would steal a lie I never wrote."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He pulled the car off at the wide place above the coast road and stopped, because this answer deserved stillness and she deserved his eyes. Below them the sea worked at the dark, patient as a clerk.

"Because I was afraid," he said. "Not of Pell. Of losing. If they'd taken the true draft, it would have been in their hands and not ours, and everything — Fenwick's testimony, your account, my statement — everything would have been theirs to bury or twist. So I gave them a counterfeit. It's what I'm for, Marguerite. It's the trade I've practiced my whole life. Reading what shouldn't be read. Writing what shouldn't be written. Standing between you and consequences."

"And I asked you to stop standing between us."

"You did."

"And you didn't tell me because—"

"Because if I'd told you, you'd have had to decide. And either way you decided, you'd carry it. If you said yes, you'd be party to a deception you've sworn off. If you said no, and they took the real one—" He stopped. Started again. "I took the decision so you wouldn't have to bear it. Which is the same crime as ever. Just wearing new clothes."

She looked at him for a long time. The wind moved her hair at its edges; she had stopped dressing for other people's comfort months ago. And he saw her doing what she always did in the worst moments — weighing, precisely, like a chemist — the act against the man, the betrayal against the result.

"They took the forgery," she said at last. "Pell believes he has my memoir. The coward's version. The myth in my hand."

"He believes it."

"And when the true book comes out in January — when Okafor publishes, when my name is on the honest account — he will compare it to what his agents stole from my desk. He will see two manuscripts. One flattering, one damning. Both in my handwriting." Her eyes narrowed, working it through. "He will know one of them is false. And he will ask himself which, and when, and why — and the answer he arrives at will be that someone in this house anticipated him. That the widow of Saint Edmund keeps a decoy grief in her drawer like a loaded pistol."

"Yes," August said quietly. "He'll know there's a true draft. He may already suspect it — why else search? But now he won't just suspect it. He'll hold proof of its existence. A forgery is a confession that an original exists."

Marguerite turned back to the windshield, to the dark sea, and he watched her jaw set in the lamplight — the set it took when she moved from weighing to deciding, the set he had watched from the back of halls for twelve years and followed, always, wherever it led.

"Then we've bought ourselves a little time," she said, "and sold him a certainty. He knows the truth is written. He knows it's close. He knows I mean to sign it." She reached over and took his hand — not gently; firmly, the grip she'd given him on the platform that morning, doubled now. "So we don't have weeks anymore, August. We have days. Whatever we mean to do — the printers, the testimony, all of it — we do it now, tonight if we must. Before he reads his stolen pages closely enough to notice what I noticed the moment you confessed."

"What did you notice?"

A ghost of something crossed her face — not quite a smile, colder than triumph, warmer than fear.

"My hand doesn't tire in the second half of a page," she said. "Yours does. Whoever copies a woman's grief ought to remember that grief gets heavier, not lighter. He'll see it. Pell sees everything. And when he sees it, he'll understand he's been played by a chauffeur — and God help us both, August, he will come for you first."

She put the car in gear herself and drove them home through the dark, faster than was wise, while August sat beside her holding the silence that was, at last, no longer entirely his to keep.
