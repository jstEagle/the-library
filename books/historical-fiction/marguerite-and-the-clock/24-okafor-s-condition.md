## 24. Okafor's Condition

The study had not changed since Edmund's death, and that was the trouble with it. The same green lamp with its frayed silk cord. The same globe, its political borders a generation out of date, so that empires that had crumbled still bloomed across Africa and Asia in confident pink. The same smell of pipe tobacco soaked into the wainscoting, though no one had smoked in this room for twelve years. Houses kept their dead the way ledgers kept debts: faithfully, without judgment, and forever.

Marguerite had moved the desk chair to face the window. It was a small rebellion, but she took her rebellions where she could find them now.

Miss Adaeze Okafor sat across from her with a leather portfolio open on her knees and the particular stillness of a person who has already decided what she is going to say and is merely choosing the order of the blows. She was younger than Marguerite had expected — eight-and-twenty perhaps, maybe thirty — with close-cropped hair and a way of looking at you that made you aware of every sentence before you spoke it. She wore a grey walking dress of severe cut and a brooch shaped like an open book, which Marguerite suspected was not ornament but declaration.

"Before I answer," Okafor said, "I want to tell you what I found in your husband's papers. Not what I was shown. What I found."

"You may as well." Marguerite folded her hands in her lap. They did not tremble. She had practiced that. "I expect we've read the same letters."

"I don't think we have," said Okafor. "Because if you had read them — truly read them, all of them, the way I have, three times now, cross-referenced against the Treasury memoranda at the Public Record Office — then you would not be sitting there with your hands folded like a woman waiting for a verdict. You would be standing."

Marguerite looked at her. Outside, October rain drew long grey fingers down the glass, and somewhere below, August was doing something to the motorcar that involved a great deal of restrained swearing, a sound she had learned to find more comforting than church bells.

"What did the Record Office give you?"

"Everything Pell's people thought was buried deep enough." Okafor drew out a folder, thin, cheap paper, government stock. "Payments. Dated. Initialled. Fourteen names over nineteen months, Mrs. Ashcombe. Not rumors. Receipts. Your husband was paid eleven pounds per name, and he haggled twice — here, and here — which I confess is the detail that haunts me most. A man who bargains for his conscience has none left to lose."

The fire ticked. Marguerite made herself look at the dates on the top sheet, though she knew them, had known them since the night she opened the locked drawer and the floor of her life tilted like a deck in weather. Edmund's handwriting. His little flourish on the *E*. She used to trace that flourish on his letters when he was alive, when it meant something tender.

"And so," Marguerite said, "you will print it. That is what historians are for. Print it, Miss Okafor, and let the century sort itself out around it. I release the papers to you freely. You need nothing further from me."

"No." Okafor closed the folder. "That's precisely what I don't want."

* * *

August heard the raised voices only faintly through the study window, which was how he preferred it. He had spent twelve years perfecting the art of being within earshot and outside comprehension, and old habits held even now, when the habit itself had become the question at the center of his life.

He wiped his hands on the rag and looked at the motorcar without seeing it. It was a fine machine, a Panhard et Levassor they'd had four years, and it gave him honest work — grease and timing chains and spark plugs, problems that yielded to attention. People were less tractable. He had spent a lifetime learning that a letter steamed open told you exactly as much as it told you and no more; a woman's silence told you everything and explained nothing.

He thought about the night before. Marguerite had come down to the carriage house at eleven o'clock, still dressed, carrying no lamp, and stood in the doorway watching him bank the stove. He'd known she was there before he turned around — he always knew — and he'd waited, because rushing her had never once worked in twelve years.

"I've asked Miss Okafor to come tomorrow," she'd said finally. "She wants the papers. All of them."

"And will you give them?"

"That depends entirely on what she asks for next."

Then she'd gone back up to the house, and August had lain awake under the eaves doing arithmetic he hated: counting the days until Pell struck, counting the witnesses still living, counting the years he had spent reading other people's mail and calling it service.

Now, through the window, he saw Marguerite rise from behind her desk, and he saw — though he could not hear it — the moment the conversation turned, because her shoulders came up and her chin came forward and she became, visibly, the woman who had faced down hecklers in Manchester while eggs burst against the platform boards. Whatever the historian wanted, she wasn't giving it easily.

He went back to the spark plugs. Some things you could not fix by listening, and some things you could not fix by not listening either. He was still deciding which kind this was when the study door opened above him and both women came out into the hall together, Okafor buttoning her coat with brisk finality.

"Mr. Villalobos," Okafor called down, and he straightened, wiping his hands again out of pure reflex. "May I ask you something before I go? Off the record — though I'm beginning to suspect there is very little off the record anywhere near this house."

"Ma'am."

"The letters that came to Mrs. Ashcombe. In the early years. Before her husband died." Her eyes were level and unembarrassed. "Were they opened before she read them?"

The rain filled the pause. August felt Marguerite's gaze land on him from the top of the stairs like a hand on his shoulder — not warning him, exactly. Watching him. Seeing what he would do.

"Some envelopes," he said, "stick in damp weather. Hard to open cleanly. I helped where I could."

Okafor studied him for a long moment, and then she smiled — a small, dry smile with no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. "Yes," she said. "That's what I thought. Thank you, Mr. Villalobos. You may be the most valuable document in this entire archive, and I say that knowing you'll hate hearing it."

She let herself out. August stood in the wet yard holding a dirty rag and feeling, distinctly, like a letter someone had just finished reading.

* * *

They argued about it for two hours.

Not the way they had argued in the greenhouse — that had been a collision, glass breaking, twelve years shattering on the flagstones between them. This was different. This was two people who had learned each other's damage sitting across a kitchen table at midnight, tea going cold, working a problem the way engineers work one: carefully, and with full knowledge that any solution will cost.

"She's right," Marguerite said.

"She's asking you to burn yourself alive in a public square."

"She's asking me to sign my name." Marguerite pressed her palms flat against the table. "There's a difference, August, and the difference is the whole point. Pell wants me erased — quietly, by statute, by euphemism, 'historical review,' God, the very words make my skin crawl. Okafor wants me *present*. Accounted for. On the page, with my own signature under my own worst truth. Do you know how long it's been since anyone asked me for my signature rather than my silence?"

"Twelve years," August said quietly. "Give or take."

She looked at him. The kitchen lamp threw their shadows up the wall, enormous and slightly misshapen, two figures taller than their grief.

"Yes," she said. "You would know."

It wasn't cruel. That was the strangest part of these new conversations — the plainness didn't wound anymore, or rather it wounded evenly, shared out between them like bread. He had confessed his silences. She had confessed her orders. Neither had forgiven the other, and both had stopped pretending forgiveness was the price of staying in the room.

"If you sign," he said, "they'll take the movement apart to get at you. The women who stayed after the reading — Fenwick's gone, God rest her, and her testimony lives in your notebook and nowhere else. The ones still standing will be asked, under oath, whether they knew. Whether they covered. Some of them did cover. I covered. Half the people who loved that myth helped build it, and you'll be handing every one of them to the Home Office with a ribbon on it."

"And if I don't sign, Okafor won't publish, and the official centenary history comes out next autumn with Edmund canonized and fourteen dead women's names filed under 'unresolved disappearances,' and Pell retires on a pension having won." She shook her head slowly. "I've done the sums too, August. I've been doing them since the drawer."

"Then do the last sum. What does it cost *you*?"

Marguerite was quiet for a long time. When she answered, her voice had dropped into a register he had heard only once before — at Fenwick's funeral, at the graveside, when she'd said *she carried it alone and called it loyalty*.

"Everything I am," she said. "That's the cost. That's what she's asking. Forty years, August. Arrests, hunger strikes, the platform in Manchester, the prison cell at Holloway — all of it built on the story that Edmund died for us. Every speech I ever gave, I gave standing on his grave. If I sign the truth, I don't just bury him properly. I unbuild myself. There is no version of Marguerite Ashcombe that survives it intact. She's asking me to become a different person in public, at fifty-eight, in front of the whole country, permanently."

August reached across the table and, for the first time in twelve years, took her hand — not as a servant steadying a lady stepping from a carriage, but plainly, hand to hand, on a flat table under a lamp.

"Then let me ask the question she can't," he said. "Is the person you've been worth protecting?"

Marguerite looked down at their hands. She did not pull away. Outside, the wind worried the last leaves off the chestnut tree, and somewhere in the dark the carriage house clock — the one Edmund had bought her, the one that kept imperfect time and had never once been corrected — ticked its stubborn, slightly fast minute.

"No," she said at last. "But she's mine. And I find, lately, that I should like to meet her."

* * *

She summoned Okafor back for Thursday, and this time they met in the front parlor, the public room, the room with the good light — a choice Marguerite made deliberately and knew Okafor would notice.

"I'll sign," she said, before the historian had even unwound her scarf. "My name, printed, to the true account. The receipts, the payments, the fourteen names, my twelve years of silence about it, all of it. I'll stand on whatever platform you put me on and say it aloud if the book demands it."

Okafor went very still. Historians, Marguerite thought, were not accustomed to getting exactly what they asked for; they spent their lives among partial records and had lost the knack of receiving whole truths.

"But," Marguerite continued, "I have conditions of my own, and you will hear all of them before you agree to anything, because I intend for this bargain to be written down — dated, signed, witnessed. I have learned what happens to arrangements that live only in memory. They get rewritten by whoever survives."

"Name them."

"First: the women. The fourteen names go in the book — fully spelled, with whatever biographies you can reconstruct — but the living women who sheltered inside the myth, some knowingly, are named nowhere except in sealed deposit with a solicitor, released twenty years after my death. They were lied to by a dead man and protected by a living fool, and they shouldn't pay my debt."

"Agreed, with the caveat that any who publicly attack you forfeit the seal."

"Second: August Villalobos is acknowledged in the record. By name, and by function. What he read, what he burned, what he kept. If I am to be dismantled in print, he goes into the light with me. I will not publish a history in which the man who carried half the weight appears as 'a household servant.' Third: the papers themselves come to your archive, not to the state. Pell gets copies over my dead body, and possibly not then."

"And fourth?" Okafor said, because she had counted Marguerite's pauses the way Marguerite had once counted hecklers, and knew there was one more coming.

Marguerite rose and walked to the window. Down in the yard, August was checking the motorcar's lamps against the early dusk, moving around the machine with that economy of motion she had watched for twelve years and was only now, truly, allowing herself to see.

"Fourth," she said. "When it's done — when the book is printed and the myth is dead and the century has turned over — I want the record to end honestly about me too. Not 'Mrs. Ashcombe, widow of the martyr.' Not 'suffragist leader.' Write what happened. That a woman loved a traitor for nine years without knowing, and guarded his ghost for twelve more, and chose the truth in the end because she could no longer bear the weight of the lie — and that she did not choose it alone. Put the driver in the dedication, Miss Okafor. Let the century know that the keeper of silences broke them first, in the end, for her sake. History is full of great men and the women who survived them. Let it have, for once, the account of two people who decided the truth was worth more than their reputations, and see what it costs them, itemized."

For the first time since Marguerite had known her, Okafor looked away first. She took off her spectacles, cleaned them on her sleeve, and put them back on, and when she spoke her voice had lost its lecture-hall edge.

"Do you understand what you're agreeing to?" she said. "Not the movement. *You.* Once your name is under that account, there is no version of events in which you come out whole. They will call you a liar, a dupe, a collaborator, an opportunist — sometimes all in the same editorial. The young women who carry your photograph in their lockets will burn it. Men who never dared oppose you while you were strong will line up to kick what's left. Pell will have you in court inside a month on statutes older than either of us. And in fifty years, when they build the monument, your name will be on it in small letters, under an asterisk, forever. I cannot protect you from any of that. A footnote is not armor. Signing that page is the single most expensive thing you will ever do, and I am required — professionally, morally — to make certain you know it."

"I have known it," Marguerite said, "since the night I opened the drawer. What I did not know, until this week, was whether knowing it would be enough to make me do it." She crossed back to the desk, took up the pen, and pulled the draft agreement toward her. "It is. Witness me."

She signed. The nib scratched; the ink caught the lamplight, wet and black and irrevocable; and Marguerite Ashcombe, widow of the martyr, put her name to the destruction of everything her name had stood for.

Okafor countersigned. Then she packed her portfolio, shook Marguerite's hand with a grip like a promise, and paused at the door.

"One more thing," she said. "A professional courtesy. I came past the census office this morning to verify the household returns — routine, given what's coming. Mr. Pell has requested a supplementary interview regarding irregularities in the 1891 enumeration of this address. Scheduled for Monday." She met August's eyes. "They've cited the driver of record specifically, Mr. Villalobos. As informant. As witness. As, I suspect, target. Whatever you've kept hidden for twelve years, I'd decide quickly what you intend to keep hidden still — because men like Pell don't interview to learn things. They interview to establish what they already believe."

The door closed behind her. The house settled into its evening quiet, clock ticking, fire settling, rain beginning again against the glass.

Marguerite and August stood in the parlor looking at each other across the width of the room, the signed page drying on the desk between them like a bridge just built over a gorge neither had yet crossed.

"Monday," she said.

"Monday," he agreed. And reached for his coat.
