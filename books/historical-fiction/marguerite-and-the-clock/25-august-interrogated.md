## 25. August Interrogated

They came for him on a Tuesday, which August would remember afterward as the only merciful thing about it. Tuesdays Marguerite went to the reading room at the Athenaeum until four, and Pell, whatever else he was, had done his arithmetic.

The men arrived in a plain hansom — no crest, no livery — and stood at the service door of the Ashcombe house with the patient faces of clerks. The taller one produced a paper. *Irregularity of household return, census of 1891,* it said, in language so dry it might have been typed by a machine that hated weather. Mr. August Villalobos was requested to accompany them to answer certain questions. Requested. The word sat on the page like a cat sitting on a bird.

August read it twice, because he always read things twice, and then he put on his coat.

"You'll tell Mrs. Ashcombe," he said to the housekeeper, who had come into the passage with flour on her hands and fear already rising in her face, "that I've gone to see about a wheel."

"A wheel," she repeated.

"The near-side rear. It's been singing for a fortnight." He touched his hat brim to her. "I may be some hours. Wheels are like that."

It was a lie, and they both knew it was a lie, and it was also the truest sentence available: something on this vehicle had been singing wrong for twelve years, and today someone meant to look underneath.

* * *

The room they took him to was not a police station. That was the first lesson, and August understood it was meant as a lesson: not a cell, not an office, but a rented room above a stationer's shop on a street whose name he made a point of memorizing. Chancery Lane, or near enough. There was a table, three chairs, a window high and frosted, and a gas lamp burning at two o'clock in the afternoon because the fog outside wanted company.

Pell sat behind the table. He had removed his coat and rolled his sleeves with the unhurried economy of a man settling in over a ledger, and beside his right hand lay a folder — a real one, government stock, string-tied — and beside his left hand, deliberately, a second object: a single loose sheet of census paper, face down.

"Mr. Villalobos." Pell did not rise. "Sit, please. This shouldn't take longer than it has to."

"That's kind." August took the chair opposite and placed his hat on his knee. He had decided, in the cab, on his posture, and the posture was this: a driver, mildly bored, mildly respectful, with nowhere better to be and nothing in his head but horses and axles.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"Census irregularity. I read the paper." He paused. "There's a mistake somewhere in our household return. Twelve years back. I'd own to being surprised — Mrs. Ashcombe is exactness itself."

"Mistakes happen," Pell agreed. "People misremember. Households shift. A servant comes, a servant goes, names get written down wrong or written down twice." He opened the folder without looking at it, the way a man opens a book he knows by heart. "Your name, though. Your name is interesting, Mr. Villalobos. You appear in the 1891 return as *coachman*. And you appear in the 1881 return for the same address as—" he turned a page with one finger, savoring it— "*groom, unmarried, aged nineteen.*"

"I was younger then."

"You were indeed. And between those two returns you acquired a quality that interests my office." Pell leaned back. "Continuity. Do you know how rare continuity is, in service? Cooks move. Housemaids marry. But a man who stays twelve years under one roof, who drives the mistress daily, who receives her post—" Pell's eyes came up, mild as milk. "Who handles her correspondence."

"I carry her correspondence. I don't read it."

"No," Pell said softly. "Of course you don't."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the stationer's bell rang for a customer, and August fixed his attention on the small sounds the way he fixed it on roads: not because they mattered, but because attention was a thing you could spend, and he meant to spend his carefully.

"Let me tell you what I think," Pell continued. "I think a man in your position reads first. Not from wickedness. From duty. A man who serves a famous woman learns that letters can be knives, and he learns to stand in front of them. Am I close?"

"You're describing a valet out of a novel."

"I'm describing you." Pell drew the loose sheet toward him but did not turn it over. "Here's what puzzles me, and I'll be plain because plainness saves us both time. In March of 1893 there is a letter delivered to the Ashcombe house, registered, from a firm of solicitors in Geneva. It is signed for — your signature, Mr. Villalobos, quite legible — and it never appears in any subsequent record. No reply. No acknowledgment. Nothing."

August looked at him with the flat patience of a man waiting for a horse to finish shying. "You have records of my signatures?"

"We have records of everything eventually." Pell smiled, and it was almost warm, which was the most frightening thing about him. "Geneva, 1893. What came from Geneva?"

"I couldn't say. Letters come; I deliver them to the house. What's in them isn't my department."

"Twelve years, though." Pell tapped the folder. "Twelve years is a long time to not be curious."

"Not really. Curiosity's for people with spare time."

For a moment Pell said nothing, and August understood the silence for what it was — a probe, thrown out to see what would surface. So he gave the silence nothing. He thought about the axle. He thought about the particular creak of the near-side rear wheel when the road turned gravel to clay. He let his face do what a driver's face does, which is nothing at all, weatherproof, built for rain and impertinence alike.

"You were present," Pell said, "the night Edmund Ashcombe died."

And there it was. Not a question — a placement, a stone set down on the table between them, and August felt the whole shape of the interrogation reorganize around it like water finding a new level. The census was the pretext. It had always been the pretext. Pell had come up through the Home Office; he didn't care how many grooms lived where. He cared about the martyrdom of Edmund Ashcombe, and he had somehow scented that the record of that night passed through this room, through this chair, through the man in the chair.

"I was," August said. "Everyone was. It was his house."

"Tell me about it."

"It rained. There were doctors. He died." August shrugged. "I held a horse."

"Held a—"

"There were carriages. Doctors arrive in carriages. Someone holds the horses or they fret." He met Pell's eyes. "That's what I did the night Edmund Ashcombe died, sir. I held two horses for four hours in the rain. If you're writing that down, spell my name right."

Pell studied him for a long moment, and August watched him decide something — watched the decision move across his face like weather crossing a field. Then Pell reached forward, turned over the loose sheet of census paper, and slid it across the table.

It was the 1891 return for the Ashcombe house, filled out in the vicar's neat hand. Every line was ordinary except one. Beside the entry for *Villalobos, August — coachman*, someone had added a notation in pencil, small and official-looking: a reference number, and beneath it, in a different ink, a single word that August had seen before, in other files, in another life he did not talk about.

*Flagged.*

"For historical review," Pell said. "You understand what that means?"

"I'm sure you'll tell me."

"It means that when the centenary histories are written — and they will be written, Mr. Villalobos, by serious people, under proper supervision — the archive will need to be complete. Every letter, every recollection, every signature from Geneva. The widow's papers especially. And the testimony of household staff, who see so much and are so rarely asked." Pell folded his hands. "You could help with that. Voluntarily. Or we could make this involuntary, and untidy, and expensive for everyone. Irregularities in a census return can be pursued as fraud, if one wished to be unkind. A false statement before a Crown officer. I don't wish to be unkind. I wish to be thorough."

August looked at the word *flagged* for a while. Then he looked up, and he allowed himself, for the first time, a small honest thing: he smiled.

"Thoroughness," he said. "Well. I can respect that. Ask me anything, Mr. Pell. I've got nothing to hide and a good memory. Comes with the trade — a driver who forgets a turning loses the fare."

* * *

What followed was two hours of the strangest conversation of August's life, and he had once spent a night explaining himself to a customs officer in Boulogne with a false invoice in his boot.

Pell asked about letters, and August answered — but he answered the way a river answers a bridge: around, beneath, past. Yes, he had handled the correspondence. Yes, some of it was delicate — Mrs. Ashcombe received threats, vile ones, from men who hated the movement; a servant learned to recognize the ugly envelopes. Had he ever steamed one open? He'd heard of such things; seemed a filthy business. Had he ever destroyed correspondence? Once a chimney fire had taken a basket of papers awaiting burning — a tragedy, Mrs. Ashcombe was quite put out. Which year? He'd have to consult a diary; drivers kept diaries of mileage, surely Pell understood that. Did he recall the contents of anything Edmund Ashcombe wrote? He recalled Mr. Edmund mostly by his boots — a gentleman who wore through heels unevenly, favoring the left, which told you he stood with his weight wrong, and August had offered more than once to have them rebuilt, and Mr. Edmund had said thank you, August, and gone on standing wrong.

This last answer he delivered with such unguarded domestic tenderness that Pell actually stopped writing for a moment. Because it was true. All of it was true. That was the craft of it, the weaponization of his old discipline turned now against its examiner: twelve years of curating what he knew had left August with shelves of facts that were genuine, harmless, and useless, and he laid them out one by one like a grocer arranging a window display. Pell could take any item off the shelf and verify it, and every verification would cost him an hour and buy him nothing.

"What did you burn?" Pell asked at last, quietly. It was the fifth time he'd asked it in some form, and August recognized the fatigue in it — the fatigue of a man who has been fishing all day in a well-stocked pond and caught only boot leather.

"Correspondence, generally. We burned papers weekly. Everyone did. Ink and coal cost money and privacy costs more."

"The night of the twelfth of October, 1887."

August let the pause stretch just long enough to be human. "Raining," he said. "I remember that much. Big fire in the study. Mrs. Ashcombe — Missus, then — sat up very late."

"What was burned?"

"Paper, sir. It was all paper."

Pell closed the folder. For a moment he simply looked at August, and August looked back, and between them passed something that was not hostility exactly — nearer to recognition. Two men who dealt in information, acknowledging each other across a table.

"You're better at this than a coachman should be," Pell said.

"I'm worse at coaching than a coachman should be, so it balances."

Pell almost laughed. Almost. He gathered his papers, squared them, tied the string with two precise motions. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn fully around.

"Mrs. Fenwick," he said. "She remembers things differently lately. Memory is a strange country — the borders keep moving. When you speak to her again, you might ask her whether the borders moved on their own." He glanced back, just enough to show one eye. "Oh — and tell your mistress the reading room closes at four. She'll want to be home early, these next weeks. Things are moving."

Then he was gone, and the room was only a room, and August sat alone for a full minute with his hat on his knee, doing the arithmetic Pell had left him.

Fenwick. Pell had spoken to Fenwick, or meant to, and wanted August to know it — wanted the warning passed along, or the poison, whichever took root. And the reading-room remark: Pell knew Marguerite's habits to the hour. Surveillance, then, ongoing, polite, total. And beneath both, the message under the message, the one August had been trained by twelve years of letters to find: *we are coming for the papers, and we will come gently, and gentleness is not the same as mercy.*

He stood, settled his coat, and went out into the fog, walking first past the stationer's window to check the street — habit, trade-craft, whatever you called a skill once it saved you — and then finding a cab.

By half past five the near-side rear wheel had been pronounced, loudly and publicly, repaired. By six he was standing in Marguerite's study with his hat still in his hands, giving her the account entire: the room, the folder, the flagged page, the questions, and last of all the parting words, delivered verbatim, because she of all people deserved the transcript.

Marguerite listened without moving. When he reached the sentence about Mrs. Fenwick, her hands went still in her lap. When he finished, she was quiet a long moment, and then she said, in a voice he had not heard from her since the drawer:

"He knows about Geneva."

"He suspects. Suspecting and knowing are different countries."

"They share a border." She rose and went to the window, where the fog had thickened against the glass like breath on a mirror. "August — if he goes to Fenwick, if he's already gone to her—"

"Then we go faster than he does." August set his hat down on the desk, deliberately, the way a man sets down a weight he intends to pick back up. "You said once you'd choose truth over loyalty. Well. He's counting on our loyalty being slower than his paperwork. So we stop being loyal to anything except the truth — and we start tonight."

Marguerite turned from the window. Whatever was in her face, it had stopped being dread and become something with edges.

"Tonight," she repeated — and rang the bell for paper and ink.
