## 26. Marguerite Comes for Him

The holding room at the county offices smelled of cold tea and wet wool, and August had been sitting in it long enough to memorize the water stain on the ceiling — a stain shaped, he decided somewhere past the second hour, like a hand reaching for something just out of its grasp.

They had taken his watch, his notebook, and his collar stud. They had not, as yet, taken anything that mattered. Pell's men were thorough but unimaginative; they had searched him for documents when what he carried was twelve years of memory, filed and cross-indexed in a skull no warrant could open. When the constable with the red ears had asked him his occupation, August had said, *driver*, and let the word sit there, plain and unremarkable, while inside himself he recited the inventory of everything a driver knows: which letters steam open without tearing, which silences are mercy and which are cowardice, which of Mrs. Fenwick's coughs mean disagreement and which mean the beginning of tears.

He was not frightened. That surprised him a little. He supposed a man who had spent a decade and a half waiting for consequences could afford to be calm when they finally arrived, punctual as rent.

What frightened him was the thought of Marguerite hearing of it. He had imagined — had counted on — her learning of his detention late, through solicitors, through channels, through the careful machinery of respectability that moved slowly enough to be deniable. He had built the whole thing like one of Edmund's letters: a document designed to be read by its intended audience only. Pell would question him, find nothing, keep him a day or two on the pretext of census irregularity, and release him into a silence both men would pretend was victory.

He had not imagined the sound, at half past four in the afternoon, of Marguerite Ashcombe's boots on the corridor floor.

He knew those boots. He had resoled them twice. And he knew — with the particular dread of a man who has spent years reading other people's footsteps — that she was not walking the way she walked into drawing rooms or committee halls. She was walking the way she had walked, once, twelve years ago, down the garden path at midnight toward the bonfire where Edmund's last letter went into the flames.

* * *

Marguerite had dressed for war that morning, though the armor was gray wool and the weapons were a hat pin and a name.

She had woken before dawn with August's absence pressing on the house like weather. The carriage stood in the yard, unclaimed. His coat hung on its peg. The kettle sat cold on the range because there had been no one to fill it and she had not realized, until the silence of that unfilled kettle, how thoroughly she had arranged her days around another person's hands. Twenty-two years she had lived in this house, and in all of it she had never once lit the stove herself.

Pell's men had come at seven, polite as curates, with a paper that said *detained for questioning regarding enumeration irregularities*, and Marguerite had read it standing in her nightclothes at the front door while the frost breathed off the lawn behind her.

"Where?" she had asked.

"County offices, ma'am."

"And under whose authority?"

"Mr. Pell's, ma'am. Home Office business."

She had folded the paper along its crease, once, precisely, the way she folded arguments she intended to use later. "Tell Mr. Pell," she said, "that I will be calling on him before lunch."

She had not told him before lunch. She had made him wait until four o'clock, because she understood — as she had understood all her life, watching men conduct the world's business — that power is largely a matter of making other people wait. She had spent the interval doing three things. She had written a letter to Miss Okafor describing the detention in full, dated and signed, and left it with her housekeeper with instructions to post it if Marguerite did not return by evening. She had telephoned — no, she had sent notes, telephones being still, in her circle, an affectation — to three women of the movement, asking them to meet her at the county offices at four o'clock sharp. And she had stood for a long time before the hall mirror, practicing.

Not the speech. She never practiced speeches; words came to her the way breath came, unbidden and reliable. She practiced the thing underneath the speech. She practiced saying, out loud, in her own empty hall, the sentence she had avoided for thirty years:

*"My name is Marguerite Ashcombe."*

It sounded absurd, spoken alone. Of course it was her name. It was printed beneath every photograph of her, attached to every resolution she had drafted, carved — she had seen it once, secretly, at midnight, touching it like a wound — into the plinth of the memorial fountain subscribed by two thousand women after Edmund died. Her name was among the most recognizable in England. What she had never done was *use* it — deploy it openly, spend it, put it between another person and harm the way a body goes between a dog and a child. For decades she had worked through others: the anonymous pamphlets funded quietly, the arrests paid off through solicitors, the dangerous meetings attended "by chance" and addressed "as a friend of the movement." Invisibility, she had told herself, was strategy. A woman visible is a woman targeted; a woman targeted can be destroyed; a destroyed woman helps no one.

But she had known, even then, in the place where she kept the truths she did not look at directly — the same drawer, metaphorically speaking, that held Edmund's papers — that invisibility was also fear. That she had hidden behind her husband's martyrdom the way August hid behind his livery, each of them the other's excuse, and the myth had grown around them both like ivy on a ruined wall, beautiful, concealing, and holding nothing up.

Mrs. Fenwick had died eleven days ago. Marguerite had watched Pell at the graveside, counting mourners behind his glove, and had understood with terrible clarity that the man was conducting an arithmetic of mortality: so many witnesses remaining, so many years remaining, so much time to finish rewriting the dead before the living ran out. He thought he was patient. He thought the century itself was on his side, that the new year would arrive carrying his version of events in its luggage, stamped and official, and that history — that great clerk — would file his lies under truth and lose the key.

He had miscalculated one thing. He had assumed Marguerite loved her reputation more than she loved the truth.

He had assumed, in other words, that she was Edmund's widow.

* * *

The county offices at four o'clock in the afternoon were a cathedral of institutional indifference: green-painted walls, gas brackets turned low, clerks moving between desks with the shuffling gait of men who have surrendered their ambitions one form at a time. Into this came Marguerite Ashcombe, followed by Miss Hartree, Miss Delacourt, and old Mrs. Pym, who was eighty-one and had once been force-fed in Winson Green prison and regarded all government buildings since as personal property she was entitled to enter whenever she pleased.

The clerk at the inquiry desk looked up, recognized her — everyone recognized her; her face had been on more newspapers than the Queen — and rose halfway out of his chair as if pulled by strings.

"Madam — Mrs. Ashcombe — is there —"

"There is." She set her gloved hands on the counter. "You are detaining a man named August Villalobos in this building. I have come to collect him."

The clerk's eyes flicked sideways, the way eyes do when authority is present but embarrassed. Pell appeared from the corridor within the minute — Marguerite gave him credit for speed — buttoned and immaculate, his face arranged into an expression of courteous regret that she suspected he practiced in his lodgings.

"Mrs. Ashcombe." A small bow. "This is unexpected."

"Is it?" She did not lower her voice. She noticed, with a strange detachment, that she had stopped lowering her voice. All her life she had modulated herself downward in public spaces, a woman's trick, a survival's trick, making herself pleasant-sized so the world would not feel threatened enough to strike. Today her voice filled the inquiry hall the way organ music fills a church, and heads turned down every row of desks, and she let them turn. "I should have thought it the most expected event of your year, Mr. Pell. You took my driver. Naturally I have come for him."

"The gentleman is assisting us with certain questions arising from the census —"

"The census was conducted in April, Mr. Pell. It is now December. Your irregularity has taken eight months to walk from your office to my door, which suggests either remarkable diligence or remarkable invention." She removed one glove, finger by finger. Around them the hall had gone quiet in the particular way government halls go quiet: not silent, but performing silence, every clerk suddenly absorbed in papers he was not reading, every ear angled like a flower toward sun. "I will ask you plainly, and I would like the ladies present to hear your answer. Under what statute is Mr. Villalobos detained?"

Pell's composure held, but she saw the cost of it in the small muscles around his eyes. "There are matters of historical review —"

"Historical review." She tasted the phrase and found it rotten. "That is the third time you have used that expression to me, and I have now had it translated. It means, does it not, that you intend to determine what may be remembered and by whom. It means you have appointed yourself the censor of the past." She turned deliberately — a full turn, unhurried, letting the whole hall see her face — and said, in a voice pitched to reach the farthest desk: "My name is Marguerite Ashcombe. My husband was Edmund Ashcombe. And I am telling every person in this room that any questioning of my household conducted without my knowledge or consent is being done against my expressed wishes, and that I will pursue the matter through every court, every newspaper, and every Member of Parliament who owes me a favor — and there are forty-three of those, gentlemen, I have kept count — until someone explains to me why the Home Office is interrogating servants about a census form."

Silence now, real silence. Somewhere a pen rolled off a desk and no one picked it up.

"You understand," Pell said quietly, and here his voice dropped into something almost gentle, almost pitying, meant for her alone though the hall strained to catch it, "that there are things which, spoken publicly, cannot be unsaid. You have lived thirty years under a certain account of your husband. I would hate to see you dismantle it yourself."

And there it was — the offer inside the threat, the comfortable lie extended one last time, wrapped in courtesy. *Die as the widow of a martyr.* She heard it exactly as he meant it, and she thought of Edmund's letters in the locked drawer, the neat handwriting selling women's names to men like Pell, and she felt something in her chest that was not grief anymore and had not been grief for some time. It was closer to the feeling of a splint coming off a broken bone — agony and relief in the same instant, the limb remembering it was free.

"Mr. Pell," she said, "you mistake me entirely, and I am going to enjoy correcting you. You believe the myth protects me. You believe I am its prisoner or its beneficiary. But I have read my husband's papers. I know what he was. I have known since October, and I have said nothing, not because I feared the truth but because I feared what saying it would cost the women who bled for this movement believing in him." She stepped closer. "You have been trying to frighten me with exposure. Sir, I have been exposing myself for weeks. You are simply too slow to have noticed. Now —" she turned to the clerk, who flinched — "I will have Mr. Villalobos released within the quarter hour, or I will have the Home Secretary's private secretary on that telephone before five o'clock, and I promise you the conversation will be shorter than this one."

* * *

August heard all of it from the holding room, through a door that someone had neglected to shut properly — or perhaps, given the quality of the listening-silence in the corridor, had been instructed to leave ajar. He sat very still on the bench with his hands flat on his knees and listened to a voice he had protected for twenty-two years protect him instead, and he found that his eyes were wet, and that he did not particularly care who saw.

It was not the words. Well — it was partly the words; forty-three Members of Parliament, indeed; she had invented that figure on the spot, he would have bet his watch, and it was glorious. But it was the sound underneath the words: a woman setting down a weight she had carried so long its shape was printed into her shoulders, and straightening, and discovering she was taller than she had believed. He had spent his whole employment imagining scenarios in which he might save her. It had never once occurred to him that she might come for him, boots ringing on government flagstones, spending her name like gold she'd finally decided was hers.

When the door opened, it opened fully, and Pell stood aside, and Marguerite came in.

For a moment neither spoke. There were things to say — a great backlog of them, months deep, the whole drowned continent of their unsaid history lying between them — but the holding room was not the place, and neither of them was fool enough to begin it there.

Instead she looked him over, briskly, the way she looked over a horse after a hard drive: face, hands, posture. His collar stud was gone. His knuckles were fine; they hadn't touched him; she noted that and something in her jaw eased a fraction.

"They've kept your effects at the desk," she said. "We'll collect them. The carriage is —" she stopped, caught herself, and August saw the corner of her mouth move — "the carriage is outside. I drove it myself. Badly. You'll have opinions, and you may keep them until we're home."

"Yes, madam," August said.

"Don't call me madam." She said it quietly, and it was not a rebuke; it was a door opening. "Not today."

They walked out through the inquiry hall together, past the rows of clerks who had heard everything, past Pell standing by the counter with his hat in his hands wearing the expression of a man who has just realized the ledger he has been auditing has been auditing him back. Marguerite did not look at him. August did. And what passed between the two men in that glance was brief and complete: *I know what you know, you know what I know, and none of it is yours anymore.*

Outside, the winter dusk had come down blue and early over the town, and the lamps were being lit one by one along the square, and three elderly women fell into step beside the carriage as naturally as an honor guard. Miss Hartree squeezed August's arm as he handed her up. "She talked the paint off the walls," Mrs. Pym confided, settling onto the seat. "I haven't enjoyed myself this much since Holloway."

August took the reins. Marguerite sat beside him on the box — not inside, beside him, in the open cold where anyone in the square could see them, and she did not seem to care, or rather she seemed to care precisely the right amount, which was to say she cared and chose anyway. The horse stepped out. The town slid past, lamplit, curious.

Neither spoke for the first mile. Then, watching the road, Marguerite said: "Tomorrow evening. Eight o'clock. The house will be empty except for us and Miss Okafor, and three women who deserve to hear it before anyone else does. I'm going to read the true draft aloud — all of it, Edmund included, all of it." Her breath fogged and tore away in the wind. "I'd like you there. Not driving. Sitting. In a chair, indoors, like a person."

August's hands were steady on the reins. His heart was not.

"And August," she added, as the gates of the town fell behind them and the dark road opened ahead toward home, "tomorrow, when I read the part about the night we burned his letter — I'm going to read it as it happened. Both our names. On the record."

He drove on into the dark, toward the house where the mantel clock waited, counting down the last hours of a century and of every secret either of them had ever kept.
