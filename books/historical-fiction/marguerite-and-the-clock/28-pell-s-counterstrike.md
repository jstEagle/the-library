## 28. Pell's Counterstrike

The summons came on a Tuesday, in an envelope that smelled of government.

Marguerite knew it before she broke the wax — not the seal itself, which was plain and unremarkable, but the paper beneath it, that particular grey stock used only by departments whose names were never printed on their letterheads. Twelve years of reading other people's correspondence had taught her the grammar of official menace: the absence of ornament, the precision of the fold, the way such letters always arrived early in the week, as if to ruin the maximum number of days at once.

She read it standing in the hall, still wearing her coat, while November rain wrote its long grey paragraphs against the windows.

*Mrs. Marguerite Ashcombe* — not *Lady*, she noticed; they had stripped even the courtesy from her name — was requested to attend an interview at the offices of the Census Review Board, Room —, Whitehall Annex, on Thursday the twenty-first, at eleven o'clock precisely, regarding *certain irregularities of record pertaining to the household enumeration of 1887.* She was advised that failure to appear might be construed under Section Fourteen of the Registration Act as *contumacious refusal to assist His Majesty's officers in their lawful inquiries.*

Contumacious. She turned the word over like a stone found in a loaf of bread. Somewhere in a dusty statute, some clerk had dusted it off for exactly this purpose — a word so old it had forgotten what it meant, kept alive for the sole pleasure of being aimed at inconvenient women.

She carried the letter through to the morning room, where August stood at the sideboard with the coffee pot, and set it down beside his elbow without a word.

He read it the way he read everything — twice, quickly, then slowly — and she watched his face do the thing it did when he was translating danger into weather. A cloud passing behind the eyes. Nothing more.

"Thursday," he said.

"Thursday."

"They'll want you alone."

"I imagine so."

"Then we should decide now," August said, setting down the pot with a care that was almost ceremonial, "what you are going to say when they ask you what you know."

"And what is that?"

"That's rather the question, ma'am."

Outside, the rain intensified, drumming on the glass like small insistent fingers. Marguerite looked at the letter again, at the word *irregularities*, and felt something in her chest that was not fear. She had expected fear. What came instead was closer to relief — the strange lightness of a woman who has already decided to burn her house down and finds the match already in her hand.

"Tell Okafor," she said. "Everything. Today. If they're going to summon me, let them summon a woman who has nothing left to hide."

* * *

The Whitehall Annex occupied a narrow frontage between two grander buildings, the architectural equivalent of a clerk who has been given a desk in the corridor. The porter who admitted her took her umbrella without meeting her eyes, and the stairs up to Room — were lit by gas jets that guttered in a draught from somewhere below, throwing her shadow ahead of her up the walls, elongating it, giving her a height she had never possessed.

Pell received her alone, in a room containing one desk, two chairs, a filing cabinet, and a map of England pinned to the wall with small brass pins, several of which clustered about a certain district of London with the density of a rash.

"Mrs. Ashcombe." He rose — he had manners, she granted him that; they sat on him like clothes cut for another man — and indicated the chair opposite. "Thank you for coming."

"You left me very little choice. I'm told refusal would be contumacious."

"It's an old word." He settled back into his chair, lacing his fingers over the file that lay closed upon the desk — his file, hers, the census page she had never seen but could picture now with terrible clarity. "Old words are useful. They frighten people who don't know them, which is most people, and bore people who do, which is everyone else."

"Is that why you've come out from behind your census forms, Mr. Pell? To frighten or to bore?"

"To negotiate." He opened the file. Inside, she glimpsed papers — her own handwriting among them, a page torn from her notebook, and photographs she could not immediately identify. "But first, the formalities. You'll forgive me. I find the formalities do half my work for me."

He read aloud, in the flat voice of a man reciting train times: the Registration Act, Section Fourteen. The Public Records Act, with its provisions respecting the falsification of historical documents. An Act of 1871 concerning seditious publication, amended 1884, obscure enough that even its own authors had likely lost track of it. And finally — laid down last, deliberately, like the winning card of a patience game — the Official Secrets legislation, under which the disclosure of material relating to named individuals in the service of the Crown could constitute an offence carrying a penalty of two years' imprisonment.

"Named individuals," Marguerite repeated.

"The men Mr. Edmund Ashcombe corresponded with." Pell's eyes came up and held hers, and there was no cruelty in them, which was somehow worse than cruelty. There was only diligence. He was a man doing his work thoroughly, the way a good groom curries a horse or a good hangman tests the rope. "Twelve years dead, but the Crown's obligations to its servants do not expire at death. Nor do the protections owed to them. Their names, their assignments, their payments — all of it remains sealed. Publication would be an offence. Assisting publication would be an offence. Possession, in certain readings, might even be made to stretch."

"In certain readings."

"The law is a generous document, Mrs. Ashcombe. It can be made to fit almost anyone who gives us cause."

She sat quite still. Through the window behind him she could see the roofline of Whitehall, the wet slate gleaming, a flag somewhere snapping in the wind. It struck her, with a clarity that nearly undid her composure, that this room had been chosen for that window — that every word spoken here would be spoken beneath the shadow of the state itself, the way confessions were once heard beneath the crucifix. They wanted her to feel small. They had arranged the furniture accordingly.

"And if I publish regardless?"

"Then you will be prosecuted. Quietly. Not for sedition — the papers wouldn't wear a martyred suffragist, even now — but for something smaller. Falsification of records. Obstruction. Perhaps a charge relating to the census irregularities of 1887, which I confess I have never entirely understood myself, but which our legal gentlemen assure me have legs." He closed the file. "You would be convicted. The sentence would be modest — fines, perhaps a short remand, bound over to keep the peace. But your name would enter the record as a woman convicted of forgery and obstruction. And Miss Okafor's centenary history would open, forever after, with a footnote explaining that its principal source was a convicted criminal."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a door slammed; the gas jets fluttered.

"And Mrs. Fenwick?" Marguerite asked. "Would she be prosecuted too? She's seventy-nine years old, Mr. Pell. Her hands shake so badly she cannot thread a needle. Will you send the Registration Act after her?"

Something moved behind Pell's face — quickly, professionally suppressed, but not quickly enough. She saw it and stored it away, the way August must have done all those years with other people's letters: the flicker that betrays the man beneath the office.

"Mrs. Fenwick is not my concern," he said.

"No. Only the dead are your concern. And the living, insofar as they insist on disturbing the dead."

"Madam—"

"Let us come to it, Mr. Pell. You did not summon a widow across London to recite statutes at her. Statutes are your opening bid, not your offer. What do you want?"

For a long moment he regarded her, and she had the sense — absurd, unwelcome, persistent — of a man looking at his own reflection across a great distance and not liking what he saw. Then he drew from the drawer a second folder, thinner, and placed it on the desk between them without opening it.

"Here is what happens next," he said. "In the spring, the centenary celebrations begin. There will be a memorial service. There will be speeches. There will be a plaque, and a history, and schoolchildren taught a clean and useful story about a brave man who died for a noble cause. That story does a great deal of work, Mrs. Ashcombe. It holds together forty organisations and ten thousand members and a parliamentary bloc that will deliver the franchise within five years if it is permitted to remain coherent. Your husband's myth is load-bearing. Remove it, and the whole edifice comes down on the heads of the women still inside it."

"You sound like a man who believes what he says."

"I am a man who has read the correspondence," Pell said, "which is more than most believers can claim."

The admission hung in the air between them, unexpected, almost intimate. He had read Edmund's letters. Of course he had. He had probably read them before August ever steamed open an envelope; he had been reading them since 1887, patient as damp.

"And the offer?"

"The offer is this." He tapped the thin folder once. "You withdraw. Not publicly — nothing so crude. You simply decline to cooperate with Miss Okafor. The true memoir is destroyed — I will accept your sworn assurance, witnessed, that no copy exists. The testimony of your driver is withdrawn. In return, the irregularities of 1887 are corrected administratively. No prosecution. No conviction. No footnote. And in the spring, at the memorial service, the widow of Edmund Ashcombe sits on the platform beside the leaders of the movement, honoured, protected, unimpeachable — the keeper of a martyr's flame. Every history written hereafter will treat you with reverence. Your name will be safe. Your house will be safe. The women you have spent your life fighting for will get their vote, on schedule, because the story that delivers it remains intact."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you die as something else." He said it gently, which was the obscenity of it. "A convicted woman. A disgraced one. The widow who betrayed the martyr. Whatever we choose to make you, and we will choose, because we hold the statutes and you hold only paper. Paper burns, Mrs. Ashcombe. Statutes don't."

She looked at him for a long time. Outside, the flag snapped; the gas hissed; somewhere a typewriter clattered its small iron prayer.

"You are offering me," she said at last, "the chance to die comfortably."

"I am offering you—"

"A comfortable lie. Dressed as mercy." She leaned forward. "Mr. Pell, I buried my husband believing him a martyr. I spent twelve years kneeling at a shrine built on his betrayal. Do you know what that costs a woman? Not the grief — grief is honest, grief is cheap. The cost is the shrine. Every speech I gave honouring him, every subscription raised in his name, every young woman who joined us because of his example — I sold them all a counterfeit coin and called it inheritance. I have already died as the widow of a martyr. I did it for twelve years, and it was not comfort. It was a slow suffocation in a warm room." She rose. Her legs held. She noted this with distant surprise, as though observing someone else. "You may keep your exit. I have already walked through the far door."

Pell remained seated. When he spoke, his voice had lost its recitation-flatness; something rawer moved beneath it, like water under ice.

"You think this is about you," he said. "It isn't. It's about the machine. The machine needs the myth. I need the machine. We are both smaller than what we serve." He gathered his files, squared their edges with a precision that seemed compulsive. "I will give you until the first of January. After that, the process begins, and I will not be able to slow it, whatever I might—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. "After that, the process begins."

"Whatever you might privately wish," Marguerite finished for him.

He did not deny it. That was the strangest part, she thought, descending the dark stairs past the guttering jets — he had not denied it. There was a man inside the office, and the man was tired, and the man had looked at her across the desk with something that was not hatred and might, in a different life, in different clothes, have been kinship.

At the door, the porter returned her umbrella without meeting her eyes. Some things, at least, were consistent.

* * *

August was waiting with the car outside, collar up against the rain, and he read the verdict in her face before she reached him — she saw him read it, saw the cloud pass behind his eyes and the weather turn.

"Well?" he said, opening the door.

"He offered me the martyr's widow. Platform seats, a plaque, immunity, the vote delivered on schedule." She got in. The car smelled of leather and petrol and the cold. "And prosecution under four statutes I'd never heard of if I refuse. Forgery, obstruction, sedition, secrets. He has a folder, August. He has pages of my handwriting."

"Okafor's proofs?"

"Perhaps. Or copies. Or nothing — perhaps the folder is empty and the threat is the contents." She pulled her gloves tighter, finger by finger. "That's the artistry of it. I can't know. So I must assume the worst, and assuming the worst is its own imprisonment."

August pulled the car out into the traffic, and for a long while neither spoke. The city slid past, wet and glittering, lamplighters working the early dusk, shopgirls hurrying home under shared umbrellas, a newspaper boy shouting something about the Transvaal. Life going on, indifferent to statutes.

"He gave you until the first of January," August said at last. It was not a question.

"How did you—"

"Because that's how I'd do it." He glanced at her. "Deadlines before holidays. Men remember dates better than arguments. And a deadline on New Year's Day lets him spend Christmas watching you waver."

"Waver." She almost laughed. "Does he imagine I'm wavering?"

"I imagine he hopes it. Hope is cheaper than force." August's hands were steady on the wheel, but she knew those hands; she had watched them steam envelopes and burn letters and copy credentials, and she knew that steadiness was a discipline, not a temperament. "Ma'am — Marguerite. If they prosecute, they'll prosecute him too. Your driver. The man who read the letters and copied the file and broke into the lodgings. They'll make him the criminal and you the dupe. It's cleaner for them. The widow misled by a servant — that story wears better than a widow who chose."

"Then we must ensure the truth wears best of all."

"With what? Paper? He told you — paper burns."

"Yes." Marguerite looked out at the rain-blurred streets, at the crowds flowing along the pavements like a river that could not be arrested or prosecuted or summoned to Whitehall rooms. "But paper published is no longer paper. It's stone. Once it's in ten thousand hands, burning it burns the hands too. That's the weakness in his machine, August. It can threaten a woman. It cannot threaten a public."

August was quiet for three streets. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lower, rougher, the voice she had heard only once before, the night he had confessed twelve years of silences to her in this same seat.

"There are things in my testimony," he said, "that touch the sealed material. Names. Dates. What I read, what I burned, what I passed on untouched. If I publish, they won't come for you first. They'll come for me. Two years under the Secrets Act, he said. He'll want more for a servant who spied on the state's correspondence, however accidentally."

"I know."

"And you're prepared for that."

She turned and looked at him fully, this man who had spent his whole adult life making himself invisible for her sake, and she found that her throat was tight and her voice, when it came, was steady.

"August. For twelve years you protected me with silence, and it nearly killed us both. If you walk into a courtroom for me now — publicly, on the record, under your own name — it will be the first protection you've ever given me that hasn't cost us the truth. I am not merely prepared. I am—" She stopped. Found the word. "I am proud of you in advance. Which is a foolish thing to say, but I find I have no patience left for prudent things."

The car turned onto the Embankment. The river ran black and heavy beside them, carrying the city's reflections out toward the sea.

"Then there's one more thing," August said. "If the deadline is the first of January — if the process begins the moment it passes — then we don't wait for Okafor's book. We don't wait for the centenary. The moment we know the threat is real, the testimony goes out. Not to historians. To newspapers. All of them. Every editor in Fleet Street, every survivor on Fenwick's list, every member of every organisation he's trying to protect from itself."

"When?"

"Now," said August. "Tonight."

The rain had begun again, fine and silver in the headlamps. Ahead, Big Ben loomed out of the murk, its clock faces glowing pale as moons, marking out the shrinking days.

"Then drive home quickly," Marguerite said. "We have a letter to write."
