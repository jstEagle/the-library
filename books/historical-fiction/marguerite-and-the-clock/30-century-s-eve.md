## 30. Century's Eve

The printing house on Caxton Lane smelled of ink and hot metal and the particular sweat of men who had been told they would not be going home. August stood in the doorway at half past nine in the evening and counted them: six compositors bent over their cases like priests over altars, two pressmen oiling the great machine that filled the back of the room like a sleeping animal, and Mr. Aldous the printer himself, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms black to the shoulder, moving between stations with the calm of a man who had been paid well enough not to ask questions and was determined to enjoy the not-asking.

"You'll want to see this," Aldous said, and led him past the stone where a compositor was setting type by hand, letter by letter, upside down and backward, so that what read as chaos from above would come out of the press as sense.

August looked. The page was Marguerite's own words — *I, Marguerite Ashcombe, being of sound mind and unsound reputation, do set down the following as the truth* — and seeing it in metal, in the hard permanent grammar of lead, did something to his chest that he had not prepared for. Twelve years he had read other people's secrets in private, held them in his memory the way a bank holds gold, letting nothing out. Now the secret was becoming public property, letter by letter, and there was no vault in England large enough to take it back.

"How many copies?" he asked.

"Two thousand for the first pull. We can run more if the paper holds." Aldous wiped his hands on an apron already beyond saving. "Your lady's testimony and yours both go in the same signature?"

"The same. Nothing separated." He had insisted on that. If the thing was to be done, it would be done whole — her name and his bound into the same gathering, so that no one could later say she had been misled by her servant, or that he had acted alone. Whatever came, they would meet it as one document.

Aldous nodded and moved off, and August took up his post by the window.

The window was why he had come early. Caxton Lane was a narrow throat of a street, one lamp at each end and darkness pooling between, and from the first-floor room he could see both mouths of it. Pell's men would come, if they came, from the Whitehall end. They had been circling since noon — August had made three passes himself through the streets around the house that afternoon, wearing his driver's coat, which no one ever looked at twice, and had marked a man loitering opposite the stationer's with the particular stillness of someone being paid to wait. Not Pell himself. One of the quiet ones, the ones who took notes.

At ten o'clock the compositor finished the last page of the memoir and the proofreader — a sharp-eyed woman named Grieve whom Aldous swore by — read it aloud in a low voice, stumbling only once, at the passage where Edmund's name was given plainly to what he had done. She read it again. She said nothing. She initialed the galley and carried it to the stone.

August watched the letters go in backward: *betrayer*, then *informer*, then *Edmund Ashcombe sold us*. The movement's founding sentence, set in reverse.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. All day he had expected to shake.

* * *

Marguerite, at home, could not sit still, and so did the one thing she knew how to do with a body that refused rest: she dressed for work.

Not the grey wool of the platform. Tonight she put on the black silk she had worn to Edmund's funeral, because it seemed right that the dress should attend both occasions — the burial of the lie and the birth of the truth — and because the weight of it against her shoulders felt like armor that had finally admitted what it was for. She pinned her hair herself. She had dismissed the maids at seven; the house was hers alone tonight except for the ghosts, and the ghosts had never once offered to help.

On the desk lay the final page. Okafor had left at eight, taking the cab to Caxton Lane with the corrected proofs under her coat, after standing in the study doorway a moment longer than necessary.

"They will call you a traitor to your husband," Okafor had said. "Some of them will mean it kindly."

"I have been a loyal wife for twelve years," Marguerite had answered. "I should like to try something else."

Now she sat before the page and read it through one last time, though she knew every line, though she had read it aloud to an empty room at midnight two nights past until the words stopped being wounds and became simply facts. *My husband informed upon us. I learned it too late, and I kept it, and my keeping of it was its own kind of betrayal.* There it was, the sentence she had fought hardest to leave in — Okafor had wanted to soften it, Fenwick's ghost had wanted to soften it, every instinct of a lifetime's diplomacy had wanted to soften it — and there it stayed, because a record that forgave the writer in advance was not a record but a eulogy.

She thought of Mrs. Fenwick, eleven days dead, whose testimony lived now only in this notebook and in these printed pages. *Witnesses are mortal,* Pell had understood, counting mourners at the graveside with those pale administrative eyes. *Paper is not.* Very well. Then let the paper speak tonight, while the century that had made all these silences possible still drew breath.

The clock on the mantel — the clock, Edmund's clock, the one object in the house she had neither sold nor hidden nor smashed — showed twenty minutes to eleven. Outside, London was making ready for the new year in the ordinary ways: fireworks being carted toward the bridges, church bells being tested, newspapers running their retrospectives of the century's glories. Somewhere in those retrospectives, she knew, Edmund Ashcombe appeared as a martyr of the cause, shot down by a government he had defied. By morning the sentence would be different in every paper that mattered, and she would be the woman who killed her own myth, and there was no version of tomorrow in which she was not, in some essential way, alone.

Except.

She heard the carriage house door, and then his step on the gravel, and then — because he always came to the study window rather than the front door, a habit so old it had become a kind of grammar — the soft knock at the glass.

She let him in. Snow had begun, fine and dry, the kind that wouldn't lie. It was in his hair. Twelve years she had watched snow land in that hair from behind a horse's back, from across a platform, from the far side of every silence they had built together, and only in the last month had she permitted herself to look at him directly, frontally, the way one looks at a person rather than a fixture.

"Caxton Lane is running," he said. "Grieve has cleared the last signature. Two thousand by dawn if we want them."

"We want them."

"Pell's man is still at the stationer's corner. A second one came at nine. They're not trying to stop the press — Aldous has a magistrate's cousin among his investors, and Pell knows a raid tonight would make the papers before the ink dried. They're waiting to see who carries the bundles out."

"Then we carry them ourselves," Marguerite said. "In the mourning coach. Let them write that down."

Something moved through his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a less disciplined man. "I had hoped you'd say that."

"August." She stopped him with his hand still on the door latch. "Before we go. There is a thing I want to say in this house, tonight, while it is still the old century, because after midnight everything will belong to everyone and this will belong only to us."

He turned. Waited. He had always been good at waiting; it was the trade he'd plied on her for twelve years without her knowing its name.

"I am not sorry," she said. "For any of it. For asking you to burn the letter. For letting you carry what I couldn't. I have tried to be sorry and I find that I am not, and I think a woman who publishes a book about honesty ought to begin by admitting that." Her voice held. She made it hold. "You were wrong to keep things from me, and I was wrong to ask, and I would do every part of it again, and that is the truest sentence I know how to make."

He stood very still in the lamplight, snow melting on his shoulders, and when he spoke his voice was rougher than she had ever heard it.

"And I would read every letter again," he said. "And burn every one you asked me to burn. And I'm not sorry either. So we're agreed, then — two unrepentant people, going out into the snow to end a century."

"Yes," said Marguerite. "Agreed. At last, something in writing."

* * *

The mourning coach went through London at a quarter past eleven with its blinds drawn, as befitted a vehicle carrying grief, and inside it carried instead two thousand sheets of the truth in brown paper parcels, stacked to the ceiling, with Marguerite and August sitting among them like passengers in a hold full of dynamite.

They saw Pell's men fall in behind at Charing Cross. August, watching through the blind's gap, counted three now — the stationer's loiterer, a second on foot, and a hansom keeping pace at the distance of a man who wished to be seen keeping pace. Not stopping them. Recording them. Building, even now, even tonight, the file that would be laid before some magistrate or minister in the new year: *the widow Ashcombe, observed distributing seditious material at the turn of the century, in company with her servant, one A. Villalobos.*

Let him build it, August thought. Paper cut both ways. His own sworn testimony was in those parcels, signed and witnessed, and every fact Pell might use had already been published by its owner. You cannot blackmail a man who has handed out copies of his crimes to two thousand strangers. It was, he reflected, the single most liberating arithmetic he had ever performed.

"They're following," Marguerite said. Not a question.

"Three of them. Pell will be at the lane itself, I'd guess. He likes to close the circle personally."

"Good." She smoothed the black silk over her knees. "I have something to say to him too."

The streets were filling as they rolled eastward — crowds drifting toward the river for the midnight illuminations, young people with paper horns, a church choir rehearsing on a corner with their breath standing round them like incense. The old century was being sung out in every key at once, and none of the singers knew that its last official act was riding through their midst in a coach built for funerals.

At Caxton Lane, August handed her down into the snow. The lamp at the near mouth of the street threw their shadows long and doubled, and there, exactly where August had guessed, beneath the far lamp, stood Mr. Pell.

He was hatless despite the cold, a thin man growing thinner, his collar dark with damp, holding a notebook he made no move to open. Up close, in the lamplight, he looked less like an instrument of the Home Office than like a clerk at the end of a very long shift — which, August supposed, was what he had been for thirty years, and would be tomorrow, whatever happened tonight.

"Mrs. Ashcombe." Pell inclined his head. "Mr. Villalobos. I wondered if you'd come yourselves. Most in your position would have sent men."

"My position," Marguerite said, "is about to be widely available. Two thousand copies, Mr. Pell, by morning. You may have one gratis — consider it a courtesy to a colleague in the records business."

"I could detain you both tonight," Pell said. "Obscure statutes. I've been reviewing them all week; there are more than you'd credit."

"You could," Marguerite agreed pleasantly. "And by the time the paperwork cleared, the book would be in every reading room in London, and your detention would be chapter thirty-one. I have learned a great deal this month about how stories get written, Mr. Pell. I intend to write mine first."

Pell looked at her for a long moment. Something passed behind his eyes — not defeat, not yet; defeat would come later, on another morning, in another room. But something adjacent to respect, or perhaps to recognition: the look of a man watching someone else pay a price he had once refused to pay.

"The warrant arrives at nine," he said quietly. "I won't lie to you about that. Whatever happens at midnight, the machinery is already moving. Names were flagged. Files exist."

"Files exist," Marguerite said. "Yes. I know precisely how that feels."

Then she walked past him, up the lane, into the printing house, and August followed, and Pell did not follow them, and did not write anything down, and stood in the falling snow until the lamp above him hissed and guttered.

* * *

Inside, the press was already turning.

It was louder than August had imagined — a rhythmic iron respiration, sheet after sheet pulled glowing-wet from the plates, hung to dry along lines strung the length of the room like laundry in some enormous, industrious cave. The compositors worked on at the next forms; Aldous moved through the steam checking margins; Grieve read finished sheets against copy with her lips moving. Nobody cheered. Nobody paused. It was, August thought, the most honest celebration he had ever witnessed: work, simply work, the truth being manufactured the way bread is, by tired skilled people who would be paid and would go home and tell their grandchildren.

Marguerite walked the length of the drying lines slowly, reading her own sentences as they cooled, touching none of them. August watched her stop at one sheet — he could not see which passage — and stand before it with her head bowed, the black silk drinking the lamplight, for the length of ten pages rolling off the press.

At five minutes to midnight, Aldous brought the last form to the stone: the colophon page, the one bearing the imprint and the date, and beneath it a line August had drafted and Marguerite had approved and Okafor had argued for and won —

*Set and printed in the last hours of the nineteenth century, that the record might enter the new one untainted by its predecessor's silences.*

"There's a custom," Aldous said, wiping his hands yet again, though they could not have been clean for hours. "When a job matters. The principal signs the stone copy. Ink and pen are here." He produced both as if producing witnesses.

Marguerite took the pen. Her hand, August saw, was perfectly steady — steadier, he suspected, than it had been at any point in the twelve years he had served her, because a hand shakes when it has something left to lose, and she had spent this month laying every loss she owned out on the table and naming it.

She signed. *Marguerite Ashcombe.* The letters went down firm and unhurried, and the compositor cut a small block from the signature for the facsimile plate, and somewhere in the machine room the press breathed on.

"Mr. Villalobos," Aldous said. "Your testimony stands as a separate declaration, but the printer's custom allows—" He hesitated, suddenly shy, this man who had faced down censors and creditors. "Well. It allows anyone whose name is in the book to sign the stone."

August looked at the pen. In twelve years he had written perhaps forty things: delivery receipts, stable accounts, one careful anonymous note returning a lost brooch. His name existed in no ledger anywhere except wage books and census returns, and one census return annotated in red.

He took the pen and wrote *August Villalobos* beneath hers, and the two names sat together on the stone in the lamplight, unequal in fame and equal in ink, and he found that his hand was steady too.

Outside, the first church bell began.

It was St. Bride's, off Fleet Street, and the others took it up street by street — the old century dying in bronze, stroke after stroke, the sound coming through the printing house windows and mingling with the clatter of the press until it was impossible to say where the bells ended and the machine began. Marguerite turned to the window. Below, the lane was filling with people walking toward the river, faces tipped upward, strangers to one another and to her, and in a few hours the newsboys would be among them crying her name and her husband's shame in the same breath.

"Twelve years ago tonight," she said, not turning, "we drove home from a rally in the rain, and Edmund was alive, and I believed every word he had ever said to me, and you sat on the box knowing otherwise. Do you remember it?"

"I remember every minute of it," August said.

"So do I." She watched the crowd, the snow, the lamps. "And I would not go back and change it. Isn't that a terrible thing to discover at the death of a century? That even the lies were load-bearing?"

The bells reached their full chorus. The press ran on. And then, through the ringing, through the iron rhythm, August heard the other sound — the one he had been listening for all night without admitting it: wheels on the wet cobbles at the mouth of Caxton Lane, more than one carriage, moving slowly, stopping.

He crossed to the window. Four vehicles stood where two had been. Men were getting down from them, unhurried, buttoning their coats, and at their center, hatless still, snow gathering on his thin shoulders, stood Mr. Pell — not advancing, merely present, the way winter is present, the way a date on a calendar is present.

The bells began to fail, one church at a time, the sound thinning toward silence, and in the sudden hush that follows midnight everywhere in England, Marguerite turned from the window and looked at August, and the new century came in around them like water coming into a lock.

"It's done," she said. "Whatever they bring. It's done."

Below, Pell raised his eyes to the lit windows of the printing house, and did not smile, and did not look away.
