## 36. The Survivors' Supper

The dining room at Ashcombe House had not held this many people since Edmund's funeral.

August counted them from the doorway — a habit he could not break and no longer wished to — and found the number almost unbearable in its smallness. Seven women. Seven chairs pulled up to the long table where thirty had once sat in mourning black, where forty had once raised toasts to the martyr of Holloway. Seven was what remained of a movement that had filled halls in Manchester and Leeds, and August understood, watching them arrive out of the February dark, that each of those seven had performed her own private arithmetic to be here tonight. Each had weighed the myth against the truth and chosen, however uneasily, the truth.

Marguerite had insisted on doing the cooking herself. August had argued — gently, in the new language they were still learning, which was plainness — that a woman who had just burned down the founding legend of her own life ought not also to be standing over a stove. Marguerite had looked at him with an expression he was still cataloguing, one of the new expressions, unguarded ones, and said, "I have spent forty years being served by other people's hands, August. Let me feed them myself for once. It will do something to me that I need done."

So she cooked. He carried. And now the soup was on the table, a plain leek soup, and seven women who had not shared a room since before the war of words began were sitting in the lamplight trying to remember how to look at one another.

Miss Okafor had come from London on the afternoon train, notebook in her bag but pointedly not on the table. She had asked permission first — "May I come as a guest, not a historian?" — and Marguerite had said yes so quickly that August knew she had been afraid of the opposite question.

* * *

The silence lasted through the soup.

It was not a hostile silence. That would have been simpler. It was the silence of people who had spent decades speaking to each other only through the medium of a dead man — through tributes, through anniversary speeches, through the careful liturgy of Edmund Ashcombe's martyrdom — and who now discovered they had nothing else in common, or feared they did. They had been a congregation. Congregations are easy. What was required tonight was something harder: to be, once again, merely women who had known each other young and had grown old along separate channels.

It was Miss Hartley — thin, upright, sixty-eight years old, one of the two who had stayed when Marguerite read the true draft aloud in January — who broke it, in the manner of a woman pulling a splinter quickly rather than slowly.

"I keep waiting for someone to propose a toast," she said. "Forty years, and my hand keeps moving toward the glass on its own. Isn't that obscene?"

A nervous ripple went around the table. Mrs. Calloway, the other one who had stayed, laughed — a short, startled laugh that seemed to surprise even herself.

"Propose one," said Marguerite. She was sitting at the head of the table, which was where she had always sat, though she had considered, seriously, sitting in the middle among them instead. Some geometries take longer to dismantle than others. "Whatever your hand wants to say, say it."

Miss Hartley lifted her glass and then, holding it, found she could not finish. Her face worked. "To — " she began, and stopped. To Edmund, the sentence wanted to be. To the martyr. To the man whose photograph had hung in every committee room for twelve years, the man whose name had sanctified every arrest and consoled every widow of the cause. She set the glass down untasted. "No," she said quietly. "I can't. I don't know yet what I *can* drink to."

"Drink to the fact that we're finding out," said Mrs. Calloway. "That's more than we had in December."

They drank to that. The glasses made a sound like small uncertain bells.

* * *

August served the fish and withdrew to the sideboard, which was where he had stood at a hundred dinners, invisible by profession. But invisibility had changed its nature in recent months, and he felt the difference acutely tonight. These women knew his name now. They knew what he had written — the testimony published on New Year's Eve, the sworn account of letters read and letters burned, printed in eleven newspapers under the headline THE DRIVER WHO READ. One of them, a stout woman named Mrs. Prentice whom he did not remember ever meeting before tonight, caught his eye across the room and inclined her head, very slightly, the way one acknowledges a fellow veteran of some unnamed campaign.

He inclined his back. It cost him more than anyone at the table could have known.

"You may sit, August," Marguerite said. Not loudly. But the whole table turned.

"There is a chair," she went on, gesturing to the eighth place, which had been laid and which everyone had noticed and no one had mentioned. "There has always been a chair. I simply never asked you to sit in it while there was anything in this house left to protect. There isn't now."

He crossed the room. It was perhaps fifteen feet, and it took him a year.

When he sat, the table was silent again, but it was a different silence — the held-breath kind, the kind that precedes either disaster or grace. Then Mrs. Prentice said, "You burned a letter of mine once."

Everyone looked at her. August's hands went cold under the cloth.

"Ninety-four," she said. "I wrote to Mrs. Ashcombe about my husband — what he did when I was arrested, what he said in court. I asked her never to let it reach the papers. My daughters didn't know until last month." Her voice was steady, but her knuckles, August saw, were white around her fork. "Did you burn it?"

"Yes," he said. "Steam first. Memorized. Burned."

"And did you tell her?"

"She told me to."

Mrs. Prentice nodded slowly, twice, and August braced himself — for accusation, for grief, for whatever tax twelve years of silence was finally coming due. Instead she said, "My daughters cried. And then my eldest said, *Mama, you should have told us; we'd have been proud anyway.*" She looked down at her plate. "Forty years I thought I was protecting them. It turns out I was protecting myself. I'm learning the difference late." She raised her eyes to Marguerite. "That's why I came tonight. Not because I've forgiven anything. Because I want to learn it before I die."

Nobody spoke. The clock on the mantel — the clock, the old brass clock that had outlasted everything — ticked into the quiet like a patient chaperone.

Then Miss Hartley, dryly, devastatingly: "Well. This is the first supper of the movement where nobody has pretended anything. I find I don't have an appetite and I can't stop eating."

And the tension broke — not all at once, not cleanly, but the way ice breaks on a river in thaw: in long groaning cracks, in pieces drifting apart, in dark water showing through where none had shown for years.

* * *

After the fish, the talk turned — haltingly, then with gathering force — to the years themselves. Not to Edmund. That was the miracle August kept waiting to be revoked: they barely spoke of Edmund at all. They spoke of the things the myth had eaten.

Mrs. Calloway described the night of the Blackfriars raid in '91, which official history had recorded as a triumph of forewarning, and told them, for the first time in eight years, that there had been no forewarning at all — that a girl of nineteen, Nell Brodie, had gone to prison partly because the movement's own intelligence had failed her, and that Nell had died in '96 of the lung trouble she caught there, and that no speech had ever named her.

"They couldn't name her," Mrs. Calloway said. "She didn't fit the story. She wasn't noble about it. She was frightened and she cried and afterward she wouldn't come to meetings. We needed our martyrs clean." She paused. "So we washed her."

"We did worse than wash her," said Miss Hartley. "We *used* her. I gave a lecture in Bristol in ninety-seven with Nell's name in it and half of it invented. I invented the half where she forgives everyone." She looked at Marguerite. "You weren't the only one keeping a ledger, Marguerite. We all kept ledgers. Yours was just the biggest."

"My ledger had a lock on the drawer," Marguerite said. "That's the only difference I can claim."

"No," said Miss Okafor, quietly, from her place near the end. Every head turned. She had promised to be a guest, and she colored at her own breach of it, but she went on. "Forgive me. I'll say only this and then I'll be a guest again. In six months of work in your archive I have read the papers of eleven organizations and the correspondence of perhaps fifty women, and I have not found one — not one — who told the whole truth on paper at the time. Everyone's record is edited. The only difference between your house and every other house in this history is that you opened the drawer while you were alive to see what was in it." She folded her napkin. "That's all. Potatoes, please."

Someone laughed — Mrs. Prentice, unexpectedly, a real laugh this time — and the potatoes went round, and something in the room settled onto its foundations like a house after a storm deciding it will stand.

* * *

Later, over a plain apple tart, Miss Hartley leaned back and said the thing that August had felt circling the table all evening like a bird looking for a window.

"What happens to us now? That's what I can't stop thinking about. We were a movement. Then we were a memory. Then we were a shrine. Now the shrine's burnt down and I don't know what we are." She looked around the table, at faces she had known for thirty and forty years. "I don't know how to be friends with any of you without the cause in the middle of us. I don't know if we ever were friends. I think maybe we were soldiers, and soldiers aren't required to like each other, only to hold the line together."

"Hold the line," repeated Mrs. Calloway musingly. "Yes. That's what it was. And now the line's — "

"The line isn't gone," Marguerite said.

They all looked at her. She had been quiet a long while, letting them talk, doing whatever private work the cooking and the hosting had been meant to do in her. But now she set down her spoon and folded her hands, and August, who had watched her speak to halls of two thousand, recognized the posture and understood that whatever came next was not improvised.

"The line was never Edmund," she said. "I know it looked like Edmund. For twelve years we marched behind a dead man's name because a name is easier to carry than a principle — it doesn't argue, it doesn't disappoint, it stays exactly as brave as the engraving makes it. But the line was never him. The line was Nell Brodie in her cell. It was you, Ada, feeding bail money through your husband's accounts so he'd never know. It was Mrs. Prentice going to Holloway with her hair cut off and coming home to a house that despised her. It was every woman who did the work knowing the work would kill her reputation and doing it anyway." Her voice did not rise. It grew quieter, which was worse, which made them lean in. "Edmund sold us and we buried the sale and built a church on the grave. Fine. The church is down. But underneath the church floor there is a foundation, and the foundation is us, and I am asking — I, Marguerite Ashcombe, who has no right to ask anyone anything anymore — I am asking whether the foundation will bear building on."

"Bearing building on for what?" Miss Hartley asked. "We're old, Marguerite. The vote won't come in our lifetime. You've said so yourself."

"The vote won't," Marguerite agreed. "But the record will. The record comes first — it has always come first; the men understand that better than we ever did, which is why they guard their archives like fortresses. Whoever writes the century writes the argument for the century after." She touched the table lightly with one finger. "Okafor's history. My memoir. August's testimony. That's the first course of it. But a history needs witnesses, and witnesses need each other. I'm not asking you to march. I'm asking you to *remember out loud*. Together. Correcting one another. Refusing to invent. It's less glorious than the barricades. It's also harder."

Silence. The clock ticked. Outside, February wind worried the bare wisteria against the south wall.

"It's the least glamorous thing you've ever proposed," said Miss Hartley at last.

"Yes."

"I'll need to check my dates against yours. I know for a fact your memory of the '93 conference is wrong in three places."

"Four," said Mrs. Calloway. "I was there too."

"Five," said August, and the whole table turned to him again, and he found, to his enormous surprise, that he was smiling. "I filed the minutes. Someone in this house had to be able to count."

And then they were arguing — really arguing, hot and specific and absurd, four grey heads bent over the geography of a conference held nine years ago, fighting about whether the Birmingham delegation arrived Thursday or Friday, and whether the resolution on the married women's clause passed by eleven votes or thirteen — and August sat in his chair at the table and listened to the sound of it and understood that this was what community after truth sounded like. Not harmony. Never harmony. Argument, held gently, between people who had decided to stay in the room.

Marguerite caught his eye across the table. Neither of them smiled. It was better than smiling. It was the look of two people who have survived a fire walking together through the smoke and finding, here and there, that some things are still standing.

* * *

The women left in ones and twos past eleven, wrapped in shawls against the cold, helped into the trap by the boy from the village whom August had hired for the evening. At the door, Mrs. Prentice stopped and took his hand in both of hers, her gloves rough wool, her grip surprisingly strong.

"They'll write about you badly, you know," she said. "Some of them. The reader of letters. The man who knew everything and said nothing for twelve years. There's no version of your story where you come out entirely clean."

"I know," August said.

"Good." She squeezed his hand. "Clean men make poor witnesses. They haven't got anything to testify *from*." And she climbed into the trap and was gone down the drive, the lantern swinging, the dark taking her.

August stood in the doorway a moment. Then he closed the door and went back through the hall, past the spot on the wall where Edmund's portrait had hung and where the wallpaper, faded in a perfect rectangle, still remembered it, and into the dining room.

Marguerite had not moved. She was still at the head of the table, amid the ruins of the supper, and Miss Okafor was beside her — had lingered, he saw, deliberately — and both women were looking at a sheet of paper that lay flat between them on the cloth like something recently shot.

Okafor looked up as he entered. Her face, usually so composed, so professionally neutral, was bright with something she was making no effort to hide.

"The proofs," she said. "From the printer. They came on the evening train — I brought them to show Marguerite and then the evening got away from us." She tapped the page. "Chapter one. Set in type. Your worst truths, madam, in twelve-point Caslon, ready to be bound into ten thousand copies." She pushed back her chair. "I think they ought to be read alone, the first time. Or — " she glanced at August, and something shrewd and kind moved in her face — "or with whoever you choose. But read them tonight. I want to hear from you tomorrow, and I warn you, the second printing decision waits for no one's courage."

She gathered her coat and her bag and let herself out, and the front door clicked, and the house was suddenly vast and quiet around the two of them and the single sheet of paper lying on the supper-stained cloth.

Marguerite did not touch it. August came and stood behind her chair, and together they looked down at the page — at the running head that read ASHCOMBE: A TRUE ACCOUNT, at the chapter title in small capitals, at the dense, indifferent, beautiful columns of type in which her whole life now stood exposed, numbered by lines for the printer's correction, permanent as carving.

"He's on the first page," Marguerite said at last. Her voice was level. Her hand, resting on the table, was not. "Edmund. Okafor starts with him. Of course she does. Everything starts with him." She drew a breath. "Read it to me, August. I've read it aloud alone a dozen times. I've read it to the women. But I find I cannot be the first voice in this room to say it out loud anymore. Will you do that? Will you read me my own book?"

He put his hands on her shoulders. Felt her lean back into him, one degree, the smallest possible weight — trust measured in degrees.

"Yes," he said, and picked up the page.
