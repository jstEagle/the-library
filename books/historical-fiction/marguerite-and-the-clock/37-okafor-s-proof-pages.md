## 37. Okafor's Proof Pages

The proofs arrived on a Thursday in March, in a crate that smelled of ink and glue and the particular cold of a printer's warehouse. August carried it into the morning room himself, declining the help of the boy who had delivered it, and set it down before Marguerite with the gravity of a man laying down something that might still go off.

Miss Okafor stood at the window with her hands clasped behind her back, watching the drive as though the printers themselves might come marching up it. She had taken the train down from London that morning and had refused breakfast, which August had noted as a symptom of either nerves or discipline; in her case he had learned the two were difficult to distinguish.

"Four hundred and twelve pages," she said, turning from the window. "Set in type. Bound loosely, so corrections can be made. This is the last moment at which the book can be changed, Mrs. Ashcombe — after this, it belongs to everyone."

Marguerite did not touch the crate at once. She sat very straight in her chair by the fire, her hands folded in her lap in the posture August knew well: the one she wore when she was about to do something that frightened her and had decided no one would see it. He had watched her wear that face before deputations, before magistrates, before the night she signed her name to Okafor's condition and let the founding myth of forty years die in a newspaper office.

But this was different, and they all three of them knew it. Speeches could be rewritten in memory. Even the memoir itself, read aloud to five people in an empty house in January, had lived only in the air between them. This — this was type. Lead and paper and permanence. Her worst truths, cast in metal by men who did not know her, set line upon line so that no power on earth could make them unmade.

"Open it," she said.

August took up the crowbar Okafor had thoughtfully included and drew the nails. The smell rose out of the crate like something alive — hot metal, fresh paper, a faint bitterness of solvent. Inside, wrapped in brown paper against the damp, lay stacks of long galley sheets, printed in columns, still smelling of the press.

Okafor lifted the first sheet and laid it flat on the table before her hostess. "Page one," she said. "I had them begin with your own words. The dedication."

Marguerite looked down at it for a long time.

*To those who told me the truth too late, and to the one who kept it too long.*

August, standing at the edge of the lamplight, felt his chest tighten around those two sentences as though they had been set about his ribs. He had read the dedication in manuscript, months ago, and had said nothing then either. Some silences, he had learned, were not failures but forms of speech.

"Read it aloud," Okafor said gently. "That is how I always check proofs. The eye forgives what the ear will not."

Marguerite began at the beginning, therefore — not with the dedication but with the first chapter, her voice low and even, the voice she used for reading scripture and verdicts. And August stood where he stood and listened to a woman read her own life back to herself in the cold impersonal columns of a stranger's press.

*My name is Marguerite Ashcombe,* the page said, in her voice. *For thirty-one years I have been known as the widow of Edmund Ashcombe, martyr of the movement. I am going to tell you now what I know, and when I have finished, you will understand why I waited.*

She turned pages. The room was very quiet. Rain had begun against the windows, a soft March rain that meant nothing and threatened everything, the sort of weather England keeps in reserve for moments of consequence.

Here came the passage August dreaded — he knew its position in the book the way a sailor knows a reef. *In the spring of 1887,* Marguerite read, *my husband met four times with a man from the Home Office whose name appears in these pages under his own initials. I know because I found the receipts. I know because I checked them twice, and then a third time, because I wanted with all my heart to be wrong.* Her voice held. It held through the sentence about the receipts and through the worse one after it, the one naming the women whose names Edmund had sold, two of whom had died in circumstances their families still attributed to misfortune.

It held until she reached the paragraph about the drawer.

*I kept his papers locked for twelve years,* she read. *I told myself I was protecting the movement. This was true. But underneath the truth there was another one, smaller and heavier: I was protecting my marriage. A dead man cannot defend himself, but neither can he be divorced. My grief needed him innocent more than the movement needed him honest. That is the confession beneath all my other confessions, and I set it down here because I believe a record is worthless if it flatters the person making it.*

Her voice failed there — not broke, but stopped, like a clock deliberately wound to silence. She pressed two fingers to her lips and looked at the page, at the black type sitting in its neat column, saying the thing she had said aloud only once before, in this same room, to five people who had sworn to carry it carefully.

"Well," she said at last. "Well. There it is."

"Do you want to stop?" Okafor asked.

"No." Marguerite picked up her tea, found it cold, drank it anyway. "No. I want to keep going. I want to hear all of it, every word, before strangers' hands correct a comma." She looked up, and August saw that her eyes were wet and that she was, unmistakably, smiling. "Do you know what I expected? I expected it to be unbearable. Everyone warns you that seeing your sins in print will be unbearable. But it isn't. It's — " she searched, and found it — "it's bearable. It sits there on the page and does not move. Twelve years it moved inside me, growing in the dark. Now it is four hundred words of type and it cannot grow any larger than it is. Miss Okafor, you have performed a small miracle: you have made my guilt finite."

"That," said Okafor dryly, "is precisely what typesetting is for." But her eyes were bright, and she looked away quickly toward the window, and August understood that the young historian had been afraid too — afraid that her author would break, or recant, or demand the whole edition pulped, and that the truth would retreat once more into the dark where it had lived so comfortably for so long.

They worked through the morning. Okafor read passages in her quick, precise voice, flagging errors of date and spelling; Marguerite corrected in pencil, her hand steady, sometimes laughing — actually laughing — at the small indignities of print. *Mrs. Ashcomb.* *The year 1891 rendered as 1819, which would have made her a prodigy.* The worst passages went by one after another, and each time August braced himself, and each time the blow fell and proved survivable, because the page held it, and the page did not flinch.

He was not called upon to speak until nearly noon, when Okafor turned to a page near the end and paused, sheet in hand, and looked at him across the table with an expression he had learned to read as professional embarrassment.

"This section concerns testimony given by a witness identified here only as A.V.," she said. "Driver and secretary to Mrs. Ashcombe from 1887. Keeper of her correspondence." She laid the sheet down and slid it, almost shyly, toward the center of the table. "There is also this. The acknowledgments page. I should like you to look at it before anyone else does."

August took the sheet.

The column of names ran down the page in the printer's orderly fashion — the movement women, the survivors, Mrs. Fenwick's name with a line beneath it reading *who did not live to see this book, and would have enjoyed it enormously*, and then, near the foot, set flush with the others as though it were the most ordinary thing in the world:

*And to August Villalobos, who read first and spoke last, without whose memory this record could not exist. The errors are ours; the silences broken were his.*

He read it twice. Then a third time, because the words did not seem to adhere to the paper but floated slightly above it, refusing to be fixed anywhere, including in his own understanding. His name. His name in type. Not *the driver*. Not *A.V.*, redacted and initialled. His full name, spelled correctly, set beside Fenwick's, beside Okafor's own, in a book that would outlast every person in this room.

"I never gave permission for that," he said, and heard his own voice come out strange.

"No," Okafor agreed. "You didn't give permission for anything. That has rather been the pattern of your life, hasn't it?" She did not smile when she said it; it was not mockery, but it was close kin to kindness. "Consider it done over your objection, Mr. Villalobos. Historians are accustomed to acting without consent. We call it research."

Marguerite had risen and come around the table, and now she stood beside him, looking down at the page over his arm. He felt her nearness the way he had felt it for years and years — a warmth at his shoulder, an attention — but different now, since January, since the night they had spoken plainly and stripped the love between them down to its frame and begun, plank by plank, to build it again on disclosed ground. She did not touch him. They had not yet arrived at touching, or rather they had arrived and retreated several times, both of them careful as people carrying water across ice.

"Read it aloud," she said softly, using Okafor's own instruction against her. "The eye forgives what the ear will not."

"I am not reading my own acknowledgment aloud."

"You read other people's letters aloud for twelve years without their consent. Read your own name once, with yours."

He looked at her. She was smiling — the new smile, the one that had survived the winter, that had been tempered in the fire of the reckoning morning and come out harder and warmer both. And so August Villalobos, fifty-three years old, keeper of twelve years of protective silence, stood in the morning room while rain ticked against the glass and read aloud, in a voice that wavered on the second word and steadied by the fourth:

"And to August Villalobos, who read first and spoke last."

The room was quiet after. The fire settled. Somewhere upstairs a door moved in the draught, the house shifting on its foundations the way old houses do when they are listening.

"It looks small," he said finally. "On the page. All of it. The whole winter — the warrants, Pell, the printing house, the funeral, the fear — it fits into four hundred and twelve pages and weighs perhaps two pounds."

"That is what the historians who write the victors' version will never forgive us for," Okafor said, gathering her marked sheets into order with brisk, capable hands. "We made it light. Truth that weighs two pounds can travel. Their version requires an empire to carry it." She tied the bundle with string. "I take the train back tonight. Corrected proofs within the fortnight, bound copies by summer. And Mrs. Ashcombe — one matter remains. Page four hundred and eleven. The final line."

Marguerite turned to it. August watched her read it silently, and watched her face change — not with pain but with something quieter, the expression of a woman finding, in an unexpected pocket, money she had forgotten she possessed.

"You left it open," she said to Okafor. "The ending. 'And what became of them afterward, the record does not yet say.'"

"The record does not yet say," Okafor agreed. "I am a historian, madam, not a prophet. I can only print what has happened. What happens next is none of my department." She pulled on her gloves, briskly, before anyone could answer, before anyone could thank her — August suspected she fled gratitude the way other people fled bad weather — and paused at the door with her hand on the frame.

"For what it is worth," she said, not turning around, "I have handled a great many archives. Most of them lie by omission, and a few lie by commission, and all of them are proud of it. Yours is the first I have ever catalogued that told the truth on every page and was kind besides. Do you know how rare that is? People believe accuracy and mercy are opposites. You two have written a book that proves otherwise." And then she was gone, down the corridor, calling for her umbrella, leaving the smell of the train and the future behind her.

* * *

They ate lunch in the kitchen, which had become their habit since the house had emptied of ceremony — bread, cheese, apples from the cellar, the plain food of two people who no longer performed for anyone. Through the window the garden showed the first green insistence of spring, the beds Marguerite had ordered turned for the archive's opening, the greenhouse where the confrontation had happened now glazed anew and full of seedlings that did not care what had been confessed among them.

"Page four hundred and eleven," Marguerite said, when the plates were cleared. She turned the galley sheet around and pushed it toward him. "Read it."

"'What became of them afterward,'" August read, "'the record does not yet say.'"

"They want an ending. Okafor wants an ending. The printers want an ending." Marguerite turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "Everyone wants the ending, and no one wants to write it, because writing it means deciding what we are."

"What are we?"

She looked up. Outside, the rain had thinned to a silver drift; the light in the kitchen had gone soft and warm, the light of the hour when the day forgives itself.

"I don't know yet," she said honestly. "That is the strangest part, August. I have been a widow, a leader, a liar, a martyr-maker. I knew what I was for forty years. Now the book is finished and I find I am — unwritten. Four hundred and eleven pages say who I was. The last one is blank, and I wake some mornings terrified of it, and some mornings — " she smiled, and it was the smile he loved, undressed of all its public architecture — "some mornings I wake delighted. Anything could go on that page."

"Not anything," August said. He reached across the table — slowly, deliberately, the way one reaches across twelve years — and turned the galley sheet so it faced her, and laid his hand flat upon the blank space at its foot. "Something goes there. Something true. We have earned that much, at least: whatever we write next must be true, or the whole book is a lie with a good first act."

Marguerite put her hand over his, there on the empty page, in the blank space that awaited them both. Her fingers were cold from the kitchen and his were warm from the fire, and neither of them moved for a long moment, and the clock in the hall — the clock that had survived everything — struck the hour with its patient, indifferent certainty, counting them forward whether they were ready or not.

Then the front bell rang.

Not the archive bell, not the tradesman's entrance. The front bell, rung twice, firmly, in the manner of someone with business and standing. August was on his feet before Marguerite had stirred, old habit rising in him like water into a lock — and through the window, past the dripping laurels, he saw the figure coming up the drive through the thinning rain, and recognized the walk before he recognized the coat.

Mr. Pell. Unannounced. Alone. Carrying nothing but a hat and a document case, and wearing, on his face, an expression August had never seen him wear before — not triumph, not threat, but something older and wearier, the look of a man arriving to settle an account he had been avoiding for years.

Marguerite rose beside him, and her hand found his sleeve, and together they turned toward the hall as the bell rang a third time.
