## 38. The Last Letter August Reads

The letter came on a Thursday in late April, when the plane trees along the avenue had finally committed to their leaves and the light through the study windows had gone green at the edges, like glass over a garden.

August saw it first, because he still saw the post first — some habits were not broken so much as converted. Twelve years of steaming envelopes had taught him the weight of paper, the tell of a hand he recognized, the difference between a circular and a confession arriving by penny post. He had retired from the reading of other people's mail; he had not retired from knowing what it looked like.

This one was heavy stock, cream-colored, addressed in a hand that was neither old nor young, neither hurried nor careful — a hand that had been trained to write well and no longer thought about it. The return address was printed, not written: *Whitcombe & Sons, Solicitors, Lincoln's Inn Fields.*

He carried it into the morning room, where Marguerite sat with the newspaper folded to a page she was not reading, her spectacles pushed up into her hair in the way she did when she was thinking about something that had nothing to do with print.

"Post," he said.

She held out her hand for it, and then, halfway through the gesture, stopped. Something crossed her face — not pain, exactly, but its cousin: recognition. She looked at the envelope in his fingers, then up at him, and August watched her arrive at the same moment he had been living in since New Year's Day: the moment when an old reflex meets a new world and does not know which one to obey.

"Lincoln's Inn," she said. "Solicitors."

"Solicitors," he agreed.

"I don't know any solicitors."

"You know everyone," August said. "That has never stopped anyone from writing to you."

And here was the thing that would have been unremarkable to any other two people in England, in any other year, and was to them a small ceremony of enormous weight: Marguerite Ashcombe did not take the letter away to open it alone. She did not carry it upstairs. She did not wait for him to leave the room.

She patted the chair beside her instead.

"Sit down," she said. "You may as well be bored with me while I do it."

* * *

It is necessary, perhaps, to say what this cost them both, because nothing about it looked like cost. It looked like a woman opening a letter in front of her — driver? secretary? companion? the newspapers had struggled all spring with what to call him, and had settled, variously, on *associate*, on *the former Mr. Villalobos*, on one memorable occasion on *her man*, which had made August laugh for the first time in weeks. It looked like nothing. That was the point of it. The whole architecture of their twelve years had been built out of things that looked like nothing: a man who lingered in a hallway, a letter that went into a drawer unread, a silence that passed between them so smoothly it might have been affection rather than concealment.

They had spent the winter learning the difference.

Marguerite turned the envelope over twice before she took up the letter knife — his letter knife, August noticed; the one he kept on the sideboard for his own correspondence, back when he had pretended to have any. She slid it under the flap with the care of a surgeon and drew out two sheets, closely written, and a third page that was heavier, official, stamped.

"It's an estate," she said, scanning. "Somebody's estate." Her eyes went down the page. "A Mrs. — " and she stopped, and August watched her hand go very slightly white around the paper.

"What is it?"

"Henrietta Fenwick's sister," Marguerite said quietly. "Ada. Ada Merridew. I met her once, at the funeral. I didn't — I sent my card. I never wrote." She looked up at him. "Henrietta left something for me."

Mrs. Fenwick. Dead since February, suddenly, between one Sunday and the next, with her testimony existing only in Marguerite's notebook and Pell counting mourners at the graveside. They had both grieved her in the way they had learned to grieve everything that year — quickly, privately, without letting it interrupt the work, because the work could not be interrupted, because witnesses were mortal and paper was not.

"She left you something," August said. "In her will."

"In her will." Marguerite said it as though testing whether the words could bear weight. She read on, and August read over her shoulder — openly, deliberately, his chin nearly touching her hair, because this was the new arrangement and they practiced it the way converts practice prayer, faithfully and slightly amazed at themselves.

*Dear Mrs. Ashcombe,* the solicitor's covering letter began. *I am directed by the executors of the late Mrs. Henrietta Fenwick (deceased 14th February) to inform you that you are named in her will as the recipient of certain papers, held in trust these many years, with instructions that they be conveyed to your hands only upon the death of the testatrix or upon your public declaration regarding the historical record of Edmund Ashcombe, whichever should occur last.*

Marguerite set the letter down on her knee.

"Either condition has now been met," August said.

"Yes."

"So we meet both."

"Yes." She was looking at the window, at the green light. "She knew. All those years, she knew — she told me she suspected, she told me others died carrying the suspicion — and she sat on papers. She held them in trust." A breath. "She was doing what I did. What we all did. Keeping the record safe until it was safe to keep it true."

"And now?"

"Now there is a box," Marguerite said, "at her solicitors' office in Lincoln's Inn, containing whatever Henrietta Fenwick considered important enough to lock away for thirty years. And a key." She lifted the third sheet — heavy, official — and turned it toward him. "Which they have enclosed."

* * *

They drove down to London together on Friday, which was itself a sentence that would have been unthinkable a year ago and was now merely Tuesday. August drove, of course — some things were simply true — but Marguerite sat beside him on the box seat rather than behind in the carriage, wrapped in a wool coat against the spring wind, and neither of them remarked upon it because remarking upon it would have suggested it was remarkable, and they had decided, formally, in the way they decided most things now, that it was not.

The city was loud with the new century. Electric lamps going up along the Embankment, motor cars growling past like disgruntled animals, hoardings plastered with the faces of men running for Parliament on platforms that would have been seditious five years ago. On a wall near Charing Cross someone had chalked, in a schoolroom hand, *ASHCOMBE LIED* — and beneath it, in a different hand, *ASHCOMBE TOLD THE TRUTH AND SO DID SHE*, and August had slowed the carriage without meaning to, and Marguerite had said, mildly, "You'll want to keep moving, the traffic behind us," and that was all. That was the whole of their life in public now: a country arguing about them at volume, and the two of them inside the argument like a couple inside a storm, dry, mostly, and holding on.

Whitcombe & Sons occupied a narrow Georgian house off Lincoln's Inn Fields, all dark paneling and brass fittings and the smell of dust that had been legally preserved. A junior clerk received them in a room the size of a confessional and vanished with the key and their cards, and returned after an interval long enough for Marguerite to observe, in a whisper, that solicitors' offices were designed to make waiting feel like penance, and August to answer that in his experience most of England was.

They brought out the box at last. It was tin, painted black once, paint gone grey at the corners, tied with string in a knot so old it had fused. The clerk set it on the table, murmured that Mrs. Fenwick's instructions had been extremely precise, and withdrew.

Marguerite untied the string. Inside, wrapped in a shawl gone soft with age, were papers — dozens of them, letters and notes and copies of copies, tied in bundles with ribbon faded from red to the color of old brick. And on top, in Henrietta Fenwick's upright, uncompromising hand, an envelope marked *For Marguerite Ashcombe, to be read first.*

"Read first," August repeated softly. "There's a familiar instruction."

"Don't," Marguerite said, but she was almost smiling. She broke the seal.

The letter was short. August read every word as she read it, their heads bent together over the page like children over one book, and this — he understood even as it happened — this was the reward. Not the contents. The posture. The fact of it.

*My dear Mrs. Ashcombe,*

*You will wonder why I never gave you these while I lived. The answer is that I did not trust myself to watch you receive them. I suspected Edmund for eleven years and knew it for four, and I said nothing, because we were all saying nothing, and the saying-nothing had become the movement itself — we mistook our silence for solidarity. When at last I spoke to you, I found you already carrying the same weight, and I could not add mine to yours.*

*So I kept the papers. Some are letters of Edmund's to men in the Home Office. Some are letters from women who trusted him, naming meetings, naming names, believing him a brother. One is a receipt. You will know which when you see it.*

*I kept them because I believed that someday a woman would come to the truth of her own husband not because a historian demanded it, but because she chose it. You have done that thing. Whatever it cost you — and I have read the newspapers, dear girl, I know what it cost — you did it freely, which none of us managed.*

*The rest is yours to burn, publish, or bury. I only ask that you not let it be either shrine or secret. We have had enough of both.*

*Yours in the old fight,*
*H.F.*

Marguerite read it twice. Then she folded it along its original creases, with great exactness, and laid it on the table, and put both hands flat on the wood, and stood like that for a while, head bowed, in the greenish light through the solicitor's dusty window.

August did not touch her. He wanted to; his hand actually moved and he arrested it, because there were moments when what she needed was not comfort but room, and he had learned — slowly, expensively, over twelve years and one catastrophic winter — to tell the difference.

"He named names," she said finally. "These women wrote to him trusting — " Her voice caught. She mastered it. "There's a receipt, August. For money paid. He itemized us."

"I know," August said quietly. Because he did. Because he had known since a locked drawer opened in autumn, since the night in the greenhouse, since every hour of the truth they had traded their reputations for. "I've seen that kind of paper before."

She looked at him then — a long look, level, without accusation and without absolution, the look of two people who had catalogued each other's sins and decided, provisionally, to go on anyway.

"Yes," she said. "You have."

* * *

They brought the box home and did not open it that night, which surprised them both. There had been a time — the whole of the winter, really — when a packet of documents in the house would have meant a vigil, candles, the reading aloud, the sorting of truth into piles labeled *publish* and *destroy*. But the memoir was printed. The testimony was public. The historians had what historians needed. Whatever was in Henrietta Fenwick's bundles could wait until morning, and for the first time in their acquaintance, waiting felt like strength rather than cowardice.

Instead they sat in the drawing room with the fire low, and Marguerite wound the mantel clock — she did this every evening now, a ritual begun on the century's last night and continued past all reason, since the clock ran perfectly well and needed winding only weekly — and August read aloud from a novel neither of them were enjoying much, and it was, by any measure, an ordinary evening, which was the rarest thing they owned.

It was Marguerite who broke the quiet, near midnight, staring into the fire.

"When I was twenty-three," she said, "Edmund used to open my letters."

August set down the novel. "Did he."

"For my safety, he said. There were spies in the movement, he said — which was true, though not in the way he meant. He'd sit across from me at breakfast and hand me my post already opened, flaps lifted, saying he'd checked nothing was dangerous." Her mouth twisted. "I let him. I thought it was devotion. It took me thirty years to understand it was ownership."

"And me?" August asked. It was a fair question and they had earned the right to ask fair questions. "What was I?"

Marguerite turned from the fire and looked at him straight, the firelight moving on her face, her hair gone fully silver now at the temples and magnificent for it.

"You were a wall," she said. "And I asked for the wall. Every brick of it, I ordered it laid. That's the difference, August, and I have had to learn it, and it is the hardest thing I ever learned: Edmund read my letters because he did not think I could survive the truth. You read them because I told you I couldn't — and then, God help me, I built a life on top of that lie about my own weakness until I believed it myself." She paused. "You weren't my jailer. You were the door I kept locked and blamed for being locked."

August sat with that. Outside, April rain had started, soft against the dark windows, the sound of a country being watered into summer.

"And now?" he asked.

"Now you're a man who reads novels aloud badly," Marguerite said, "and sits beside me in carriages, and lets me open my own post." She reached over and took his hand — took it, deliberately, the way she signed documents, with full intent witnessed by no one and needing no witness. "Now you're just a man. It's taken me sixty-one years to say that to someone and mean it as praise."

* * *

The next morning they opened the bundles together at the long table in the study, in full daylight, with the windows open to the smell of wet gardens.

They sorted as equals. Marguerite handled the papers first — her husband, her grief, her right — and passed each one to August after, and he read what she had read, and together they made three piles: *archive*, for Miss Okafor's history; *private*, for the families of the women named, who deserved to learn the truth from hands kinder than a printer's; and a small third pile, which grew to four items, of things so poisoned with Edmund's handwriting that Marguerite finally touched a candle to them herself, watching them curl, saying nothing, her face like carved slate.

Near the bottom of the second bundle, August found the receipt.

He knew it before he unfolded it — the Home Office watermark, the neat clerical figures, the date. Eleven pounds, ten shillings. November 1887. Eight months before Edmund died. Payment *for services rendered to H.M. Government, per arrangement.*

He held it a moment longer than he needed to. Then he passed it across the table without a word, because passing it was the whole of the vow now, the daily liturgy of their rebuilt life: *nothing hidden, nothing steamed, nothing burned without two signatures.*

Marguerite read it. Her jaw tightened. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a short, hard, astonished sound with tears standing in it.

"Eleven pounds," she said. "They gave him eleven pounds ten, and the government gave Pell a salary, and the historians gave the myth a monument, and it all rested on — " she tapped the paper — "*this*. On a scrap of paper small enough to lose in a hymnbook." She set it gently in the archive pile, face-down. "Okafor can have it. Let it be weighed in type, where it belongs. Paper should be accountable to paper."

"There's one more bundle," August said.

It was at the very bottom of the box, wrapped separately in oilcloth, tied with wire. Inside was a single letter, older than the rest, the paper browned, the seal broken decades ago. The direction on it read: *To whoever keeps the house after me.* And beneath it, in a younger version of Henrietta Fenwick's hand: *If this is ever opened, it means someone finally told the truth. God forgive us how long it took.*

Marguerite broke it open. Read. Went still.

Then she turned the page toward August, her hand not quite steady, and he read:

*A list. Names. Nine women, dated 1885 through 1890 — women whose arrests, whose imprisonments, whose ruined health had always been attributed to bad luck, to informers unknown, to the general cruelty of the age. Beside each name, in Fenwick's hand, a note of how she had learned the truth, and from whom, and when. The ninth name was fresh ink compared to the others. The ninth name was dated January 1900.*

*Mrs. Fenwick had added it after the publication. After the truth came out. She had lived long enough to see it confirmed against her own private record, and had died eleven days later, and had left this for the house that finally told the truth.*

At the foot of the page, below the ninth name, one more line, in that same upright hand:

*There is a tenth name I never proved. Ask the widow. She will know who I mean. She has always known.*

Marguerite read it three times. The rain had stopped; sunlight was coming through the study windows now, lying in pale bars across the table, across the piles of paper, across her hands.

"Tenth name," August said quietly. "Who?"

And Marguerite Ashcombe, who had spent a winter learning to hide nothing, sat with the letter in both hands and did not answer — and August watched, with a feeling like the floor tilting, the first shadow cross her face in months that looked, horribly, like an old habit putting on new clothes.

"I need to write to someone," she said at last. "Someone I haven't spoken to since before Christmas."

She was already reaching for paper.
