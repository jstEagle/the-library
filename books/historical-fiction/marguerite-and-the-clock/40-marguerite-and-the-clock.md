## 40. Marguerite and the Clock

The letter had come in the ordinary post, which was itself remarkable — the extraordinary had learned to knock politely since January — and August carried it into the morning room where Marguerite sat with her tea and her proofs and the particular stillness she wore on days when the archive expected her.

"Mr. A. Halloran, Esq.," he said, setting it beside her cup. "I'm told that's me."

"I know who you are." She did not look up from Okafor's galley pages. "I've known for some years now, though it took a century's turning to make it official."

He stood there with his hands at his sides, hands that had steamed open four hundred envelopes and burned one letter unopened and copied credentials by candlelight and written his own name across twelve newspapers in one reckless December night. The envelope looked small against all that history. Official crests always did.

"Are you going to open it?" Marguerite asked.

"Yes."

She raised her eyes then, and he saw her watching him the way she had watched him for thirteen years and would watch him, God willing, for thirty more: as though he were weather she intended to understand completely.

"It isn't sealed," she said. "I can see it from here. It's only tucked."

It was true. The flap was merely folded under. He opened it standing, in the grey spring light, and read it twice, and then he laughed — a short, disbelieving sound that made her put down the proofs entirely.

"What is it?"

"They're offering me a pension." He set the page down on the table between them. "For services rendered. Twelve years of 'discreet and loyal attendance' upon Mrs. Ashcombe, duly noted in their files, and they'd like to make it worth my while to remain discreet a while longer."

Marguerite picked up the letter herself and read it slowly, and he watched something move across her face — not anger; he had seen enough of her anger to know its weather — but something older and wearier, like frost leaving a window.

"'Discreet,'" she repeated. "They still think of you as discreet."

"I was," August said. "That was rather the whole tragedy of it."

"And now?"

"Now I am a man whose testimony is printed in three editions and quoted in Parliament." He smiled. "Discreet is behind me, madam. It went out with the old century."

She tore the letter in half. Then, methodically, as she did everything, into quarters, eighths, confetti. She dropped the pieces into her teacup, where they floated and darkened and sank.

"That," she said, "is the last correspondence this house will ever answer on behalf of someone else's secret."

* * *

The clock had come with the house, before either of them. It stood on the drawing-room mantel, walnut-cased, brass-faced, older than Edmund, older than the movement, older than the century that had ended while printers ran its obituary off a hand press in Southwark. It had survived two house searches, one funeral, one public disgrace, one reconciliation conducted without absolution, and the small daily earthquake of two people learning to live without concealment. During the worst weeks, when Pell's men circled and Fenwick's grave was fresh and the papers called Marguerite everything from traitor to saint depending on the editor's digestion, August had noticed that neither of them ever wound it. They let it run down. Neither said why.

It had started again on the first of January. He could not remember which of them had reached for the key first.

Now it was June, and on a bright evening with the windows open to the smell of cut grass, Marguerite stood before the mantel with the brass key in her hand and did not wind it.

August watched from the doorway, a glass of wine untouched in his hand. He had learned, in these months, to watch her without pretending otherwise — no more studied glances away, no more professional blindness. That had been the strangest gift of honesty: not that he saw more, but that he was permitted to be caught seeing.

"You're thinking loudly again," she said, without turning.

"I'm told it's a habit of mine."

"The clock stopped."

"It does that. It's old."

"It doesn't stop, August. It's never merely stopped. It waits." She turned to face him, and the light from the window fell across half her face, and he thought — as he had thought every day for months, with the wonder of a man who has been granted an undeserved season — that she was more beautiful now than in any photograph of her celebrated youth, because every line in her face had been earned in the open. "Do you remember what I said at midnight?"

New Year's Eve felt like another man's memory. The print shop smelling of ink and coal smoke, the compositor's boy asleep on a sack of type, Marguerite's pen scratching the final signature while the bells of St. Saviour's began their counting. She had signed *Marguerite Ashcombe* — not the widow's style, not the movement's honorific, just her name, the whole of it, on the last page of the true account — and then she had looked up at him through the press room's haze and said:

*"The century kept its appointment. So shall we."*

"I remember," he said.

"Everyone remembers the bells and the crowd outside and Pell arriving with his warrant to find the truth already on sixpenny sheets in every gutter in London." She smiled thinly. "What nobody remembers is that the clock here stopped that night too. I checked. Ten minutes to midnight, it simply gave up, as if it knew its century was over. And we wound it anyway, and it has been disagreeable about time ever since."

"Like its owner."

"Flatterer." But she came toward him then, and took the wine glass from his hand and drank from it herself, which was the kind of liberty she had only permitted herself since the reckoning morning, and which still undid him slightly each time.

"The truth is filed," she said quietly. "Dated. Catalogued in Miss Okafor's careful hand, cross-referenced, footnoted within an inch of its life. Fenwick's testimony, your testimony, my confession of every silence I ever purchased. It exists now independent of us. Whatever they do with it — and they will do things with it, historians are worse than journalists — it is no longer ours to guard."

"No," August agreed. "We're retired from guarding."

"Does it feel like retirement? Or like amputation?"

He considered this honestly, because she had taught him that honest consideration was a form of love, perhaps the deepest form available to people like them. "Both. On alternating days. Some mornings I wake reaching for the kettle to steam the post and feel like a man missing a limb. Other mornings—" he looked at her, and let himself look fully, "—other mornings I wake and the day has nothing hidden in it, and it's the lightest thing I've ever known."

She set down the glass. She went back to the clock.

* * *

Marguerite had spent fifty-eight years learning that memory was a country with bad borders. People crossed it at will, smuggled what they liked, redrew the maps to suit the current government. Her whole life had been a war over whose map would survive: the Home Office's, the movement's, Edmund's ghost's, hers.

And now, standing before the clock with the key warm in her palm, she understood at last that the war was over, and that she had won nothing so simple as victory. What she had won was custody. The truth was filed, dated, and theirs — hers and August's and Fenwick's dead faithful mouth and the two women who had stayed in the empty house that February night while the third walked out into the snow rather than hear Edmund named a betrayer. Filed meant finished. Dated meant past. Theirs meant she could finally stop defending it and simply keep it, the way one keeps a garden: not by hiding it, but by tending.

She fit the key into the winding arbor.

"Do you know what I thought the clock was, all those years?" she said. August had come to stand beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him through his sleeve, and she leaned into that warmth deliberately, publicly, on the record even if the record was only the two of them and a walnut case. "I thought it was a witness. Something that watched us and ticked judgmentally through every silence. Edmund's lies, my complicity, your steam kettles. I used to avoid this room."

"And now?"

"Now I think it was only ever a clock." She turned the key. Inside the case the mainspring gathered itself, patient as anything that has waited a hundred years and expects to wait a hundred more. "It didn't judge. It didn't keep our secrets or expose them. It kept its appointment — noon, midnight, noon again — whether we told the truth or not. We were the ones who decided what the hours would hold." The spring drew tight; she stopped winding, gently, the way Okafor had taught her to handle fragile documents. "The century kept its appointment, August. So did we. Not perfectly. But wholly."

"Holly is a strong word for how we got here."

"Holy is a strong word for how anyone gets anywhere." She closed the clock's little door. Its pendulum swung, gathering rhythm, and the ticking filled the room like a pulse returning to a wrist. "There. Filed."

"Filed?"

"Wound, dated, running. Everything in its place." She laughed at herself, softly. "Listen to me. Thirteen months ago I couldn't say the word *betrayed* about my own husband, and now I catalogue my feelings like an archivist."

"Okafor's influence."

"Okafor's influence," she agreed. "May she footnote us kindly."

Outside, the long summer light lay gold across the lawn, and somewhere beyond the wall a cart rattled past carrying, for all she knew, a newspaper with her name in it, or his, or neither — it hardly mattered anymore. The book sold modestly; the myth faded slowly; strangers wrote letters now that she opened at the breakfast table with August reading over her shoulder, and some of the letters were cruel and some were grateful and one, last week, had been from the young woman who walked out into the snow, saying she had read the book twice now and was ready, if the offer still stood, to see the archive. Marguerite had written back the same day: *Come in spring. The truth keeps well.*

She had not known until she sealed that envelope — openly, unsteamed, witnessed — that forgiveness could be issued forward, in advance, to someone not yet brave enough to receive it. She suspected it was the only kind that mattered.

"August," she said.

"Mm."

"When the clock stops next — and it will, it's contrary — I should like it to be you who winds it. Not because you're staff. Because you live here."

He was quiet a moment. She had learned to let his silences exist without interrogating them; that, too, had been part of the curriculum, harder for her than any speech.

"I resigned," he reminded her. "In writing. You refused the resignation."

"I refuse it annually. It's becoming a tradition. Next January I'll refuse it with cake."

"And after that?"

"After that," Marguerite said, taking his hand and turning him toward the window, toward the light, toward whatever hours the old machine would count out for them — "after that we'll see what the new century asks of us. And this time, we'll answer it aloud."

Behind them, steady and indifferent and entirely forgiven, the clock ticked on.
