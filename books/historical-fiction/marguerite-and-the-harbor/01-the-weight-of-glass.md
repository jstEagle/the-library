## 1. The Weight of Glass

The first storm of the season announced itself the way all honest things do — gradually, then all at once. By morning the horizon had gone the color of pewter, and by afternoon the swells were running long and green out past the point, stacking themselves against the breakwater like men queueing for bread that might never come.

Coral Quintana climbed the hundred and eleven steps with the oil can balanced against her hip, counting them out of habit rather than need. She had counted them so many times over six years that she no longer heard her own boots on the iron treads; the number simply lived in her body now, the way her mother's lullabies lived there, the way Tomás's laugh lived there, two winters deep and still warm at the center.

At the top, the lantern room breathed around her — glass on five sides, the great Fresnel lens turning its slow, patient circles, throwing its blade of light across the water every four seconds. Four seconds of dark, four of light. She had come to think of it as breathing. The sea exhaled; the lamp answered. It was the oldest conversation she knew.

She trimmed the wick first, always. Her father had taught her that before he taught her to swim: *the flame is a living thing, mija, treat it like one.* She pinched the char from the cotton with her fingers, unafraid of the small burn, and watched the light steady itself, grow tall and white and certain. Then she wiped the glass.

This was the part she loved and would not have admitted to loving — the weight of it. The panes were thick as communion tablets, and when she pressed her cloth against them in slow arcs, she could feel the storm gathering behind them like a crowd pressing against a cathedral door. Salt had crusted along the lower sashes already; she scraped it away with her thumbnail, working pane by pane around the circle, until the whole room stood clean and the world outside came in sharp as a daguerreotype: the black water, the white manes of spray, the town huddled below with its shutters going up street by street, one clap after another, like a slow heartbeat closing itself off.

*Let them shutter,* she thought. *I'll keep the eye open.*

By the time she descended, the rain had arrived — not falling so much as arriving sideways, flung against the tower in fistfuls. She crossed the yard bent double, and inside the keeper's house the warmth closed around her like a hand. She lit the stove, set the kettle on, and began the other ritual, the one that kept grief in its proper drawer.

Flour. She tipped the tin and listened — a third left, perhaps less. Rice, enough for ten days if she stretched it. Kerosene for the house lamp, two fingers' worth. Lamp oil for the tower was army business now, delivered on a schedule, but everything else was hers alone to count, hers alone to make last. She wrote the figures in pencil on the inside of the pantry door, beneath three years of previous columns, so that the door had become a kind of calendar of scarcity — each season's handwriting a little tighter than the last.

Tomás's coat still hung by the door. She did not look at it. Looking at it was a luxury she permitted herself only on Sundays, and this was Thursday.

She was measuring rice into the pot when she heard the knock — no, not a knock. A scuffling, a small urgent drumming at the kitchen door, and then the door itself opening without ceremony, because Marguerite had never once in eight months knocked.

"I saw it from the seawall," the girl said, breathless, streaming wet, her black hair plastered to her cheeks. "The supply boat turned back. Old Ferro was shouting at the pilot from the pier. They're not coming again until the weather breaks."

Coral closed her eyes briefly. The supply boat meant flour, kerosene, the letter she'd been waiting three weeks to receive from the church charity in the capital — the one that might say whether there was a place for a twelve-year-old girl in some distant orphanage, or whether there wasn't.

"Then we make the rice stretch," she said. "Sit down. You're dripping on my floor."

"I live here."

"You drip here. Sit."

Marguerite sat. That was the arrangement between them, unspoken and therefore binding: Coral fed her, scolded her, taught her letters at the kitchen table by lamplight, and neither of them ever used the words *orphan* or *taken in* or anything else that would require the other to feel gratitude or pity. The girl's parents had been lost with the *Santa Elena*, going on two years now — the same autumn gale that had taken Tomás, though different waters, different ships, the same indifferent sea. Half the dock children had scattered to aunts and grandmothers and the charity of strangers. Marguerite had scattered nowhere. She had simply begun appearing, first for meals, then for lessons, then with a bundle of belongings she never mentioned and Coral never asked about, until one morning Coral had laid a second blanket in the oil room at the base of the tower and found it rumpled and slept-in the next day, and that was that. Some things are decided the way the tide decides a border: quietly, twice a day, until the map has no choice but to agree.

"Is it starting?" Marguerite asked, nodding toward the window, where the rain ran down the glass like something trying to get in.

"It started hours ago. You're late to everything."

"I am not." But the girl smiled, and Coral turned to the stove so the smile wouldn't be seen doing what it did to her — loosening something in the chest that had been wound tight since the second winter, since the day the harbormaster's boy came up the path with his hat in his hands and Coral had known, before he spoke, from the way the gulls went silent.

* * *

After supper, when Marguerite had gone down the spiral stair to the oil room with her candle and her contraband novel, Coral locked the kitchen door, moved the cedar chest away from the north wall, and knelt.

The loose panel came away in her hands. Behind it, in a cavity barely deeper than a Bible, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the ledger.

It was not an official book. It had begun as a ship's log she'd bought for three coins from a chandler going out of business — blank pages, good paper — and for two years she had filled it in a hand far steadier than her heart. Not ships. Names.

She opened it to the ribbon and read back through the entries the way other people pray.

*Rosalía Ferrán, taken by her sister inland, March. Address unknown.*

*The Vidal brothers, ages nine and eleven, sent to the capital orphanage, April. Mother detained, whereabouts unrecorded.*

*Don Emilio Sarto, fisherman, boat requisitioned, family removed to the camp beyond the ridge, May. No forwarding permitted.*

Page after page. Two winters of them. Every family the war had pulled apart like wet rope, every neighbor vanished into the machinery of the occupation, recorded here because nowhere else in the world were they recorded at all. The army kept lists, yes — cargo manifests, requisition orders, deportation rolls written in the beautiful impersonal script of clerks who numbered people instead of naming them. But those lists belonged to the army. This one belonged to the town. To the tide. To her.

Tonight there were three new names waiting in her memory, gathered over the past week like burrs on a hem.

She dipped her pen and wrote:

*Petra Anaya and daughter Luz, age four, house on Calle del Sol seized for billeting, October 9th. Gone to relatives in the valley, if the valley will have them.*

*Casmir Oduya, harbor pilot's apprentice, conscripted off the pier, October 11th. Seventeen years old. His mother does not know.*

*Widow Bren, evacuated with the last of the hill families, October 12th. Refused to take her cat. The cat remains at the church door. I am feeding it.*

That last line she almost struck out — a ledger should be dignified, Tomás would have teased her, even a secret one — but she left it. Dignity was for the victors' histories. Here, the cat mattered. Here, everything mattered, because here nothing was too small to be erased elsewhere.

She blew on the ink, wrapped the book in its oilcloth, slid it back behind the wall, and replaced the panel and the chest above it. Then she sat a while in the dark with her hands folded, listening to the storm work itself up to a fury against the tower.

Two winters ago she had kept vigil in this same chair, watching the sea do its violence, knowing — knowing in the way one knows a tooth is bad — that somewhere out in that black churn the *Marisol* was fighting for its life with her husband aboard. The lamp had burned true all that night. She had done her work perfectly. And the sea had taken him anyway, and she had learned the lesson that organized her life since: you cannot save anyone by shining a light. You can only make sure they are not forgotten for having been lost.

The ledger was her lamp now. It burned lower, smaller, hidden behind a wall instead of raised on high — but it burned.

Above her, faint through the floors, she could hear the great lens turning, and beneath that, thinner still, the sound of Marguerite reading aloud to herself in the oil room, stumbling over the long words, insisting on them anyway. Coral closed her eyes and let the sounds braid together: wind, wheel, child's voice. Her household. Her impossible, borrowed, temporary household.

*Temporary,* she reminded herself, and the word tasted like the salt she couldn't scrub from the glass.

Because the war was not finished with this coast. Because the troop trains had been passing more often, and the flags over the customs house had changed twice in a year, and because yesterday, nailed to the post office door where the fishing notices used to hang, she had seen a printed sheet with an eagle-and-anchor seal she did not recognize and a line of bold type she had read three times standing in the drizzle while her neighbors read it beside her in silence:

*By order of the Provisional Authority: effective immediately, this district is designated for military requisition. All private records, correspondence, and documents of public interest shall be surrendered for cataloguing by the Historical Commission within ninety days.*

Ninety days. She had counted the words twice, the way she counted the stairs, the way she counted the seconds between darkness and light. Ninety days, and then soldiers would go door to door with inventory lists, and every private paper in this town — every letter, every deed, every birth record — would be carried off to be read by strangers, recopied by strangers, or burned by strangers, according to whatever story the strangers wished to tell about them afterward.

And somewhere behind a cedar chest, ninety days from surrender, lay the only true account of what had happened to these people.

Coral rose, banked the stove, and climbed back up into the lantern room without deciding to. The storm had reached its full voice now; the glass sang with it; below, the town was a handful of dying candles. She put her palm flat against the cold pane and held it there, as if she could press her strength through it and out across the water.

"Ninety days," she said aloud, and the wind took the words and gave nothing back.

Four seconds of dark. Four of light. Somewhere far out in the rain, riding the long green swells, a shape was coming toward the point that she could not yet see — but the sea had begun, quietly, to bring it in.
