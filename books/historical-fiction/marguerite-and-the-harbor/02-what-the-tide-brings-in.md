## 2. What the Tide Brings In

The storm had spent itself by morning, the way storms do in this harbor — violently, and then all at once, as though the sky had simply grown tired of its own temper. Coral Quintana stood at the base of the tower with her hands wrapped around a tin cup of chicory coffee, watching the sea give back what it had taken.

It always gave something back. Driftwood in pale gray bones. Fishing floats from ports she had never seen. Once, a ship's figurehead, a woman with her face worn smooth as an egg, which Marguerite had dragged up the beach and named Beatrice and kept propped against the oil room door for luck. The tide was a creditor that returned its debts in strange coin.

Coral walked the tideline the way she did every morning after a blow, her boots sinking into sand the color of wet ash, her eyes working the wrack line methodically. Left to right, then back again. It was the same discipline she brought to the lamp: a sweep, a check, a sweep. You did not look for any one thing. You looked for what was wrong.

What was wrong, this morning, lay half-buried at the far curve of the beach where the harbor bent toward the headland: a skiff, maybe eighteen feet, hull splintered along the starboard side like a walnut cracked by a careless hand. Navy gray, or had been. The paint was scoured down to raw wood in long pale stripes. A stenciled number clung to the transom, half legible beneath the salt.

She stood over it for a long moment, coffee cooling in her cup, and listened.

The skiff groaned as the retreating surf tugged at it, a sound almost like speech. Beneath it, fainter, another sound — wet and labored. Breathing.

He was wedged in the bow, or what remained of it, tangled in a coil of line and a scrap of canvas that might once have been a sail. A man, young-ish, though the sea had scrubbed the years off him and left something ageless — gray-faced, lips blue as mussel shells. His coat was military wool, dark and heavy and soaked through, and his arms were wrapped around a canvas satchel the way a drowning man wraps around a spar. He had not let go of it even now. Even unconscious, even half-dead, his knuckles were white around the strap.

Coral set down her cup on a rock and crouched.

"Hello," she said, in the tone she used for the lamp and for frightened animals and for Marguerite on the bad nights. Steady. Unhurried. "Can you hear me?"

Nothing. The breathing went on, shallow and rattling, with a small whistle at the top of each inhale that she did not like at all.

She looked at the skiff. She looked at the satchel. She looked up the beach, toward the town — the crooked line of roofs, the smoke rising thin and blue from the bakery chimney, the gulls wheeling over the fish market like a thought that would not leave. And she looked at the tower behind her, white and patient, its gallery railing dripping.

Regulation said she reported wrecks. Regulation said a great many things. Regulation had never once, in her experience, said anything useful.

"Right," Coral said, to the man, or to herself, or to the sea. "Up you come, then."

* * *

He weighed no more than a bundle of wet rope, which frightened her more than if he had been heavy. She got him up the beach in stages, dragging, resting, dragging again, the satchel strapped across his chest because she could not pry it loose and did not try. Twice she stopped and simply breathed, her breath pluming white in the washed-clean cold, and told herself the story she had already begun composing: *found him on the tideline, brought him in, meant to report it, weather wouldn't allow.* The story was mostly true. That was the best kind.

Marguerite met her at the oil room door with Beatrice clutched under one arm and her dark hair unbrushed and her eyes already wide.

"Is he dead?"

"No."

"Is he a soldier?"

Coral looked down at the man laid out on the oil room cot, at the ruined coat, at the satchel he still gripped. "He's a drowned rat," she said. "Fetch the kettle. And the salt."

Marguerite went, and came back, and hovered. She was eleven that winter and had the dock children's gift for appearing exactly when something was happening. Coral stripped the coat off him — a struggle, with the strap — and got him under blankets and set to rubbing warmth back into his hands, which were a mapmaker's hands, she noticed. Long-fingered, ink-stained at the cuticles even now, even after the sea. There was a smell under the salt and the wool: lampblack, and the sharp mineral tang of drafting ink.

The satchel she set on the high shelf, out of reach, and did not open. She told herself that too was only until the weather cleared.

* * *

He woke at dusk.

Coral knew it before she turned from the stove, the way you know a lamp has caught — a change in the quality of the air. The breathing had stopped rattling. When she turned, his eyes were open, gray as the sea that had delivered him, and fixed on her with a wariness she recognized. It was her own wariness. It was the look of someone counting exits.

"The skiff is done," she said. "Hull's stove in. Nothing to be salvaged but the wood."

He said nothing. His gaze went past her — to the satchel on the shelf. She watched him find it, watched the small muscle in his jaw tighten, and then, deliberately, watched him make himself look away. A disciplined man. A careful man. A man, she thought, who was used to being careful in rooms where carelessness meant more than a scolding.

"You've been asleep a day and a night," she said. "You took in half the harbor. Drink this." She held out the cup — broth, weak, with a spoonful of the good brandy in it that she kept for storms and grief.

He took it. His hands were still trembling, fine and constant, like the vibration of a struck string. He drank without thanking her, and she noted that too — not rudeness, she thought, but a man rationing himself, hoarding even his manners.

"You're on my island," she said, when the cup was empty. "The harbor light. I keep it. My name is Quintana. The girl is Marguerite, and she'll ask you a hundred questions, and you don't have to answer any of them."

"I—" His voice came out ruined, sanded down to a whisper. He cleared his throat and tried again. "I should report—"

"To whom?" Coral said, and let the question sit there in the lamplight between them.

The oil room was warm and close, all brass and iron and the sweet heavy smell of kerosene. Through the little salt-fogged window, the harbor lay dark, and above it the tower rose, and at the top of the tower the lamp turned its slow white wheel — *sweep, check, sweep* — out over a sea that had given her a man and a mystery in the same morning and asked nothing in return, which was, in Coral's long experience of the sea, the most suspicious thing it could have done.

"I'm a supply clerk," the man said at last.

Coral looked at the ink-stained hands. At the drafting smell that still clung to his skin beneath the brine. At the satchel on the shelf, canvas-stiff, military-stamped, guarded even in sleep.

"Of course you are," she said.

His name, when he finally gave it — and he gave it slowly, the way a man sets down something fragile on the edge of a table — was Voss. Elias Voss. He said it as though testing whether it still fit, and she had the odd, unbidden thought that he had been carrying that name a long way, and that it had gotten heavier with every mile.

* * *

Three days the weather held him there, as she had told herself it would. Three days of southerly gale, the bar a white chaos of broken water no sane boat would cross, and so there was no crossing, and no reporting, and no one to report to anyway, because the army launch that made its weekly circuit of the light did not come, and did not come, and did not come.

Voss slept. Then he didn't. By the second day he was on his feet, moving through the oil room with a convalescent's careful economy, and by the third he could not bear his own stillness. He mended what he could reach — a hinge, a cracked shutter, the wick-trimmer Marguerite had bent beyond saving. His hands wanted work the way other men's wanted drink. Coral watched him do it and said nothing, and learned him the way she learned weather: by small signs.

He flinched, very slightly, every time a boat crossed the harbor mouth.

He ate like a man who had known rationing that was not the polite civilian kind.

And once — she was trimming the lamp, and came down the spiral stair and found him standing at the oil room window with the satchel finally open in his hands, looking down at what was inside with an expression she had seen only twice before in her life: once on her husband's face, the winter before the sea took him, and once in her own mirror, the winter after.

She did not ask. He closed the satchel before she reached the bottom of the stair, and they drank their broth in silence, and the lamp turned overhead, *sweep, check, sweep*.

It was Marguerite who asked, of course. Children ask what adults are too careful to. She planted herself in front of him on the fourth evening, Beatrice the figurehead propped at her side like a chaperone, and said, "What's in the bag?"

"Marguerite," Coral began.

"Charts," Voss said.

Coral went still.

"Old ones," he said, to Marguerite, in a mild voice that did not match his eyes, which had come up and found Coral's across the room. "Charts of the coast. I was told to destroy them. The storm had other ideas."

"Why were you told to destroy them?" Marguerite asked, because she was eleven and merciless.

"Because," Voss said, "maps are a way of deciding what exists. And someone has been deciding."

The lamp turned. Outside, far down the harbor road, past the crooked roofs and the bakery smoke, Coral saw — or thought she saw, in the failing light — a truck. Army canvas, army engine, crawling the coast road toward the town, and stopping.

She told herself the weather would clear soon. She told herself he would be gone before anyone came asking.

She was already, she understood dimly, telling herself stories.
