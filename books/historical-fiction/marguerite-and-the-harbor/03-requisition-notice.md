## 3. Requisition Notice

The notice went up on a Tuesday, nailed to the bakery door with four brass tacks that caught the morning light like small, indifferent stars.

Coral saw it from the gallery of the lighthouse, where she had gone to scrape salt from the storm panes, and for a long moment she could not make out what the paper was. White, official, too clean for the crooked street it defaced — that was her first impression. The town's notices were usually written on butcher's paper in a fishmonger's hand, curled at the edges, gone soft with sea damp within a week. This one would not go soft. This one had been printed on a press somewhere far away, by people who had never smelled this harbor.

She climbed down the ninety-one steps with her heart going strangely quiet, the way it did before weather.

A knot of neighbors had already gathered at the bakery door. Old Marta Ferran stood closest, reading aloud in a slow voice, one finger tracing the lines as though she could hold the words down by touching them. Coral pressed into the crowd, smelling wet wool and woodsmoke, and read over a fisherman's shoulder.

*By order of the Provisional Military Authority:*

*Effective immediately, the township of Puerto Cielo and its harbor installations are requisitioned for strategic purposes. All civilian residents will vacate within ninety (90) days.*

*All private papers, records, correspondence, registers, and documents of any kind are subject to confiscation for review by the Historical Commission. Failure to surrender such materials constitutes obstruction of military governance.*

*Ninety days.*

Someone said it aloud, though it was printed there plain enough. Ninety days. A season. The length of time it took the lamp oil to turn over twice. Marta Ferran began to cry without any noise at all, tears simply arriving on her face like rain on glass, and her daughter put an arm around her and stared straight ahead at nothing, which was its own kind of crying.

Coral read the paragraph about papers three times. *Registers.* The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Every baptismal book, every ledger of debts at the chandlery, every letter in every drawer — and hers, hers most of all, hidden behind the false wall in the oil room, sixty-one names in her own careful hand, the only record anywhere that these people had existed, had lived here, had been scattered or drowned or taken. Sixty-one names the army did not know it wanted until now.

She thought: *they will not have them.*

She thought it the way you think a prayer, not knowing yet to whom.

* * *

They came through the streets that afternoon with clipboards.

Coral watched from her window above the oil store as two soldiers and a thin civilian man in a grey suit moved house to house along the harbor road. The soldiers carried inventory books bound in canvas; the civilian carried nothing but a fountain pen and an expression of mild, patient regret, as if he were a doctor delivering the same diagnosis all day and had grown gentle about it.

At the Delgado house they laid everything out on the front step. Coral could see it plainly across the road: the family photographs in their tin box, letters tied with string, Rosa Delgado's recipe book with its grease-softened pages, the shipping receipts old Tomás had kept for forty years because he believed — he had told Coral once, laughing — that a man's paper trail was his proof he'd been here. The soldier wrote each item in his book. The grey man glanced at some, initialed others. The photographs they set aside in a separate pile. Coral could not tell whether that pile meant keeping or burning.

Rosa Delgado stood in her doorway with her arms folded, watching strangers count her life into categories. She did not weep. When the soldiers finished, she gathered the string-tied letters back off the step herself and held them against her chest, and the grey man made a small note, and Coral understood that even grief was being inventoried now.

Marguerite appeared beside her at the window, silent as a gull riding a swell. "What are they writing?"

"Lists."

"Of what?"

"Everything." Coral kept her voice level. "Everything people own. Everything people remember."

The girl considered this with the terrible clarity of eleven-year-olds. "You can't list remembering. There isn't room in a book."

"No," Coral said. "There isn't."

But there was room in a ledger. There had always been room in hers. That night, after Marguerite slept in the oil room with the lamp's slow heartbeat above her, Coral took down the loose brick behind the shelving and sat on the cold floor with the book open on her knees, reading names by shielded candlelight. Not writing. Reading. As if the names might already be leaving her, as if she needed to check they were still there, the way you check your pockets in a dream.

Alonzo Quintana. Her husband's name, first of all, though he needed no ledger; the sea had kept him, and she kept him too, in the place behind her ribs where grief had worn smooth as beach glass.

Sixty-one entries. Some were only names and dates of departure. Some held more — *the Vidal boy, taken for labor, mother still waiting at the chapel steps every Sunday.* Some held almost nothing, because there had been almost nothing left to record: *family gone. No word. House empty since March.*

She closed the book and did not return it to the wall at once. She sat holding it, feeling its weight, which was nothing — less than a kilo of paper and string binding — and everything, because weight is not always a property of objects.

Ninety days. She began, without deciding to, to count.

* * *

In the oil room, Elias Voss lay awake and listened to Coral move below, and knew exactly what she was doing, because he had done it himself once, in another country, with other names.

He had been the one who drew up the seizure schedules. He knew the arithmetic of requisition the way sailors know tides: so many houses per company, so many days per district, so many families processed per clerk per hour. He knew that the Historical Commission was not interested in truth. Truth was bulky. Truth contradicted itself, contained awkward dates, named men who were now heroes for things they should hang for. The Commission wanted material — raw, disordered, incriminating in the wrong direction — which it would recopy into a clean authorized version and then dispose of. He had seen warehouses of confiscated paper. He had signed the disposal orders.

And he knew something else, lying there in the dark smelling of lamp oil and salt, that Coral Quintana did not yet know: that lighthouse keepers' logs fell under the same regulation. That her daily records — wind, ships passed, supplies, personnel — were themselves confiscatable documents, and that a keeper's log noting an extra mouth fed, an extra man sheltered, was a confession in ink.

He heard her climb back up. Heard the brick settle. Heard Marguerite turn over in her sleep and murmur something — a name, maybe, or just a child's sound against the dark.

*Three of us,* he thought. *One of whom I brought here like weather.*

He had told himself, on the skiff, in the water, on her floor wrapped in blankets while she pretended not to study his face: *only until the weather clears.* But the weather had cleared days ago and he had not gone, and she had not asked him to, and both of them were pretending not to notice that a story was being built around him, plank by plank, the way a town builds itself.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would tell her what he knew about the Commission. What happened to papers after they were surrendered. She would not thank him for it. It would frighten her, and fear was the last thing a woman holding a lighthouse alone needed more of.

He told himself he would tell her anyway. Whether that was penance or cowardice wearing penance's coat, he could not yet say.

* * *

On the fourth day after the notice, they came to the lighthouse.

Not the grey man — a lieutenant, young, precise, with a clipboard and two soldiers who stood at the base of the tower looking up with the uneasiness men feel beneath tall things. Coral met him at the door with her hands wiped on her apron and her face arranged into the blank courtesy the times required.

"Lieutenant Sorel. Historical Commission preliminary survey." He did not offer his hand. "Routine. We'll need to see the working areas."

"The light doesn't stop for surveys," Coral said. "It's lit at dusk whatever happens."

"It will be lit until the evacuation," Sorel agreed, as if granting her something. "After that the navy assumes operation." He stepped past her into the oil store, and Coral followed him with her pulse tapping at her throat like a moth at glass.

He was thorough. He counted the drums of oil, noted the winch, examined the ventilation flue. His pen scratched in short, tidy strokes. At the shelving behind which the loose brick sat, he paused, ran one finger along the boards, and looked at the wall for perhaps three seconds longer than a wall deserved.

"Storage behind here?"

"Cellar. Flooded half the year. Nothing worth listing."

His pen hovered. Then he wrote something — one line, unhurried — and moved on. At the door he turned. "The keeper's logs will be collected with the rest of the commission materials. Daily records, personnel notations, correspondence. Standard."

"Standard," Coral repeated.

"Ninety days," Lieutenant Sorel said, and looked at her the way the brass tacks had caught the light: precisely, indifferently. "Less, if the schedule advances. It often advances."

He went down the path and the soldiers followed, and Coral stood in her doorway listening to their boots fade along the harbor road, and inside her the dread that had been a fog all week condensed, drop by drop, into something harder and clearer than dread.

*It often advances.*

That evening she trimmed the wick, lit the lamp, and let its beam swing out over the darkening water. Below, Marguerite was setting three cups on the table instead of two, because she had stopped asking whether the third cup was for anyone and simply set it, and Elias came up the stair as he always did now, drawn by lamplight like everything else on this coast.

Coral waited until he was seated. Then she said, quietly, in the voice she used for reporting weather:

"They're coming back for the papers sooner than the notice says. And when they come, I intend to have nothing here worth finding."

Elias set down his cup. In his eyes was the look of a man recognizing, with relief and terror in equal measure, the exact shape of the work ahead.

"Then," he said, "we'd best begin tonight."
