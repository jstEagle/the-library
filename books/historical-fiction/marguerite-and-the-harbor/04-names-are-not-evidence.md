## 4. Names Are Not Evidence

The oil room smelled of brass polish and tallow, of the sea salt that crept into everything on this coast and would not be scrubbed out. Elias Voss sat with his back against the cold stone wall, a blanket around his shoulders that smelled of someone else — of lamp smoke, of a woman's careful hands — and watched Coral Quintana decide what to do with him.

She had not decided yet. He could see it in the way she held the candle, angled so its light fell on his face but hers stayed in shadow. A soldier's trick, or an interrogator's. She was neither, and yet she had the instinct for it. Three days she had kept him here, fed him fish stew and bitter tea, changed the dressing on his shoulder where the reef had opened him like an envelope. Three days of silence broken only by the necessary words: *eat, drink, sleep, your fever is breaking.* And now, at last, she had brought the candle down to his level and sat across from him on an upturned crate, and he understood that the weather outside no longer mattered. The question inside this room did.

"You were going to burn them," she said. It was not a question either.

"The charts. Yes."

"And you didn't."

"I couldn't." His voice came out rougher than he intended, scraped raw by seawater and three days of lying still while guilt did its own slow work on him. "They were under seal. Burning them is a court offense. Letting them drown is only negligence." He almost smiled at the arithmetic of it, the way a man learns to think inside a machine built to crush him. "I told myself the sea could have them. The sea keeps better secrets than fire does. Fire leaves ash, and ash can be read."

Coral said nothing. The candle flame leaned in some draft from beneath the door, righted itself. Somewhere above them the great lens turned through its long patient arc, sweeping light across water that wanted none of it, and Elias felt again the strange vertigo of being inside something that existed purely to save strangers.

"The commission," Coral said. "The historical commission. You've heard what they're doing."

He had. He had heard it in the officers' mess, over brandy that tasted of victory, before he ever understood what he was hearing. He had heard it from men who spoke of paper the way other men spoke of territory.

"I've seen what they're doing," he corrected her. "There's a difference."

"Then tell me."

* * *

He made himself comfortable against the wall first, because the telling would take time and because comfort was a kind of cowardice he could still afford. Outside, the storm had spent itself; what remained was a thin persistent rain ticking against the glass, the harbor breathing below.

"There's a building in the colonial capital," he began, "three floors, limestone, unremarkable. Before the war it stored customs records. Now it stores the war itself. Every document seized from every town on this coast goes there. Birth registers. Property deeds. Parish books. Letters found in desks. Diaries found in trunks. Photographs." He paused. "Ledgers."

Coral's hands went still where they rested in her lap. He had noticed those hands before — capable, cracked at the knuckles, always half-curled as though around something she was protecting. Now he understood what they protected.

"Go on," she said, and her voice gave away nothing, which told him everything.

"The commission has a mandate. I've read it — it's a beautiful piece of writing, if you like beautiful lies. *To preserve the historical record of territories brought under orderly administration.* That's the phrase. Orderly administration. What it means is that history is now a supply line, and like any supply line it gets inspected before it reaches the front. Nothing moves into the official record unless it serves the record they've already decided to write."

"And what record is that?"

"That the war came here gently." He heard how the words sounded in the lamplit room, and hated them, and made himself finish. "That the towns were evacuated in good order, with gratitude. That families were relocated, not scattered. That the dead are few and accounted for and died cleanly, at the front, facing the correct direction. There is no room in that record for boats lost with all hands. For men taken off the docks at dawn. For women who—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "For anyone who was simply gone one morning, without paperwork, without explanation, without a name anyone official thought worth entering anywhere."

The rain ticked on. Above them, the lens swept its arc.

"What happens to what they seize?" Coral asked.

"Three fates." He raised fingers, cartographer's fingers, ink-stained at the cuticle even now, even after the sea. "The first fate is recopying. A clerk sits down with the original and copies it — but only the parts that fit. A parish register loses its marginalia. A merchant's letters lose the pages that mention the requisitions. Names get spelled differently, dates adjusted, causes of death improved. The copy enters the archive. It looks authentic. In fifty years no historian will be able to tell the difference, because the copy will be the only thing left."

"And the second?"

"Destruction. Anything too contradictory to be edited is burned. Whole shipments of it. I saw the incinerator manifests once, when I was still—" He caught himself. "When I still worked in the mapping office. Tonnage, Coral. They weighed what they burned. They recorded the weight in their ledgers. Somewhere in the capital there is a very precise figure for how much truth this town contained, measured in kilograms."

Her breath left her slowly through her nose. When she spoke, her voice was level in the way ice is level. "And the third fate?"

"The third is the worst, and it's why I'm telling you this instead of keeping my mouth shut and waiting out my fever." He leaned forward, and the blanket slid from his shoulders, and he let it. "Some documents aren't copied and aren't destroyed. They're *used*. The commission doesn't just sanitize the past — it manufactures it. They take seized papers and forge new ones from them. A family's own letters become evidence that the family requested relocation. A town's own registers become proof the population was declining anyway, that the evacuation merely hastened an inevitable emptying. Your own handwriting, turned against you posthumously. The dead co-authoring the lie about themselves."

* * *

Silence filled the oil room then, the particular silence of a person recalculating everything. Elias watched Coral Quintana do it — watched her eyes move to the stairwell, up toward whatever she kept above, whatever she had been guarding since the day she pulled him out of the surf with more urgency than a drowning stranger warranted.

He had known since the second day. A man learns to read houses the way he reads terrain: where the sight lines run, where the walls are thicker than they should be, where a woman's glance snags and returns, snagged and returned twice now to the same stretch of shelving behind the oil drums. He had said nothing. Saying nothing had been his payment for the fish stew and the bandages and the blanket that smelled of care.

But the alliance he needed required more than silence now. It required trade.

"There's a ledger," he said quietly.

Her whole body changed — not dramatically, nothing a stranger would catch, but he was not a stranger anymore, not after three days in her keeping. Her shoulders drew in. Her chin lifted a fraction. The candle flame reflected doubled in her eyes.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Yes, you do. And I'm going to tell you why you should tell me, and then you're going to decide whether to throw me back into the sea." He held her gaze. "Because if that ledger stays in this lighthouse when the requisition teams come through, it has three fates, and none of them is survival. Recopied, it becomes a list of the officially displaced — which means it becomes a list of people the administration can claim never resisted, never suffered, never mattered. Destroyed, it becomes ash with a weight in kilograms. Or worse — used. Do you understand? A private record of the disappeared, in official hands, becomes raw material. They will take your names and build their fiction out of them."

"You don't know what's in it."

"No," he admitted. "And I haven't looked, and I won't look, and that's part of what I'm offering. But I know what it must be, because I know this coast, and I know the pattern. I drew the pattern." The confession climbed his throat like something drowned coming up. "I was a cartographer, Coral. Do you know what a cartographer does in a war like this? He doesn't draw maps of where things are. He draws maps of where things *were*. Villages marked in pale gray ink, annotated: *sparsely inhabited, low economic significance, clearance recommended.* I wrote *clearance recommended* beside towns full of names. I put the ink on the paper that sent the trucks."

The words hung between them. He had never said them aloud. Saying them to the sea, to the storm, to God — any of those would have been easier. Saying them to this woman, whose harbor his maps had surely touched, whose neighbors' villages were perhaps gray annotations in his own hand —

"You knew," she said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing: confirming.

"I knew. And when I finally couldn't carry it anymore, I took sealed charts from the office and walked into the water with them, and I told myself that was penance. It wasn't. Penance would have been staying and burning the archive and dying for it. Stealing charts and running is just desertion wearing penance's coat." He laughed, short and joyless. "So here is my offer, and you may weigh it however you like. I know the requisition bureaucracy from the inside. I know the inventory forms, the seizure protocols, the inspection schedules. I know which clerks skim, which captains pad their reports, which storage depots burn early to hide shortages. I know that the commission's teams work from checklists, and checklists can be fed. I can help you keep whatever you're keeping out of their hands — genuinely help, not advise from a hammock. In exchange, you never speak my name to any authority, never mention that the drowned cartographer washed up alive, never say the word *deserter* in any room where it could reach ears that matter. Silence for silence. Your ledger for my name."

* * *

Coral rose. She crossed to the narrow window slit and stood looking out at the harbor, where the last storm-light was failing over black water. Elias waited. He had learned to wait in offices where careers were decided by silences; he could wait here, where lives were.

When she turned back, her face was unreadable as chart-paper, blank where the markings go.

"If I agree," she said, "you'll tell me everything. How the inspections work. What they search for. How documents are flagged. All of it, whether it flatters your expertise or shames you."

"All of it."

"And you'll work. My lamp needs trimming, my stores need counting, my roof leaks over the oil room. A deserter who eats without working is just a guest, and I can't afford guests."

"I'll work."

"And Marguerite—" Something moved through her voice at the girl's name, soft and fierce at once. "There's a child sleeping upstairs who has lost everyone the war could take. If your presence brings danger down on this house, you will leave it. Whatever the weather. Whatever your wound. I will row you out myself."

"Understood."

She studied him a moment longer, weighing him as he imagined she weighed everything — cargo, tide, risk. Then she nodded, once, a captain's nod, and turned toward the stair.

At the foot of it she stopped. She did not look back, but her voice reached him clear and low across the oil-smelling dark:

"The ledger holds forty-one names. Children first. Tomorrow night we start copying it, all three of us — because you're right that paper dies, Voss, and I want these names somewhere paper can't reach."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of small feet shifting in sleep, and the lamp turning, and turning, and turning.
