## 5. The Copying Nights

### Coral

The lamp turned above us, and beneath it we made our counterfeit.

Coral had learned the arithmetic of the light years ago — how much oil the night demanded, how many turns of the clockwork, how long a wick could be trusted before it guttered and lied. She knew now to trim it leaner than the manual advised, to bank the surplus in the dark of the oil room where three people bent over a table that had never before held anything but logbooks and brass fittings and the small honest instruments of keeping watch.

The ledger lay open between them. The copy — a school exercise book Marguerite had fetched from the wreck of the chandlery, its pages smelling of mildew and salt — waited beside it, blank as a held breath.

"Again," Elias said softly. "From the top of the page."

Coral recited. *"Almeida, Tomas. Fisherman. Taken by patrol boat, the fourteenth of March. Wife, Serafina; daughter, Lucia, age six. Last seen at the north quay."*

Elias closed his eyes and repeated it back, word for word, his voice flat as a man reading coordinates. He had told her once that cartographers memorized coastlines this way — not the shapes on paper but the paper itself, so that if every chart burned, the country would still exist inside their skulls. She had not known whether he was comforting her or confessing something. With Elias it was often both.

Outside, the storm season pressed its shoulder against the glass. Rain came sideways off the harbor, and the whole tower shuddered like an animal dreaming. Coral found she was no longer afraid of storms. Storms were honest. They announced themselves.

It was the quiet nights she feared now — the nights when a launch might slide into the harbor without running lights, and boots might come up the spiral stair without calling out.

"Your turn," she said to Marguerite.

The girl sat with the pen already in her fist, upright as a small official. She had appointed herself to the children. No one had asked her to; she had simply taken up the duty one evening, in the tone she used for things that were not open to discussion, which she had learned, Coral suspected, from watching her.

"The children's entries are mine," Marguerite said now, as if Coral had argued. "You write the grown-ups too small. Their letters hide."

"They don't hide, they're just—"

"They hide." She bent over the page. Her hand moved slowly, each letter built rather than written, but it did not tremble. *"Costa, Inês, age nine. Sent to the mission at Santa Clara. Mother's whereabouts unknown. She took the blue ribbon from her hair and gave it to me before the truck came."*

Coral looked away, out at the revolving beam, and let the dark have her face for a moment.

She had not known about the ribbon. There were things in the ledger's margins she had never asked Marguerite to say aloud, and the girl had begun, night by night, to put them down herself — small salvages no commission would ever think to confiscate because they weighed nothing at all.

"You write them well," Coral said finally.

"I write them true." Marguerite blew on the ink. "That's different."

* * *

### Elias

Elias counted the hours by the oil.

He had served under men who measured everything — rations, rounds, the exact distance at which a village stopped being a village and became a grid square awaiting erasure — and he found he had brought the habit with him like a disease he couldn't sweat out. Each night he calculated: two liters for the lamp overhead, a half-liter for the table candle, and if they copied until the third hour, the stores would last eleven days. Twelve if the weather stayed foul and the patrols stayed in their barracks, warm and stupid.

The weather had no such intentions. On the fifth night the rain broke open and the harbor went silver, and through the lantern glass Elias saw the patrol launch come around the breakwater with its searchlight sweeping the water like a slow accusing finger.

"Down," he said. "Both of you. Now."

They had drilled it enough that it happened without speech — Coral snuffing the candle, Marguerite sliding the ledger and the copy into the oil room's false recess behind the spare wicks, all three of them going still as the searchlight climbed the tower and laid its white bar across the lantern room, across the table, across the salt-streaked glass, and moved on.

In the dark, Coral's hand found his sleeve. Not his hand — his sleeve, a precise and deliberate distance short of his hand. He understood. There were distances a woman maintained even while standing in the dark with a man she had chosen, because choosing him was still a thing she did nightly, against her own ledger of prudence.

"They're counting," Marguerite whispered from the floor. "The boat passes at ten and at two. Tonight it's early."

Elias felt the old professional cold move through him, the cartographer's clarity that saw patterns in other men's movements. "Early means they've noticed something. Late hours, extra oil drawn from the stores. Someone reads the requisition sheets." He almost smiled at the irony — that he of all people should be undone by paperwork. "We should shorten the nights. Copy faster, sleep less."

"Sleep less," Coral repeated, in the voice she used for weather reports. "And then?"

"And then we finish. And then there is nothing here to find but a lighthouse and a girl who lives in the oil room." He heard Marguerite shift at that — she hated the fiction of being only a ward, a charity case, a nameless orphan off the docks — and he added, gently, "Who writes the children better than any of us."

The searchlight swept past again, dimmer, seaward-bound. When Coral relit the candle the flame stood very straight, as if it too had been holding its breath.

"We go until dawn," she said. "All of us."

* * *

### Marguerite

Marguerite kept her own list, in her head, of everyone the book remembered.

There were forty-one children. She said them to herself while she pumped water, while she trimmed wicks, while she walked the gallery rail in the grey mornings pretending to count gulls. *Almeida, Lucia. Costa, Inês. Duarte, Rui, age four, who cried for a dog named Bexiga.* Forty-one names was a rosary, and she said it the way the mission women had taught her to say prayers before she'd decided the mission women didn't know everything.

Tonight — the seventh copying night, though she thought of them all as one long night with meals in it — she wrote the entry for the Ferreira boys and found her eyes doing something traitorous, blurring the lines of the page.

"You're tired," Coral said.

"I'm not."

"You're smudging."

Marguerite scrubbed her face with her wrist and kept writing. It mattered that the letters come out clean. Somewhere in a foreign city, in a building full of drawers, someone would someday open this exercise book, and they would know a nine-year-old had written these pages, and they would understand that a nine-year-old had been the one left to do it. That was the whole point. Grown-ups wrote history in big important handwriting, and then the big important handwriting got confiscated, and what was left was a child's pencil pressing hard enough to go through the paper.

"Elias," she said, because the question had been sitting in her chest for three days, "when they burn things — papers, I mean. Do they burn all of them? Even the ones in pencil?"

He looked at her over the candle flame. He had a way of looking at her questions as if they were surveyor's marks, something to be precisely located before answered.

"Pencil burns the same as ink," he said.

"Then we memorize harder." She tapped her own temple. "This doesn't burn."

Something crossed his face then — not a smile, something older and more damaged than a smile — and he reached over and turned her exercise book toward himself and read the entry she'd just finished. The Ferreira boys. Ages six and eight. Boarded onto the transport with their mother, destination unrecorded.

"You press hard," he said quietly. "Good. Press harder."

* * *

### Coral

On the ninth night, the knock came at the door below.

Three knocks, spaced like a metronome — army time, not fisherman time. Coral closed the ledger with a sound like a struck match and was moving before her heart finished its first hard beat. Marguerite took the books. Elias took the stairs two at a time to the lantern room, to be discovered legitimately at his post, a keeper's assistant minding the light, because a deserter caught hiding was a prisoner and a man caught working was nearly a citizen.

Coral opened the door on a young corporal with rain sheeting off his cap brim and a lantern held high, its light deliberately rude, swinging across her face, past her, into the room.

"Burning a deal of oil up here, mistress. Stores show double draw this fortnight."

"Lamp's my responsibility, Corporal. If she drinks, she drinks. Would you have the ships run aground instead?"

He wasn't listening. His light had found the table, the scattered logbooks, the school exercise book lying open — thank God, thank God — to a blank page. Marguerite stood beside it with a slate in her hands, chalk in her fingers, wearing the expression Coral had come to think of as the girl's armor: blank, obedient, faintly simple.

"Doing sums," Marguerite said, before he asked. "I'm behind. School shut when the soldiers came." A pause, exactly long enough. "When you came."

The corporal looked at her a moment too long. Young men always believed they were the first to suspect a thing; it was their oldest vanity. "Late hours for sums."

"She sleeps badly since her parents," Coral said, letting grief do the work that explanation couldn't. "Light keeps her calm. Light keeps everyone calm. That's what it's for."

He wrote something in a pocket book — she watched the pencil move and could not read it upside down — and touched his cap and went down into the rain, and his lantern shrank along the causeway until the dark took it whole.

Coral shut the door and stood with her forehead against the wet wood.

Above her, faintly, she heard Elias resume the recitation, his voice coming down the stairwell like something lowered on a rope: *"Almeida, Tomas. Fisherman. Taken the fourteenth of March—"*

Marguerite appeared at her elbow, slate hugged to her chest, and for once the armor was gone and there was only a thin girl in a borrowed sweater, asking the question Coral had been refusing to ask herself.

"How many nights do we have left?"

Coral listened to the voice above, steady as a sounding line, naming the lost one by one into the dark.

"As many as we need," she said, and knew, even as she said it, that the corporal's little pocket book had just taken that choice out of her hands.
