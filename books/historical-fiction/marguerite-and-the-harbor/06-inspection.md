## 6. Inspection

The morning the soldiers came up the cliff path, the gulls went silent first.

Coral noticed it from the gallery — that sudden hush, as if the whole harbor had drawn a breath and held it. She had learned to read the birds the way other women read weather or husbands' faces: they scattered before a squall, wheeled before a shark's fin broke the water, and fell quiet before men with rifles. She set down her polishing cloth and looked south along the coast road, where the fog was lifting off the headland in ragged sheets, and saw them coming. Six of them, gray-green against the gray-green grass, boots striking the stone in a rhythm too disciplined for the wind to break.

Behind her, in the tower's warm heart, Marguerite was singing under her breath — a dockside song about a girl who married a fisherman and drowned him for his boat — and the sound of it was so ordinary, so stubbornly alive, that Coral stood one moment longer than she should have, simply listening, knowing the song would have to stop.

"Marguerite," she called down the stairwell. "Company."

The singing cut off. A pause. Then small feet on iron steps, quick as rain.

"The sugar?" the girl whispered when she reached the gallery, eyes already wide and working.

"Not yet. Maybe never. Go on in and mend something. Look busy and bored." Coral smoothed the girl's hair once — an indulgence she could not afford but took anyway — and sent her down into the lamp room, where a torn wick-trimming apron waited on the bench like a prop in a play neither of them had rehearsed.

By the time the knock came — three blows, official, knuckles rather than fists — Coral had arranged herself at the door with a ledger of lamp-oil tallies open on the table beside her, the picture of a keeper interrupted at honest work. The true ledger lay behind the false wall in the oil store, beneath forty years of dust and two hundred pounds of sealed drums, and she made herself think of nothing at all when she heard the knock, because thinking has a smell, she believed, and fear can be read on a face like weather on water.

Captain Reyes stood at the center of the six, his cap dry despite the mist, which told her he had ridden most of the way and walked only the last stretch for effect. He was younger than his rank suggested, with a face like a well-kept ledger itself — balanced, ruled, every entry legible. His eyes went past her shoulder into the tower without asking leave.

"Mistress Quintana." He did not offer his hand. "Requisition Order Forty-One extends to all navigational installations. I'm here to inspect the light, your stores, and your papers."

"My papers are oil tallies and tide tables," Coral said. "You're welcome to both. They're duller than sermons."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile; a notation. He stepped inside without invitation, and the soldiers fanned out through the lower rooms with the unhurried thoroughness of men who had done this a hundred times and would do it a hundred more. They were polite about it. That was the worst part. One lifted the lid of her flour crock and set it back exactly as he'd found it. Another ran a finger along the mantel and examined the dust. A third opened the cupboard where Marguerite's three dresses hung, each one mended past mending, and looked at them for a long time without touching.

In the lamp room, Marguerite sat bent over the apron, needle moving, and did not look up when the captain entered. Coral watched him watch her — watched him take in the girl's thin wrists, the dock-bought shoes two sizes too large, the fact of a child in a lighthouse where no child was registered.

"You keep an orphan," Reyes said. It was not a question.

"I keep a helper. Her mother drowned two winters back. There was nowhere else." Coral kept her voice level, kept her hands folded. "Half this harbor is raising somebody else's children now. The war saw to that."

"The war sees to everything," Reyes agreed mildly. "That is rather the commission's point."

* * *

They worked outward from the living quarters toward the stores, and Coral followed them the way one follows a slow tide — unable to stop it, able only to note what it takes. In the oil store, the air was thick and sweet with kerosene, and the soldiers moved the drums aside with practiced hands while Captain Reyes stood in the doorway consulting a clipboard.

"False walls are common in smuggling country," he remarked, not looking up. "This coast has a long memory for contraband."

"This coast has a long memory for everything," Coral said. "It's what we're known for."

One of the soldiers rapped along the north wall — the wall — and Coral felt each knock land in her chest like a fist on a door. Rap. Rap. Rap. Behind that plaster and lathe lay a space two feet deep, and in that space, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay four years of names. The Quintana ledger. Every family the war had scattered, every boat that hadn't come home, every child born in exile and entered in her careful hand so that somewhere, someday, someone might know they had existed. She had written the last entry only three nights ago, by candle stub, while Elias held the light and Marguerite slept with her cheek on the table among the spare copies.

Rap. Rap.

The soldier paused at a spot where the plaster sounded different — Coral could hear it herself now, a faint hollowness, a note out of tune — and turned to look at the captain.

And it was Marguerite who saved them. Later Coral would turn the moment over a hundred times, trying to understand how a child of eleven had seen the geometry of danger faster than either adult in the room. But there in the doorway, the girl appeared with the apron still in her hands and her face arranged into the particular sullenness of children caught doing wrong, and said:

"It's the sugar."

Every head turned. Reyes raised an eyebrow.

Marguerite scuffed her enormous shoe against the floor. "I hid it. Sugar's worth more than money now, and Mrs. Ferro at the landing gives me bread for it. Coral doesn't know how much I've got. Please don't take it, it's mine, I earned it—" and her voice cracked on cue, a genuine crack, because underneath the performance was a real child who really had bartered sugar through a war, and the truth underneath the lie rang so clean that no one thought to doubt it.

The soldier looked at the captain. The captain looked at Coral, who let her mouth fall open in the shape of a woman betrayed by a thieving child, and said, "Marguerite," in a voice of wounded dignity, and the scene assembled itself around them like weather.

Reyes crossed to the wall himself. He rapped twice at the hollow spot, and Coral's heart counted the seconds between the sounds — one, two, three, four — each one a name, each name a grave or a life depending on what happened next. Then he nodded to the soldier, who pried loose a section of lathe with the flat of his bayonet.

Sugar came pouring out. Eleven pounds of it, in paper bags gone soft with the damp, hidden in a cavity barely deep enough to hold them — because Elias, God keep him, had built the false wall shallow, hiding the deep cavity behind a second skin of brick and mortar that even now the bayonet scraped without finding. White crystals spilled across the floorboards, glittering in the lamplight like something precious, and Marguerite snatched at the bags with genuine outrage, and two of the soldiers laughed, and the tension in the room broke like a cloud passing off the sun.

"Confiscated," Reyes said, but he said it lightly, waving off the girl's protest. "War tax on hoarding. You'll be compensated in scrip." He wrote something on his clipboard — Coral could not see what — and then he did the thing she would remember longest: he crouched down to Marguerite's height, so their eyes were level, and studied her face with the patience of a man reading a chart.

"You're quick," he told her. "Quick girls do well in the new order. There will be schools. Records. Everything written down properly, so no one has to hide anything ever again." He straightened, brushed the plaster dust from his knee, and smiled at Coral. It was a courteous smile, a correct smile, and it chilled her more than any threat could have. "Thank you for your cooperation, Mistress Quintana. We'll want a full inventory of your personal papers within the week. Diaries, correspondence, household accounts. The commission is thorough."

"And if I have nothing worth reading?"

"Then you'll have nothing to worry about." He settled his cap. At the door he paused, half-turned, and added, almost as an afterthought: "Your lamp burns late, Keeper. My men have noticed. Late lamps make people curious, and curious people write reports." He tapped the clipboard once with two fingers, a small dry sound like a lock turning. "I'd hate for curiosity to complicate an otherwise orderly requisition."

Then they were gone, down the cliff path, six gray figures dissolving into the fog, and the gulls found their voices again all at once, screaming, as if they had been holding the scream in reserve.

* * *

Coral stood in the doorway until the last of them vanished. Then she closed the door, put her back against it, and discovered that her knees had opinions of their own. She slid down to the floor — undignified, unkeeperly — and Marguerite came and sat beside her among the spilled sugar, and for a while neither of them spoke. The lamp above them turned its slow eternal circle, sweeping its beam across the water and around again, faithful as a heartbeat.

"You lied beautifully," Coral said at last.

"I didn't lie." Marguerite picked up a bag of sugar and hugged it to her chest. "It's my sugar. I just... pointed you at the right truth."

Coral laughed — a short, startled sound, half sob — and pulled the girl against her side. Above them the beam swept round. Sweep, dark, sweep, dark. Somewhere out in that darkness Elias was walking back from the signal station where he'd spent the night copying shipping schedules, and he would come up the path whistling, unaware, and she would have to tell him.

She rehearsed the words while the beam went round. *He knows about the lamp hours. He wrote something down. He looked at her like she was a file to be opened later.*

When Elias came, an hour later, salt-stained and tired, Coral met him at the door and said it plainly, because there was no gentle way left. He listened without moving, and she watched the color leave his face by degrees, the way color leaves the sky after sunset.

"A full inventory within the week," he repeated. "Personal papers. That's not routine, Coral. Routine confiscation waits for the deadline. This is a targeted inquiry." He looked past her, up the spiral stair, toward the oil store and the wall behind the wall, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to the flat calm she had learned meant the opposite of calm. "We don't have weeks anymore. We may not have days. We start copying tonight — everything, all of it — and we find a boat."

Out in the harbor, far below, a navy launch cut a white line across the darkening water, patrolling, counting, writing everything down.
