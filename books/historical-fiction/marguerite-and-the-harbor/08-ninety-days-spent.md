## 8. Ninety Days, Spent

The boots did not come for the lighthouse first.

Coral heard them scatter through the town below like rain finding every gutter at once — doors hammered, dogs set barking, a woman's voice rising and cut short. From the gallery she could see lanterns moving in the streets, dozens of them, swinging in the grey pre-dawn like fireflies caught in a net. The harbor lay black and heaving under the last of the storm, and against it the troop transport sat at anchor with its running lights burning, patient as a spider.

Marguerite had not been at Beatriz's. She had been found instead on the harbor road, walking home through the rain with her arms crossed tight over her chest, and she had climbed the tower stairs ahead of them both without a word, and she had not spoken since. Now she stood in the oil room with her back against the false wall — their wall, the one that hid nothing anymore because there was nothing left behind it but the smell of old tallow — watching Coral's face the way she watched the horizon for sails.

"They're early," she said finally. It was the first thing she'd said in an hour.

"Reyes moved the date." Elias stood at the window with his shoulders rigid, reading the streets below the way he once read coastlines. "They always do it early. Catch people asleep, catch papers unburned. It's doctrine." He said the word *doctrine* like something he was spitting out of his mouth.

Coral pulled the oilskin coat from its hook. Her hands were steady. That surprised her; she had expected them to shake, had braced all night against the trembling, and now that the moment had arrived her body had gone quiet as deep water. Two winters ago she had learned what it was to stand in a storm and do the next thing, and then the next thing, and then the next thing after that, until the storm passed or she drowned. This was only another storm. It would pass, or it wouldn't.

"Then we do it now," she said. "While they're still in the town. The fisherman sails with the tide."

* * *

Tomas Ferreira kept his boat at the far end of the quay, past the customs shed, in the one stretch of water the army hadn't bothered to guard because no soldier believed anything worth taking came out of a fishing skiff. Coral went down through the gullies behind the salt houses with Marguerite's hand locked in hers and the package wrapped in sailcloth strapped flat against her ribs beneath the coat. Elias walked twenty paces back, hat low, moving like a man who had spent years learning how not to be looked at.

The tide was going out. It always seemed to Coral that the sea took things away at this hour deliberately, showing her exactly how it was done.

Tomas was already aboard, coiling line in the dark. He was sixty if he was a day, with hands like cured rope and a face the weather had long since stopped bothering to write on. He looked up when they came down the ladder, and he did not ask questions. He had been told three nights ago, in this same darkness, and he had only nodded and said, *The archive at Puerto Libre has a stone cellar and a priest who owes me money.* That was the whole of it. That was enough.

"Below the floorboards under the ice box," he said now. "If they board me, they'll find fish and a Bible and an old man who stinks of herring."

"And if they take you to Puerto Libre anyway?"

"Then I'll deliver it myself and drink your health on their coin." He almost smiled. Almost. His eyes went past her to Marguerite, small and pale in the grey light, and softened. "You keep hold of her, señora. Boats can be replaced."

Coral unwrapped the sailcloth.

She had sewn the copied ledger into oilskin herself, three nights running, while Elias read the names aloud and Marguerite repeated them back like a rosary — every family, every boat lost, every child born in the war years and entered in Coral's careful hand, forty-one pages in duplicate, the ink still faintly smelling of the lamp that had lit it. The original lay upstairs in her desk drawer where it had lain for six years, and by noon it would be in a soldier's crate, and after that in a commission warehouse, and after that — Elias had been clear about this, gentle even, which made it worse — after that it would likely be nothing at all. Recopied. Corrected. Made to say what the victors needed their history to say.

But this copy would cross the water. This copy would sit in a stone cellar under a foreign archive with a catalog number and a lock, and someday a historian with clean hands and a stubborn heart would open it, and the names would speak.

She held it out to Tomas.

Her arms knew the weight of it. Six years of lamplight, of listening at doorways, of writing down what the tide brought in and what it took away. Her husband was in those pages — not by name, but in the entry for the boat that never came home, two widows listed, one orphan girl taken in by the lighthouse keeper. Marguerite was in those pages. Coral had written her in herself: *MARGUERITE, age unknown, parents lost with the San Cristóbal, remaining: yes.*

"Señora." Tomas took it with both hands, the way men took babies or communion. "You'll want a receipt," he said, and for a moment the joke hung there between them, terrible and necessary, and then he tucked the package into his coat and became again just an old fisherman preparing for a run to neutral water.

Marguerite stepped forward.

Coral had thought the girl would hang back. But Margurite reached into her pocket and pressed something into Tomas's palm — a button, brass, off her dead father's coat, the one thing she owned that had come across the water. Coral saw the girl's jaw tighten against crying.

"So they know it's true," Marguerite said. "That real people sent it."

Tomas closed his fist around the button. He looked at her for a long moment, and then he bent and touched his forehead briefly to the crown of her head, an old gesture from an older country, and climbed back aboard without trusting his voice.

The engine coughed, caught, settled into its working rumble. Lines came off the cleats. The skiff swung its bow toward the harbor mouth, toward the grey line where the sea ended and the sky hadn't decided yet, and Coral stood on the wet stones and watched until the dark swallowed it.

The truth had left her hands. She felt the absence of its weight against her ribs like the phantom ache of a tooth long pulled.

What remained was memory. Forty-one pages, read aloud night after night, until Elias could recite the fishing families backward and forward and Marguerite could name every child under twelve in the district, and Coral — Coral had written each name into herself so many times that she sometimes dreamed in ledger-hand, the letters marching down the inside of her skull. They could burn paper. They could not burn what lived behind her eyes, unless they burned her, and Reyes would find that harder than it sounded.

"Come away," Elias murmured. "There are soldiers on the quay road."

* * *

They came for the original at mid-morning.

By then the town had been turned inside out. Soldiers moved door to door with inventory lists, and neighbors stood in the street holding whatever they'd been allowed to carry, and the commission's grey vans idled outside the church and the schoolhouse and the notary's office, filling with other people's lives. Doña Inés from the bakery sat on her own doorstep with flour still on her apron, watching two corporals carry out her account books, and she did not weep, and somehow that was worse than weeping.

Captain Reyes came up the tower stairs himself.

He climbed slowly, unhurried, a man who had learned that dread did half his work for him. He filled the oil room doorway in his pressed uniform, and his eyes went around the room — the lamp mechanism, the supply shelves, the narrow cot where Marguerite slept, the desk — with the thoroughness of a man reading a chart.

"Lamp keeper Quintana." He had never once called her Coral, though they had known each other's names for years. "You'll have received notice of the historical commission's authority over private records."

"I received notice ninety days ago, Captain. You've come eighty-eight days early."

"The schedule is a military matter." He held out his gloved hand, palm up. "All documents. Correspondence, logs, registers, personal papers."

Coral opened the desk drawer.

She had rehearsed this a hundred times in the dark hours — which papers to give, in what order, with what reluctance. She laid them out one at a time on the desktop like cards: the official light station logbook, stamped and current. Her husband's death notice, folded soft with handling. A bundle of old letters from her mother, dead nine years. Shipping manifests she'd copied for the harbormaster. And last, with the smallest pause she could manage, the ledger itself.

Six years of names. She put it into the Captain's hand.

He weighed it. She watched him weigh it, watched his thumb move across the spine, and understood suddenly that he was not a fool — that he had perhaps never been a fool at all, that the eighty-eight days might have been chosen precisely because a clever man lets the hunted believe themselves finished before he closes the trap.

"This is the famous register," he said. Not a question. "The one the fishermen talk about. Every soul the war scattered."

"Every soul I know of, Captain. I'm only one woman with a lamp. I don't know everything."

"No," Reyes agreed mildly. "One rarely does." He tucked the ledger under his arm, and his gaze drifted — lazily, she thought, but she had stopped believing in his laziness — toward the oil stores. Toward the wall. Toward the seam in the paneling that no longer hid anything but that a suspicious man might measure with his eye.

Marguerite was standing by the cot, very still, very straight.

"You have a daughter," Reyes said.

"I have a ward."

"The records list no family for you, Quintana. No kin at all. Yet here is a child, and a stranger sleeping in your oil room these two months past—" He let it hang. He was good at letting things hang. Somewhere below, a gull cried over the water where a fishing skiff had been an hour before, and Coral fixed her eyes on the Captain's collar stud and gave him nothing.

"My house is my business, Captain. As your orders are yours."

Something flickered behind his eyes — respect, or its close cousin, suspicion refined into patience. He clicked his heels together, a small precise sound, absurd in an oil room that smelled of tallow and rain.

"You'll remain at your post until the light is formally decommissioned," he said. "I'd advise you and yours to be packed. The last civilian ferry leaves tomorrow at dusk, whether you're on it or not."

His boots went down the stairs. At the turn he paused, just once, just long enough.

"Puerto Libre is a long way to send a button," he said, and was gone.

Marguerite's hand found Coral's and gripped it, cold as ballast. Down in the harbor the grey vans were loading, and the light on the tower above them turned and turned over water that gave back nothing.

"He knows," Elias breathed from the doorway. "Coral — he knows, and he let us go anyway. Why would a man like that let us go?"

Tomorrow at dusk. One boat. One chance.

And somewhere between here and the quay, a trap with their names on it, waiting to be sprung.
