## 9. The Last Boat

**Coral**

They came for her at the hour when the lamp should have been lit, which was, Coral thought as the boots rang up the iron stair, a cruelty of timing or an oversight — she could not decide which. Captain Reyes climbed into her watch room the way weather climbs in from the sea: without apology, without hurry, filling every corner.

"Mrs. Quintana." He did not sit. He never sat. He stood at the window with his hands clasped behind him, watching Marguerite's small figure on the quay below, hauling their one trunk toward the ferry shed. "You've been thorough. I'll give you that. Every crate counted. Every lamp part catalogued. The commission will find your paperwork exemplary."

"I keep good records," Coral said. "I always have."

"Yes." He turned. His face was not unkind; that was the terrible thing about it. It was the face of a man doing arithmetic he had done so long ago he no longer noticed the numbers were people. "The original ledger, then. The one your neighbors tell me you kept for years. Births. Deaths. Departures."

"There is no such ledger."

"Coral Quintana." He said her whole name like a man setting a stone down carefully so it would not crack. "Three fishermen swear they saw you writing in it on the jetty. The schoolteacher's widow says you recorded her son's drowning in it before the army even issued its report. You are asking me to believe a woman of your precision destroyed the only complete record of this town's population."

"I am asking you to believe," Coral said, "that I burned it. Paper burns, Captain. So do towns."

The lamp behind them ticked as the mechanism turned the lens, and the great beam swept out over the harbor and came back around like a slow arm gathering everything in. Reyes watched it pass across his own face, once, twice.

"You understand what happens to keepers who obstruct a historical commission."

"I understand what happens to towns that get requisitioned," Coral said. "I wrote it all down somewhere."

For a moment — she would remember this moment for the rest of her life, in kitchens and harbors and languages not yet hers — something moved behind his eyes that was almost respect, or almost exhaustion; with men like Reyes the two wore the same coat. He took out a cigarette, did not light it, put it away again.

"The ferry leaves at six. There will be a search of every passenger and every parcel. If anything surfaces — a scrap, a name in a child's hand — the amnesty is void. Do you understand me?"

"I understand that you have a job," Coral said, "and I have a lamp to trim. Good evening, Captain."

He stood there a moment longer, studying her the way he might study a chart with a coastline drawn where no coast should be. Then he went down the stairs, and his boots faded, and Coral put her hand flat against the cold glass of the lantern room and felt the storm coming in off the water like a held breath about to break.

* * *

**Elias**

Elias walked himself to the guard post at half past four, because it was the only way left to spend the hour.

He had planned it in the copying nights, back when the plan was still a kindness and not yet a sentence: if suspicion fell, it must fall on him alone. A deserter caught at the docks with forged movement orders was worth nothing to anyone but the firing detail. A keeper and her ward, implicated, were worth a great deal — to the commission, to Reyes, to the machinery that ground names into official paper. So he had written the confession the night before, three pages in his cartographer's hand, neat as a survey line: Elias Voss, formerly of the Topographical Section, self-surrendered, found in possession of documents he declined to describe. Let them dig for what did not exist while the ferry pulled out.

The sentry at the post looked at him, then at the papers, then whistled up the lane.

They put him in the customs shed among the confiscated crates — a town's attic stacked to the rafters, chairs wrapped in sacking, a christening gown folded on top of a sewing machine like a sleeping bird. Reyes came within the hour, read the pages twice, and looked up with that flat, patient gaze Elias remembered from briefing rooms where maps were redrawn and villages quietly deleted.

"You're the cartographer," Reyes said. "Voss. They told us you'd drowned."

"Reports are often wrong."

"So are confessions." He set the pages down. "You're giving yourself up two hours before embarkation to protect a lighthouse keeper. That's not desertion, Voss. That's devotion, and it's worse for you." He tapped the table. "Where is the ledger?"

"Gone. Burned, scattered, sunk — pick the version that helps you sleep."

Reyes studied him for a long time. Outside, the storm arrived properly at last, rain beginning to hammer the tin roof in sheets, and beneath it, faintly, the harbor bell began its blind counting.

"You'll be held for transport," Reyes said finally. "There's a prisoner barge behind the ferry. If your keeper boards clean, perhaps I'll lose your file in the confusion. Perhaps I won't." He rose, buttoned his coat. "War makes archivists of us all, Voss. Some of us just prefer certain pages blank."

When he was gone, Elias pressed his ear to the wall and listened to the rain and the bell, and began, methodically, to count the sounds of the shed: the door, two hinges; the window, one bar, rusted low; the crates, stacked to shoulder height against the north wall. A mapmaker learns to see rooms as coastlines. Everything has a passage, if the tide is right.

And the tide, tonight, was chaos.

* * *

**Coral**

The ferry shed at dawn looked like the inside of grief. Two hundred souls and their bundled lives, soldiers moving through the crowd with lists, children crying in the way children cry when the crying has gone on too long to be about any one thing. Marguerite stood pressed against Coral's side under the dripping eave, the trunk between them, and inside the trunk, beneath a false bottom lined with oilcloth, lay nothing at all — because there was nothing left to lie about. The copied ledger had gone east with old Baptiste two days past, sewn into a herring barrel. The original had been seized that morning from the ruins of the false wall, carried off by a corporal who handled it like kindling.

What remained was memory, and memory fit in a pocket.

"They're calling the Quintanas," a soldier shouted. "Quintana party, ferry gangway, now!"

Coral took Marguerite's hand. It was small and cold and gripped hers back hard enough to hurt, and she thought: two winters ago I had a lamp and a ledger and a silence, and now I have this grip, and it is worth more than both.

At the gangway a lieutenant checked names against lists while another soldier opened parcels. He looked at Marguerite. "Ward of the keeper?"

"My niece," Coral said, in the voice she used for storms. "Her mother was a Quintana. Check your register."

He checked. Somewhere in some requisitioned office, someone had transcribed Marguerite's name onto a list, because lists breed lists, and the transcription said niece, and so the girl passed through the gate of paper into the boat. Then the soldier reached for the trunk.

"Household goods," Coral said. "Lamp spares. You're welcome to inventory it on board, Lieutenant, but there are two hundred people behind me and one of you."

A whistle blew from the quay — something happening, something always happening — and the soldier waved the trunk through with the particular contempt of a man glad to be rid of one more box.

They stood at the ferry's stern rail as the mooring lines came in. The town lay gray under the rain: the crooked streets, the fish sheds, the church with its requisitioned bell, and above it all the lighthouse, dark for the first time in Coral's adult life, its lamp dismantled and crated by order of a commission that had decided the light was military property.

Marguerite's hand found hers again. "It looks smaller from here," the girl said.

"It always does," Coral said. "That's how they get you."

Then, along the quay, the shouting started.

* * *

**Elias**

The window bar gave on the third pull, exactly as the rust had promised, and Elias dropped into the alley behind the customs shed as the first prisoners were being marched to the barge. Rain everywhere. Torchlight smeared yellow on wet stone. He had thirty seconds of anonymity in which to become a shape and not a name, and he spent them well — a borrowed slicker off a hook, a crate of lamp oil toppled into the lane, and suddenly the whole quay was shouting about fire, soldiers running everywhere at once, the embarkation dissolving into the beautiful animal panic of two hundred frightened people and forty men trying to list them.

He went over the fence by the coal yard, waded the flooded gutter, and came out on the pier just as the ferry's horn sounded its last long note — the note of a thing leaving whether or not you were on it.

He saw them at the rail. Coral, unmistakable even through rain, her hair plastered dark, her hand raised — not waving, pointing, pointing aft, and he understood: the stern ladder, the cargo skiff towed behind, the one route no soldier was guarding because no one fled a country by clinging to its garbage.

He ran. The pier swam under his boots. A sentry shouted something; a torch swung; and then the crowd itself closed around Elias like a hand — dockworkers who knew whose charts had mapped their coves, whose name had been whispered through the copying nights, and who, without a word, without looking at him, became a wall of backs between him and the light.

He went down the ladder into the black water, caught the skiff's towline, and held on as the ferry gathered way and the harbor opened its mouth and swallowed them all.

* * *

**Coral**

They hauled him over the stern rail half-drowned for the second time in his life, and Coral did not laugh and did not weep; she simply put her hands on either side of his face and held it, rain and seawater streaming between her fingers, as though checking a coastline she had memorized against the thing itself.

"You absolute fool," she said.

"Your fool," Elias said. "If the position's still open."

Behind them the town was going out. Not slowly, not street by street the way a town sleeps, but in sections — the army cutting power quarter by quarter as it cleared, so that the darkness advanced like a tide across the hillsides. Last of all, the church bell tolled once, unexplained, and stopped.

Marguerite stood between them at the rail, soaked to the skin, her chin lifted the way it lifted when she was determined not to cry, and recited, quietly, into the wind — names. Eleven of them, the children's entries, the ones she had written herself and sworn to carry. Coral joined her on the fourth. Elias, teeth chattering, on the seventh. By the eleventh they were all three saying it together, a small litany running ahead of the boat into the open sea:

*Aurelio Marín, aged six. Sefa Quintana, aged nine. Tomás—*

The lighthouse rock slid astern, black and blind, and then it was only rain, and gray water, and the ferry driving east toward a coast where nobody knew their names — except, now, the three of them, and whoever waited at the end of a fisherman's herring run, in an archive where a true record sat in a barrel, waiting to be read aloud.
