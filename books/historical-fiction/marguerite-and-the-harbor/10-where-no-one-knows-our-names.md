## 10. Where No One Knows Our Names

A year is a long time to learn the sound of a new sea.

The harbor here does not batter; it murmurs. The waves come in low and patient, folding themselves against black volcanic sand, and the wind carries salt and woodsmoke and, on certain evenings, the green smell of the terraced gardens above the village. I had forgotten that a coast could be gentle. Ours had never been. Ours arrived like a fist through a door — but then, everything about our old life arrived that way in the end.

We live at the top of the village now, in a house with a blue door and a flat roof where Marguerite dries fish on racks Elias built from shipping crates. The house came with the widow's garden, half gone to fennel, and with a name on its lintel worn nearly smooth by weather. No one here knows what we were before. To the women at the well, I am the quiet one who mends nets with an odd, precise stitch. To the priest, I am the woman who asks too many questions about the parish records. To Marguerite, I am still — she insists on it, though she is taller than me now, taller than any child has a right to become in a single year of eating regularly — *Coral*, keeper, her keeper, whatever else the world decided to call us.

Elias works the boats. His hands, which once drew the borders of vanished places, have learned rope and cork and the honest arithmetic of a catch. He comes up from the water each evening smelling of brine and tar, and when he sets his boots on our stone step he always pauses, just a moment, as if asking permission of a door that no longer needs to be guarded. Some nights I watch him from the roof and think: there goes a man who redrew maps so that villages would disappear, and there goes a man who carried a ledger through a storm in his coat because a woman told him names are not evidence. Both men are true. We let them both live in him, the way this harbor lets the tide come in twice a day without ever once apologizing for going out.

* * *

The letter came on the morning tide, which is fitting, since everything important in my life has come off the water — my husband, my ruin, my found family, and now this.

It was forwarded three times, creased soft as cloth, stamped in two languages neither of us speaks well. The archive. The neutral port. The fisherman, old Baptiste, who took the copied ledger sewn into oilcloth beneath his false floor, must have kept his promise even after the war ended, even when keeping promises stopped being profitable. The letter was written by someone called an archivist, in careful, formal language, translated once and then again until it read like scripture:

*The collection has been accepted and catalogued. It comprises one hundred and fourteen entries, including marginalia in three hands. A historian attached to the commission-in-exile has begun work with the material. She writes to say that the names are being read aloud, in order, every Friday evening, in a hall where anyone may come and listen.*

I stood in the doorway with that page shaking in my hands while the gulls screamed their opinions overhead, and I could not make my legs carry me inside. One hundred and fourteen. I had counted them so many times in the dark — in the lighthouse, in the ferry's hold, in the first rented room where Marguerite slept between us like a small warm wall against the past — that the number had worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket. But seeing it in another person's handwriting, in ink meant for permanence, undid something in me that had been holding very still for a very long time.

Read aloud. In order. Every Friday.

I thought of Señora Ibarra, who taught the third form and kept her husband's photograph under the sugar tin. Of the Almeida boys, taken off their father's boat mid-haul. Of old Tomás, who refused evacuation twice and was removed the third time by four soldiers and a stretcher. Of Marguerite's mother, whose name I wrote in the ledger myself because no one else remained who remembered it whole — *Lucía Ferrán, dockworker's wife, last seen Tuesday, went looking for her brother at the customs house.* Marguerite had insisted on copying that entry herself, pressing so hard the pencil tore the paper, and I had let her, because some names must be written with anger or they are not truly written at all.

They were not evidence. They were not statistics for the victors' historians to smooth into a tidy paragraph about necessary displacements. They were people, and somewhere across the water, in a hall with bad lighting and good intentions, a stranger was saying their names out loud to whoever would listen.

I sat down on the step and cried the way I had not cried at the funeral, nor at the requisition, nor on the last boat — great ugly sobs that frightened the cat and brought Elias up the hill at a run with a boathook still in his hand, ready, God help him, to fight the entire ocean for me. When I showed him the letter he read it twice, sat down beside me, and said nothing at all, which was exactly right. He only put his arm around my shoulders, and we watched the fishing fleet come home together like two people watching a fire they'd spent years trying to light finally catch.

Marguerite read it third, standing over us, her shadow long in the evening light. She read it with the terrible composure children build out of necessity, and then she said, "One hundred and fourteen," in a voice that cracked on the last syllable, and I pulled her down between us and held on.

That night she asked me to say the names with her, all of them, the way we used to do in the oil room when the searchlights swept the cliff. We got through forty before sleep took her. I finished alone, into the dark, the way a keeper finishes a watch.

* * *

The beacon was Elias's idea, though he denies it.

There is a shelf of rock below the village, black and barnacled, that juts into the harbor mouth like a lower lip. Boats scrape it in fog. Twice this winter the fishermen had cursed it by name. One evening in March, Elias stood on the roof drying himself after a crossing and looked at that rock the way he used to look at blank margins on a chart, and I saw the thought arrive in him whole.

"You still remember how," he said. Not a question. "Lamps. Angles. How to make a light sit exactly where it's needed."

"I remember everything," I said. "That's the trouble with me."

He laughed — really laughed, the first time in months — and the next morning we were at the ironmonger's buying what we could afford: a secondhand lens, cracked at the edge; a copper housing that had been a ship's ventilator in its previous life; paraffin, wicks, a clockwork turntable salvaged from a broken gramophone. It took us six weeks of evenings. Marguerite painted the housing in stripes because she said a beacon ought to look like it belonged to somebody, and chose red and white, "like a candy, so children won't be afraid of it." Elias machined the mountings with a patience I had not seen in him since before the war, filing brass by lamplight while I calculated the flash interval — four seconds bright, six seconds dark, a rhythm I invented because it matched nothing official and therefore belonged to no government but ours.

We lit it on the anniversary of the requisition. Neither of us announced this. We simply climbed down to the rock at dusk with matches and a flask of coffee and the letter folded in my pocket, and when the sun went into the sea I struck the flame and set the little lens turning.

Four seconds bright. Six seconds dark.

It was not much. Any naval officer in the world would have laughed at it — a gramophone motor and a cracked lens on a barnacled shelf, guarding a harbor of eleven fishing boats. But the boats began to use it. Old Baptiste — not our Baptiste, another one, every coast has a Baptiste — altered his approach by a full point to clear the rock and left a basket of squid on our doorstep the following week with no note. Children rowed out at low tide to touch the housing and argue about whether the keeper was a man or a ghost. And on clear nights, when I stood on our roof and looked down at that steady patient pulse at the harbor mouth, I felt something I had thought was buried with the lighthouse: the old, clean certainty of a job done properly, a danger answered, a dark place made legible.

You cannot keep the sea. I learned that twice — once when it took my husband, and once when the army took the tower and everything the tower meant. But you can keep a light. You can decide, every single night, that the water will not get to be the only thing deciding who arrives safely. That is not duty anymore, not the way I understood it under a government's orders. It is something smaller and harder to confiscate. It is a promise you make with your own hands and keep with your own fuel, answerable to no commission, recorded in no ledger but the one I still carry behind my ribs.

* * *

On Friday evenings now, we do a strange thing, and the village has learned not to ask.

After supper, we light three candles at the table — one for the living, one for the dead, one for the ones nobody can say for certain which they are — and we read names aloud. All one hundred and fourteen, in order, the way the historian reads them in her hall across the water. Marguerite has the children's entries. Elias takes the men lost at sea, because his voice does not shake on those and mine still does. I take the rest. Our neighbors must think us mad, or devout, or both; the widow next door leaves extra candles on our step sometimes, which I choose to understand as blessing rather than pity.

Last week, a second letter came. Thinner than the first. The historian again — she signs herself only with initials, careful even in exile — reporting that the original ledger, the one Captain Reyes seized from my oil room, has surfaced in the commission's files. Recopied, she writes, in the victors' sanitized edition, with entries struck and others rewritten. But a junior clerk, some unnamed person with a conscience and access, compared the official version against ours and discovered the discrepancies. Forty-one names appear in our copy that appear nowhere in theirs. The historian ends with a question, formal and trembling all at once:

*She asks whether the keeper of these records would consent to testify — to give her own name to the record, publicly, so that the hundred and fourteen may be attached to a witness rather than floating free.*

I read it standing at the window, watching our small beacon turn its four-and-six rhythm against the coming dark.

For a year I have been no one. That was the bargain — safety purchased with anonymity, our names traded away like the tower itself. And it has been a good bargain. Marguerite is growing straight and unafraid. Elias sleeps through the night now, most nights. I mend nets and grow fennel and am nobody at all.

But the names were never nobody's. That was the whole point, the whole war within the war — that a hundred and fourteen people do not dissolve because a captain confiscates a ledger, do not become *relocated persons* or *necessary displacements* because historians with better ink say so. Someone witnessed them. Someone counted them out of love instead of bureaucracy. And a record that floats free, however carefully archived, can still be argued with. A record with a living witness behind it cannot.

Marguerite came and stood beside me at the window, reading over my arm the way she does, her breath warm on the paper. Below us the beacon turned, faithful and stubborn, lighting a harbor that did not ask for it and is better for having it.

"They want your name," she said.

"They want the truth to have a face," I said. "It's not quite the same thing."

"Isn't it?" she asked — fifteen years old, sharp as her mother must have been, watching me with eyes that missed nothing. "You always said names are how we refuse to disappear."

I looked at the letter a long time. Somewhere across the water, in a hall on a Friday evening, a stranger was about to reach the F section of a list, and Lucía Ferrán would be spoken aloud to a roomful of the living, and my daughter's mother would exist, officially, irrevocably, in the historical record of the world.

"Yes," I said finally, and reached for a pen. "Yes, they do."

And beneath the heading the historian had left blank — *witness:* — I wrote my name in full, the way I write everything: clearly, permanently, so it cannot be erased.
