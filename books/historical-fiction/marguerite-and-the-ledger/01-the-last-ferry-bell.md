## 1. The Last Ferry Bell

The road down into Marguerite was the color of old bone, and Ivo Hartwell walked it the way she had learned to walk every road for ten years: as though she were nobody, going nowhere, with nothing in her pockets worth a second glance.

It was a discipline of the whole body. Shoulders rounded just enough to suggest a woman tired of walking but not so tired she'd welcome conversation. Eyes down at the ruts and stones, never lingering on faces. A limp she could summon or dismiss at will — left knee, faint, the kind that made men look away out of politeness rather than curiosity. She had collected these habits the way other women collected lace or recipes, one at a time across a decade of borders, and now they fit her so closely that some mornings she woke and could not find the woman underneath them without searching.

She found herself searching more often lately.

Below her, the town lay along the river like something spilled and half-frozen there: slate roofs furred with snow, chimneys standing up thin and gray against a sky the color of dishwater, and beyond it all the bridge — the reason she had come this far north on roads that were closing behind her like stitches being pulled from a hem. The bridge crossed the Vell at its narrowest point, iron-boned and black, running from Marguerite's northern edge up into the pass and the territories beyond, where the delegations were gathering and where, in eleven days, men who had never heard her brother's name would sign a peace built on his silence.

In the satchel under her coat, flat against her ribs where a heart would be if she'd been allowed to keep hers, was the dispatch. Sealed in oilcloth, wrapped twice, tied with the wax-stamped cord that meant *carry this yourself, trust no hands between yours and the address.* She had carried it four hundred kilometers through three provinces whose names were already becoming obsolete. She would carry it one more day, across that bridge, and then it would stop being hers, and she would be — what? Empty. The thought arrived with neither relief nor dread, only a strange blankness, like stepping out of a lit room into a corridor.

*One more day,* she told herself, and did not quite believe it, because she had learned that believing things about tomorrow was a luxury her trade did not extend.

The last kilometer of road ran between bare orchards, their branches black and patient, and past a shrine where someone had set a jar of frozen plums before a painted saint whose face had weathered to a smudge. Ivo touched two fingers to the jar's rim without thinking — an old habit from a country she no longer claimed, a gesture she'd have sworn she'd abandoned years ago. That was the trouble with silence, she thought. It didn't empty you. It only stored everything, undisturbed, like water in a deep well. Ten years down that well, and still the smallest stone made a sound.

Marguerite smelled of woodsmoke and river ice when she reached the first houses. A dog announced her to a yard where fishnets hung frozen in sculpted folds; a woman beating a rug paused, looked at Ivo's boots, her satchel strap, her face, and went back to beating. Ivo catalogued the exchange automatically — *one witness, female, fiftyish, unremarkable, will not remember me by morning* — and hated how fluently she still spoke that language.

The ferry landing sat below the town on a shelf of dark stone, and the ferryman was already there, which surprised her, because the light said mid-afternoon and the cold said nothing would move on this river willingly. He was a broad man gone soft at the middle, mending a net with fingers that moved without his attention, and he watched her come down the steps with the unhurried assessment of someone who had watched every kind of person arrive at this shore for forty years.

"You'll be wanting the crossing," he said. Not a question.

"Tomorrow, if the ice allows." She kept her voice level, mildly bored. "I'm told the bridge is closed to civilian traffic."

"The bridge is closed to everything," the ferryman said. "Garrison order since the armistice talks started. They want the north road quiet." He bit a knot loose with his teeth. "Ferry's still mine, though. Ice permitting."

"Then I'll pay for ice-permitting."

He looked at her then, properly, and Ivo felt the familiar prickle at the back of her neck — the sensation of being read. She knew what he saw: a young woman, travel-worn, papers that would say she was a clerk's assistant carrying survey correspondence south to north, unremarkable coat, unremarkable boots, a limp that invited pity and discouraged questions. She knew because she had built the picture herself, piece by piece, in a room in Vellane with a forger named Casimir who drank her coffee and lectured her on the theology of documents.

"A document is a story," Casimir had said, pressing the transit paper flat with both palms as if blessing it. "The stamp is the miracle that makes people believe it. But listen, girl — the best forgery is true. Put one real fact in every lie. Give her a real hometown. Let her know the price of bread. A lie with nothing true in it is a costume; a lie with one true thing sewn in is a skin."

So Ivo knew the price of bread in three towns she'd never lived in, and the ferryman's inspection found nothing to catch on, and still he looked at her a moment too long.

"Where are you coming from?" he asked.

"South. Up the valley road."

"Roads are bad above Kessel."

"I heard."

"Bad enough that a woman walking alone should have turned back a week ago." He said it without menace, almost gently, the way a man remarks on weather. Then he returned to his net. "Come back at first light. If the river lets me, I'll take you. If it doesn't—" He shrugged, a mountain range moving. "If it doesn't, you'll want a room. Widow Marchand takes lodgers. House on the church square, green shutters, can't miss it."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." He nodded upstream, toward a bend in the Vell where the water showed white and sluggish around dark shoulders of rock. "Hear that?"

Ivo listened. At first there was only wind off the water and the creak of the mooring rope. Then, beneath it, a sound she felt in her teeth before she understood it in her ears — a long, low groan rolling up the river valley, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Ice is coming down from the high country," the ferryman said. "Three days, maybe four, and the river closes. After that, nobody crosses until spring thaw. Nobody." He held her eyes. "You understand what I'm telling you, girl. This is the last bell. Miss it, and Marguerite keeps you till April."

*I only need tonight,* she wanted to say. *I cross at dawn and by noon I'm a day's walk toward the pass and none of this concerns me anymore.* But she had survived ten years by never saying what she wanted to say, so instead she inclined her head and said, "First light, then," and climbed back up the steps into the town.

Behind her, the river groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere far up the valley something cracked like a rifle shot.

* * *

The church square was small and swept, the snow pushed into gray banks against the walls, and the guesthouse stood exactly as described: green shutters, a lantern burning early in the window, a wooden sign carved with a pike leaping a stylized wave. Ivo stood across the square for a full minute, doing what she always did before entering any building she couldn't leave quickly — reading it. Two exits visible: front door, kitchen door. Smoke from the chimney, steady, meaning a fire tended by someone home. A boy's sled tipped against the step, meaning family, meaning witnesses, meaning complications. Light in an upstairs window, curtained.

*Ordinary,* she concluded. *Safe as anything is.*

She was tired. That was the danger. She could feel exhaustion the way a sailor feels a leak — not yet flooding, but present, gaining, whispering that it would be fine to rest, to sleep deep, to let the vigilance slacken for one night in a warm room. Ten years ago, tiredness had nearly killed her outside Tarnow, when she'd slept twelve hours in a barn and woken to cavalry in the yard. She had not made that mistake again. But the mistake never stopped proposing itself.

She knocked.

The woman who opened the door was seventy if she was a day, straight-backed as a fence post, with white hair pinned in a crown and eyes the pale, penetrating gray of winter light on snow. She looked at Ivo the way the ferryman had, but longer, and with more method — boots, satchel, hands, face — the way a customs officer reads a manifest.

"You'll be wanting a room," the woman said.

"If you have one."

"I have four. I have one." She stepped aside. "Odile Marchand. Come in before you bring the cold with you."

Inside, the guesthouse smelled of beeswax and boiled linen and, underneath, something older — paper, ink, the dry mineral scent of a house that kept records. Ivo registered it without knowing yet why it mattered. A fire burned in the parlor hearth. On the table by the window lay an enormous book, bound in green leather gone bald at the corners, open to a page dense with handwriting.

"You're the courier everyone's pretending not to notice," Odile Marchand said, taking Ivo's coat before she could object. "The garrison posted notices. All couriers to report to the tollhouse for detention until after the signing." She hung the coat with brisk care. "Your papers say clerk's assistant, I imagine. Survey correspondence."

Ivo kept her face pleasant and empty. "That's right."

"Mm." Odile studied her. "My late husband did survey work. Forty years of it. He never once needed a courier to hand-carry what the post could carry, and he never once sent a woman alone up the valley road in December." She said it without accusation, almost fondly, the way one notes a flaw in a well-loved clock. "But it's not my business. Rooms are rooms. One crown a night, supper included, and you'll take it at the common table because I don't feed lodgers separately. It breeds secrets."

*It breeds secrets.* Ivo almost smiled. If only the woman knew what her common table was about to breed.

"One night," Ivo said. "I cross at dawn."

"The ferryman will tell you the same thing he tells everyone. First light, ice permitting." Odile led her up narrow stairs to a room under the eaves — small, clean, a bed with a quilt the color of rust, a washstand, a window looking north toward the bridge. "Water's in the jug. Supper's at seven. And—" She paused at the door, hand on the frame, and for a moment the brisk landlady mask slipped and something else looked out, something old and watchful and very tired. "Whatever you're carrying, girl — and I'm not asking — this town has seen wars spend themselves here before. We keep our heads down and our accounts straight, and we outlast whoever's marching. Remember that, and you'll do fine."

She closed the door. Her footsteps descended, even and unhurried.

Ivo stood in the small room and let herself, for exactly sixty seconds by the mantel clock downstairs whose tick she could just hear, stop performing. She sat on the bed. She pressed her palms flat against her knees. She breathed.

The room smelled like someone else's life — beeswax, bread, the ghost of pipe smoke in the curtain fabric, a child's chalk marks faintly surviving on the doorframe where heights had been measured and repainted over and measured again. When had she last slept in a room that smelled of anyone? Boarding houses smelled of bleach and strangers. Safehouses smelled of fear, which has a smell, faint and sweetish, like fruit going bad. Her own rooms — the ones she rented for a season and left before the neighbors learned her name — smelled of nothing at all, because she had learned to own nothing that could remember her.

This room remembered people. Chalk marks. A quilt mended in three places with three different threads, meaning years, meaning someone had bothered. She pressed her thumb against the doorframe chalk line and felt the decade press back — all the names she hadn't spoken, all the doors she'd watched close, all the mornings she'd risen in a stranger's house and become a stranger again before her feet touched the floor.

*Sixty seconds,* she reminded herself, and stood, and became nobody once more, smoothly, the way a hand closes.

At the common table that evening there were four others: a tinker with frostbitten knuckles, two timber-men arguing amiably about a contract, and a boy of perhaps nine with a serious face and a school notebook, who ate with the concentration of the young and stared at Ivo's satchel with unconcealed fascination. Odile served barley soup and dark bread and salted fish, and presided with the economy of a general. Conversation was permitted; questions were not. Ivo gave the tinker a story about survey work that cost her nothing — one true fact sewn in, the price of bread in Kessel — and he nodded and believed her, or believed the stamp of her manner, which amounts to the same thing.

Under the table, her thumb rested against the satchel strap. Under her ribs, the dispatch lay sealed and silent. Eleven days until the signing. One night until the crossing. Everything accounted for, everything on schedule.

Except that when the boy — Tomas, Odile called him, *Tomas, elbows* — finally went up to bed, he left the notebook behind on the bench, and Ivo, gathering it to return it, saw what was drawn on the open page and went very still.

It was the river. The whole Vell from the mill weir to the bridge, mapped in a child's careful pencil — channels, sandbars, depths marked in little numbers, the ice conditions noted day by day in a column down the margin. And there, halfway across, where no adult in this town would ever think to look, a stretch of shallows annotated in a round schoolboy hand:

*Freezes solid first. Walkable in hard frost. Papa says don't tell.*

Ivo closed the notebook and set it back exactly as it had lain, and upstairs in her room she lay awake listening to the river groan in the dark, and understood that the last ferry bell might not be the only way out of Marguerite after all.

She also understood, with the cold clarity that ten years had taught her to distrust and obey, that a child who mapped escape routes in pencil was a child the garrison would find very interesting indeed — and that she had one night, perhaps two, before the ice decided everything.
