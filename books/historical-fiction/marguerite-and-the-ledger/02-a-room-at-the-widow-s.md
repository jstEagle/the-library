## 2. A Room at the Widow's

The snow began an hour before dawn, and by the time the light came it had erased the road.

Ivo stood in the doorway of the waystation at the foot of the pass and watched the world go white. Not the polite, decorative snow of cities that swept its own steps and salted its own walks — this was mountain snow, the kind that fell with intent, filling the switchbacks one by one until the pass was no longer a road but a rumor. Somewhere up there, under two feet of fresh powder and falling fast, lay the route north she had counted on.

She did not swear. Ten years as a courier had taught her that swearing was a luxury, like hope: pleasant, occasionally useful, never to be relied upon. Instead she ate the last of her bread, checked the seal on the dispatch inside her coat — intact, dry, pressed flat against her ribs where a second heartbeat might be — and considered her options with the cold arithmetic that had kept her alive through three wars' worth of borders.

Option one: wait at the waystation for the pass to clear. The garrison would post men there within a day; couriers were being collected like stray dogs this season, she'd heard it said in three towns already. Option two: turn back south, toward checkpoints she had barely slipped past once and could not slip past again. Option three: the river town below, Marguerite, where the ferry still ran and the bridge — so the ferryman had told her yesterday, with the gloomy satisfaction of a man delivering bad news he'd been saving all week — would close within days when the ice came down from the high country.

Marguerite, then. One night, perhaps two, in a room with walls, before crossing north at first light. She shouldered her satchel and went down out of the mountains while the snow erased them behind her.

* * *

The town announced itself first by sound: the bell of a church whose tower she could not yet see, ringing eight o'clock with the unhurried authority of a place that had been ringing eight o'clock for four hundred years. Then the smell — woodsmoke and river damp and, underneath it, something yeasty and warm, bread baking somewhere close. Her stomach made a low, undignified noise, and she permitted herself, for half a block, to be merely hungry instead of vigilant.

Then she saw the bridge, and hunger left her entirely.

It was iron, old iron, green-black with river weather, arcing over a channel of water so dark it looked solid. Ice had begun at the banks — plates of it, pale and jagged, creeping inward from both shores like a slow negotiation between winter and the water. The middle of the river still ran open, black and swift, but the open water was narrow now, a corridor closing by inches every hour.

A man stood at the near end of the bridge with a lantern he didn't need in daylight, and when he saw her looking he called out, "Closed by Thursday, maybe Friday! Ferry after that, if you're lucky!" He sounded pleased about it, in the way of men who have made their peace with winter and enjoy watching others fail to make theirs.

"Which way to lodging?" she asked.

"Widow Marchand's." He pointed with the lantern, up a street of stone houses leaning companionably against each other. "Blue door. Tell her Ferro sent you, she'll charge you less." A grin. "Tell her anyone else sent you, she'll charge you double."

* * *

The blue door was exactly as described, set into a house that was larger than its neighbors and better kept, with shutters painted the same stubborn blue and a brass knocker worn smooth by a century of hands. Ivo knocked, waited, knocked again.

The woman who opened the door was sixty or older, small and straight-backed, with white hair pinned so severely it looked structural rather than cosmetic. She wore an apron over a dark dress, flour on one wrist, and she looked at Ivo the way a jeweler looks at a stone brought in off the street — not hostile, but weighing.

"A room," Ivo said. "One night. Maybe two."

"Maybe," Odile Marchand repeated, as though the word were a coin she suspected of being clipped. She stepped back to let Ivo in anyway, which told Ivo something about her: hospitality first, judgment second, or possibly both at once.

Inside, the house smelled of bread and beeswax and, faintly, of lamp oil and old paper — a smell Ivo could not immediately name and found herself, absurdly, reluctant to leave. The front room held a stove black and polished as a beetle, a table scrubbed pale, six chairs, and a staircase going up into warm shadow. On the wall hung a clock that ticked with the confidence of something that had never once stopped.

"You'll want supper," Odile said. It was not a question. "There's soup. There is nothing else. Sit."

Ivo sat. The chair creaked under her, and the sound was so ordinary, so domestic, that something behind her sternum loosened a fraction of an inch — and she noticed it loosening and pulled it tight again. This was the danger of rooms. She hadn't slept in a room that smelled of someone else's life since — she did the arithmetic and decided not to finish it.

Odile set down soup, bread, a spoon, and then, without asking, held out her hand. "Papers."

Here it was, then. Ivo produced the transit folder from inside her coat — the outer folder only, the dispatch sealed separately beneath her shirt where no landlady's hand would find it — and watched the widow read.

Odile read the way she did everything, Ivo suspected: thoroughly, unhurriedly, missing nothing. She turned the transit pass over. Held it closer to the lamp. Ran her thumb along the edge of the stamp, which was good — Ivo had paid a forger in Vellacourt who was very good — but which had been stamped slightly too perpendicular, the kind of detail a bored clerk wouldn't notice and an observant woman might.

"This clerk," Odile said. "Name of Perrin."

"What of him?"

"He has a cousin who married into the Halvard family. They come here summers, before the troubles." She set the papers down and looked at Ivo across the table, and her eyes were pale gray and absolutely without mercy or malice. "Perrin stamps his passes crooked. Yours is straight."

The soup steamed between them. The clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled with a sigh.

"The garrison changed clerks in autumn," Ivo said. Her voice came out level, which cost more than it used to. "New stamp. New habits."

"Mm," said Odile Marchand, in a tone that agreed with nothing.

She took the papers, opened a drawer beneath the stairs, and wrote Ivo's name — the false one, Anouk Ferrand, traveler — into a ledger that lived in that drawer the way other houses kept their Bibles. Ivo caught only a glimpse of it as the widow bent over the page: a fat book, cloth-bound, its spine cracked soft with use, pages dense with entries in a hand that had grown older across thirty years but never careless. Names. Sums. Small annotations in the margins — a word here, a date there, a debt forgiven, a favor carried forward.

Something in Ivo's chest went very quiet and very alert, the way it did when she passed a checkpoint and saw, out of the corner of her eye, exactly which guard was watching the wrong door.

"Room at the top of the stairs," Odile said, closing the ledger. "Water in the jug. Don't smoke, don't sing after ten, and don't ask me what's in my cellar." She paused at the foot of the stairs, flour still dusting her wrist, and added, almost as an afterthought: "The lock on your door works. Use it. Not because anything will happen tonight. Because it's a habit worth having."

* * *

The room was small and clean and had a bed with a quilted coverlet gone thin at the fold lines, a washstand, a candle stub, and a window overlooking the street and, beyond the rooftops, the river. Ivo locked the door, checked the window latch, and then allowed herself the luxury of sitting down on the bed with her boots off.

Warmth. That was the treachery of places like this. Cold kept a courier sharp; warmth got into you slowly, like water into wool, and by the time you noticed you'd soaked through, some part of you had already decided to stay. She had seen it happen to others. A man named Corvic had taken one room in one border town in the winter of '17 and been captured in the spring because he'd come back for his coat.

She would take nothing back for anyone. She would cross tomorrow if the ice allowed, Thursday if it didn't, and Marguerite would become another town she could describe accurately and remember deliberately badly.

But the room smelled of bread and beeswax and someone else's life, and downstairs a clock ticked for a household that expected itself to keep existing, and when she closed her eyes Ivo found that her body — her traitor body, which had marched and frozen and starved on command for a decade — had already begun, without consulting her, to relax.

She gave herself one hour. Then she went downstairs.

* * *

The front room was empty, but the drawer beneath the stairs was not fully closed.

Later — much later, in the years when people in Marguerite would tell the story differently than it happened — Ivo would say she had gone down for water. And in fact she did carry the jug down, and in fact she did fill it at the tap in the scullery. But between picking up the jug and setting it down, she passed the drawer, and the drawer stood open the width of two fingers, and the ledger lay inside with its cracked spine catching the lamplight.

She did not plan what happened next. Planning would have made it a decision, and decisions could be refused. What happened next simply happened, the way her hand moved to a knife or her feet went quiet on stairs: the oldest training in her, the part that understood that information was the only currency that never devalued.

Two fingers wider. Enough to see the open page.

Most of it was what a guesthouse ledger ought to be: names of lodgers going back years, sums owed and paid, a notation that the schoolteacher had settled his account in firewood during the hard winter of '19 and Odile Marchand had accepted it at a fair rate, which told Ivo more about the widow's character than any conversation could have. But interleaved among the lodgers were other entries, other books' worth of debts — and Ivo's eye, trained in a hundred archive rooms to sort the ordinary from the dangerous at a glance, snagged on them like cloth on a nail.

*Aurel Vasseur, miller — grain withheld from garrison requisition, October '18 — debt discharged by silence re: wagon movements.*

*Dr. Lemoine — treated rebel wounded, unpaid — marked PAID IN FULL by order of [illegible], on condition of continued attendance at [crossed out].*

And then a name that stopped her breath.

*Col. Edran Vasque — lodged three nights, June '14, party of four — bill unpaid — referred to his aide, Capt. Halloran — HALLORAN REMINDED TWICE — see loose page, back of book.*

Vasque. Colonel Edran Vasque, who sat now at the armistice table in the capital with clean gloves and a reputation for moderation. Colonel Edran Vasque, whose signature stood third from the top on the death warrant dated the fourteenth of March, eleven years ago — the warrant that bore her brother's name in the list of the condemned, in a clerk's tidy hand, between a schoolteacher and a boy of seventeen.

Ivo stood in the lamplight with a jug of water in her hands and did not move for a long time.

It was nothing, she told herself. Lodging records. A colonel who skipped his bill. Half the officers in the republic had stayed in half the guesthouses of the borderlands, and a dinner party in 1914 damned nobody.

Except. Except that the entries did not stop at lodging. The ledger tracked favors like currency because favors *were* currency here — grain moved and unmentioned, wagons routed, names struck from lists, silences bought and sold — and a book that recorded thirty years of such transactions in a river town through which every officer, every courier, every negotiator had passed on the way north was not a guesthouse ledger at all.

It was testimony. Thirty years of it, indexed, dated, in a single hand.

Her own dispatch — the sealed thing against her ribs, the reason she had crossed two frozen ranges — contained a fraction of what this book contained, gathered by a network at the cost of four lives she could name. And it had been sitting in a drawer under a staircase in a town of four hundred people, kept by a widow who stamped her own accounts with more care than any government she'd ever served.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked.

Ivo closed the drawer with two fingers, silent as snowfall, and was standing at the sink with the running tap when Odile Marchand came down the stairs in a shawl, carrying a candle.

"Couldn't sleep either?" the widow asked.

"The river," Ivo said. "It makes noise."

"It does that." Odile set her candle on the table and looked at her lodger — the forged papers folded away upstairs, the straight-stamped pass, the girl who filled a water jug at midnight with hands that never trembled and eyes that had been reading things they shouldn't. "Ice coming down from the high country. By Thursday it'll shear the bridge, mark me. You'll want to make your crossing soon, Anouk Ferrand, traveler."

"Yes," Ivo said. "Soon."

Odile nodded slowly, picked up her candle, and paused at the foot of the stairs.

"In thirty years," she said, not turning around, "no one has ever touched that drawer. The lock is old, but it holds. And I am a light sleeper." She climbed three steps, stopped. "Goodnight, Anouk."

The candlelight went up the stairs and away, and Ivo stood alone in the warm kitchen with the cold jug in her hands, listening to the river groan against its new ice — and understanding, with terrible clarity, that she had not come to Marguerite to deliver one dispatch.

Somewhere in that book was the name of the man who had signed her brother's death warrant, written in a widow's careful hand, and she would not leave this town without reading every page.
