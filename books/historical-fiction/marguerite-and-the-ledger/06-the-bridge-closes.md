## 6. The Bridge Closes

The sound woke the whole town before it woke the sun.

Ivo had been sleeping badly for three nights — the kind of sleep that was really only a long negotiation with wakefulness, her body curled around the satchel like a dog around a bone — and so she was already at the window of her room when the river spoke. It was not a crack, exactly. It was a groan, deep and structural, the sound a cathedral might make if it were being pulled apart by its own foundations. Then came the report of ice failing somewhere out in the dark, sharp as rifle fire, and then a long, grinding exhalation, as if the river had turned over in its bed and found it could no longer hold its shape.

She pressed her forehead to the cold glass and watched.

Dawn was only a gray suggestion behind the eastern ridge, but the river showed itself anyway, pale on pale, a white road running through the valley's throat. The bridge stood above it — the bridge she had counted on, the bridge whose northern span meant everything: the dispatch delivered, the ledger left behind, herself on the far side of this whole tangled town by Thursday's post. Even from here she could see something was wrong with it. The span listed, very slightly, like a man leaning against a wall to stay standing.

Then, with a sound like a great door closing, the middle section dropped.

Not collapsed — *settled*. The ice had heaved against the piers all night, Ivo understood dimly, the frozen river expanding as rivers do when they lock themselves solid, and the pressure had found the weakest pier and simply pushed until the masonry gave. The span tilted into the new gap like a plank sliding off a table. Snow sifted down from the deck rails in two soft white plumes. And below, the river — which had been a crossing — became a border.

Down in the street, voices were already gathering. She heard boots on packed snow, the ferryman's voice rising over the others, someone weeping quietly, someone laughing in the unhinged way people laugh when the alternative is worse. Ivo dressed without hurry. There was no hurry left anywhere in the world. Her fingers found the satchel strap under the mattress, checked the seal, tucked it back. Eleven days. That was what the garrison notice in the square had said, pasted up beside the curfew order in official black type: the armistice would be signed on the twenty-third, in the capital, by delegates who would travel north through Marguerite because it was the last open rail line and nobody had thought to close it yet.

Eleven days, and now no road out but the pass, and the pass had been shut since before she arrived.

She went downstairs.

* * *

Odile Marchand stood in the doorway of the guesthouse with her shawl pinned wrong, which Ivo had never seen before, and that small asymmetry told her more than any speech could have. The landlady who read every paper twice, who knew the price of eggs in three neighboring valleys and the debts of every family within a day's walk — even Odile had been caught unarranged by the morning.

"It held longer than I thought it would," Odile said, not turning. "The bridge. My husband bet me it would go by the feast of Saint Eligius. He won. He's been dead nine years and he still wins."

"The pass—"

"Closed until the thaw. You'd need wings or a death wish." Odile turned then, and her eyes did the thing Ivo had learned to dread and depend on in equal measure: they went over Ivo's face like a hand checking a pocket. "You'll stay, then. Through the signing."

"I don't have a choice."

"Nobody in Marguerite has a choice. That's what makes us good company." But there was no warmth in it this morning, or rather the warmth was there underneath, banked like coals under ash. "Breakfast is at seven. The garrison will want a count of lodgers by noon. Captain Veyra's orders — every soul accounted for, every name written down." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "They're making their own ledger, you see. Everyone is, these days."

Ivo ate her bread and cheese at the long table alone, because the other lodger — a wool factor named Pellissier who had been stranded a week before her — had taken his bowl to the kitchen and eaten standing up, watching her sidelong. She noticed it the way she noticed everything now: the way his weight shifted toward the door, the way he swallowed before speaking to the cook. A week ago he had been merely an inconvenience. Now he was a pair of eyes that might, for the price of a favor or a fright, look too closely at a courier girl with a satchel she never let out of reach.

*This is what a siege looks like from inside*, she thought. Not walls. Eyes.

She washed her bowl, thanked the cook, and stepped out into the street to see the river for herself.

* * *

The crowd at the foot of the bridge was larger than the sound had warranted, but crowds in winter towns are hungry for spectacle; a fallen span was better than mass, and warmer. Ivo kept to the edge, hood up, one shoulder against the corner of the tollhouse wall, and listened.

"—heard it go at half past four, I was awake with the boy—"

"—the garrison says six weeks minimum, engineering party has to come up from—"

"—six weeks! The signing's in eleven days, they'll bring the delegates through on the east line and we'll wave at them like beggars at a wedding—"

The ferryman, Halvard, stood at the waterline with his arms folded, looking at the broken span the way a man looks at a dead horse. Ivo drifted closer to him, because of everyone in Marguerite he was the one who understood the river, and understanding the river suddenly mattered more than anything else in the world.

"Could it carry a boat?" she asked quietly. "Before it locks completely. If a boat put out tonight."

Halvard didn't look at her. "You've been asking about crossings since you got here, girl."

"I'm a courier. Crossings are my trade."

"Your trade's done for the winter." Now he looked, and his face was old and weathered and entirely without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "That water's got pans the size of church floors grinding in it. A boat would be kindling inside thirty meters. In a week the whole surface sets, and then—" he shrugged, "—then it's a floor you can walk on if you know where, and a grave if you don't. The channels shift under the ice. Springs come up where there was none in summer. Old men drown out there every year who've crossed it forty times."

"Does anyone know where?"

"A boy does." He almost smiled. "Tomas Marchand draws the river in a notebook. Channels, springs, shallows. His grandmother scolds him for wasting paper. But the boy knows that river like the priest knows his psalms." He shook his head. "Not that knowing will matter to you. Nobody's walking the ice with the garrison watching both banks, and after the signing—" he turned back to his dead crossing, "—after the signing, whatever happens, happens. Best thing a person can do in Marguerite this winter is stand very still and be boring."

Ivo thanked him and moved away, her mind already working against her will, mapping, calculating, refusing the calculation. A boy's notebook. A frozen river. Eleven days. Every path led through the same terrible arithmetic: to get out at all, she would have to cross a killing field of ice carrying a ledger full of names, past patrols, with a town full of frightened people who could each, with a word, end her.

And beneath the arithmetic, quieter and colder, sat the other thing. The name. The entry she had seen in Odile's book four nights ago — *Colonel Aurel Steiner, guest of the house, autumn 1913, settled in favors* — the neat clerk's hand recording the hospitality of the man who had signed her brother's death warrant as casually as he recorded a delivery of hay. Steiner would be at the table on the twenty-third. Steiner's signature was on her brother's file. Steiner's name was in a widow's ledger in a river town, and Ivo was trapped eleven days' walk from the table where it could matter.

Or — the thought arrived like ice water — trapped eleven days *from* it, with nothing to do for eleven days but decide.

* * *

She spent the middle of the day doing what bored couriers do: nothing, visibly. She mended a seam. She read three pages of a farmer's almanac left in the parlor. She asked Odile idle questions about the town's history and received answers that were accurate, complete, and armored. She walked to the well and back. At the well, Frau Brandt the baker's wife gripped her elbow with floury fingers and said, too loudly, "Terrible about the bridge, miss, you'll be stuck with us poor souls," and her eyes flicked once toward the garrison billet across the square, and Ivo understood that she had just been seen — catalogued, weighed, perhaps priced. Frau Brandt's brother owed money. Everyone's brother owed money. Odile's ledger knew it, and now, Ivo realized with a slow sinking, the whole town knew that the stranger knew the ledger existed. Word traveled faster than couriers in Marguerite. Word was the only thing that had ever traveled fast in Marguerite.

By late afternoon the light had gone the color of dirty pewter and the cold came down off the ridge like a verdict. Ivo climbed to her room, barred the door, and took out the satchel.

She didn't open it. She had stopped opening it after the second night; the contents were printed on the inside of her eyelids now. Instead she sat with it in her lap and made herself look at the situation plainly, the way her handlers had taught her in the first year, when she was fourteen and still cried: *State your assets. State your liabilities. Do not flatter either.*

Assets: one sealed dispatch, duplicate copies sewn into her coat lining. One ledger — no, not hers, Odile's, but knowledge of it, pages of it, names that reached the armistice table. A chapel priest who ran letters. A boy who drew rivers. A sergeant at the tollhouse who owed a debt. And eleven days.

Liabilities: no route out. A garrison captain billeted downstairs as of tomorrow — Odile had received the notice that afternoon, and her face when she'd read it had been a locked room. A wool factor who watched her eat. A baker's wife who touched her arm in public. A town where every kindness was a transaction and every transaction was recorded somewhere, by someone, in somebody's hand.

And the deepest liability, the one she set down last because setting it down made it real: she had begun to care. About the smell of bread in the kitchen at five. About Tomas's crooked handwriting. About Odile's shawl pinned straight or crooked. Ten years of silence had taught her that caring was the first thing you surrendered and the last thing you got back, and she had felt it coming back in this town like feeling returning to a frostbitten hand — painful, dangerous, impossible to stop.

A knock at the door. Soft. Two knuckles, no more.

Ivo slid the satchel under the blanket and opened the door on Odile, holding a candle and a folded paper.

"You'll want to see this before the captain arrives tomorrow," Odile said. "It's the billeting order. Veyra takes the blue room. Mine." A thin smile. "Thirty years I've slept behind that wall, and now a captain of the provisional government sleeps there instead, and asks questions of everyone under my roof." She held out the paper. "Read it. Memorize it. Give it back."

Ivo read it. Standard language. Billet assigned, requisitions authorized, *all persons resident to present themselves for registration*. At the bottom, in different ink, added later: *Note regarding the foreign courier currently lodging — establish purpose of extended stay.*

"They know about you," Odile said, taking the paper back and feeding it to the candle flame. It curled and brightened and was gone. "Pellissier wrote to the garrison the night the bridge fell. I watched him seal the letter. I'm telling you because you should hear it from me and not from a man with a revolver." She looked at Ivo for a long moment, and in the candlelight her face was every one of its sixty-odd years and none of them. "Whatever you're carrying, girl — and don't tell me, I haven't asked and I don't want to know — you have ten days to decide what it's worth and who pays. After that, decisions get made for you."

"And you? What have you decided?"

Odile blew out the candle. In the dark her voice was dry as old paper.

"I decided nine years ago, the day they struck my son's name from a list. I've just been waiting for the market to change." The door closed softly behind her.

Ivo stood in the dark a long time, listening to the house settle around her — the tick of cooling beams, wind testing the shutters, far below the river grinding its teeth as it froze itself shut. Somewhere out there the ice was thickening by the hour, laying down its white road and its white graves in equal measure. Somewhere south, a colonel was packing dress uniform for a signing. Somewhere upstairs, a woman was sleeping in a room that smelled of thirty years of other people's debts.

Eleven days.

She lay down fully clothed, one hand on the satchel strap, and stared at the ceiling until the dark stopped being a ceiling and became the underside of the ice, and she was under it, and the names were above her, written on the frozen river in a hand she recognized, and the whole town was walking across her.

In the morning, Captain Veyra would arrive. In the morning, the counting would begin.

She slept anyway. It was the only order left to give.
