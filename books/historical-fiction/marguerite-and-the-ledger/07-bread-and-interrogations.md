## 7. Bread and Interrogations

The garrison truck came up from the south road at half past four, its engine grinding through the snow like something that had forgotten how to stop. Ivo heard it from the washroom window, where she had been scrubbing a basin for the better part of an hour — not because the basin was dirty, but because her hands needed occupation and her mind needed a wall to press against. She watched two soldiers dismount, then a third figure in a grey-blue officer's coat, tall, unhurried, brushing snow from her shoulders with the absent elegance of someone who had never once been cold.

The captain looked up at the house as if she already knew every room in it.

Ivo dried her hands. Downstairs, Odile was already moving through the kitchen with the particular briskness of a woman who had decided, some minutes ago and without consulting anyone, exactly how this evening would go. The bread came out of the oven. The good tablecloth — the one with the faded cornflowers — went onto the table. A second place was set beside the lodgers' places with a precision that said: *you will sit where I put you.*

"Captain Veyra," Odile announced when the knock came, before the officer could speak. "I know. The requisition notice arrived Tuesday. You'll want the corner room. It has the stove flue."

Something flickered across Veyra's face — not surprise, exactly. Recognition of a move she hadn't expected to be met so early. "Madame Marchand."

"Odile. In this house everyone is called what they are." She stepped back from the door. "Come in out of the weather. Your men can stable themselves; the shed's at the back and there's hay enough, though you'll be billed for it."

Veyra laughed — a real laugh, low and warm, the kind that made a room feel safer than it was. "They warned me about you at headquarters."

"I should hope so. It saves time." Odile took the captain's coat without asking and hung it on the peg by the door, next to Ivo's threadbare one, and Ivo watched the two coats swing together on their hooks like conspirators.

* * *

There were five lodgers now, counting the schoolteacher who had come down from the pass before it closed and never managed to leave. Supper was served at six, and Veyra arrived at the table precisely on time, hair still damp from washing, uniform jacket open at the collar in a studied informality. She had the face of a woman who listened for a living — fine-boned, watchful, with eyes the color of river ice that went soft when she wanted them to.

"So," she said, unfolding her napkin. "A full house. Madame Marchand, you must tell me how you manage it. Half the town seems to eat here."

"The other half owes me money," Odile said, ladling soup. "It keeps the conversation honest."

The teacher laughed. The miller's wife did not — she had been pale all week, Ivo noticed, since the day soldiers came through asking after the miller's grain accounts. Veyra's gaze passed over the woman once, gently, like a hand smoothing a wrinkle, and moved on.

Ivo kept her eyes on her bowl. Ten years of practice told her exactly how much attention to pay: enough to seem present, not enough to seem interested. She was Marguerite's newest lodger, a courier stranded by the closed bridge, a girl with transit papers signed by an office that no longer existed. She was nobody worth a captain's evening.

"And this is our courier," Odile said.

The words landed on the table like a dropped spoon. Ivo felt the whole room reorganize itself around her — the miller's wife's breath catching, the teacher's curiosity sharpening, Veyra turning with the unhurried interest of a cat presented with a wrapped parcel.

"A courier." Veyra smiled at her. "How romantic. Do they still call it that, or has the war made a fancier word?"

"They call it whatever pays best," Ivo said. "Mostly it's carrying invoices."

"Invoices." Veyra tasted the word. "And your route? Before the bridge?"

"South, mostly. Textile houses. Nothing anyone would want to read." She held the captain's gaze for exactly the right interval and then returned to her soup, letting the silence do the work. The trick was never to finish a lie too quickly. A lie finished quickly sounded rehearsed; a lie left slightly ragged, interrupted by appetite or boredom, sounded like truth wearing old clothes.

"You must have been in the capital in the autumn," Veyra said lightly. "Everyone passes through the capital."

"Through, yes. Never long."

"It burned, you know. The archive. October, I think it was — the night of the big rain. Terrible loss. Records going back two hundred years." Veyra turned her spoon over in her fingers. "You'd have been there around then. Where were you that night, Miss... Hartwell, isn't it?"

The question arrived the way a knife arrives at dinner — placed neatly beside the plate, available for use. Around the table, the miller's wife had stopped breathing entirely.

"The seventeenth?" Ivo frowned, and let the frown cost her something, let it look like effort. "The rain night. I remember because my boots leaked and I sat in a station café until nearly dawn complaining to the waitress. Ask anyone at the Kestrel café near the freight yard — I was there often enough they knew my order." This was true. That was the architecture of a good lie: load-bearing walls of fact, with rooms built inside them that had never existed.

"The whole night?"

"Couriers sleep in cafés, Captain. Beds are expensive and trains are late."

Veyra nodded slowly, and wrote nothing down, which frightened Ivo more than writing would have. "Well. Someone should investigate those waitresses' memories. They're better than most clerks'."

* * *

Later, while the lodgers drifted to their rooms, Ivo lingered by the stove, ostensibly reading last month's newspaper. From the kitchen doorway she could see Veyra and Odile at the far end of the table, a pot of tea between them, and she heard the captain say, in that warm reasonable voice:

"—the archive fire, of course. And the tollhouse ledgers missing from the customs office. And now a courier sleeping under your roof whose papers were stamped by a ministry that dissolved in September. You run a very interesting guesthouse, Madame Marchand."

"Interesting is the wrong word," Odile said. "Old. Everything here is old. My ledger is older than the republic, Captain, and it says you owe me for two nights, breakfasts included. Shall I enter it now or do officers pay at departure?"

"You keep a ledger."

"Thirty years of debts and favors. Every soul in this valley is in it somewhere. Even yours will be, by Friday." A pause, the sound of tea being poured. "It's a family habit. We write things down. My grandmother did it during the last occupation. The Austrians searched this house three times and never understood that the book on my counter was worth more than everything in the strongbox."

"And you're telling me this why?"

"Because you're clever, Captain, and clever people are insulted by being underestimated. I'd rather you think me a foolish old woman chattering about her bookkeeping than wonder what else I'm hiding." Another pause. "More tea?"

Ivo turned a page of the newspaper she wasn't reading. *She lies better than I do,* she thought, with something close to awe. *She doesn't build walls. She hands the enemy the door and lets them decide it's a trap.*

* * *

Ivo lay awake past midnight in her narrow room, listening to the house settle. Somewhere below, floorboards creaked — Veyra, awake too, or walking the halls the way captains walk, cataloging doors and distances. Twice, Ivo heard her pause outside the lodgers' rooms. Once, outside hers, the footsteps stopped, and stayed stopped for the length of ten slow heartbeats, and Ivo lay flat with her breath thinned to nothing, the ledger's pages sewn into the lining of her coat pressing against the wall behind her head like a second spine.

Then the steps moved on.

In the morning, the smell of baking bread rose through the floors, and with it the murmur of voices — Veyra and Odile again, easy, almost friendly, two women discussing flour prices and the price of coal and, once, briefly, the name of a man Ivo did not know, followed by a silence with edges on it.

At breakfast, Veyra was charming again. She complimented the bread. She asked Tomas about his schoolwork and praised his handwriting. She thanked the miller's wife for the eggs and asked, gently, whether her husband slept better these days, and the woman's spoon rang against her bowl like a small alarm.

And when the captain finally rose, buttoning her coat, she paused at the door and looked back at the table — at the teacher, at the miller's wife, at Ivo — with that soft river-ice smile.

"Such a peaceful house," she said. "I do hope it stays that way. It would be a shame if anything happened to make me ask harder questions." She settled her cap. "I'll be dining with you all again tomorrow. And the day after. Until the signing, I expect. Consider me — a lodger."

The door closed behind her. No one moved. Outside, her boots crunched away down the frozen street, unhurried, a woman with nowhere she needed to rush.

Odile gathered the plates without a word. But when she passed Ivo's chair, her hand rested on the girl's shoulder for one moment — light, brief, deliberate — and squeezed once.

That evening, when Ivo went down to fetch water, she found a scrap of paper folded inside the flour bin where Odile knew she looked for the rising dough. On it, in the landlady's precise ledger hand, a single line:

*Father Anselm. Tomorrow. Tell him the bread needs blessing.*
