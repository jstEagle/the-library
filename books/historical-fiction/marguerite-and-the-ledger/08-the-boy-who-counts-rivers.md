## 8. The Boy Who Counts Rivers

Tomas Marchand knew the river better than he knew his own face, because the river changed and his face did not. Every morning before school, and every afternoon after, and twice on Sundays when Grand-mère let him, he went down to the bank below the guesthouse garden with his notebook — a proper school notebook, bought in the capital two years ago by a man who had paid for a week's lodging with kindness instead of coin — and he drew what the water had done overnight.

He drew the channels. He drew where the ice had thickened white as chalk and where it stayed dark and thin over the fast water, like a window left open in a wall of stone. He drew the sandbar that surfaced in autumn and drowned again in spring, the one the ferryman called *la langue du diable*, the devil's tongue, because it lay in the middle of the current and licked at boats that came too close. He measured with a knotted string and wrote the numbers small in the margins, and when he ran out of numbers he invented new ones, though he always felt guilty about that, the way you feel guilty about lying to God even when nobody can prove it.

The river was called the Vell. Nobody in Marguerite thought this was interesting. Tomas thought it was the most interesting fact in the world: that the thing they lived beside, the thing that fed them and froze them and carried their grain away to be sold, had a name older than any name in Grand-mère's ledger.

The ledger itself was a kind of river, he suspected. He wasn't allowed to touch it. He wasn't even allowed to look at it for too long, which only meant he looked at it whenever he could, sideways, the way you watch a grown-up crying at a funeral. It sat on its shelf behind the front desk like a fat black cat that might scratch. Names went into it and did not come out. Debts went into it and sometimes came out again, forgiven, crossed through with a single clean line that Grand-mère drew without hesitating, the way Father Anselm blessed things. Tomas had decided long ago that forgiveness was just a kind of arithmetic, and that his grandmother was the best mathematician in the republic, whatever her papers said about her schooling.

On the morning after the ice first groaned all night long — a sound like a giant turning over in bed three rooms away — Tomas found the courier woman crouched at the end of the garden path, looking at the river the way people looked at locked doors.

She'd been in the house nine days now. Nine days of Ivo Hartwell eating breakfast with her back to the wall, walking the corridor on the balls of her feet like a cat who'd learned that floors tattle, saying thank you so precisely each time Grand-mère brought up soup that it sounded like she was paying for it. The lodgers had a private ledger of their own, unwritten, and everyone had entered the same note under her name: *not staying*. Except that nine days was longer than passing through, and the pass was closed, and the bridge was closing, and even the boy who counted rivers could do that sum.

"You're standing where the bank gives way," Tomas said, because warning people was polite and also because he liked being right. "Every winter it sloughs off a little more. In four years there won't be a garden."

She turned. Up close her eyes were the color of the river in February, that green-brown that couldn't decide between mud and moss. "Four years," she repeated. "You keep accounts on the ground, too?"

"I keep accounts on everything." He held up the notebook, slightly defensively, the way other boys held up fists. "It's how you know what's true."

Something moved across her face then, quick as a fish under ice — there and gone before he could name it. Later, much later, he would understand that she had been looking at him the way you look at something you've decided you cannot afford, and that what crossed her face was the price tag.

"May I?" she said, nodding at the notebook.

He gave it over the way he gave nothing else in his life: completely.

* * *

She read him the way he read the river — patiently, from the banks inward. That was the strange part, the part he turned over at night under the blankets like a smooth stone. Grown-ups didn't read him. Grown-ups patted him or corrected him or asked him to carry things. This woman sat down on the cold bench by the dead raspberry canes with her coat buttoned wrong — buttoned wrong! — and turned his pages one at a time with dry careful fingers, and asked questions that were not the questions grown-ups asked.

Not *why do you do this?* but *how do you know the channel shifts east after the first hard frost?*

"Because it does," Tomas said. "It always does."

"Always is not an explanation."

"It is if it's true enough times." He took the notebook back and opened it to November, last year, and showed her the little arrows he'd drawn. "See? Before the frost the main channel runs past the millrace, deep and fast, never freezes. After six nights below —" he squinted at his own marginal numbers, doing them in his head because writing slow answers was for people with worse memories — "below ten degrees, the millrace skins over first, because it's narrow, and then the current has nowhere to go except against the far bank, and it cuts a new channel there, and the old one silts. By January the deep water is where the shallow water was."

Ivo Hartwell was very quiet. The river made its noise underneath them, the muffled grinding of a giant trying to get comfortable.

"And the shallows," she said, and her voice was still casual, still light, but Tomas had spent ten years learning to hear what water said under its skin, and he heard the current change. "Do you map those too?"

"The ford above the chapel? Everyone knows the ford."

"Do they."

"In summer. Cows cross it. But it's not a summer question, is it." He said it mildly, watching her, because he was not stupid; he was eleven, which was different, and the difference was that he noticed more and understood less, and he had learned to trade on both. "In hard frost the shallows freeze to the bottom first — no, wait." He frowned at himself, corrected the sentence the way Grand-mère corrected entries. "They don't freeze to the bottom. They freeze *through*. Solid. White ice, foot-thick, because there's hardly any water under it to freeze. You could walk across at the gravel bar below the willows if the frost held for —" counting — "nine days, ten to be safe. Ten nights below twelve degrees."

"How many have we had?"

"Six." He watched her hands. She had folded them, but one thumb was pressing hard against the other knuckle, pressing and pressing, like she was taking his pulse through her own skin. "Seven tonight, if the sky stays clear. Clear skies are colder. Clouds are blankets. Didn't anyone teach you weather where you're from?"

"No," she said. "Where I'm from we mostly taught each other to be quiet." And then she smiled, quickly, as if apologizing for the truth, and stood up, and said she had letters to write, and went inside, and Tomas sat on the frozen bench with his heart going strangely and wrote in the margin of today's page, in his smallest hand: *Day seven. Clear.*

* * *

That afternoon he did something he had never done. He walked her to school.

Not exactly to school. To the corner where the lane bent and the schoolhouse bell could be heard and the escorting would become embarrassing. But he walked beside her down the high street, and it was market day, thin as markets were now with half the shops shuttered since the garrison requisitions began, and he watched her see the town.

Everyone watches a town differently. Grand-mère saw debts. Father Anselm saw souls. Captain Veyra — who had arrived two days ago with her beautiful grey coat and her beautiful terrible smile and taken the blue room — saw, Tomas was fairly sure, exits. Ivo Hartwell saw the town the way he saw the river: channels, depths, places where the surface lied.

"That baker," she said quietly. "His window's full but his chimney doesn't smoke."

"He bakes at night," Tomas said. "For the garrison. They pay him in flour he doesn't have to account for. Everybody knows." A pause. "Except everybody pretends they don't, so it isn't gossip, it's just weather."

"Is that how it works here? If everyone already knows, it isn't a secret?"

"If everyone knows and nobody says, it's a foundation," Tomas said, pleased with the phrase, having built it himself out of stones he'd found lying around his grandmother's kitchen. "You can build a house on it. Say one word out loud and the whole house comes down on your head."

She stopped walking. Just for a step, just a hitch, like someone catching a root under snow. Then she kept going, and he kept going beside her, and neither of them said anything until the corner, where the bell was ringing and the other children were streaming past with their breath smoking, and one of them, Lise Aubry, looked at Ivo Hartwell with wide eyes and then looked at Tomas with wider ones, and Tomas understood that by evening the whole town would know the courier woman had walked to the school corner with Odile Marchand's grandson, and that this was either nothing at all or the most important thing that had happened in Marguerite in a month, depending entirely on which channel the information froze into overnight.

"Tomas." She crouched a little, so their eyes were level, which no adult did; adults loomed or knelt formally, and crouching was something else, something that admitted you might want to run in either direction. "The maps in your book. Do you show them to people?"

"Lise saw them once. She said I'd drown someday and she hoped she got my boots."

"Would you show them to me again? All of them? Tonight, after supper?"

"Why?" He asked it directly because he had earned directness; he had shown her the devil's tongue and the millrace and day seven, clear. "Why does a courier care where the river is shallow?"

And here was the thing he would remember all his life, the hinge of everything: she didn't lie to him. He watched the lie come up in her like a bubble through dark water — he actually saw it form, saw her mouth open around the shape of some easy grown-up nothing — and then she swallowed it, and what came out instead was silence, three whole seconds of it, ringing, while somewhere behind them the baker's shutter banged in the wind.

"I can't tell you," she said finally. "That's not a lie, it's the truth wearing a locked door. But I'll tell you this much. Someday the river may matter more than anything else in this valley. And if that day comes, I would rather know what you know than guess at it."

Tomas considered this with the seriousness it deserved. Around them the schoolyard emptied, the bell's last echo dying over the rooftops, and the wind came down off the pass smelling of iron and coming snow.

"All right," he said. "But if you use my maps, you take me with you. At least partway. Because the numbers aren't enough. The ice changes its mind. You need someone who can hear it changing."

"That is not a negotiation a responsible adult would accept."

"I know," Tomas said. "That's why I offered it to you."

* * *

Supper that night was venison stew, thin and good, and the blue room's occupant came down to share it, which she did every third night, making conversation like a woman laying paving stones — flat, even, load-bearing. Captain Veyra asked Tomas about his schooling, his health, whether he slept well, whether he had ever been across the river in winter, whether he loved his grandmother, whether he loved his country, in that order, each question arriving dressed so beautifully he almost didn't feel it go through him.

"I love the river," Tomas said, "and the river belongs to whoever crosses it," which was a sentence he invented on the spot and which made Ivo Hartwell set down her spoon and made Captain Veyra laugh her silver laugh and say, *what a republican little fish you are,* and made Grand-mère, at the head of the table, say nothing at all in a way that filled the room like smoke.

Afterward, in the kitchen, while Grand-mère washed dishes and the fire ticked, Tomas spread his notebook on the scrubbed table under the lamp, and Ivo Hartwell pulled her chair close, and for two hours they went through it page by page, season by season, year by year. She asked him to say the numbers aloud while she looked away, and he realized she was memorizing them, sealing them somewhere behind her eyes the way he sealed the channels, and he felt the strange hot pride of being useful to something enormous whose shape he couldn't see.

At the November pages she stopped. Her finger rested on the gravel bar below the willows, on his little dotted line of a crossing, on *nine days, ten to be safe.*

"Day seven tonight," she said softly. Not a question.

"Clear skies," Tomas said. "If it holds till Thursday, the ice will hold you."

"*Us,*" she said, absently, correcting herself the way he corrected entries, and then heard what she'd said, and went still.

Grand-mère set a dish down in its rack with a click like a lock turning. She hadn't turned around. She didn't need to. In that kitchen, with the lamp low and the wind worrying the shutters, Tomas watched the two of them across the width of a notebook full of rivers — his grandmother who kept every debt ever owed in Marguerite, and the courier woman who owed one now, though nobody had named it yet — and he understood, with the terrible clarity of the young, that something had just been decided in this room, and that he had drawn the map it would follow, and that no one had asked him whether he wanted the ice to hold.

Outside, the river ground and shifted in the dark, finding its winter channels, patient as arithmetic.

And down the street, past the shuttered bakery and the sleeping tollhouse, a light was burning late in the guesthouse office that was not theirs — the blue room's occupant, awake, writing in a book of her own.

Tomas blew out the lamp and went to bed, and dreamed of white ice holding, and of the sound it makes, just before it doesn't.
