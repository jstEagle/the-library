## 9. Checkpoint Two: The Chapel Steps

The copying took three nights, and Ivo hated every hour of it.

She worked in the narrow hours after the garrison's patrol lamps had made their second pass along the river road, when the guesthouse settled into the particular silence of a house full of sleepers who were all, in their own ways, pretending. She sat at the little table beneath the window with a candle shielded behind Odile's flour tin and copied the ledger's damning pages onto thin paper bought from the stationer under the pretense of writing letters home — a home that did not exist, to people who did not know she was alive.

Her hand was steady. That was the strange part. Ten years of forged transit papers, of invented names written in invented hands, had given her a forger's discipline: the ability to make her wrist obey her will even when her will was screaming. The entries came out of her in a fair, unremarkable script, indistinguishable from any clerk's — *Colonel Amsel, paid in grain, October 1917, for the transport of prisoners north; Captain Veyra's predecessor, receipted for munitions moved through Marguerite without manifest; Brigadier Halloran, whose signature appeared on a requisition dated three days before her brother's name appeared on nothing at all.*

That last one she copied twice. Her pen pressed too hard on the second pass, and the ink bled through, and she let it bleed. Some things deserved to be legible from both sides of the page.

By the third night she had eleven sheets. She read them against the original by candlelight, entry by entry, number by number, until she could recite half of them with her eyes closed — which was the point, though she didn't yet admit it to herself. Paper burned. Memory, if you were stubborn enough about it, did not.

Then she folded the copies into oilcloth the size of a prayer book and waited for Sunday.

* * *

Father Anselm's chapel stood at the top of the mill hill, a squat stone building older than any nation currently claiming it, its bell cracked since before the war and rung anyway, out of defiance or habit or both. Ivo had attended Mass twice since arriving in Marguerite — once because couriers went to church, churches being where strangers became neighbors, and once because she had genuinely wanted to sit somewhere warm where no one asked her questions.

She understood now, watching the congregation file up the hill through new snow, that the chapel was something else entirely. Half the town was there, breath steaming, boots squeaking. And threaded among them, unmistakable once you knew to look, were people carrying letters.

It was Tomas who showed her, in his way. The boy walked ahead of her up the hill with his school notebook buttoned inside his coat, and at the door he stopped to let an old woman pass, and the old woman pressed something flat into his mitten with the smoothness of long practice, and Tomas carried it inside and slipped it into the pew behind the kneeler, and never looked down once. He was nine years old and he had been doing this, Ivo realized, probably since he could walk. The whole town was a post office with a cracked bell.

"You're staring," Odile murmured beside her, not looking at her either.

"I'm learning."

"Learn quietly." Odile smoothed her skirt over her knees. "And whatever you're planning — and don't insult me by denying it, girl, I've watched you count the exits of my own parlor for two weeks — do it after the blessing. Father Anselm takes his time with the blessing when there's a reason to."

Ivo turned to look at her then. Odile faced the altar with her hands folded, her face composed into the mild attention of a woman listening to scripture, and Ivo understood that she had just been given permission, and instructions, and a warning, all in eleven words, none of which would ever be acknowledged aloud.

"I need to speak with him," Ivo said, barely above breath.

"After the blessing," Odile said again, and closed her eyes.

* * *

Mass was longer than it needed to be, and Ivo suspected every minute of the extra length was deliberate. Father Anselm was a small man, sixty-odd, with ink-stained fingers and a voice like dry leaves, and he preached that morning on the Gospel passage about the talents buried in the ground — on the servant who hid what he'd been given because he was afraid, and how the master called him wicked and lazy, and how the ones who risked, who put their portion out into the world where it could be taken or multiplied, were the ones welcomed in.

He said it mildly. He said it looking at no one. And Ivo felt the words land on her like a hand on the shoulder.

Afterward the congregation drained slowly down the hill, and Ivo lingered as the slowest of the stragglers, helping Odile gather gloves and hymnals, until the chapel held only the priest, the two of them, and the smell of candle wax and cold stone.

"Madame Marchand." Anselm kissed Odile's cheek like a brother. Then he looked at Ivo, and his eyes were quick and gray and entirely without surprise. "And you are the young woman who prays like a soldier. Knees together, eyes on the doors. Sit down, child, before someone sees you standing so straight."

"I need to move something south," Ivo said. "Eleven pages. They have to reach the delegation before the signing, and they can't go through the tollhouse, and they can't go in my coat, because I'm the one person in this town the captain is already curious about."

Anselm did not ask what the pages said. That was the first mercy. He poured himself a cup of tea from the pot warming on the sacristy stove, poured two more without asking, and sat down across from her with the unhurried air of a man who had been waiting years for this conversation.

"The line runs twice a month," he said. "Wednesday and Saturday, after vespers. Letters go down in the candle crates to the convent at Saint-Aubin, and from there—" He waved a hand, southward, onward, into the machinery of whatever quiet network kept ink moving through a country dissolving at its seams. "The sisters don't read them. I don't always know what I carry. It's better that way. God reads everything; He doesn't need my help."

"These pages can't be lost."

"No," Anselm agreed. "They can't." He sipped his tea. "Which is why you'll understand when I tell you the answer is no."

Ivo set down her cup. "Father—"

"Not no forever. No for today." His voice stayed gentle, but underneath it was bedrock. "Captain Veyra arrived eight days ago, and in eight days she has charmed the blacksmith, dined with the miller, and asked me — smiling, over brandy — whether I still correspond with my old seminary friend in the capital. Which I do. Which means she knows the line exists, even if she can't prove what it carries. If I move your pages Wednesday, I move them under her eyes. And if she finds them, she finds them *with you attached*, child, because she has already decided what you are. She's simply waiting for you to confirm it."

"So I should sit here and wait for spring while the armistice gets signed over their graves."

"I should wait," Anselm said, "and pray, and use the time God has given you to think instead of bolt." He leaned forward. "You've been running for ten years. I can see it in how you sit — like a chair might be pulled away. Running teaches a person speed. It does not teach a person judgment. You want to send those pages out because sending them feels like doing something. But the question isn't what feels like action. The question is what survives."

Ivo stared at him. Behind him, the morning light came through the east window in dusty red and gold, falling across the altar, across the ledger pages hidden in her coat, across everything, equally, like something that had no side in the matter at all.

"If I wait," she said slowly, "and the bridge opens, and I cross with the originals myself—"

"Then the copies are insurance. Nothing more." Anselm smiled, and it transformed his whole face into something shrewd and kind. "But you'll make them anyway. And you'll bring them to me, and I'll keep them in the one place in Marguerite even Captain Veyra hesitates to search — because searching a sacristy is a statement, and statements are expensive, and that woman counts costs better than anyone I've ever fed. Bring them Saturday. After vespers. Come in through the side door like a woman bringing candles."

* * *

She never got her Saturday.

Veyra came to the chapel on Friday.

Ivo heard it first as a change in the quality of the afternoon — the bell ringing for none of the usual hours, and then the sound of boots on the mill hill road, more boots than a patrol, moving with the particular unhurried confidence of people who already owned whatever they were walking toward. She was in the guesthouse kitchen when Odile appeared in the doorway, flour to her elbows, and said only: "Chapel. Now. Through the orchard."

They came up through the trees behind the churchyard wall, snow squeaking underfoot, and reached the side door as Captain Veyra's voice floated out through the open front doors, warm as mulled wine.

"—no cause for alarm, Father, none at all. Merely routine. The colonel likes thoroughness before a ceremony, and I do so hate to disappoint the colonel."

Ivo pressed herself against the cold stone of the side porch, Odile's grip iron on her sleeve. Through the gap in the door she could see the nave: six soldiers fanning out between the pews with the methodical boredom of men searching a place they expected to be clean, and Veyra herself standing in the aisle in her gray greatcoat, hat in her hands like a woman paying respects, her smile aimed at Father Anselm, who stood at the altar steps with his hands folded and his face arranged into patient sorrow.

"Routine," Anselm said. "Of course. Though I confess, Captain, I don't know what routine requires in a church. We keep candles, vestments, and God's forgiveness. Only the last of those is dangerous, and you're welcome to search for it."

A ripple of laughter from the soldiers. Even Veyra's smile deepened a degree. "You're wasted on a rural parish, Father. Come. Walk with me. Show me your beautiful church, and we'll both be bored together, and my men can freeze in peace."

And they walked. Down the aisle, past the pews where the town's letters lay hidden in a dozen seams and hollows, Veyra's gloved hand trailing along the wood like a lover's. Ivo's mind ran the map frantically — the pew hollows, the confessional panel, the loose flagstone by the font where she'd watched Tomas slip a note two Sundays ago — cataloguing everything in that building that could hang someone, and finding it all suddenly, catastrophically exposed.

"We should leave," she breathed.

"We should stay," Odile said. "If we're seen leaving now, we're seen *running*. There's one way out of this church that doesn't look like flight, and it's through the front door on God's arm. Come."

And Odile Marchand, seventy-one years old, walked her through the side door into the nave itself, into plain sight, and steered them both toward the second pew from the back, and knelt.

So Ivo knelt.

The stone floor bit through her skirts. She bowed her head, folded her hands, and became, with ten years of practice, exactly what she appeared to be: a lodger, a stranger, a woman at her devotions, boring as bread. Her heart hammered so hard she could feel it in her teeth. Twenty feet away, Veyra's voice drifted through the sacristy arch, admiring the vestments, asking after the candle inventory, laughing at something Anselm said — and beneath the charm, audible now that Ivo knew to listen for it, the sound of drawers opening. Of paper being lifted and squared and set down. Of a search conducted as hospitality.

The soldiers worked outward from the altar. Ivo tracked them by their reflections in the dark window glass — a trick from another life, another war — watching one man run his hand along the pew backs, watching another crouch at the confessional and test the panel with his thumb. The panel gave. The soldier reached in and drew out a bundle of letters tied in twine, and Ivo's stomach dropped through the frozen earth, and beside her Odile did not so much as shift her weight.

"Captain." The soldier held the bundle up.

Veyra emerged from the sacristy. She took the letters, thumbed through them — and Ivo saw her face do something complicated and brief, there and gone, some private arithmetic — before the smile reassembled itself.

"Parish correspondence," she said lightly. "Requests for marriage banns, I expect. Prayers for rain." She weighed the bundle in her hand, and Ivo watched her decide — watched the exact moment the cost of making a scene in front of a kneeling congregation outweighed the value of a handful of unopened letters. "Return them to the Father. We wouldn't want to delay anyone's wedding."

She turned, and her gaze swept the nave one last time — and caught on the two women in the second pew.

For a moment, nothing. Snow-melt dripped somewhere. The candle flames leaned.

"Madame Marchand," Veyra said pleasantly, crossing to them, her boots loud on the stone. "How devoted. And your lodger — the courier, isn't she? The one waiting for the bridge." She looked down at Ivo kneeling on the cold floor, and her eyes were pale and patient and absolutely certain of her own victory, whenever she chose to collect it. "How is the waiting going, Miss Hartwell? Spring must feel very far away."

"Closer every day, Captain," Ivo said, and raised her head, and met the pale eyes with the flat calm of a woman who had knelt in worse places than churches. "I find the chapel helps."

Something flickered across Veyra's face — amusement, or its twin. "They usually do," she said. "That's rather the tragedy of them." She settled her hat back on her head. "Enjoy your prayers, ladies. Father — thank you for your hospitality. My men will see themselves out."

The boots receded down the aisle. The front doors boomed shut. Cold air rushed in and the candle flames stood up straight again, and for a long moment nobody in the chapel moved.

Then Father Anselm walked to the second pew, untied the twine from the returned bundle of letters, and slid them one by one back into the pew hollows with steady, ink-stained fingers.

"Well," he said quietly. "Now she knows the shape of the room. Whatever you meant to do, child — do it tonight."
