## 12. The Town Decides

The tavern on the square had no name that anyone remembered, so they called it the tavern, the way they called the river the river and winter winter — things too constant to need christening. It was Brannoc's habit to arrive early, before the lamplighter made his rounds, to take the bench nearest the forge-side wall where the stones held a ghost of heat from his own fire across the lane. Tonight he came late instead, because his hands had needed washing twice, and even then they smelled of iron and quenching-water, of the day's work: four hinges for the chapel door, a new shoe for Father Anselm's mule, and — though he would deny it if asked — a long iron bar cut to length without any customer's order, as if his hands knew something his mouth did not.

The room was fuller than he had ever seen it. Not festive-full, not wedding-full. Full the way a lung is full before the cough.

He counted without meaning to, the way a smith counts strokes: the miller Halvard and his wife Marta pressed into the corner by the stairs, Halvard's hat wrung in his fists like a bird with a broken neck; Sergeant Pell's father, old Tomas-not-the-boy but the elder Tomas Ferreol, who had lent Odile Marchand forty crowns against a bad harvest in '09 and never once mentioned it since; the schoolteacher Vidal; three widows from the tannery row; the ferryman's widow in her black wool that had gone grey at the cuffs; and along the back wall, quiet as furniture, two men Brannoc did not know by name but knew by posture — garrison men out of uniform, or informers, or both. Their presence sat in the room like a stone dropped in a well. Everyone talked around it.

Captain Veyra's proclamation had been nailed to the customs post at noon: *Any person delivering information regarding the contents of certain civic records to parties beyond the armistice commission will be considered an enemy of the transitional authority. Any person surrendering such records voluntarily will be regarded as a friend of the same.* The word *friend* had been inked with a flourish. Someone had already thrown mud at it. The mud had frozen there, a brown medal over the captain's elegant signature.

Brannoc took his bench. Marta caught his eye across the room and looked away too quickly, and he thought: *there*, there is the crack in the evening already, there is the thing that will happen whether we say it aloud or not. A town is a forge. You can bank the coals or you can let them scatter, but you cannot pretend they are cold.

* * *

It was Halvard who stood first, which surprised no one who knew what the miller had been carrying since the ice closed the bridge. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, flour in the creases of his knuckles permanently now, and when he rose his chair legs shrieked and every conversation died at once.

"Friends," he said. "Neighbors." His voice cracked on the second word and he steadied it with a swallow of beer he didn't want. "I'll say what everyone's chewing on. Say it plain and be done." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "The ledger. Odile's book. Give it to the captain."

The silence afterward had texture. Brannoc felt it against his skin like the first cold breath off the river.

"Give it," said Vidal the schoolteacher, not quite a question, not quite anything else.

"To buy goodwill." Halvard's hands were shaking and he gripped the back of his chair to hide it. "Veyra says friend. Friend means my sons keep their billets, means the requisitions pass us by, means when the delegation comes through Thursday nobody's door gets kicked in looking for papers. I have two boys. Seventeen and fourteen. You've all seen them haul sacks." His voice climbed, pleading now, ashamed of its own pleading. "One book. Old women's debts and dead men's favors. What is it against two living boys?"

"It isn't old women's debts," said the ferryman's widow, very quietly, and Halvard flinched as if she'd struck him, because everyone in the room knew whose debts were written in that ledger, and how high up they ran, and why a captain of the transitional garrison wanted them so gently, so reasonably, so *badly*.

"They're only words," Halvard said. "Ink on paper. Burn the thing ourselves and we're clean. Nobody can hang a town for a book that doesn't exist."

And that was the sentence, Brannoc thought later, that turned the night. Because Halvard had said *burn*, and the word went through the room like a spark through chaff, and you could see each face take it differently — some with relief, some with horror, and in more faces than you'd expect, both.

Old Ferreol spoke next, from his corner, without standing. He was nearly deaf and shouted slightly, which gave everything he said an odd authority. "I owe that book forty crowns," he announced. "Borrowed against the '09 harvest. Never paid. Odile never once sent her boy or her grandson to my door. Forty years I could have been hounded. Instead she wrote it down and left me my dignity." He thumped his stick. "You burn a man's memory of what he owes, you don't free him. You orphan him."

"She'd hand it over herself if it saved your sons," someone said — one of the quiet men by the back wall, smooth-voiced, and the room went still again, because that voice knew things it shouldn't know, and everyone understood at once exactly how much danger the evening was in, and how far past talking they already were.

* * *

Odile Marchand had been sitting near the fire the whole time, in the third booth, with a cup of mulled wine going cold in front of her. Brannoc realized he hadn't heard her say a single word. She had listened the way she read — completely, without blinking, letting people finish their sentences even inside themselves.

Now she set down the cup, and the small sound of ceramic on wood silenced the room faster than shouting would have.

She did not stand quickly. She was seventy-one years old and her knees had opinions, and she stood the way she did everything, deliberately, as if the action were being entered somewhere in a record. She smoothed her apron. She looked at Halvard with something that was not unkindness, which was somehow worse.

"You're right about one thing," she said. "They're only words. Ink and paper." She paused. "So were the orders that took the Vasseur boy last spring. So was the list of names posted outside the commissary in the first month of occupation. Words. Somebody's ink. And every one of them signed by a man who is now sitting in a warm room in the capital, negotiating peace on our behalf, and calling himself a moderate."

Nobody breathed.

"I brought the book," Odile said.

Brannoc's heart stopped and restarted sideways. She had brought it. Into this room, with those two quiet men against the wall, with Halvard half-turned informer and half-turned father — she had carried thirty years of Marguerite under her shawl like a loaf of bread.

She opened it on the table in the third booth, and did not hold it up, and did not raise her voice. That was the terrible genius of her. She simply began to read, in the flat clear tone of a woman calling out a bill of sale, and the room leaned toward her the way a field leans toward rain.

"Entry the eleventh of October, 1916. Received of Colonel Aurel Stenn, Fourth Mountain Regiment, payment in kind against debt incurred: silence, regarding the disposition of supply stores requisitioned from this district, stores which reached his regiment and not the civilian population as ordered." She looked up. "Colonel Stenn sits at the armistice table tonight. Third seat from the chairman. They call him the humanitarian."

A sound went through the room, low, like ice shifting on the river.

"Entry the second of March, 1919. Debt acknowledged by Prefect Lassaire: two hundred crowns, drawn against funds designated for bridge repair, disbursed instead to persons unnamed, in consideration of which this house agrees to strike from its pages certain entries concerning the prefect's... associates." Her finger rested on the line. "I never struck them. I wrote his confession instead, and filed it where he'll never find it. Prefect Lassaire is one of the signatories of the document they mean to sign Thursday. Peace with his name on it. Peace bought with bridge money while the bridge fell down and drowned the Kessler family crossing it."

Marta Halvard made a small noise and put her hand over her mouth.

"And the third," Odile said, and here her voice changed, and everyone heard it change, the way you hear a floorboard give warning before the stair breaks, "the third entry I will read for the miller especially, since he worries for his sons. Entry the ninth of November, 1920. Captain Serafina Veyra, then a lieutenant, billeted in this house during the census actions. Settled her account in full, save one item, which she asked me to hold open until such time as she returned. Item: one favor, unspecified, owed to this house and callable at any hour, in perpetuity." Odile closed the book gently, both hands on its cover, a woman laying a child down to sleep. "Your Captain Veyra has been in my debt for fourteen months. She knows it. That is why she wants the book so politely. Not to protect you. To erase what she owes."

* * *

The room did not erupt. Brannoc had expected eruption — shouting, chairs, Halvard storming out. Instead the tavern went very quiet, the particular quiet of many people arriving at the same thought at the same moment, and the thought was this: *she cannot arrest us all.*

Old Ferreol laughed first, a dry bark, deaf-man loud. "Forty crowns," he said happily. "Let the whole army come hear it."

Then Vidal the schoolteacher stood, and in his classroom voice, the one that carried to the back benches without effort, said: "I move that the ledger of Odile Marchand be declared the common property of this town, that its keeping be shared among us, and that any hand raised against it answers to every hand in this room."

"Aye," said Brannoc, and his voice came out like the hammer coming down, and it startled him, how easy it was, how long he had been waiting to say it.

"Aye," said Marta Halvard, standing beside her husband, her chin wet but her spine straight, and Halvard looked at his wife, and at his sons' futures, and at the book, and put his big flour-white hand flat on the table and said, "Aye," and meant it, or would learn to mean it, which in Marguerite amounted to the same thing.

The ferryman's widow said aye. The tannery widows said aye. Even the boy sweeping the floor, who couldn't have been more than twelve, said aye in a squeak that broke the tension into something almost like laughter — almost.

Only the two men by the back wall did not speak. They were leaving. Brannoc watched them go, unhurried, coats buttoned, and felt the cold walk in behind them like a draft. Whatever happened next would happen fast, and it would come at night, and it would come for the weakest door first.

Odile gathered her book and wrapped it back in her shawl. As she passed Brannoc's bench she paused, and her hand rested a moment on his shoulder, light as ash.

"Thursday," she said, "they bring the delegation through the square. Whatever you've been cutting iron for, blacksmith — finish it."

She was gone into the snow before he could answer. Outside, far up the river road, a lantern swung — a patrol, early, or a messenger running ahead of worse. The tavern door shut. The fire settled. And every soul left in the room understood, without another word being spoken, that the countdown had begun.
