## 13. Betrayal at the Mill

Sela Ferrant had ground wheat for the garrison every winter since she was seventeen, and she knew the sound a man makes when he has already decided — that particular quiet, the way her husband's breathing changed in the dark before he spoke. So when Aldous came up from the millrace on the fourth night after Father Anselm's arrest was still only a rumor, stood dripping at the kitchen door with flour in his hair and frost in his beard, and said nothing at all, Sela set down her mending and waited.

"The boys," he said.

She knew. Of course she knew. He had been saying *the boys* for three days now, the way a man worries a loose tooth — at supper, at the forge-adjacent gossip of the tavern doorway, in his sleep. The boys: Piet, nineteen, who drove the grain cart and had once, stupidly, drunkenly, told a corporal in Veyra's company that his father could move anything across this river if the price was right; and Janek, sixteen, who had been caught carrying a letter for the priest without knowing what was in it, and had been let go with a warning so gentle it was worse than a blow.

"They haven't touched them," Sela said.

"Not yet." Aldous sat heavily, his wet boots marking the floorboards she'd scrubbed that morning. "Not yet is not never."

The lamp between them guttered. Outside, the river groaned under its new skin of ice, that long whale-song moan the whole town pretended not to hear anymore, though no one slept through it either. Sela thought of the letter sewn into the hem of the gray coat upstairs — no, that wasn't right, that was the other thing, that was what she was supposed to be able to say she didn't know about. She pressed her palms flat against her apron.

"What are you asking me?" she said.

"I'm not asking." His voice cracked like the ice did. "That's the shame of it, Sela. I'm telling you."

* * *

Here is what Sela Ferrant would remember afterward, in the years when remembering was all Marguerite had left: not the decision itself, which arrived less like a decision than like weather, but the walk. The walk down the frozen lane to the tollhouse annex where Captain Veyra kept her office, past the chapel where light still burned — Father Anselm keeping his vigil as though God needed reminding — past the well where Tomas Marchand had chalked his little maps of the river channels until the frost licked them away, past everything she loved, carrying the thing that would begin its unmaking.

Her boots made the only sound in town. Even the dogs were quiet.

Captain Veyra received her in a room that smelled of ink and orange peel, absurdly, and offered her a chair before Sela had finished removing her hood. The captain was younger than her reputation. She had a way of looking at a person as though reading them aloud, silently, checking for errors.

"Mistress Ferrant." A smile, warm as a stove. "Your husband's mill has fed my men all season. Sit. You're shaking."

"I've come about my sons."

"Ah." Veyra folded her hands. She did not pretend not to know what Sela meant. That was the horror of her, Sela would think later — she never lied when the truth worked better. "Piet and Janek. Good boys. Strong boys. Piet especially — such a talkative boy. I do enjoy him."

"You have them listed." Sela's mouth had gone dry as bran. "On some list. For questioning."

"There are many lists." Veyra rose, went to the window, where the river showed white to the far bank. "There's a list of men who can carry grain. There's a list of men who can carry letters. Sometimes a boy appears on both lists, and then one must decide which list matters more. Do you know what I find, Mistress Ferrant? In my experience? Mothers decide faster than fathers. Fathers need to feel noble about it first. Mothers just want their children alive." She turned back. "You walked here alone."

It was not a question, but Sela answered it anyway, because there was nothing left in her that could lie. "I walked here alone."

"And?"

And so Sela gave her the chapel route. All of it. The loose stone behind the sacristy where Father Anselm passed letters out to the boy who swept the schoolhouse; the timing, matins and vespers, when the priest's movements explained any loiterer; the fact — this was the coin Aldous had actually sold, the piece Piet's drunken tongue had bought — that pages were moving through the chapel now, copies of something older and larger, bound for the north bank, and that the woman lodging at Widow Marchand's was the hand behind them.

Veyra listened without writing anything down, which Sela understood, dimly, was the worst sign of all. A written note could be lost. What Veyra carried out of that room she would carry forever.

"You've done your sons a great service," the captain said at the door, and pressed two oranges into Sela's hands — oranges, in this winter, in this starving republic — and Sela walked home holding them like coals.

She put them on the table and looked at them until Aldous came in, and neither of them ate, and neither of them slept, and the oranges sat there gleaming in the lamplight like evidence.

* * *

They came for Father Anselm on Friday, during the morning service.

Sela was there because she could not stay away — because some part of her needed to watch the wound she'd made, the way a child presses a bruise. The chapel was half full, breath rising silver above the pews, old Marta Kessel's cough keeping time with the liturgy. Father Anselm stood thin and straight at the altar, and when the doors opened and six soldiers came in with their boots ringing on the flagstones, he did not even turn around.

"Father Anselm." Veyra herself stood in the doorway, bareheaded, courteous. "By authority of the provisional command. You'll come quietly."

The congregation froze. Somewhere a baby began to cry and was hushed too fast. Sela's hands found each other in her lap and held on.

The priest turned then, unhurried, and looked at the soldiers, and then — Sela felt this look land on her own breastbone like a nail — at the congregation, gently, the way he looked at everyone, as if each face were a page he was sorry to finish.

"Gentlemen," he said to the soldiers, "you've interrupted the Kyrie. We'll resume when I return." And to Veyra, gathering his stole off his shoulders with those careful scholar's hands: "May I ask what is sought?"

"Certain papers."

"Then I'll save you the trouble of the sacristy." He smiled, tired and true. "The key is in my pocket. I'd rather hand it to you than have you break the lock. It's older than either of us."

Veyra searched him anyway — thoroughly, publicly, while the town watched — and found nothing on his person at all, and something flickered across her face then that Sela would turn over and over in memory: not triumph. Disappointment. As if she had expected the story to go differently, and was annoyed at being handed a simpler one.

They took him out through his own doors, past his own altar, and the congregation sat in the terrible silence of people rehearsing their innocence. Old Brannoc the blacksmith rose halfway from his pew and sat back down, his jaw working, his huge hands gripping his knees. Sela saw Odile Marchand near the back, upright as a fence post, her face giving away precisely nothing — and that nothing frightened Sela more than weeping would have.

* * *

They found the papers in the sacristy by afternoon. Everyone knew by evening, because Veyra wanted everyone to know: seven pages, folded inside the false bottom of the vestment chest, in a neat clerk's hand — lists of names, sums, dates going back months, correspondence that implicated half the northern delegation in payments and promises. The sergeant read extracts aloud at the tollhouse steps. By nightfall the tavern hummed with it: the priest had been running documents, the priest had been paid, the quiet saint of Marguerite was an agent all along.

Sela heard it standing in her own doorway, and felt the strange double weight she supposed every betrayer must carry: relief and revulsion, perfectly balanced, perfectly useless.

"It's done," Aldous said behind her. "It's done and the boys are safe. Piet came home an hour ago. They released him without a word."

"Yes."

"Sela." He touched her shoulder. "It's done."

But she kept staring across the lane at the chapel, dark now, its windows like closed eyes, and she thought about the seven pages, and something nagged at her the way a wrong note nags at a singer. She had seen Ivo Hartwell copy pages — she hadn't meant to see, but she had, through the guesthouse window one gray afternoon, the courier bent over the ledger with the patience of a scribe, tracing line after line in her small fierce hand.

Seven pages. Sela counted backward through everything she knew, everything Aldous had sold, everything Veyra had wanted. Seven pages seized in a single chest, in a single hiding place, discovered within hours of an arrest that had produced no papers on the man himself.

A man who kept his keys in his pocket and offered them freely does not hide documents badly. A man who runs letters past three checkpoints for years does not fold his life's work into one box.

"Oh," Sela said softly, into the dark, where no one could hear her and be damned along with her. "Oh, they're false. They're decoys. She planted them."

She didn't know how she knew. She knew the way one knows bread is burning — by smell, by dread, by the sudden certainty that the house is already filling with smoke. The real pages were somewhere else entirely. They were already moving, or about to move, or moving tonight, over the ice—

Over the ice.

Tomas Marchand's chalked maps. The shallows below the old weir, where the current ran fast enough to keep the ice thin — no, thick, safe, the boy had argued about it with his friends outside the schoolhouse while Sela shelled beans two windows away, *the shallows freeze first and hold best, my grandfather proved it with a cart horse.* The river road that existed only in a child's notebook.

And tomorrow, or the next night, or whenever the frost hardened one degree further, a woman with a courier's satchel and a dead brother's silence would walk out onto the white river with the truth sewn into her coat, and the whole garrison would be watching the bridge, and the roads, and the chapel that no longer mattered — watching everywhere except the one place a boy's arithmetic had made possible.

Sela stood in her doorway with the taste of oranges still sweet and rotten in her mouth, and understood that she had saved her sons by selling a lie she hadn't known was a lie, and that the truth was leaving town without her, and that there might be — there had to be — one thing left she could do.

She went inside, took down her lantern, and woke her husband.

"Aldous," she said. "We have to warn the Marchands. Tonight. Before the patrol lamps go out on the bank."
