## 15. The Cost of Crossing

The ice held them for the first hundred yards the way a promise holds — completely, until it didn't.

Ivo went first, the way she had insisted, though Tomas had argued with the particular stubbornness of a boy who had mapped this river in his school notebook and considered it, in some private and unassailable sense, his. He walked three paces behind and one to the left, exactly where he had told her to walk, because the channel bent there and the current beneath ran thin and fast and kept the ice honest only in the coldest hours. It was the coldest hour now. The stars were hard and close. Their breath came out in small white flags that the wind took south, toward the town they were leaving, toward the checkpoint they were not using, toward everything the ledger in her coat was meant to escape.

She could feel it against her ribs with every step. The true book. Not the decoy pages she had planted in the sacristy for Father Anselm's line to carry — those were ash and misdirection now, seized with the priest, God keep him — but the original, Odile's thirty years of debts and favors and names, sewn into the lining of her coat in oilcloth in three flat packets, riding against her heart like a second, heavier pulse.

"Count your steps," Tomas whispered behind her, though she had not asked him to. He counted everything. Rivers, bridges, the seconds between lightning and thunder, the days since his father's name had been struck from a list. "Forty-one. Forty-two. The channel's under us now. Don't stop, even if it sounds wrong."

It sounded wrong at fifty-three.

Not a crack — she would have known a crack, would have thrown herself flat the way the ferryman had taught her the one winter she'd crossed the northern ice as a girl, before silence became her trade. This was softer than a crack. A sound like a sigh, like snow settling off a bough, like the whole river exhaling beneath them. The ice did not break so much as *open*, the way a hand opens, and Tomas — who had been mid-count, *fifty-four* — simply dropped out of the world.

The sound he made was worse than the sound the ice made. Not a scream. A kind of astonished grunt, the noise of a boy whose body had been taken from him without consultation, and then the river had him, and the black water closed over the place where he had been standing, and Ivo was already moving.

She did not remember deciding to move. Later, in the hut, she would try to reconstruct it and fail: the drop to her knees, the flat spread of her weight across ice that groaned and flexed under her like a living thing, the satchel strap torn from her shoulder and thrown out across the weak patch so that it lay bridging the hole, the cold closing around her forearm to the elbow as she plunged it in. The river in winter is not cold the way a winter night is cold. The night is cold the way a wall is high. The river is cold the way a fist is a fist. It took her arm and squeezed the warmth out of it in the space of one heartbeat, and in that same heartbeat her hand found cloth, found hair, found the back of a collar, and she pulled.

Tomas came up gasping, and the gasp was the dangerous thing — she had heard drowning men described, had read the reports, and she knew that a boy who breathed in water in water this cold had perhaps four minutes before the cold finished what the river started. She got her other hand in his coat. The ice shrieked. Somewhere beneath them the current ran fast and indifferent, tugging at his boots like a patient creditor.

"Kick," she said. "Kick your legs. Up onto the ice, not out of the water — *onto* it, flat, crawl."

He kicked. He was nine years old and half her weight and terrified, and he kicked, and the ice held him the way it had failed to, and then he was out — not out, never out, no one was out on this river — but *spread*, flat on the white surface, streaming, his breath coming in whoops and sobs that steamed in the starlight. Ivo rolled away from the hole, rolled until she reached ice that had been solid all along, and then she got her hands under him and dragged, and he crawled, and together they went the way animals go, low and stupid and fast, fifty yards, a hundred, until the bank rose up black and kind and they collapsed against it among the dead reeds.

For a long moment neither of them said anything. The river went on talking to itself under the ice, unhurried, as if it had all the time in the world, as if it did this to boys every winter, which it did.

"I counted," Tomas said at last. His teeth were already beginning to chatter. "I got to fifty-four. I want it written down somewhere that I got to fifty-four."

"It's written down," Ivo said. "I'll put it in the ledger."

And then she heard it, and her stomach turned over.

The ledger was not in her coat.

* * *

It took her three seconds of pure animal panic to find it — three seconds in which the whole night reorganized itself around a single black absence — and then she saw it, ten feet back along their crawling track, a dark shape lying on the ice where the oilcloth packets had torn loose from her lining under the drag of the water. She went back for it on her belly like a soldier under fire, and the ice held, and she came back with the book, and it was not until they reached the hunting hut that she understood what the river had already done.

The hut was one of Brannoc's — the blacksmith kept them, he had told her, all along the far bank, for the charcoal-burners in autumn and the hunters after the first snow, and this one had a stone hearth, a stack of split birch, and a door that shut out the wind with a sound like a held breath released. Ivo got Tomas out of his soaked coat and boots, got him wrapped in the horse blanket that lived on a nail by the door, got a fire built with hands that would not stop shaking, and it was only when the flames caught and threw their light across the floor that she saw the water dripping out of the ledger's oilcloth, and saw, in the firelight, the first name going soft.

The ink was running.

Not all of it. The early entries, the ones in Odile's older hand, were iron-gall and had bonded with the paper through thirty winters and would survive a flood. But the recent pages — the last three years, the war years, the years that mattered, the pages with the armistice-table names in them — those had been written fast, in the ink Odile bought cheap from the stationer in the capital, and the river had gotten into the packets through a seam Ivo had sewn badly, in the dark, in a hurry, and the water was walking the letters off the page like men deserting a losing army.

"No," she said. It came out very small. "No, no —"

She got the packets open. She got the pages separated, one by one, with the delicacy of a woman handling a wounded bird, and laid them across the two wooden crates and the upturned stool, and the firelight came through the wet paper and showed her the names dissolving — *Captain* with its tail running, a date bleeding into a sum, the signature she had crossed a frozen country to carry turning into a grey bloom like frost on glass.

Tomas watched her from inside the blanket, only his face showing, pale and blue-lipped and alive, thank God, alive. "Is it ruined?" he asked. "Did I ruin it? Because I fell in—"

"You didn't ruin anything." She heard her own voice go flat and courier-calm, the voice she used for lying, and hated it, and made it gentle instead. "The river ruined it. You saved my life by knowing where the channels ran. That's not nothing, Tomas. That's the whole night."

"But the names."

The names. She looked at them, at the soft grey ghosts where the letters had been, and something in her went very quiet and very clear, the way it had gone quiet the night her brother was taken, when she had understood that silence was not the absence of speech but a thing you could carry, a weight with a shape.

"We have until they dry," she said. "While they're wet, the words are still in the paper. They come back if we're careful — most of them. And the ones that don't come back—" She knelt by the fire and fed it another split of birch. "The ones that don't come back, I know. I've read this book, Tomas. Every page. Twice."

"Then write them down again."

"I have no ink. No pen. And no second book." She looked at him across the fire, at the boy wrapped in a horse blanket with river-water drying in his hair, and made the decision the way you make all the decisions that matter — without wanting to. "So we'll do it the old way. The way people kept records before there was paper. I'll read them aloud, and you'll hold them. A memory's a kind of ledger too. It's just harder to burn."

* * *

They worked until the small hours.

She dried the pages by turns, holding each one at a careful distance from the coals, near enough to steam, far enough not to scorch, turning it when the paper began to curl. The hut filled with the smell of birch smoke and wet wool and, faintly, the iron-and-vinegar smell of old ink releasing itself back into the air. And as each page dried she read it — not silently, not to herself, but aloud, in a clear voice, the way a clerk reads, the way a witness testifies, while Tomas sat wrapped in his blanket with his eyes closed and his lips moving, repeating the names back the way he repeated river channels, dates, the number of seconds between lightning and thunder.

"Fourteenth of March, nineteen-nineteen. Received of Captain Aurel Voss, Third Garrison, two hundred crowns, in consideration of which the undersigned held certain papers in her keeping and did not deliver them to the commission of inquiry."

"Again," said Tomas.

"Fourteenth of March, nineteen-nineteen. Received of Captain Aurel Voss—"

"Voss," he said. "V-O-S-S. Like the ferryman's dog."

"Like the ferryman's dog," Ivo agreed, and found, to her surprise, that she was almost smiling. "Twenty-second of June, nineteen-twenty. Dinner for six, paid by Lieutenant Danchek, who requested the back room and that the girl who served not be the girl who usually serves. Note: the girl who usually served was the daughter of the printer whose press was confiscated the week before."

Tomas's eyes opened. "That's the man," he said. "The one from the armistice table. Danchek. I've seen his picture. He has a mustache like two dead mice."

"Tomas."

"What? It does."

She turned the page over the coals. The steam rose off it in a thin ribbon. "Ninth of November, nineteen-twenty. The widow Kessler paid nothing. She had nothing. She was given bread, coal, and the room at the back for the winter, against the spring, if the spring came for her, which it did not. The debt is forgiven. Entered here so that it is not forgotten that some things are given and not owed."

Tomas was quiet for a moment. "That one's about Grandma's back room," he said. "The widow Kessler. She used to sit by the stove. She died in—" He stopped. "She died."

"Yes," Ivo said. "And this is how she's still in the book. Not what she owed. What she was given. That's the part they'd burn first, if they burned it. That's the part that makes the rest of it true." She held the page up to the firelight and watched the grey bloom of the drowned ink, and the name beneath it that had survived because Odile had pressed hard that day, pressing hard because it mattered. "Do you understand why we're crossing a river at night for this? It's not the captains. Anyone can find a captain. It's this. The widow Kessler. Your grandmother forgave a debt and wrote it down so the forgiving would outlive her. And there are men at a table right now signing a peace that will decide who gets to be remembered, and they are all in this book, and none of them are in it the way they want to be."

"Grandma says a ledger's just a promise with a date on it."

"Your grandmother," Ivo said, "should have been running the country."

"She says women who keep books scare men who make them." Tomas pulled the blanket up to his chin. His shivering had finally eased; the fire had done its work, and exhaustion was coming up in him like a tide. "She says that's why they always come for the books first."

Ivo turned another page. The names came back as the paper dried — not all, but most, rising out of the grey like figures out of fog, and where they did not come back she spoke them from memory, and Tomas took them in and held them, nine years old, the newest page in the oldest book in Marguerite. Outside, the wind walked along the riverbank and the ice groaned and boomed in the dark, the river rearranging itself, and inside, in the birch-smoke light, a courier and a boy kept the record alive the way it had been kept in the beginning, mouth to ear, name to name.

Near dawn, Tomas fell asleep mid-name — *Lieutenant Danchek, mustache like two dead mice* — his head tipping against the wall, his breath going long and even. Ivo covered him more deeply in the blanket and sat back on her heels and looked at the pages strung around the hut like laundry, like prayer flags, like the evidence of a whole town's heart, drying.

Her arm ached where the river had held it. The ledger was damaged, and she would carry the damage like a scar — some entries gone, some blurred, the record itself now bearing the marks of what it had cost. But it was not cargo anymore. She understood that, sitting in the firelight with a sleeping boy and a book of names. Cargo could be seized, sunk, burned. This was testimony now, and testimony lived in two places: the paper, and the people. The river could take the ink. It could not take the reading of it.

They would rest until the light was full, cross the last miles to the rail line, and put the book into the hands of the neutral delegation before the signing. Two days. She counted them the way Tomas counted everything — carefully, and out loud, in her head.

It was full morning when they came down out of the hills toward the road to Marguerite, stiff and hungry and alive, and saw the town below them in the grey light — and saw that every bell in Marguerite was ringing, all of them at once, no rhythm to it, the alarm-toll, the sound the town made for fire or for invasion.

And on the wind, faint but unmistakable, came the smell of smoke.
