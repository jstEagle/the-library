## 2. Ledger of Small Debts

The sea chest smelled of tar and cedar and, beneath both, of something older — the particular staleness of paper that has been loved too long in the dark. Ivo kept it at the foot of the bed in the low room above the net shed, and twice a year — once after the spring storms, once before the winter ones — the chest was opened, the letters lifted out, read in order, and returned.

There were eleven of them. Marguerite Delac wrote a fine, upright hand, the hand of someone trained to make treaties legible, and she numbered her letters as though they were entries in an account: *Letter the Fourth*, *Letter the Ninth*. She had said once, over a table strewn with dispatch cases in a city that no longer existed under any name it then bore, that debts should be written down or they become stories, and stories grow in the telling until no one can pay them.

So she wrote them down. Eleven letters in fifteen years. And every one of them, without exception, began the same way.

---

*Letter the First*

*Valcourt, 14th of the Harvest Month*

*Ivo —*

*They will tell you in the morning that you are to carry nothing but a cipher and a name that isn't yours, and they will believe this makes you safe. I have watched couriers come through my office for three years now, and I have learned what actually keeps a man alive: it is not the false name. It is knowing which doors will open.*

*You carried my translation of the Armistice annex through two checkpoints last winter, sewn into your coat lining, and never once asked what it cost me to write it. I noticed. Translators notice everything; it is the whole disease of the profession.*

*Here is what I can give you in return, and it is not small. My family keeps a house in Sainte-Marine — my mother's house, salt-stained, ugly, worth more than any title we ever held. The door is heavy and blue and it does not lock from the outside. If ever you are running, Ivo Joubert, you go to the blue door and you say the word* l'attente *and my mother will feed you before she asks your business, because that is how she was raised and how I raised Solène, who is four years old and already stubborn enough to be trusted.*

*A door is a promise that doesn't require courage at the moment it's made. It only requires courage later, when it must be kept, by whoever is standing on either side of it.*

*Consider it made.*

*M.D.*

---

Ivo had gone to the blue door eleven months later, in the rain, with a broken collarbone and a satchel of documents that would have meant a firing squad in three separate jurisdictions. Marguerite's mother — old Madame Delac, small as a wren and twice as fierce — had opened it before the second knock, looked at the blood seeping through Ivo's coat, and said, "Kitchen. Boots off. There is soup." Not one question for six hours. Then: "You'll be wanting to know if the tide serves tonight. It does. My son-in-law's boat is in the third slip."

That was the shape of the promise in practice: not poetry, but soup, and boots off, and a boat in the third slip.

Marguerite herself Ivo had known first as a voice behind a partition in the Foreign Ministry's translation bureau — correcting a clause in a shipping convention with a patience that made the deputy minister look like a scolded schoolboy — and later, as the war curdled, as the woman who sat across from Ivo at midnight tables translating surrender terms while artillery made the windows buzz like trapped flies. She had a habit, when the shelling came close, of laying her palm flat on the page she was working on, as though the words might blow away otherwise. Ivo thought of that palm often, in the years after, laid flat against the dark.

The last diplomatic mission — the last of anything — had been the evacuation of the ministry archives from Valcourt, nine days of wagons and forged manifests, and it was on the sixth night, in a barn that smelled of hay and horse fear, that Marguerite had taken Ivo aside.

---

The barn lamp guttered and threw their shadows huge up into the rafters, where swallows slept in rows like notes on a staff. Outside, sentries walked the perimeter with lanterns hooded, and the sound of the war was a long mutter on the southern horizon, thunder deciding whether to commit.

"You're going west with the last dispatches," Marguerite said. It wasn't a question. She'd seen the manifest; she saw everything. "And I'm staying."

"The ministry falls back to—"

"The ministry falls apart," she said, gently, the way she corrected clauses. "Ivo. Sit. You've been standing at attention since Valcourt and it doesn't suit you."

He sat on a feed crate. She remained standing, arms crossed, in her travel-roughened coat, hair coming loose from its pins in a way that would have scandalized the bureau. Somewhere beyond the wall a horse shifted and sighed.

"I want to give you something to carry," she said, "and it weighs nothing, which is why it's difficult."

"Madame Delac—"

"Marguerite. We are about to stop being a country; the least we can do is stop being formal." A ghost of a smile, there and rationed away. "My family is in Sainte-Marine. My mother, my brother Tomas and his wife, and Solène. When this ends — however it ends — there will be lists. There are always lists. Winners make them and losers appear on them. I am a translator for a dead republic; I will be on some list somewhere, I've made my peace with the arithmetic of that. But my daughter—" Her voice did something then, a small structural failure, immediately repaired. "My daughter is four years old and appears on no list yet, and I intend to keep it so."

"What do you want me to carry?"

"A promise." She sat down beside him on the crate, finally, and looked at her hands. "Not a document. Documents get found. A promise lives where they can't requisition it." She turned to face him fully, and Ivo understood that he was being looked at the way she looked at a treaty clause she intended to bind someone with. "You will be invisible. That's what you're good at — I've read your reports; you write yourself out of them beautifully. So be invisible near Sainte-Marine. Be a fisherman, a drunk, an idiot, whatever the coast requires. And when the lists come, and they will come, you will be a pair of eyes that knows my family's faces and watches over them, and a voice that remembers mine, so that if Solène ever needs to hear that her mother was more than a line in a proscription file, someone can tell her."

"And you?" Ivo asked. "Where do you wait?"

"Wherever they put me," she said. "That's the trade. They take me, perhaps. Perhaps worse. But my mother taught me that a family is not the people in the house; it's the people who will open the door. Keep the door, Ivo. That's all. Just — keep the door."

Outside, the muttering thunder committed itself, closer now, and the swallows stirred in the rafters. Ivo gave his word. He remembered giving it precisely: he had been aware even in that moment, even with the war walking toward them across the fields, that the memory was being made — that this barn, this lamp, this woman's tired eyes would be carried in him longer than any dispatch, and that he was accepting a cargo that could not be delivered and could not be set down.

She had pressed his hand once, hard, and gone back to her translations, palm flat on the page.

---

*Letter the Fifth*

*Sainte-Marine, 3rd of the Thaw Month, Year One of the Empire*

*They have renamed the streets. The harbor road is now Avenue of Concord, which is funny if you know that the harbor master was arrested in February, and the granary steps are called Terrace of the Martyrs, which is not funny at all because two of the martyrs were boys from the herring fleet whom I had taught to read.*

*Forgive me. I promised myself these letters would not be chronicles of grievance. But there is no one here to whom I can say plainly what I see, and a letter is a door of a kind too, isn't it? Even one that may never be answered.*

*Solène is eight. She has her grandmother's temper and my habit of finishing other people's sentences, which her teacher calls insolence and I call efficiency. She asks questions I cannot answer — not because I don't know the answers but because the answers are now crimes. What was the republic like? Was it better? Mama, why do we say the old names only inside the house?*

*Inside the house. That is where we live now, Ivo. The Empire has been very generous with the outdoors.*

*Tomas says the fishing is unchanged. Fish, he says, have no politics. He says it loudly, in the tavern, and I love him for it and am afraid of him for it in equal measure, which I suspect is what family is.*

*I don't ask anything of you. I don't even know if these reach you, or where you are, or under what name the tides keep you. But the blue door stands, and my mother still cooks for whoever knocks, and Solène lost her first tooth this winter and left it on the windowsill for the tide-fairy, because in Sainte-Marine even the fairies are maritime.*

*Account current. No debts outstanding except the large unpayable one, which I record here so it does not grow into a story.*

*M.D.*

---

Eleven letters. Ivo knew them the way sailors know channels — by depth and hazard and the exact point where the light changes. The early ones were almost light, full of Solène's teeth and Tomas's loud politics and the small commerce of a fishing town learning to live under new flags. The middle ones darkened by degrees, the way water does over a dropping anchor. By Letter the Eighth, Madame Delac was dead — quietly, in her sleep, in a house with a blue door that no longer opened for strangers because strangers had become reportable. By Letter the Tenth, Tomas had stopped saying anything loudly anywhere.

And the eleventh letter, the last, was different from all the others. Shorter. Written in a hand that was still upright, still precise, but pressed harder into the paper, as though the pen were being used to hold something down.

---

*Letter the Eleventh*

*Sainte-Marine, the last week of the Sowing Month, Year Fifteen*

*Ivo, or whatever the sea is calling you these days —*

*I will be brief, because I have learned that brevity is the last luxury available to women like me, and I mean to spend it.*

*Surveyors came yesterday. Imperial surveyors, very polite, very clean boots, with brass instruments and a ledger bound in grey leather. They are counting the boats. Every hull, every slip, every net rack — measured, valued, entered. And with each boat, a man. Name, age, trade, year of arrival, place of origin if claimed.*

*Do you understand what I am telling you? Fifteen years ago you became a fisherman on paper in this harbor, and paper, Ivo, is patient in a way that empires are not. Everything we buried is going to be dug up politely, in daylight, by men with clean boots who will smile and thank us for our cooperation.*

*The customs house is compiling the municipal records. All of them. The harbor ledgers especially. I watched them carry the old books out of the harbormaster's office like pallbearers.*

*I have spent fifteen years writing to a man who may be a fisherman, a corpse, or a fiction, and I have never once regretted the postage. But today I am going to break my own rule and ask something, because asking is the only currency I have left and I find I would rather spend it than die solvent.*

*If the lists come — and they are coming, the proscription notices are printed, I have seen the type in the printer's window, my own name among the standing type — then my family will need a door that is not made of wood. Wood burns. Paper burns. I need the other kind, the kind we agreed on in a barn while the world ended.*

*Solène is nineteen. She has my handwriting and her own opinions, which are louder than Tomas's ever were, and she does not remember the republic, and I have raised her to survive exactly the world she was born into, which I now understand was a terrible thing to do to a child. She deserves one person whose loyalty predates the Empire. Even a fictional fisherman. Especially a fictional fisherman.*

*Count the boats, Ivo. Count them and tell me: is there room in one of them for a debt fifteen years outstanding?*

*The tide serves at dawn. It usually does. That is the one honest thing left in this town.*

*Yours in account current,*

*M.D.*

---

Ivo read it four times. Then folded it along its fifteen-year-old creases, which had gone soft as cloth, and set it atop the stack, and sat in the low room above the net shed with the lamp turned low and the sound of the harbor coming through the walls — water on pilings, a gull arguing with the dark, the far creak of a hull against its slips.

Fifteen boats in the outer fleet. Fourteen skiffs and tenders besides. Ivo counted them in the dark without meaning to, the way Marguerite had asked, the way a debtor counts.

The surveyors would reach the inner harbor within the week. The municipal records would follow the brass instruments into grey-leather ledgers, and somewhere in those pages a line waited, patient as paper: *Joubert, Ivo. Fisherman. Arrived Year Zero. Origin unknown.* Fifteen years of practiced dullness, of no letters and no opinions and no past, and all of it reducible now to one entry in a book that men with clean boots were preparing to carry away.

Ivo banked the lamp, slid the letters beneath the false bottom of the sea chest, and lay down without undressing.

Dawn was coming. It usually did.
