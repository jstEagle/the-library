## 3. Requisition

The drums began before dawn.

Ivo Joubert heard them from the loft above Bastien's boathouse, where he had slept for eleven years on a straw pallet that still smelled faintly of tar and herring. He lay still in the grey half-light and counted the beats the way he had once counted rifle volleys — not out of fear, exactly, but out of an old habit of measuring trouble by its rhythm. These were not alarm drums. They were proclamation drums: slow, ceremonial, spaced like footsteps of a man who knows everyone is listening.

He dressed without hurry. That was the first discipline. A man who dresses quickly has somewhere to be; a man with nowhere to be dresses as though the morning will wait for him, because it always has.

By the time he came down the outside stair, the fog was lifting off the harbor in long pale ribbons, and the town square of Sainte-Marine had already begun to fill. Not with soldiers first — with townspeople, drawn out of their doorways the way water is drawn toward a breach, all of them wearing the same expression: the careful blankness of people who have learned that curiosity can be expensive.

Then the soldiers came.

There were perhaps forty of them, marching up from the quay in column behind two officers on horseback, their boots striking cobbles with a sound too clean for this town. Sainte-Marine's streets were made for fishermen's boots, soft-soled, salt-warped, dragging a little on the wet stone. The imperial boots rang. They rang against the shutters where hands had just withdrawn, against the silence of the square, against fifteen years of quiet that Ivo had helped build plank by plank the way one builds a hull — patiently, secretly, so that no one ever notices the vessel holding them up until the day it fails.

The older officer dismounted at the base of the customs house steps. He was heavy through the shoulders, with the ruddy, well-fed face of a man who had spent his war behind lines, and he unrolled a document with the unhurried ceremony of someone reading scripture to the illiterate. A junior clerk stood beside him with a second copy, and behind them, at the top of the steps, two soldiers drove iron nails into the shuttered door of the customs house itself, hammering the proclamation into place with six blows each, as if the number mattered.

Ivo did not go to the front of the crowd. He went to the edge of it, near the fish stalls, where a man could stand with his arms crossed over an oilskin and be nobody. He had learned long ago that crowds have a shape, and that the safest place in any crowd is neither its center nor its rear but its shoulder — close enough to hear, far enough to leave, beside enough other bodies that no single gaze would find him memorable.

The officer read.

*"By authority of His Imperial Majesty's Commission for the Maritime Provinces, let it be known to all inhabitants of the port of Sainte-Marine—"*

The words came out in the flat cadence of something recited many times in many towns, and Ivo understood, with a cold settling in his stomach that had nothing to do with the sea air, that Sainte-Marine was only the latest name in a list. The requisition had come here the way weather comes: not because of anything the town had done, but because the season had turned.

*"—the harbor of Sainte-Marine, its quays, moorings, and anchorage, are hereby placed under imperial administration for the duration of current operations."*

A murmur moved through the crowd like wind through barley. Beside Ivo, old Madame Guissart clutched her basket of mackerel a little tighter, as though the fish might be requisitioned next. Perhaps they would be.

*"—the municipal granary, with all stores contained therein, passes to the army commissariat, effective immediately."*

That one drew actual sound — a low, unhappy note from the men who owned boats and fed crews out of that granary through the lean months. One voice started to rise above the rest and stopped itself, strangled mid-syllable, when a soldier turned his head. Fifteen years ago, Ivo thought, that voice would have finished its objection and been joined by twenty more. The republic had taught even its enemies to argue. The Empire taught everyone else to swallow.

*"—the troopship Corvane, currently at anchor, is taken into service as a transport of His Majesty's Third Expeditionary Force, to sail upon orders."*

He knew the Corvane. Everyone did — a black-hulled steamer that had appeared off the point three weeks ago and sat there like a held breath, riding at anchor with her lamps burning at night, taking on coal by barge. The children had invented stories about her. The adults had done what adults in Sainte-Marine did best: said nothing, and watched.

And then the officer reached the clause Ivo had been waiting for, the one he had felt coming for days the way sailors feel a change in pressure behind their teeth.

*"—all books, registers, rolls, ledgers, and records of every kind held by the municipality or its officers, including the harbor register and all lists of inhabitants, arrivals, departures, debts, dues, and obligations, are declared property of the Crown, to be collected, conveyed, and examined at the discretion of the Commission."*

All records.

The phrase was small and administrative and it landed in Ivo's chest like a grapnel finding hold. He kept his face still. He had gotten very good at keeping his face still; it was possibly the finest achievement of his life, this face, this nothing-face, this mask of mild attention worn by a man listening to news about granaries.

But underneath the oilskin, under the crossed arms and the fish-stall smell he had cultivated like a garden, his mind was already moving down a corridor he had kept locked for fifteen years, opening doors one after another, counting what lived behind each one.

The harbor ledger. Kept by old Perrin the harbormaster, a fat, ruled book renewed every ten years, containing every arrival, every departure, every fee paid, every fine assessed, every name that had ever tied a line to a Sainte-Marine bollard. Including — entered in Perrin's careful commercial hand, dated with a precision that had always struck Ivo as slightly ironic —

*Ivo Joubert, fisherman. Arrived [date]. Origin: unknown.*

Fifteen years ago. He had chosen the date himself, standing at Perrin's counter while the old man licked his pencil, choosing a day far enough back to be unremarkable and recent enough to explain why no one remembered him arriving. *Origin unknown* — Perrin had shrugged and written it without comment; half the coast was full of men whose origins were unknown, the war having scattered them like seed. It had seemed such a small lie at the time. A pebble in a wall of stones.

Now the wall was being loaded onto a cart.

* * *

They brought the ledger out within the hour.

Ivo watched it happen from the doorway of the net shed, ostensibly sorting twine, actually positioned where he could see everything and be seen seeing nothing. Four soldiers carried it — four, which told him how seriously they took it, since the ledger weighed no more than a sack of flour — in a canvas sling like something wounded. Perrin walked beside it, wringing his cap, protesting in the whining register of a man whose entire adult identity had been bound in oiled leather and was now being carried away from him.

"There are fees outstanding," Perrin was saying. "There are — sir — there are obligations recorded in that book going back twenty years, unpaid mooring charges, damages owed, you cannot simply—"

"Obligations," repeated the younger officer, the one with the clerk's satchel slung across his chest, "will be adjudicated by the Commission." He said it kindly, almost gently, the way one explains death to a child. "You may petition for copies."

"Copies!" Perrin's voice cracked. "Of what use is a copy to me? The book *is* the—"

"Perrin." That was Bastien, appearing from wherever Bastien always appeared from, wine-red in the face and conciliatory, one hand landing on the old harbormaster's shoulder with practiced weight. "Perrin, my friend. They'll take care of it. Paper survives better than we do, eh?" And then, turning to the officer, with the smile that had gotten Bastien through three regimes: "These fishing folk, monsieur, they love their books. Half of them can't read, but they'd fight you for the ledger anyway. Sentiment."

The officer smiled the thin smile of a man who has heard local color before and found it adequate. "Where do you keep the older volumes?"

Bastien blinked. "Older?"

"The harbor ledger is renewed each decade, is it not?" The officer consulted a paper. "Prior volumes. Prior registers. Birth rolls, parish lists if the commune holds them, debt schedules, anything in municipal custody."

Ivo felt the world tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts when a wave passes underneath that you never see.

The prior volume. The one from fifteen years ago. The one with the fresh ink and the false date and the entry that had seemed so harmless — a name, a trade, a shrug, *origin unknown*. He had thought about that book perhaps once a year, the way one thinks about a tooth that aches only in cold weather, and every year he had concluded the same thing: no one cares about a fishing ledger. No one crosses an empire to read a fishing ledger.

He had been wrong about empires before.

"They're in the back office," Perrin said miserably. "In the cabinet. There's — there's the flood year, the bindings are damaged—"

"We'll take them regardless. Condition noted." The officer gestured, and two soldiers went inside, and Ivo stood among his nets with his hands moving through the familiar work of coiling twine while his blood moved through channels of its own, faster, colder.

It took less than half an hour. The soldiers emerged with an armload of books — the current ledger in its sling, three older volumes stacked beneath it, a bundle of loose papers tied with string, and, absurdly, a tin box of pens and seals that one of them had apparently decided counted as records. All of it went into the satchels and saddlebags of the detail at the customs house, and the younger officer — the one with the satchel — buckled the main compartment with a click that Ivo heard across the square as clearly as if it had sounded inside his own skull.

Inside that satchel, folded among the ordinary sediment of fifteen years — mooring fees, storm damages, the births of boats, the deaths of others — lay the sentence of his life, written in pencil-licked shorthand by an old man who had never suspected it was anything but commerce:

*Ivo Joubert, fisherman. Arrived. Origin: unknown.*

Unknown. For fifteen years, *unknown* had been his fortress. He had built it himself, stone by stone: no letters sent or received, no opinions offered, no stories told even when drink made the whole table generous. When children asked where he'd learned to read tide-tables, he said his father had been a pilot. When they asked why a pilot's son fished alone, he said his father drowned. Both lies were load-bearing. He had never tested them, never needed to, because the beauty of *unknown* was that no one ever asked twice. Curiosity required fuel, and he had starved it scrupulously.

And now the whole architecture of it sat in a leather satchel on the hip of a nineteen-year-old clerk who would carry it to a desk, open it under lamplight, and read it the way clerks read — completely, mechanically, missing nothing, because noticing discrepancies was the entire craft he'd been trained in. Somewhere in that young man's future was a page with Ivo Joubert's name on it. Somewhere after that was a question. Questions, in the Empire's arithmetic, had only two outcomes: answers, or arrests.

* * *

The cart left at midday.

Ivo followed it — not closely, not deliberately, just as a man might walk the same direction as a cart, toward the upper road, with a handcart of his own and no particular business. The escort was light: six soldiers, the younger officer riding ahead. The books rode in a locked chest lashed into the bed of the cart, and the chest rode with the small, smug permanence of all official property, as though it had already arrived wherever it was going.

At the crest of the hill, where the road bent and the whole harbor opened below — the boats, the breakwater, the black shape of the Corvane at anchor with her smoke beginning to climb — the cart paused while a wheel was checked, and the officer turned in his saddle and looked back at the town.

Ivo looked too, following the man's gaze, trying to see what an empire saw.

It saw a harbor. A granary. A ship. Records. It saw assets and instruments and liabilities, and it did not see — could not see, because it had no category for such things — the web of small mercies underneath. It did not see the Delacs' door that had opened for strangers in the war's worst winter. It did not see the names in the margins of that ledger's story: the widow who'd hidden a courier in her cellar for nine days, the brothers who'd rowed a man out to a neutral trawler at night, the family called Delac who had fed and sheltered and vouched and never once, in all the years since, asked Ivo for anything except what he had already promised.

Marguerite's family. Her mother, ancient and fierce, still shelling peas on the same stoop. The cousins. And Solène — Solène, whom he had last seen at fourteen, glaring at him across a kitchen table with her mother's exact eyes, demanding to know why the fisherman never stayed for supper.

If the ledger spoke, it would speak of all of them. Debts, dues, obligations — Perrin had recorded everything, blessed meticulous old man, including the seasons when certain families paid their harbor fees late because their sons were away doing things that were never written down anywhere. The Empire would not know what it was reading. But it would read it. And the Corvane waited at anchor like a punctuation mark at the end of the sentence.

The wheel was fixed. The cart rolled on, north toward the provincial capital, and Ivo stood on the hill with his empty handcart until it disappeared, feeling something crack inside the wall he'd built — not collapse, not yet, but crack, the way ice cracks before it moves: a sound, a shift, a certainty that the stillness was over.

For fifteen years he had believed that safety was a thing you could maintain, like a hull, if you were careful enough, humble enough, invisible enough. Standing on the hill in the autumn light, watching the road where the records had gone, Ivo Joubert understood at last that he had never been safe. He had merely been unread.

Someone, soon, was going to read him.

He turned back toward the town, and saw, below on the quay, a small knot of soldiers unrolling a fresh sheet of paper against the customs house door — another posting, shorter than the first, and around it the townspeople gathering with that new, careful blankness. He could not make out the words from the hill.

But he could see old Perrin reading them, and then turning away sharply, pressing his hand flat against his mouth as though holding something in — grief, or a name, or both.
