## 4. What the Ledger Knows

The nets needed mending, and Ivo Joubert was glad of it, because a man with a net in his hands had an excuse to sit still and stare at nothing while his mind did its terrible work.

He sat on the low stone wall behind the drying racks, the morning fog burning off the harbor in slow rags, and let the memory open like a wound he'd been pressing shut for fifteen years.

The ledger. The municipal ledger of Sainte-Marine. He had seen it perhaps two hundred times — every fisherman had, when they signed their catches, paid their fees, recorded their arrivals — but he had never truly *read* it until now, when it lay in a soldier's satchel in the customs house, waiting for eyes trained to find what men hid.

Ivo pulled a strand of net twine through his fingers and began, methodically, the way he'd once been taught to memorize dispatch routes: entry by entry, line by line, everything that page could tell an enemy about him.

First. His arrival. *Joubert, Ivo. Fisherman. Arrived the 14th of Brumaire, Year One of the Republic's Fall.* That was the date he had chosen on the dock, half-frozen, his courier's coat traded to a dead man's widow for oilskins, inventing a history in the time it took the harbormaster to sharpen his pencil. Fourteen Brumaire. A safe date, or so it had seemed — late enough in the season that no one would ask why a fisherman arrived after the fleets had gone out, early enough that no one would connect him to the exodus from the capital. But the Empire's clerks were not the republic's sleepy harbor officials. They would cross-check. And there was nothing to cross-check against. No license predating it. No boat registered before it. No record anywhere in any archive of an Ivo Joubert existing before the fourteenth of Brumaire, fifteen years ago. To a careful reader, that absence was not innocence. It was a signature.

Second. The fees. Every year, on the first of Vendémiaire, Ivo had walked to the customs table and paid his harbor dues like everyone else. And every year, for the first four years, he had paid them wrong — not wrong enough to be refused, because the old coin still circulated in the coastal towns like sediment at the bottom of a river, but wrong enough to be noticed by anyone who went looking. Republican *sols*. Struck with the broken fasces and the words LIBERTÉ ÉGALITÉ FRATERNITÉ worn nearly smooth. He had kept them deliberately, those coins, buried in a jar beneath his hearthstone, spending them slowly, because some part of him — the part that had carried dispatches under fire and believed the republic might return — could not bear to let the last of its money go. Sentiment. He had told himself it was sentiment. Now it looked like evidence. Four years of fees, entered in the ledger in the clerk's neat hand, each annotated in the margin with the small notation the old harbormaster had used for irregular coinage: a tiny asterisk, and somewhere, God willing lost, a footnote reading *old money, accepted at face.* An imperial auditor would read those asterisks the way a tracker reads scat. Here lived a man who held on to the defeated currency. Here lived a man who had not wanted the republic to die.

Third. Worse than either. Third was a single line, entered eleven years ago in the spring, in the harbormaster's tired hand:

*Delac, dependents, three. Arrived overland. Referred by —*

And then the referral, which Ivo had begged the old man, with money and then with something closer to pleading, to leave blank. The old man had left it blank. But he had entered the name Delac, three dependents, overland, and he had entered it on the same page, four lines below, as *Joubert, Ivo, fee paid,* because Marguerite's mother and sister and young Solène had come off the coast road at dusk and Ivo had been standing at the customs table settling his own account, and someone had to vouch for three travel-worn women who spoke with capital accents, and the old harbormaster had looked at Ivo with eyes that missed nothing and written them down together.

Four lines apart. Fifteen years apart in intention, four lines apart on paper. Anyone reading the ledger with intent would see it. Would see that the fisherman with no past had stood surety for the family of a proscribed translator, and would draw the only conclusion the arithmetic allowed.

Ivo became aware that the twine had gone slack in his hands, and that he was staring at the middle distance where the fog had been, and that Bastien was standing in front of him holding two cups of coffee and wearing the expression of a man who has rehearsed a speech all night and means to deliver it before breakfast gets cold.

"You look," Bastien said, "like a man doing sums."

"I am."

"Then do them faster, because I've done mine and the answer is *run*."

Bastien set one cup on the wall beside Ivo and lowered himself onto the stone with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions. He was broader than Ivo, grayer, with a face built for taverns — wide, weathered, quick to fold into warmth — and fifteen years of cultivating the imperial harbormaster with wine and flattery had given him a girth and a polish that sat oddly on a man who still gutted his own fish. He drank, watching Ivo over the rim.

"I talked to Gourvenec's steward last night," Bastien said. "The audit isn't just the ledger being carted away. They're bringing in a verification team from the mainland. Clerks. Real ones, with seals on their writing cases. They'll go through every entry against every other record we have — licenses, tithe rolls, parish books if they can get them. Everything."

"Then everything matches," Ivo said. "I've been a fisherman for fifteen years. My nets are licensed. My taxes are paid."

"Your *birth* isn't." Bastien's voice dropped, though the drying racks were empty and the fog had taken the nearest boats. "Ivo. Listen to me. I love you like the brother I never got cheated out of, so I'll say it plainly. There is no you before this town. You know it and I know it and once a mainland clerk sits down with the files, he'll know it too. And then he'll start asking why, and the answer will be sitting right there on the same page, four lines up: *Delac, dependents, three.*" He set down his cup. "The Delacs are already on the proscription lists. It's only a matter of days — the surveyors are counting names now, not boats. When your page comes apart, theirs comes apart with it, and every person who ever did them a kindness goes into the same fire. Me included, probably. I've fed them for eleven years, Ivo. My wine has bought their bread."

"So you want me to run."

"I want you to *live*." Bastien's hand closed hard on Ivo's shoulder. "There's a coaster out of Pouldreuzic, Thursday tide, a captain who owes me and asks nothing except cargo that doesn't complain. You could be in Breton waters by Friday, working under another name — you're good at other names, you've had fifteen years of practice. Let the ledger burn with your page in it. By the time they trace anything, you're smoke."

"And the Delacs?"

Bastien looked away, toward the water, and Ivo watched his friend's face do the thing faces did in Sainte-Marine these days: the slow closing, the shuttering of windows one by one, the expression of a town learning to unsee.

"The Delacs," Bastien said quietly, "are a list. You can't fight a list. You can only keep yourself off it."

"They're not a list." Ivo heard his own voice come out flat and hard, a voice he hadn't used since the war. "They are an old woman with bad lungs and a girl of nineteen who still says good morning to soldiers who won't say it back, because her mother taught her that courtesy is armor. Marguerite Delac stood in front of me in the chancellery fifteen years ago and asked me for one thing, one thing, Bastien, and I gave my word on it. Not to the republic. Not to the network. To *her*."

"And keeping that word gets you hanged."

"Staying alive has gotten me nothing worth hanging for."

The silence between them filled with the sounds of the harbor waking — hulls knocking against pilings, a gull arguing with heaven, the far-off bell of the customs house marking the hour. Somewhere beyond the granary, Ivo knew, Solène was walking to the well with her shoulders carefully rounded into anonymity, practicing dullness the way other girls practiced dancing. Eleven years she had lived within a mile of him, and he had never once spoken to her. Eleven years of watching from across the market square while she grew from the child in the letters into this watchful young woman, because contact was danger and danger was debt and he had promised Marguerite safety, and safety, he had told himself for fifteen years, meant distance.

It occurred to him now, sitting on the wall with his fear finally fully awake inside him, that he had confused the two words all along. Safety and silence. Silence had kept him invisible. It had kept the network's survivors unnamed. It had also kept a dying woman waiting for word that never came, and a girl growing up fatherless among neighbors trained to look through her. Silence had bought him fifteen years, and the bill had just arrived in a soldier's satchel.

"Bastien," he said. "If I run, they take the Delacs anyway. The list doesn't need my page to work — it needs only time. And when they take them, they'll ask who helped them settle here, and the ledger answers that question even without me in it. Running doesn't save me. It just makes sure I'm not here when they need—"

"When who needs?" Bastien said sharply. "You think you can do something? You, against a garrison? You've spent fifteen years being nobody, my friend, and nobody cannot stop a requisition. Nobody cannot forge a manifest. Nobody stands on a dock and watches, because that is all nobody is *for*."

"I know," Ivo said.

And he did know, and the knowing should have ended the argument, and instead it settled into him like ballast, low and permanent, shifting his center of gravity toward the customs house. He looked at his hands — a fisherman's hands now, scarred and rope-hardened, the courier's ink stains long since grown out — and understood that the choice Bastien offered was no choice at all, had never been one. A man could run from a name. He could not run from a promise; he had tried, for fifteen years, and the promise had simply waited, patient as tide, and now the tide was turning whether he waded in or not.

"Thursday's coaster," Ivo said at last. "Keep the option warm. I may yet need it."

"For you?"

"For whoever's left standing."

Bastien studied him for a long moment, and then he sighed, the huge resigned sigh of a man who has known the answer before asking the question, and drained his coffee, and stood.

"You're going to do something stupid," he said.

"Probably."

"At least do it *informedly* stupid. The verification clerks arrive within the week. Gourvenec's steward says the senior one is called Vasseur and the junior one is young enough to still apologize when he bumps into you. If you must meddle, learn the ground first." Bastien picked up both cups, then paused, and his voice gentled into something almost like the old days, before empires, when they were just two fishermen arguing about tides. "Fifteen years, Ivo. I never asked what you were before. I never wanted to know. Whatever it was — whatever's driving you now — just don't let it spend us all."

"It won't," Ivo said. "That's the one sum I've finished. Whatever I do, I do alone."

"That," said Bastien grimly, walking backward toward the fish stalls, "is exactly what worries me. Because alone is how men get caught, and caught is how men talk, and talking is how the rest of us end up on pages."

He went, and the market noise swallowed him, and Ivo sat back down with the net in his lap that no longer needed mending.

Four lines. Four lines of a dead harbormaster's handwriting, and a jar of republican sols under his hearthstone, and a false date that pointed at nothing because there was nothing behind it. He turned each piece over in his mind again, cataloging, the old courier's discipline asserting itself like a muscle remembering load: *what does the ledger know, what can it prove, who can read it, how many days.* Nine days, the surveyor's boy had said the troopship Corvane would lie at anchor. Nine days for a verification team to reach the page that unraveled him, and the girl, and the grandmother, and Bastien with his generous wine.

Nine days. He began, very calmly, to plan — and stopped, because across the harbor, near the customs house steps, he saw a slight figure in a gray shawl pause at the newly posted proclamation board, read it for a long moment with perfect stillness, and walk on with her head high and her basket swinging, past neighbors who found urgent business in their doorways.

Solène Delac had read her own name on the proscription list.

And she was not running either.

* * *

She came to him at dusk, as he'd known she would — not that day, but three days later, when the fear had ripened past denial — standing in the doorway of his hut with the sea wind pulling at her shawl, and the first thing she said, in a voice that shook with fury rather than tears, was:

"My mother wrote to you. She told me she wrote to you. Fifteen years, and you let us believe—" She stopped, jaw tightening. "You're the one her letters called *a friend of the republic.*"

Ivo rose from his chair, an old man suddenly in his own bones, and reached for the sea chest where fifteen years of silence had been waiting to be opened.
