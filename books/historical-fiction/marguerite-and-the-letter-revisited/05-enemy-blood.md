## 5. Enemy Blood

The list went up on the customs house door at the gray hour, when even the gulls had not yet decided to be awake.

Solène Delac learned of it the way she learned of most things in Sainte-Marine — sideways, by watching. She was coming down from the upper town with a basket of mending for Madame Roux, whose eyes had gone soft as boiled onions and who paid in bread and gossip in equal measure, when she saw the three soldiers at the door of the customs house with a bucket of paste and a rolled sheet of paper under one arm. They worked without hurry. That was what frightened her first: not the paper itself but the unhurried way they brushed the paste on, smoothed it flat, stood back. Men posting a menu. Men announcing a fair.

She did not need to cross the street to read it. The type was large enough that half the square would be able to read it together, which she understood — even at nineteen, even having spent her whole life learning to be small — was the entire point. A list no one reads alone is a weapon; a list read in company is a lesson.

But she crossed anyway. She told herself it was because Madame Roux's bread depended on her knowing the news before Madame Roux did, and there was some truth in that, and truth was useful armor. She crossed the square with her basket on her hip like any girl with errands, and she stood at the edge of the little crowd gathering in the cold morning air, and she read.

PROSCRIPTION — FIRST LIST
By order of the Imperial Survey, Department of the Interior.
The following persons, being declared enemies of the state by blood or association, are subject to detention, examination, and transfer.

And then names. Solène had imagined this moment so many times in the dark years — since the Empire came, since the surveyors arrived with their brass instruments and their ledgers, since the word *requisition* entered the language of the fish market alongside the price of sardines — that she had assumed the reality of it would strike her like a wave. It did not. It struck her like arithmetic. Her eye went down the column of strangers' names, foreign-sounding mostly, republican names, names from the old civil service, from the old army, from the old everything — and then:

DELAC, Marguerite (at large).
DELAC, dependents — Solène (b. 18—), Yvonne (grandmother), Tomas (cousin).

*Dependents.* As if she were a line item. As if nineteen years of learning to gut fish faster than boys twice her size, of reading her grandmother's prescriptions aloud because Yvonne's letters swam, of memorizing the tide-tables her mother had loved — as if all of it could be folded into a single bureaucratic word and filed.

Her mother's name said *at large.* Somewhere in her chest a small, traitorous flare of pride went up at that. *At large.* Fifteen years, and Marguerite Delac was still *at large*, still uncaught, still a problem the Empire had to write down twice.

Then she heard the breathing of the crowd change around her, and the pride went out like a pinched wick.

It was not a sound exactly. It was the absence of one — the particular silence of people deciding, all at once and without speaking, where their eyes will rest. A man beside her, Père Anselme's nephew, a broad-shouldered man who had carried her on his shoulders at the midsummer bonfire when she was six, turned his face toward the harbor with the studied blankness of a man studying gulls. A woman Solène had sold mussels to every market day for four years gathered her skirt and moved two steps away, then another two, the way water finds its level. No one looked at her. That was the horror of it. In Sainte-Marine everyone looked at everyone; looking was the town's oldest trade. To be unseen here was to be erased.

The soldier nearest the notice — young, chapped-lipped, bored — glanced at her, glanced at the list, glanced back. His brow made a small movement, an arithmetic of its own. *Dark-haired girl, right age, standing too still, reading too long.*

Solène walked away. Not fast. Fast was a confession. She walked at the speed of boredom, basket on hip, and let her shoulders round into the shape of a girl thinking about nothing, and she took the long way around the square past the fish stalls where the smell covered everything, and only when she reached the shadowed throat of the lane behind the cooperage did she let her hands shake.

They shook for a long time. She watched them do it, almost curious, the way you watch weather. Then she put the mending down on a barrel, sat on the cold stone step, and thought.

*

Here is what Solène Delac knew about her mother, arranged in the order she had learned it:

First: that mothers leave. This is the first thing every child knows, before language, before memory properly begins. Mothers go out and come back, and the interval between is the whole architecture of childhood.

Second: that her mother's leaving had lasted longer than other mothers'. Long enough that the going became a story, then a rumor, then a kind of furniture — something always present in the room whether spoken of or not.

Third — learned at nine, overheard through the wall of the room where Yvonne kept the strongbox and the candles — that her mother had been a translator, a good one, that she had worked for the Republic in its last sick season, that she had carried words between men whose words were worth killing over, and that when the Republic died, the words she'd carried were counted against her by the men who inherited the counting.

Fourth — learned at twelve, pieced together from Yvonne's mutterings and Tomas's silences and the careful way neighbors never quite asked — that her mother was not dead. That dead would be simpler. That she was somewhere east, or north, or nowhere, and that once a year, sometimes, a letter came with no return address, written in a hand that slanted hard to the left, and that these letters were read by candlelight and burned within the hour, always, always burned, though Yvonce — *Yvonne*, Solène corrected herself, grief makes even names slip — though Yvonne wept each time the flame took them.

Fifth — learned last month, learned by theft, which she did not regret — the letters were not all burned.

There was one her grandmother had missed, or perhaps had kept deliberately; Solène had found it sewn into the lining of the old sea chest that had come to them, unexplained, years ago, along with a sum of money and a note in a man's hand: *For the family. There will always be a door.* The letter inside the chest lining was in her mother's left-slanting hand, older, the ink browned. Most of it Solène could not make sense of — names she didn't know, a place called Vasseuil, the phrase *the network is blown* repeated like a bell — but one passage she had read so many times the paper had gone soft at the folds:

*If I cannot come back, and it seems now that I cannot, there is one man whose word I trust above my own. He carried my letter out when no one else would carry anything. He has a fisherman's name now and I will not write it here, because I am careful, and you must be too. But if ever the day comes when you are afraid and there is no one — find the friend of the republic. My letters call him that. He will know.*

That was all. No name. Her mother, careful to the end, careful past the point of cruelty, had hidden her daughter's rescue inside a riddle.

Solène had spent a month being angry about the riddle. Now, sitting on the cold step behind the cooperage with her name drying on a customs house door, she found the anger had changed into something more useful: direction.

Because she knew. She had known for weeks, maybe longer, in the way you know a thing you don't let yourself say. Sainte-Marine was small. The man who lived at the end of the shingle spit in a hut too neat for a fisherman, who mended nets with a precision no net needed, who read tide-tables the way priests read scripture, who had appeared fifteen years ago from nowhere with money and a chest and a note in a careful hand — the town talked, the way towns talk, and Solène listened, the way daughters of absent mothers learn to listen.

Ivo Joubert. Fisherman, arrived fifteen years ago, origin unknown. Friend of the republic.

She got up, collected her mending, and began to plan how to knock on a stranger's door without being seen knocking.

* * *

She waited for full dark, and then she waited past full dark, until the patrol had made its rounds of the harbor road and turned inland toward the granary, where there was nothing to guard and therefore always something. Tomas would have told her not to go. Tomas, twenty-three, big as a door, would have insisted on coming, and his bigness was precisely the problem; Tomas could not cross a room unnoticed, bless him. Grandmother would have told her not to go by simply looking at her, which was worse than telling.

So she went alone, down the shingle spit where the stones shifted and clattered underfoot like whispering teeth, past the black hulks of overturned boats, to the hut at the end with its one lit window.

She stood outside it for a long moment. Through the gap in the shutter: lamplight, a table, a man's forearm moving in the slow rhythm of net-mending, the needle flashing. Just a man mending nets. The most ordinary sight on this coast. And underneath it, invisible as a watermark, the other thing — the thing her mother had trusted — and Solène felt suddenly the vertigo of it, of standing between two versions of the world, the one where this was only a fisherman and the one where he was the last door standing.

She knocked. Three times, soft, the way you knock when you want to wake someone without waking the night.

The rhythm of the needle stopped. A silence with weight in it. Then: "Who's there?" A voice pitched low, neither warm nor cold — the voice of a man who had spent fifteen years making his voice into a locked room.

"My name is Solène Delac."

She said it clearly. She had decided on the walk down that she would not whisper it, would not treat it as a stain. It was her name. If it was poison now, then let it act fast.

The silence stretched. She heard, faintly, the creak of a chair, footsteps, the bar being lifted — and noticed, with the part of her mind that never stopped noticing things, that he had not asked *which* Delac, had not hesitated over the name, had known it on contact the way a key knows its lock.

The door opened a hand's width. Lamplight fell across her face, and across hers fell his: a lean, weathered man somewhere past forty, with the stillness of deep water and eyes that assessed her in a single pass — height, build, resemblance, risk — the way she imagined a courier used to assess a road.

"You shouldn't have come by the spit," he said. "There are eyes on the harbor road."

"There were eyes everywhere today." Her voice held. She was surprised by how well it held. "You've seen the list."

"I've seen the list."

"Then you know why I'm here." And then, because she had rehearsed this on the shingle and rehearsed it wrong, because all her rehearsals dissolved the moment the door opened and there was simply a tired man holding a lamp — her voice cracked, not with fear but with something older and less manageable. "My mother wrote about you. Fifteen years ago. She wrote that if ever there was no one, I should find the friend of the republic. Well." She lifted her chin, and hated that her chin was trembling, and lifted it higher. "There's no one. The baker won't sell to us. Madame Roux, who taught me my letters, closed her shutters when I passed this afternoon like I was rain coming. My cousin can't walk through the square without boys spitting near his boots and calling it accident. So I'm here. Because of a sentence in a letter my mother wrote before I could write myself."

He looked at her for a long time. Not unkindly. Not kindly either — she would learn, later, that kindness was a currency he had stopped carrying — but with attention, which was rarer and worth more.

"The list," he said quietly, "names you as a dependent."

"It names me as furniture." She heard the bitterness land between them. "My grandmother. My cousin. Me. Detention, examination, transfer. Transfer to where, do you think?"

"I think," Ivo Joubert said, "you had better come in before we discuss it on open ground."

He stepped back. The door opened fully. Lamplight spilled onto the shingle, and Solène stepped across the threshold of the stranger's house — the door her mother had promised, fifteen years late and narrower than promised — and heard behind her the small definite sound of the bar dropping back into place.

* * *

The hut was spare and immaculate, and that told her more about him than any confession could have. Nothing wasted, nothing displayed, nothing that remembered a different life — except, on a shelf above the hearth, a sea chest with iron corners that she recognized with a jolt, because its twin stood in her grandmother's room.

He set tea on the table without asking whether she wanted it, and she realized he had known she would be cold before she knew it herself. They sat opposite each other. For a while neither spoke, and the silence was strange — not hostile, not easy, but the silence of two people measuring the same wound from opposite ends.

"You have her letters," he said at last. It was not quite a question.

"One. One you didn't burn, apparently." She watched him flinch — actually flinch, a small motion around the eyes — and stored it away. "You've been writing to her. Or she's been writing to you. Which?"

"Neither. Not for years." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, a precise, controlled gesture. "There was correspondence, in the early years. Through channels. Then the channels closed, one by one, and then there was silence, and the silence was — safer. For her. For you. Every letter is a thread, and threads get pulled."

"Fifteen years of threads not pulled." She hadn't meant to say it yet; it came out with an edge she didn't try to blunt. "Do you know what that silence did? Do you know that my grandmother burned those letters crying? That I grew up believing my mother might be dead, might be in prison, might have another family somewhere — anything, because nobody would say? You were here. Fifteen years. Fishing." The word came out like a stone dropped in a well. "While we waited."

"Yes." He said it simply. No defense offered, none prepared, and somehow that was worse — or better; she couldn't tell. "And I would do it again. That's the part you won't want to hear. Every year I didn't write was a year the surveyors had nothing to find. Your mother understood that better than anyone. It was her rule before it was mine."

"Her rule." Solène laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked in the middle. "Everything is her rule. Be careful. Be small. Be patient. And look where her rules got her. *At large.* On a list. And now it gets me too — dependent, second class, enemy blood." She leaned forward. "So I'm done with rules. I came here tonight to ask you one question, and I don't care how much caution it costs you. What does the friend of the republic do now? Because the ship comes in nine days — everyone's talking about it, the Corvane, the troopship — and I heard the harbormaster's clerk say the proscribed ones go aboard for trial on the mainland, and I know what happens after trials on the mainland. Everyone knows. So: what do you do?"

For the first time, the man across the table seemed to become aware of his own hands. He looked at them as if they belonged to a younger man, a man who had carried dispatches through a collapsing war, and Solène watched fifteen years of practiced invisibility strain against something rising underneath it, like a keel touching bottom.

"I keep a promise," he said finally.

"A promise to whom?"

"To your mother. That you would always have a door." His eyes came up and met hers, steady and bleak and absolutely certain. "I'm looking at it keeping itself, Solène Delac, and I'm telling you now, so you understand what you've walked into: doors that open for enemies of the state don't stay quiet for long. If I help you — and I'm going to help you — then by tomorrow night there'll be no more invisibility for either of us. Everything I built to keep you safe without your knowing it, I'm about to spend. All of it. In nine days."

Outside, far off across the water, a ship's bell sounded once through the fog — the Corvane's anchor watch, marking the hours of a tide that was already coming.

Solène set down her untouched tea. "Then spend it," she said.
