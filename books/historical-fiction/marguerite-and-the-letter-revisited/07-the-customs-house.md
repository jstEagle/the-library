## 7. The Customs House

The summons came the way all dangerous things came to Sainte-Marine: politely, and on paper.

Ivo found it pinned to his door with a brass tack, the kind of tack that cost more than a week's fish. *Ivo Joubert, boatwright, is required to present himself at the customs house on Tuesday next, at the eighth hour, for the purpose of assessing the hull of His Imperial Majesty's transport Corvane. Failure to comply will be construed as obstruction.* The signature was a flourish of ink that had never touched salt water.

He read it three times in the doorway, letting the morning fog bead on the paper, while behind him the town went about its business of pretending nothing had changed. But everything had changed, and everyone knew it, and the politeness of the paper was precisely what made it terrible. The Empire did not break down doors here. It did not need to. It simply asked, in beautiful handwriting, and waited to see who would refuse.

The strange thing — the thing he turned over that night like a stone found where no stone should be — was that the summons was almost true. He *had* been a boatwright once, before he'd been a courier, before he'd been a fisherman. His hands remembered caulking as they remembered cipher, which is to say: completely, and against his will. Someone in the garrison's quartermaster office had gone looking through the harbor rolls for a man who could survey a troopship's hull in nine days, and had found Ivo Joubert, fisherman, arrived fifteen years ago, origin unknown, trade listed variously as net-mender, ship-fitter, and once — in a clerk's generous moment — *artisan*.

They had summoned exactly the right lie. That frightened him more than anything else.

Bastien read the order over his shoulder at the Pointe du Corbeau, where the two of them mended lines in view of anyone who might be watching, which was always.

"You'll go," Bastien said. Not a question.

"I'll go."

"Of course you'll go." Bastien bit a length of tarred twine and spat the end into the water. "A man refuses an imperial summons, they come asking why. A man complies, they forget he exists. That's the whole art of living under an empire, Ivo. Be useful, be boring, be gone."

"And if they ask me things?"

"They won't. You're furniture, my friend. Beautiful, seaworthy furniture." Bastien squinted at him sideways, and for a moment the old smuggler's shrewdness showed through the tavern-keeper's softness. "But you'll look anyway. At the ledgers. Don't insult me by denying it — I've watched you not look at things for fifteen years. You'll go in there and find out whether our names are in those books, and how close to the front."

Ivo said nothing. The tide was going out; the harbor bar would be baring its teeth by evening.

"Just remember," Bastien said, quieter now, coiling rope with hands that had done it ten thousand times, "whatever you see in that office — a ledger is only paper. Paper can burn. Paper can drown. Paper can be misfiled." He tied off the coil and set it down. "I'm saying this because I know you, and I know that look you get when you start doing arithmetic with your own life in one column."

"I don't do arithmetic."

"No. You do worse. You do *sacrifice*, and sacrifice doesn't check its sums." Bastien picked up his gear and stood. "Tuesday, then. Wear the good coat. Empires respect a coat."

* * *

The customs house had been the counting house of a shipping concern before the war, and it wore its new authority like a borrowed coat itself: the same granite walls, the same tall windows facing the harbor, but now hung with the black-and-gold of the Empire and staffed by soldiers who stood at the doors with the particular stillness of men being paid to be seen.

Ivo presented himself at the eighth hour precisely, in the good coat, carrying his tools in a canvas roll worn shiny with use. A corporal checked his name against a list — *Joubert, I., boatwright, local* — and waved him through without looking up. Being furniture had its privileges.

Inside, the building smelled of lamp oil and fresh ink, that smell of a place where the past was being rewritten daily. Soldiers carried crates past him in the corridor, stenciled with inventory marks. Through an open door he saw shelves going up along a wall that had once held ship models — municipal shelving, cheap pine, already sagging under the weight of ledgers.

And there they were. The town's books. He knew them on sight the way a man knows his own reflection in dark water: the harbor register with its cracked spine, the tax rolls, the parish copies, the mooring lists going back forty years. Fifteen years of Sainte-Marine's small bureaucratic memory, stacked and cataloged and carted out of the town clerk's keeping into the Empire's.

His own name sat somewhere in that stack. *Joubert, Ivo. Fisherman. Arrived [date]. Origin unknown.* And beside it, in the same careful hand, entries he could recite from memory though he'd only ever glimpsed them over a clerk's shoulder: *Delac, dependents, three.* The fees paid in coin that hadn't been legal tender since the Republic died. The arrival date that didn't match any license, any witness, any truth. Fifteen years of invisibility, reduced to six lines of ink in a book now guarded by two soldiers and a lock.

A young clerk — narrow-shouldered, ink-stained to the second knuckle, younger than the war he served — looked up from a desk buried in papers. "Joubert? The boatwright?" His voice had the flat courtesy of a man reciting rather than speaking. "Lieutenant Vasch wants the Corvane surveyed by Thursday. Hull, seams, rudder stock, the winches. She takes troops and stores, so they care more than usual." He handed over a chit. "Report findings to me. Ferrand. I keep the records."

*I keep the records.* Ivo took the chit with a steady hand and thanked him in the toneless way of workingmen everywhere, and thought: *there he is. The man who will hold my life in six lines of ink.*

Ferrand was young enough that the Republic would have been a rumor to him, a thing mothers mentioned. But there was something in the way he squared the papers on his desk — each edge aligned, each pile equal — that suggested a mind that disliked disorder deeply enough to be, someday, useful or dangerous. Ivo filed that away with the other observations and followed a soldier down the stone steps to the quay.

* * *

The Corvane lay at the deep-water berth like a knife left in bread.

She was bigger than anything Sainte-Marine had hosted in years — a converted liner, black-hulled, her passenger fittings stripped and her gunwales lined with the plain iron stanchions of military transport. Scaffolding hugged her stern where dockworkers were recaulking a seam. Soldiers moved along the quay in files, loading crates marked with the double-eagle stamp. The noise of her preparation filled the harbor: hammers, block-and-tackle creak, the bark of a boatswain who had never learned that sound carries over water.

Ivo worked. That was the mercy of craft — it demanded all of you and left nothing over for fear. He sounded the bilge, tested the rudder stock for play, ran his thumb along seams that some careless yard had caulked too thin near the sternpost. He wrote his findings in the blunt shorthand of his trade, and the writing kept his face still while his eyes did other work.

Because the Corvane was not just a troopship. He understood that within the first hour, and understanding it felt like the harbor floor dropping away beneath him.

At the after hold, a mate's mate with a clipboard was directing the stowage of strongboxes — document cases, iron-bound, dozens of them, each stenciled *RECORDS — PRIORITY MANIFEST*. Not supplies. Records. The Empire was emptying the province's administrative memory into this ship's belly, and whatever Sainte-Marine had been for fifteen years was sailing east in those boxes, page by page, name by name.

"Careful with that lot," the mate was telling a sweating stevedore. "That's the provincial archive. Goes to the tribunal office on the mainland, sealed till it gets there." He tapped the stencil. "Every name in the province, friend. Somebody upstairs thinks half of them are liars."

Ivo measured a seam and wrote *sound* in his notes.

Near the gangway, a lieutenant — Vasch, presumably, by the way everyone bent around him — stood reviewing a manifest with a harbor official. Ivo positioned himself at the rail above them, ostensibly checking a fairlead, and let their voices rise to him on the wind the way voices do over water, broken and rearranged.

"—boarding the night before sailing," Vasch was saying. "All dependents held aboard overnight. Cleaner that way. No farewells on the quay, no scenes."

"The town finds it harsh," the official ventured.

"The town finds everything harsh. They'll adjust." A page turned. "Proscribed dependents, thirty-one souls from this district. Trial on the mainland, disposition thereafter." Another page. "Sails on the morning tide, the ninth day from today. Have your pilots contracted by the sixth."

*Nine days.*

The number went into Ivo like a splinter, small and clean and instantly infected. Nine days until the morning tide took the Corvane east with the records in her hold and thirty-one people locked below — among them, somewhere in that count, the Delacs, whose proscription had been posted on the granary wall ten days ago and whose door Ivo had promised, in another life, would always stand open.

Thirty-one souls. He did the arithmetic he'd sworn off doing. One pilot's contract bought one berth. One forged correction slip might buy a category — *harbor laborers, contracted, no audit* — if a certain meticulous young clerk could be persuaded that errors existed and could be corrected. One cipher book, sewn in a keel these fifteen years, could buy almost anything from the right buyer, and cost everything it proved.

Nine days. The tide tables hung in his head unbidden, the way they always had, courier's habit: the ninth day's high water came at 6:14 in the morning. Slack water, the gentlest moment to take a ship out over the bar. Whoever sailed her would want slack water.

Whoever hid aboard her would need it too.

* * *

He finished the survey by late afternoon and carried his notes back up to the customs house, where Ferrand received them at his desk among the stacked lives of the town. The young clerk read the pages with genuine attention, frowning at the thin caulking aft, making a marginal note in a small precise hand.

"Lieutenant Vasch will want the hull sound," Ferrand said, mostly to himself. "He'll have your report regardless of what it says." Then he looked up, and for a moment the recitation fell away and something more human looked out — tired, watchful, faintly troubled. "You've worked ships long, Joubert?"

"Fifteen years here. Before that, elsewhere."

"Where elsewhere?"

"Shipyards." Ivo met his eyes with the mild blankness of a man who has answered this question a thousand times and been bored by every occasion. "North mostly. Nothing worth the telling."

Ferrand studied him a heartbeat longer than courtesy required — the look of a clerk who noticed discrepancies the way other men noticed weather. Then he stamped the report, squared its edges against the desk, and said, "Thursday's fine work. You'll be wanted again before sailing, I expect. Pilots and assessors, we keep the list."

Outside, the fog was coming in off the sea, taking the harbor apart piece by piece — first the Corvane's masthead, then her hull, then the quay lamps, until the world was reduced to the width of a street and the sound of water worrying the pilings. Ivo walked home through it slowly, letting the white air hide him the way it had hidden him for fifteen years.

But the hiding felt different tonight. Thinner. Because now he knew the shape of the thing coming: a ship, a date, a tide time, a stack of ledgers in a pine shelf, a young clerk with careful hands, and thirty-one names bound for a mainland tribunal. The clock had started, and it ticked in tide-tables.

In his sea chest, under the nets' tarred smell and the spare line, Marguerite's letters waited in their oilcloth wrapping. Tonight, he decided, he would take them out. All of them. And the last letter — the one with the promise folded inside it like a keel holds a cipher — he would read aloud, if it came to that, to whoever needed to hear it.

Six days to contract a pilot. Nine days to sail. And somewhere between those numbers, a girl with her mother's jaw was living in a town that had stopped seeing her.

Ivo climbed his stairs in the dark and began, silently, to make his lists.
