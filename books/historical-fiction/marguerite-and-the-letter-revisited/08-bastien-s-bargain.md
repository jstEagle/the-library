## 8. Bastien's Bargain

Bastien Roux had learned, over fifteen years, that the way to a man's secrets ran through his cellar.

Not his heart — hearts were cheap, and the harbor-master of Sainte-Marine had never once been accused of having one. His *cellar*. Every Thursday evening for a decade and a half, Bastien had climbed the hill to the customs house with a bottle tucked under his arm like a christening gift: a white from the Côte de Verre in the good years, a rough island red in the lean ones, and once — the year the Empire annexed the salt pans and half the town stopped speaking to him for a season — a bottle so old its wax seal had gone the color of dried blood. He had never asked anything in return. That was the whole art of it. A favor asked too soon was a debt; a favor never asked was an investment, compounding quietly in the dark like coin buried at the foot of a vine.

Tonight the investment came due.

He stood in his own kitchen, which smelled of tar and onions and the ghost of every fish he'd ever gutted, watching Ivo Joubert sit at his table with the particular stillness Bastien had come to know as fear. Fifteen years of mending nets beside this man, fifteen years of shared coffee on cold mornings and shared silences on colder ones, and Bastien could read him the way he read weather. The stillness meant the ledger. It had to be the ledger. Three days since the soldiers carted it off to the customs house, three days since the whole town's history went into a satchel, and Ivo had not slept — Bastien could see it in the gray around his eyes, in the way his hands lay flat on the table as if holding it down against a swell.

"You haven't touched your wine," Bastien said.

"I'm working."

"At what?"

"Remembering."

Bastien poured himself a measure and sat across from him. Outside, the gulls argued over the harbor refuse, and somewhere down the lane a soldier's boots rang on cobbles with the unhurried arrogance of men who knew no one would tell them to stop. Bastien had made his peace with those boots long ago, or told himself he had. That was the other thing about investments. You told yourself stories about them until the stories wore through.

"The harbor-master dines with me tomorrow," Bastien said. "Thursday. He expects the white from Verre, and he expects me to laugh at his joke about the Breton pilots, and he expects to leave before the cheese course because his wife counts his hours like a moneylender." He turned the glass slowly on the table. "I can get you aboard the Corvane."

Ivo looked up. For a moment something moved behind his eyes — hope, maybe, or its close cousin, calculation — and then it closed again, careful as everything else about him.

"Aboard. As what?"

"Pilot. Contracted pilot, harbor-certified, one berth in the officers' passage. The Corvane draws four fathoms loaded and her master has never taken her out of this harbor; he's a mainland man who doesn't know our shoals or our tide-lies. The harbor-master knows it. He's been complaining about it for a week, loudly, to anyone who brings him wine." Bastien let himself smile, thin and professional. "I've spent fifteen years teaching that man that I know everyone worth knowing in this harbor. Tomorrow night I introduce him to the finest pilot on the coast. A man of unimpeachable dullness. A man who has never once, in fifteen years, given anyone a reason to remember his face."

"And you'll vouch for that face."

"With my name behind it. Yes."

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. The lamp guttered and steadied. Bastien watched him work through it — he could see the arithmetic running behind his friend's eyes, patient and terrible, the way Ivo did everything, counting costs no one else could see.

"One berth," Ivo said finally.

"One name. One berth." Bastien spread his hands. "The contract is drawn per pilot, not per household. There's no cargo space for passengers, no rations allotted beyond crew and troops, and the manifest gets sealed at the gangway. I can put you on that ship, Ivo. I cannot put anyone else on it."

"I didn't ask you to—"

"You're going to," Bastien said. "So let's not pretend otherwise. Let's have it out now, at my table, with the door barred, instead of at the gangway where soldiers are standing."

He regretted the sharpness the moment it left his mouth, but only a little. Fifteen years of friendship earned a man the right to one sharp sentence per decade, and he'd been saving up.

Ivo looked down at his flat, still hands. "Solène Delac is nineteen years old. Her grandmother is seventy-one and her hips are bad and she hasn't left their street since the requisition. Their cousin is already in the customs house cellar. And Marguerite—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Marguerite Delac is declared an enemy of the state, and her daughter is going to wake up one morning on a troopship bound for a trial on the mainland unless someone does something. And you're telling me you can save one man."

"I'm telling you what exists. One man is what exists." Bastian leaned forward. "Listen to me. I love you like a brother, which is a strange thing to say to a man whose real name I don't even know, but there it is. If you stay in this town past the morning tide, you die. Not might die — *will*. Your arrival date is in that ledger, and Ferrand the clerk is already pulling threads off it — the boy came to the harbor-master yesterday asking after fishing license records going back twenty years. Twenty years, Ivo. They're auditing us like accounts. When they find what's wrong with your page, they won't try you here. They'll ship you out in irons on some later transport, and no one on this coast will lift a finger, because that's what we've become. People who don't lift fingers."

"So I should sail away clean while a girl I promised to protect walks up a mainland gangplank into a courtroom."

"So you should *live*," Bastien said, and heard his own voice crack on the word like green wood. "Fifteen years I've watched you practice being nobody. You're better at it than any man I've ever met. You taught me half of it — how to be dull, how to be forgettable, how to make a life out of small safe motions. And now, the one time it matters, the one time all that discipline pays out, you want to throw it away for—"

He stopped himself. Too late. Ivo's head had come up, and there was nothing dull or forgettable in his face at all.

"For a promise," Ivo said.

* * *

They sat with it a while, the word hanging between them like smoke. Bastien got up and fed the stove because his hands wanted work, and the kitchen filled with the smell of peat and the tick of iron heating, and still neither of them spoke.

It was Bastien who broke first. He always broke first; it was the arrangement between them, unspoken these fifteen years, that Bastien would feel things aloud so Ivo wouldn't have to.

"When you came here," he said, turning back from the stove, "you were nothing. A drowned rat off a coastal trader with sea-boots two sizes wrong and an accent you hadn't sanded smooth yet. I took you on because old Maren vouched, and Maren vouched because someone paid her debt, though she never said who. First winter, the fever took half the fleet crews, and you worked three boats' worth of nets alone and never took a sou above your share. I thought: this one is hiding from something. Everyone who's that good at giving is hiding from something." He sat back down. "But I never asked. Fifteen years, Ivo. Do you understand what that's worth? In this town, under that flag? I never once asked what you were hiding from, because asking is the first stone of the wall, and I decided — I *decided*, God help me — that whatever you were, you weren't the kind of man a person needs to ask about."

"I know what it's worth," Ivo said. "That's why I came to you tonight."

"No. You came to me because you'd run out of every other door." Bastie laughed, short and bitter. "And I opened the last one, and look — it's exactly wide enough for one."

"It's more than I had this morning."

"It's less than you need, and we both know you're going to try to make it more, and that's what frightens me." Bastien gripped the edge of the table. "Here's what I know about ships and manifests, because I've drunk enough wine with clerks to know how they think. The Corvane sails with troops, records, and — the harbor-master said it at dinner last week, laughing into his cup, the ghoul — 'proscribed dependents for processing.' Solène Delac is on that list. She goes aboard whether anyone rescues her or not. Do you understand? She's not locked in a cellar waiting for a hero. She's *already scheduled*. Which means if you're aboard as pilot, you will stand ten feet from her on that deck, in front of officers, with a contract in your pocket and nowhere in the world to put her."

"That's why I need nine days," Ivo said quietly. "The ship sails on the morning tide nine days from now. Nine days to find the seam in it."

"The seam. The seam." Bastien dragged a hand down his face. "You talk about empires like they're nets with a torn mesh. Sometimes they're just walls, Ivo. Sometimes the honest answer is that a man saves himself and carries the rest in his heart until his heart gives out, and that's not cowardice, that's just the price of being alive in a century like this one."

"Is that what you believe?"

Bastien opened his mouth. Closed it. Because the truth was no — the truth was that he'd spent fifteen years buying wine for a man he despised, telling himself it was survival, and every bottle had been a small bet that someday the account would pay out in something better than safety. That was why he'd cultivated the harbor-master. Not for protection. For *leverage*, held against a day when leverage would matter. He had just never imagined the day would ask him to spend it all on one berth, and that one berth would feel like both a fortune and an insult.

"I believe," he said carefully, "that tomorrow night I'm going to sell you to that fat man as the best pilot on this coast, and I'm going to do it well, because it's the only thing I have ever done well that mattered. And I believe that when you walk up that gangway, I am going to hate myself either way — for helping only you, or for not stopping you from doing whatever mad thing you're planning instead. And I believe—" his voice dropped, went rough, "—that if you get yourself hanged over a girl whose mother I drank with twice and liked enormously, then I will spend the rest of my life pouring wine for men I hate and calling it prudence, and I would rather be hanged alongside you."

"Then help me find the seam," Ivo said.

Bastien looked at him for a long moment. Then he reached for the bottle, refilled both glasses, and pushed one across the table.

"Nine days," he said. "The harbor-master dines Thursdays and drinks past sense by the second hour. He keeps a copy of the guard rotations in his desk drawer because he trusts me enough to take it out and complain about staffing while I pour. He complains about Ferrand — the young clerk is 'too precise,' which from that man's mouth means honest. He complains most of all about the passenger manifest, because the mainland sends auditors now and he has to reconcile three lists that don't agree." He tapped the table, once, like knocking on a hull listening for water. "Three lists, Ivo. Troop rosters, labor contracts, dependents. Nobody reconciles them but one tired fat man with bad handwriting. That's not a wall."

Ivo took the glass. For the first time all night, his hands weren't still.

"That's a net," he said.

"Don't make me regret teaching you optimism," Bastien muttered. But he was smiling — crookedly, unwillingly, the smile of a man who has just heard the exact size of the cliff he's agreed to jump off. "Tomorrow night, the white from Verre, and your name goes into a pilot's contract. After that, every piece of gossip that crosses that man's table comes to you. Guard rotations. Manifest schedules. Where they're keeping the Delac cousin, and how many keys between him and daylight."

"Why?" Ivo asked. Not suspicious — worse, genuinely asking. "Why spend it? Fifteen years of cultivation, all of it, on this."

Bastien swirled his wine and looked into it, at the reflection of the lamp, small and trembling, like a light seen across open water.

"Because I watched you mend three boats' worth of nets the winter everyone else was dying," he said, "and I have been waiting fifteen years to see what a man like that does when the fever comes again. Now pour yourself out, finish your remembering, and go home before the patrol changes. We start tomorrow — you'll need to learn to walk like a pilot by Thursday, and pilots walk like they own the water, not like they owe it something."

Ivo rose, and at the door he paused, hand on the latch, and for a moment looked older than the war and younger than the town, all at once.

"Bastien. Whatever happens at that gangway —"

"Don't," Bastien said. "Save your speeches for the sea. They keep better in salt air."

He barred the door behind his friend and stood a long time in the quiet kitchen, listening to the boots on the cobbles fade, and understood that he had just spent fifteen years of caution in a single conversation — and that somewhere across town, a girl named Solène Delac was learning the same lesson he had just learned:

That arithmetic is a cruel friend, but friendship is a crueler arithmetic, and in the end every man must choose which sums he can live inside.

The white from Verre would go up the hill tomorrow. The investment would come due. And nine days out, on a morning tide, the Corvane would sail with a pilot who owed his berth to a liar's oldest lie: that one name was ever really enough.
