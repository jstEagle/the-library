## 9. Nine Days

The first lesson was bread.

Ivo set the loaf on the table between them, a coarse gray loaf from Bastien's cousin's oven, and told Solène to carry it home through the market as though it were nothing. She looked at him as if he'd lost his mind — which was fair; he had been losing pieces of it for fifteen years and had grown comfortable with the deficit.

"It's bread," she said.

"That's what you'll be thinking when you cross the square. Not *this is the loaf that hides me*, not *walk slowly*, not *don't look at the soldiers*. Just bread. You bought bread because your grandmother asked for bread, and you're carrying it home because that is what people do with bread."

"And you think I don't know how to walk?"

"I know you know how to walk. I watched you come down the lane yesterday." He had, though she hadn't known it. "You walked like a person counting her own footsteps. Like someone rehearsing innocence. The soldiers didn't notice you because they're bored, not because you were invisible. Bored men notice rehearsal. It breaks the monotony."

Solène sat back, arms crossed, and for a moment she had her mother's exact posture — Marguerite Delac in the consulate garden, fifteen years ago, listening to a courier explain why a letter couldn't simply be carried like any other letter. Ivo felt the memory land in his chest like a fish dropped on deck, still thrashing, and made himself keep talking.

"Guards are not looking for guilt," he said. "Guilt is hard to see. They're looking for *interest*. A man who looks at them twice. A woman whose eyes go to the customs house door and then away too fast. Fear has a rhythm to it — it quickens near the thing feared. So we slow everything down. You will be the dullest creature in Sainte-Marine. Duller than the gulls. The gulls at least steal things."

* * *

They practiced for three days before he let her near the harbor.

In the mornings they walked the long way from the Delac house — now the widow Aubry's spare room, since the Delac cousin's lodging by the granary had become too visible after the proscription lists went up — down through the terraced gardens where old women cut cabbage with knives worn thin as communion wafers. Solène learned to greet people without greeting them: a nod calibrated to recognition but not warmth. She learned that a person with nothing to hide does not avoid eye contact but also does not seek it; that the eyes of the unremarkable rest on middle distances, on weather, on the middle of other people's foreheads.

"You're thinking again," Ivo said on the third morning, watching her cross the churchyard ahead of him.

"I'm walking."

"You're performing walking. Your shoulders have opinions."

She stopped and turned, and there it was again — the fury banked down to coals, the grief underneath it like groundwater. "My shoulders should have opinions. My mother is on a list. My cousin is in a cell. And you want to teach me to be *boring*."

"I want to teach you to be alive," Ivo said, more sharply than intended. A woman gathering herbs two rows over glanced up, and Ivo let the silence stretch until the woman returned to her cutting. When he spoke again it was low. "Boring is armor. I wore it for fifteen years. It kept a roof over my head and my name off documents and my hands out of nooses. You don't have to love it. You just have to wear it until we're past the gangway."

"Past the gangway," she repeated. "You keep saying that like it's a place."

"It's nine days away. That makes it practically a country."

Something moved across her face — not quite a smile, but the shadow a smile casts when it passes behind bars. She turned and kept walking, and this time her shoulders were silent.

* * *

He hated admitting it, so he admitted it only to himself, at night, staring at the beams of the loft above the widow Aubry's kitchen: she was better at this than he had ever been.

It shouldn't have surprised him. Invisibility was a discipline for those who had something to lose by being seen, and Solène had been losing things her whole life without ever learning why — a father who never came up in conversation, a mother who read foreign newspapers and burned them afterward, a childhood of small corrections: *not that name, not that story, not here, not now.* She had been trained without knowing it, the way children of drinkers learn to read a room. What Ivo had built painstakingly out of fear, she had absorbed as grammar.

By the fifth day she could cross the fish market under the eyes of two garrison sentries and come out the far side having counted, without appearing to count, the number of crates being loaded onto the Corvane's lighters. By the sixth day she had identified, correctly, which of the clerks at the customs house took snuff and which one merely pretended to, and why the pretender mattered: "Because he touches his nose when he lies about the time. He told a fisherman the office closed at four when the posted hours say five. He wanted the man gone before the afternoon rotation changed."

Ivo had stopped pretending, somewhere around day five, that he was teaching rather than collaborating. They worked now the way he had once worked with Marguerite — heads together over a table that held no paper, everything committed to memory, everything spoken in the flat vocabulary of fish and weather. The mackerel were running late meant the evening guard doubled at the granary gate. The sardine boats would need ice meant the customs house yard was busiest at dawn, worst for approach, best for cover.

And Bastien — Bastien gossiped the way other men breathed, and had never once considered that his breath might be drawn and measured.

* * *

"He tells the same story three times to three different men," Solène said, "and changes one detail each time. Then he waits to see who corrects him."

Ivo put down the net needle. They were sitting in the boat shed, ostensibly repairing a trawl that needed nothing, while rain came off the sea in gray ropes. "Say that again."

"The harbor-master's dinner last night. Bastien told Colonel Vasseur that the pilot for the Corvane's sailing would need tide-scrimmage rights up the channel — nonsense phrase, there's no such thing. The colonel corrected him. But the quartermaster didn't, and the quartermaster is the one who signs the boarding manifests." She was winding twine around her fingers as she talked, an unconscious habit, making small loops and unpicking them. "So the quartermaster either doesn't know his own paperwork or doesn't listen. Either way, he's the weak joint. And Bastien got all of it just by being wrong on purpose."

For a long moment Ivo could only look at her. Fifteen years ago, in another life, in a consulate garden smelling of oleander, a translator named Marguerite Delac had done exactly this — taken a room full of powerful men's careless words and found the seam in them. *They always tell you the truth once,* she had said then, *usually by accident, usually twice.* He had thought that gift died with the republic, or at least gone underground with everything else. Here it was, nineteen years old, wearing a fisherman's oilskin two sizes too large.

"Bastien doesn't know you watch him," Ivo said.

"He knows everyone watches him. That's why it works." She paused, and the loops of twine stilled. "Does he know what we're doing? Really?"

"He knows enough to hang from, and no more. That's how I'm protecting him."

"Is it?" she said quietly. "Or is it how you're protecting yourself? One less name to owe."

The rain filled the pause. Ivo found he had no answer that wasn't a confession, and confessions were currency he was saving — or hoarding, depending on who was counting. Solène seemed to understand this, or to tire of it; she went back to the twine, and the subject sank like a stone in a well, leaving ripples neither of them mentioned.

* * *

By the seventh day they had the customs house mapped.

Not on paper — Ivo burned every note the hour it was made, and taught her to do the same, holding scraps over the boat shed stove until the ink curled and vanished, *that's what a name looks like when it's free* — but in the shared architecture of their two memories, checked against each other nightly like merchants reconciling accounts.

Day watch: six men on the yard wall, two at the water door, relieved at noon with a quarter-hour of overlap during which the yard gate stood open for the ration cart. Night watch: four men, but the two at the records wing were drawn from the garrison proper, not the customs detail — bored soldiers, not suspicious ones, and bored soldiers could be timed. Ferrand's window, second floor, east light: lit until eleven, dark after, except on audit nights, when it burned past midnight and the shadow behind the frosted glass bent over its work like a man praying over figures.

"Ferrand works late on the nights the ledger's open," Solène observed.

"Yes."

"He found something. Didn't he. Something in the ledger about you."

Ivo tied off a knot that didn't need tying. "The ledger knows things about everyone. That's what ledgers are for."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer that keeps you safe to not know."

She set the twine down deliberately, the way her mother used to set down a pen when a translation had gone badly — flat, final, a small violence of objects. "You keep doing that. Deciding what I can carry. I've carried worse than you know. I carried us, these past weeks, while you were being dull at people." Her voice didn't rise; that was the frightening part. It came down instead, quieter, closer to the bone. "Whatever Ferrand found, whatever you're planning to give him or trade him or burn — I'll find out on the gangway, won't I. With everyone watching. That's how you do things. That's how you did things with my mother. Silence, silence, silence, and then a door closing."

Ivo looked at her and saw, superimposed, the garden, the oleander, Marguerite's hand pressing a sealed packet into his — *if anything happens, my family will always have a door* — and the door he had kept shut for fifteen years in the name of keeping it available.

"Nine days ago you were a stranger reading my mother's letters," Solène said. "In nine days we sail. Whatever team we are, Ivo Joubert, we'd better actually be one."

* * *

On the eighth evening, Bastien came to the boat shed drunk on purpose, which was his highest form of seriousness.

He sat on an upturned dory, produced a bottle of the harbor-master's own brandy — *borrowed*, he said, meaning stolen, meaning earned, in Bastien's moral ledger — and talked for an hour about tides, pilchards, the price of oakum, and the Corvane's sailing, while Ivo and Solène listened with the particular attention of people memorizing scripture.

The ship would take on her last stores at first light. The dependents — the word landed in the shed like a dropped anchor — would be brought aboard the night before, held below, and the manifest sealed at midnight. The harbor-master himself would stand at the gangway at sailing, because the Empire liked its departures witnessed. And — Bastien refilled his cup with a hand that was almost steady — the pilot's contract papers had come through, signed, stamped, delivered into Ivo's alias's hands that afternoon. One berth. One name. The arithmetic they had all agreed not to speak aloud, spoken at last in Bastien's cheerful, terrible way.

"One name," Bastien repeated, toasting the darkness beyond the door. "Which means, my friend, that whatever else happens on that gangway, you'll be standing on it alone."

After he left, weaving amiably between the rain barrels, Solène sat in the lamplight turning the empty cup in her fingers.

"One berth," she said. "One name. And yet you've spent eight days teaching me to walk through walls."

"I've spent eight days teaching you to walk through *doors*," Ivo said. "Walls are Ferrand's department."

Her head came up. "Ferrand."

But Ivo only began coiling rope, and outside, far off toward the customs house, a second-floor east window burned past midnight — the shadow at the desk bent low, and beside the lamp, faintly, the pale rectangle of a ledger opened to a page that would not stay clean much longer.
