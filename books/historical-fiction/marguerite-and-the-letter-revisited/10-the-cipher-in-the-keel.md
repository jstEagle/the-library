## 10. The Cipher in the Keel

The dream always began the same way: with the smell of tar and burning paper.

Ivo woke before dawn in the loft above the net shed, as he had woken for fifteen years, and lay still until the dream's last image loosened its grip — the courtyard of the Chancery annex, the couriers' dispatch cases stacked like cordwood, the secret police feeding them page by page into a brazier while a lieutenant read out names that were already dead. He had stood in that crowd in a fishmonger's coat he'd stolen from a drying line in Vairemont, and no one had looked at him twice, because Ivo Joubert the courier was supposed to be among the ashes, and men did not look for the dead.

He got up, dressed in the dark, and went down the ladder without letting the third stair creak. This too was discipline. Fifteen years of it.

But tonight — this morning, rather — the discipline had a different shape. Because today was the ninth of Brumaire, and on the ninth of Brumaire, every year since the fall, Ivo went to the old graving slip at the far end of the harbor where the *Léonie* sat on her keel blocks, half-rotted, officially condemned, unofficially forgotten by everyone except the harbor-master who collected her mooring fees and never once asked why anyone would pay them.

* * *

Sainte-Marine at that hour belonged to gulls and to the tide. Ivo walked the long way around, past the salt sheds, past the chapel with its leaning bell-tower, checking the street behind him not out of habit but out of ritual — the difference mattered; habit you could break, ritual broke you if you stopped. The fog was coming in off the water in slow gray ropes. A cat watched him from atop a stack of crab pots with the professional indifference of all Sainte-Marine's animals, who understood better than its people that some residents preferred not to be greeted.

The graving slip was behind the ruined sail loft, screened by blackthorn and a fence that had been falling down for so long it had achieved a kind of permanence. The *Léonie* rose out of the weeds on her blocks like the ribcage of something the sea had given back reluctantly. She had been a coastal packet once, one of the republic's fast mail boats out of Port-Avelin — thirty-two feet, clinker-built, painted government green with gold scrollwork at the bow, though the paint had gone to gray scale and the scrollwork to bare wood. When the republic's ports were seized, her crew had run her aground here rather than surrender her papers, and the Empire, finding her worthless as a warship and too much trouble to break up, had let her die where she lay.

Which was exactly what her last captain had intended.

Ivo set his lantern inside the hull, low, shielded by his coat. The light found the familiar geography: the sprung deck planks furred with moss, the mast step rotted to brown lace, the ghost of a name-board reading — just barely, if you knew to look — *LÉONIE / POR—AVELIN*. He knelt amidships, where the floorboards over the bilge had been laid loose by hands other than the shipwright's, and lifted the third one from the port side.

Beneath it, set into a cutaway in the frame timber, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed linen and sewn into the keel itself with tarred twine, was the last undelivered dispatch of the Republic of Valcourt.

It was not, strictly speaking, a dispatch. It was a cipher book.

Ivo unwrapped it the way he did once a year, on the ninth of Brumaire, no more often, because handling it more often meant knowing it too well, and knowing a thing too well made a man careless about fearing it. His fingers remembered the work anyway. Outer cloth, tied with a reef knot. Inner linen, greased against damp. Then the book itself: small, octavo, bound in plain gray boards with no title, two hundred and forty pages of close-ruled columns. On the flyleaf, in a hand Ivo had known as well as his own — Marguerite Delac's hand, the hand that had drafted treaties and translated ultimatums and written him letters he still kept in the sea chest — a single line of instruction:

*For the use of accredited couriers only. If this book cannot be delivered, it must not be found.*

Below it, unsigned, because everything official had been unsigned by then, a date: the fourth day of the last month of the republic's last full year.

He turned the pages slowly. The columns were dense with the republic's final field cipher — the *Verdier system*, third revision, the one that had never been broken because it had barely been used. Encoded within it, if anyone had ever thought to look past the raw tables, were the authentication codes for every republican courier still operating in occupied territory: challenge phrases, countersigns, the numbered sequence that proved a man carried orders rather than inventions. Proof of loyalty. Proof of identity. Proof, in the Empire's legal language, that Ivo Joubert had been an instrument of the lawful government of Valcourt and not — as the proscription lists put it, in their tidy bureaucratic shorthand — *a collaborator of convenience*.

That phrase again. He could hear it in the voice of the tribunal clerk at Vairemont, reading charges against men whose faces he still saw sometimes in crowds. *Collaborator of convenience.* It was the category they had built for couriers who survived. Not enemies — enemies got trials. Not patriots — patriots got graves. Collaborators of convenience got nothing at all: no case, no hearing, no record, just a silence into which any accusation could be poured. The Empire did not need to prove Ivo had betrayed the republic. It only needed him unable to prove he hadn't. And the one object in the world that could prove it lay sewn into a dead ship's keel, where it had lain for fifteen years because delivering it had become impossible and destroying it had become unthinkable.

He wrapped the book again. Knot, cloth, waxed linen, twine. He pressed the floorboard home and sat back on his heels in the cold dark of the hull and let the annual accounting begin, as it did every year, the same ledger he kept nowhere but in himself.

If he surrendered the cipher book to the imperial authorities, he would be cleared. There were men still living — retired archivists, a defected chancery under-clerk in the imperial capital itself — who could verify the Verdier system's authenticity. The book would establish his service record, restore his legal existence, perhaps even earn him the indemnities the Empire occasionally granted to proven loyalists of the defeated state, as a gesture of magnanimity. He would be able to walk through a checkpoint without his stomach turning to water. He would be able to say his own name aloud.

And the book would also establish, beyond argument, that the network had operated through Sainte-Marine. That boats had landed at night on the Pointe des Ombres. That certain houses — the Delac house first among them, named in Marguerite's own careful hand in the margin of page sixty, a notation she had added herself, *safe house, verified, family willing* — had sheltered republican personnel. The book was proof of his innocence and an indictment of everyone who had ever fed him, hidden him, or looked away from him at the right moment. Bastien. The old ferryman Roussel, dead now, God rest him. Half the town, in one way or another, though most of them had only known they were helping "the widow's cousin" move crates. Innocence, purchased with a town's arrest.

Or he could destroy it. Burn the pages, melt the binding, scatter the ash in the tide race where no clerk would ever dredge it. Then no one could ever be condemned by what it contained — including him. He would remain exactly what the world believed: a fisherman of no particular origin, arrived fifteen years ago, quiet, competent, forgettable. He would remain safe.

Safe. He said the word to himself the way a man tests a rotten plank with his boot heel.

There had been a time — the first years, the hungry fugitive years — when destruction had seemed obviously correct. Burn it and be done. But he had sat in this rotting hull with flint and steel in his pocket three times now, three separate years, and each time he had opened the flyleaf and seen the line in Marguerite's hand — *it must not be found* — and understood that she had not written *destroy it*. She had written *not found*. As if she had already foreseen this exact hour, this exact man, kneeling in a dead ship with fire in one pocket and a promise in the other. *Not found* left the door open. *Not found* said: there may yet be someone to deliver it to. Marguerite had spent her life among documents; she knew that a thing destroyed could never testify, but a thing merely hidden waited.

So the book waited. And Ivo paid for it, annually, in this currency: one night of the year, alone in the dark with everything he was forbidden to show.

* * *

He stayed until the fog began to thin and the eastern sky over the point went the color of pewter. Before leaving he did the other thing he did on the ninth of Brumaire, the thing that took longer than the inspection and cost considerably more.

He remembered.

He had recovered the book eleven days after the fall of Port-Avelin, in the interval when the republic still technically existed west of the Sère and everyone on both sides already knew it didn't. The chancery was evacuating. Marguerite had come to him at the harbor wall with her collar up and her eyes red-rimmed from three days without sleep, carrying the cipher book wrapped exactly as it was wrapped now, and a sealed letter besides, and she had spoken very fast and very quietly, the way she spoke when the situation was worst.

"The fleet takes the archive south, but the fleet will be taken — everyone knows it, don't pretend otherwise. So the codes go another way." She had pressed the bundle against his chest with both hands. "Sew it into the *Léonie*'s keel. Captain Arzel will ground her at Sainte-Marine; he owes me, and he's too stubborn to scuttle her properly. No one breaks up a condemned packet boat for years, if ever. It waits there."

"And if I'm caught?"

"Then it stays lost, and we were never clever enough to matter." Her mouth had done the thing it did instead of smiling — a quick asymmetry, grief and humor sharing the same muscle. "Ivo. Listen. Whatever happens to me — and things are going to happen to people like me, we both know the shape of it — my family is in Sainte-Marine. My mother, my sister's girl Solène, the cousins. Whatever happens, they have a door. You promise me a door."

He had promised. He had promised the way young men promise, entirely, instantly, with no idea what the promise would weigh. And then she had gone back toward the burning chancery district because her translation desk was needed for the armistice terms, because Marguerite Delac did not know how to stop working while there was still a country to translate for, and eleven days later the republic's own secret police — not the Empire, the *republic's*, eating its own children in the final spasm — had taken her from the armistice office on charges of divided loyalty that no one ever substantiated and no one ever retracted.

He had arrived at the prison gate forty minutes too late with her exoneration letter in his coat. Forty minutes. He had counted them for years, the way misers count coin, until counting became its own punishment and he forced himself to stop.

The letter had gone undelivered too. It was in the sea chest now, beneath the false bottom, beneath the spare lines and the gutting knives, folded around the promise she had extracted from him at the harbor wall. Two undelivered things, then: a cipher book that could save him and condemn a town, and a letter that could have saved her. He had built his life in the space between those two failures, and called the building invisibility, and pretended it was a discipline rather than a wound.

A gull screamed somewhere over the point. Ivo buttoned his coat, checked the floorboard one last time by touch — flush, invisible, indistinguishable from fifteen years of rot — and climbed out of the *Léonie* into the graying morning.

* * *

He walked back along the waterline as the town woke. Smoke rose straight from the bakery chimney. Old Tanguy was hosing down his punt, and raised a hand, and Ivo raised one back, and neither man said anything, which between them was a whole conversation. The tide was making, filling the harbor with slow clean water that covered everything — the weed-slicked stairs, the rusted ring bolts, the place where a fast packet had come aground in the last days of a dying country.

Fifteen years, he thought, and the book has waited, and I have waited, and neither of us knows anymore what we're waiting *for*.

Because that was the truth he avoided on three hundred and sixty-four days of the year: the cipher book had stopped being evidence long ago and become a kind of hostage. His proof of loyalty was worth nothing unless he surrendered everything to claim it — his safety, his neighbors' safety, the whole architecture of quiet he had built to keep the Delacs alive. Loyalty, it turned out, was not measured in what a man carried for his country. It was measured in what his country's death forbade him to show. He could not be honest. Honesty was the one luxury his oath had revoked, permanently, and he had paid the installments every single day for fifteen years without missing one.

He was so tired of paying.

He reached the net shed and stopped, because there was a light in the loft window that he had not left burning, and a smell of woodsmoke that was not his own, and for one lurching instant the old reflexes came up out of their grave fully armed — *they've come, they've found it, run* — before his eyes sorted the silhouette in the doorway and gave him a name instead of a threat.

Bastien. Standing in his doorway before dawn, holding his cap in both hands like a man arriving at a funeral, which for Bastien — who had not removed his cap indoors in fifteen years — was worse than any threat.

"Ivo," Bastien said, and his voice was wrong, flattened, the voice of bad news rehearsed on the walk over. "You need to come down to the square. They've posted notices. Soldiers, a dozen of them, and a man with a portable desk."

Ivo stood very still. Behind him the tide kept filling the harbor, patient, indifferent, covering everything it covered.

"What kind of notices?"

Bastien wet his lips. "Requisition," he said. "The harbor. The granary. The records." He hesitated, and Ivo watched him decide whether to say the rest, and saw that he couldn't stop himself. "They're taking the ledgers, Ivo. All of them. The harbor book. Every name in it."
