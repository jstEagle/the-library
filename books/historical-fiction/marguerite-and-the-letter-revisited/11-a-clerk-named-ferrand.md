## 11. A Clerk Named Ferrand

The customs house had a rhythm, and Ivo learned it the way he had once learned tides: by standing still and watching.

He came at different hours on different days. Once as a man with a dispute over harbor fees, hat in hand, complaining in the flat drone of someone who wanted nothing more than to be done and gone. Once as a boatwright's assistant sent to fetch a stamped permit for tar and pitch, yawning through the wait. Twice he simply sat on the low wall across the lane, mending a section of net that did not need mending, and let his eyes do the work while his hands performed their honest theater.

In fifteen years of practiced invisibility, Ivo Joubert had become a connoisseur of watching. He knew how to look without turning his head, how to count soldiers by the sound of their boots on stone, how to read a room from its doorways. The customs house was a converted merchant's hall with a colonnade that threw long shadows in the afternoon, and it had three doors worth knowing: the public entrance on the lane side, the garrison door where the soldiers came and went with their boots ringing, and a narrow service passage behind the records office that smelled of lamp oil and old paper.

It was through the records office window — high, barred, and mercifully never curtained against the sea light — that Ivo first studied the clerk Ferrand.

* * *

The boy — no, not a boy; twenty-three perhaps, twenty-four — worked at the third desk from the window, the one with the best light and the worst chair. Ivo noticed the chair first. Every other official in the building had requisitioned something comfortable for himself; the harbor-master had an actual upholstered thing brought from some confiscated house, and even the garrison sergeant had a cushion. Ferrand sat on a straight-backed wooden chair with one leg shimmed by a folded scrap of paper, and he had not fixed it properly because fixing it would have meant claiming the repair, and claiming things was apparently not in his nature.

He was meticulous. That was the second thing, and it revealed itself over four days of observation like a portrait emerging from washes. Other clerks worked in drifts — papers shuffled, stacked, abandoned mid-task when a superior called. Ferrand worked in lines. He squared his pages before writing on them. He blotted every signature. When he made an error — Ivo watched him make exactly two in six days — he did not scribble it out but cut the page cleanly from its string and began again, setting the spoiled sheet aside in a separate pile, face down, as though ashamed of it.

And he was uneasy. That was the third thing, and the most important, and Ivo saw it in details so small that anyone who had not spent fifteen years cataloguing the grammar of fear would have missed them entirely. It was in the way Ferrand's pen paused — always, always — at the same word in the manifests he copied out. *Dependents.* Ivo could not see the word from the wall, but he could see the pause: a half-second hitch in the otherwise metronomic hand, the nib lifted, hovering, then set down again with a small extra pressure, as if pressing the word flatter into the page might make it mean less.

It was in the way Ferrand ate his lunch — bread and cheese from a cloth, alone at his desk, facing the window rather than the room. And it was in what he did on the fifth day, when a garrison lieutenant brought down the order concerning the proscribed dependents to be held aboard the Corvane overnight before sailing. The lieutenant dropped the order on the harbor-master's empty desk with the carelessness of a man handling other people's fates, said something about the morning tide, and left.

Ferrand looked at the order for a long time. Then he picked it up, read it twice, and copied it into the record book in his careful hand — and Ivo, watching from forty feet away through barred glass, saw him stop after copying it and sit with both hands flat on the desk, quite still, for the better part of a minute. A man praying, or a man doing arithmetic with his conscience.

Ivo had seen that stillness before. He had worn it himself, once, in another life, in another war, holding papers that could save or hang people while men with boots waited for him to finish copying.

*So,* he thought, netting needles flashing through nothing in particular. *There is a crack in the wall.*

But a crack was not a door, and Ivo had not survived this long by mistaking the one for the other. Suspicion was a craft too, and the Empire trained its clerks in it. A kindness offered too soon would read as a purchase attempted. He would have to let the man come to curiosity on his own — or engineer the ground beneath him so gently that curiosity seemed like the clerk's own idea.

Trust, Ivo had learned, was not given and not taken. It was built, plank by plank, in weather, by someone who knew which way the grain ran.

* * *

On the seventh day, the opportunity arrived wearing the uniform of pure accident — which is to say, Ivo had arranged it three days in advance and simply waited for the tide of ordinary events to carry it in.

Ferrand, it emerged, kept a habit: each evening at the closing bell he carried a leather folio of commission papers from the records office down the lane to the harbor-master's residence for countersignature, and returned with it near dark. The route took him past the chandlery, past Bastien's shuttered wine shop, and along the short stretch of cobbled quay where the fishing boats lay keel-hauled for winter scraping. Ivo had walked the route himself, twice, at the same hour, noting the lanterns (two, both poorly trimmed), the loose cobble near the chandlery steps (a known ankle-breaker; the town council had argued about it for years), and the exact width of the shadow cast by the customs house corner at dusk in late autumn (long enough).

He did not plan to take anything. Taking was a confession of intent, and intent, once visible, could be tried. He only needed the papers to be *lost* — briefly, publicly, recoverably — and for the man who found them to be a fisherman with no reason in the world to want anything from the Empire except to be left alone.

The wind helped. Wind was the oldest accomplice in Sainte-Marine. On the evening of the seventh day it came off the water in gusts hard enough to make the lanterns gutter, and when Ferrand crossed the exposed stretch of quay, hunched into his coat, one hand clamping his cap and the other the folio — one hand, Ivo noted, when it should have been two — a gust took the loose flap of the folio and peeled three sheets out of it like petals off a flower. They went skittering across the cobbles, white in the dusk, toward the black water beyond the keel-blocks.

Ferrand made a sound Ivo heard clearly even over the wind — a short, strangled syllable that was not a word but was entirely legible — and lunged. He caught one sheet under his boot heel. The second lodged against a mooring ring. The third went over the edge of the quay, dipped toward the water —

— and stopped, pinned against the hull of an upturned dinghy by the body of a broad-shouldered fisherman who had, by pure chance, been kneeling there checking a seam of caulking.

Ivo held the paper up between two fingers, already reading it upside down out of simple human reflex — a manifest header, columns of tonnage, nothing dangerous — and turned around with the expression of a man mildly inconvenienced by weather.

"Yours?" he said. "Wind's got teeth tonight."

Ferrand stood in the lamplight breathing hard, and Ivo watched the suspicion arrive in the young man's face like a tide coming in: fast, cold, covering everything. Here was a stranger holding imperial commission papers. Here was a fisherman who had *read* them, or appeared to. Here was everything a nervous clerk had ever been warned about.

"I'll take those," Ferrand said. His voice was younger than his face. "Please."

"Course." Ivo handed the sheet back — carefully, by its edge, the way one returns a blade — and retrieved the second from the mooring ring, brushing off a smear of tar with his thumb before offering it. "Tar on the stones everywhere this time of year. You'll want to check the backs."

"Thank you." Ferrand took it. He did not check the backs. He was looking at Ivo now with that particular scrutiny — the counting kind, the kind that measured a man against a list — and Ivo gave him nothing to measure. He was a fisherman. He was cold. He wanted his supper. All of it true, all of it worn loosely, like a coat that fit because he had worn it for fifteen years.

"The third one," Ferrand said suddenly. "There were three."

"This the third?" Ivo patted the dinghy's hull beside him, where the last sheet lay face-down against the planking, sheltered from the wet. He turned it over, glanced at it — a requisition schedule, the Corvane's name at the top — and held it out. "Didn't look. Well." A shrug, honest as salt. "Looked at the heading. Old habit. Can't read a bill of lading without reading the top of it."

"Why not?"

The question was quick, almost sharp, and Ivo understood that it was not really about bills of lading. It was the question underneath every question the Empire asked: *why are you looking at anything?*

"Because my brother lost a boat to a bad manifest once," Ivo said. It was a good lie, or half a lie — there had been a brother, and there had been a boat, and the two facts had never actually touched. "Cargo listed wrong, insurance refused. Man works his whole life and loses it to somebody's sloppy column." He nodded at the folio. "Yours are neat, though. Saw that much. Columns lined up proper."

Something moved behind Ferrand's eyes — surprise, quickly banked, then a flicker of something warmer and more dangerous: pleasure. Praise, from a stranger, for the one thing he evidently cared about being good at.

"They're countersigned tomorrow," Ferrand said, and then seemed to hear his own voice giving information to a fisherman on a quay, and closed up like a ledger. "Thank you for the papers. Good evening."

"Evening." Ivo gathered his tools, unhurried, and added, to the young man's retreating back, in the tone of a man mentioning the price of herring: "Loose cobble by the chandlery steps. Takes ankles every winter. Walk wide of it in the dark."

Ferrand stopped. Turned halfway. Looked at Ivo for a long moment with an expression Ivo could not entirely classify — part suspicion still, but part something else, something that had begun, very quietly, to wonder.

"You know the lane well," Ferrand said.

"Born here," Ivo said, and the lie sat in his mouth like a smooth stone, worn round by fifteen years of handling. "Good night to you."

* * *

After that, Ivo let the silence work.

This was the hardest discipline, harder than the watching: doing nothing. Every instinct honed in the war said *press*, *follow*, *hasten the ripening*. But trust pressed too early curdles into obligation, and obligation curdles into resentment, and resentment reports you to the garrison. So Ivo stayed away from the customs house for two full days. He mended nets that needed mending. He drank one glass of Bastien's rough red and listened to gossip about guard rotations without asking a single direct question. He slept badly, as he had slept badly for fifteen years, and rose before the light as he always did, and told himself that patience was also a form of action.

On the third day, the clerk came to him.

Not to the wall, not to the quay — to the slipway below the boatwright's yard, where Ivo was scraping barnacles off a lugger's garboard with the boatwright's permission and the boatwright's absence. Ivo heard the footsteps on the shingle and knew them before he turned: quick, even, the walk of a man who squared his pages.

"Master Joubert." The name landed strangely — his false name, spoken by an official voice, in an official context Ivo hadn't anticipated. "I'm Ferrand. Customs service." A pause. "You found my papers."

"And your cobble," Ivo said, laying down the scraper. "Did you walk wide of it?"

A hesitation. Then, with the faintest reluctance of a man admitting to having taken advice: "Yes."

"Good." Ivo wiped his hands on his thighs and waited. He did not offer conversation. Offering was pressure, and pressure was visible.

Ferrand shifted his weight. Up close, in daylight, he looked younger and more tired than through barred glass — a thin young man with ink stains worked deep into the skin of his right hand, and eyes that had the particular rawness of someone who had recently seen something he could not unsee. There was a paper under his arm. Ivo noted it without noting it: a folio, sealed, official.

"I've been sent," Ferrand said, "to assess the harbor craft for the requisition inventory. Boats, tackle, seaworthiness. The Corvane will need local pilots, and the pilot contracts require verification of—" He stopped, visibly annoyed at his own transparency, and started again, stiffer. "Your boat is the *Maren Louise*. Twelve feet, sprit rig, licensed fifteen years ago."

"Fifteen years come spring," Ivo agreed pleasantly.

"Yes." Ferrand looked at the lugger, at the shingle, anywhere but at Ivo's face. "The harbor-master wants three pilots contracted for the sailing. Your name came up. Bastien Aubry spoke for you."

"Bastien speaks for everyone. It's cheaper than buying rounds."

Something twitched at the corner of Ferrand's mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where one would go if it had somewhere to live. He looked down at the folio under his arm, and Ivo watched him wrestle with whatever he had actually come to say, the words queuing behind his teeth like passengers at a closed gate.

Finally: "The papers you returned. They were commission documents. Countersignature drafts. If they'd gone into the water, the delay would have been—" He stopped. Started again. "Most men would have read them thoroughly. Some would have sold them. One or two would have invented a debt the Empire owed them and collected it."

"And instead you're contracting me to pilot a troopship," Ivo said. "Which of those men does that make me?"

The question hung between them, sharper than Ivo had intended, and he watched Ferrand decide whether to take offense. The young man's chin came up a fraction — pride, real pride, the meticulous kind — and then settled.

"It makes you a man whose columns line up," Ferrand said. "So far."

"So far," Ivo agreed. "That's all any of us get, clerk. So far."

Ferrand held out the folio then — the actual business, the pilot's contract, blank in the particulars, awaiting Ivo's mark. But he held it a moment longer than the transaction required, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped, and the words came out in the careful cadence of something rehearsed on a sleepless night:

"There's an order coming through. Concerning certain passengers. Proscribed dependents, to be held aboard until the mainland." His eyes came up and fixed on Ivo's, and there it was — the arithmetic-with-the-conscience look, naked now, asking without asking. "The manifest categories for harbor laborers are... rarely audited twice. I mention it only because a pilot should know his ship's complement."

The shingle ticked and settled beneath their feet. Somewhere out in the roadstead, the Corvane lay at anchor, grey and patient, her holds waiting.

Ivo kept his face still, kept his hands loose, kept fifteen years of discipline wrapped around the sudden hammering of his heart. *Do not reach for it. Do not show him it matters.*

"A pilot should," Ivo said gravely. "Thank you for the professional courtesy."

Ferrand nodded once, squared the folio against his hip — the gesture of a man aligning himself with something — and walked back up the shingle toward the lane, stepping wide, without appearing to think about it, of the loose cobble by the chandlery steps.

Ivo stood a long time in the cold, watching him go, feeling the shape of what had just happened settle over him like fog off the water. The clerk had come to him. The clerk had *told* him. Not enough — never enough, not yet, trust was a slow plank-by-plank build and this was only the first board laid — but enough to know that somewhere inside the Empire's neat, squared, meticulously blotted machinery, a young man with ink-stained hands was doing conscience-arithmetic late at night, and arriving at sums that frightened him.

Seven days remained before the morning tide.

Ivo picked up his scraper and went back to the barnacles, and above the sound of the blade on the hull he could almost hear the clock starting — not the garrison's drum, not the harbor bell, but something quieter and more relentless: the sound of a promise, fifteen years patient, beginning at last to move.
