## 12. Solène's Mistake

The message was written on the back of a fish-market tally, because no one reads a fish-market tally.

Solène had copied it out three times before she got it right — small, cramped letters, the kind her grandmother used for accounts, the kind that looked like nothing more than arithmetic. *Cousin Odile. The granary holds you two nights more. There is a woman in the third house past the chapel, Mme Aubry, who owes your mother a debt from the herring year. Say the word "salt" and she will hide you until the tide after next. Do not run at night. Do not run at all. — S.*

It was not much of a plan. She knew that. Ivo would have hated it — Ivo, who taught her to walk like a woman carrying nothing but bread, who mapped guard rotations in pencil so faint it looked like the paper had been breathed on rather than written. Ivo would have said: *wait*. Ivo had been waiting for fifteen years and it had bought her mother a cell in some garrison town and her cousin a cell in the granary, and Solène had decided, somewhere in the last six days, that waiting was simply another word for letting other people do your losing for you.

So she folded the tally into quarters, tucked it into the lining of her left boot where a coin would sit if she'd had one, and went down into Sainte-Marine on the morning of the sixth day with a basket of mussels she did not intend to sell.

* * *

The town had learned a new grammar in six days.

Before the requisition, Sainte-Marine had spoken in the old language of harbors — greetings shouted across water, bargains struck half aloud, griefs announced by staying home. Now there were imperial sentences laid over all of it: the drum at dawn and dusk, the white paint on the customs house doors, the way conversation died in a street when boots sounded at either end of it. Solène walked through this new grammar the way Ivo had taught her — eyes down but not darting, hands occupied, pace even. *Be dull*, he'd said. *Dullness is a country with open borders.*

She took the long way to the granary, the way a woman might drift who had nowhere particular to be. The granary stood at the head of the quay, squat and gray, its doors now marked with the Empire's inventory stencils. Two soldiers at the door, one picking his teeth, the other reading something with his lips moving. A cart stood nearby being loaded with sacks — grain going out to the Corvane, or to the garrison kitchen; it hardly mattered. What mattered was the cart, and the rhythm of loading, and the fact that both soldiers' attention bent toward the work whenever a sack came down wrong.

Cousin Odile had been taken four days ago, swept up in the same denunciation that had put the Delac name on the proscription list twice over — once for Marguerite, wherever Marguerite was, and once, apparently, for the sin of sharing blood with her. Odile was sixty-one years old and had spent her whole life gutting fish and raising other people's children, and the Empire had decided this made her a courier of sedition. Solène had seen her through the granary's high window on the second day, a gray shape behind glass, hands moving as if still cleaning fish, because the body keeps its habits even when the world stops making sense.

The plan was simple enough to be stupid: get the tally to Odile. Not through the door — through the window, on a morning when the wind off the water was strong enough to cover the sound of a stone wrapped in paper. The window faced the alley behind the cooperage. The alley had one entrance, which was the problem, and one pile of barrel staves, which was the solution, or so it seemed at nine o'clock with her heart going like a gaff hook in her side.

She waited in the mouth of the alley for the cart's rhythm. Sack. Sack. Shout. Sack. On the shout she crossed the cobbles, pressed herself under the window, and drew breath to whisper upward—

"Oi."

Not from above. From behind.

A soldier — young, red-faced, the teeth-picker — stood at the alley mouth with his musket slung and his hand already on the strap. "This is a closed lane. Inventory access only." His eyes went from her face to the basket of mussels to the wall beneath the window, where her shadow was too long and too close. "What are you doing back here?"

"Sheltering from the wind," Solène said, and heard how thin it came out. "The mussels—"

"The mussels are in your basket." He stepped forward. Under the window, in the shadow of the sill, lay the stone. Her stone. Wrapped in the tally. Placed there in the three seconds between his voice and hers, placed badly, placed visibly, sitting on pale cobbles like an accusation.

He saw it. She watched him see it — watched his eyes narrow, watched the arithmetic move across his young face: stone, window, girl, alley.

"Hold there."

She held. It was astonishing, afterward, how completely the body obeys in such moments, how the legs that wanted to run simply locked, how the mind offered nothing but a bright blank noise like the sea in a shell. The soldier bent toward the stone. His fingers were a foot away from it. One turn of the cloth and the tally would open like a confession, and *Delac* would be spoken aloud in an alley, and everything — grandmother, Ivo, the pilot's contract she wasn't supposed to know about, the whole fragile machine of their seven remaining days — would grind against the rocks.

Then a voice said, pleasantly, from the lane's mouth:

"There you are. I've been walking the whole quay for you."

Ivo Joubert came into the alley carrying a plank.

It was, Solène thought later, the most ordinary plank in the world — weathered pine, boat-length, with a rope handle worn smooth. He carried it the way men carry planks, slightly askew, breathing through the effort of it, and he looked at the soldier with the mild apologetic squint of a workman whose day has been inconvenienced.

"This lane's closed," the soldier said, straightening, his hand leaving the stone.

"So I heard. That's why I'm late." Ivo shifted the plank. "Boatwright. They sent for me to look at the Corvane's rudder pintles, and your corporal told me to come round the cooperage, and then nobody's here, and I've carried this blessed thing—" he let the weight of the plank sag theatrically, "—from the far slipway. You wouldn't help me carry it to the customs house, would you? My back's older than the Republic."

The soldier blinked. The sentence had done what good sentences do — filled the air so completely there was no room in it for suspicion. But his eyes flicked once more toward the stone, and Solène saw Ivo see that flicker, and saw Ivo do something she would remember for the rest of her life: he set the plank down, directly on top of the stone, as if setting down a plank, and straightened with a groan.

"Now," Ivo said cheerfully, "before my spine files a complaint. Which way to the customs house?"

The soldier hesitated — looked at the girl with the mussels, at the plank, at the empty lane full of nothing worth reporting. "You," he said to Solène, "move along. Market side."

"Gladly," Solène said, and her voice worked, barely.

"And you'll want the front of the customs house, not the harbor door," the soldier added, almost kindly now, gesturing Ivo through. "Come on, then, I'll show you. Corporal will skin me if the rudder-man gets lost."

They went. All three of them, absurdly, the soldier leading, Ivo staggering under his plank, Solène drifting away marketward with her useless basket, and not until she had turned two corners and put the chapel between herself and the quay did she stop, set the mussels down, and discover that her hands were shaking so hard she had to hold one with the other like a child learning to pray.

* * *

She found him at the slipway at dusk, coiling line with those slow deliberate hands, and she did not wait for a greeting.

"You could have been taken."

Ivo didn't look up. "Could have."

"Do you understand what they do to boatwrights who obstruct inventories? To anyone? They tried a mussel-seller from Petit-Port last month for carrying seditious oysters. *Oysters.* And you walk into a closed lane with your whole history in your pocket and start performing for a boy with a musket—"

"I performed for him precisely because he has a musket," Ivo said quietly. "Boys with muskets want two things: someone to obey, and someone to believe them important. I gave him both. You gave him a mystery."

"A mystery?" Her voice cracked upward. "I gave him a *stone*, Ivo. Because I was trying to reach my cousin, who is in that building, who is sixty-one years old and terrified, while everyone else in this town perfects the art of looking at the ground! Someone had to do something. Someone had to—"

"To what?" He set the coil down. In the last light his face was hard to read, which she knew by now was not accident but architecture. "To pass her a note telling her to hide with Mme Aubry? Who has three children and a husband already questioned twice? So that when Odile is traced there — and she would be traced, Solène, within a day, because the granary lists every visitor and every parcel — Mme Aubry's family goes onto a list too. Is that the something? Is that the rescue?"

"It's better than nothing!"

"No." He said it without heat, and that was the worst of it. "Nothing is sometimes better than something. Nothing doesn't hang anyone. I have spent fifteen years learning exactly how much damage a kind impulse can do, and I would rather teach you cowardice than watch you learn it the way I did."

"Then teach me!" She was shouting now and did not care; the gulls lifted off the pilings; somewhere down the quay a soldier laughed at something that wasn't this. "Teach me how to be invisible! Teach me how to keep my head down for fifteen years while my mother rots in whatever hole they put her in! Teach me how to receive a letter saying *my daughter will always have a door* and never once open it!"

The silence after that was total. Even the water seemed to pull back.

"You read the letter," Ivo said at last. His voice had gone very low and very flat, the voice of a man standing at the edge of deep water. "In my chest. I know, because it was moved. Two fingers' width, and the ribbon retied left-handed. I let it be, because you had the right. But don't stand on that quay and tell me I never opened the door. I built the door. I paid rent on it for fifteen years in a coin you can't see — in every night I didn't write, every name I didn't say, every time Bastien asked me who I really was and I lied to the one friend I had left. You think silence is free? Silence is the most expensive thing there is. I have been paying for yours since before you could walk."

"Then why?" It came out of her as a sob disguised as a question. "Why stay silent? If you loved her, if you promised — why let us think you'd forgotten? Why let *me* grow up thinking my mother's friends were cowards?"

"Because word travels like fire in a small town, and a letter is a torch." He stepped closer, and for the first time since she'd known him he let her see his face without the workman's squint, and what was underneath was so tired it frightened her. "Every person who ever helped your mother is alive because no one could follow a thread back through me. If I had written to you — one letter, one — and it had been opened, read, copied, filed, then the Delac name grows a branch, and the branch reaches Mme Aubry, reaches Bastien, reaches every hand that touched us. Your mother understood that. It's why her letters stopped coming. She wasn't abandoning you, Solène. She was starving the trail. And she asked me to do the same, and I hated her for asking, and I did it anyway, because love that can't be proven is still love. It's just love with nowhere to put itself."

Solène stood with the wind pulling her hair across her mouth and found that she was crying without any drama at all, the way rain starts, everywhere at once. "And today?"

"Today was different. Today I broke my own rule, and I'll likely pay for it — the boy will remember my face, and faces are currency in a garrison town." He picked the coil of line back up, and his hands, she noticed, were not entirely steady either. "But you have to hear this, and hear it once. Your recklessness and my silence are the same wound, girl. We both decided we knew what the people we loved could survive. You decided Odile could survive your note. I decided you could survive my absence. Both of us guessed. Both of us guessed wrong, or right, and none of us will ever know until it's too late to matter. So from tonight, we stop guessing alone. Whatever you want to risk, you bring to me first. And whatever I decide to protect you from—" his jaw tightened, "—I'll tell you that too. Even when it shames me. *Especially* then."

The tide was turning; he glanced at it the way other men check a clock. Then he said, in a voice stripped of everything except the thing itself:

"They're moving her tomorrow. Odile. Onto the Corvane, with the other dependents, to be tried on the mainland. I heard it from the corporal himself, today, while he was walking me to my own trap." He met her eyes. "Six days until the tide, Solène. And now you know why I needed you furious instead of careful. Careful people freeze. Furious people — furious people help me carry planks."

Down the quay, the customs house lamps were being lit one by one, and among the small yellow windows Solène picked out — because Ivo had taught her to pick things out — the one that burned latest every night, the clerk Ferrand's window, behind which a careful young man was even now bending over pages that contained all their names.

Tomorrow, Ivo said, he would need a message delivered. Properly, this time. To the customs house, of all places.

Solène wiped her face with her sleeve, and said she knew just how to be forgettable.

* * *

That night she dreamed of the alley, but in the dream the soldier picked up the stone, and the stone was a letter, and the letter was in her mother's handwriting, and it said only: *You should have waited.*

She woke before the drum, and lay listening to the harbor breathe, and began — carefully, furiously, kin at last — to plan.
