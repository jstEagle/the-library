## 13. The Harbor-Master's Dinner

Bastien Guillory had spent fifteen years learning the precise weight of a bottle.

Not the weight of the wine inside it — any fisherman could heft a jug and guess its fullness — but the social weight of it: which vintage to carry into which house, how much of its price to admit to, how to set it down on a table so that it seemed an afterthought rather than an offering. He had learned that a bottle of the cheap white from the hills above Sainte-Marine opened doors in the garrison barracks, where men drank to forget they'd been posted to a fishing town at the edge of nowhere. He had learned that the customs officers preferred something with a label, though most of them could not have named what they were drinking if you asked them plainly. And he had learned, over long and patient years, exactly what Arnaud Vasseur, harbor-master of Sainte-Marine, liked to drink on the nights when his wife was away visiting her sister in Valcourt-sur-Mer.

She was away tonight. Bastien had confirmed it that morning through the fishwife Odette, who heard everything worth hearing and half of what wasn't, and who owed him for three winters of first pick of the herring catch. So when he climbed the stone steps of the harbor-master's house as the light went amber over the breakwater, he carried not one bottle but two: the good red from across the border, smuggled in with a consignment of salt cod two seasons back and kept hidden behind a false panel in his cellar for exactly such an evening as this.

Vasseur answered the door himself, which was a good sign. A man who answers his own door is a man who has decided to trust you before you've said a word.

"Guillory." The harbor-master was a heavy man gone soft around the jaw, with the complexion of someone who spent his days indoors reading other people's paperwork and his evenings undoing the day's restraint. His uniform coat hung open; his collar was already loose. "You're early."

"I'm punctual," Bastien said. "There's a difference, and only one of it gets you invited back." He held up the bottles. "For the table. Not for flattery."

"Everything is for flattery with you, Bastien. That's why I like you." Vasseur stepped aside to let him in, and the warmth of the hallway — a fire lit, bread baking, the smell of a duck roasting somewhere deeper in the house — closed around them both like water.

* * *

The dinner went the way Bastien had built it to go, brick by patient brick, year upon year.

He had begun cultivating Arnaud Vasseur in the second autumn after the Empire absorbed the coast, when the new customs house went up on the ruins of the old toll office and every fisherman in Sainte-Marine understood, without anyone saying it aloud, that their livelihoods now depended on the goodwill of strangers with stamps and seals. Some of the fishermen had sulked. Some had blustered. Old Morvan had gotten drunk at the Anchorline and said things about imperial clerks that cost him six months of inspections and a fine that ate his winter. But Bastien Guillory, who had been a middling fisherman with a middling boat and a talent for nothing in particular except noticing what people wanted, had simply started showing up.

He showed up with wine. He showed up with news of the harbor — which channels were silting, which buoys the winter storms had dragged, which boats were running light on their declared catches. He showed up with small favors that cost him little and accumulated interest the way tide accumulates against a seawall. He learned that Vasseur's son had died at Sarnholt in the last campaign of the annexation wars, and he never once mentioned it, and never once let an anniversary pass without appearing at the harbor-master's door with something unremarkable and kind. He learned that Vasseur's wife despised him mildly and permanently, and that dinner alone with a cheerful guest was the closest thing to happiness the man permitted himself.

Fifteen years of dinners. Fifteen years of being the harmless man, the funny man, the local man who knew the water and wanted nothing except to be useful. It had bought Bastien quiet berths for his own boat, forgiving eyes during lean seasons, and — twice, when the audits came around — the gentle loss of certain pages from certain files. He had told himself for years that this was simply prudence, the same prudence every man in Sainte-Marine practiced in his own fashion. Only in the last month, since the requisition orders went up on the customs house door, had he understood that he had been building toward something all along without knowing what it was.

Now he knew. Now there was Ivo Joubert, standing at his kitchen table three nights ago with a face like weathered slate, saying: *I need to be on that ship, or I need to be beside it, and either way I need your help.*

And Bastien had said yes. He hadn't even made Ivo wait a decent interval. He had said yes the way a man says yes to a drowning swimmer, without thinking about whether the rope will hold.

Tonight was the rope.

* * *

"So," Vasseur said, tearing the duck with his hands because his wife wasn't there to correct him, "the Corvane. You've heard she sails on the morning tide nine days hence."

"The whole town has heard. Half of it can hear her boilers from the point."

"Nine days." Vasseur chewed, swallowed, reached for the wine Bastien had opened. "And then this town goes back to being a town, thank God. You have no idea what it's like, Guillory. Requisitions. Manifests. Every clerk in the district suddenly convinced that Sainte-Marine is hiding the entire republican treasury under its fish racks." He laughed, and Bastien laughed with him, and both of them knew the laugh meant nothing.

"A pilot," Bastien said, in the tone of a man raising a matter of business because business was what friends discussed over duck. "The Corvane will want a local pilot for the outer channel. The shoals off the Marenne point shift every spring. Your naval officers don't know that water."

"They don't know anything about this water." Vasseur waved his fork at the window, beyond which the harbor lights were coming up one by one, small and gold against the darkening sea. "I've told Valcourt three times. Three times! Their charts are forty years old. If they run her aground on the Marenne bar it'll be my name in the report, not theirs. It's always my name."

"Then name a pilot who does know it. Ivo Joubert."

Bastien watched the name land. Watched Vasseur pause with the glass halfway to his mouth, not because the name meant anything dangerous to him — it didn't, couldn't, Ivo Joubert was nobody, had been nobody so successfully for fifteen years that even Bastien sometimes forgot there had ever been another name underneath — but because the harbor-master's mind worked slowly and thoroughly, like a press squeezing oil.

"Joubert," Vasseur said. "The quiet one. Mends nets for half the fleet. Reads tide-tables like a priest reads scripture." He took the sip after all. "Can he handle a ship that size? She's a troopship, not a herring boat."

"He handled deepwater freight out of the southern ports before he came here. Ask him about the channel and watch him answer — he doesn't guess, Arnaud. Men who guess drown pilots. Joubert measures."

It was true, and Bastien was grateful it was true. It was easier to sell a truth than a lie; lies needed maintenance, and Bastien had enough to maintain.

Vasseur was silent for a moment, turning his glass by its stem. Then: "There's a contract form. Pilotage, contracted passage, standard fee paid at disembarkation. It puts his name on the ship's manifest, which means it puts his name in front of the military governor's office, which means—" he shrugged, heavy shoulders rising and falling like a moored hull, "—which means if there's anything wrong with Ivo Joubert, it becomes my problem instead of yours. You understand that."

"I understand it," Bastien said. "Which is why I'm asking you and no one else. Because you'd know. And you know there's nothing wrong with him."

"Do I?" Vasseur smiled, and for a moment the smile had the customs house behind it, the ledgers, the seals, the slow machinery of the Empire grinding through the town's paper skin. "Fifteen years, Guillory, and I've never once audited that man's file. Do you know why?"

"Because he's never given you cause."

"Because you vouched for him." Vasseur pointed the glass at him, gently, almost fondly. "Year after year, dinner after dinner, some little thing comes up — a license renewal, a berth assignment, a question about who's reliable — and you say *Joubert's solid*, and I write it down, and I sleep well. You've been his pilot's license longer than I've been his harbor-master, do you realize that? You've been his signature."

Bastien felt something turn over beneath his ribs, slow and cold, like a hull shifting against its moorings. He had known, in the abstract, that his friendship with Vasseur was a currency he spent on Ivo's behalf. He had not quite known that he was the ledger itself.

"Well," he said, and heard his own voice come out steady, warm, easy — fifteen years of practice in three syllables. "Then consider it my signature too. Put him on the contract. He'll get your ship through the Marenne channel with her keel untouched, and you'll get a clean report, and we'll drink to it."

Vasseur looked at him for a moment longer than was comfortable. Then he laughed, the big helpless laugh of a lonely man being managed and not minding, and raised his glass. "To the Corvane, then. And to your quiet friend. I'll have the papers drawn tomorrow. He signs at my office, notarized, before the sailing. Standard terms."

They drank. The duck was excellent. Outside, the tide was turning, and neither man remarked on it.

* * *

It happened at the cheese, which was how these things always happened — not at the moment of decision but afterward, in the slack water, when a man's guard had drained out of him along with the wine.

"The records, though," Vasseur said, leaning back until his chair creaked. He had a rind of cheese in one hand and the second bottle — the good red, which Bastien noted with distant pride had been pronounced superior within minutes — in the other. "That's the part that's going to kill me. Did I tell you Ferrand found a discrepancy in the granary accounts? The boy. My junior clerk. Found it in an afternoon, going through fifteen years of municipal bookkeeping with a magnifying glass like it was scripture. A discrepancy of eleven sous, Guillory. Eleven. From before the war."

"Ferrand." Bastien turned the name over carefully. "The young one. Always writing."

"Always writing. Never drinks. Looks at me like I owe him money." Vasseur snorted. "Good clerk, though. Too good. Which is the problem, because—" he paused, and Bastien saw the thought arrive behind his eyes like weather coming up over the horizon, "—because the orders came down yesterday. Final verification protocols. Everything the Corvane carries gets reconciled before she casts off. Troops against muster rolls. Cargo against manifests. And the records—" Vasseur gestured vaguely with the bottle, and a drop of dark wine fell on the cloth and spread like a small wound, "—the municipal records, the harbor ledger, all of it, verified against the passenger lists. Every name on every document cross-checked against every person stepping aboard. Discrepancies go to the mainland for trial."

*Trial.* The word sat in the room between them, plain and terrible, wearing no disguise at all.

"Trial," Bastien repeated. His voice was still easy. He marveled at it from somewhere far away, the way a man might marvel at his own hand continuing to hold a fork while the rest of him went under ice. "Trial for bookkeeping errors?"

"For discrepancies." Vasseur chewed, unhurried. "That's the word they use. A discrepancy is a misfiled page or it's a false name or it's a man who arrived in this harbor fifteen years ago with no origin and no history, and the tribunal on the mainland doesn't much care which, because from where they sit it's all the same shape. Treason-shaped." He laughed again, but it was thinner now, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "You should see the forms. There's a form for denunciation, a form for verification, a form for referral. Somebody in Valcourt has been very busy designing forms."

Bastien poured himself more wine. His hand did not shake, and he hated it a little, how steady his hands had become at exactly the moments they should tremble. "And the pilot's contract? Would Joubert's name be on these passenger lists?"

"On the manifest, certainly. Contracted personnel, category four." Vasseur waved the concern away like smoke. "Category four isn't audited twice, if that's what's worrying you. Pilots, stevedores, harbor labor — the army doesn't care who ties the lines as long as the lines get tied. No, no, it's the records themselves that worry me. Fifteen years of this town's paper, Guillory, going under a clerk's eye line by line, and every discrepancy—" he tapped the table with one thick finger, once, twice, like a man knocking on a door he hoped was empty, "—every discrepancy gets tried. On the mainland. In front of a tribunal that hasn't acquitted anyone since spring."

He said it lightly. That was the worst of it. He said it the way he said everything, wrapped in complaint and self-pity and the mild grievance of a middle-aged man burdened with paperwork, and underneath the lightness the machinery turned and turned, and Bastien sat across the table from him smiling his fifteen-year smile and watching the whole edifice of his careful life reveal itself to be load-bearing in ways he had never examined.

Because he knew what was in those records. Not everything — Ivo knew everything, Ivo had recited it in Bastien's kitchen with the terrible flat calm of a man reading his own death warrant — but enough. The false arrival date. The fees paid in old republican coin, entered without comment by a customs officer who had since retired to the interior and was probably dead. The entry that read *Delac, dependents, three,* in a clerk's careful hand, dated to a week when a woman translator and her children had come down from the capital with nothing but suitcases and a letter of introduction, and the harbor-master of that day — Vasseur's predecessor, old Toussaint, dead now, mercifully, or maybe not, maybe the tribunal would dig him up too — had entered them into the ledger the way one enters any family arriving by sea.

Every discrepancy tried on the mainland. And the Delacs were still in Sainte-Marine, still carrying their proscribed name like a brand, and in nine days the Corvane would sail with the ledger aboard and the girl Solène aboard as a hostage-guest of the Empire, and somewhere in the stack of documents in Ferrand's meticulous hands sat the page that would hang them all, sitting next to the page with Bastien's own name on it, because Bastien Guillory had signed the harbor fee receipts for the Delac household for fifteen years, had collected their mooring dues and their market stall levies, had been the friendly face of the customs house to a family that could afford to be friendly with no one.

His signature. Everywhere, his signature.

"—and of course I told them," Vasseur was saying, "I told Valcourt straight away, you cannot reconcile fifteen years of provincial bookkeeping in nine days, it's absurd, the arithmetic alone — but you know how it is. Orders are orders. Ferrand's already started. The boy works nights now." He refilled his glass, missed slightly, caught the spill with his thumb. "Between us, Guillory, I give it a month before that young man has a breakdown or a promotion. Possibly both."

Bastien laughed. It came out sounding right. It always came out sounding right; that was his gift and his curse, that the sound of him was reliable even when nothing underneath it was.

"You've gone quiet," Vasseur said, squinting at him through the candlelight and the wine. "Planning something. You always go quiet when you're planning something."

"Always," Bastien agreed. "But tonight I'm only planning breakfast. More duck, Arnaud?"

"There's no more duck. There's never more duck. That's why I eat it the way I do." Vasseur settled back, sated and slow, and the candles burned down between them, and the talk drifted onto safer ground — the price of salt, the incompetence of the garrison commander, the scandal of the lighthouse keeper's daughter — and Bastien rode the current of it, nodding, laughing in the right places, all the while feeling the cold spread upward through him like water filling a hold.

*Complicity*. He had worn the word comfortably for fifteen years, the way a man wears an old coat: it had shaped itself to him, it had kept him warm, he had stopped noticing its weight. But words curdle. Tonight, over the remains of a duck and the dregs of a good bottle, in the house of a lonely man who trusted him completely, Bastien Guillory felt the word turn in his stomach and spoil — because complicity implied choice, and choice implied alternatives, and he had just realized, sitting in the warm yellow light with his oldest investment paying out exactly as designed, that he had spent fifteen years building a machine whose only output was other people's danger, and that he had never once, not once, asked what it would cost the people it fed on.

Ivo had come to him three nights ago and asked for one berth. One name. And Bastien had said yes, gladly, generously, and had gone home pleased with himself, and had slept soundly.

One berth. While the ledger sailed east with everything in it. While the discrepancies went to trial. While Solène Delac — nineteen years old, sharp-eyed, desperate, the living image of a mother Bastien remembered laughing once at a harbor feast, just once, before the world contracted to silence — boarded that ship as property of the Empire, catalogued and cross-referenced, her existence reduced to a line in a column that a boy named Ferrand would verify line by line by line.

*Discrepancies will be tried.*

He had done this. He, Bastien Guillory, with his bottles and his dinners and his fifteen years of harmless charm. He had smoothed the path so well that the Empire was rolling down it at speed, and everyone he had ever pretended to protect was tied to the front of it.

* * *

He left late, as he always left late, with Vasseur clapping him on the shoulder at the door and telling him, warmly, stupidly, "You're a good friend, Guillory. The best this town has." The night air hit him like a bucket of harbor water. The stars were out, hard and cold over the black line of the Marenne point, and down in the harbor the Corvane lay at her moorings with her riding lamps burning, a great dark animal breathing steam in its sleep, and beyond her the customs house stood lit in one window — the corner window, Ferrand's window — where a thin shadow bent over a desk, writing, always writing, working his way down through fifteen years of a town's life one line at a time.

Bastien stood on the steps a long time, the cold working through his coat, doing the arithmetic he had refused to do at the table.

Nine days. The contract signed tomorrow — Ivo safe, Ivo aboard, Ivo delivered. That part was done; that part he could be proud of, if pride were still available to him. But the rest of it — the ledger, the girl, the grandmother, the cousin already in custody, the pages with his own name recurring through them like a stain — the rest of it was moving now, moving on rails he had greased, and it would not stop for his regret, and it would not stop for his friendship, and it would not stop for anything less than a man willing to burn down everything he had built.

He thought of Ivo's face in his kitchen. *I need your help.* He had assumed he knew the size of the ask.

He walked home the long way, along the quay, past the shuttered fish stalls and the sleeping boats, and at his own door he stopped, key in hand, and looked back once at the lit window of the customs house.

Then he went inside, took a stub of pencil and the back of a fish-market receipt, and wrote four words in his cramped, illiterate-looking hand, which he had spent fifteen years teaching people to underestimate:

*Ivo. Come tonight. Urgent.*

By midnight, folded small and sealed with tallow, it would be under the third floorboard of Joubert's net shed, where it had lain waiting for fifteen years for a message that had never needed sending — until now.
