## 15. Ferrand's Question

The summons came the way frost comes to a window — quietly, from the edges, so that by the time you noticed it, it had already taken hold.

Ivo was mending a net on the quay when the boy arrived, maybe twelve years old, wearing the grey coat of a customs runner and an expression of enormous self-importance that sat badly on his thin face. He stopped three paces away, as though Ivo were something that might bite, and unfolded a slip of paper with hands that trembled slightly in the morning cold.

"Ivo Joubert?" he read aloud, then looked up, disappointed, as if he'd expected someone grander. "You're to come to the customs house. The clerk's office. Before the noon bell." He hesitated, then added, with the cruelty of the young, "He said — the clerk said — *not* the harbor-master's office. His own."

Ivo set down the netting needle slowly, giving himself the four seconds it took to fold the fear flat and put it away where he kept all the other things no one could see. Fifteen years of practice. The needle went into its groove in the wooden shuttle; the shuttle into the box; the box under the tarp. Small motions, unhurried, a man finishing a task because tasks were what fishermen finished.

"Did the clerk say why?"

"No, sir." The boy was already backing away. "He just wrote the name twice. Underlined."

Underlined. Ivo watched the boy go, his grey coat swallowed by the grey morning, and felt the day tilt beneath him like a deck in a swell. *Before the noon bell.* Three hours. Enough time to run — Bastien's boat was hauled out for recaulking at the far end of the beach, but there were others, there were always others, and the fog still came thick off the point most mornings before the sun burned it away. Enough time to run, if running were the thing a man did who had spent fifteen years learning not to.

It wasn't. That was the whole trouble. He'd taught himself so thoroughly how to stay that staying had become the only reflex left.

* * *

The customs house had been built in the republic's confident years, all pale stone and tall windows meant to suggest that trade was a form of virtue. Now the imperial eagle hung over the door on a banner that hadn't been up long enough to fade, and the tall windows had been shuttered against stones, though no stones had yet come. Soldiers flanked the entrance with the loose-limbed boredom of garrison duty in a town that offered no resistance worth mentioning. One of them checked Ivo's name against a list, pronounced it correctly — which startled him more than mispronunciation would have — and waved him through into the smell of paper, lamp oil, and cold coffee.

Ferrand's office was at the top of the stairs, small, and fanatically orderly in a way that told Ivo more about the man than any interrogation could have. Every document squared to the edge of its tray. Ink bottles ranked by size. A map of the harbor pinned flat with not one corner curling. On the desk, open, weighted down with a brass letter-knife at each page: the harbor ledger.

Ivo knew that book. Had signed it fifteen times over fifteen autumns, paying his mooring fees, renewing his berth, entering each year the same terse line in his careful fisherman's hand: *Joubert, I. Boat: the Anselie. Occupation: fisherman.*

"You came," Ferrand said, without looking up. He was younger than Ivo remembered from the quay — the uniform made men look older, or perhaps it was that Ivo had spent two days watching him through windows and across crowds, studying him at a distance, and distance always aged people. Up close, Ferrand might have been twenty-five. There was ink on the side of his right hand, the permanent stain of a man who wrote for a living, and shadows under his eyes that spoke of nights spent with ledgers rather than wine.

"You sent for me."

"Yes." Ferrand finally raised his head. He had the kind of face that would be handsome in ten years, once life had finished arguing with it, and eyes that did not do Ivo the discourtesy of pretending this was a routine matter. "Sit, please. There is tea. It's terrible. It came up from the mainland with the quartermaster stores and I suspect it was terrible before it was tea."

Ivo sat. The chair was hard and straight-backed, positioned — he noted, with a professional appreciation for detail — exactly where the light from the window fell across the visitor's face and left the clerk's in shadow. Someone had thought about this room. Or Ferrand had.

"The ledger," Ferrand began, resting one hand flat on the open page, "is a dull document. Most documents are. Names, dates, fees. The Empire requires dullness the way a body requires salt — invisibly, constantly, and it dies without it." He turned the page with the brass knife. "And then, twice a decade perhaps, a ledger does something impossible, and the auditor must decide whether he has found corruption, error, or something else entirely. Do you know what I found last night, Joubert?"

"I couldn't say, sir."

"No. But your face says you could guess, and I find I prefer faces to answers this morning. Faces don't rehearse." Ferrand turned the ledger around so it faced Ivo, and tapped one line with the knife's tip. "Your arrival date."

Ivo looked at it. Fifteen years of careful handwriting stared back at him, his own lie preserved in iron-gall ink: *Joubert, I. Arrived: 14th of the Harvest Month.* The fourteenth of the harvest month, the year the republic died — the week after the last courier routes collapsed, the fortnight after he'd walked out of the war with a cipher book sewn against his ribs and a dead man's name in his mouth.

"There is no fishing license issued in Sainte-Marine for that year," Ferrand said. "Or the following year. Or the year after that. Licenses begin again in the third spring — the Empire's administrative reach took time, you understand, they were busy hanging people closer to the capital — and the earliest license on record for the *Anselie* postdates your arrival in this harbor by two full years." He folded his hands. "Which means that either you bought a boat you could not legally crew, fished illegally for two years while every other fisherman in this town reported you and somehow none did, or—" the knife tapped the page once more, gently, almost affectionately, "—you arrived under a different set of circumstances than the ones you recorded, in a year when recording things accurately could cost a man his neck."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a soldier laughed at something, the sound rising through the floorboards like a bubble through water.

Ivo had rehearsed for this moment for fifteen years without knowing it. He had rehearsed it mending nets, hauling lines, standing in bread queues — every hour of invisible living had secretly been rehearsal for the moment the invisibility failed. And now that it had failed, now that a young man with ink-stained fingers had laid the impossibility bare on a desk between them, Ivo found that all the rehearsals dissolved, and what remained was simpler and more dangerous than any prepared answer.

The truth. Or the shape of it.

"You've read the rest of the ledger," Ivo said.

"I've read all of it. Twice."

"Then you know the fees for those first two years were paid in old coin."

A flicker crossed Ferrand's face — not surprise; confirmation. "Republican mint-marks. Yes."

"And you know whose names are entered in the autumn of my second year. Delac, dependents, three. Harbor fees waived — hardship provision." Ivo kept his voice level, kept his hands loose on his knees. "You know what the Empire does with that entry. You've seen the proscription lists. You posted them yourself."

Something moved behind the clerk's eyes — the thing Ivo had been cultivating for nine days now, the small kindnesses, the returned papers, the slow deliberate work of making himself legible to this one man — something that was not yet trust but was no longer pure suspicion. Curiosity, maybe. Curiosity was dangerous, but it was also a door, and doors opened both ways.

"I didn't summon you about the Delacs," Ferrand said.

"No," Ivo agreed. "But we're both aware they're in the room."

For a long moment the clerk studied him, and Ivo let himself be studied — really studied, for the first time in fifteen years, by anyone whose study mattered. It was an extraordinary sensation, being seen. Like surfacing after too long underwater, the light painful and the air too rich.

"Why?" Ferrand said at last. It came out quieter than his other words, almost unwillingly. "That's what kept me at this desk past midnight. Not *what* — the arithmetic is simple enough, any auditor can find the discrepancy. *Why.* Why would a man with no past — no license, no origin, no family, nothing in fifteen years but mooring fees and mended nets — why would such a man spend that past protecting a proscribed translator's children? You owed them nothing the law recognizes. There's no payment in it. No safety in it. It's the single most expensive thing in this entire ledger, and it isn't even written here — it's written around it, in the gaps." He leaned forward, into the lamplight, and for a moment the uniform fell away and there was only a tired young man asking a question the Empire had no column for. "So tell me why, Joubert. And understand — I'm not the tribunal. What you tell me goes no further than this desk unless you make it go further. But I will know if you lie to me. I read lies for a living."

Ivo looked at the ledger. At his own false date, sitting in its row of true ones like a nail driven into clean wood. He thought about Marguerite's letters in the sea chest, her polite dreadful handwriting, *the surveyors have come to count boats.* He thought about Solène's furious, grieving face — *safe like her mother?* — and about the promise, read aloud, that he had made in a diplomatic anteroom while the republic was already bleeding out somewhere beyond the windows.

He thought about the cipher book, sewn into the keel timbers of the *Anselie*, fifteen feet of oak between it and discovery, the last undelivered dispatch of a dead country.

"You said you're not the tribunal," Ivo said.

"I'm not."

"But you forward what you find. The records go to the mainland after boarding. Verified against passenger lists. Discrepancies tried." He said it evenly, without accusation — a man confirming the terms of a contract, not pleading. Ferrand's jaw tightened, which was answer enough. "So whatever I tell you becomes evidence, or it becomes nothing. Those are the choices."

"Those are the choices," Ferrand admitted. "Unless the discrepancies never reach me in a form I'm obliged to forward. Clerks have discretion over clerical matters. Misspellings. Transposed dates. Entries made in error." He paused. "Errors happen constantly, Joubert. Any honest auditor will tell you. The question is whether an honest auditor chooses to notice them."

The trap and the gift of it, offered together, in the same sentence. Ivo understood then that he had not been summoned to an interrogation at all. He had been summoned to a negotiation that Ferrand himself could not openly name — that perhaps Ferrand did not yet fully admit to himself — and that everything now depended on whether Ivo could give this meticulous, uneasy, insomniac young man a reason worth the rope it would take to hang them both.

Not mercy. Mercy was too cheap and too expensive at once. A reason.

"You asked why," Ivo said. He heard his own voice change — drop the fisherman's flattening, the regional vowels he'd worn like oilskins for fifteen years. He saw Ferrand register the change instantly, the way one professional registers another's craft. "I'll tell you. But not the way the tribunal would want it told. The truth first, and you can decide afterward what shape to put on it."

Ferrand sat back. Waited.

"Fifteen years ago I carried sealed dispatches for the republican foreign ministry. Not a soldier — a courier. Trusted with paper, which meant trusted with lives, because the paper was always about lives. My last route ran through the diplomatic quarter, and there I worked with a translator named Marguerite Delac. She decoded half of what I carried and encoded the rest. She had a husband who was mostly away, and a daughter who was four years old and afraid of pigeons." Something in Ivo's chest loosened painfully at the memory — the little girl solemn over her bread, the pigeons scattering. "When the routes collapsed, when it became clear which way the war was going, she asked me for one thing. Not escape — she had her own arrangements, her own network, better ones than mine. She asked me for a door. A place that would exist, later, afterward, wherever she ended up and whoever her daughter had become. She said — I've never forgotten the phrasing, translators remember phrasing — she said, *a promise made in peacetime is the only currency that survives the war.*"

"And you gave her your word."

"I gave her my word. And then I disappeared, because disappearing was the only way to keep it. A courier who stays visible is a courier who can be interrogated. Everyone who ever helped me would hang with me. So I buried myself in this town, in this name, in these nets, and I stayed buried, and I told myself the silence was protection." Ivo's hands had closed, without his noticing, into fists on his knees. He opened them deliberately. "It was. And it was cowardice. Both things, the same act. That's the answer to your *why,* Ferrand. A promise, and fifteen years of telling myself that keeping quiet was the same as keeping faith. Whether it was — that's not mine to judge anymore. It may not be mine to fix anymore either. But that's the truth, or close enough to its shape that I won't insult you by polishing it."

The lamplight hissed faintly. Below, the noon bell began to ring, and neither of them moved.

Ferrand looked down at the ledger for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — lower, stripped of the clerk's officialese, carrying something older and wearier.

"My father was a records clerk in the republican customs service," he said. "Third district. When the Empire came, men like him were given a choice: swear, or be catalogued with everyone they'd ever stamped papers for. He swore. They catalogued him anyway, two years later, on the testimony of a courier who was promised immunity in exchange for naming names." His thumb moved along the edge of the ledger page, back and forth, a small unconscious motion, like a man smoothing a wrinkle that wouldn't smooth. "My father always believed the courier lied. That he'd never actually known my father's name, but gave it because they demanded a list and he wanted to live. He died believing it. And I have spent six years in imperial service checking other men's entries, and I have never once found proof either way, because the courier's deposition was sealed, and sealed things stay sealed, and that is the entire architecture of the Empire — sealed rooms, everywhere, with ordinary men inside them."

He looked up.

"Is that why you risked it? Is that the shape? Because I need to know, Joubert, before I do anything at all — whether you're a good man who got trapped, or a careful one who planned this, because those require different handling, and I am about to make a decision that my training tells me is insane."

Ivo held the young man's gaze. Here it was, then — the moment the chapter plan had bent toward all along, the gambit laid open on the table between the brass letter-knives. He could give Ferrand comfort. He could give him the story that let them both sleep. Or he could give him something rarer, which was the unpolished weight of it, and let the weight itself be the argument.

"I'll tell you one more thing," Ivo said. "And then you'll know everything I know, and you can handle me however careful men handle madmen. There is a document in this harbor — one document — that proves things about the old republic's last weeks that certain people on the mainland would burn churches to possess, and other people would pay fortunes for. Proof of exonerations. Proof of testimonies that never arrived — including, if the routing codes mean what I think they mean, a testimony given for a third-district customs clerk." He watched Ferrand go absolutely still. "I have kept it safe for fifteen years. It is the only thing I own that cannot be replaced, and tomorrow night I intend to trade it — not to the tribunal, not to the mainland, but to the one man in Sainte-Marine who reads lies for a living and has discretion over clerical errors."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the noon bell to finish dying away, for the laughter downstairs to resume and stop again, for the light in the tall shuttered windows to shift one degree toward afternoon.

Ferrand closed the ledger. Squared it to the edge of the desk with both hands, precisely, the way a man squares a thing when he needs his hands to be doing something lawful.

"Harbor laborers," he said quietly, not looking up. "Contracted crews for the sailing. It's the one manifest category no one audits twice — the paymaster signs, the bosun counts heads, nobody reads names." He picked up his pen. "Come back at dusk, Joubert. Use the coal cellar stair. And bring whatever it is you think I'll want to see — because if we're going to commit treason together, I insist on reviewing the documentation."

He was already writing, head bowed, the pen moving in small precise strokes, when Ivo let himself out — and it was only on the stairs, passing the soldiers who did not look at him, that Ivo allowed his hands to shake, and understood that the shaking was not fear.

It was the feeling of a man who has been carrying something alone for fifteen years, and has just heard another pair of footsteps fall in beside him.
