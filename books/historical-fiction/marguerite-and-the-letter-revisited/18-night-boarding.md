## 18. Night Boarding

The Corvane slept at her moorings like a beast that had learned not to dream.

Ivo had known warships before — had run dispatches past them in the last spring of the republic, when their searchlights swept the shallows and every fishing boat on the coast learned to be a shadow. The Corvane was nothing so grand. A converted merchantman with gunports that had never fired and a troop deck built for two hundred men who were currently ashore drinking the town's remaining brandy. She rode low and patient at her berth, lanterns burning at bow and stern, and the only sentry worth fearing was the one on the gangway, and Bastien's gossip had already bought his rotation: he would go below for wine at the third bell, and his relief would come late.

Ivo stood in the boathouse at the end of the quay and waited for the third bell with the patience of fifteen years.

It was strange, how quiet the fear was. Ivo had expected it to roar. For nine days it had roared — since the customs house, since Ferrand's summons, since the cipher book had passed from the false bottom of a sea chest into the coat of a young clerk whose hands trembled when he thought no one watched. Nine days of roaring. But now, with the hour finally arrived, the noise had drained out of it entirely, and what remained was something closer to calm. The calm of the last net mended. There is a moment in every long labor when the work stops being frightening because there is no longer time for it to be.

Ferrand's papers lay against Ivo's chest, inside the oilskin jacket, folded twice.

There were three of them. The first was the pilot's contract itself — genuine, signed by the harbor-master after Bastien's dinner, naming *Ivo Joubert* pilot of record for the Corvane's departure on the morning tide. That paper was honest, and its honesty was the strangest thing Ivo owned: a document that said, in imperial ink, *this man belongs aboard*. Fifteen years of documents saying the opposite — *unknown origin*, *no license on file*, *see attached inquiry* — and now, at the end, one that opened doors.

The second was a correction slip, real customs-house stock, watermarked and numbered, bearing Ferrand's careful clerk's hand:

*Correction to Manifest, Corvane, sailing tide of the 14th. Per audit of harbor labor rolls, the following contracted workers were misfiled under detained persons and are hereby transferred to Contracted Harbor Labor, Category C, per Article 61, discretion of auditing clerk: Delac, Solène. Delac, Yvonne (née Aubry). Delac, Tomas.*

Three names. One grandmother, one girl, one cousin dragged off the granary floor with a bruise blooming on his cheekbone. Ferrand had written them at midnight, alone in his office, and had blotted the ink himself so it could not smear, and had said only, when he passed the fold across his desk as if handing over a receipt for lamp oil: "Article 61 gives me discretion over clerical errors. It does not give me courage. Don't waste mine."

And the third paper was the thing that made the whole machine turn: a duplicate page of the passenger manifest, unnumbered, blank in the laborer columns except where Ferrand's hand had entered three lines in advance — *Delac, S.; Delac, Y.; Delac, T. — Category C, quay wages, rations drawn* — ready to be substituted for the original page once the originals were struck. A forgery, but a clerk's forgery, which was the only kind that survived an empire. Soldiers forged with knives. Bureaucracies were undone with paper cut to the exact measure of other paper.

The third bell rang across the water, thin and brass-bright.

Ivo took up the pilot's satchel — charts, tide-tables, a lead line, all of it genuine, all of it dull — and walked out onto the quay.

* * *

Sainte-Marine at night smelled of tar and cold salt and, faintly, of woodsmoke from the garrison's cookfires, and beneath all of it now the smell Ivo had spent nine days unable to stop noticing: wet rope and fear. The town had gone inward. Shutters that had stood open for generations were closed. Old Madame Guérin, who had sold fish on that quay for forty years, had been seen crossing herself when the granary door banged shut behind the Delacs yesterday afternoon. Nobody looked at anybody. The whole harbor had become a congregation of people praying silently to be overlooked.

Ivo had stood in that crowd. Had watched the soldiers take Solène by the arm — she had not struggled; she had gone the way Ivo had taught her, dull and forgettable, and the forgetting had failed anyway because a list had already named her — and had watched Yvonne Delac straighten her back like a woman boarding a coach rather than a prison ship, and had watched Tomas stumble, and had done nothing.

*A pilot cannot be a rescuer.* That was the sentence Ivo had repeated on the walk home, and it had not helped, because it was true in exactly the way arithmetic is true, and arithmetic has never once consoled anyone.

But the promise had not broken. That was the thing Ivo held now, walking the dark quay with the satchel knocking gently against one hip. It had bent. It had bent the way green wood bends, nearly to snapping, and it was still whole, and tonight was the night it either held or shattered forever. Marguerite's letter, read aloud to her daughter in a room that smelled of nets — *tell him the door must stay open even if I am not behind it* — had been spoken into the air eleven days ago and had not yet been kept.

Tonight it would be kept or buried.

The gangway sentry was gone. His post stood empty, lantern swinging, wine already doing its slow work somewhere below decks — Bastien's final gift, purchased with a bottle of the good Margaux he had been saving for fifteen years against some occasion better than this one, though Ivo doubted any occasion could have deserved it more. Ivo crossed the gangway without hurrying. Hurrying was the tell of guilty men, and Ivo Joubert, pilot of record, had nowhere to be except aboard.

A second sentry at the top turned, saw the satchel, saw the contract produced without fumbling, and waved the pilot through with the magnificent incuriosity of men at the end of a watch. "Tide's at first light," he said. "You're early."

"Tide doesn't wait for pilots," Ivo said. "Pilots wait for tides."

The sentry grunted, satisfied that the world still worked, and Ivo went down into the ship.

* * *

Below decks, the Corvane was a country of corridors.

The troop deck came first: hammocks slung in ranks, the smell of soldiers' boots and tallow, everything stowed for sea. Then a bulkhead, then the cargo hold proper, and here the lamplight thinned and the air changed, taking on the particular chill of a space meant for things rather than people — baled canvas, crated shot, the great sealed chests stamped with the customs seal that held the harbor ledger and every municipal record of Sainte-Marine, bound for the mainland and the auditors who would read fifteen years of a town's small kindnesses as a catalog of treasons.

And beyond the crates, behind a temporary partition of stretched canvas and one bored guard who had fallen asleep sitting down, the detained.

Ivo heard them before seeing them. Not voices — voices would have carried — but the smaller sounds. A cough. The shift of a body against planking. And then, cutting through all of it, clear as a gull over flat water, a voice Ivo had taught to be dull and forgettable and which refused, at the last, to manage it.

"—she's seventy-one years old, and if she catches a chill below this deck you can explain to your captain why his hostage-guest died of damp before she was ever worth anything to anyone—"

Solène. Arguing with the guard. Of course she was arguing with the guard. Nine days of lessons in invisibility and in the hour of need she chose visibility, chose it loudly, chose it for her grandmother's sake, and some traitorous part of Ivo wanted to laugh.

The sleeping guard did not wake, which saved everyone a great deal of trouble. Ivo moved along the partition to the gap in the canvas and slipped through.

The holding space was perhaps thirty feet of deck between crates, lit by a single shielded lantern, and it held eleven people. Eleven. Ivo had expected the Delacs and perhaps two others; the list had grown in the last day, as lists do, fed by denunciations and old grudges and one neighbor's fear. A cooper Ivo half-recognized. Two brothers from up the coast. An old schoolmaster, hands folded, eyes open, awake the way the others pretended to sleep — awake the way prisoners learn to be, hearing everything.

Yvonne Delac sat against a crate with her back perfectly straight, silver hair pinned with what Ivo knew to be her last good comb, hands in her lap like a woman at church. She saw Ivo first.

She did not gasp. She did not speak. She simply looked, and her look said: *fifteen years, and this is what my daughter's friend turns out to be — a shape in the dark arriving at the last possible hour.* Then, slowly, she inclined her head, the smallest degree, a queen acknowledging an ambassador from an unfashionable court.

"Madame," Ivo breathed. "Where is—"

"I'm here." Solène came around the crate, and stopped.

For a moment neither of them spoke. In the lantern light her face was thinner than it had been nine days ago, smudged, a scrape along one jaw from the granary floor, and her eyes went over Ivo — the oilskin jacket, the satchel, the pilot's insignia chalked on the collar — reading it all with the quick fluency she had learned too fast.

"You're coming aboard," she whispered. "You're actually—"

"Not staying." Ivo drew her aside, out of earshot of the others, and unfolded the correction slip between two fingers so she could see the names. Watched her read them. Watched her lips move on *Delac, Solène* and understood that until this moment she had not truly believed there would be a paper anywhere in the Empire carrying her name that led anywhere but to a trial.

"Category C," she read. "Contracted harbor laborers."

"No one audits hired hands twice," Ivo said. "Soldiers count rifles and gentlemen count passengers. Men who load ballast are furniture. It's the safest category on any manifest ever drawn."

"Ferrand wrote this?"

"Ferrand wrote this."

She looked up, and for the first time since the granary there was something in her face besides fury and fear — something younger, almost startled, the look of a person handed back a piece of themselves they'd already mourned. "Then we're going with you."

"You're going *ahead* of me. Listen." Ivo kept the voice at the level of the lantern's hum, barely above breath. "In less than an hour the watch changes and the hold gets a head-count before dawn mess. Between now and then, three names have to move from this canvas pen onto the laborer roll, and the only way that happens cleanly is through the purser's office, and the only way we get to the purser's office is as working bodies. So." Ivo glanced at Yvonne, at Tomas slumped against the far crate with his bruised cheek, at the seven strangers watching from the shadows with the terrible hope of people who have learned not to hope. "The others I cannot help. I'm sorry. Three names is all the paper allows, and more would hang Ferrand and us together."

"The schoolmaster knows," Solène said quietly. "He's been counting our whispers all night. He'll keep the guard's attention when the time comes. He told me — he said he taught rhetoric for thirty years and he'd like one final lecture." Her mouth twisted. "He plans to be very boring."

"Bless him, then. Now — Tomas first. Can he walk?"

"He can walk. He's ashamed, which is heavier than the chains would've been, but he can walk."

"Good. Ashamed men keep their heads down." Ivo refolded the slip. "Here is the order of the world for the next hour. We leave this hold in pairs, as laborers summoned for dawn ballast-loading — the duty roster on the bulkhead says six hands called for four bells, and the roster is genuine, because I checked it twice today while surveying the channel. You and your grandmother will be the last pair. If anyone questions you, you say nothing but 'quay wages, rations drawn.' Those five words. Nothing else, no matter what's asked. Can your grandmother do five words?"

"She raised my mother during the occupation," Solène said. "She can do five words standing on her head."

* * *

It went, in the end, almost the way it was planned, which Ivo would remember afterward as the most frightening part — because things that go according to plan feel borrowed, feel like a debt accruing interest somewhere out of sight.

Tomas went first, escorted by Ivo past the sleeping guard, up two ladders, along a passage where a marine snored behind a curtain, into the purser's office — a closet of shelves and ledgers where a boy of seventeen sat yawning over a tally-book, and where the substitution happened in ninety seconds: original page out, duplicate page in, the correction slip laid on top with Ferrand's number on it, and the boy, blinking, checking only the seal and the signature block, because seventeen-year-olds check seals and signatures and nothing else, because nothing else has ever mattered in their short lives.

"*Quay wages,*" the boy read. "Laborers report to the mate, not to me. Next."

Tomas went to the mate. The mate, harried, sleepless, counting crates by lantern-light, ticked three names off his own copy without reading them, because the purser's office had said they existed and the mate's entire philosophy of life was that paperwork descended from above and was not to be questioned.

The cooper tried to follow. Ivo put a hand flat on his chest and felt the man's heart hammering through his shirt, and said, very softly, "Not tonight. Tonight you're furniture. Live to be a man again another season." And the cooper — to his eternal credit, Ivo would think later — nodded once and stepped back into the dark, and began, unprompted, a long and magnificently tedious conversation with the schoolmaster about grain tariffs, pitched precisely loud enough to fill the hold with the sound of harmless men.

Yvonne Delac crossed the gangway leaning on Ivo's arm like any old woman assisted by any polite stranger, and the sentry at the top — the relief, arrived at last, sober and bored — glanced at her, glanced at the manifest the mate thrust at him for the laborer count, found *Delac, Y.*, and returned to contemplating the horizon, which offered no contradictions.

And then Solène.

She came up the ladder with her chin set and her hands empty and her grandmother's comb gleaming in her hair, and she passed within arm's reach of the sentry, and the sentry looked at her the way men look at weather — registering it, finding it unremarkable — and Ivo, standing beside her, felt fifteen years pass out of the body all at once. Every careful alias, every unanswered letter, every morning of becoming a little more invisible so that no ledger would ever find the shape underneath: spent. Spent in one hour, on one canvas partition, on one slip of paper in a boy's neat hand. And it felt not like loss but like setting down a weight so old the shoulders had forgotten their own outline.

At the top of the gangway Solène exhaled, one long shudder of breath, and gripped Ivo's sleeve.

"Now what?" she whispered. "Now we just — sail?"

"Now we stand on this deck," Ivo said, "as the most legitimate people aboard, and we watch the sun come up, and we don't celebrate until the anchor is off the ground. Because—" and here the borrowed feeling returned, the sense of interest accruing, because Ivo had counted heads and done arithmetic even while refusing to, "—the harbor-master cross-examines the pilot at the gangway. It's custom. He'll want my charts, my tide-work, my credentials. And Bastien's contract is real, but Bastien isn't here, and the harbor-master drinks, and drunk men ask the wrong questions at the right times."

Below them, in the grey before first light, the harbor of Sainte-Marine lay still and sleeping, unaware that three of its names had already escaped it. Somewhere on shore, a lamp still burned in the customs house — Ferrand's lamp, Ivo would have bet anything, the lamp of a man sitting up with a cipher book and his father's ghost, making sure the morning's records told exactly the story they needed to tell.

Out past the mole, the sea waited, grey-green and enormous, belonging to no empire at all.

The sky was going pale at the edges. On the quay, small and unmistakable even at that distance, a familiar figure in a harbor-master's coat was walking toward the gangway with a bottle-shaped bulge in one pocket and a ledger under his arm, moving with the deliberate, dangerous steadiness of a man who had decided to be sober at exactly the wrong hour.

"That," Ivo murmured, "is going to be a problem."
