## 19. The Morning Tide

The tide would turn at half past five, and Ivo had not slept.

He had tried — lying dressed on his bunk in the pilot's quarters above the customs house, listening to the Corvane's anchor chain groan against the ebb, counting the hours the way a condemned man counts stairs. Sleep came in shallow draughts and left him each time with the same image: Solène's face in the hold, lit by a single lantern, watching him move her grandmother's name from one column to another as though names were cargo that could be reweighed. *Contracted harbor laborers, four.* The ink had still been wet when he'd blotted it. Ferrand's correction slip had made it legal. Nothing about it had made it true.

At four o'clock he gave up on sleep and went down to the water.

Sainte-Marine in the dark was a town he knew by touch more than sight: the cobbles slicked with spray, the tarred rope of the mooring posts, the smell of weed and cold iron that rose off the harbor at low tide. Fifteen years of mornings had taught him this hour the way other men learned prayers. But tonight every familiar thing had been rearranged. Lanterns burned where lanterns never burned. Soldiers stood where fishermen stood. And out in the roadstead, black against the last of the night, the Corvane rode at her anchors with her running lights lit — a troopship of the Empire, two hundred tons of gray oak and gunmetal, taking on water for the mainland.

She would carry troops. She would carry records. And somewhere below her waterline, in a hold that smelled of bilge and tallow, three people who should have been prisoners were sleeping under false names because an old man's hands had not shaken quite enough to ruin a forgery.

Ivo stood at the harbor steps and let himself feel it — just for a moment, just once — the full weight of what the morning required. Then he put it away, the way he'd put away everything else for fifteen years, and went to find his boat.

* * *

The pilot gig was Bastien's, lent for the occasion with a solemnity neither of them had acknowledged aloud. Ivo checked her lines, her oars, the little lead of sounding line coiled in the bow. His hands knew the work and did it without him, which was a mercy, because the rest of him kept drifting back to the gangway — to the plank that ran from the Corvane's entry port down to a barge, and to the man who would be standing at the top of it with a ledger open and a pen behind his ear.

Harbor-Master Quenel. A broad, pink-faced man with a voice like a foghorn run on cheap brandy, who had shaken Ivo's hand at the contract signing and said, *Glad to have a steady man for the tide-work, Joubert. These army pilots don't know our shoals from their own backsides.* Quenel liked competence. Quenel also liked order, and order, this morning, meant papers verified against lists, and lists verified against ledgers, and discrepancies forwarded to the mainland for trial.

Discrepancies. The word sat in Ivo's chest like a swallowed hook.

He rowed out alone in the gray before dawn, the town falling away behind him, and came up under the Corvane's flank so close he could hear the ship breathe — the tick of her timbers working, the gurgle at her scuppers. A sentry leaned over the rail above him, musket slung, face young and bored.

"Pilot for the tide," Ivo called up. "Joubert."

The sentry squinted at the lightening east, then jerked his thumb toward the entry port aft. "Gangway's on the barge side. Papers ready."

Papers ready. Ivo tied off the gig and climbed, and the plank swayed under him the way planks always sway, but this morning it felt less like a gangway than a gallows ladder, and he climbed it anyway, because there was no other way across the water, and everything he loved was already aboard.

* * *

Quenel was not at the gangway.

For one wild moment Ivo thought the morning might simply pass over him like weather. He presented his contract to the junior officer at the head of the plank — a lieutenant with chapped hands and the permanent frown of a man responsible for things he didn't understand — and was waved through onto the deck, into the organized chaos of departure. Soldiers stacking kit. Stevedores hauling the last crates of records, each stenciled ARCHIVES — VALCOURT DISTRICT, each one a small coffin for somebody's old life. The Delacs' names were in those crates. So was his own. *Ivo Joubert, fisherman, arrived fifteen years past, origin unknown.* Somewhere in that paper mountain was the sentence that could hang him, being carried aboard by men who thought they were carrying paperwork.

He had almost reached the pilothouse when the voice found him.

"Joubert! There you are."

Quenel came along the deck with a mug in one hand and a sheaf of documents in the other, and Ivo felt the bottom drop out of the morning. The harbor-master's face was cheerful. That was worse than suspicion. Cheerful men asked questions out of friendliness, and friendly questions could not be refused.

"Tide turns in forty minutes," Quenel said. "You'll take her out past the Middle Shoal on the flood, then hand her to the sea pilot at the buoy?" He didn't wait for the answer; he knew the answer; he'd written the answer into the contract himself. Instead he flipped through his sheaf, licked his thumb, and held up a page. "But first. New orders from the district office. Passenger verification." He smiled apologetically. "You know how it is. Somebody upstairs discovered that ships have people on them."

"I'm crew," Ivo said. "Contract pilot. It's all there."

"It is, it is." Quenel found the page he wanted and held it out. "Sign here. Acknowledgment of articles. Standard form." And then, casually, terribly, while Ivo took the pen: "Funny thing, though. My clerk pulled your file this morning. Routine." He watched Ivo sign. "Says here you've been in Sainte-Marine fifteen years and there's no fishing license on record before year six. Odd, that. Licenses go back further than that. Bastien Arnaud has one from year two, and Bastien couldn't spell 'herring' without help."

The pen scratched. Ivo finished the signature with a steady hand and looked up, and gave Quenel the face he had spent fifteen years perfecting — the face of a man mildly puzzled by paperwork, a man whose whole soul was occupied by tide-tables and nothing else.

"Year six," Ivo said. "That sounds right. I came after the troubles. There wasn't much licensing done during the war, was there? Half the harbor office was burned, I heard."

"Was it?" Quenel frowned. "I'd have said the harbor office survived the war better than most things."

"Maybe I'm thinking of the granary." Ivo shrugged, a fisherman's shrug, patient with the stupidity of clerks. "Everything burned in those years. Ask anyone."

And that was when Bastien arrived.

Ivo heard him before he saw him — the particular rolling gait, the wheeze of a man who had drunk steadily since sundown and eaten nothing since noon. Bastien came up the gangway barge-side with a crate of wine on his shoulder — a gift for the officers, nominally; a passport for himself, actually — and set it down at the lieutenant's feet with a flourish, and then his eye found Ivo and Quenel, and Ivo watched him understand the shape of the conversation from ten feet away, the way old sailors read weather from ten miles off.

"Harbor-master!" Bastien boomed. "You've found my pilot, then. Good. Good man, that. Best hands on this coast." He wedged himself between them with the unapologetic bulk of long friendship. "Ivo Joubert. Known him — what is it now, Ivo? Since before the war?"

"Since year six," Ivo said quietly. A warning. *Don't embellish.*

"Year six, he says." Bastien laughed, too loud, and clapped Quenel on the shoulder. "Harbor-master, let me tell you something about year six. Year six, this coast was nothing but wrecks and rumors. Every boat that put out was suspected of carrying republican dispatches, and every boat that stayed in was suspected of hiding the men who had. Nobody wanted pilots. Nobody wanted anything but to be left alone." He leaned in, confiding, breath thick with the evening's brandy. "Except the fishing fleet still had to feed the town. And there was exactly one man willing to take the Middle Shoal channel in the dark, every night, no questions asked, no questions answered. That was your Joubert. That's why there's no license from those years — because the man was doing the work nobody official would sign his name to. I vouch for him. Put my own name under his if you like. I'll sign anything you want, right now, drunk as I am, because drunk is when a man tells the truth."

It was a lie. Most of it. Ivo had come to Sainte-Marine in year six, yes, but no one had trusted him with the shoal channel for years afterward; trust had been earned slowly, invisibly, the way everything in his life had been earned. But it was a lie built on true bones, and it was delivered with such magnificent, drunken sincerity that Quenel blinked twice and looked from Bastien to Ivo and back.

"You'd sign that?" Quenel said. "Your name, under his?"

"Happily. Hang me with him if he turns out a spy." Bastien spread his arms wide, offering his chest to the muskets, grinning. "Though between us, harbor-master, if Ivo Joubert is a spy, then I'm the Empress's cousin, and you can arrest us both and explain to the fleet why their best pilot swung from a yardarm the week the records sailed."

A silence. Somewhere aft, soldiers cursed a stuck winch. The tide, invisible beneath them, had begun to turn; Ivo could feel it in the way the ship's cable took up its strain, a faint new tension running through the hull like a pulse quickening.

Quenel snorted. "Nobody's hanging anybody." He tucked his papers away. "Get your signatures, get your wine off my deck, and get this ship moving. I want her past the point before the ebb makes fools of us all." He turned to go, then paused, and looked back at Ivo with something almost like kindness. "Year six," he said. "Hard years. We all did what we had to do to stay afloat. Just make sure the rest of your file stays as boring as your face, Joubert."

"Aye," Ivo said. "Boring as Sunday."

Quenel went. Bastien exhaled the length of his whole body, sagging against the wine crate as though his bones had been briefly borrowed by a younger man.

"Don't ever ask me to do that again," he said.

"I won't."

"Liar." Bastien wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. His eyes, when they met Ivo's, were not drunk at all. "They're aboard, then? All three?"

"All three. Hold berth fourteen, under laborer articles."

"Then get them off this ship alive, old friend, and I'll forgive you for every secret you ever kept from me." Bastien picked up his crate again, hoisted it, and added, softer, almost lost under the winch noise: "Ferrand came down at first light. Before anyone else. Went through the record crates with a lamp and a knife, quiet as a thief. Took out maybe six pages, put in six others. I saw him do it and I looked at the sky and saw nothing at all."

Ivo closed his eyes for half a second. Six pages. The Delac entries. His own arrival date. Whatever else Ferrand had judged worth saving. Misfiled — or rather, filed correctly into some drawer where no auditor would look for twenty years, which in the Empire's arithmetic amounted to the same mercy.

"He's a good clerk," Ivo said.

"He's a dead man walking if anyone counts those crates twice," Bastien said. "So pray no one does. Now go. Take my ship out, Joubert. Take her out clean."

* * *

The Corvane weighed anchor at half past five.

Ivo stood at the pilothouse rail with the sea pilot's mate beside him and gave his orders in the flat, unhurried voice of a man who had done this a thousand times, because the only way through the next hour was to actually be the thing his papers claimed. *Hands to the capstan. Headsail sheets. Ease her to starboard, we'll swing on the flood.* The ship answered him the way big ships answer, slowly, reluctantly, like a great animal deciding to humor a small request, and Sainte-Marine began to slide past her flank — the customs house with its new flag, the granary with its broken door, the crooked street where a girl had once asked him where he'd learned to read tide-tables and he had lied to her gently, the way he lied to everyone, the way lying had become the native language of his survival.

Solène appeared at the rail below him.

She wore a stevedore's jacket two sizes too large and a cap pulled low, and she stood among the contracted laborers with her hands folded and her eyes down, dull as dishwater, forgettable as furniture — and she was better at it than he had ever been, he thought, better than he had ever been at anything. Only when her gaze lifted, briefly, and found his at the rail above, did the mask slip, and what showed underneath was not fear. It was something harder and brighter than fear. She nodded once, a small precise motion, and looked down again, and was a laborer again, and Ivo's chest hurt with something that had no honest name — terror and triumph indistinguishable, braided together so tightly he could not have said which one was making his hands tremble on the rail.

Her grandmother did not appear. Old Madame Delac stayed below, as arranged, small and silent in the shadow of the hold. But Ivo knew she was there the way he knew the tide was turning — not by seeing, but by feeling the whole world strain in one direction.

The Middle Shoal came up on the port bow, a brown smudge of broken water in the early light. Ivo conned her past it by heart, calling his soundings, feeling the keel thread the deep lane he had known since year six — the channel no official would sign his name to, the channel that had been his first and longest act of loyalty to this coast. Behind them the harbor fell away. The customs house shrank to a toy. On the quay, small as a painted figure, a man in a clerk's dark coat stood watching the ship go, one hand raised — not waving, Ivo decided. Saluting. The way one clerk salutes another across an ocean of paperwork.

Then the point was abeam, and the open water took them, gray-green and endless, and the Corvane settled into her stride with a long contented creak, and Ivo realized his knees were shaking and locked them, and realized he was breathing and made himself stop counting the breaths.

Below his feet, in berth fourteen, three people with struck names slept or sat or stared at the hull planking, carried out of the Empire's reach on forged articles and a drunk man's oath and a young clerk's quiet knife. The ledger that could hang them lay somewhere in the crates aft, its relevant pages wearing other pages' clothes. The cipher book — the last proof of his own innocence — was gone from his life forever, traded in the dark for this morning, and he found he could not make himself regret it even slightly.

The sea pilot's mate touched his sleeve. "Middle buoy ahead, sir. Sea pilot's boat putting out."

"I see it." Ivo looked at the buoy, at the waiting boat, at the mainland haze beyond it where trials waited for discrepancies and the Empire kept its accounts. They were not safe. Not yet, perhaps not for a long time. Stateless, nameless, bound for a neutral port on papers that would not survive a careful reading.

But the wind was fair, and the tide was theirs, and for the first time in fifteen years, Ivo Joubert was not sailing alone, and not sailing away from anyone either.

"Steady as she goes," he said, and the Corvane ran on toward open water, carrying his whole stolen family into the morning.
