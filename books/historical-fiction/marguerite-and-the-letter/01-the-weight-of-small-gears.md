## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The watch lay open on the bench like a small drowned thing, its heart exposed, and Silas Pemberton bent over it in the grey light that came through the workshop's single window — a window so furred with grime and the dust of ten thousand filings that the morning arrived filtered, apologetic, as though even daylight had to be admitted through Whitcombe's strict economy.

He worked with a loupe screwed into his eye socket, his breath held in the way his master had taught him seven years ago: *You do not breathe on the work. The breath carries moisture, and moisture is the enemy of all things fine.* He was nineteen years old and his hands were steady — steadier than Whitcombe's now, though he would have died before saying it aloud. In his left hand, tweezers. In his right, a file no longer than a thumbnail. Between them, the balance staff of a merchant's watch, snapped clean by some fat-fingered apprentice in a counting house up Cheapside who had tried to force the crown when it resisted.

The merchant's name was Tallow. Mr. Josiah Tallow, importer of tallow and tallow-adjacent goods, a man whose girth suggested he consumed a respectable portion of his own inventory. He had brought the watch in three days ago with the aggrieved air of one who has been personally wronged by the universe.

"Three generations," Tallow had said, laying it on the counter as if setting down an infant at a foundling hospital. "My grandfather's watch. And your master will understand — I expect *perfection*. I expect it *quickly*."

Whitcombe had bowed the way he always bowed to men like Tallow — a shallow, precise inclination that gave away nothing and promised everything — and had said, "It will keep better time than it ever has, sir," which was not a boast but a threat delivered softly, and Silas had loved him for it.

Now the morning wore on, and the balance staff took shape under the file, brass turning to brass perfection by increments finer than thought. Outside, London went about its business. A cart rattled past the window; somewhere down the lane a woman was arguing with a fishmonger about the freshness of whiting, her voice rising and falling with the metronomic indignation of the truly certain. A church bell struck nine — St. Sepulchre's, a quarter mile off and four minutes slow by Silas's private reckoning, because everything in the world was either fast or slow and only the things he made himself kept honest time.

*History happens elsewhere,* he thought, not for the first time, and the thought arrived with the comfortable weight of an old coat. It happened in Paris, where they were cutting off heads with machines invented, someone said, by a doctor with a genius for mechanics — a guillotine, they called it, a machine for making time stop permanently, and Silas had felt a professional horror at the description when he'd read it in the newspaper: the blade dropped on a simple gravity escapement, no recoil, no refinement, just weight doing murder's arithmetic. It happened in the sugar islands, where the slaves were in revolt. It happened in the House of Commons, in the courts at the Old Bailey, in the cabinets of ministers whose names he knew only as lines of print. History was a great clock that ran in other rooms, audible sometimes as a distant striking, and here in this workshop there were only small gears, small debts, small mercies, and the enormous daily labor of keeping a business alive one hairspring at a time.

He did not know — could not have known — that history was at that moment walking toward the workshop on two legs, wearing a hood, carrying a child and a letter. But that knowledge belonged to the night, and this was still morning, and mornings in the workshop had their own liturgy.

At half past nine the door opened — no knock, because customers knocked but family entered — and Ezekiel Whitcombe came in from the back room, wiping his hands on a leather apron gone black with age and oil. He was sixty-one years old and built like something meant to bear load: barrel-chested, thick through the wrist, with a face that seemed assembled from spare parts of more handsome men and a voice pitched low, always low, as though the whole world were a sleeping child he did not wish to wake. He had taken Silas in when Silas was nine years old, when Silas's father vanished into the night leaving nothing but debt and disgrace and a boy who did not understand why the neighbors' faces had changed toward him. Whitcombe had never once spoken of Delacroix in all the years since, and this silence was the largest single fact of Silas's life, larger than any affection, larger than any word.

"How goes the Tallow?" Whitcombe asked, peering at the movement without touching it, hands clasped behind his back in the posture of a man who has learned that the surest way to ruin an apprentice's work is to hover over it with fingers.

"It goes. The staff is true within two tenths." Two tenths of what, he did not say; between them, after seven years, units were unnecessary. "I'll cut the pivot tonight and she'll run by Thursday."

"She'll run tomorrow," Whitcombe said, "because Tallow pays on collection and we need the eleven shillings before Thursday." He said it without drama, the way he said everything, and then he stood looking out the fouled window at the lane for a long moment, and Silas felt the change in the room before he understood it — the way one feels a change in barometric pressure before the weather arrives.

"Master?"

Whitcombe turned. He had a letter in his hand — a real letter, on good paper, the kind of paper that announced its own expense the way a bishop announces his office — and he looked at it the way a man looks at a hand of cards that will either make him or beggar him.

"The Royal Observatory commission," he said. "Greenwich."

Silas set down his tweezers very carefully, as though sudden movement might dislodge the words from the air and scatter them. "The observatory."

"The Astronomer Royal wants a new master regulator for the transit room. Their old one — a Graham, mind you, a Graham! — has grown erratic these five years past. Losing seconds, gaining minutes, sulking like a widow. They've had three men look at it and none could cure it." Whitcombe's voice remained level, but Silas could hear beneath it a vibration like a spring wound nearly to breaking. "Quill — the first astronomer, a Mr. Quill, who does their rating and comparisons — Quill saw a regulator of ours. Years ago. The one I made for Alderman Prewitt, do you remember? Before your time on the bench. He never forgot it. He put our name forward."

"Our name." Silas tasted the plural and found it sweet and terrifying. "The observatory would take work from *this shop*."

"The observatory would take work from this shop," Whitcombe confirmed, "if we can convince them we are equal to it, which brings me to the difficult portion of the sentence." He laid the letter on the bench beside the open watch, deliberately away from the filings, and smoothed it once with a broad thumb. "There are others proposed. Mudge. Arnold's people. Men with reputations like cathedrals. We have a reputation like — " he gestured around the cramped workshop, at the lathe worn shiny with use, at the drawers of wheels and pinions, at the narrow stair to the sleeping loft where Silas had climbed every night for a decade — "like this room."

"And if we fail—"

"If we fail, everyone will know we failed, and no one will bring us anything again but broken watches and pity." Whitcombe said it plainly, without self-pity, the way he might report the price of brass. Then something moved across his face — not softness exactly, Whitcombe did not waste motion on softness, but the next thing to it, the shadow softness casts. "If we succeed, boy, we eat like craftsmen instead of beggars. You'll have your own bench. Your own name over the door eventually, if you want it, and you want it, don't pretend otherwise, I've watched you look at the blank space above mine these three years."

Silas had not known he was so legible. He looked down at the watch, at the tiny balance wheel poised on its jeweled bearing, oscillating with its patient tick-tick-tick that measured out nothing and everything.

"I want it," he said quietly. "More than I've wanted anything."

"I know." Whitcombe picked the letter back up, folded it along its crease with the exactness of a man who folded everything — letters, aprons, grievances, hopes — into the smallest possible compass. "They'll send for us within the fortnight. The clock is stripped, they say. Escapement removed entirely. Whoever takes the work must rebuild it from the plates up and make it answer to the stars themselves. Stars, Silas. Time kept against the heavens." He shook his head slowly. "In forty years at the bench I have made clocks that pleased aldermen and clocks that lied to their owners and one clock, one, that I believe told the truth. This may be my second. Ours, I mean." He corrected himself as though the word had cost him something. "Ours."

He went back through to the front of the shop then, and Silas heard him begin the small sounds of opening up: shutters, ledger, inkpot. Ordinary sounds. Sounds that had framed every morning of Silas's remembered life.

He returned to the balance staff. The file whispered. Tick, went the watch's train, released tooth by tooth by the pallets, and outside the fishwife had won her argument or lost it, and somewhere in France a blade fell on somebody's neck, and none of it — not one atom of it — touched the workshop yet.

But late that night, long after Whitcombe had climbed to his rooms above the chandlery next door, long after the candle had burned down to a thumb's width and Silas sat alone finishing Tallow's pivot by feel more than sight, he heard it: footsteps in the lane, stopping. A pause outside the door too long to be accidental, too quiet to be innocent.

And then — faintly, unmistakably — a child crying.

* * *

He told himself it was a cat. He told himself this while blowing out the candle, while standing motionless in the dark workshop listening, while crossing the floor on feet that had decided independently of his reason. Cats did not cry in notes. Cats did not fall silent, as this sound fell silent, the moment his shadow crossed the frosted pane of the door.

Through the glass, distorted by the old panes, he could see the shape of a figure in the doorway — hooded, slight, a woman by the set of the shoulders — and beside her something smaller, shifting, wrapped against the cold.

Silas stood with his hand on the latch and did not lift it.

Later — much later, years later — he would understand that this was the hinge on which his whole life swung, this one unremarkable instant: a tired apprentice, a cold doorstep, a latch under his palm. Every grand machinery of fate requires a small gear to engage it, and the gear, in this case, was his own unwilling hand.

He lifted the latch.

Cold rushed in, and lamplight from a street lantern two doors down fell across the step, and the hooded woman turned — only her chin visible below the wool, a pale sliver of a face, young, exhausted, frightened in a way that went deeper than this night, a fear worn smooth like a stone carried too many miles.

"Are you Pemberton?" she asked, and her voice caught strangely on the name — *Pemberton*, not *Delacroix*, the name his father had buried and Whitcombe had given him, and how could a stranger know even that?

"No—" he began, because strictly speaking—

"You're his son." It was not a question. She pressed something into his hands: heavy paper, sealed, a letter. And behind her, half-hidden in the folds of her cloak, a girl — seven years old perhaps, eight, with enormous dark eyes and her mother's carved-out exhaustion already beginning in her small face. "Take her. Take the letter. Keep them both, and whatever comes—" The woman glanced over her shoulder down the empty lane, and every muscle in her body pulled toward flight. "Whatever comes, forget my face. Do you understand? Whatever they ask, whoever comes — you never saw a face."

"Who are—"

Boots. Down the lane, unhurried, deliberate — the boots of men walking with purpose, and the woman went rigid as a struck bell.

"Forget my face," she breathed, and pushed the girl across the threshold into Silas's arms, and the child's weight landed against his chest — warm, trembling, real, heavier than any balance wheel, heavier than any clock he would ever build — and the mother turned and was gone into the dark between the lanterns, quick and silent as water finding its level.

Silas stood in his own doorway holding a child and a sealed letter, watching the boots come on down the lane, hearing them slow, hearing them pass — so close he could smell wet wool and pipe smoke — hearing them continue on into the night without stopping.

The girl looked up at him. She did not cry. Whatever crying she had left, she had spent it on the step.

"They said to forget her face," the child whispered, in a small careful voice, as though reporting an instruction she'd been given and intended to follow. "But I don't think I can."

Neither, Silas discovered, could he.

He drew her inside and closed the door on the night, and above the bench the unfinished watch lay open among its gears, ticking on in the dark, keeping its small honest count of the seconds — indifferent, precise, and utterly unaware that the history of the Pemberton family had just walked through the door.
