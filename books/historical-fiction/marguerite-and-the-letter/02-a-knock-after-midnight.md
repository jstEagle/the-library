## 2. A Knock After Midnight

The knock came at the hour when honest men are asleep and guilty ones pretend to be.

Silas heard it first as a sound in his dream — three soft raps, like a knuckle tapping the inside of a watch case — and woke with the conviction that something in the workshop below had slipped a tooth. He lay a moment in the narrow room above Whitcombe's shop, listening to the rain work at the shutters, telling himself it had been only the settling of the house. Old buildings spoke at night. They creaked and sighed and knocked their timbers together like old men's knees, and a boy who jumped at every one of them would never keep his wits long enough to finish an escapement.

Then the knock came again. Three raps. Deliberate. Soft as a held breath.

He was out of bed before he had decided to be, feet finding the cold boards, hand finding the candle and flint on the shelf by habit rather than thought. The flame caught, guttered, steadied. In its small circle of light his own face looked back at him from the black glass of the window — a thin young man of nineteen with ink-dark hair pressed flat on one side and eyes that were too old for him, everyone said so, too old and too careful. He did not look like a boy who had been asleep. He looked like a boy who had been waiting.

Below him, through the floorboards, he could hear Mr. Whitcombe turn over in his room across the landing, the bedsprings groaning, then silence. The old man slept like a man keeping accounts even in dreams; he would not wake for a knock unless it grew insistent. Silas stood a moment longer, weighing it. A knock after midnight in the year 1793 was not a thing to be answered quickly. There were men who knocked softly because they meant no harm, and men who knocked softly so as not to wake the neighbors, and the two kinds sounded exactly alike until the door opened.

He took the candle and went down.

* * *

The workshop at night was a different country. By day it was crowded, warm, alive with the small industry of six benches and the smell of oil and brass and Whitcombe's pipe smoke; by night it became what Silas privately loved best about it — a still life of itself, the tools laid in their ranks, the half-finished movements sleeping under their cloths, the great regulator clock in the corner ticking with the patience of a heart that had never once considered stopping. He knew every inch of it in the dark without needing the candle: the third floorboard from the stair, which complained; the bench where the merchant's watch from Tuesday sat waiting for its balance spring; the window at the front, whose latch stuck and had stuck for four years, because Whitcombe believed a lock that worked was an invitation to try it, whereas a lock that fought you discouraged ambition.

Silas set the candle on that bench and put his eye to the gap in the shutters.

Rain silvered the street. The lamp at the corner had gone out — it always went out past midnight, the lamplighter being a man of firm principles regarding municipal expense — and beyond its dead iron post the world dissolved into wet grey dark. At first Silas saw nothing. Then, directly beneath the window, close enough that he might have touched her had the pane been open, a figure detached itself from the doorway's shadow.

A woman. Hooded, the cloak dark and heavy with rain, standing very straight in the way people stand when they have been crying and have made an arrangement with themselves about it. She did not knock again. She simply stood, and the rain fell off the edge of her hood in a thin bright thread, and Silas understood with a lurch that she had already knocked — that the knocks which woke him had come minutes ago, and she had waited through them without moving, as though she expected no answer and wanted none, as though the knocking had been for form's sake, or courage's sake, or some other sake he could not name.

She turned her head slightly, and the hood tilted toward the door.

And beside her, half-hidden in the fold of her cloak, stood a child.

Silas's breath fogged the shutter crack. A girl — small, seven years old perhaps, though it was hard to tell in the dark — clutching the woman's skirt with one hand and something pale against her chest with the other. A bundle. A letter? The woman bent and said something into the child's ear, too low for the rain to let through, and the child shook her head, and the woman said it again, more firmly, and this time the child went still in the particular way of children who have been told something they will spend their whole lives trying not to understand.

Then the woman lifted the latch-stub outside the door — Whitcombe's stubborn latch, which could be worked from without if one knew the trick of it, a fact Silas had pointed out to him twice and been ignored twice — and pushed the door open onto darkness, and stepped back, and did not go in.

"Go," Silas heard her say. Not a whisper. Something between a whisper and a command, worn smooth by repetition, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed this on a long road. "Go inside and stand where I showed you. Do not cry out. Do you hear me? Whatever happens, do not cry out."

"I want to come with you." The child's voice was small and level, and it went through Silas like a needle through silk. "Mama—"

"You are coming with me." The woman crouched, and for one moment the hood fell back enough that the corner lamp's faint grey light found her face — young, sharp-boned, exhausted, beautiful in the way of things about to break. Her eyes came up, quite suddenly, directly to the shutter crack, directly to Silas's eye, as though she had known all along that someone watched, and she said — clearly, distinctly, in a voice that carried across the wet street and through the gap in the boards and lodged somewhere beneath his ribs:

"Forget my face."

She pulled the hood down over it. And then she was gone — not running, nothing so loud as running, but walking fast into the dark with the rain closing behind her like water behind a stone, and within ten breaths there was nothing in the street but the dead lamp and the drumming eaves.

The child stood in the open doorway, a small pale shape against deeper dark, and did not move.

* * *

For a long moment — he would remember afterward how long, because he counted it against the regulator's tick, forty ticks, fifty — Silas did nothing at all. This was the shameful arithmetic of that night and he never once managed to falsify it in memory: fifty beats of a master clock while a child stood dripping in a doorway, and Silas Pemberton stood behind a bench doing sums. If he opened the door he was responsible. If he woke Whitcombe he explained everything. If he sent her away — where? Into what night, after what mother, toward what boots that might be following?

Because that was the thought that kept him nailed to the floor. *Boots.* The woman had walked like a person being followed, or like a person who had stopped being followed only recently and did not trust the silence. Nobody left a child on a stranger's doorstep at midnight in the rain unless staying was worse than leaving, and whatever she was fleeing did not stop existing merely because it had lost sight of her.

Forty more ticks.

Then the child sneezed — a small, furious, compressed sneeze, the sneeze of someone determined not to be an inconvenience — and Silas moved.

He crossed the floor, took up the candle, and opened the door. The cold came in with the smell of wet wool and night air and, faintly, woodsmoke from a chimney somewhere up the hill. The girl looked up at him. She was smaller than he'd guessed — seven at most, thin, with dark hair plastered to her forehead and enormous grave eyes that examined him with an unhurried thoroughness no adult ever employed. She was holding a letter wrapped in oilcloth against her chest with both arms now, the way other children held dolls.

"You're not Mama," she said.

"No," said Silas.

"She said to wait inside."

"Yes."

"Are you inside?"

It took Silas a moment to understand the question, and when he did, something in his chest turned over. It was, he would think much later, the first genuinely difficult problem anyone had ever put to him — harder than any broken train of wheels, because it admitted no clean repair. *Are you inside?* Was he? Was this house hers now, or his, or nobody's? Did a doorstep count as inside? Did a country?

"You'd better come in," he said, "before we both catch our deaths arguing about it."

She considered him. Rain dripped off the hem of her cloak and pooled around her shoes. Then she stepped past him into the workshop with the grave self-possession of a duchess entering a ballroom, walked precisely three paces in, stopped, and stood there, dripping, hugging the letter, looking at the benches and the tools and the ticking regulator with an expression he could not read at all.

Silas shut the door. Shot the bolt. Stood with his hand on it a moment, feeling the wood press cool against his palm, aware that he had just done something irreversible — that doors, once bolted from within, make declarations.

"What's your name?" he asked.

She didn't answer. Perhaps she hadn't been instructed to. She only looked at him, and shivered once, violently, head to foot, the shiver of a small body that had been standing in the rain far longer than the few minutes Silas had watched it.

He fetched the blanket off the settle by the cold stove and put it around her shoulders without asking leave, the way you'd wrap a movement in cloth to keep off the dust, and she let him, and the letter stayed clutched outside the blanket, dry under her arms, as though it mattered more than warmth. Which, Silas was beginning to suspect, it did.

* * *

Boots came at half past one.

He heard them from the top of the stairs, where he had installed the girl in Whitcombe's own chair by the dead stove with instructions to be a piece of furniture, and where he himself stood with the candle pinched out and his heart going like a runaway flywheel. Boots — several pairs, unhurried, heavy-nailed, the tread of men who expected the street to belong to them. They came up from the river end, passed the workshop, slowed —

Slowed. Not stopped. Slowed, the way a hand slows over goods it is pricing. Silas could hear the difference between men passing and men considering, and these men were considering. One of them said something low, and another laughed without humor, and there came the scrape of a boot toe testing the step of the door, once, idly, the way a man kicks a barrel to see if it's empty.

Inside the workshop, in the dark, the girl did not cry out. She had been told not to, and she did not, and Silas — crouched at the stair-head with his hand pressed flat against his own sternum as though he could hold his heart down by force — thought: *she has done this before. She knows how to be quiet. Someone has taught a seven-year-old how to be quiet in the dark.*

The boots moved on. Down the lane, around the corner by the chandler's, fading into rain-noise. Gone, or pretending to be gone. Silas waited a hundred ticks of the regulator before he allowed himself to breathe fully, and even then it felt borrowed.

When he came back into the main room, the girl was watching him from the blanket. In the grey light through the shutters her face was pale and composed, and her eyes followed him with that same unnerving gravity.

"They've gone," he said.

"I know," she said. "They went past before. When Mama was still here. They went past and Mama said, *now*, and that's when she knocked."

Silas sat down slowly on the edge of the bench, among the scattered tools of the merchant's watch, and looked at her. So the knocking had not been for courage. It had been timed — measured against the patrol's circuit, delivered in the gap between passings, with a precision that made something in his craftsman's mind sit up and take unwilling notice. The woman had planned this the way he planned a repair: observing, waiting for the mechanism's rhythm, acting in the space where the gears cleared.

Which raised the question he had been declining to ask since the moment the door closed.

"The letter," he said. "What's in the letter?"

"It's for whoever opens the door." She recited it like catechism, word-perfect, worn smooth: "*Give it to whoever opens the door. They will know what must be done. Forget my face, Marguerite. Forgetting me keeps you safe. Being forgotten is being safe.*"

Her voice did not break on the last words. That was somehow worse.

Silas looked at the oilcloth packet in her lap. Sealed, he could see now — a wafer seal, pressed with a signet he couldn't make out in the dark. A letter that named its reader in advance. A letter written by a woman who knew she would not be there when it was opened, and who had entrusted it to chance and a doorstep and the first fool to come down the stairs.

He should wake Whitcombe. That was the correct procedure, the procedure of an apprentice with nothing to hide and nothing to lose: wake the master, tell all, share the weight. But even as he formed the thought, he saw its underside — Whitcombe's ledger, the numbers written in red that Silas was not supposed to have seen; the observatory commission that was to save them or bury them; the old man's terror of anything, *anything*, that smelled of the law's attention in this year of all years. A foundling and a sealed letter at midnight was not a burden to lay on a drowning man.

Besides — and this thought arrived quietly, without permission, and refused to leave — besides, the letter was addressed to *whoever opened the door*. Whoever opened the door was him. He had opened the door. Whatever must be done, apparently, fell to him, and it seemed cowardly to hand the duty sideways to a sleeping old man along with the risk.

"Marguerite," he said, trying the name. It sounded foreign in the workshop, among the English tools and English ticking — a French name, a name from the other side of the water, from the world of printers and pamphlets and heads in baskets that lived in the gazettes and had never once, in nineteen years, touched the inside of this shop. History happened elsewhere. History happened to other people.

"Yes?" said Marguerite.

"You'll stay tonight. Tomorrow—" Tomorrow. What was tomorrow? He found he could not see past it. "Tomorrow I'll think what's to be done."

She nodded, unsurprised, ungrateful, accepting the arrangement as though it were weather. Then she curled up in Whitcombe's great chair, blanket and all, letter hugged to her chest, and closed her eyes with the instantaneous completeness of childhood, and within minutes her breathing had gone slow and even, and the only sounds in the workshop were the rain and the regulator and the small whistle of her nose.

Silas did not sleep. He sat on his stool at the bench with the merchant's watch open before him and no memory of picking up a single tool, watching the grey strengthen by degrees toward morning, and turning the sealed packet over and over in his hands.

Once — just once, when the light was nearly come — he held it up near the shutter crack and tried to read the impression the signet had left in the wafer. It was a device of some kind. Letters, perhaps, or a mark. The wax was poor quality, the impression shallow, and he could make out almost nothing.

Almost nothing. Except that the little he could make out — the angle of a stroke, the spacing of what might be letters — snagged on something at the back of his mind, the way a burr snags a fingertip. Something familiar. Something he had seen before, years ago, in another life, in connection with another name — a name spoken in this very room by a younger Whitcombe, late one night, over a bottle, and never spoken again.

He could not retrieve it. It sat just below the surface of memory like a gear sunk in grease, visible, unreachable.

Outside, the rain thinned. Somewhere up the lane a cart rattled, and a cock crowed, absurdly domestic, and Marguerite slept on in the master's chair with her cheek against its wing, guarding her letter even in dreams.

Silas set the packet down on the bench between the tweezers and the burnisher, in the exact center of his working space, where he could not avoid seeing it.

He had opened the door. Now, it seemed, he would have to find out what he had opened it onto.
