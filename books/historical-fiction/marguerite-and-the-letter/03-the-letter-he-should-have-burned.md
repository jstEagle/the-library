## 3. The Letter He Should Have Burned

The workshop had never felt so large.

Silas stood in the middle of it with the candle raised, listening to the way the silence pooled in corners that had always seemed friendly by day — the shadow behind the great wheel-lathe, the dark under the bench where shavings gathered like drifts of grey snow, the black rectangle of the window where Marguerite's small face had pressed an hour ago and then vanished upstairs to the cot beneath the eaves. The room was the same room it had been every night of his apprenticeship. It only looked different because he now knew it held a secret, and secrets change the proportions of everything they touch.

He set the candle on the bench. Beside it lay the letter.

It had been sitting there since the woman left it, face down on a scrap of chamois leather as though it were something delicate being repaired — which, Silas thought grimly, it was not. It was something else entirely. It was the kind of thing that got men hanged, though he could not have said how he knew that. He only knew it from the weight of it, from the way the wax seal had been pressed with a signet rather than a thumb, from the fact that the hooded woman had gripped his wrist hard enough to leave marks when she said *forget my face*.

Forget her face. Forget the girl. But keep the letter.

That arithmetic did not balance. He had turned it over all through the supper Whitcombe had grudgingly shared — cold mutton, the old man watching him over the top of his spectacles, saying nothing about the child because, Silas realized now, there were very few things Whitcombe ever said aloud that he had not already decided in silence years before. And now the house slept. The clock over the door ticked its patient two-second heartbeat, the loudest thing in the world, counting out the time Silas was spending not deciding.

He picked up the letter. It was lighter than a watch movement and heavier than an anvil, which was impossible, and he knew it was impossible, and it was true anyway.

*Burn it,* said the sensible voice, the voice of ten years' training, the voice that understood that a workshop survived by doing good work for unremarkable people and asking no questions. *A sealed letter is a sealed letter. She asked you to keep it, not to read it. Put it in the strongbox under the floor and let it be someone else's trouble.*

But the woman's eyes came back to him — the one glimpse he'd allowed himself before dropping his gaze the way she'd demanded — and those eyes had been full of a terror too specific to be ordinary fear. Ordinary fear runs. That fear had been carrying something. Carrying it the way a mule carries a load it cannot put down without the whole cart coming apart.

And there was the girl. Seven years old, maybe eight, with flour-white cheeks and a smut of soot on her chin, who had asked — in the smallest voice he had ever heard a question asked in — whether the clocks would go on ticking if she went to sleep. As if she needed to know the world had a pulse before she could trust herself to stop watching it.

Silas broke the seal.

The wax parted with a small crack, absurdly loud, and he flinched toward the stairs like a boy caught thieving, half expecting Whitcombe's tread. Nothing. Only the clock, ticking, ticking, indifferent as God.

He unfolded two pages of close-written script and held them to the flame.

---

The first page began without preamble, without address, in the manner of a man who has written many letters he does not dare sign.

*I do not know who will read this. I must write it as if it may be anyone — a friend, a stranger, or the very man I write against. If it is the last, burn it and God forgive me for wasting your time. If it is either of the others, then listen, because what follows is true, and truth is the only property I have left.*

Silas's breath had already gone shallow, but he did not yet understand why. The handwriting sat at the edge of his attention like a tune heard through a wall — familiar, nagging, not yet named. He read on.

*A printer's wife was murdered in this town in the spring of the year. Her name was Aubry — no. Forgive me. The woman taken up for it is called Aubry. The wife who died was Madame Delacroix, née Vasseur, and she printed pamphlets at her husband's press until her husband fled and the press was seized. She was strangled in her own shop between the striking of nine and the quarter past, on the night of the fourteenth of April, and the man who strangled her wore the chain and seal of a justice of the assize.*

Silas stopped reading. Not because he chose to. Because his hands had begun, very slightly, to shake, and the words swam.

He set the pages flat on the bench. He pressed his palms against the wood until the trembling ran out of them into the grain. Then he made himself pick them up again, because there are some sentences that grow worse in the gaps between readings, and he had learned — from watches, if nothing else — that a thing you refuse to look at does not stop moving.

*The man's name is Harrow. Josiah Harrow, Justice of the Assize, commissioner of the new commission. He came to the print shop that night in plain clothes with two men whose faces I knew from the old days, and he demanded the proofs of a pamphlet naming certain magistrates in connection with the grain contracts. Madame Delacroix told him the proofs were sold or burned. He believed her. But she laughed at him — she had a laugh that could open a door or close it — and something in the laughing decided him. He took the cord from the parcel-shelf and he did it quietly, the way a man does a thing he has done before, and the two men held the door.*

*I was in the cellar with our daughter. I heard the chair go over. I heard the silence after, which was worse.*

Silas closed his eyes.

The workshop smelled of oil and metal and candle-tallow and, faintly, of the lavender water Whitcombe insisted upon despite its cost, and none of these smells belonged to the sentence he had just read, and the mismatch was unbearable. Somewhere above him a child slept who had been in that cellar. Who had heard the chair go over. His stomach turned over slowly, like a heavy wheel starting up from rest.

He read on, faster now, wanting it finished, wanting it out of him.

*I dared not speak. A man who has fled one charge of sedition cannot give evidence; his word is worth less than the paper it would ruin. I wrote instead. I sent this letter by ways I trusted, and it was returned to me unopened twice, and the third messenger I believe was bought. So I entrust it now to chance and to whoever finds themselves holding it. Carry it to the assizes. Give it to the advocate for the defence — any advocate, they are all hungry enough. Mrs. Aubry is innocent. I saw Harrow's hands. I will swear to it before God if it comes to that, though swearing will cost me my neck, and perhaps yours.*

*Do not look for me. I am dead. Let the dead do this one piece of work and then lie still.*

There was no signature.

There did not need to be.

---

Because Silas knew the hand.

He knew it the way he knew the smell of lamp-black, the way he knew the particular drag of a file across brass. He had watched that hand for the first seven years of his life, bending over broadsheets and bill-heads and, once, memorably, a forged permit that got them both out of a checkpoint outside Lyon — the letters leaning forward like runners, the crossbars struck through with a single impatient flourish, the *h*s with their peculiar high shoulders like a man raising his eyebrows at the world.

His father's hand. Gabriel Delacroix's hand.

Gabriel Delacroix, who had walked out of a rented room in this town ten years ago to draw the constables away from the press and had never come back, and whose death Silas had mourned twice — once when the news came, secondhand, that a printer answering his description had drowned in the canal at Rouen; and again, privately, every winter thereafter, on the anniversary of the leaving, which was worse than the anniversary of the dying because it admitted no certainty and therefore no end.

Ten years dead. Ten years a grief worn smooth as a river stone from handling.

And here was the stone, cracked open, alive inside.

Silas sat down. He did not remember deciding to sit; his legs simply resigned. The bench creaked under him and the sound seemed to come from far away, from another room, another life. He read the letter again from the beginning, because reading was the only act he trusted himself to perform, and this time he read it as a craftsman reads — checking joins, testing tolerances, hunting for the flaw that would let him reject the whole piece.

The dates fit. He hated that they fit. He remembered the spring of the murder — the town had talked of little else for a month; a woman dead in her own shop, a widow's friend arrested, whispers about pamphlets and magistrates that everyone dropped the moment anyone official came near. He had been seventeen and had thought, with the arrogance of seventeen, that such things happened to other people, in other towns, in the capitals where history was manufactured. History had apparently been manufacturing itself four streets from his bench.

The details fit. The cord from the parcel-shelf — he could see the shelf in his mind's eye, though he had never seen that shop, because every print shop kept one, low and within reach, because parcels were a printer's daily bread. The hour, between nine and the quarter past — a detail only a witness would fix so precisely, and only a father would fix it by the sound of a chair falling while his daughter sat beside him in the dark.

And the hand fit. God help him, the hand fit perfectly. No forger could reproduce that forward lean, those eyebrowed h's, that impatient crossing, without twenty years of living inside the fingers that made them.

Which meant his father had written this letter recently. Within the season. Within the year.

Which meant Gabriel Delacroix was not in the canal at Rouen.

Which meant—

Silas put the letter down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until the darkness bloomed orange. His ordered world — and it had been ordered, that was the whole point of it, that was the achievement of ten years of Whitcombe's discipline and Silas's own hungering after the clean logic of trains and escapements, wheels and pinions, cause and consequence, a universe you could take apart and understand and put back together so that it ran — his ordered world was lying in pieces on the bench, and the pieces did not want to go back together.

His father was alive. His father had abandoned him — no. Had died. Had lived. Had chosen to be dead rather than come home, and had written a letter begging a stranger to risk the gallows on the strength of words unsigned, and had somehow known that this workshop, of all workshops, this doorstep, of all doorsteps, would receive it.

Had he known? Was that possible? Had the hooded woman been given an address rather than a chance? Silas tried to reconstruct the night — the knock, the rain, the woman's gloved hand, the girl's enormous eyes — looking for the mechanism of it the way he'd look for the mechanism of a watch, and found he could not. The pieces were too few. Someone had built this situation, and he was standing inside it without a drawing.

One thing he did know, with the cold certainty of a balance-wheel settling: the letter was not safe and neither was he. A document naming a judge as a murderer did not lie quietly in a poor man's workshop. Men like Harrow employed men like Cade — he didn't know the name yet, wouldn't for weeks, but he knew the type, the soft boots, the questions asked of neighbours — and the first law of hidden things was that hiding them well only delayed discovery and increased its price.

*Burn it.* The sensible voice again, quieter now, almost pleading. *You owe your father nothing. He left you. He let you grieve him for ten years. Whatever debt existed between you, he spent it the night he walked out and let a child believe—*

But the child in the cellar had grown into a girl asleep above his head, and Mrs. Aubry — innocent, the letter said, seen with his own eyes, *I saw Harrow's hands* — Mrs. Aubry would stand trial soon, and the assizes did not wait for comfortable feelings, and an innocent woman did not hang because Silas Pemberton's heart had been inconvenienced.

He thought of his master. Tomorrow — today, he corrected himself, glancing at the clock, which said the hour was past two — tomorrow the observatory commission began in earnest, the job that would make or break them both, and Whitcombe had staked everything on Silas's hands being steady. Steady hands did not carry letters to advocates. Steady hands did not shelter fugitives. Steady hands cut wheels and polished pivots and asked nothing of the world except to be left alone with the work.

Silas looked at his hands, splayed on the bench, tallow-lit, trembling faintly at the fingertips.

They looked exactly the same as they had yesterday. That was the vertigo of it — that a man's world could crack through its whole thickness and his hands go on looking like a clockmaker's hands, and the clock over the door go on ticking, and the town go on sleeping, as if nothing had changed. But everything had changed. There was a live man where a dead man had been. There was a murderer wearing ermine four streets away. There was a child upstairs whose testimony, if she ever gave it, would hang a justice of the assize — and who was, at this moment, worth more dead than alive to certain parties, and did not know it, and was trusting him.

He folded the letter along its original creases. He did not burn it. He told himself he was merely delaying the decision — that a wise workman sleeps on a difficult assembly, that morning light shows flaws and solutions alike — but he knew the truth of it even as he wrapped the pages in oilcloth and knelt and prised up the loose board by the hearth where Whitcombe hid the wage-book. Some things, once seen, cannot be unseen, and some letters, once read, cannot be burned, because burning them would make you complicit in the silence, and Silas Pemberton had spent his whole life learning how mechanisms worked, and he could not unknow that a silenced witness was also a mechanism, and that he had just agreed, however reluctantly, to be one of its moving parts.

He replaced the board. He snuffed the candle. In the dark, the master clock over the door went on measuring out the seconds, faithful, blind, keeping perfect time for a world that had just stopped making sense — and somewhere above him, faint through the floorboards, Silas heard the small sounds of a child turning in her sleep, and lay down without undressing, and did not sleep at all.

Tomorrow he would climb to the observatory and begin the greatest work of his life. He would need steadier hands than these.

Above the workbench, unaware of what it now concealed, the clock ticked toward morning.
