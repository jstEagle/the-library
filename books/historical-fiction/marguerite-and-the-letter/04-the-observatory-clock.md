## 4. The Observatory Clock

The master clock of the Ashcombe Observatory had been stripped of its dignity as well as its escapement.

Silas stood before it on his first morning of the commission, a canvas tool-roll under his arm and Marguerite's presence folded into the back of his mind like a letter pressed between the pages of a book he dared not open in public. The great case rose nearly to the rafters of the instrument room — walnut, dark with two generations of polish and the oil of countless hands — and inside it the movement hung exposed, brass wheels the size of dinner plates, the great driving barrel wrapped with cable thick as a finger. Someone had been at it before him. The escapement was gone entirely: anchor, pallets, escape wheel — all removed, leaving only the bare arbor jutting from the plates like a broken tooth. He would have to make the whole train of the beating heart anew.

"Mr. Quill will be down directly," said the caretaker, an old man named Hobbes who moved through the tower as slowly as the pendulum itself. "He's particular about dust. Mind you sweep as you go."

"I always sweep as I go," Silas said, and meant it as a small boast, because sweeping was one of the few things in which Whitcombe's workshop had never been found wanting.

He set up his tools on a bench beneath the south window, where the light came down clean and grey over the moors, and began — as every honest repairman begins — by doing nothing at all except looking. The clock's failure was written all over it if a man knew how to read. The pivot holes were worn to ovals; the great wheel had been allowed to run with a dry arbor for so long that the brass had galled and scored. And there, at the top of the going train, the absence where the escapement should be, deliberate, surgical. Not neglect. Removal. Somebody had taken that clock apart on purpose, and recently — the screw-holes still showed bright metal where the heads had been backed out.

He thought of nothing else for a quarter of an hour, and it was the first quarter of an hour in three days in which he had thought of nothing else at all.

Then the other thing came back, as it always did, sliding into the space between one gear and the next: *the letter is in the lining of your left boot. You are standing in a public building with a dead man's handwriting strapped to your ankle.*

Not dead. That was the word that refused to sit still. Ten years dead — buried, mourned, cursed quietly by Silas himself more than once on cold nights when the workshop felt like a cage — and alive, alive enough to write a hand that trembled only at the descenders, alive enough to beg a stranger to carry treason to the assizes. Alive enough to leave a child on a doorstep and say *forget her face.*

"You'll want to start with the crutch," Hobbes offered from the doorway. "Or the frame."

"The frame," Silas said. "Always the frame. A clock is only as true as what holds it up."

It was what Whitcombe said, every apprentice's first lesson, and saying it aloud steadied him in a way that surprised him. Words learned at another man's bench could still hold a man upright.

* * *

Marguerite had been installed in the workshop before dawn, in the old coal store behind the drying cupboard, which Whitcombe had cleared and fitted with a pallet, a blanket, a candle stub, and a chamber pot, and which she had regarded that morning with the grave suspicion of a cat being shown a basket. There was food. There was water. There was a rule, delivered by Silas with all the authority his twenty-two years could muster, which was not very much but was apparently sufficient:

"You do not come out. You do not make a sound above a whisper. If anyone knocks on the street door, you go behind the flour sacks and you stay there until my voice tells you it's finished. Do you understand?"

"Yes," she had said. Then, after a moment: "Is it finished when they knock again?"

"It's finished when I say my own name out loud."

"My name too?"

"No." He had crouched to her level. "Never your name. Your name stays in the coal store even if the whole world comes through that door. Do you understand me now?"

She had nodded then, solemnly, and something in the way she did it — the way a child agrees to carry a weight she doesn't fully measure but understands to be heavy — had made his chest hurt in a manner he had no time to examine.

Now, three miles away and half a day's walk from her, he caught himself doing sums of fear. The workshop was watched — he'd seen the man across the lane twice yesterday, a fellow with a drover's coat and no drover's business. Whitcombe had said nothing about him, which meant Whitcombe had seen him too, which meant Whitcombe was afraid, which meant everything. If they searched the shop while he was here, pinned to the observatory by the most important commission of his life, there was a coal store and a seven-year-old girl and a locked door between them and whatever Cade's people did to children who had been told to be forgotten.

And if he left the clock — if he abandoned the bench, even for a morning — the commission died, and with it the workshop, and with the workshop, everything Marguerite was hiding from the world in order to survive.

The deadline ticked. He heard it everywhere: in the drip of the eaves outside, in his own pulse, in the ghost of an escapement that wasn't there yet. Three weeks, Mr. Quill had said, before the Astronomer Royal's man came to verify the timekeeping. Three weeks to rebuild a beating heart and keep a child breathing in secret, and memorize a letter of forty lines against the hour it might be torn from his boot.

He took out his memorandum book — plain paper, the kind apprentices used for drawing wheel teeth — and began, under cover of sketching the worn pivot holes, to copy the letter from memory. Four lines. Then he checked it against the original, in the privy, alone. Two errors. He corrected them and started again.

By the third attempt he had eight lines perfect, and Mr. Quill was standing behind him.

* * *

He heard the astronomer before he saw him — or rather, he heard the silence the man brought with him, the way conversation in the tower died and even Hobbes's shuffling feet went quiet. When Silas turned, Mr. Quill was examining the empty escapement arbor with an eyeglass screwed into one eye, a thin man of fifty-odd dressed in black wool gone greenish at the cuffs, with hair the colour of unpolished pewter and hands that looked as though they had been designed for finer work than they were given.

"You're young," Mr. Quill said, without turning around.

"Yes, sir."

"How young?"

"Twenty-two in the spring."

"Mm." The eyeglass came down. "Whitcombe's boy? No — Pemberton. The indentured one. He spoke of you." Something flickered across the astronomer's face, quickly suppressed. "He said you had good hands and a bad habit of thinking while you worked."

"That's — yes, sir. Both true."

"Thinking is not a bad habit in a repairman. It's a bad habit in a soldier, perhaps, and fatal in a clergyman." Quill drifted along the case, trailing one finger an inch above the wood without touching it. "You've noticed the escapement was removed deliberately."

Silas blinked. "Yes, sir. Recently. By someone who knew what they were doing."

"Someone who knew exactly," Quill said. "I did it myself, four months ago. I sent the parts to London for examination and they were lost on the road. Which means either the roads are worse than the Post Office admits, or someone in this kingdom wanted the Ashcombe master clock incapable of keeping time." He said it mildly, the way a man describes weather, and Silas understood that mildness was the man's armour and possibly his weapon. "Do you know why an observatory must have a clock that cannot lie?"

"Because the stars don't wait, sir. Every second wrong is an observation ruined."

"Every second wrong is a *truth* ruined," Quill said. "A star transits when it transits. The clock's business is to testify honestly to when. It is a witness, Mr. Pemberton. The most reliable witness in England, when it is well-made — better than any man, because it has no interest to serve and no fear to flatter." He turned those pale eyes on Silas at last, and Silas felt them pass over him like a lamp passed along a wall, finding every crack. "You've been reciting something under your breath for the past ten minutes. Lines of some kind. Poetry?"

"A prayer, sir."

"Hm. I was raised Methodist; I know what prayers sound like. That wasn't one." But he let it go, drifting back toward the stairs, pausing at the door. "Three weeks. My colleague from Greenwich arrives on the twenty-ninth. If this clock keeps time within two seconds a day when he leaves, Whitcombe's workshop eats through the winter. If it does not—" He didn't finish it. He didn't need to. "Work well, Mr. Pemberton. And sleep. Men who don't sleep make clocks that lie."

* * *

That evening he walked home in a wind that smelled of coming rain, taking the long way past the tannery to shake off the sense of being followed, and let himself into the workshop through the yard door with the pattern of knocks he and Whitcombe had agreed on — two, pause, three — feeling like a conspirator in his own house.

Marguerite was asleep when he checked the coal store, curled with her thumb near her mouth and the blanket pulled to her chin, and beside her head, arranged with terrible care, a row of objects: a brass gear-blank she had smuggled from his scrap bin, three buttons, a dried apple ring, and a flat stone. Her collection. Her proof, perhaps, that the world still contained things worth counting.

He stood in the doorway longer than was wise. She was evidence. He knew that now, had known it since the night she arrived — a living witness to whatever had happened to her mother, to Delacroix, to the judge whose name sat in the letter like a bullet seated in a chamber. Anyone hunting the letter would eventually hunt the girl. Anyone hunting the girl would find the coal store, the flour sacks, the rule about names. He was not hiding a child; he was hiding testimony, and testimony grew heavier every day it went unwitnessed.

But asleep, she was only a child. And Silas, who had spent his life believing that history happened elsewhere, to other people, in capitals and courtrooms and printing houses he would never see, understood with a lurch that history was now sleeping in his coal store and ticking in his boot and waiting for him in the tower on the hill, and that there was nowhere else to stand.

He closed the door softly and went to the bench, and by the light of two candles worked until midnight on drawings for the new escapement: the pallet geometry figured and refigured, the lift angles calculated for the observatory's nine-foot pendulum, each line drawn twice because a mistake here would cost days they did not have. Whitcombe appeared at his shoulder around eleven, silent as ever, looked over the figures, and said only, "Your locking faces are a half-degree shy," which was both a correction and, in the grammar of that workshop, an embrace.

On the fourth morning the rain came properly, and with it a complication Silas had dreaded and planned for and dreaded anyway.

He could not leave Marguerite alone all day again. Not because of the watcher in the drover's coat — though the watcher was there, and had been joined by a friend — but because she had begun asking questions. Small ones. Dangerous ones.

"When can I stop being in the coal store?" she'd asked at breakfast, pushing an egg around her plate.

"When it's safe."

"When will it be safe?"

"When the bad men stop looking."

"Are they looking today?"

"They're always looking today," Silas had said, and immediately regretted the honesty of it, because her chin had come up in a way that reminded him uncomfortably of someone deciding something.

So he made an arrangement. The observatory tower had a lower room — a storeroom beneath the instrument floor, reached by a stair, used by Hobbes for rope and lamp oil and smelling accordingly. Silas carried Marguerite there before dawn on the fifth day, wrapped in an adult coat with the collar up, past a caretaker who was either too old or too discreet to ask why his workman had brought his little sister. He gave her the same rules: silence, the corner behind the rope coils, the signal — his voice, saying his own name.

"And if you forget to say it?" she asked.

"I won't forget."

"But if you do."

"Then you stay put until morning, and you eat the bread in the bag, and you are angry with me tomorrow."

"I'm already angry with you today," she said, without heat, settling behind the ropes like a bird into a nest, "because the coal store smells." And she was, he realized as he climbed the stairs to the clock, the first person in ten years to tell him an uncomfortable truth plainly and expect nothing bad to happen as a result. He found he liked it. He found it terrified him.

* * *

The escapement took shape over the following week the way a ship takes shape in a bottle — patiently, in impossible confinement, one careful motion at a time. He cut the pallet arms from hard brass stock, filed the locking faces to the half-degree, polished the impulse planes until they threw the candlelight in clean sheets. The escape wheel he cut tooth by tooth, sixty of them, checking each against a template scribed from Quill's surviving drawings of the original design, and when the wheel first dropped into the train and he turned the great barrel by hand and felt — felt, through his fingertips, through the bones of his wrist — the tick and lock, tick and lock of a heartbeat returning to a body that had lain four months breathless, he had to stop and press his palms flat on the bench and breathe.

Below him, three floors down, a child sat silent among the ropes. In his boot, forty lines of a dead man's handwriting waited to be needed. On the twenty-ninth, a man from Greenwich would judge whether his hands were good enough to save everyone he loved.

He was fitting the suspension when Quill came down the tower stairs faster than Silas had yet heard him move, and stopped at the foot of them with a newspaper still folded in his hand, and looked at Silas for a long moment with the lamp-passing gaze.

"Pemberton," he said. "What do you know about the Aubry woman?"

The name went through Silas like a wire. "Nothing, sir. Who is—"

"The printer's widow's friend. They've arrested her for the murder. The trial's set for the assizes." Quill unfolded the paper with fingers that were not quite steady. "Harrow is presiding. Judge Harrow." He said the name the way a man says the name of a storm he has decided not to run from. "I ask because you went white just now, and men who go white at a name usually have a reason, and I am a man who has learned — at considerable cost — to pay attention to reasons."

Silas kept his hands on the clock. Kept them very still, on the warm brass of the suspension block, where no one could see them shake.

"I don't know anything, sir," he said. "Only what's in the paper."

Quill held his gaze a moment longer. Then he folded the newspaper, laid it on the bench between them — deliberately, face-up, the trial notice showing — and climbed back up the stairs, leaving it there like a key left in a door.

Below, faint through three floors of oak and stone, Silas could almost believe he heard the pendulum begin its first slow swing.
