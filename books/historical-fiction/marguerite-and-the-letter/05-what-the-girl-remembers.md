## 5. What the Girl Remembers

Marguerite counted the days by the sounds of the workshop.

There was the morning sound, which was Whitcombe's cough and the scrape of his stool across the floorboards, and there was the noon sound, which was Silas filing brass in a long even whisper, like rain that had made up its mind. And there was the afternoon sound, which she liked best of all: the tick of the small clocks waiting on their shelf to be mended, a dozen little hearts beating out of time with one another, patient as sheep. She had decided they were sheep. She had decided a great many things since the night her mother set her down on a stranger's doorstep and told her to forget.

She had not forgotten. That was the trouble. She had tried — she had squeezed her eyes shut on the stairs each night and pressed her palms against them until sparks swam, and still the remembering came back, the way water comes back into a hole you dig in the sand near the river. Mama had said *forget my face*, and Marguerite understood now that this was impossible, because a face was not a thing you carried in your pocket and could leave behind on a chair. A face was where you kept yourself. If she forgot Mama's face, some part of Marguerite would go out like a candle, and no one would know which part, and it could never be relit.

So instead she forgot other things, smaller things, to make room. She forgot the name of the street where they had lived. She forgot what the stairs smelled like. She forgot the color of the ribbon she had lost at the market, though she remembered losing it, and how Mama had said *never mind, never mind* in the voice that meant her heart was hurting but she would not say so. It seemed to Marguerite that forgetting was like the workshop itself: you could only hold so many things on the shelves, and if something new must be kept, something old must be put out into the dark.

She kept, therefore: the carriage. The bread. The man's glove. And the smell of ink.

* * *

It had been evening, the kind of evening when the light goes gold and then grey and then gone, and Mama had been printing something — Marguerite knew it was printing because of the smell, which got into everything, into the bread and the hair and the sheets, a sharp dark smell like the inside of a chestnut. Their rooms were above the print shop, or beside it; the memory had put the stairs somewhere different every time she climbed them in her mind, and this bothered her, the way a crooked picture bothers you. But the smell was always the same, and the sound of the press was always the same, a great wooden animal breathing in and out, breathing in and out.

Mama had wrapped bread for her. That was the strange part, the part that came back most often and least usefully. There had been no time for supper, and yet Mama had taken a loaf — the end of a loaf, the heel, which was Marguerite's favorite because it was hardest and lasted longest — and wrapped it in a printed page, folding it over twice, tucking the corners in the way Mama tucked blanket corners, neat as a parcel. And she had knelt down, so that their faces were level, and said:

*Marguerite. Listen to me. Whatever happens tonight, whatever you see, you are going to be brave, and being brave means being quiet.*

And Marguerite had said, because she was seven and honest, *Am I going to see something?*

Mama's hands had stopped on the bread. Her hands were stained black at the fingertips, always, from the ink, and Marguerite used to think her mother had dipped her fingers in night itself. Now the hands trembled, just slightly, and the bread crumpled a little under them, and the printed page crackled, and Marguerite heard — though she did not understand until much later, until this very week, lying awake in a stranger's attic — the words that were on that page read themselves in her memory like a slow voice: *the true account of the death of—* and then the rest was folded under, hidden by the bread, the way a coin is hidden under a napkin in a conjurer's trick.

*You may see something,* Mama said. *If you do, look at your shoes. Look at your shoes and count to one hundred, and I will come find you. I promise you I will come find you.*

Then the knocking began below, heavy and rude, fists rather than knuckles, and the great wooden animal of the press went silent as though it too were afraid.

* * *

They went down the back way, which Marguerite had not known existed, through a door behind the drying lines where the printed sheets hung like pale laundry, rows and rows of them, ghost-clothes. Mama carried her. Mama ran. The streets were wet — it had rained earlier, or perhaps the streets were always wet in that town, in her memory they were always shining — and the running made a slapping sound, and Marguerite held the bread against her chest with both arms the way one holds a doll, and she did not eat it. She could not have said why. It seemed important not to eat it. Perhaps because Mama had made it for her, wrapped it with such care; perhaps because eating it would make the evening ordinary, and the evening was not ordinary, the evening had fists in it.

They came out onto a wider street, and Mama set her down behind a stack of barrels by a chandler's wall, and crouched, and looked at her a long moment with an expression Marguerite had no word for then and has only found recently, watching Silas work: the expression of someone checking a mechanism they cannot stop.

*Count to one hundred,* Mama whispered. *Look at your shoes.*

Then Mama stood up and walked out into the middle of the street, alone, deliberately, the way a person walks when they want to be seen — and Marguerite, peering around the barrel because she was seven and disobedient in exactly one direction, saw the carriage come.

It was a fine carriage. That was the thing she could never make anyone understand, if anyone had ever asked, which no one had. It was not a rough cart full of rough men. It was black and gleaming, with lamps already lit though the dark was hardly come, and the horses were matched greys, and there was a crest on the door that caught the lamplight — a shape like a bird, or a hand, she could never settle which, and she had tried drawing it eleven times on scraps of paper in the attic until Silas found the drawings and went very still and asked her, gently, where she had seen it, and she had shown him, and he had sat down heavily on his stool as though his legs had been taken out from under him like the legs of a table.

A man got down from the carriage first. He wore gloves. She remembered the gloves because of what happened with the bread later, but also because one glove was smooth and one had a button at the wrist, and this asymmetry had struck her even then, even at seven, as wrong, the way a clock that ticks unevenly is wrong. He spoke to her mother, and his voice did not carry to the barrels, but his posture did: he stood the way the schoolmaster had stood over the boy who stole, unhurried, certain of the ending.

Then another man got down, and this one was older, and the others — there were two more, at the horses' heads — took off their hats to him. Took off their hats. In the street, in the wet, to this man, and Marguerite thought: *he is somebody.* That was the whole of her seven-year-old judgment, and it was, she would learn, the most terrible testimony she possessed: *he is somebody, and everybody knows it, and nobody stops him.*

Her mother spoke to the old man. Her mother's back was straight. Marguerite could see the bread-page crumpling in her own arms and her mother's straight back and the hats coming off, and she looked down at her shoes as she had been told, and counted.

One. Two. Three. The counting was hard because counting wanted the breath and the breath wanted to cry. Fourteen. Fifteen. Her shoes had a buckle on the left one and none on the right; the right buckle had gone to mend the strap of the market basket, and Mama had said it was a fair trade, and Marguerite had believed her. Thirty-one. Thirty-two. There was a sound of scuffling, of a body not going willingly — she heard it without looking up, heard her mother's voice rise once, only once, saying a word she could not catch, a name perhaps, or a plea, cut off the way a string is cut. Forty-eight. Forty-nine. A door closed. Not slammed. Closed, with the particular soft authority of a fine carriage door, well hung, well oiled, expensive. Sixty. Sixty-one. Wheels on wet stone, gathering speed, going away.

She counted to one hundred. She counted to two hundred. She sat behind the barrels until the chandler's cat came and sat on her feet, and the bread grew cold in her arms, and her mother did not come to find her.

That was the whole of it. That was everything. And it had been sitting in her like a stone in a shoe for a year, walked upon daily, until the hooded woman on the doorstep — who was not her mother, whose face she had indeed obeyed the instruction about, having looked at her shoes — had left her here, in this house of ticking hearts, with a letter she did not know existed and instructions she did not understand, to forget.

* * *

In the daytime she was useful. She had resolved to be useful, because usefulness was the coin with which children bought their keep, and she had watched enough of the world to know her purse was thin. She swept the shavings. She sorted screws into cups by size, an occupation she found genuinely pleasant, the small silver heaps like grain. She learned that the flat ones were called punches and the spiral ones drills and the toothed wheels wheels, and that Silas's hands did things her hands could watch forever: how he held a wheel up to the window and sighted along its teeth, how he breathed out before making a cut, as though the breath steadied the world.

He talked to her while he worked, in a low voice, half to himself. He asked careful questions, questions that circled like flies and never landed: Was she warm enough in the attic. Did she sleep. Did she remember anything of the journey, of the woman, of — and here the fly would circle away again — of home.

Today he asked nothing. Today he was quiet in a new way, a tight way, and Marguerite, who had become a connoisseur of adult silences the way other children became connoisseurs of sweets, recognized it. It was the silence of a man carrying something breakable.

She sat on her stool by the window with her cup of screws, and watched him, and decided to give him something. It was not calculation, quite. It was more like the feeling she had when she saw a clock run down: something good was stopping, and she knew, in the wordless way she knew things, that she was the key that fit.

"Mister Silas," she said.

"Silas is enough." He did not look up from the escapement, the poor stripped thing, its pallets bare, its teeth naked. "Or sir, if you must."

"Silas. About my mama."

His file stopped. The whole room seemed to lean in — even the little clocks on the shelf, she thought, even the sheep.

"She wrapped my bread," Marguerite said, "in a printed page."

"I know," he said softly. "You told me. The night you came."

"I didn't tell you all of it." She looked down at her shoes, out of habit, and then made herself look up, because this was testimony and testimony wanted eyes. "The page had writing on it. Big letters at the top. I couldn't read then. I can read a little now — Whitcombe has been teaching me, he doesn't know I noticed — but I remember the shape of the top line. It was like a fence. Tall letters, all in a row. And underneath, smaller, lots of lines, close together, like—" she searched "—like the teeth of a very small wheel, all the way down the page."

Silas had turned on his stool to face her fully. His hands were in his lap, and she saw that they were not steady, and this frightened her a little and steadied her more, because it meant the thing she carried mattered.

"There was a carriage," she went on, the words coming faster now, the stone finally rolling out of the shoe after a year of walking. "A black one, with grey horses, and lamps lit early. And men took off their hats to an old gentleman, and my mama spoke to him with her back straight, and then they took her, and the door shut soft, like this—" she demonstrated on the air, delicately, horribly, "—and I counted to two hundred and she never came."

The workshop was silent except for the sheep on the shelf, ticking their uneven ticks.

"The man with the gloves," Silas said. His voice had gone strange, flattened, like metal hammered thin. "The one who spoke to your mother first. Tell me about him. Slowly. Everything."

"He had one glove plain and one with a button. He wasn't afraid of anything. He stood like—" she frowned, hunting "—like the clock stands. Like it couldn't fall over even if it wanted to."

"And the old gentleman. The one they took their hats off to."

Marguerite closed her eyes. She went back down the back stair, past the ghost-sheets, out to the barrels, to the wet shining street, and she looked at him as hard as a seven-year-old can look, which is harder than anyone believes. Fine coat. White at the collar. A face like a closed door, and powdered, and above it — she had seen it when he turned toward the lamp, turning to give the order, she was sure, sure as the buckle missing from her shoe —

"A chain," she said. "A gold chain, with a badge hanging from it. Round. With a ribbon under it. He wore it like a king wears things."

When she opened her eyes, Silas Pemberton had gone the color of the unpolished brass, and he was looking at her the way he looked at a movement he had just discovered broken — with dread, and with wonder, and with the beginning of understanding.

"Marguerite," he said, and his voice was very quiet, "do you know what a judge's chain looks like?"

She shook her head.

"No," he said, almost to himself, rising, knocking his stool so it rocked. "No, of course you don't. You're a child. You're a child who saw—" He stopped. He pressed both hands flat on the bench, and the little clocks ticked on, indifferent, and outside in the street a cart rattled past, ordinary, carrying turnips or coal or nothing at all. "You're not a witness to hide," he murmured. "You're a witness they'll hang to silence. My God. They didn't leave you with me to be forgotten. They left you because you were the last place the truth was still alive — and they'll come looking for where it lives."

Marguerite did not entirely follow. But she understood the shape of it, the way she understood the shape of a wheel before she knew its name, and she picked up her cup of screws and held it against her chest the way she had once held a heel of bread wrapped in the true account of a murder, and waited for him to tell her what happened next.

He did not tell her. Instead he went to the window, and stood very still, looking down the street toward the town — toward the assize courts being swept and garlanded for the judges' coming — and Marguerite saw him mouth one word, over and over, like a man winding a spring that would not take.

Harrow.
