## 6. Harrow Comes to Town

The town had put on its best face for the assizes, the way a debtor puts on his good coat to meet the bailiff. Bunting hung from the corn exchange like festive scars; the gutters had been swept of a season's muck; boys with brooms chased leaves along the High Street and were themselves chased by beadle's apprentices. Silas watched it all from the observatory window with a file motionless in his hand, thinking that a town could dress itself for justice the way it dressed itself for a fair, and that neither costume changed what was sold underneath.

Three days. That was what Quill had said at breakfast, in his vague, star-distracted way: the judges came up from London on Thursday, the courts sat Friday, and by Saturday the whole apparatus would have chewed through its calendar — poachers, forgers, one or two unlucky smugglers, and a woman named Aubry whose name meant nothing yet to anyone but him.

He went back to the escapement.

The master clock lay open before him like a patient on a table, its great plates lifted off, its wheels spread in oiled ranks across the baize cloth he had brought from Whitcombe's bench because the observatory's own surfaces were scarred with fifty years of careless restings. The stripped escapement was the heart of the commission: someone — Quill would not say who; Quill seemed embarrassed even to imply it — had attempted a repair years ago and abandoned it mid-mutilation, leaving the pallets filed to shapes no horologist would confess to and the escape wheel with two teeth chipped so fine you needed a loupe to find the wounds. It was delicate work. It was, Silas admitted to himself as he seated the first new pallet under the lamp, the finest work of his life so far, and he was doing it with half his mind, which was like cutting a thread with half a pair of scissors.

Because the letter was in his head. He had burned the paper itself three nights ago over the workshop candle, feeding it in inch by inch until only the wax seal survived, a little red coin of another man's authority, which he had ground beneath his heel. But paper was never really destroyed by burning. It was translated. Every line of it now lived behind his eyes, recited nightly the way other men recited psalms: *the bearer is unknown to me and must remain so; what follows was witnessed by my wife, who died of it; Judge Edmund Harrow did upon the night of the ninth of November last strike down—* He could not finish the sentence even inside his own skull. His mind flinched from the words the way a finger flinches from a flame, and then returned, compulsively, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

*Harrow.* The word Marguerite had seen him mouth at the window. He mouthed it still, silently, while his hands did their honest work, filing a pallet stone to within the width of a hair, testing it against the escape wheel's teeth, listening with his whole body for that sound every clockmaker lives inside of — the tick, the tiny surrender and recovery of a wheel giving way and catching again. Give way and catch. That was what he was doing too. Give way, catch. Give way—

"Mr. Pemberton."

Quill's voice, mild as milk, from the doorway of the transit room. Silas started badly enough that the pallet stone jumped from his tweezers and rang on the plate like a struck bell.

"You're nervous," Quill observed. He came into the lamplight holding two cups of something hot and bitter-smelling, and set one down among the wheels without asking whether hot liquid belonged near an escapement, which told Silas everything about how seriously the astronomer took clocks. "You've been nervous for a week. You file beautifully when you're calm and adequately when you're not, and I'd rather have beautiful."

"I'm sorry, sir."

"Don't be sorry. Be calm." Quill lowered himself onto the stool opposite, knees cracking, and peered at the mechanism with the same squinting patience he gave the sky. "My father was a physician. He used to say a patient always knows when the surgeon's hand is afraid, even asleep, even under the knife. Machines are the same." He sipped. "Is it the girl?"

Silas's blood went cold and stayed cold. "Sir?"

"The child who comes at dusk with your supper basket and leaves before full dark. My housekeeper has counted her visits. Housekeepers count everything; it is why they are housekeepers." Quill's eyes, pale and unhurried, came up from the clock to Silas's face. "I am not asking what she is to you. I am asking whether she is safe here."

For a long moment Silas weighed lying, and found the weight of it heavier than the truth. "I believe so, sir."

"Hm." Quill looked back at the escapement. "Then see that you keep believing it, and work calmly, and we shall have this thing striking true by the judges' arrival, which matters more than either of us can afford to say aloud." He rose, joints protesting. At the door he paused. "There is a carriage coming up the hill road. Very grand. If it is coming here, you will want to look like a craftsman rather than a frightened boy. There is a difference, and powerful men can smell it."

* * *

The carriage was indeed coming there.

Silas heard it before he saw it clearly: the iron shoe of the hill cobbles taking the horses' weight, the wet squeal of axle, the particular rhythm of four horses driven by a man who wanted the world to know he paid for four. Then it swung into the observatory yard, and he stood at the window and watched the crest on the door panel resolve out of the morning mist — a balance scale above a closed book, gilded — and felt the floor tilt very slightly under him, though he knew perfectly well the floor was level, having leveled it himself.

Judge Edmund Harrow descended without assistance, which surprised Silas; he had built the judge in his mind as old and gross, some cathedral of flesh and wig, because guilt ought to look like something. This man was perhaps fifty-five, lean, straight-backed, dressed in travelling black with silver at the buckles, and he climbed the observatory steps with the easy tread of a man who had spent his life ascending places where others waited below him. Behind him came a clerk carrying a folio, and behind the clerk —

Silas's breath stopped.

Behind the clerk came a second gentleman, unannounced, unhurried, dressed plainly in drab wool as if he had made a study of not being noticed and succeeded everywhere except with men who repaired things for a living. He moved the way a well-made detent moves: no wasted motion, everything held in tension. And his eyes, before he had crossed half the yard, had already taken in the windows, the side door, the wood store, the roofline, and — Silas was certain of it, certain the way one is certain of a cracked jewel — the window of the little room where Marguerite's cot was hidden behind a screen of clock cases.

*Forget my face,* the woman had said. Someone had come hunting faces after all.

By the time the knock came Silas had composed himself into what he hoped was craftsman rather than frightened boy. He opened the door to the judge himself.

"Master clockmaker." Harrow's voice was warm, deep, the voice of a man accustomed to rooms going quiet for him. "Edmund Harrow. I am told the observatory's clock is being restored, and I confess I could not pass through this town without seeing it. I have a weakness for mechanisms." He smiled, and the smile was genuinely pleasant, which was the worst thing about it. "May I impose?"

"It would be an honour, sir." The words came out level. Somewhere far away in himself, Silas admired their levelness.

They came into the instrument room in a rustle of wool and boot-leather. The clerk hovered by the door. The plain gentleman did not hover; he stationed himself, quietly, near the window, and looked out at the view of the town as though memorizing it, and Silas understood that he was doing precisely that.

"She stopped in 'eighty-eight, I'm told," Harrow said, approaching the great clock where it stood stripped and gleaming. "Neglect, mostly. These instruments die of neglect the way marriages do — nobody decides anything, things simply go unwound." He bent to examine the movement with his hands clasped behind his back, a man careful to admire without touching. "And you are restoring her alone?"

"My master holds the commission, sir. I execute it. Silas Pemberton, apprentice to Mr. Josiah Whitcombe, clockmaker, of Tanner's Lane."

"Pemberton." Something moved behind the judge's eyes — not recognition, Silas thought; consideration. A man tasting a name to see if it was worth keeping. "A good trade. An honest one. Gears cannot lie, can they? Either the train runs or it does not. I sometimes think the law should be built like a clock." He straightened and smiled again. "Every tooth accounted for. No play in the system."

"No, sir. Though a clock forgives small errors. It averages them out."

Something flickered across Harrow's face — quickly gone, quickly smoothed — that might have been surprise, or might have been pleasure, and Silas could not tell which frightened him more. "Does it, by God. Yes. Yes, it does." The judge turned a slow circle around the instrument, and Silas watched him watch it, and understood suddenly that this was not a detour on a journey. Judges did not climb hills to inspect clocks. Someone had told him about the restoration; someone had told him about the workshop; and he had come to put faces to reports, the way a buyer inspects stock.

"Your hands," Harrow said abruptly.

Silas looked down. His fingers were black with plate-oil, nicked at two knuckles, stained at the cuticle by years of brass and steel.

"They're remarkable," the judge said softly. "Do you know, in thirty years on the bench I have learned that hands tell the truth when tongues will not? A forger's hands are soft in the wrong places. A liar's are restless." He did not reach out; he simply looked, and his looking had weight, like a thumb pressing a seam to test it. "Yours are a workman's hands. Steady. Careful." A pause, thin as foil. "What do they tell me, I wonder, that your mouth hasn't yet?"

"Only what they've been taught, sir. Filing, fitting, and how to hold still while better men talk."

The judge laughed — a real laugh, full-bodied, delighted — and clapped Silas once on the shoulder with a hand surprisingly light for its size. "Ha! Keep that tongue, Pemberton. It will serve you better than your files." He turned to go, then paused at the door, silhouetted against the bright yard, and spoke without turning round, conversationally, as if remarking on weather:

"They hanged a woman at Maidstone last spring for a murder she did not do. I know she did not do it because the man who did confessed to me afterward, drunk, in a tavern in Ashford, and I had him taken up quietly and transported, and the record amended, and I slept perfectly well. Do you know why?" Now he turned, and his face was kind, and terrible, and utterly unguarded, as though he were offering a gift. "Because order is worth more than truth, boy. Truth is a luxury of quiet times. In loud times, the law must simply *run* — like your clock there. Strike the hour, however imperfectly, or the whole town stops knowing when it is. A stopped clock tells no time at all. Remember that, whatever else you remember."

He settled his hat. "When does she run again?"

"Before the judges arrive, sir."

"Then I look forward to hearing her strike." The smile once more, warm as a hearth, cold as a grate. "Good day, Master Pemberton."

* * *

Silas stood in the doorway and watched the carriage go, and it was not until the sound of it had faded down the hill road that he realized the plain gentleman had not left with it.

The man was standing by the yard wall, ostensibly admiring the view, actually waiting, and when the carriage was safely away he pushed off the wall and strolled toward the door with his hands loose at his sides, and stopped just outside arm's reach — exactly outside arm's reach, Silas noted, the distance of a man who had thought about arms.

"Cade," the man said, offering nothing further by way of explanation. His voice was dry and unhurried, a voice that read documents aloud for a living. "I'm told you've been doing fine work up here. Fine work gets noticed." He produced, from inside his coat, a folded paper, and held it out. "Notice, then."

It was a printed notice, fresh ink, government stock: a proclamation regarding seditious publications, naming penalties, describing persons of interest. Among the descriptions, halfway down, in the flat language such documents use: *one Delacroix, printer, late of this county, fugitive, said to have associates still harboured within it.*

"He was a printer, they say," Cade remarked, watching Silas read. "Good ones are rare. Bad ones get people killed — themselves, usually, but they're careless with the blast radius." He reclaimed the notice, folded it away, and smiled for the first time, and his smile was nothing like Harrow's; Harrow's smile was furniture, polished and functional. Cade's was an opening move. "You'll have heard nothing, of course. Printers' sons never do."

The world did not tilt this time. It went very quiet instead, the way a room goes quiet when a mainspring snaps somewhere inside a case — everything outward unchanged, everything inward ruined.

"I don't know what you mean, sir."

"No," Cade agreed pleasantly. "Nobody ever does. It saves a great deal of time if everyone starts by not knowing." He touched his hat brim — an odd, almost courteous gesture — and stepped back. "Enjoy the assizes, Mr. Pemberton. They come but twice a year, and they touch so many lives." He turned, then added over his shoulder, lightly, as a man might mention a mutual acquaintance: "Give my regards to the child."

Silas did not move until the man had gone down through the yard gate and been swallowed by the town below. Then he shut the observatory door, and leaned against it, and found his hands — those truthful, remarkable hands the judge had praised — shaking so badly he had to press them flat against the oak to stop them.

Marguerite. He was moving before the thought finished forming, through the instrument room, past the naked clock, down the passage to the little screened room, where he found her sitting cross-legged on the cot, sorting screws into families by size with the grave concentration she brought to everything, entirely unaware that two men had climbed the hill that morning and one of them knew she existed.

She looked up and smiled at him, and the sight of it went through him like a blade through silk.

"Silas," she said. "There's a man walking up the lane again. The plain one. He turned round at the bottom of the hill."

* * *

That evening he walked down into the town, because he could not bear the observatory's height and silence, and because there was a question eating him that he could no longer refuse to feed. The assize preparations were nearly done; the courthouse windows glowed; constables lounged at corners in their new-buckled shoes. On the corner by the King's Arms, a knot of townsfolk stood reading a bill freshly pasted to the board, and Silas drifted close enough to see it by the light of the inn's lamps.

The trial calendar. Three days of business. Poachers Tuesday morning. Forgers Tuesday afternoon. And on Wednesday, in the second court, before the Honourable Justice Harrow: *Marie Aubry, widow, of this parish, for the wilful murder of—*

He did not read the rest. He had already begun to walk, faster and faster, past the garlands and the swept stones, back toward Tanner's Lane and the lit window of Whitcombe's workshop, because there was a decision assembling itself inside him the way a train of wheels assembles — tooth finding tooth finding tooth — and it needed a bench to be built on, and it needed building tonight.

Behind him, unnoticed, at a distance no honest walker keeps, a figure in drab wool left the shadow of the King's Arms sign and followed him down into the dark.
