## 7. Whitcombe's Debt

The debt had a date, the way all debts do. It was the fourteenth of October, 1783, and it had come to Ezekiel Whitcombe in the shape of a boy standing in his doorway with rain running off his chin and a parcel wrapped in oilcloth held against his chest as though the contents might drown.

He had known, before the boy spoke a word, whose child he was looking at. The face gave it away — not the features so much as the set of them, that particular stubborn angle of jaw that Silas Delacroix had worn when he argued politics over Whitcombe's supper table in the years when argument had still been safe. Ten years of friendship, of shared ale and borrowed books, and then the presses, and then the pamphlets with names on them that made magistrates reach for their hats, and then — nothing. A man gone into the night ahead of the constables, leaving behind a wife who lasted two winters more and a son nobody wanted to claim.

"I've nowhere else," the boy said. He was eleven, perhaps twelve; hunger made it hard to tell. "They said at the parish you take apprentices."

Whitcombe had taken apprentices, once. He had one journeyman now, half-trained, and a workshop above a chandler's shop that smelled perpetually of tallow and brass filings, and a business that limped along on watch repairs and the goodwill of three merchants who paid late. The parish officers said *you take apprentices* because they said it hopefully, the way men say things they do not believe.

"They said right," Whitcombe heard himself answer. "Come in out of the wet."

* * *

It is a strange arithmetic, the keeping of another man's child. Whitcombe had done the sums often enough since, usually at night, usually after the second pipe, and the figures never came out comfortable.

The first cost was the room. He had slept in the back of the workshop itself, among the lathes and the great wheel of an unfinished turret clock that had occupied him for six years and would occupy him for six more. He built a partition, bought a truckle bed from a widow on Cutler Street, and moved his own cot into the cold corner by the door where the draught came under the sill like a patient animal. His hands, which had never ached in forty years, began to ache that winter and never entirely stopped. He did not mention this either.

The second cost was subtler. A man who takes in the son of a fugitive printer takes in the father too, whether he wills it or not. Whitcombe had burned nothing — that was the point of him, that he burned nothing — but he understood what he was holding. Somewhere in the boy's parcel of oilcloth there were papers; he never asked what. Somewhere in the boy's silences there were questions about a father; he never asked those either. He simply fed the child, taught him the feel of a pivot between thumb and forefinger, and waited for the years to do what years do, which is to bury a thing deep enough that it stops bleeding.

There had been a third cost, though Whitcombe rarely let himself count it. There had been a woman — Margaret Hobbes, a glover's daughter with ink-stained fingers and a laugh like a hinge that needed no oil — who had looked at him across a market stall in the spring of '84 and asked, in her direct way, whether he meant to spend his whole life married to clocks. He had answered honestly: yes, and now also to an apprentice. She had considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything, and had said she would think on it, and then her father's trade failed and the family went north to Leeds, and Whitcombe told himself the timing was coincidence and mostly believed it. He wrote to her once. He kept the draft unsent in a drawer with his dead wife's thimble, and some evenings he took it out and read the first line — *Margaret, you asked me once if I meant* — and put it back, because the letter was ten years old and the woman was married in Leeds and the sentence would never have a second line.

Silas never knew. That was the design of the whole enterprise, Whitcombe reflected: love expressed as silence, loyalty performed as ordinary routine. The boy thought he'd been given a trade. What he'd been given was a life, assembled quietly from the parts of another man's.

* * *

And now, nine years on, there was a girl in his workshop.

Whitcombe had noticed her the way he noticed most things — slowly, then completely. He had come down early on the morning after the knocking-at-the-door, meaning to bank the fire, and had seen the small shape curled in the corner behind the bench where they kept the packing straw: a child of seven or eight, asleep sitting up with her arms around her knees, dark hair falling across a face too thin for its years. And he had seen Silas standing over her with a candle, wearing the expression of a man who has opened a letter he cannot unopen.

Neither of them had spoken. Silas had looked at him — a long look, the look of a boy caught stealing, except that the theft was a human being — and Whitcombe had picked up the poker, stirred the coals, and said only, "She'll want something warm when she wakes. There's milk if we're careful with it."

That was three days ago. In three days he had learned her name — Marguerite, though she said it shyly, as if names could be confiscated — and had learned to set a third place at meals without comment and to buy bread in quantities that required no explanation to the baker. In three days he had watched Silas carry the observatory commission like a man carrying water in cupped hands, terrified of spilling, and had watched the same boy memorize something off a scrap of paper and burn the paper in the stove, and had said nothing about that either.

But he knew what was coming. You cannot keep a child hidden in a town the size of theirs, not with royalist agents sniffing after printers' ghosts and judges arriving for assizes week. Whitcombe was sixty-one years old and he had survived by understanding exactly how much trouble a man can hold in a room without the room noticing. The room was beginning to notice.

So on the fourth evening, while Silas bent over the merchant's watch and Marguerite sat cross-legged on the floor sorting screws by size with the grave diligence of a child given her first real work, Whitcombe took out his pipe and spoke to the fire rather than to either of them.

"My brother Thomas had a saying," he said. "He said a debt isn't what you owe a man. It's what you'd do if he came to your door again."

Silas's file stopped moving. "Sir?"

"Nothing." Whitcombe drew on the pipe. "Mind the escapement. You'll want light tomorrow."

He said nothing about the girl. He said nothing about the letter he was nearly certain existed — Silas had been walking like a man with stones in his pockets for three days, and Whitcombe had buried enough secrets to know the weight of one being carried. He said nothing about the fact that when Cade's men had passed the window that afternoon, slow as cats, he had moved himself deliberately between the glass and the corner where the child sat sorting screws, an old man's body offered up as a screen without ceremony or mention.

Some debts you pay in coin. Some you pay in silence, daily, for nine years, and then once more when it matters.

Above the chandler's shop, in the drawer with the unsent letter, the first line still began *Margaret, you asked me once if I meant* — and downstairs, the girl named for someone's lost Margaret sorted silver screws into a saucer, and Silas worked past midnight on a clock that had to be perfect, and outside in the street a man in a grey coat paused beneath the window, listened to the ticking of forty watches through the shutters, and walked on.
