## 9. Cade Searches the Workshop

Silas

The knock came at half past nine in the morning, which was somehow worse than midnight. Midnight was when conspirators knocked. Nine in the morning was when the law did.

Silas had been filing a pallet arbor for the observatory's remontoire, and the rasp stopped in his hand as though the file itself had heard something his ears were still denying. Across the bench, Marguerite looked up from the tray of screws she'd been sorting — she'd appointed herself to that task three days ago, with the grave authority of a child who understood that her survival depended on being useful and invisible in equal measure.

The knock came again. Three blows, unhurried, spaced like the ticks of a clock that knew exactly how much time remained.

"Through the back," Silas breathed. "Now."

But even as he said it, he knew the back gave onto a yard walled on two sides, and beyond the wall, the lane where any watcher could stand and wait as patiently as a spider. He had seen the man yesterday — he admitted it to himself only now, in the way one admits a debt long avoided — a grey-coated figure across from the observatory, apparently studying the sundial, apparently for an hour. Cade. The name had come to him on the evening air outside the King's Arms, spoken by ostlers who did not know they spoke of death: *Cade, the agent — quiet one, pays in guineas, asks after printers.*

"Seditions printers" — that phrase too, drifting up through the floorboards of the taproom like smoke through a crack. They were hunting Delacroix. And a printer's letter lay folded in oilcloth inside the false bottom of Silas's tool chest not four feet from where Marguerite stood.

The knock came a third time, and with it a voice, smooth as tallow: "Master Pemberton. I can hear you moving. Shall we dispense with the theatre?"

Marguerite's eyes were enormous. She did not cry out; she had already learned, at seven years old, that crying out was a luxury of children whose mothers had not been taken. That understanding broke something in Silas even as it steadied him.

"The clock case," he whispered, pointing to the great regulator standing against the north wall, its case open for the fitting of new chime barrels, its interior a cathedral of dark walnut and brass. "In behind the movement. Breathe small. Whatever happens — whatever you hear — you do not move until I say your name twice. *Twice.* Do you understand?"

She nodded once, and then, before climbing in, she pressed the sorted screws flat into their compartments with both hands so they would not rattle — a small act of care that nearly undid him — and disappeared into the dark of the case like water closing over a stone.

Silas slid the case door to within a finger's width of shut, threw his coat over the chest that held the letter, and opened the street door wearing what he hoped was the face of an interrupted craftsman: mild annoyance over a thin lake of fear.

* * *

Marguerite

Inside the clock it smelled of beeswax and old wood and, faintly, of the lemon oil Master Pemberton rubbed into the case, and Marguerite thought: *this is what a church smells like, if God were smaller and made of gears.*

She fitted herself into the space beneath the going train, knees to her chest, cheek against a shelf board that still held the ghost-shape of a removed wheel. Through the gap left by the door — a finger's width of lamplight, no more — she watched the room lie sideways and upside down, and she counted.

Counting was what Papa had taught her before everything else went away. *When you are frightened, ma petite, count the seconds between the things that scare you.* Between the knock and the opening of the door: eleven seconds. Between the opening of the door and the first footstep: four.

Then the man came into view, or rather his boots did — black boots, very clean, with silver buckles that caught the lamp. Above the boots came a coat the colour of wet ash, and above that, eventually, a face that was not frightening at all, which was the most frightening thing about it. It was a kind face. A face for asking directions. His eyes moved over the workshop the way she imagined a fish moved through water: without hurry, without anywhere it could not go.

"You are Pemberton." Not a question. "The apprentice. Whitcombe's boy."

"I'm Silas Pemberton, sir."

"Cade." The man did not offer a hand. He offered instead a paper, unfolded with two fingers, holding it at arm's length as if it might be damp. "A warrant. You may read it; I know apprentices read."

Silas read it aloud, because his voice needed employment: "*Warrant to search the premises known as the sign of the Dial, in Candlewick Row, for seditious print, presses, types, and papers thereunto belonging—*" His voice snagged on the word *papers* and he cleared his throat and finished.

"Seditious print," Cade repeated pleasantly, removing his hat and setting it crown-down on Whitcombe's best bench as though he had been invited to stay a fortnight. "Which is a broad net, Mr. Pemberton. Almanacs have been called seditious. Sermons. A child's primer, once, in York — the alphabet was deemed inflammatory." He smiled as though sharing a joke. "You will forgive me if my net is broad. Broad nets catch narrow fish."

He turned over the first bench.

Marguerite felt the sound in her teeth — wood striking boards, drawers pulled and dropped, the bright scatter of tools across flags. Her whole body wanted to bolt, and she held herself the way Papa had taught her to hold her breath under the mill pond: *small, small, be nothing, be a stone.* She counted the seconds between each crash. Four. Seven. Three. The counting kept her soul in her body.

Through the gap she saw the man lift down jars of oil and hold them to the light. He unstoppered one and smelled it. He ran a fingertip along the underside of shelves, checking, she understood dimly, for dust that had been disturbed — for hiding places recently opened. This was a man who searched the way other men ate: thoroughly, and every day.

And then he turned toward the clock.

* * *

Silas

Every instinct Silas possessed screamed at him to speak, to move, to put himself bodily between Cade and the case — but he knew with a craftsman's certainty that sudden motion draws the eye the way a drawn bow draws the arrow. So he stayed where he stood, wrench in hand, and let his mouth run ahead of his terror.

"That's the observatory regulator," he said. "Commission work. If you damage it, sir, you'll answer to the Royal Society, and they answer to nobody."

Cade paused, hand flat against the walnut. "Is that a threat?"

"It's arithmetic." Thank God for Whitcombe's training; thank God for the shopkeeper's dull courage of a man defending property. "The escapement is stripped for repair. The plates are off. There is nowhere in that case but gears."

Cade considered this, and then, maddeningly, he smiled and opened the case anyway.

The door swung wide. Lamplight poured into the interior like water into a lock, and Silas saw — or believed he saw, or died a small death seeing — the shadow of a child curled beneath the strike train, motionless as cast lead. Cade bent. His head went into the case to the shoulders. His breath stirred the pendulum, which began a slow, soft swing, tick... tick..., and the sound of it filled the shop like a heartbeat that belonged to someone else.

"A fine piece," Cade said, voice hollowed by the wood. "Whose design?"

"Graham's pattern. My own finishing." Say something true, Silas told himself. Liars falter; craftsmen drone. "The deadbeat escapement is re-cut. The chime barrels are being replaced. It is, at present, a box full of parts."

"A box full of parts." Cade withdrew, straightening, and his eyes — pale eyes, patient as frost — travelled from the case to Silas's face and rested there with an attention that had weight, like a hand laid flat on a table. "You have ink on your fingers, Mr. Pemberton."

Silas looked at his hands. Oil, yes — black oil, ground into the whorls, indistinguishable at a glance from printer's ink. He had never loved his trade more than in that moment.

"I'm a clockmaker," he said. "Everything in this shop is black."

For a moment — a long moment, long enough for the pendulum to swing seven times, Marguerite would tell him later, she had counted — Cade said nothing at all. Then he laughed, softly, and it was almost a genuine laugh, and that was worse.

"Yes," he said. "I suppose it is."

* * *

Marguerite

The man took the shop apart the way a storm takes apart a hedge.

Marguerite counted everything, because counting was the rope she climbed down into the dark and the rope she climbed back up. She counted forty-one tools set on the floor. She counted the drawers of Whitcombe's desk, all eight, all emptied, the ledgers fanned open and thumbed. She counted the seconds the man spent reading a page of Whitcombe's handwriting — nineteen — and decided that masters wrote in a way that bored soldiers.

Once, his boot came down six inches from the tool chest with the false bottom. Six inches. She could have measured it. Inside that chest lay the letter with her papa's writing, the letter that named the judge, the letter that had brought all this weather to their door — and she understood, with a clarity that frightened her more than the searching did, that if the man found it, Master Silas would hang, and she did not fully understand why, but she understood it the way she understood that fire burns: early, completely, and forever.

So she did the bravest thing she had ever done, braver than running, braver than hiding: she stayed still. When a screw slipped somewhere near her knee — one of her own sorted screws, betrayed by her own careful hands — and began a tiny ringing roll toward the front of the shelf, she caught it between two fingers in the darkness, silent, and closed her other hand around it, and held it until her fingers ached.

The man's head passed within a hand's breadth of hers. She could smell pomade and horse and, beneath it, something like vinegar. His eyes, when they swept past the gap in the door, were the colour of the sky before snow, and they saw nothing, because she was not there. She was a stone. She was a gear. She was part of the machine, and machines do not breathe, except she was breathing, small and shallow, counting: *one, two, three, four.*

*Please don't find me,* she did not pray, because Mama had said God was busy in 1793. *Please don't find me, please don't find me, please.*

The head withdrew.

* * *

Silas

Cade worked for the better part of two hours. He was not a ransacker; that was the horror of him. He lifted, examined, replaced. He measured the workshop with his eyes as a surveyor measures a field, and Silas understood that even if today found nothing, the measurements would keep. Every visit after this would be shorter, and more certain.

Near the end, he drifted to the window and stood looking out at Candlewick Row with his hands clasped behind him, and asked, conversationally:

"Do you know a printer? A Frenchman. Delacroix."

The name fell into Silas like a stone into a well, and he listened — helplessly, honestly — for the splash. Ten years since he'd heard that name spoken aloud. Ten years since Whitcombe had sat him down at this same bench, aged thirteen, and said *your father has gone away, lad, and it is better for everyone that we speak of it rarely,* and Silas had obeyed, had buried the name so deep that hearing it now was like hearing his own childhood pronounced seditious.

"No, sir."

"His daughter was left in this town some months ago. A girl of about seven. Dark hair. Speaks with a little accent, they say." Cade turned from the window. "One does wonder where a child like that goes."

Silas felt the lie assemble itself out of spare parts, the way he assembled anything: from what lay to hand. "Half the town has accents, sir. We're a port road."

"Mm." Cade retrieved his hat, set it on his head, and regarded the wrecked shop with something almost like regret — a gentleman's regret, costless. "You'll forgive the disorder. Duty is a blunt instrument; one tries to wield it finely." At the door he paused, and delivered, gently, the sentence Silas would carry into sleep for weeks afterward:

"We will meet again, Mr. Pemberton. Men who hide things always require a second visit. It is practically a law of nature." He tapped the doorframe twice, companionably. "Mind the girl, if she exists. Children leave prints — little oily fingerprints, on cups, on windows, on clock cases. Very hard to wipe away entirely. Like ink."

The door closed. Boots receded down Candlewick Row, unhurried, spaced evenly, like the ticks of a clock that knew exactly how much time remained.

Silas waited until the footsteps faded, then until a cart passed, then until he could bear it no longer. He crossed the floor, put his mouth to the gap in the case, and whispered, "Marguerite. Marguerite."

*Twice. He'd said twice.*

The case door creaked, and she unfolded out of the dark like a paper flower put in water — stiff, trembling, alive — with one small screw clenched so tightly in her fist that it had printed its thread into her palm. She looked up at him, and her chin was steady, and her eyes were not, and she said, in a whisper scraped raw:

"He was reading Papa's name. I counted how long. Nineteen seconds for Whitcombe, but for Papa—" she swallowed "—for Papa he didn't read at all. He already knew it by heart."

Outside, far down the row, faint but distinct, a bell began to ring the half hour, and Silas stood in the wreckage of the shop with the child's hot hand in his, understanding that the search had found nothing and had therefore found everything — that Cade had not needed the letter to know the shape of it, and that the next knock would not be polite.

They had days. Perhaps hours. And somewhere in the cells below the assize court, a woman was waiting on a trial that would not wait for them at all.
