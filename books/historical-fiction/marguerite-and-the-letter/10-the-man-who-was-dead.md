## 10. The Man Who Was Dead

The letter's second instruction was written smaller than the first, as though its author had hesitated even in ink.

*If the first cannot be done — if fear is greater than duty — then come to me. Bindery of Ash & Reed, at Marlow Staithe, on the coast road east. Ask for the bookbinder Reed and say only: the clock at the observatory has stopped. He will understand.*

Silas read it eleven times by candlelight while Marguerite slept curled in the blanket chest he had lined with sacking, her breath a small tide in the dark. *Come to me.* Not *go* — *come*, as though the writer knew where Silas would be standing when he read it, knew him across ten years and one faked grave. The handwriting crawled up his arm like something living. He knew it better than his own. He had learned his letters copying it, tracing the downstrokes of capital D's until Whitcombe caught him at it and said gently, *Your father wrote a fine hand, boy, but a hand can write lies as easily as prayers.*

He had believed for ten years that the man was dead. There had been a coffin, or the shape of one — Whitcombe had paid for it, Silas learned later, paid for a burial that held no body because there had been no body to hold. A printer fled from a sedition charge did not get funerals; he got rumours. The story Whitcombe told the parish was fever, quick and shameful, and the town, which had liked Delacroix well enough but not well enough to ask questions, let the story lie down in the ground and put a stone over it. Silas had been nine. He had wept into Whitcombe's coat and been glad, even then, in some small dishonest chamber of himself, that the shame of the name was buried too.

Now the dead man had an address.

* * *

He told no one he was going. That was the hardest part — harder than lying to Quill about the escapement, harder than the hour before dawn when he lifted Marguerite from the chest and she woke and looked at him with those grey, unchildlike eyes and asked nothing.

"I must go out," he whispered. "Two days. Perhaps three."

"Where?"

"To find someone." He could not make the word fit his mouth: *father*. It sat in him like a gear with a stripped tooth, turning and catching, turning and catching.

Marguerite considered him with the gravity of a much older creature wearing a child's face, which was itself a kind of disguise. "Is it the person from the letter?"

"Yes."

She nodded once, as though that settled it, and lay back down. At the door he paused. She spoke again from the dark, so quietly he almost missed it:

"Don't forget mine either."

*Forget my face.* The woman at the door, seven nights ago, hooded, desperate, pressing the letter into his hands. And now the child asking to be remembered by the same word. He stood a moment with his hand on the latch, unable to answer, then went out into the cold.

* * *

Marlow Staithe was two days' journey by the carrier's cart and a long walk besides: herring country, gull-haunted, the air tasting of salt and tar. Silas arrived at dusk with his boots ruined, his coat smelling of fish crates, and forty miles of rehearsed speeches dying in his throat.

The bindery stood apart from the staithe proper, up a lane where the cobbles gave way to mud, a long low building of grey stone with a slate roof and windows glowing amber. He smelled it before he saw it clearly — hide glue and leather and the sharp vinegary breath of fresh print, a smell that went through him like a blade through cloth, because it was the smell of his earliest childhood, of a workshop above a printing house, of big hands lifting him onto a stool and saying, *careful, this press has teeth.*

*Ash & Reed*. Even the name was a coward's joke. Delacroix — *of the cross* — hiding behind ash and reed, the humblest materials going.

He stood outside the door for the better part of a quarter hour. Inside, someone moved between benches; he heard the soft slap of boards, the scrape of a stone weighting paper. His heart performed against his ribs like a badly regulated train of wheels — too fast, uneven, threatening to stop entirely. He had imagined this meeting a hundred ways across forty miles. In none of them could he make the man's face.

He knocked.

The movement inside stopped. A long silence, long enough for Silas to count his own pulse. Then footsteps, unhurried, and the door opened on a chain — no, on a bar drawn back slowly, cautiously, by a man who had kept the habit of caution for ten years and wore it like clothing.

"You're late," said his father. "I expected you the first week."

* * *

The interior was warm and close and orderly in the way of workshops run by frightened men: everything squared, everything to hand, every tool in its place so that a sudden departure could happen in under a minute. Silas noted it professionally, helplessly — the packed go-bag by the rear door, the loose board over what might be a cache, the window facing the marsh rather than the road. A man who had built his life around leaving quickly.

His father closed the door, shot the bar, and turned.

Ten years had silvered him. The black hair Silas remembered was iron-grey at the temples, tied back plainly; the broad hands were scarred with knife-work, binder's scars instead of printer's. But the eyes were unchanged — dark, quick, and full of a calculation that never stopped running, and looking into them Silas felt himself split cleanly in two: the child who wanted to be held, and the craftsman who saw the mechanism of the man and understood, for the first time, how it worked. Both halves spoke at once, and neither was heard.

"You got my letter," Delacroix said. It was not a question.

"The woman left it. With a child." Silas heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "Her daughter. Seven years old. You sent your daughter to a stranger's door in the middle of the night with soldiers in the street, and your instruction was — what was it — *forget her face*."

"It was the only way. Anyone seen carrying that letter dies. A child is invisible. I made sure of the street, the timing—"

"You made sure of nothing. You weren't there."

"No," his father said simply. "I wasn't. That is what being a wanted man means, Silas. You do everything at one remove. You love through intermediaries." He moved past to the fire, stirred it, and Silas watched the scarred hands and hated how familiar they were. "You came. Good. Sit. We have little time."

"We have ten years," Silas said, and did not sit.

Delacroix stopped stirring. For a moment — only a moment — the calculation failed, and underneath it Silas glimpsed something raw and exhausted, a man holding up a ceiling with both arms. Then it sealed over again.

"Yes," his father said quietly. "We have ten years. And I will give you all of them, every hour you want, if you'll hear the important part first. So sit down, son, because I am going to tell you why you should take that letter and burn it."

* * *

He talked for a long time, pacing the length of his bindery, and Silas listened and mended watches in his head to keep from screaming, a habit of concentration Whitcombe had taught him without knowing its future use.

Harrow. The judge. Ten years ago Harrow had been not yet a judge but a commissioner of inquiry, and Delacroix's press had printed things he needed buried — accounts of confiscated estates, names of men who had sold confiscation papers to themselves, and worst of all a deposition concerning the death of a printer's wife named Aubry, strangled in her own shop while her husband hid in the cellar with the forms of the next edition under his arms. Harrow had ordered the raid. Harrow had been there. Whether his own hands or another's, the deposition did not say; it said only enough to hang him, and Delacroix had printed three copies and hidden them, and Harrow's men had burned the rest, and the widow Aubry's friend — a woman named Celeste Aubry, no relation, who had sheltered the husband after — had taken the charge in his place when the hunt came up empty.

"And she stands trial in five days," Silas said. "For a murder your testimony could stop."

"My testimony." His father smiled, and it was terrible, because it was the smile Silas knew from childhood, the one that preceded a trick. "My testimony would hang four men, Silas, and only one of them wears chains. Do you know what happens to crown's evidence in these years? Nothing good. Do you know what happens to a man caught harboring seditious print, twice? The rope. They don't even bother with a trial for the second offense — there's a magistrate in this county who owes Harrow his seat, and I'd be dead within the month, and my death would prove nothing except that I existed, which is precisely the thing I have spent ten years proving otherwise."

"So Mrs. Aubry hangs instead."

"If it comes to that. It may not. Letters are fragile things — Lisle or whoever holds it will find it can be dismissed as forgery, and likely will. A dead man's accusation is worth nothing. That is why the letter says to burn it, Silas. That is the instruction I meant most."

"The letter gives two instructions. Burn it, or carry it to the assizes. It doesn't say *find the author and ask his permission*."

"Because I assumed you'd have sense." Delacroix's voice rose, cracked, dropped. "You're Whitcombe's boy. He taught you patience. He taught you that a mechanism survives by staying hidden, by doing its work inside the case where no one sees it move—"

"He taught me that a clock that won't show the time is worthless!"

The silence afterward was very long. Somewhere outside a gull screamed and was answered. Delacroix stood with his back half-turned, one hand resting on the bench among the bone folders and the awls, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and level and more frightening than any shouting.

"You don't know what you're asking. You think this ends with a trial. It ends with Cade. You've met Cade's work, even if you haven't met Cade — he's the reason the woman ran through the night instead of walking in daylight, he's the reason I live behind a bar on my own door. If I stand up in open court and say the name Delacroix, Cade does not wait for process. He burns first and files after. Your master's shop. The girl. You. Everyone who ever bought a watch from Pemberton's bench. Is that the truth you want to buy, and with whose coin?"

"Mine," Silas said. "It should be mine. It's been mine since I was nine years old and you let me bury an empty box."

That struck home. He saw it land — saw his father's shoulders change, saw the ten-year architecture of the man shudder like a wall taking a blow it was not braced for.

"I did it to keep you alive," Delacroix said, and now his voice was not level at all. "Do you understand that? Every lie, every disappearance, the grave, the new name, all of it — I chose that you should grieve rather than hang. Grief is survivable, Silas. I checked. I've been checking for ten years."

"And I'm the proof."

"And I'm the proof," his father agreed, and for one unguarded instant he looked old, and mortal, and sorry — looked, in fact, like the man from the memories, the one who had lifted him onto stools. Then the shutter came down, because it always came down, because ten years is long enough to build a wall into the bones. "No. I won't testify. Find another way if you must — find the advocate, find the deposition copies, God help you — but you will not spend my life to buy yours, and you will not bring that child anywhere near this coast. Go home, finish the clock, burn the letter, and forget my face. It seems to be the family instruction."

* * *

Silas walked out into the dark because staying meant striking him, and he was not certain which of them he wanted to strike.

The lane smelled of salt and rotting weed. Behind him the bindery windows glowed, and he stood in the mud looking at them and understood suddenly, completely, the shape of his life: he was a repairman. It was all he had ever been. He repaired watches, he repaired clocks, he had spent seven days repairing a stranger's escapement while memorizing a dead man's letter — and here, at last, stood the broken piece no one had ever let him at, the one mechanism in the world he was not permitted to fix, because fixing it required dismantling the whole works, and the whole works included a man who preferred to run wrong forever rather than be opened up in front of witnesses.

He thought of Marguerite in the blanket chest, asking to be remembered. He thought of Whitcombe counting coins by candlelight. He thought of a woman in the cells at the assize town whose name he did not yet know, sleeping her last free nights without knowing the man who could save her was alive and binding books forty miles away, refusing.

He thought of the clock. Of the punched holes he had found in the third plate, matching the pinpricks along the letter's margin, which he had told no one about, which his father did not know he had found.

His father did not know. Which meant his father did not know everything the clock contained.

"Reed," Silas said aloud to the empty lane, trying the false name like a tool of the wrong size. Then, louder, toward the lit window, using the true one for the first time in ten years and feeling the air change: "Delacroix."

Inside, a shadow crossed the glass and froze.

"There's more in that clock than your letter," Silas called. "You hid names in the plates, didn't you. You hid them years ago, before you ran — and you've forgotten what else you put there. But I haven't. I've read every hole."

A long silence. Then the bar scraped back, and the door opened, and his father stood in the lamplight with a face Silas had never seen on him before — the face of a man watching his own mechanism fail.

"What," Delacroix said slowly, "else did I put there?"
