## 11. An Innocent Woman in the Cells

Marguerite had learned the workshop's sounds the way other children learned prayers. The tick of the regulator on the back wall, steady as a heartbeat. The whisper of Silas's file against brass. The groan of the floorboard by the door that Whitcombe had promised to fix and never would. She kept her own small corner now, between the coal scuttle and the shelf of oil pots, with a stool too tall for her and a rag of her very own for polishing the cases that came in dull and left shining.

She was not supposed to be there. She knew this the way she knew the shape of her own teeth — with her tongue, constantly, secretly. But nobody had sent her away either, and so she stayed, quiet as dust, and watched Silas work, and waited for the hours when he forgot himself enough to talk.

It was Quill who changed everything.

Mr. Quill came on a Tuesday, when the fog off the river had turned the window glass grey as pewter. He was the astronomer from the observatory, a long loose-limbed man whose coat sleeves were always too short and whose pockets were always full of things that clinked. Marguerite liked him immediately, because he looked at her directly when he spoke, which almost no one did. Adults had developed the habit of looking slightly above her head, or slightly through her, as though she were a draft they preferred not to catch.

"Miss Pemberton," he said gravely, inclining his head to her as though she were a duchess, and she felt herself go pink with pleasure at the surname, borrowed though it was.

"She's not—" Silas began.

"I know perfectly well who she is," Quill said, "and I have known for three weeks, and I have told no one, because I am not a fool." He set his hat on the bench and produced from his pocket a paper twist of boiled sweets, which he offered to Marguerite with the solemnity of a man presenting a treaty. "Lemon. From the apothecary on Bridge Street. He waters them down, but beggars and astronomers cannot choose."

Marguerite took one and held it in her cheek without sucking, making it last.

Quill had come about the clock, of course. He always came about the clock. But today he lingered after the business was done — the report of the escapement, the parts still wanted, the days remaining until the commission must be fulfilled or forfeited — and stood turning his hat in his hands, and did not leave.

"Silas," he said at last. "There is something you ought to hear. Not for any reason. Only — a man hears a thing and cannot carry it alone."

Whitcombe was out delivering the mended chronometer to a ship's chandler, and the workshop was theirs, and Marguerite sat very still on her stool with the sweet going smaller and smaller under her tongue.

"They've taken up Mrs. Aubry," Quill said. "You'll have seen the bills. *The widow Aubry, charged with the wilful murder of—*"

"I've seen them," Silas said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the way it did when he was measuring something very fine. "I don't read them."

"No," said Quill. "I thought not. Well." He cleared his throat. "The printer Delacroix was murdered last year — that much is known, whatever else is hushed. His wife died before him, years before. And Mrs. Aubry was her friend. Close as sisters, they say. When the constables went looking for someone to hang the murder on, they went to the dead woman's friend, because it is easier than going anywhere near the truth."

"Who is the truth?" Marguerite asked.

The question came out of her before she could weigh it, the way questions always did. Both men turned to look at her. Quill's face did something complicated; Silas's face did nothing at all, which frightened her more.

"That," Quill said gently, "is rather the point of trials, miss. To find out."

"And if the trial doesn't look?"

Silence. The regulator ticked into it, forty times, fifty times, while Quill studied his hat and Silas studied the bench.

"The assizes sit Monday," Quill said finally. "Three days hence. Judge Harrow presides." He put his hat on. At the door he paused, and Marguerite saw that his hand was not quite steady on the latch. "I liked her, you know. Mrs. Aubry. She used to bring bread to the observatory on cold nights when I was keeping the transit observations alone. She asked me once how far away the stars were, and when I told her, she laughed — not because it was funny, but because she was glad of it. Glad there was something farther away than her troubles." He looked back at them both. "A person like that didn't kill anybody. That's all I came to say. A person like that didn't kill anybody, and on Monday we will all stand by and watch them hang her anyway, because watching is what we're for."

The door closed behind him. The fog crept a little way in across the sill and lay there like something drowned.

* * *

That night Marguerite slept in the loft above the workshop, on a pallet that smelled of hemp and lamp oil, and she did not sleep at all.

She thought about mothers. It was a thing she did sometimes, in the dark, the way other children counted sheep: she laid out her memories of her mother like cards on a table and turned them over one by one, checking that none had faded. Her hands kneading dough, flour to the wrists. Her voice reading aloud, stumbling over Latin words in the printed sheets she brought home wrapped around the bread. The smell of ink and yeast together, which even now, whenever she caught it anywhere, made her chest ache in a way that was not entirely pain.

Her mother had been taken away in a judge's carriage. She remembered the wheels on the cobbles, the horses' breath steaming, the man inside the carriage with his face half in shadow and half in the light of their one candle — a heavy face, patient the way a cat is patient. She had been very small. She had hidden in the flour bin because her mother told her to, and through the crack of the lid she had watched, and she had not made a sound, because her mother had said *not a sound, my darling, not one*, and she had obeyed.

She had never told anyone about the face. There had been no one to tell. There had only been the hooded woman, and the doorstep, and the words *forget my face*, and then Silas's frightened eyes in the dark of the workshop, and then years of being small and quiet and forgotten on purpose.

But she had not forgotten the face. She had not forgotten anything. Children were supposed to forget — everyone said so, everyone counted on it — but Marguerite's memory was a strongbox, and every terrible thing she had ever seen sat inside it, polished and exact.

And now they were going to hang Mrs. Aubry for what that patient-faced man had done.

She got up in the dark and climbed down the loft ladder, barefoot, one rung at a time, and crossed the workshop floor to where Silas slept in the cot behind the screen. He slept badly these days; she knew because she listened. Tonight he was awake already. She could tell by his breathing — the shallow, listening kind.

"Silas," she whispered.

He sat up at once, no surprise in it. "What is it? Are you ill?"

"I know about the letter."

The silence that followed was so total she could hear the fog pressing on the windows. When he spoke again his voice was strange, stripped down to its frame. "What do you mean, you know about it?"

"I know there is a letter. I know you read it over and over when you think I'm asleep — your lips move, and you hold it near the candle, and then you burn the candle stub after so the wax won't show. I know you copied it out once and hid the copy inside the hollow leg of Whitcombe's good chair. I found it months ago. I can't read very well yet, but I'm learning, and I read some." She took a breath. "I know it says Judge Harrow killed my papa. I know it's in my papa's handwriting. I know you know a man who isn't dead wrote it."

"Marguerite—"

"You knew all along." Her whisper cracked in the middle. "All those weeks you knew, and you let me be nobody, and you let Mrs. Aubry—"

"I don't know that Mrs. Aubry has anything to do with us. With it. With any of it." He swung his legs out of the cot and sat facing her, and even in the dark she could see how young his face was without its working expression, how tired underneath. "Listen to me. If that letter comes out of hiding, people die. Not metaphorically. Cade is real. Harrow is real. They burned a press with a man inside it once, in this city, and called it an accident of the trade. You are alive because nobody knows you exist. That is not cowardice. That is arithmetic."

"Then the arithmetic is wrong," Marguerite said.

He stared at her.

"My mama hid me in a flour bin so I wouldn't see," she said, and her voice shook and she let it shake, because she was done being small and quiet. "I saw anyway. I remember anyway. Being hidden doesn't stop a person from knowing things. It just stops the things from mattering to anybody else." She wiped her nose with the heel of her hand. "Mr. Quill says she brought bread to the observatory on cold nights. Mr. Quill says she laughed about the stars. My mama used to wrap bread in printed pages. Maybe they knew each other. Maybe Mrs. Aubry is the only person left alive besides me who remembers my mama laughing. And we're going to let them hang her so that nothing has to change."

"It's three days," Silas said hoarsely. "The trial is in three days. What do you imagine we can do in three days? I haven't finished the clock. Whitcombe hasn't been paid since Michaelmas. If I go to Lisle or the assizes or anywhere with this, the workshop burns, and Whitcombe with it — he took me in when I was nobody, Marguerite, when my father ran and left me standing in his doorway like a parcel nobody claimed. I owe him—"

"You owe him true things," Marguerite said. "Not comfortable ones."

She hadn't meant to say it. The words came out of the strongbox too, from somewhere deep where her mother's voice lived, saying *not a sound, my darling* — except that her mother had also said other things. *One day you will speak, and when you speak, speak plainly.*

Silas flinched as if she'd struck him. In the dark she heard him breathe, twice, three times, the way he did when a spring fought him and he would not force it.

"You're seven years old," he said at last, helplessly.

"I'm eight in March."

"You're eight in March," he agreed, and put his face in his hands, and for a long moment the only sound was the regulator ticking, and the fog, and somewhere far off a dog giving one brief bark at nothing.

When he looked up, he did not look resolved. He looked like a man standing on ice and hearing it move beneath him.

"If I did anything," he said slowly, "it would have to be without Whitcombe knowing. It would have to be small. A copy — only a fragment — nothing that names Papa. Nothing that leads back here. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I'm not promising. I'm describing a thing I might fail to do."

"Yes," said Marguerite.

"And you would stay here. Behind the screen. Whatever happened, however long I was gone, you would stay."

"Yes," said Marguerite, who was already deciding, in the patient cat-quiet place at the bottom of her heart, exactly how long a promise like that could stretch.

* * *

In the morning Whitcombe came back from the chandler's with money actually in his pocket — two guineas, part of a debt eleven months old — and was so pleased about it that he bought sausages, and they ate breakfast like a family, all three of them, and Marguerite watched Silas not meet his master's eye across the table and understood that the secret had grown another wall.

After breakfast she polished cases. After the cases she practiced her letters on the slate Quill had given her, copying out words from the discarded bills of sale on the bench. After that she swept. And all the while, in the strongbox, the plan assembled itself piece by piece, the way Silas assembled a movement: this wheel here, this pivot there, each part fitted to its neighbor, until the whole thing would turn.

She knew the advocate's chambers were on Chancery Lane, because Quill talked. She knew Silas kept the copied fragment in the chair leg, and that Whitcombe went to the tavern on Thursday evenings and stayed past nine. She knew the fog came early off the river and that a child walking quickly in a hooded cloak was invisible to a town that had already agreed not to see her.

She knew, most of all, what her mother would have done. Her mother had wrapped truth in bread and carried it through streets full of soldiers. Compared with that, Chancery Lane was nothing at all.

Thursday came gray and cold. Whitcombe put on his good coat at dusk and pressed a penny into her hand — *for a bun, mouse, mind you lock the door behind me* — and his boots faded down the street. Across the lane, half-hidden by a doorway, a man in a drab greatcoat shifted his weight and settled in to watch the workshop, the way he had watched for three nights running.

Marguerite ate the bun. Then she took the hollow chair leg apart with the small screwdriver she'd stolen from Silas's bench a week ago, folded the fragment of the letter into the lining of her cloak where her mother's hands had taught her such things hide best, and pulled the hood up over her hair.

The lock, she had discovered, opened from the inside without a key if you knew which board lifted.

Outside, the fog received her like water closing over a stone.
