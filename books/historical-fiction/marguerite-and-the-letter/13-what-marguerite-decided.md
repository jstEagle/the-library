## 13. What Marguerite Decided

Marguerite had learned to count the hours by the sounds of the workshop, though she was not allowed to be in it while Silas worked. She counted them from the little room behind the shelves of clock parts, where Mrs. Whitcombe — dead these six years, but her chair still stood by the window — had once done the accounts. From there she could hear everything: the tick and tock of a dozen unfinished clocks, each keeping its own stubborn time; the scrape of Silas's file; the low murmur of Mr. Whitcombe's voice as he argued with some mechanism that would not obey him.

She had been counting for three days now. Three days since she had heard Quill's boy say, in the yard of the observatory, that Mrs. Aubry would hang on Thursday.

Marguerite did not know Mrs. Aubry. She knew only what the children had pieced together through Quill's kindness: that Mrs. Aubry had been her mother's friend, that she had taken in printed pages when the presses were seized, that she had been found with blood on her apron the night Marguerite's mother died, and that Judge Harrow himself would preside at her trial. The children said *hanged* the way they said *winter* or *fever* — something that came, that could not be argued with.

But Marguerite had seen the letter. She was not supposed to have seen it. She had watched Silas read it by candlelight on the second night, his face going grey as tallow, and after that he kept it folded inside his shirt, against his skin, like a thing that might hatch. But she had seen it. And three nights ago, when Silas slept at the bench with his head on his arms, she had taken it out and copied it.

Not all of it. Her hand was small and her letters were learned from a primer with a cracked spine, and the whole letter was too much. But the middle part — the part that named Judge Harrow, the part that said *he came to the shop at midnight and my wife let him in because he wore the face of a friend* — that part she copied twice over until it was true, until her copy looked almost like grown-up writing. She had hidden the copy inside the hollow foot of the old chair, wrapped in wax cloth against damp.

She told herself she was not going to do anything with it. She told herself this the way one tells oneself one is not going to touch a hot stove: knowing it all the while for a lie.

* * *

On Wednesday morning it rained, a fine persistent rain that turned the cobbles the colour of pewter and made the gutters run. Silas had gone up to the observatory before dawn — she heard him leave, heard Whitcombe grumble about the escapement, heard the door close. Whitcombe was asleep in his chair, his chin on his chest, an open ledger sliding off his knee. He had sat up half the night, she knew, doing sums that always ended badly.

Marguerite took out the copy.

It was very small in her hands, smaller than it had seemed when she wrote it. A scrap of paper with a child's careful letters. She thought: *no one will believe this.* And then she thought: *they don't have to believe me. They only have to believe it enough to ask questions.* That was what Silas was afraid of, she understood now — not that the letter was false, but that questions were dangerous, that asking them got people hanged or burned out or vanished into the night the way her mother had vanished. Silas carried his caution like the letter, folded against his heart.

But caution had not saved her mother. Caution had left Mrs. Aubry in a cell with three days left to live.

She put on her hooded cloak — it had belonged to the house, to some long-gone child, and its hem dragged — and she pushed the copy deep into her shoe, flat under her heel, because no one searching a child would look in her shoe. She had learned things in seven years that grown people forgot: where to hide, how to be small, how to walk past danger with your eyes down and your breath slow.

The advocate's chambers were in Chancery Lane. She knew this because she had listened — listening was the other thing she was good at — when Lisle's clerk had come to the observatory to speak with Quill, and Quill had spoken of him in the yard afterward, loudly, to no one in particular, the way careful men speak secrets they want overheard. *Lisle will want corroboration,* Quill had said. *Lisle will want more than paper.*

Well. Paper was what she had. It would have to begin somewhere.

* * *

The town in rain was a different country. The market stalls were shuttered or canopied, the sellers huddled under oilcloth, and the streets ran with water that caught the grey light and broke it. Marguerite walked quickly, head down, counting turnings the way Silas had taught her to count gear teeth — *twelve to the wheel, mind the ratio* — left at the sign of the Three Crowns, right past the chandler's, straight on until the lane narrowed and the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators.

Chancery Lane smelled of ink and wet stone. The advocate's chambers were above a stationer's, up a stair worn hollow in the middle, and the door bore a brass plate so tarnished she could barely read it: *T. LISLE, ATTORNEY.*

Her courage, which had carried her four streets like a lit candle, guttered at the bottom of those stairs.

She stood in the doorway out of the rain and made herself think of her mother's hands. This was strange, because she could not properly remember her mother's face anymore — the instruction *forget my face* had worked on her like a charm, and when she tried to summon it, it slid away like fish through fingers — but the hands she remembered. Hands floury from bread, ink-stained from helping at the press, one hand smoothing her hair back from her forehead. Hands that had wrapped a printed page around a loaf because paper was scarce and bread was hot.

A page that wrapped bread. A judge who wore the face of a friend. A woman in a cell who had only ever been guilty of friendship.

Marguerite climbed the stairs.

* * *

The clerk was a young man with ink to his elbows and the affronted expression of someone interrupted at copying. He looked at her the way one looks at weather.

"Advocate Lisle is engaged."

"I've brought him something," Marguerite said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "About the Aubry trial. Tomorrow."

That changed his face. She watched the word *tomorrow* land on him like a dropped weight. "Who sent you?"

"No one." She held out her hand, palm up, and waited. It was a trick she had learned from watching Silas with difficult customers: silence does more work than speech. The clerk looked at her small wet palm, then at her face, and something in him — curiosity, or boredom, or the simple human inability to resist a mystery delivered by a child in an outsized cloak — made him sigh and knock at the inner door.

Lisle was younger than she had imagined, narrow-faced, with quick grey eyes that went from her to the window to the street below and back again before he said a single word. He closed the shutters first. Then he said, "You'd better sit down," and she understood that he was a man who had learned to be afraid in useful ways.

She gave him the paper from her shoe. It was warm from her foot and slightly warped with damp, and she watched him smooth it flat on his desk and read it, and she watched his stillness change quality — from the stillness of a man humoring a child to the stillness of a man reading something that might hang him.

"Where did you get this?"

"It's copied. The original is safe." She had rehearsed that sentence on the walk and was proud of how it sounded. "I know the person who has it."

"The handwriting—" He turned the sheet toward the light. "This is a child's hand."

"Yes," said Marguerite. "I'm a child. I'm also the daughter of the printer's wife who was murdered, and I saw the judge's carriage outside our door that night, and nobody has asked me one single question about it in two years." Her voice cracked on the last words, which she hated, but perhaps the crack helped, because Lisle set the paper down very carefully, as if it were made of eggshell, and looked at her for a long moment.

"You understand," he said quietly, "that if this is a forgery, whoever produces it at trial will be transported, if they're lucky. And if it is genuine, the men who want it buried will not stop at transport."

"I know about Cade," said Marguerite. "He watches our street. He wears a grey coat and he pretends to look at the chandler's window but he never buys anything, and he stands where he can see both our door and the observatory gate."

Lisle's eyebrows rose a fraction. "How old are you?"

"Seven. Nearly eight."

"God help us all," said the advocate, but he said it softly, and he reached for a fresh sheet of paper, and he began, in a lawyer's compressed shorthand, to write.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way bad weather comes over a hill.

There was a noise on the stair — boots, two pairs, heavy, unhurried, the boots of men who had never once hurried for anyone — and Lisle blew out the candle that wasn't needed anyway and said, "Closet. Now," and Marguerite went, folding herself into the dark among hanging robes and a smell of camphor and old wig-powder, pulling the door to until a blade of light remained.

Through it she saw the door open without a knock. Two men entered. One was large and soft-spoken and wrong in a way she couldn't name; the other was Cade, in his grey coat, rain-beaded, carrying his politeness like a drawn knife.

"Mr. Lisle." Cade's voice was pleasant. "We're making inquiries regarding seditious print. A warrant, you'll find, quite in order." Papers were produced. Lisle read them slowly, insolently slowly, and Marguerite pressed her back against the closet wall and made her breathing small.

"You'll search, then," Lisle said. "Mind the ink pots. My clerk cries when they're spilled."

They searched. Drawers opened and shut. The large man handled everything as though it might bite him. Marguerite thought of the original letter folded against Silas's chest across the town, and of the copy lying on the desk not four feet from Cade's elbow, and her heart hammered so hard she was certain it must be audible, certain the whole closet thumped like a clock case.

Cade picked up the copy.

He read it. Marguerite watched his eyes move along the lines through the crack in the door, and she saw the moment the words landed — a small stillness, a tilt of the head, the particular attention of a hunter hearing a twig snap. He read it twice. Then he smiled, and the smile was the worst thing she had ever seen, because it was not angry. It was pleased.

"A child's exercise," Lisle said smoothly. "My clerk amuses himself. Half the town writes scandal about the assizes; it's the season for it."

"Is it." Cade turned the paper over. "Curious phrase here. *He wore the face of a friend.* Very particular. Very... witnessed." He looked up. "Where is the child who wrote this?"

"There is no child."

"No," Cade agreed pleasantly. "Of course not. There never is." He folded the copy into his coat, and Marguerite felt the theft go through her like a hook through flesh — her mother's testimony, her own careful letters, gone into that pocket. "Thank you for your hospitality, Mr. Lisle. We'll see ourselves out."

The boots went down the stairs. The door closed. Marguerite stayed in the closet until Lisle opened it, and when he did, his face was grey.

"They have your copy," he said. "Which means they know a copy exists, which means they know the original exists, and they will tear apart every house on your street by nightfall looking for it. Child — where is the person who keeps the original?"

"The observatory," whispered Marguerite. "With the clock."

"Then God and the master clockmaker had better be quick," said Lisle, "because Cade will not wait for the trial. Go home. Go now, and go carefully—"

But she was already moving, already down the stairs and out into the rain, and she did not go carefully, because terror had burned away all her craft, and she ran through the shining streets with her hood down and her breath tearing, and it was not until she cut through the chandler's yard that the grey coat detached itself from the doorway ahead of her and stepped into her path.

"Well," said Cade, crouching, smiling his pleased smile, rain dripping from his hat brim. "There never is a child. And yet."

* * *

What saved her was not cleverness. What saved her was Silas.

She heard him before she saw him — a shout from the lane's mouth, raw and young and furious, nothing like the quiet careful voice she knew — and then he came through the rain at a dead run with a wheelwright's iron bar in his fist and murder written plainly on his face, and Cade rose from his crouch with the unhurried ease of a man who had all day, and the two of them faced each other across the puddled yard while Marguerite scrambled backward against the wall.

"She's my sister," Silas said, breathless. "She's simple-minded. She wanders. I've been after her an hour." Each lie cost him visibly; she could see him paying for them.

"Your sister." Cade looked at her, then at Silas, then at the iron bar, weighing things. Rain ran down his face and he did not wipe it. "The clockmaker's apprentice. Yes. I've admired your hands, Master Pemberton. So steady. Such patience." He smiled again. "Steady hands are wasted on clocks. Give my regards to your master — and tell him we'll call again soon. We seem to keep missing each other."

He stepped aside. He let them pass. That was the most frightening thing of all — he let them pass, walking backward, watching, until the lane swallowed him.

Then Silas dropped the bar in the mud and grabbed her by the shoulders, hard, and for a moment he could not speak at all, just shook her gently, once, twice, his face white and streaming.

"You went alone. You went *alone*, Marguerite—"

"He's got my copy," she said. "Silas, he's got my copy, and he knows about the original, and they'll come tonight—"

"Stop. Stop." He pulled her under the chandler's awning, out of the rain, and knelt so their faces were level, and she saw that his fear was turning into something else, something harder, the way water turns to ice not by changing its nature but by standing still in the cold. "Listen to me. You copied the letter. You crossed the town. You faced Lisle, and then you faced *that man*, and you came out alive and running." His voice broke slightly. "I have been hiding that letter against my heart for a fortnight, telling myself tomorrow, telling myself it isn't safe, and a seven-year-old girl did in one afternoon what I haven't dared in fourteen days."

"You were being careful," Marguerite said. "Careful is good."

"Careful is what lets Mrs. Aubry hang on Thursday." He wiped the rain — or not the rain — from his face with his sleeve. "Not anymore. Do you hear me? Not anymore. You and I are going to finish this together, you and I, and Whitcombe will hate it, and it doesn't matter." He took her hand. Her small cold hand in his larger cold one. "Partners?"

Marguerite looked at him — at this boy who mended time for a living and had spent a fortnight pretending the letter would keep — and she nodded.

"Partners," she said.

They stood a moment longer under the awning while the rain came down on the town, on the observatory, on the clock that held her father's secret in its punched and patient plates. Then Silas straightened, and his jaw set in a way she would learn to know very well in the years after.

"We have until tonight," he said. "Come home. We have work to do — and Marguerite—" he paused at the edge of the awning, looking out at the drowned grey street, "—when we get there, I'm going to teach you how to read a cipher plate. Because if Cade takes the letter, the clock is going to have to testify. And it will need someone small enough to reach inside."
