## 14. The Advocate's Bargain

The rain had stopped before dawn, and the town woke glistening, as though it had been polished overnight by some enormous careful hand. Water dripped from the eaves of Cooper's Row in a rhythm Silas could not help counting — four drops, pause, three drops, pause — because his mind had been doing nothing else all night but measuring what remained of their time. Three days until Mrs. Aubry stood in the dock. Two nights, if he counted as the courts counted, from sundown to sundown. And somewhere across the roofs, behind a green door on Chancery Lane, an advocate named Lisle was eating his breakfast, unaware that a clockmaker's apprentice and a seven-year-old girl intended to walk into his chambers and turn his profession upside down.

Marguerite walked beside him, her hand tucked in his, her shoes leaving small dark prints on the wet cobbles. She had insisted on coming.

"Advocates frighten people," she had said, when he'd tried to leave her with Whitcombe. "They frighten them by being taller and using long words. I'm not frightened of either."

He had no answer to that. He suspected he would never have an answer to half of what she said, and was beginning to understand that this was not a defect in her but a feature, like the escapement of a good clock: she released the truth one tooth at a time, exactly when it was wanted.

Chancery Lane smelled of ink and old paper and, beneath it, the river. The green door bore a brass plate worn nearly illegible: T. LISLE, ADVOCATE. Silas knocked twice, and a clerk with ink to his elbows opened the door a suspicious four inches.

"Tell Mr. Lisle," Silas said, "that I've come about the Aubry trial. And tell him—" he hesitated, feeling the absurdity and the necessity of it together, "—tell him I bring evidence that cannot be forged."

The four inches became a full opening. They were shown into a narrow room lined with law books to the ceiling, where the window light fell in a grey bar across a desk buried under briefs. Lisle himself was younger than Silas had expected — perhaps five-and-thirty, with a scholar's stoop already forming and eyes that moved over visitors the way Silas's own eyes moved over a mechanism: cataloguing parts, noting wear, estimating what the whole might be worth.

He looked at Marguerite longest.

"You are the child from the printer's house," he said. Not a question.

"I'm the child who remembers," Marguerite said.

Lisle drew out two chairs with the economy of a man who wasted no motion he could bill for, and sat, and folded his hands. "Then you had better both sit down, and you had better begin at the beginning, because I have heard eleven versions of this case and ten of them were lies told by men in better coats than mine."

So Silas began. He began with the knock after midnight, and the hooded woman, and the words *forget my face*. He watched Lisle's face as he laid out the letter — the real letter, carried against his chest in an oilcloth wrapping since the night Cade had torn through the workshop — and he saw the advocate's professional stillness crack, just once, at the description of the handwriting.

"And you believe this to be Delacroix's hand," Lisle said quietly, "because Delacroix was your father."

"My father is dead," Silas said. It came out flat and strange, a sentence he no longer believed and could not yet stop saying. "Ten years dead. But the hand is his. I learned my letters copying his printing proofs. There are habits in it — the way he crosses the *t* with a single stroke dragged leftward, the way the *r* tails into the next letter. A forger can copy a hand. A forger cannot copy twenty years of habit without thinking about it, and no man thinking about forgery writes so naturally."

"A fair argument. Inadmissible, but fair." Lisle took up the letter with tweezers from a drawer, as though it were a specimen, and read it through in silence. When he set it down, he did not look at Silas but out the window, at the wet roofs, and Silas understood he was being given time to compose whatever he was about to say, and resented it, and waited anyway.

"This is dynamite," Lisle said at last. "It names Harrow. It gives the manner, the hour, the reason — the woman saw him leave the print-shop with blood on his cuff, and he knew it. If I stand up on Monday and read this letter to the assizes, the court will do one of two things. Either they will hang the right man's reputation, or they will hang me for subornation and the girl here for trespass of every kind, and Mrs. Aubry besides." He turned back. "A letter alone, Mr. Pemberton — even a true letter, even *this* letter — is a document. Documents can be forged. Harrow's people will say so before I finish reading it. They will produce a writing master who swears the hand imitates Delacroix's published pamphlets, which are public property, available to any forger. They will ask who kept this letter for months without producing it. They will ask why a dead man's son surfaces with it precisely when a conviction needs overturning." He spread his hands. "I need more than paper. I need something that cannot be forged. Do you have it?"

Silas felt the moment arrive the way one feels the click of a detent — the point past which the mechanism must either engage or slip.

"The observatory clock," he said.

Lisle's eyebrows rose. "The town clock."

"The master regulator, in the east tower. Mr. Quill's commission. My father worked on its escapement before he fled — that part I only learned last week, from the plates themselves." He described it then: the punched holes in the escape wheel's crossing, the pattern that had matched the pinpricks along the letter's margin, the second cipher keyed to the first, the names hidden in brass where no agent would think to look and no fire could easily reach. He spoke carefully, the way he'd rehearse a difficult repair, and Marguerite added two corrections — "the third hole is smaller than the others, it matters, I measured it with my thumbnail" — which Lisle received with a gravity that made Silas love him a little.

When he finished, Lisle was silent for a long moment.

"You're telling me," he said slowly, "that the evidence corroborating a murdered woman's testimony is currently keeping time above the town."

"Not keeping time yet," Silas admitted. "Not properly. That's rather the trouble. The clock is stripped. I've been rebuilding it for a fortnight, and now I know why the plates were punched the way they were, and I know how to make the cipher legible without destroying the mechanism — but it isn't done, and there isn't much time, and there is a royalist agent named Cade who has already searched our workshop once and will be watching everything I touch between now and Monday."

Lisle stood and went to the window. Rain-washed light lay along the sill like a blade.

"Here is the bargain, then," he said, without turning. "And I'll give it to you plain, because you've given me plain. I will take the letter. I will use it. But not as a lone instrument — I'd be cut to ribbons by Thursday's papers and Mrs. Aubry would swing anyway, and I do not lose, Mr. Pemberton. I have never once lost, and I don't intend to learn how on a widow." He turned. "I will use it only if three things are true. First: you testify. On oath, before the court, your name entered into the record — Pemberton, apprentice clockmaker, recipient of the letter. No anonymity. Once I open that door, you walk through it in front of everyone, and Cade and every friend Harrow has will know your face forever after."

Silas nodded. He had known this was coming; it landed harder than he'd expected nonetheless, a weight settling into the chest.

"Second: the cipher. You produce the clock — or proof of the clock — in corroboration. Not a copy of the plate, not your sworn word that the holes match. Something the jury can see with their own eyes, or as near to that as God and Mr. Quill permit. Which means—"

"Which means the observatory commission becomes evidence," Silas said. "The clock comes into the trial. Quill's name. The work itself."

"Precisely. And third—" Lisle's voice dropped, and gentled, which was somehow worse, "—you should consider very carefully whether you want me to say the rest, or whether you've already said it to yourself."

Silas had. He said it aloud anyway, because saying it made it real and he was tired of things living only in the dark of his own skull.

"If the clock testifies, and we win, then every enemy Harrow has — and Harrow has enemies, else he wouldn't be so careful — will remember that Whitcombe's workshop built the instrument that broke him. And if we lose, Cade burns us to the ground and calls it an accident, and no one asks twice." His throat was dry. "My master has wagered everything on this clock. Everything. There is a debt on the shop older than I am. If the commission fails, or if the commission succeeds and then the shop is ruined for succeeding — either way—"

"Either way," Lisle agreed, "your master's livelihood hangs on the outcome of a murder trial he has not agreed to be part of." He returned to the desk and sat. "That is the braid, Mr. Pemberton. Your truth, the girl's memory, the widow's neck, the judge's career, the agent's warrant, and one honest craftsman's forty years of work — all plaited into a single cord, and Monday we find out whether it holds or snaps. I won't pretend otherwise. Advocates who pretend otherwise sell their clients rope and call it ribbon."

Marguerite, who had been sitting with the terrible patience of children in adult rooms, put her hand flat on the desk.

"You forgot one thing in the braid," she said.

Lisle regarded her. "Did I?"

"You forgot that Mrs. Aubry didn't do anything wrong, and neither did my mother, and neither did Silas's father, and everybody who did do something wrong is frightened, and frightened people with power break things." She said it without heat, as a matter of inventory. "So the question isn't whether the cord holds. The question is whether we're brave before Monday or brave after, and brave after costs more. My mother taught me that. She also taught me it cost her everything, so I know it's true."

The room was quiet. Somewhere below, a cart rattled down Chancery Lane, and the sound seemed very far away.

Lisle looked at her for a long moment, and then at Silas, and something in his face shifted — the appraiser giving way to something older and less comfortable.

"Well," he said. "You may tell your master, when you tell him — and you must tell him, tonight, before you touch another gear; I'll not have a man's fortune conscripted without his knowledge — that if he consents, I will move heaven and the assize clerk both to see his work protected. There are ways. Depositions taken early. The clock examined by the court's own officers, so that it enters the record under the court's protection rather than at the pleasure of Judge Harrow." He allowed himself a thin smile. "Harrow presides over the trial, but he does not own the procedure, however much he believes otherwise. There are rules older than his appetite."

"And if Whitcombe says no?" Silas asked. "If he tells me to burn the letter and finish the clock and keep our heads down?"

Lisle spread his hands. "Then I fight with what I have, and likely lose, and Mrs. Aubry hangs on Wednesday, and you live with it, and so do I. That is the other shape of the bargain." He stood, and extended his hand across the desk. "Go and ask him. Come back by eight o'clock tonight — come yourself, alone; the girl stays indoors where Cade's men aren't counting faces. Bring me his consent or his refusal, and bring me word of the clock's condition. And Mr. Pemberton—" the grip tightened briefly, "—the letter does not leave your body again. Not for a bath, not for sleep, not for anything short of fire."

* * *

The walk home took longer than the walk there, because Silas took the long way, along the river wall, because he needed the wind and the water and the sight of something larger than his own dilemma. Gulls hung over the mudflats screaming their idiot grievances. Barges crawled upstream toward the bridge. The world went on trading and hauling and shouting, indifferent to whether an honest man's workshop burned on Wednesday or flourished on Thursday.

*Whitcombe,* he thought. *Forty years of it.* The bench worn hollow where his master leaned. The ledger with its patient columns of debt. The way the old man's hands shook now, not from age but from the arithmetic he did each night by candlelight, subtracting the future from the present and finding the remainder smaller each season. This clock — this beautiful, cursed clock — was to have been the rescue. The commission that would clear the debt, restore the name, buy back the lease. And Silas held in his chest the power to make that clock famous or infamous, solvent or evidence, and whichever it became, Whitcombe had not chosen it.

There was a version of the day where he turned back. Where he went to Lisle and said *no*, and let the letter stay hidden, and finished the escapement beautifully and anonymously, and lived the rest of his life as a competent craftsman with excellent hearing — because that was what he'd have, forever after: the sound of a woman's name read out at the gallows, and the memory that he'd had the one instrument in England that could have stopped it, folded away in oilcloth against his ribs.

Marguerite's voice, in his head, in her flat inventory voice: *brave after costs more.*

No. He would not carry it. Whatever Whitcombe decided, Silas knew, walking the river wall with the wind pulling at his coat, that he could not be the man who chose silence. He could only hope — and it was a thin hope, thinner than the wire of a suspension — that the man who raised him was still, underneath the fear and the debt and the forty careful years, someone who remembered why he'd taken in a traitor's son at all.

* * *

Whitcombe was at the bench when Silas came in, and he knew at once — masters always know — that something had happened. He set down his graver and wiped his hands on his apron, and looked from Silas's face to the oilcloth bundle still strapped against the boy's chest, and his shoulders dropped half an inch, as though a weight had been transferred to them preemptively.

"Sit down, lad," he said. "And start at the beginning. You've the face of a man carrying news that's heavier than he is."

Silas sat. Marguerite, unbidden, fetched the stool on the far side and sat too, close enough that her shoulder touched his arm, and stayed.

He told it all. Lisle's terms. The testimony, the clock, the exposure. The braid of fates. He watched Whitcombe's face go through it — the flinch at *evidence*, the paling at *Cade*, the long stillness at the end, when Silas said, "I'll not do it without your leave, Master. Say the word and I give Lisle his refusal tonight, and we finish the clock, and we keep our heads down, and I—" his voice caught, and he hated it, "—and I live with what that costs. But you must say it knowing everything. I won't have it done to you in the dark."

Whitcombe was silent a long while. He rose, and went to the shelf where the finished regulator stood — the small one, the one he'd built across a winter of bad trade and worse nights, the finest thing in the shop — and he touched its case with one finger, the way a man touches a thing he may be about to lose.

"When your father ran," he said at last, not turning around, "they came here asking after his press. I told them I'd seen nothing, which was a lie, and lying to crown agents was worth transportation if they'd proved it. I lied anyway. Do you know why?"

"Because you loved him," Silas said.

"Because he was *right*," Whitcombe said. He turned, and his eyes were wet and furious. "What he printed was true, and truth was sedition that year, and I have spent ten years telling myself that a lie told for safety cancels a truth told at risk, and it doesn't, lad, it never has. Every night I wind that regulator and I hear it tick and it says the same thing it has always said: the hours pass whether you spend them well or ill, and you do not get them back." He came and put both hands on Silas's shoulders, and his grip was strong as a vice, steady as a train. "Finish the clock. Testify. Put my name in your mouth before the assizes if the law requires it. We did not take up this trade to make pretty toys for cowards — we took it up because a wheel that runs true is a kind of honesty, and I'll not teach you dishonesty now, at the end, when it counts."

Silas's eyes stung. Beside him, Marguerite reached over and patted Whitcombe's apron twice, solemnly, like sealing a contract.

Whitcombe laughed — a cracked, startled sound — and then grew grave again. "But hear me. If we do this, we do it as craftsmen. Nothing sloppy, nothing rushed, nothing that Cade can point to and call forgery. That clock will run perfect, boy. Perfect. Let them examine every wheel. Let them hold it to the light. Our work will testify better than our tongues." He glanced at the window, where the evening was gathering blue-grey over the rooftops, and at the distant tower of the observatory, black against the last light.

"It's gone six," he said. "Lisle wants his answer by eight. Go and give it — and then get up that tower and start work, because you've got two nights, lad, and a dead man's cipher waiting in the brass, and I'd wager my life—" he smiled, grimly, "—it appears I am wagering considerably more than that — that Cade is watching the tower door already."

Silas rose. At the door he paused, and looked back at his master standing amid the benches and the shavings and the forty years of patient debt, upright as a pendulum at rest.

"Master," he said. "Whatever happens—"

"Happens to us both," Whitcombe said. "Now go. Time is the one client that never waits."

Outside, the first lamp of evening was being lit at the corner, and beyond it, up the hill, the observatory dome caught the last copper light — and beneath it, faintly, Silas could already imagine it, the great stripped clock waiting in the dark with its secret punched into its heart, patient as testimony, ready to speak.
