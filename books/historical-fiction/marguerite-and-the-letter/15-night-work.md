## 15. Night Work

The candle had burned to a stub the width of Silas's thumb before he admitted to himself that he was afraid.

Not of the work — the work was the one thing that had never frightened him. A clock did not care who watched it. An escapement could not be bribed or threatened or hanged. It asked only for patience and a steady hand, and both of those he had in quantities that would have astonished anyone who had seen him fumble his words before Advocate Lisle two evenings past. It was everything around the work that had teeth: the assizes opening in four days, Mrs. Aubry's neck measured for the rope, and across the street, in the shadowed doorway of the cooper's shop, the shape of a man who had not moved in three hours.

Cade.

Silas kept the candle low and the shutter cracked no wider than a knife's blade. From that slit he could see the whole of Cooper's Row as far as the lantern at the corner, and the doorway where the agent stood with his collar up against the drizzle. The man did not pace. He did not smoke. He simply stood, the way a spider stands, which is to say without any appearance of standing at all — as though the doorway had grown him out of its own timber.

*He knows,* Silas thought, and then corrected himself, because fear exaggerated things. *He suspects.* There was a difference, and the difference was measured in hours now rather than days.

He let the shutter fall and turned back to the bench.

The master clock's escapement lay before him in pieces arranged with the discipline of a surgeon's tray: anchor, pallets, the escape wheel with its thirty teeth, each tooth tipped and polished, the crutch, the suspension spring. Quill's great regulator had been stripped to its skeleton when Silas first climbed the observatory stair, and he had spent three weeks coaxing it toward life. Now he was about to do something no honest clockmaker would ever countenance doing to an instrument of this quality. He was going to make it lie — or rather, he was going to make it tell the truth twice over, once to the eye and once to whoever knew how to ask.

Because the cipher could not be read as the plates stood. That was the cruelty of it, the elegant cruelty of his father's design. The punched holes — forty-one of them, drilled through the brass of the escapement bridge and the count-wheel plate in a pattern that matched the pinpricks along the letter's margin — were legible only if you dismounted the bridge entirely, laid it flat beside the letter, and held both to the light. And to dismount the bridge meant stopping the clock. Stopping the clock meant dismantling half the movement in open view of Quill, of the observatory staff, of any visitor who happened to climb the tower on a Tuesday afternoon — including, should he choose to visit again, Judge Harrow himself.

Lisle had been clear, in his dry lawyer's way, as they'd parted by the advocate's door in the rain. *A letter can be called a forgery. Ink is cheap and so are men willing to sell it. But if the machine itself carries the same names, punched into its brass years before any of this came to trial — that, Mr. Pemberton, is testimony no witness can recant. Bring me the mechanism and I will bring the court to it.*

Bring me the mechanism. As though a public observatory's master clock were a pocket watch one might carry under one's coat. No — Lisle would bring Harrow, the jury, the town, up the tower stair; Silas had already won that much from him. But the demonstration had to be swift, undeniable, and above all *reversible*. The clock had to be stopped, read, and running again inside the space of an hour, and it had to be done without destroying either the evidence or the instrument. If the plates were damaged, if the holes could be claimed to be fresh-cut, recent, made for the occasion — then everything died on that tower, and Mrs. Aubry died with it, and probably Silas too, and Whitcombe's workshop after them.

So: the escapement had to be rebuilt.

He turned the bridge over in his fingers, feeling the weight of it, the faint oil-smell of forty years of service. The original pallets were worn to a shine; the escape wheel's teeth had each been dressed by some long-dead craftsman with more love than haste. He could not simply swap parts — Quill would know within a day, and Quill, whatever his kindnesses, answered to the university, and the university to men whose names appeared in the same rooms as Cade's. Whatever Silas built had to look, to any eye but a jeweler's loupe, like the clock's own heart continuing undisturbed.

What he needed was a second set of pallets cut to a slightly different geometry — shallow enough that the bridge could be lifted clear through the front plate's aperture without first removing the wheel train. A fool's geometry. A geometry that traded two seconds of accuracy per day for the ability to open the clock like a book.

Two seconds a day. Quill would grumble. Quill would note it in his logbook in his small furious hand. But Quill would not stop the clock, because astronomers never stopped clocks; they corrected for their sins arithmetically instead.

Silas smiled at that — a thin, tired smile, gone almost before it arrived — and reached for the saw.

* * *

He worked through what remained of the night in the manner he had learned from Whitcombe: not in bursts but in tides, hands moving while the mind stood slightly apart and supervised. Cut, file, check. Cut, file, check. The candle gave way to a second candle, and the second to the grey seepage of dawn around the shutter's edge, and still the file whispered its small argument against the brass.

Marguerite woke sometime in the black middle hours and came to sit on the stool in the corner, wrapped in Whitcombe's spare coat, her feet not reaching the floor. She did not speak. She had learned already that Silas at the bench was not to be interrupted, but she would not go back to bed either, and after a while he understood that she was keeping watch in her own fashion — her eyes on the shutter, her ears on the street, a seven-year-old sentinel who had learned at her mother's knee what boots passing at midnight might mean.

"Is it going well?" she asked once, very quietly, when he set down the file to flex his aching hand.

"The pallets are coming true." He held one up to the candlelight, turning it so the fresh-cut surfaces caught fire. "When a clock is right, you can't hear it think. That's what we're listening for."

"What does thinking sound like?"

"A tick that hesitates." He set the pallet down. "A tick that's afraid."

Marguerite considered this with the gravity she brought to everything, her dark eyes enormous above the collar of the borrowed coat. "Then ours mustn't be afraid," she said. "Even if everyone else is."

He had no answer for that, so he bent again to the work, and she stayed on her stool until dawn, and neither of them spoke again, and it was — he would remember this all his life — it was the best hour of those four days.

* * *

By full light the new pallets were fitted, the crutch adjusted, and the clock — he had carried the trial movement up to the tower in the blue hour, working in the cold observatory while Quill slept below — the clock was running again, and running, as near as his ear could judge, as sweetly as before.

But the pallets were only half the problem, and it was the other half that had kept him staring at the ceiling of the workshop through the previous afternoon while Marguerite napped and Whitcombe pretended to sort screws. The bridge itself, with its forty-one holes, still had to come off for the reading. Off meant the clock stopped. Stopped meant minutes — twenty of them at least, with careful hands — during which a judge and a jury and an agent named Cade would all be standing in a stone room with nothing to look at but a dead clock and the man who had killed it.

Unless the bridge never came off at all.

He had the idea sketched on the back of a scrap of Whitcombe's invoice paper before he fully believed it: a sliding mask. A thin brass plate, pierced with forty-one corresponding holes in the letter's pattern, mounted on the *face* of the existing bridge on two small pins, free to slide an inch along a slot. In its resting position it covered the original holes completely — flush, invisible, just another bit of decorative piercing such as fine regulators sometimes wore. When slid along the slot, its holes would align with the punched holes beneath, and a candle behind the assembly would throw the doubled pattern onto a sheet of paper pinned to the far side — every hole confirmed, every hole *doubled*, in ten seconds, without a single screw turned.

It was not beautiful horology. Whitcombe would have called it a conjurer's trick and been half right. But it was reversible, it was fast, and it could not be faked — because the mask's pattern had been cut from the letter itself, and the letter was in Lisle's strongbox, and neither Silas nor any conspirator could have known the arrangement of forty-one pinpricks unless the letter and the clock had shared an author.

There remained the problem of the mask itself: forty-one holes, drilled and filed into brass a sixteenth of an inch thick, positioned to tolerances finer than anything he had ever attempted outside a balance spring. Two days' work, honestly reckoned. Perhaps three, working alone, working tired, with Cade in the street.

He had one day.

* * *

Whitcombe found him at noon asleep face-down on the bench, a file still gripped in one hand, and woke him not with words but with the smell of onions and mutton, which in Whitcombe's household constituted an alarm bell of the most serious kind. They ate standing up, the master and apprentice, from the same pot, while Marguerite sat between them on a flour barrel like a small chairperson of their parliament.

"You've not slept," Whitcombe said.

"I've slept."

"I've watched you sleep. It looked like drowning." The old man spooned more mutton onto Silas's plate without asking. "The man across the street has been relieved twice since morning. Same coat, different boots. He's being paid by the hour, which means someone is paying by the hour, which means someone believes there is something here worth hourly money."

Silas said nothing, which was answer enough.

Whitcombe set down his spoon. For a long moment he looked at his apprentice — looked at him the way he looked at a movement opened on the bench, seeing all the wheels at once — and then he did the thing that Silas would understand only years later, when he was old enough to have apprentices of his own: he asked no questions. He simply took the scrap of invoice paper with the sketch on it, studied it upside down, turned it round, and said:

"The slot wants to be cut with a piercing saw before drilling, or the plate will buckle. And your pins want taper, not parallel, or the mask will bind when the brass swells with the heat of the room." He handed the paper back. "I'll take the frame. You drill. Between us we might shame the devil by tomorrow night."

"Master—"

"Don't." Whitcombe's voice was quiet and absolutely final. "You'll say thank you, and I'll say don't mention it, and we'll both pretend this is about a commission and not about a hanging. Now eat. Brass doesn't wait for sentiment."

* * *

They worked the rest of that day and deep into the next night as they had not worked together since Silas was twelve years old and all of life was learning the names of tools. Whitcombe filed the mask blank true and flat; Silas laid the letter's margin — copied, hole for hole, onto oiled paper by candlelight with a fine needle — against the brass and began the drilling. Each hole wanted spotting first, then centering, then drilling through at slow speed lest the drill wander and ruin everything. Forty-one times. His eyes burned. His shoulders had become two knots of wire. Outside, the rain came properly at last, drumming on the roof slates, and he thought with something close to tenderness: *let it rain. Let Cade stand in it. Let him drown in it.*

Marguerite slept in the back room, truly slept, worn out by her own vigilance. Once, near midnight, she cried out — a single syllable, a woman's name, perhaps her mother's — and both men froze over the bench until she settled again, and neither of them mentioned it afterward.

It was near three in the morning, with thirty-eight of the forty-one holes cut and the thirty-ninth spotted and ready, that the knock came.

Three soft taps. Not the door — the *shutter*, the workshop's side shutter that faced the alley where the cooper stored his hoops. Both men went still as the dead. Whitcombe's hand found the poker. Silas blew out the candle and moved to the shutter on sock-feet, and put his eye to the crack.

Nothing. Rain, and the alley's black, and the distant lantern at the corner burning yellow through the wet.

Then he saw it. On the sill, inside the closed shutter's reach — impossible unless the shutter had been opened and shut again without a sound, which was itself impossible — sat a small parcel, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with waxed thread, no larger than a prayer book. Rain-darkened. Recently set down.

"Whitcombe," he breathed. "Look."

The old man came, poker lowered, suspicion plain on his face. Neither of them opened the shutter. Silas worked the latch inch by inch, ready to slam it, and pulled the parcel in, and rebarred the wood, and stood holding it while his heart performed badly.

Inside the oilcloth: a wooden box lined with flannel, and in the flannel, nested like relics —

An escape wheel. Finished. Perfect.

Not merely good — *perfect*, cut with a delicacy Silas had seen perhaps twice in his life and executed himself never. Thirty teeth, each shaped with that particular compound curve that lets a pallet enter and leave without shock, the crossings of the wheel thinned and rounded so the air itself seemed to move through them more politely. At its center, stamped small and deep into the hub where only a maker would ever find it: a fleur-de-lis, and beneath it a single word in letters two millimeters high.

*DELACROIX.*

Silas sat down slowly on the stool, because his legs had opinions of their own.

"He's alive," Marguerite said from the doorway.

She stood there in her nightdress with her arms crossed against the cold, hair loose and wild, looking at the wheel in Silas's cupped hands with an expression no child should own — not wonder exactly, and not grief, but the flat certainty of someone recognizing a signature.

"You knew his mark," Silas said.

"Maman kept a printing block. The little flower. She kept it in her apron pocket." Marguerite came forward and touched the fleur-de-lis with one fingertip, the way one touches a sleeping animal. "She said the man who cut it was the finest hand in the city, and that he had run away from everything except his work."

Silas looked at the wheel and understood, in the space of a few heartbeats, several things at once and none of them gently. That his father had been in this city — in these streets — recently enough to know what was needed. That he had watched the workshop, watched the shutter, watched for the candle, the way Silas had watched for Cade. That he knew his son's bench, his son's habits, his son's needs, well enough to cut an escape wheel to fit a movement he had never seen — or had seen, Silas thought with a lurch, *had seen*, years ago, when it was new, when he was still Delacroix the printer and not a dead man keeping a country bindery, back when the observatory clock was commissioned and perhaps even handled—

And that he had chosen, again, to leave without a word. To set the thing on the sill in the rain and vanish, giving the gift and withholding the giver, protecting himself and calling it protection of others.

"He was here," Silas said aloud. "Tonight. While we worked. He stood in this alley."

"Then he isn't ashamed," Marguerite said, "or he'd have left it at the church door." She said it simply, with a child's merciless logic, and it went into Silas like a splinter he would carry a long time.

Whitcombe, silent throughout, finally spoke from the shadows by the bench, and his voice was strange. "That wheel is worth more than my whole stock. That wheel is worth more than my *life*, boy, if Cade learns whose hands cut it." He paused. "Fit it. Fit it tonight. And tomorrow we finish the mask, and the day after, God forgive us all, we hang a judge."

Silas fitted the wheel by candlelight with hands that trembled only at the beginning. It entered the train like water finding its level. When he released the weight, the clock took it up, and the escapement turned, and the sound it made was the sound he had described to Marguerite — a tick with no hesitation in it, a tick afraid of nothing.

Outside, the rain eased toward morning. Across the street, in the cooper's doorway, the watcher shifted his boots and settled in for another day.

Three days to the assizes. One mask to finish. And somewhere in the wet dark of the city, a dead man walking, close enough to touch the shutter — and gone again before his son could open it.
