## 16. The First Day of Trial

The courthouse at Ashcombe had been a granary before the assizes came to town, and it had never entirely stopped smelling of one — dust and dry rot and old wheat ghosting beneath the sharper smells of tallow, ink, and the sweat of three hundred bodies pressed into benches built for half that number. Silas stood at the back with his cap crushed in his fists and felt the crowd as a pressure against his spine, a single breathing animal that shifted and murmured and went silent all at once when the doors beside the judge's bench opened.

Judge Harrow entered the way a cold draught enters a warm room.

Silas had seen him twice before — once at a distance in the market square, once closer, terribly closer, in the observatory where the man had admired the master clock and lingered over Silas's hands as though reading them like a dial. But he had never seen him seated in judgment, robed in black with the white bands at his throat, and the sight did something to Silas's understanding of the world. It was not that Harrow looked different. It was that everything around him looked different: the bench became an altar, the mace-bearing beadles became acolytes, the whole dusty granary arranged itself into a church with Harrow for its god. Power, Silas thought, was mostly a matter of where a man stood and what stood behind him.

Harrow took his seat. He did not look at the crowd. He looked at a paper before him, unhurried, as though the room were empty and he were alone with his breakfast.

"That's him," Marguerite whispered.

She was pressed against Silas's side, hidden in the shadow between him and a fishmonger's wife whose bulk made a wall of them both. They had argued about bringing her — Silas had argued, at least; Marguerite had simply appeared at his elbow on the courthouse steps wearing Quill's spare coat buttoned to her chin and an expression that ended discussion. She was seven years old and she was looking at the man who had ordered her mother taken, and she was not crying, and something about that frightened Silas more than anything else in the room.

"Yes," he said quietly. "That's him."

"He has small eyes."

"Hush now. Watch."

* * *

Mrs. Aubry was brought up from the cells below through a trapdoor in the floor of the court, which seemed to Silas a deliberate cruelty — the accused rising out of the earth itself, as though conjured from the grave she would soon occupy. She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with the raw knuckles of a woman who had washed other people's linen all her life, and she walked to the rail without being pushed. Her hands were bound before her with cord. She did not look at the jury. She did not look at Harrow. She found one face in the crowd — Silas could not tell whose — and held to it like a handhold on a cliff.

The charge was read: the murder of Madame Céleste Delacroix, printer's wife, in the autumn of 1786; the concealment of the body; the false witness borne against the dead woman's husband.

Seven years ago. The year Silas's father had died — or had not died. The year a letter had been sealed and hidden and carried by a hooded woman through the dark to a workshop door.

Beside Mrs. Aubry stood the advocate, Mr. Lisle — young for the work, thirty at most, with ink-stained cuffs and a barrister's wig that sat on him slightly askew, as though he had put it on in a hurry and meant to take it off again soon. He had met Silas twice since their bargain: once to rehearse what Silas knew, once to tell him, flatly, *not yet*. Wait until I call for corroboration, Lisle had said. A letter can be dismissed. A letter alone will hang us all. Give me the first day. Let me see how the ground lies.

So Silas waited, and watched the ground lie.

* * *

Lisle began well. That was the tragedy of it — he began beautifully.

"My lord. Members of the jury." His voice carried without strain; he had the knack of sounding as though each juror were the only person present. "You are asked today to believe a simple thing: that a washerwoman, a woman who cannot write her own name, strangled a printer's wife in her own home, alone, at night, and then concealed the body so thoroughly that it was found only because a dog dug where no dog had business digging. You are asked to believe this because a man in fine robes tells you it is so."

A stir ran through the benches. At the front, Harrow turned a page.

"But I ask you instead to believe a harder thing," Lisle went on. "I ask you to believe that Madame Delacroix was killed because of what her husband printed — that a press which set type about a certain judge also, in that judge's mind, set a death warrant. I ask you to believe that the true murderer sits above you in silk and ermine, that the evidence against Mrs. Aubry was manufactured, and that this whole proceeding—"

"Mr. Lisle." Harrow's voice was mild. That was the terrible thing about it; Silas had learned that much from clocks, that the most dangerous failures announced themselves softly. "You address the jury as though they were a mob at a hanging. Confine yourself to the evidence. If you have evidence, produce it. If you have only speeches, sit down."

"I have evidence, my lord."

"Then let us have it, and less theatre."

Lisle bowed — a fraction too slowly, Silas thought, a bow with a blade inside it — and produced the letter.

He had it copied, of course; the original never left its hiding place, sewn into the lining of the coat Silas wore at this moment, pressing against his ribs like a second heartbeat. But Lisle had engaged a scrivener of good reputation to make a fair copy, witnessed and attested, and it was this he held up now, unfolding it with the reverence of a priest elevating a relic.

"A letter, members of the jury, written in 1786 by Gaspard Delacroix — the printer himself, the fugitive whose wife lies in a pauper's grave — naming the man who killed her. Naming—" and here Lisle paused, and every soul in the granary leaned forward, "—a judge. Naming Judge Harrow."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well. For three full seconds nobody breathed. Then the noise broke loose — a roar of whispers, benches creaking, someone near Silas saying *God's teeth* under his breath, the beadles banging their staves and shouting for order.

And Harrow smiled.

It was a small smile. It did not reach anywhere near his small eyes. He let the uproar run exactly long enough to demonstrate that he was untroubled by it, and then he raised one finger, and the beadles' staves, and the hush that fell was total.

"Mr. Lisle," he said, "you have just read aloud, in a public court, a document accusing a serving justice of the King's bench of murder. I commend your nerve. Now — indulge me. Where did this letter come from?"

"It was delivered into safe keeping, my lord, and has remained there these seven years."

"Delivered. By whom?"

"The circumstances of its delivery are—"

"*By whom?*"

Lisle's jaw tightened. "The person who conveyed it wished to remain unknown. My lord, the letter's provenance is not at issue. Its contents—"

"Everything is at issue," Harrow said, "in a case where a woman's neck is at stake. Let us be clear about what we hold. We hold an unsigned paper. We hold a claim that it was written by a man who fled this country seven years ago to escape charges of seditious libel — a man who is conveniently absent, conveniently beyond examination, conveniently unable to confirm or deny that he ever wrote a word of it. We hold a scrivener's copy — not the original, I note, but a *copy*, made recently, by whom? By your paid scrivener. And we are asked to accept all this as evidence against a justice of the realm." He folded his hands upon the bench. "Tell me, Mr. Lisle. Have you ever seen a forged letter?"

"I have seen this one, my lord."

Laughter — quick, startled, half-suppressed — rippled through the gallery, and died at once when Harrow's gaze lifted. But Silas saw the jurors exchange looks, and his stomach turned over. The laughter had been aimed at Lisle. It had landed there.

"You mistake me," Harrow continued pleasantly. "I mean: have you examined the hand? Compared it against known specimens of Delacroix's writing? Verified the paper, the ink, the age of either? Or did you simply receive a story — a romantic story, a story of mysterious midnight deliveries and secret keepings — and decide it served your purpose?"

"I have witnesses who can speak to the hand, my lord."

"One witness. A clockmaker's apprentice." Harrow said the words gently, almost kindly, and turned his small eyes toward the back of the room, and for one vertiginous instant Silas was certain the man was looking directly at him, at the cap in his fists, at the coat with its sewn-in heart of paper. "A boy. Who claims — does he not, Mr. Lisle? — that the man who wrote this letter is his own father. Whom he believes dead. Whom he has not seen in ten years. A boy who stands to gain — what shall we say? — a certain notoriety from such a discovery. A boy whose testimony, I need hardly remind the jury, would be worth precisely nothing in any court in England without the original document and a chain of custody no honest advocate would dare claim."

He paused. He let the word *boy* hang in the air like smoke.

"And even granting — generously — that the hand is genuine, that the letter is what it purports to be: what does it prove? It proves that one fugitive libeller accused another man of a crime. That is all. A dying man curses his doctor; do we hang the doctor?" Harrow spread his hands, and the gesture was almost sorrowful. "Gentlemen of the jury, you are being asked to convict a justice of murder on the unsupported word of a runaway printer, relayed by an unknown hand, preserved by persons unknown, produced by an advocate who cannot say where it came from and cannot show you the original. I have presided in this court eleven years. I have heard many ingenious fictions. This is among the more elegant."

* * *

It went on for hours, and it went on exactly like that.

Every thread Lisle offered, Harrow cut. The witness who had seen a judge's carriage in Printer's Lane on the night of the murder — *did you see the arms upon the door, friend, or merely a carriage, of which there are forty in this county?* The neighbour who remembered Madame Delacroix saying she feared for her life — *feared for her life from whom, madam? From everyone, I collect; printers' households are full of fear; fear proves nothing.* The bread wrapped, Marguerite had said, in a printed page — a page bearing the damning article, a page that should never have been in a washerwoman's kitchen. On that point Lisle pressed hard, and for a moment the ground seemed to shift: a printed sheet, dated, from the seized and destroyed press —

"Found where?" Harrow asked. "In the ashes of Mrs. Aubry's fire. Burned. Partially burned, I understand. How convenient that the portion which survives is the portion which damns her, and how very odd that a murderer should wrap her victim's bread in the very evidence against herself." He shook his head, slow and sad. "Unless, of course, the page was placed there afterward. Unless the whole kitchen was dressed like a stage. Gentlemen, I do not tell you what to conclude. I merely observe that every piece of this puzzle fits together with a neatness that honest crimes rarely achieve."

Silas understood then, watching him, that Harrow was doing something worse than lying. He was teaching. With every question, every mild correction, every sorrowful shake of the head, he was instructing three hundred people in how to think about this case — seeding the jury with his own conclusions so that when they retired they would not deliberate but recite. He had turned the courtroom into a mechanism and he was regulating it beat by beat, and the horror of it, the thing that made Silas's hands ache to be doing anything else, anything at all, was that it was beautiful work. It was the finest escapement Silas had ever watched operate. Every tooth engaged. Nothing wasted. No motion without purpose.

And Mrs. Aubry sat at the rail through all of it, her bound hands in her lap, growing smaller by the hour, like a candle burning down in a draft.

Once, late in the afternoon, she turned and looked up into the gallery — searching again for that one face — and her gaze crossed Marguerite's, and stopped.

Silas felt the child go rigid against his side. Mrs. Aubry looked at the girl in the borrowed coat for a long moment, and her face did something complicated and terrible — recognition, and grief, and a fierce shake of the head, almost invisible, *no, child, no, don't* — before a beadle's staff tapped her shoulder and she faced forward again.

"She knows me," Marguerite whispered. "She used to bring my mother's washing back. She used to give me honey cake."

"I know."

"Silas. She's going to die."

"Watch," he said, and hated the word, and hated himself for needing her quiet.

* * *

At dusk the court rose. Harrow adjourned proceedings until ten the following morning with the same unhurried courtesy he had shown all day, gathered his papers, and descended through the door behind the bench without once having looked at the prisoner as a living woman rather than a problem in procedure. The crowd drained out into a blue-grey evening that smelled of rain coming, and Silas stood a long while among the emptying benches, listening to the granary settle back into itself.

Marguerite pulled his sleeve. "What happens tomorrow?"

"Lisle calls his strongest witnesses. What's left of them." Silas rubbed his eyes. Under his ribs the original letter lay warm and useless as a stone. *A letter can be dismissed,* Lisle had told him, and he had believed it was a figure of speech, a strategist's caution. He had not understood that it was a prophecy. "Then the jury decides."

"And if they decide wrong?"

"Then Mrs. Aubry hangs on Thursday."

Marguerite was silent for a moment. Rain had begun outside, ticking softly against the high windows, and in the failing light the empty court looked suddenly like what it had been before the law moved in: a place built to store food for winter, standing empty.

"There has to be something the judge can't argue with," she said at last. It was not a question. She said it the way she said everything now, with the flat certainty of a child stating weather. "There has to be something he can't call a forgery. Something he can't burn."

Silas looked down at her, and then out through the darkening window, eastward, toward the observatory on its hill, where the master clock stood finished and running in its tower — every plate cleaned, every wheel trued, every punched hole waiting in the brass like stars in a chart.

He had promised Lisle he would wait. He had promised Whitcombe he would stay invisible. He had spent two weeks learning to keep still.

But the clock was up there. The clock had been keeping the true time all along.

"Marguerite," he said, "how would you feel about going back to the observatory tonight?"
