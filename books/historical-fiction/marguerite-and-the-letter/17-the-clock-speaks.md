## 17. The Clock Speaks

The summons came at half past seven in the morning, delivered by a clerk with ink-stained cuffs and the harried look of a man carrying more paper than sense: the court would hear the clockmaker's evidence at ten o'clock, and the court would come to him.

Silas read the note twice, then a third time, because his hands had begun to tremble and he wanted something to blame besides fear.

"They're coming *here*," Marguerite said from the doorway of the back room, her braid still loose from sleep. "The whole court? The judge too?"

"The judge too." Silas set the note down on the bench beside the escapement plates, which lay in their careful rows like a dissected animal. "Harrow has no choice. Lisle filed the motion last night — corroboration by mechanism. Either the court examines the clock or the letter stands alone as forgery."

"Will it work?"

He looked at her. Seven years old, and she asked questions that cut straight through to the marrow of things. He had learned not to lie to her; lying to Marguerite was like lying to a small, patient mirror.

"I don't know," he said. "But it will be true when I say it. That's all I can bring them. The rest is theirs to weigh."

Behind him, from the stairwell, came the slow, deliberate sound of Whitcombe descending — one step, a pause, another step. The old man had not slept either. Silas could see it in the gray of his face, in the way his hands hung at his sides as though they'd forgotten their trade overnight.

"You've heard," Silas said.

Whitcombe nodded once. He crossed the workshop, touched the great regulator standing against the wall — the finished piece, the one they'd carried out of the fire on his own scorched back, its case blistered but its heart intact — and then turned to face his apprentice as though facing an opponent across a chessboard.

"Silas. My boy. Sit down."

"I can't sit down. I have three hours—"

"*Sit.*"

Silas sat.

Whitcombe lowered himself onto the stool opposite, joints cracking, and for a long moment he said nothing at all. Outside, the town was waking toward the assizes; carts rattled past on the cobbles, and somewhere a bell was being tested, struck once, twice, its voice uncertain.

"When you were nine years old," Whitcombe began, "your father left you on my doorstep with a bag of tools and a debt I didn't owe. And I took you in. Not because I was good — don't ever make that mistake about me — but because your father asked, and because I saw your hands." He held up his own, broad and scarred and steady. "Hands are everything in this trade. They tell you what a man is before his mouth does. Yours were already honest at nine. They still are. It's the only inheritance Delacroix ever gave you that wasn't poisoned."

"Master—"

"Let me finish, because I may not have the courage again." Whitcombe leaned forward. "Today you stand up in front of that court, in front of Harrow, in front of Cade's men who burned my workshop and very nearly burned *me*, and you show them what's inside that clock. And if it goes wrong — if Harrow calls it trickery, if the crowd turns, if Cade decides a second fire is cheaper than a first defeat — then everything ends. Not just the commission. Everything. You. The girl. Me, this time for certain."

"I know."

"Do you?" The old man's voice cracked, and he mastered it. "Because yesterday you were the boy who fixed watches and kept his head down. Today they'll write your name in the papers. There is no coming back from today, Silas. Win or lose, the quiet life is over."

Silas thought of Mrs. Aubry in the cells, three days from the rope when Lisle found her, her hands folded in her lap like a woman already dressed for her own funeral. He thought of Marguerite in the rain, clutching a copied fragment of the letter to her chest, soaked through and unrepentant. He thought of his father in the country bindery, saying *I cannot*, and meaning *I am afraid*, and how those two sentences had lived side by side in Silas's own chest for weeks without his noticing.

"I know," he said again, and stood up. "And I'm going to do it anyway."

Whitcombe looked at him for a long moment. Then the old man did something he had never done in twelve years: he bowed his head slightly, the way a craftsman acknowledges better work than his own.

"Then we'd best make sure the clock is perfect," he said. "Fetch the lamp oil. If they're coming to judge us, we'll give them nothing crooked to look at."

* * *

They came at ten minutes past ten, in a procession that filled the lane from the observatory gate to the corner of Cooper's Row.

First the constables, four of them, walking stiffly in pairs. Then the jury, twelve men in their Sunday coats, craning their necks at the observatory dome as though expecting the building itself to testify. Then Advocate Lisle, lean and quick-eyed, with Mrs. Aubry between two bailiffs — she had been brought from the cells to witness whatever happened here, and she walked with her chin lifted in a way that made Silas's throat tighten. Then the clerks, the spectators who had abandoned the courtroom en masse, half the town it seemed, pressing along the walls and fences.

And in the middle of it all, unhurried, robed in black, Judge Harrow.

Silas watched him come up the path and understood for the first time why men feared him. It was not menace. It was the opposite — a perfect, polished courtesy, the smile of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice because every institution in the country spoke for him. His eyes found Silas over the crowd and rested there, mild and appraising, the way they had lingered on Silas's hands at the observatory a fortnight ago. A man admiring a mechanism. A man deciding whether it was worth the trouble of dismantling.

Behind Harrow, at the edge of the procession, stood Cade.

The royalist agent had shaved since Silas had last seen him, and the neatness made him worse. He caught Silas looking and inclined his head — almost friendly, almost amused — and tapped two fingers against his breast pocket, where a warrant might live, or a pistol.

Lisle reached Silas first. "Everything ready?"

"The clock is assembled. The plates are cleaned. The demonstration takes eleven minutes if nothing goes wrong."

"And if something goes wrong?"

"Then it takes longer," Silas said, "and is more convincing."

Lisle allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "I've seen judges laugh advocates out of court for less theater than you're planning. But I have also seen—" he glanced back at Mrs. Aubry, "—what happens when we bring nothing but paper. Paper burns, Mr. Pemberton. Show them iron."

Harrow ascended the observatory steps, paused beneath the dome, and surveyed the instrument room with the expression of a landlord inspecting a tenant's improvements. Quill the astronomer hovered at the periphery, agonized — his life's sanctuary converted into a courtroom — until Harrow said pleasantly, "You keep excellent time here, sir. I trust it stays so," and Quill went pale and silent.

"Very well." Harrow settled into the chair the constables brought him, arranged his robes, and looked at Silas with courteous attention. "Mr. Pemberton. You have claimed — and your advocate has argued with more enthusiasm than evidence — that a clock built six years ago by a fugitive printer contains, hidden in its works, the names of persons concerned in the death of Madame Delacroix. You understand, I hope, how this sounds. A machine that accuses. It is the stuff of plays."

"Yes, my lord," Silas said.

"Yet the court has indulged you." A thin gesture at the crowded room. "Partly, I confess, out of curiosity. Partly because Mr. Lisle is persistent. So. Amuse us."

There it was — the door opened exactly wide enough to be shut again. Silas felt the whole room leaning toward him: the jury, the town, Marguerite's hand finding his sleeve, Whitcombe standing rigid by the wall with his burned hands clasped behind him.

He walked to the master clock.

* * *

It stood taller than any man in the room, its case of dark oak, its brass pendulum hanging still — he had stopped it at dawn, the first time in its six years it had been permitted silence. Its face was pale in the lamplight. Around it he had laid out his tools in strict order: screwdrivers by size, the magnifier, the tray of removed plates, each wrapped in cloth.

"My lord. Gentlemen of the jury." His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "A clock is a system of counted motions. Every wheel, every tooth, every beat of the escapement is accounted for in the maker's drawings. If I show you a part that serves no purpose in the counting — a feature with no mechanical reason to exist — then either the maker was a fool, or the part exists for some other purpose entirely."

"Proceed," said Harrow, mildly.

Silas opened the case.

He worked aloud as he went, because Lisle had coached him — *narrate, always narrate; a silent craftsman looks like a conjurer* — naming each wheel as he drew it out, the great wheel, the intermediate, the escape wheel, laying them on the cloth with the reverence of a priest handling relics. The crowd pressed closer. Someone in the back whispered, and a constable hissed for quiet.

"Now." He lifted the anchor — the escapement's pallet frame, the beating heart of the whole machine — and held it to the light. "Every surface here is functional. These pallets engage the teeth of the escape wheel. These arbors carry the force. But observe the back plate of the anchor."

He turned it over.

Gasps moved through the room like wind through wheat.

Punched into the brass, in rows too regular to be corrosion and too irregular to be ornament, were holes — dozens of them, varying in depth and spacing, some clustered, some isolated. To an eye that didn't know what it looked at, they might have been flaws in the casting. To an eye that did—

"What am I looking at?" demanded one of the jurors, a fat merchant squinting forward.

"Holes," said Silas. "Drilled at six different depths, in a sequence. Alone, meaningless. But hold the plate to light—" he raised it toward the high window, and the sun came through the brass in a scatter of bright points, a constellation, "—and each hole casts its shadow at a different distance, according to its depth. The shadows fall on a scale. Read together, depth and position give a letter. It is a code, gentlemen, executed in metal. It cannot be erased without destroying the plate. It cannot be dismissed as wear, or accident, or later tampering — the drilling was done before the parts were gilded. Six years ago."

"Lies and theater," someone called from the back — and Silas knew the voice before he turned. Cade had pushed forward, hands clasped behind him, wearing the reasonable face of a man protecting the public from fraud. "Any engraver with a grudge could punch holes in brass and call it prophecy. Where is your proof this was done by the printer Delacroix? Where is anything but a clockmaker's story?"

"A fair question," said Harrow, and there was something in his voice now, a hairline crack of interest that he could not entirely smooth away, "and one the court shares. What answers it, Mr. Pemberton?"

"This." Silas took up the second object waiting on the bench — not the letter itself, which Lisle held in safekeeping, but a copy of it, certified page by page in Lisle's presence two nights ago. "The letter that names you, my lord, was written on paper bearing pinpricks. Small punctures, nearly invisible, in groups. We believed at first they were careless marks. They are not. They are the same code."

He unfolded the copy, held it flat, and read aloud, slowly, translating as he had practiced forty times in the dark of the workshop while Marguerite slept: "*The hand that ordered Madame Delacroix taken was the hand of Josiah Harrow, justice of the peace…*"

"Stop." 

Harrow's voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing — it dropped instead, half a tone, into a register Silas felt in his sternum. The room froze.

"You will forgive me," the judge said, "if I decline to be instructed in my own crimes by a hole-punched clock. This is a fabrication. The girl's testimony was coached. The letter is forged. And now a tradesman produces a magic lantern show and asks the crown of England to applaud." He rose, robes settling around him like closing water. "This proceeding is concluded. I commend Mr. Lisle's ingenuity, and I—"

"One thing more, my lord." 

Lisle stepped forward, and his voice was quiet, almost apologetic, which was how Silas knew the trap had been set days ago and Harrow had walked into it with his eyes open. "Before the court withdraws — the letter's pinpricks were examined by three separate printers' journeymen, sworn to secrecy until this hour, who confirm the cipher matches the clock's plate *hole for hole*. But Your Lordship need not take even that on faith. Because the original plate — drilled six years ago, before the gilding, before the murder, before anyone imagined this day — bears one further mark. A maker's mark. Punched in the same code, in the same hand." He paused. "Read it, Mr. Pemberton."

Silas lifted the anchor plate to the window light one final time, found the small cluster near the lower margin that he had saved for last, and read:

"*Delacroix.*"

The name fell into the room and rang there like a struck bell.

* * *

Afterward, Silas could never reconstruct the order of events — only their weight.

The murmur first, rising from the jury box like steam. The merchant juror saying loudly, "Six years! Who drills a clock six years deep to cheat a widow?" A woman near the door beginning, absurdly, to weep. Mrs. Aubry's hands rising to her mouth. Quill staring at his beloved clock as though it had spoken with a human voice — which, Silas thought, in every way that mattered, it had.

And Harrow.

Harrow stood very still amid the turning of the room. He did not look at the clock. He looked at Silas — a long, level, unblinking look in which there was no anger at all, which was worse than anger, which was assessment: the weighing of a problem, the pricing of a solution. Then his gaze slid sideways, briefly, to Cade at the edge of the crowd. Nothing passed between them that any court could name. A glance. Less than a glance.

"My lord," Lisle said into the noise, "in light of corroborating evidence, I move that the trial of Mrs. Aubry be reconvened, that the letter be admitted, and that this plate be entered into the record of—"

"The court will recess," Harrow said, "while I consider whether these proceedings have been conducted lawfully." And he gathered his robes and descended from the chair, and the constables closed around him, and the crowd parted before him as crowds had parted before him all his life —

— except that this time, they parted slowly. Reluctantly. With faces that had stopped fearing and begun, visibly, dangerously, to think.

Marguerite's hand slipped into Silas's. She was watching Harrow go with an expression far too old for her face.

"He knows we know," she said quietly. "What does a man like that do when he knows?"

Silas did not answer, because across the room Cade had turned from the door and was looking at the master clock — at the great, beautiful, incriminating machine — with the thoughtful air of a man estimating how long a building takes to burn.

And outside, faint but unmistakable above the murmur of the town, Silas heard hoofbeats. Several horses. Riding fast, toward the observatory hill.
