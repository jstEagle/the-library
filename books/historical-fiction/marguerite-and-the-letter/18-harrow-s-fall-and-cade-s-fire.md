## 18. Harrow's Fall and Cade's Fire

The observatory had never held so many people. Silas stood at the head of the stair with the clock's rear plate open behind him and the whole town pressed into the dome room above — shopkeepers in their Sunday coats, the fishwife who still smelled of herring, Quill's students crammed shoulder to shoulder beneath the great brass telescope, and the jury itself, summoned up from the assize court by Lisle's audacity and Harrow's own inability to refuse a demonstration without admitting there was something to demonstrate.

Judge Harrow sat in the chair Quill had been obliged to offer him, his hands folded on the head of his cane, his face composed into an expression of judicial patience that did not quite reach his eyes. Cade stood by the door. He had not taken off his hat.

"Mr. Pemberton," Harrow said, and his voice was mild as milk, "you are a clockmaker. You are not an advocate, not a witness of record, and not, so far as I am aware, sworn to anything but your trade. Explain to the court what you imagine you have found."

Silas's mouth was dry. He thought of Whitcombe's face that morning, gray as old pewter, saying *don't do this, boy, don't do this*, and he thought of Marguerite asleep against the clock case three nights running while he worked, and he thought of Mrs. Aubry's hands folded in her lap in the dock, waiting for the rope.

He turned to the clock.

"The escapement of this regulator was rebuilt eleven years ago," he said, pitching his voice to carry under the dome. "Not by the original maker. The work is finer than the rest of the mechanism — too fine for a repair. It was rebuilt by a man who intended something more than timekeeping." He lifted out the rear plate, the one he had cleaned by candlelight over four nights, and held it to the light. "Punched holes, Your Honor. In the pattern of the wheels. At first I took them for decoration. Then I laid the letter beside them."

He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it forty times in the dark, and now that it came, it came steady. "The holes correspond to pinpricks in the paper — marks so small no one would see them unless they knew to look. Together, plate and letter spell names. Dates. A confession." He looked at Harrow directly, and found to his surprise that he was not afraid anymore. "They spell *Harrow*."

The dome room made a sound like the sea drawing back from a shore.

"Forgery," Harrow said, rising. "A forged plate, a forged letter, produced by a tradesman with an interest in—"

"Then let the plate be examined by any smith in this town," Silas said. "Let it be compared against the other work of the clock. Let the pinpricks be matched hole to mark. Iron does not lie, Your Honor. It only waits."

"Seize him."

It took Silas a moment to understand that the words had come from Harrow, that the judge had said it aloud, in front of forty witnesses and his own jury — *seize him* — the way a man swats at a fly. Cade moved first, hand going inside his coat, and two of Harrow's men came up the stair behind him.

But the crowd did not part.

It was not a heroic thing. That was what Silas would remember afterward — how unheroic it was. The fishwife simply did not move out of the stairwell, because she had paid her penny to see and she had not finished seeing. Then a student stood in front of her. Then the butcher's apprentice, arms crossed, enormous, immovable as a door. Quill stepped forward with his spectacles flashing and said, in the voice of a man announcing a lecture, "I must ask you to observe the decorum of this observatory, gentlemen," and somehow that absurd sentence became a wall.

Cade looked at the crowd. He looked at Harrow. And Silas saw him make a calculation — quick, cold, complete — and arrive somewhere none of them expected.

"You're finished," Cade said quietly to the judge. Not loudly. Almost kindly. "You understand that? You've finished yourself." He put his hat back on properly, pulled it down, and left. His men followed. Nobody stopped them. Nobody dared.

Harrow stood alone in the middle of the dome room with his cane and his composure, and the composure went last, draining out of him like water through a cracked bell. He opened his mouth. Whatever he meant to say, he never said it, because the fishwife spat at his feet, and the sound of the crowd changed — became something older than law — and Harrow turned and went down the stairs as fast as a man of his age could go, and the crowd went after him, not touching him, never touching him, just *following*, a slow river of bodies carrying him down toward the market square where the assizes were sitting and where Lisle, Silas knew, was even now rising to speak.

Quill put a hand on Silas's shoulder. "Go home," the astronomer said. "Whatever happens next, it won't happen here. Go home."

Silas went. But he did not go fast enough, and he saw it before he reached the bottom of the hill: a thread of black smoke standing up against the gray sky above Tanner's Lane, thin and straight as a plumb line.

* * *

Marguerite had been left at the workshop with strict instructions and a locked door, and she had obeyed the instructions for nearly two hours, which she considered generous. She was mending a hem by the window when she saw the smoke, and then she saw the men.

There were three of them, coming up the lane at a walk, not hurrying, the way men walk when they want to be seen. She knew one of them. The hat. The way he carried his shoulders like a coat hung wrong. Cade.

She did what Silas had taught her. She did not scream; she counted. One man stayed at the corner, watching the street. Two came to the door. There was a smell of oil and pitch, sudden and strong, and then a sound like a match being struck inside a drum, and orange light bloomed across the cobbles outside the window, and Marguerite understood all at once, with the terrible clarity children keep for exactly such moments, that they were not robbing the workshop.

They were erasing it.

She ran for the back stair, the one down to the drying yard, and got the door open, and the smoke was already coming through the floorboards in ropes, and she heard the front windows go — a soft glassy crash, almost polite — and then the fire found its voice, and it roared.

* * *

Silas ran. He ran down the hill with his coat flapping and his lungs full of knives, and the crowd at his back turned as one animal when they saw where he was running, and some of them ran with him. He came around the corner into Tanner's Lane and the heat hit him like a wall swung open — the workshop's front was a lattice of flame, beautiful and obscene, every window a mouth, and the sign above the door, *WHITCOMBE & PEMBERTON*, the new sign, the proud sign, painted only in the spring, was curling and blackening at its edges.

"Marguerite!" His own voice sounded foreign to him. "MARGUERITE!"

And then she came out of the smoke around the side of the building, coughing, her hair wild, her sleeve scorched, alive, alive — and he caught her and held her hard enough to hurt and felt her heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird, and for three full seconds the world contained nothing else.

Then he heard the second voice, and his blood went cold.

"She's safe! The girl's safe — I've got her!" It was the cooper's wife, pulling Marguerite away from him, and that meant the voice he'd heard was—

Whitcombe came out of the smoke.

He was carrying the regulator. Of course he was carrying the regulator. Eleven months of work, the commission piece, the thing that would have saved them, wrapped in a leather apron against his chest, and he walked out of the burning doorway with the peculiar unhurried dignity of a man carrying a sleeping child, and Silas saw that his sleeves were on fire and that he did not seem to know it.

"Master—" Silas screamed it. He didn't remember screaming it. "MASTER!"

They brought him down together, Silas and the cooper and two others, and beat the flames out of his clothes with bare hands and coats, and the smell was the worst thing Silas had ever known, and Whitcombe looked up at him from the cobbles with his eyebrows gone and half his hair and his face blistered red and black, and he smiled.

"It's not damaged," Whitcombe said. His voice came out in rags. "The regulator. Check the balance spring. I wrapped it proper."

"Master, hold still, we've sent for the surgeon—"

"Check the balance spring," Whitcombe said again, insistently, and then his eyes rolled back and he sagged, and the cooper's wife began to weep openly while she pressed wet cloth to his arms.

The bucket chain formed without anyone ordering it — the whole town, it seemed, passing leather buckets hand to hand, though everyone present understood the workshop was already lost. They were saving the neighbors' roofs, nothing more. Silas knelt in the gutter holding his master's burned hand as gently as he had ever held any mechanism, and Marguerite knelt opposite him, her small scorched fingers resting on the old man's wrist, feeling the pulse there the way Silas had taught her to feel a train of wheels through a case — faint, quick, stubborn.

"He's breathing," she said. "Silas. He's breathing."

Down the lane, unhurried, Cade paused at the corner and looked back at the fire, at Silas kneeling in the water and ash, and touched the brim of his hat — a small, precise, courteous gesture, the salute of one professional to another — and walked away into the smoke.

Silas watched him go. He did not move to follow. There was nothing in him that could move except his hands, and his hands were holding Whitcombe's pulse, counting it, keeping time.

The regulator lay on the cobbles between them, wrapped in its scorched leather apron, untouched. Around it the workshop that had raised him went up in sparks that rose straight into the darkening sky like a swarm of burning bees, like a thousand tiny gears flying apart, like everything he had ever owned returning at last to the air.

Marguerite's hand closed over his.

"Is he going to die?" she asked. Her voice was very small, and very steady, the way voices get when a child has decided to be brave and is paying for it.

Silas bent his head over his master's ruined hand and listened to the blood tick beneath the skin, faint and quick and stubborn, and answered the only true thing he knew.

"Not tonight," he said. "Tonight, he keeps time."
