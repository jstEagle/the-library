## 19. Ashes and Accounts

The smoke did not leave the workshop for three days.

It hung in the joists of the house next door, in Whitcombe's own hair, in the wool of his coat, so that everywhere he went he carried the ruin with him like a second skin. He had stood in the street through the worst of it, an old man with his arms pinned to his sides by two constables who thought they were saving his life, and he had watched the fire eat the place where he had spent forty years of mornings. The sign — *Whitcombe, Clockmaker* — had gone last, curling at the edges, the gilt letters blistering one by one before the whole board dropped into the flames with a sound he would hear for the rest of his days: not a crash but a sigh, almost relieved, as if the shop itself had been waiting to be let go.

Now he stood in the wreckage in his shirtsleeves, though the morning was cold enough to show his breath, and reckoned accounts the way a clockmaker reckons everything: piece by piece, tooth by tooth, what was lost against what remained.

The benches were charcoal. The drawers of brass stock had fused into a single ingot, a grotesque sculpture of melted wheels and pinions that some part of him itched to salvage and a larger part knew was fit only for the scrap-man. His father's loupe — the one he'd worn at his eye since he was younger than Silas — had been in his waistcoat pocket when he ran back in, and he found it now, cracked down the middle, still useful if he held it just so. He turned it over twice and put it away. A man could weep over a loupe or he could get on with the day. He was too old and too tired to do both.

What remained, then. Let the books be honest.

There was the regulator.

It sat on the trestle outside the burnt shell, under an oilcloth Marguerite had fetched from somewhere, wrapped like a wounded thing. Whitcombe lifted the cloth each time he passed it, not because he doubted it lived but because he needed to see it living. The case was scorched along one flank; the lacquer had gone the colour of old tea where the heat had licked it. But the movement inside — Silas's movement, built to the observatory standard, every wheel cut true — had survived without a scratch, and when he touched the pendulum gently with one finger it swung, and swung, and kept its time as though fire were merely weather.

He had gone in after it. That was the fact of himself now, the fact he turned over at night when sleep declined to come: an old man had walked into a burning building for a clock, and come out again, and the price of it was written on his body in bands of gauze from wrist to elbow. The surgeon had used words like *fortunate* and *remarkable*, and Whitcombe had nodded and thought of the smell of his own scorching sleeve, and said nothing. What was there to say? He would do it again tomorrow. Any craftsman worth his salt would understand. It was not a clock. It was proof — proof that the boy he'd taken off the street twenty years ago could build something no flame could argue with, and proof, more stubbornly, of everything else: the letter, the cipher, the names punched in brass, the whole terrible machinery of truth that Silas had carried into the assizes while Whitcombe had begged him to burn it.

The regulator had been in the courtroom's orbit, you see. It was the demonstration model, the little sibling of the great clock up the hill. Lisle the advocate had wanted it produced alongside the observatory plates, and so it had been sitting wrapped and ready by the door when Cade came back with his torch. If it had been upstairs among the benches it would be slag now, and with it half the corroboration that had hanged nobody and saved one woman entire. Instead it lived, and Mrs. Aubry lived, and Judge Harrow —

Harrow was running. Everyone knew it by the second evening, the way a town knows weather coming. A magistrate's chair does not empty itself quietly; there were clerks, and there were letters, and there was the small feverish traffic of men who had served a falling man and wished suddenly to have never known him. By the third day the story had shape: Harrow had left in the night with one servant and a strongbox, bound for the coast, meaning — everyone assumed — a boat to France or Holland, some port where English writs grew thin.

Whitcombe heard the news from the baker's boy and felt nothing he could properly name. Not triumph. Triumph requires surprise, and he had stopped being surprised somewhere between the crowd surging in the courtroom doorway and Cade stepping back into the street with fire in his hand. What he felt was closer to arithmetic. A man who had murdered a printer's wife and let an innocent woman walk toward the gallows for it was now a fugitive with a strongbox and one servant, and the country that had made him a judge was chasing him with all the slow, grinding patience of a tide. Justice, Whitcombe reflected, wiping soot from his hands with a rag already grey with use, was not a thing the law contained. The law was a clockwork someone had built, and like all clockworks it failed sometimes, and needed hands — young hands, brave ones, foolish ones — to open the plates and set it right.

Silas had opened the plates. Whatever happened at the coast, that much was done and could not be unwound.

* * *

They found Harrow on the fourth day, and the town learned of it the way towns learn everything: imperfectly, loudly, and all at once.

A riding officer out of Rye had taken him at an inn yard near Winchelsea, of all places — not fleeing but *waiting*, the story went, waiting for a boat that had been delayed by weather, sitting in a plain coat with his strongbox beside him on the bench like any merchant gone to seed. There was something in that detail which Whitcombe could not shake. A judge, caught sitting. All those years on the bench above other men's lives, weighing and pronouncing, and at the end he was the man on the bench, waiting, while the weather decided.

Cade had vanished. That was the other news, and it was the news that made Whitcombe lock the door at night and keep a poker within reach. No warrant for Cade existed yet — or none that anyone admitted to — and a man who burned a workshop in front of a hundred witnesses and simply walked away is a man who believes he has friends still standing. Whitcombe had seen him once, across the courtroom crowd, and the look the agent gave Silas had stayed with him like a splinter. Not hatred. *Accounting.* As though Silas were a debt entered in a ledger, to be collected when convenient.

Well. Whitcombe had kept ledgers all his life. He knew how debts behaved. They waited, and they gathered interest, and pretending they were paid did not make them so. He said nothing of this to the children. There are fears a house can carry and fears it cannot, and he chose — deliberately, the way he chose everything now, knowing the cost of choosing wrong — to carry this one himself.

* * *

On the fifth morning, Marguerite appeared at his bedside before dawn with tea she had made herself, which was to say: hot water, stewed leaves, and far too much sugar, brewed with the ferocious concentration of a seven-year-old performing surgery.

"You're not to get up," she announced. "Your hands are bandaged. I've decided."

"Have you." Whitcombe sat up anyway, wincing, and accepted the cup because refusing her would have been cruelty. "And what am I permitted, in your medical opinion?"

"To sit. And to tell me how the escapement works. Silas says I'm to ask you, because he's mending the roof of Mr. Quill's shed and he talks to himself when he works and doesn't answer questions."

"He's right about that. He's talked to himself since he was nine." Whitcombe drank the terrible tea and looked at her over the rim — this child with the soot finally washed out of her hair and a gravity in her eyes that no child should own, earned the hard way. "Very well. The escapement. Attend, madam, for this is the whole secret of clocks..."

He told her about the tick and the tock — how a wheel forever wants to run away with itself, spinning its strength out in one wild rush, and how the pallets catch it, stop it, release it, catch it again. How the ticking is not the sound of the clock going but the sound of it being *stopped*, ten thousand times a minute, so that it goes instead of merely falling. She listened with her whole body, chin on her fists, and when he finished she was quiet a moment.

"So it stops the fast thing," she said, "and gives the time out slowly."

"In essence, yes."

"That's what Silas did," Marguerite said, matter-of-fact, dreadful, clear. "With Judge Harrow. He stopped him."

Whitcombe looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the light was coming up grey over the roofs, and somewhere down the lane a cart was already selling milk, ordinary as ever, the world insisting on its rounds. He thought of all the things an adult might say — about justice being complicated, about courts and evidence, about not putting such weight on a child — and discarded every one of them, because she was not wrong, and lying to her would be the first brick in rebuilding exactly the kind of world that had killed her mother.

"Yes," he said. "That's what he did."

She nodded, satisfied, and went to fetch more coal, and Whitcombe sat with his bandaged hands around the warm cup and let himself, for one full minute, feel the grief he had been holding off like a creditor. Forty years. Benches he had planed himself. His father's tools in their rolls, gone. The smell of oil and boxwood that had been the smell of every morning since he was apprenticed — gone, and with it the particular silence of a workshop at night, which is a kind of company. He had told himself he was too old to start again and then remembered, with something that was nearly a laugh, that he had started again once before — a frightened journeyman with a dead friend's son on his doorstep and a sedition charge hanging over the boy's father's name, and he had opened the door anyway.

He had opened the door then. He supposed he would open it again.

* * *

Silas came at dusk, smelling of Quill's shed and river mud, and the three of them ate bread and cheese off a cloth laid on the floor because Whitcombe had sold most of the furniture to pay the first of the debts. Afterwards, by candlelight, they did the accounts together, the boy writing, the girl fetching, the old man dictating figures he knew by heart: what the insurance would cover (a fraction), what the scrap-man would pay (less), what remained of the observatory commission (paid in full, God rest Quill's honourable soul, plus a bonus the astronomer had pressed on them with wet eyes).

At the bottom of the column, Silas paused with the pen.

"What remains," Whitcombe said, "is us. Write that."

"Master—"

"Write it. *One regulator, saved. One keeper's post, offered, pending answer. Two apprentices — *" he looked at the girl, who was pretending very hard not to listen while listening with every bone in her body, "*— one of them small. Debts: considerable. Reputation: improved beyond price. Prospects:"* he took the pen from Silas's hand, his bandaged fingers clumsy but steady enough, and wrote the word himself in his careful old-fashioned script: *good.*

Silas read it and something moved through his face — grief and guilt and a hope he was still learning to trust. "I cost you the workshop," he said quietly. "You begged me to burn the letter, and I—"

"I did beg you. And I have been ashamed of the begging ever since, and I expect to remain ashamed of it for a comfortable number of years, so don't rob me of the penance by apologising." Whitcombe capped the ink bottle with a click. "A workshop is timber and brass. I have rebuilt both before. But a man who knows what he stands for — that is not rebuilt, boy. That is either built once, early, or never. You built yours in front of a full courtroom while I was telling you to hide. So no. You owe me nothing but your labour, and I intend to collect it daily and at hard terms."

Marguerite, unable to contain herself another moment, climbed onto the bench beside them and put one small finger on the ledger. "And the new shop will have both names," she said. "Pemberton and Pemberton. Because your father is coming back. Isn't he. Silas got a letter."

The brothers-in-all-but-blood looked at each other over the child's head. Of course she knew. She knew everything; she always had.

"There's a letter," Silas admitted. "From the bindery. From my father. He writes that—" and here the boy's voice did something complicated, "—he writes that he wants to stand in the shop when it rises. That he owes the ground rent of his life to it, in a manner of speaking. That he'll come when I send for him."

"And will you send?"

Whitcombe watched his apprentice struggle with it — the man who had let him believe in a death for ten years, the handwriting he had known in an instant, the whole wound of it. And he saw the moment the struggle ended, the way one sees the pallet catch the wheel: a stopping, and then a going.

"Yes," Silas said. "Tomorrow. First light."

Marguerite wriggled with satisfaction, and Whitcombe blew out the candles except one, and sat a while after the children slept in the next room — the girl in his bed, the boy on a pallet across the door, positioned, Whitcombe noted with a dry old smile, exactly where a man would lie if he expected trouble from Cade and meant to meet it first.

The last candle guttered. In its light the saved regulator ticked on the trestle, scorched and steadfast, keeping perfect time amid the ruins — and Whitcombe, listening to it, understood that the account was not closed but merely balanced differently than he had ever expected. He had lost the workshop and gained a household. He had lost his tools and gained, of all unlikely inheritances, a daughter's fierce clarity and a son's unbending spine and — God help them all — a printer.

He rose, stiff and sore, and added one final line to the ledger by the dying candlelight, beneath *Prospects: good.*

*Received, this day:* he wrote. *Everything I did not know I lacked.*

Down the lane, late, a footstep paused outside the door — one beat, two — and moved on. Whitcombe stood very still in the dark, hand on the poker, and listened until the street was silent, and told himself it was only a neighbour.

But in the morning, wedged under the door, he would find a printed card, cheap paper, no name: a single line in a clerk's neat hand — *Debts are patient.*
