## 21. The Master Clock Runs

The morning of the trial's verdict had come and gone like any other morning, which was itself a miracle. Silas Pemberton stood in the observatory's great dome room with his sleeves rolled to the elbow and his hands still smelling of oil and brass polish, and he listened to the master clock tick.

It was not the ticking it had made when he first climbed these stairs with his toolbox under his arm — that broken, stumbling pulse, a heart with a stone caught in its chambers. This was something else. This was the sound a clock makes when every tooth of every wheel has been counted, weighed, forgiven. A deep, patient *tick*... *tick*... *tick*, so steady that after a minute Silas stopped hearing it as a separate thing at all and heard it instead as the room's own breathing.

Above him, through the slit windows of the dome, the sky was the color of pewter washed in milk. October was leaning toward November, and the mornings came later and colder each day. Below, the town went about its business — carts on the cobbles, gulls arguing over the fish market, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys into windless air. Somewhere down there, Mrs. Aubry was walking out of the assize house a free woman. Somewhere down there, Judge Harrow's name was being read from the bench not as an honor but as an indictment. And somewhere down there, in a cell that smelled of straw and despair, a man named Delacroix sat waiting to learn whether honesty, offered late, would be counted as anything worth sparing.

Silas could do nothing about any of it now. That was the strange mercy of this morning. The letter had done what letters can do; the clock had done what only clocks can do. What remained was in other hands, and his own hands were free at last to do the only thing they had ever truly wanted: to make a machine keep time.

He climbed the ladder to the movement and checked the great driving weight one more time, though he had checked it twice already since dawn. The cable ran true over the pulley, no kinks, no fray. The escapement — *his* escapement, rebuilt in those fevered nights while Cade's shadow moved along the street below — turned with the smoothness of poured water. He watched the pallets catch and release, catch and release, and felt again the old quiet joy that had brought him to Whitcombe's bench at nine years old, when a watch opened under his fingers had seemed like a city seen from above, all its streets lit and purposeful.

"Silas."

Mr. Quill stood at the foot of the ladder, holding two cups of coffee in hands that trembled slightly, as the astronomer's hands always did before an observation night. His spectacles were fogged at the edges from the cold of the stairwell. "You've been up here since six. I could hear you thinking through the floor."

"I wanted to hear it strike," Silas said. "Properly. From inside."

Quill handed up a cup. "The whole town will hear it strike. Half of them have forgotten there ever was a bell on this hill. They'll stand in their doorways like startled hens." He sipped, grimaced at the quality, sipped again. "You know what I told the committee? I told them the clock was beyond repair. Stripped escapement, plates corroded, no maker's marks, nothing. I said we'd need a new movement entirely, and the committee groaned and reached for their purses, and then I said — or rather, a young man said he'd try."

"You said that," Silas corrected him. "I said I'd see what I could do. There's a difference, and my master would want me to say so."

"Your master," Quill said, "is downstairs inspecting our barometer with the expression of a man auditing a rival's accounts. Come down when the hour strikes. There is a thing I wish to put to you, and I'd rather do it standing on solid ground than halfway up a ladder with your blood all in your ears."

* * *

Silas stayed anyway. Some appointments a man must keep with himself.

At five minutes to eleven he took up his position by the dial work, where he could watch the motion works through the inspection plate — the cannon pinion turning, the minute hand's ghost visible even here behind the scenes, the hour hand following at its dignified remove. He had set the clock against Quill's transit observations three nights running now, and three nights the error had been less than two seconds. Two seconds in a week. Old Huygens himself, could he have risen from his grave and climbed these stairs, might have raised an eyebrow of approval, and old Huygens had not been easy to please.

The minute hand crawled toward the twelve. Silas found his own breath shortening, absurdly, as though he were the mechanism about to be tested.

*Tick.* The lifting piece rose on the cam. *Tick.* He could feel the tension gathering in the strike train the way one feels a sneeze gathering, the whole weight of the great barrel winding itself toward release. Eleven fifty-nine and thirty seconds. Forty-five. Fifty. The last ticks seemed to come slower, each one a held note.

Then the train let go.

The bell above them — four hundred pounds of bell-metal cast before either of them was born — spoke for the eleven o'clock hour, and the sound went through the dome room like weather. It was not a pretty sound, exactly. It was vast and bronze and faintly rough-edged, a voice that had smoked too many pipes, and it filled the skull and shook dust from beams that had not been disturbed in years. But it was *true*. Every stroke fell exactly where it should fall, spaced with the perfect democracy of a well-cut escape wheel, and Silas stood in the middle of it with his eyes closed and his coffee going cold in his hand and understood, with a completeness that hurt somewhere beneath the ribs, that he had never in his life made anything whole until now.

Eleven strokes. Silence after, ringing silence, the kind that has a shape.

When he opened his eyes, Marguerite was standing at the foot of the ladder.

She had a way of arriving without arriving — a skill, Silas suspected, learned in the worst school there is, the school of children who must learn to be unseen. She wore the grey dress Whitcombe's neighbor Mrs. Tallow had cut down for her, and her hair was braided with a crookedness that announced Mrs. Tallow's fingers rather than her own. In her arms she carried the cat that had adopted the workshop sometime in September and refused all offers of eviction.

"Did you hear it?" she asked. Her voice had lost some of its flinching quality in recent weeks, though it kept a habit of asking permission even for questions. "From outside. The whole market stopped. Even the fishwives. Mr. Tallow says he hasn't heard it since he was small and his father lifted him onto a wall."

"It struck well," Silas said, and heard the pride in his own voice and did not trouble to hide it. Pride, he had learned, was another thing that could be spent honestly or dishonestly, and he meant to spend his honestly from now on.

"Can I hear it from inside?"

"There's no better seat in the house." He swung the ladder steady for her, and she climbed with the cat clinging to her shoulder like a grey epaulette, and together they stood beneath the great wheels while the clock ticked on toward noon with nothing whatever to fear.

* * *

Downstairs, the observatory's long room smelled of paper, ink, cold tea, and the particular mineral scent of instruments that live near stone walls. Quill had cleared a space at the big table — a rare courtesy; astronomers guard their table space the way dragons guard hoards — and spread upon it a single sheet covered in his cramped, upright hand.

Whitcombe stood nearby with his arms folded, wearing the face he wore when he wished to appear uninterested in a matter that interested him greatly. He had aged ten years in ten weeks, Silas thought, and stood straighter than he had in ten years too. Fire does one thing to a man and justice does another, and Whitcombe had taken a full measure of both.

"Sit," Quill said. "Both of you. Marguerite — yes, you may sit also, if the cat sits on the floor."

The cat, with the insolence of its kind, sat on the table. Nobody moved to stop it.

"The committee met yesterday evening," Quill began, "without you, which was deliberate, because committees behave worse when they're nervous and they are always nervous around craftsmen. I'll spare you the speeches. The substance is this." He tapped the paper. "The clock runs. It runs better than it has run in forty years, if my transit comparisons mean anything, and I am given to understand they mean something. It will need a keeper. Not a repairer — a keeper. Someone to clean it weekly, adjust it seasonally, wind the weights, watch the rate against the stars, and report to this institution any deviation greater than a second. The post carries a salary of forty pounds a year, a room in the north annex, and the title of Keeper of the Clock, which I grant you sounds grander than it pays."

Silas looked at the paper without touching it, the way one looks at a letter that might change everything.

"Forty pounds," Whitcombe said flatly. "And a room. And the boy living a mile from my bench."

"The boy," Quill said gently, "whose master's workshop burned to the ground eight days ago, whose tools are ash, whose stock is ash, and who has spent those eight days sleeping on your floor and working nights to keep this clock alive. I'm not stealing him, Master Whitcombe. I'm feeding him. You're welcome to feed him too — the door of this observatory swings both ways, and I'll tell you plainly that half the instruments in this building need a Pemberton's hands on them far more urgently than the clock ever did."

There was a silence in which Silas became aware of his own heartbeat, quick and uneven, nothing like his clock's.

"Say your part," Whitcombe said at last, gruffly, to Silas rather than to Quill. "This isn't mine to accept or refuse."

Silas looked down at his hands. They were scarred now in places they hadn't been in September — a burn across the left thumb from the fire, a deep nick from the escapement work — and they were steadier than they had ever been. Ten weeks ago he had believed history happened elsewhere, to other people, in capitals and courtrooms and printing houses, while men like him merely cleaned the gears. Now history had slept in his cellar, hidden in his clock, and stood trial in his presence, and he knew better. History happened wherever someone chose, at some cost, to tell the truth. It happened at benches. It happened in workshops. Sometimes it happened at the top of a tower, in a room full of turning brass.

"Master," he said, "I'll take the post — but not as a leaving. As an anchoring." He met Whitcombe's eye. "Rebuild the shop. Rebuild it with your name on the lintel, and my father's beside it if they let him come home, and mine under theirs when I've earned it. I'll keep the clock in the mornings and cut wheels for you in the afternoons, and Quill can have whatever hours are left over. The town needs a clockmaker again. It needs one more than it needs a keeper, truthfully — but it can have both, if nobody is too proud to share."

Quill's mouth twitched. "I have never in my life been accused of pride."

"Then you'll be the first astronomer in England," said Whitcombe, and there — briefly, like sun through moving cloud — was the old dry light in him, the man who had once laughed at a nine-year-old's first crooked gear and kept it in a drawer for twenty years.

He put out his hand then, and Silas took it, and neither of them said anything worth writing down, because the things that mattered between them had never needed saying aloud. Love expressed as silence, Silas thought. He was learning to answer in the same language.

* * *

They walked home as the afternoon thinned. Whitcombe went ahead with the barometer he had finally, grudgingly, agreed to service, and Marguerite fell into step beside Silas with her hands pushed into the pockets Mrs. Tallow had sewn for her, which she treated as the greatest luxury of her new life.

The town had changed its face since the trial. Not everywhere — grief and ruin do not wash off streets in a fortnight, and the black shell of the workshop still stood at the end of Tanner's Row like a pulled tooth. But people nodded to Silas now in the street who had never met his eyes before. The fishwife with the loud voice pressed a herring on Marguerite and refused payment. Two boys trailed them for a hundred yards hoping, evidently, to glimpse the famous clock, and were disappointed to find only a tired young man and a small girl, and drifted away.

Marguerite was quiet for most of the walk, which was unlike her lately. She had grown talkative the way starved things grow hungry all at once, questions tumbling out of her by the dozen — why gears were triangular, why the moon kept different hours, whether cats could tell time. So her silence now had a texture to it, and Silas had learned, at cost, to attend to textures.

Halfway up the hill she stopped.

"Silas."

"Mm?"

She looked at the cobbles. Her boots — good boots, bought with Quill's money, laced wrong as always — were very still. "That night," she said. "The first night. When my mother knocked."

Silas went very careful, the way one goes careful near a wound. "I remember it."

"She said forget my face. That was the instruction. Forget my face, and keep her safe, and don't ask." Marguerite's chin came up a fraction, fighting some older habit of shrinking. "Everyone keeps saying it's finished now. The judge is caught, and Mrs. Aubry is free, and Papa—" she stumbled on the word, testing it, "—Papa is going to be spared, Mr. Lisle says, because he helped. And everyone says it's over."

"And?"

Her eyes came up, grey as the October sea, and steady. "Nobody asked me if I could forget her face. They just told me to. And I tried — I truly tried, the way she said — but I can't. I know the exact place her front tooth is chipped. I know she hums when she's frightened so you won't notice she's crying." Her voice wobbled once and hardened. "So I wanted to ask properly. Now it's safe. Now nobody can be hurt by it." She drew a breath that shook only at the edges. "May I stop forgetting her? May I remember her out loud? Because keeping her inside is very heavy, and I've carried it since March, and I should like to set it down."

The clock in Silas's chest — the human one, the badly-made one, the one that had run fast and slow and stopped altogether in these ten weeks — turned over once and resumed.

He knelt on the wet cobbles, heedless of his trousers, so that their eyes were level. Behind her, at the top of the hill, the observatory dome stood against the darkening sky, and inside it the master clock ticked on toward the next hour with perfect faith, keeping time not against them but for them, all of them, the dead and the nearly-dead and the delivered.

"Marguerite," he said, "you may remember her as loudly as you like, for as long as you like, and I will listen to every word of it. Her face, her tooth, her humming — all of it. We'll write it down, if you want. We'll write it all down and keep it beside the letter, and no one will ever instruct you to forget again."

Something in her shoulders unlocked, some small sentry that had stood armed since March finally standing down. She nodded, once, businesslike, wiping her nose with the back of her hand in the manner of one concluding negotiations.

"Good," she said. "Because I've remembered quite a lot. And there's something else I remembered — something about the night she left me. Something she said to the woman who carried me, before the knock. I didn't think it mattered before, but now Papa's coming home, and I think—" she frowned, working it through, a child assembling a mechanism she couldn't yet name, "—I think it's about *him*. About who else was looking for us. And Silas — I think she said they'd look for the letter again."

Far off across the roofs, the observatory bell began to strike the hour — and with it, at the edge of town, a coach that neither of them had sent for was already coming up the coast road, carrying a passenger whose arrival would test whether forgiveness, like a clock, could be mended once it had been broken.
