## 22. Marguerite and the Letter

The workshop smelled of beeswax and iron filings, and Marguerite had learned that the two smells together meant morning.

She came down the outside stair at half past seven, as she always did, because Silas said a clockmaker's day began when the town's clocks agreed on the hour, and if you were late you were already lying to everyone. The sign above the door had been repainted in the spring — **PEMBERTON & PEMBERTON** — and she still stopped beneath it sometimes, the way other people stopped before church, to feel the strangeness of it. Two names. One of them hers by nothing but choice. Whitcombe had painted it himself, standing on a wobbly ladder with his sleeves rolled, refusing help, and when he finished he'd looked at it for a long time and said, "Well. It'll do," which was how he said *I am proud past all bearing*.

Inside, the benches were clean. That was new still — a year after the fire, and Marguerite could not walk across the floorboards without remembering that they were new floorboards, that underneath them lay the old ones, charred and broken, that Whitcombe had insisted be carried out and buried behind the garden wall like something dead that deserved a grave. He never spoke of the night Cade set the fire. He never spoke of the burns on his hands either, though the left one still stiffened in cold weather, so that he held his teacup in a way that looked like affectation and was really arithmetic: counting what the hand would forgive him for lifting.

Silas was already at the long bench, bent over a movement no bigger than a saucer, his loupe screwed into his eye. He did not look up when she came in, but he moved his elbow two inches to the left, clearing space for her stool, and that was how he said good morning.

Marguerite hung her cloak on its peg — her own peg, at her own height, drilled into the wall by Silas without comment on the day she arrived for good — and took down the frame.

She did this every morning too. It was not superstition exactly. Or perhaps it was, but she had decided that some superstitions were simply manners continued past their necessity, and this was one of those.

The frame hung beside the bench, at eye level, where a master clockmaker might hang a certificate or a portrait of a patron. Instead it held a sheet of paper gone soft and brown at the folds, its wax seal cracked down the middle like a little dried plum, its handwriting slanting and urgent and, to anyone who did not know, entirely ordinary. A letter. Just a letter. If a customer asked — and they sometimes did, customers being curious creatures — Silas would say, "It's the reason we're all alive," and smile in a way that made them afraid to ask more.

Marguerite touched the glass once, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal.

*Forget my face.*

Her mother had said it on the doorstep, seven years ago now, or nearly — the anniversary was in November, and last November they had eaten supper in silence and nobody had explained why, because nobody needed to. Forget my face. And Marguerite had tried, God knew she had tried, huddled in the workshop while boots passed in the street, pressing her palms against her eyes until she saw stars. But faces are not like letters. You cannot burn a face. It comes back in dreams, in the turn of a stranger's head in the market, in the shape of her own reflection growing older in the glass — because every year her chin lengthened and her eyes deepened and the woman in the mirror became less a child's memory and more a prophecy, until Marguerite understood that forgetting her mother's face would have required forgetting her own, and that was a price even obedience could not pay.

So instead she had done the opposite thing. She had remembered everything. She had told Silas about the judge's carriage with its blinds drawn, about the printed page wrapped around the loaf of bread, the ink bleeding through into the crust. She had sat in a courtroom at nine years old, her legs not reaching the floor, and said it all out loud while Judge Harrow watched her with eyes like wet stones, and she had not cried until afterward, and then only a little, and only where Silas could see.

And now the letter was not evidence anymore. The assizes were over; Harrow was a story people told quietly, the way you tell stories about lightning; Mrs. Aubry had been freed in April and sent them a ham at Christmas with a note that made Whitcombe blow his nose very hard and blame the dust. The letter belonged to no court now. It belonged to the wall.

"Stop communing with the paper," Silas said, without looking up, "and come look at this."

* * *

He had the escapement laid out on a chamois cloth, disassembled into its parts in a row, each piece in its place the way he always arranged things — anchor, pallets, escape wheel, the small jeweled pivots kept in a paper fold like sweets. Marguerite climbed onto her stool and put her elbows on the bench, which was allowed, though not her whole arms, which was not.

"What is it?"

"This is the escapement." He said it the way he said everything important: plainly, as if the words were too heavy for decoration. "You've heard me say the word a hundred times. Now you're going to learn what it means."

"I know what it means."

"You know what it does. Those aren't the same thing." He picked up the anchor between finger and thumb. "Everything in a clock wants to run away. The weight pulls, the spring pushes, the wheels want to spin until there's nothing left of them. The escapement is the part that says *no*. Not stop — stop is easy, any wedge can stop a wheel. It says *no, not yet*, and then *yes, now*, a thousand times an hour, forever. It takes the running-away and cuts it into pieces small enough to count." He set the anchor down. "Time isn't a river, Marguerite. Everyone says it's a river. It's not. It's a wheel that would spin wild if something brave didn't keep catching it."

"And the tick," she said. "The tick is the catching."

Something moved through his face — quick, there and gone, the way weather moves over water. She had learned to watch for it. It was the look he got whenever she said something that proved she had been listening all along, when he thought she was only keeping her hands busy.

"The tick is the catching," he agreed. "Now. Your father—" He stopped. Corrected himself, gently, the way he always corrected himself now, both of them learning a new grammar for their family. "Delacroix sent us a part, once, in the middle of the worst night of our lives. Do you remember?"

"I remember the rain. I remember you coming back with me under your coat and Mr. Cade's man nearly—"

"Yes. Well. Among other things, that night taught me that an escapement can be built by someone who will never see the clock it goes into. Who trusts the measurements of a stranger. Who works blind, in the dark, for people he loves and has never met." Silas picked up the escape wheel and turned it so the lamplight ran along its teeth. "That's the second thing an escapement is. Not just the catching. The trusting. Every clock is two people agreeing on a rhythm — the maker and the keeper — even when they're strangers. Even when they've never spoken."

Marguerite thought about that. She was eleven now, and she had learned that Silas's lessons were never only about clocks, and that pretending otherwise was rude.

"Like you and Papa Delacroix," she said. "Before he stood up in court."

"Before he stood up in court." Silas's voice went quiet. "He worked blind for ten years. Then he chose to see. That's the whole trade, if you want to know. Anyone can cut gears. Choosing to see — that's the apprenticeship."

He slid the chamois cloth toward her. On it, apart from the others, lay a second escape wheel — smaller, rougher, its teeth unfinished, bright metal showing where file-marks crossed like plowed fields.

"That one's yours," Silas said.

Marguerite did not move. In a year and more of living above the workshop, of sweeping filings and sorting screws and learning the names of tools, she had never been given a part. She had been given a peg for her cloak and a stool at the bench and a place at the table, and those had been everything, more than everything. But this was different. This was metal.

"It's not finished," she said, because her voice needed somewhere to go.

"No. It won't finish itself, either." He set a file in her hand — a small one, worn smooth at the handle from years of his own grip, and she understood that this too was a kind of giving, that the smoothness was ten years of Silas Pemberton pressed into the wood and now handed over. "Teeth first. Even teeth. Count your strokes. When you've done the wheel, I'll show you the pallets, and when you've done the pallets, we'll fit it to the little regulator on the shelf, and when that runs—" he shrugged, elaborately casual, fooling neither of them, "—when that runs, you'll have made time. Actual time. Out of nothing but patience and a file."

"And if I ruin it?"

"There's more metal in the drawer." He paused. "There's always more metal in the drawer. That's the third lesson, and it's the best one."

* * *

She filed all morning.

The sound of it filled the workshop — a small silver whisper, stroke and lift, stroke and lift — and around it the other sounds arranged themselves like instruments finding their places in a piece of music: Whitcombe humming tunelessly at the far bench where he polished cases, the great regulator on the wall ticking its low patient heart, and beneath everything, faint but constant, the sound of the observatory clock carrying down from the hill, striking the quarter-hours over the town. Quill had offered Silas the keeper's post in the spring, and Silas had taken it, and now three mornings a week he walked up through the mist to wind and check the master clock whose guts had once hidden his father's cipher, and Marguerite liked to imagine the two of them up there together, the man and the machine, keeping faith.

At noon Whitcombe called a halt, as he always did, and produced bread and cheese and apples from the canvas bag, and they ate standing up among the benches because sitting down was for people with leisure. Marguerite noticed — she noticed everything now; testimony teaches you that — that Whitcombe ate with his bad hand curled around the apple rather than gripping it, and that he caught her noticing, and that he winked, which was his way of saying *mind your own business, girl*, which was his way of saying *thank you for minding*.

"Pemberton," he said to Silas, mouth full. "The Rector's been in again. Wants a clock for the vestry. Says he'll pay in prayers."

"Tell him we take coin."

"Tell him yourself, I'm not your clerk." But he was smiling around the cheese, and Marguerite saw what she had seen a hundred times and never tired of seeing: that Whitcombe had gotten back something he'd lost. Not the old workshop — that was ash under the garden, mourned and buried. Something else. Something that had room in it for three.

After lunch she went back to her wheel.

By mid-afternoon her shoulder ached and her thumb had a blister coming and eleven of the thirty-six teeth were done, each one counted, each one even, and she held the wheel up to the window and watched the light come through the gaps and thought: *I made these holes. I decided where the light gets through.* And then, because her mind had been circling it all day the way a moth circles a lamp, she let herself think the other thing.

*My mother decided where the light got through too.*

She set the wheel down carefully and went and stood in front of the framed letter again.

"Silas," she said.

"Mm." He was fitting a mainspring, his whole attention in his fingers.

"When I'm grown. When I can find her. Will you help me look? For my mother?"

The workshop went very quiet. Even Whitcombe's humming stopped.

Silas set down the mainspring. He came and stood beside her, and for a moment they both looked at the letter — at the browned paper, the cracked seal, the handwriting that had named a murderer and saved a widow and raised a dead man from his grave.

"She said forget her face," Silas said at last. "But I've been thinking about that instruction for a year, and I'll tell you what I've concluded. She wasn't asking you to forget. She was asking the world to. There's a difference. The world forgot, and it nearly hanged an innocent woman for it. You remembered, and that's why Mrs. Aubry is eating Christmas ham and Judge Harrow is a cautionary tale." He looked down at her. "So yes. When you're grown. We'll look. All three of us. And Marguerite — she'll know your face too. Wherever she is. Whatever name she goes by. Faces are the one thing that can't be filed false."

Marguerite nodded, because her throat had closed, and after a moment Silas put his hand briefly on top of her head — the whole blessing, the entire ceremony, everything he knew how to say without words — and went back to his bench.

Whitcombe cleared his throat loudly and announced that if the Rector's clock was going to be refused, someone should fetch more candles, as the light was going.

* * *

Evening came early in November. By four the lamps were lit and the windows had gone dark and the workshop was a small gold island with the night pressing at the glass, and Marguerite sat at her stool finishing tooth twelve, stroke and lift, stroke and lift, while the men talked shop in the low murmuring way that meant the day was closing.

The knock came at six.

Three knocks, unhurried, spaced evenly — and Marguerite's file stopped, because a year ago knocks had meant boots and warrants and fire, and some sounds the body never unlearns. But Silas glanced up, listened, and something in his face eased before he even reached the door.

He opened it, and the cold came in smelling of woodsmoke and coming frost, and standing in it was a tall man with grey coming into his dark hair, holding his hat in his hands like a schoolboy, like a penitent, like a man who had spent a year learning how to arrive at a door he had no right to.

"Delacroix," Whitcombe said, gruff, already pulling out the fourth stool, which lived by the stove now and had for months.

"Pemberton." His father's voice. Her father too, if she wanted it to be, and she was deciding, slowly, in her own time, that she did. "I was passing through. I brought—" he lifted a small wrapped bundle, awkward, "—brass stock. For the girl's wheel. If she's still working on it."

"I'm on tooth twelve," Marguerite said. "They're even. Come and see."

And Delacroix smiled — the same smile Silas made, she saw it plain as a signature, the family written in the face after all — and he came in out of the cold, and the door shut behind him, and the clock on the wall caught the wheel, and let it go, and caught it again.

Tick, said the workshop. Tick. Time, cut into pieces small enough to hold.
