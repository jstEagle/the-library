## 1. The Letters I Wasn't Meant to Read

The letters came to Cromwell House the way secrets come to any great house: by the morning post, in bundles tied with string, and through Petra Crane's hands before they reached anyone else's.

It was not, strictly speaking, her duty. Her duty was the Daimler — its brass lamps polished, its tyres inspected, its engine turned over each morning so that Marguerite Ashworth might descend the steps at nine sharp and be carried into London like a small storm in a grey motoring coat. But Mrs. Petch, the housekeeper, had weak eyes and a horror of admitting it, and so the sorting tray migrated years ago to the kitchen table where Petra took her tea, and Petra — who had learned to read at eleven from a discarded copy of *Jane Eyre* and had never quite forgiven herself for how hungry she'd been for it — read what passed beneath her fingers.

Not everything. She had rules. Bills she skimmed; invitations she barely touched; anything marked *personal* in Marguerite's looping hand she set aside unopened, mostly. It was the movement correspondence she devoured: the appeals for funds, the reports from branches in Manchester and Bristol, the furious exchanges with the WSPU, the careful coded language women used when they suspected their post was watched. Petra knew things about England that no chauffeur ought to know. She knew which safe houses had been raided, which magistrates could be softened with a donation, which of Marguerite's lieutenants drank. She kept all of it folded away inside herself like linen in a chest, and told no one, and was, on the whole, content.

Contentment, she had learned, was a discipline. You rose at half five. You checked the tyres. You did not look too long at anyone.

* * *

Marguerite Ashworth was fifty-one years old and carried herself like a woman who had decided, sometime around the turn of the century, that she would not be made small by anyone ever again. She was not beautiful in the way magazines meant it; she was handsome the way a courthouse is handsome — all severity and intention, grey eyes that weighed a person to the ounce. When she said *Petra*, the name sounded like a verdict being delivered, usually a favourable one.

"You've read my post again," Marguerite said one Tuesday, not looking up from her correspondence as Petra held her coat.

"The Bristol branch writes in a hand I can't help noticing," Petra said. "Their secretary is frightened. Someone's been asking questions at her lodgings."

"And you noticed this because—"

"Because frightened people press harder. The pen digs in."

Marguerite did look up then, and for a moment something moved behind the courthouse face — not warmth exactly, but an accounting. "You will tell me these things," she said. "Always."

It was the closest thing to tenderness the household offered, and Petra took it the way a poor woman takes coin: quickly, without counting change.

* * *

Edmund Ashworth came and went like good weather — pleasant, unreliable, gone before you could trust it. He was fifty-five but wore his age loosely, silver at the temples, a laugh that arrived half a second ahead of his jokes. He sat at his sister's table four nights out of seven and charmed even Mrs. Petch, who trusted no living soul except the vicar and her rheumatism.

He also gave money. Generously, publicly, with his name printed in the suffrage papers in bold type: *E. Ashworth, Esq.* And here was the thing Petra's fingers knew before her mind admitted it — the sums never added up. A cheque of forty pounds announced in the newsletter would appear in Marguerite's ledger as twenty-five. A promised hundred for the Bristol fund would arrive as sixty, with a note blaming the delay at his bank. Marguerite, who could audit a government department from memory, never once queried it. That was the fault line running under the whole house: not that Edmund stole, but that Marguerite chose not to see it, and everyone pretended the floor was level.

Petra began keeping her own arithmetic in a cheap notebook hidden inside the spare-tyre box. She told herself it was habit. She told herself a great many things.

* * *

The child came on a Thursday, at dawn, when the sky over the mews was the colour of dishwater.

Petra was up early for a run to Ealing — a deputation, banners already rolled in the boot — and had just gotten the Daimler's bonnet up when she saw the shape at the garden gate. Small. Still. Watching the house with the patience of something that had learned waiting was safer than knocking.

She crossed the wet gravel. Up close the child resolved into a girl of perhaps eight or nine, in a coat two sizes too large and buttoned to the throat, hair cut short and badly, like someone had done it in a hurry with sewing scissors. Her lips were blue at the edges. In one mittened fist she clutched a piece of paper so tightly it had gone soft as cloth.

"Are you the driver?" the girl asked. Her voice was flat, rehearsed.

"I'm the driver."

"Mama said ask for the back door. Front doors are for people who matter." She held out the paper.

Petra took it. The handwriting was hurried, elegant, and unfamiliar:

*This child is nothing to you. Forget this face. Feed her, keep her warm, say nothing, and no harm comes to anyone. Burn this.*

Below, pressed hard enough to tear: *Her name is Nell.*

Petra looked down the empty street, at the gas lamps guttering out, at the girl whose face was already arranging itself into blankness — a practised blankness, a child taught to be forgettable, and God, Petra thought, what does that cost a person, learning that at eight?

"Can you run?" Petra asked.

"I can walk fast."

"That'll do."

* * *

She put Nell in the stillroom — Mrs. Petch's territory but cold and unused since autumn, off the kitchen passage, with a daybed and a bolt on the outside of the door that Petra left pointedly unlocked. She fed the girl bread and dripping and sweet tea, watched her eat with the terrible efficiency of a child who had learned meals might not repeat themselves.

"Who's your mama?" Petra asked.

Nell chewed. Swallowed. "I'm not to say."

"Did your mama write that note?"

A pause, a fraction too long. "I'm not to say that either."

"And the man who brought you?"

Something flickered then, quick as a match — fear, real fear, gone as soon as it came. "There wasn't a man," Nell said, in the same flat voice, and looked at the window.

Petra let it go. She fetched a blanket that smelled of camphor, and when she tucked it round the girl's shoulders the child flinched first and then, astonishingly, leaned in — a small, deliberate weight against Petra's arm, testing whether leaning was permitted. Petra, who had not been leaned on since she was fifteen and had sworn off it, found her own body answering before her rules could intervene. Her hand settled on a bony shoulder. Stayed there.

"Sleep," she said. "No one's coming up those stairs without knowing I'm in the way."

Nell looked at her with eyes too old for the rest of her face, and nodded once, and slept sitting upright, the way animals sleep, ready to run.

* * *

At nine sharp Marguerite descended the steps, grey coat, gloves, verdicts. Petra opened the motor car door and did not mention the stillroom, the note burning in the kitchen grate, or the fact that she had missed the Ealing deputation entirely and would have to invent a reason involving the carburettor.

But at the last moment Marguerite paused, one gloved hand on the doorframe, and looked back at the house — at some vibration in it, perhaps, some displacement of air that only women who have survived raids learn to feel.

"Petra," she said. "Is there something in this house I should know about?"

The question hung in the cold morning light. Behind the stillroom's little window, Petra knew, a small face might be watching. Somewhere in her coat pocket, the burnt corner of a note she hadn't quite destroyed bore words she couldn't stop reading: *forget this face.*

"Not yet, ma'am," Petra said. "But I think there will be by tonight."

Marguerite studied her a long moment — the weighing look, the full ounce — then got into the car.

"Then we shall be quick about our errands," she said, "won't we."
