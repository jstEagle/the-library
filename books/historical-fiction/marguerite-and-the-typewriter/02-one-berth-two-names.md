## 2. One Berth, Two Names

Petra learned the child's rhythms before she learned her name.

Three days Nell had been in the house — three days folded into the narrow maid's room off the scullery, fed on bread and milk and Mrs. Tallow's suspicious gruel, and still she flinched when a door opened too fast. She ate like someone expecting the plate to be taken back. She slept curled against the wall, facing it, as though the room might be entered from behind.

Petra sat on the edge of the cot that second evening with a tin of condensed milk and a spoon, because sugar was the one language every frightened child spoke fluently.

"You don't have to tell me anything," Petra said. "But you can't stay nameless forever. Marguerite will want to know what to call you."

The spoon paused halfway to the girl's mouth. Her eyes were grey, sea-glass grey, with a wariness far older than eight or nine years old.

"Nell," she said at last. "Just Nell."

"Just Nell," Petra agreed, and something in her chest — the part she had bricked over years ago, when she'd learned that watching was safer than being seen — cracked along an old fault line.

* * *

Marguerite Ashworth woke at seven, as she always did, to tea and the day's correspondence arranged in order of urgency. Petra had been laying out those letters for two years now, and for two years she had read them first — a sin she committed without remorse, because a driver who read the mail knew which roads were safe, which callers were dangerous, which envelopes held summons and which held bombs in the shape of invitations.

This morning there were eleven letters. One bore a crest Petra did not recognize: a gull above crossed keys, stamped from Boulogne.

She slit it open in the motor room, where no one came before nine.

*"Madam — regarding the passage arranged through your brother's office for the woman calling herself Vera Lindqvist. The berth was paid in full. The woman did not arrive. We are told she was detained. We would be told by whom, and whether our fee is returned."*

Detained. The word sat in Petra's stomach like swallowed ice.

She was still holding it when Marguerite swept into the hall at half past eight, already armored in dove-grey wool and moral certainty, and said, "Where is the child?"

"Asleep."

"Wake her. Edmund is coming to luncheon and I'll not have him tripping over mysteries. He's been generous enough about this — he's offered to make inquiries, find where the girl belongs—"

"Has he." Petra kept her voice level, her hands busy with Marguerite's gloves, smoothing the leather over those capable fingers. "And what inquiries has he made so far?"

Marguerite looked at her sharply. "You disapprove of my brother now?"

"I disapprove of nothing I can prove, ma'am."

It was the wrong thing to say, or perhaps the right one, because Marguerite went very still, and then very cold, and took her letters without another word.

* * *

The name fell out of the child on the third morning, the way a coin falls from a torn pocket — accidentally, and with terrible consequence.

Petra was brushing out Nell's hair, the girl perched on a kitchen stool in one of Mrs. Tallow's borrowed aprons, when Mrs. Tallow remarked that the child must have come a long way, dressed so fine once, all that good cloth cut down and dyed.

"I had a traveling coat," Nell said into her lap. "Blue. With brass buttons. Mr. Eddy said fine coats got noticed at the dock, so we traded mine for a plain one, and he kept the difference. He always kept the difference."

The brush stopped.

"Mr. Eddy," Petra said carefully. "Who was Mr. Eddy?"

"The man who does the boats." Nell turned, and her grey eyes went wide with a child's sudden arithmetic — the sum of what she'd just said. "He was kind," she added quickly, the reflex of a child who has learned kindness is conditional. "He found Mamma a berth when nobody else would. He said England wasn't safe for ladies like her anymore, and France would take us, and he only charged what was fair."

"What was fair, Nell?"

"Thirty pounds." The number came out flat, rehearsed, a grief recited until it lost its edges. "Mamma sold her ring and her watch. And then the police came the night before the boat. They knew our address. Only four people knew our address." Nell counted them on small fingers. "Mamma. Me. The lady who wrote ahead. And Mr. Eddy."

Outside, the gravel announced a motorcar. Petra's blood went slow and heavy as lead.

Through the window she saw Edmund Ashworth step down from his gleaming Napier, silver-tipped cane tapping, smile already arranged — a handsome man aging gracefully into his own charm, the way ivy ages into a wall while quietly pulling it apart.

He had kept the difference.

* * *

Marguerite received the story standing at her study window, her back to Petra, her reflection ghosted against the garden's autumn gold.

"No," she said.

"Marguerite—"

"You will not say it. You heard a frightened child repeat gossip. Edmund has given eleven years to this movement. He has stood bail for women he'd never met. He has—" Her voice caught on itself. "He carried Emmeline's case up three flights when her heart was bad. You don't do that and sell women to the police."

"People do worse for less than thirty pounds a head."

"He is my brother." She turned then, and Petra saw the truth beneath the fury: not denial but terror, terror wearing denial's face. "He is the last of my family, Petra. If you are asking me to weigh a child's half-remembered fears against thirty years of—"

"I'm asking you to weigh a ledger."

That stopped her. "What ledger?"

"There's a shipping office off Leadenhall Street. Gull and Keys. It bills itself as passenger brokerage." Petra had spent her afternoon there, hat low, hands in her pockets, doing what she did best — being unremarkable, being furniture, being invisible in a doorway while clerks talked over her head. "One of their boats leaves for Boulogne six days from now. There's a berth unclaimed on the manifest. And there's a ledger book the junior clerk balances every Thursday, when the senior man takes his dinner."

"And how, precisely, do you know what a clerk balances on Thursdays?"

"Because I've driven you past that office forty times and you never once looked at me," Petra said. "Nobody looks at me, Marguerite. It's the one gift God gave me."

Something moved across Marguerite's face — hurt, maybe, that Petra should say such a thing about herself, that she should wear invisibility like a coat she'd chosen. But the fear won. It always won, where Edmund was concerned.

"If you go back there," Marguerite said slowly, "and if you find what you think you'll find — bring it to me first. Not the police. Not anyone. Me. Swear it."

"I swear."

"Then go. And Petra—" For one unguarded moment her hand found Petra's sleeve, gripped hard. "If you're wrong, I will never forgive you. And if you're right—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

* * *

The ledger lived in a drawer that locked, but the drawer's lock was older than the clerk's diligence, and Petra's hairpins had opened harder doors than this.

She copied it by lamplight in the motor room that night, her hand steady, her breath not.

The entries told everything. Berths sold at thirty pounds each to women whose true names ran down the margin in pencil — *Lindqvist, née Adler; the Brandt sisters; A.K., wanted for arson; M.R., wanted for worse.* Payments received. Then, in a different ink, payments made out to a private account: *E.A., commission.* And beside certain names, in the same second hand, a notation Petra understood with a sickness that rose in her throat like bile:

*Reported. Detained.*

Sixteen women who had paid their thirty pounds and bought, instead of a boat to France, a cell in Holloway. Edmund Ashworth had built a machine with two mouths — one selling escape to hunted women, one feeding their names to Scotland Yard — and grown fat on both ends of the transaction. And somewhere in the arithmetic, Marguerite's own cause had become his inventory.

Petra sat back. The lamp hissed. Six days until the boat. One berth unclaimed.

And under the week's entries, dated three days hence, written in Edmund's own confident hand, a line she read twice, three times, her skin going cold:

*M.A. — arrangement confirmed. No berth required.*

Not Marguerite's arrest, then. Not yet. An *arrangement*. With whom, for what — the ledger wouldn't say.

Petra folded the original pages into the lining of her driving coat, sewed them shut with shaking fingers, and put the copy in her handbag where it could be burned quickly if it must be.

Tomorrow, Edmund Ashworth was coming to luncheon.

And tomorrow, Petra decided, watching dawn grey the windows of the garage, she would be waiting for him first — alone, before Marguerite could stand between them, before the family could close ranks around its rot. Because whatever *arrangement* meant, whoever had confirmed it, there was one person in that house who would not let it come to pass.

She had spent her whole life being unseen.

It was time to be seen.
