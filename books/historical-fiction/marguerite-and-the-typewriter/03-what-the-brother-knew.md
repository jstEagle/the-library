## 3. What the Brother Knew

Edmund Ashworth kept his study at the top of the house, a room that smelled of pipe smoke and orange peel and money that had never worked a day in its life. Petra climbed the stairs at half past nine on Thursday morning with her heart beating in her wrists, because she had made her decision the night before, lying awake in the narrow bed behind the garage: she would not go to Marguerite first. She would go to him. A woman who read letters learned this much — that you did not hand a loaded truth to someone who might drop it.

He was at his desk when she entered without knocking, sorting banknotes into a leather folio with the contentment of a man feeding pigeons. He looked up, and his face did what it always did — arranged itself into warmth so quickly and so completely that Petra understood, for the first time, that the warmth was the mask, not the thing beneath it. Most people's charm sat over their faces like light. Edmund's sat like lacquer.

"Miss Crane," he said pleasantly. "Come to polish something? You're always polishing something. It's very restful to watch."

"I know about the *Calais Star*," Petra said. "I know about the berth fees. I know about the women you sold back to the police after they'd paid you."

The pigeon-feeding hands went still. For three full seconds Edmund Ashworth looked at her as though she were an equation he hadn't expected on his blackboard, and then he laughed — softly, genuinely, which was somehow worse than any other response she could have imagined.

"So the driver reads," he said. "I wondered. You have the eyes of a reader. Always down, but never empty." He leaned back in his chair, lacing his fingers over his waistcoat. "Go on, then. Say the rest. You've rehearsed it; it would be unkind to waste your rehearsal."

"The ledger. The one you keep in the lining of your portmanteau, under the shirts with the French collar. The one with dates and names and sums, going back two years. The one where Nell's mother is written down as *paid in full, delivered*."

Something moved behind his face then — not guilt. Calculation, quick as a fish turning. And beneath the calculation, briefly, something almost like respect, which Petra found she despised more than anything else in the room.

"You've been in my luggage."

"I've been in everyone's luggage. That's what servants are for, if you're honest about it. We are the people who see everything while being nobody."

Edmund rose, unhurried, and crossed to the window. Below, in the square, the plane trees were dropping the first copper leaves of October onto the pavement, and somewhere a milk cart jingled past, ordinary as bread. He stood looking at all that ordinariness for a moment before he spoke again, and when he turned back his voice had changed — dropped its playfulness the way an actor drops a prop.

"My sister has told you a story," he said. "A noble story. Women fleeing, and we the rescuers. And it was true once — I grant you, it was true for nearly a year, and it cost me real money and real risk, and Marguerite never knew how much of either came out of my pocket rather than the fund." He tapped the folio of banknotes. "But causes age, Miss Crane, faster than people do. The war's coming — don't look at me like that, everyone who counts already knows it — and when it comes, half these hunted girls will be enemy aliens and no one will thank us for them. So yes. I adjusted. I charged those who could pay for passage, and I reported those who couldn't pay, because the police pay too, and better than the fund does, and they pay *promptly*."

"Nell's mother could pay," Petra said. "She paid everything she had. And you delivered her anyway."

"She paid what she said she had," Edmund corrected gently. "It was less than she believed. People are always poorer than they believe. It's practically a moral law." He came around the desk, and Petra forced herself not to step back. "Now. Here is what happens next, and you may choose your part in it. On Saturday morning, two detectives from Scotland Yard will call at this house — not for the girl. For Marguerite. There is a warrant already drawn; there has been since Tuesday. Obstruction, conspiracy, harboring — they'll dress it in legal Latin, but the substance is that my sister has become inconvenient, and inconvenient women get arrested, and arrested women stop being listened to." He let that settle. "Unless, of course, certain documents fail to reach certain hands. Unless a ledger goes into the Thames and a driver remembers nothing. In exchange: fifty pounds now, and a berth on Saturday's boat. Yours. The last one."

The word *berth* landed in Petra's chest like a coin dropped down a well. She thought of the boat she'd watched from the embankment as a girl, of every door she had ever slipped through quietly, of a whole life built on the principle that the wise woman travels light and tells no one where.

"And if I say no?"

"Then you'll be interviewed as an accessory, the girl will be taken into care and handed to the same constables I sell to, and my sister will spend the spring in Holloway reading about herself in the papers while the cause eats itself alive wondering who informed." He smiled, and it was the lacquered smile again, and Petra realized he had never once, in all these years, been smiling *at* anyone. He had only ever been smiling *near* them, the way a man smiles near a mirror. "You're not a bad bargain-hunter yourself, Miss Crane. Don't pretend the arithmetic offends you."

"That's exactly what offends me," Petra said quietly. "That you think it wouldn't."

And that was when the door opened.

Marguerite Ashworth filled the doorway the way winter fills a valley — suddenly, totally, changing the temperature of everything. She was still in her morning coat; she had clearly been standing outside long enough to hear the shape of the conversation if not every word, and her face held the particular grayness of a woman watching her family and her faith arrive at the same conclusion from opposite directions.

"How long?" she said. Not to Petra. To her brother. "How long have you been selling them?"

Edmund sighed, and put the folio of banknotes neatly back in his drawer, and shut the drawer with a small courteous click. "Maggie—"

"*How long.*"

"Since the spring before last." He said it lightly, almost apologetically, and Petra saw Marguerite's hands close at her sides. "You were building a movement. Movements need funding. I provided funding. That some of it came from fares rather than donations is a question of bookkeeping—"

"It's a question of *women*." Marguerite's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it; it descended instead, getting quieter and heavier, like a stone going down a well. "Nell's mother. You wrote her down in your little book as freight. Delivered."

"If you'd ever once looked at the accounts properly—"

"I trusted you *not* to need looking after." And then Marguerite turned to Petra, and her eyes were wet and furious and asking, and Petra understood that the question was: *did you know, and did you hide it from me too?*

"I found the ledger yesterday," Petra said. "I came here to make him confess before I brought it to you. I wanted you to hear it from his mouth."

"A touching alliance," Edmund murmured. "Do enjoy it while the house isn't full of policemen. Which, incidentally—" he glanced at the clock on the mantel, and for the first time all morning his composure flickered, "—is sooner than I said."

The bell below went. Not the drawing-room bell. The front bell, hard and repeated, the ring of people who do not wait.

* * *

They moved fast after that, the way water moves when a dam goes. Edmund went down himself, smooth as silk, all charm and solicitude — *Officers, what an honor, do come in, my sister will be devastated to have missed you* — buying minutes with his voice while Petra ran.

She ran first to the kitchen grate, where she had hidden the copy she'd made of the ledger's pages, copied out in her own careful hand through two nights when she should have slept. She fed the pages to the fire and stood over them until the names curled and went black — the women she could no longer save by paper, whose safety now lived only in her memory. Then she took the original from the false bottom of the coal scuttle where no one had thought to look for it, wrapped it in oilcloth, and pressed it flat against her ribs inside her coat, where it lay like a second heartbeat. The copy would burn for the police. The truth would travel with her.

Nell was in the linen closet, as instructed, small and silent among the folded sheets, clutching the cloth cat she carried everywhere. Petra knelt, took the child's face in both hands — the face someone had once commanded the world to forget — and said the only words that mattered.

"We're going to the car now. Fast feet, quiet mouth. Just like we practiced."

Downstairs she could hear Edmund laughing at something a detective had said, the laugh carrying up the stairwell, generous and hollow as an empty church. Marguerite stood in the hall in her morning coat with two men in dark suits before her, and when her eyes found Petra across the width of that beautiful, betrayed house, she did not plead and she did not warn. She lifted her chin — the smallest motion, a queen granting permission — and Petra understood: *go.*

Petra went. Out the kitchen door, down the steps, into the grey morning, with Nell's hand locked in hers and forty years of secrets riding against her ribs. The motorcar started on the second crank.

As she pulled out of the mews, glancing back once at the tall windows of the house where everything she loved was being dismantled room by room, Petra did the arithmetic Edmund had sneered at her for knowing — and found it came out differently than he'd promised.

One berth left on the *Calais Star*, sailing Saturday.

Two days. One child. And a ledger with enough names in it to burn a kingdom down.
