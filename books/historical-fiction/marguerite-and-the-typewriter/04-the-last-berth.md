## 4. The Last Berth

The Thames at Tilbury wore its morning like a shroud — grey water, grey sky, gulls crying their thin accusations above the masts. Marguerite Ashworth stood on the dock with the wind pulling at her hat ribbons and the ticket in her glove burning like a coal against her palm.

One berth. One name on the manifest of the *Adélaïde*, sailing on the morning tide for Calais, and beyond Calais, for the people who could hide a woman whose own government had begun to draw a noose around her. Inspector Reeve had been courteous about it, in the way men are courteous when they believe they have already won. *A formality, Mrs. Ashworth. Nothing personal.* As though the destruction of a woman's life were ever anything but personal.

Beside her, Nell stood very still, one small hand gripping the hem of Petra's coat. The child had not spoken since the car. She had watched the police come up the drive at Cavendish Row with eyes too old for her face, and she had watched Petra burn the ledger copy in the study grate while keeping the original pressed flat inside her boot, and she had said nothing at all — which was, Marguerite was learning, how Nell said most things that mattered.

*Forget this face,* the note had demanded six days ago. The face looked up at her now from beneath a crooked hat some well-meaning constable's wife had donated, and Marguerite found she could do nothing of the kind. The face had lodged itself behind her ribs like a splinter working toward the heart.

"You'll want to board soon," Petra said quietly. "They're taking luggage forward."

"I know."

"The tide won't wait."

"I know that too." Marguerite heard her own voice, clipped and cold, the voice she used in committee rooms full of men who wished her gone, and she hated it. She made herself turn. Petra stood with her cap in her hands — she had taken it off, Marguerite noticed, as if this were a church or a graveside — and behind the driver's steady composure there was something raw and waiting.

*Say it plainly,* Marguerite told herself. You have spent forty-seven years saying nothing plainly to anyone who mattered. Say it now, at the end, where it can do the least damage and the most good.

"Nell," she said, and crouched despite her knees, despite the dockside mud, despite forty years of being taught never to lower herself before anyone. "Do you know what a berth is?"

The girl shook her head.

"It's a bed on a boat. A place that belongs to someone. And I have bought one — a place on that ship, going to France, where no policeman can reach you, where there are women who will feed you and teach you and never once ask you to forget your own face. Do you understand?"

Nell's chin trembled. "It's yours, though. Missus Ashworth's."

"It is mine," Marguerite agreed. "And I am giving it away, which is my privilege, because I have decided something this morning that I should like you to remember all your life, however long God grants it. I have decided that blood is a lottery and love is a choice, and I am a woman who prefers to choose."

She took the ticket from her glove and pressed it into the small cold hand.

"They will ask your name at the gangway. Give them any name but Nell Crane-Ashworth — no." A correction, unbidden, irreversible: "*Ashworth-Crane*. Family first. It's only proper."

The child stared at the ticket as though it might bite her, then at Marguerite, then — last, longest — at Petra. And whatever passed between those two, driver and child, in that look, Marguerite did not try to read. Some letters were not hers to open. She had learned that much at last.

* * *

Petra walked Nell to the gangway herself. Marguerite watched them go — the tall figure bent slightly toward the small one, murmuring instructions Marguerite could not hear, and thought: *I have known this woman three years and I know her handwriting in every letter she has ever copied for me, and I never knew until Tuesday that she reads every word I receive.*

The betrayal should have been the end of it. Instead, standing in her study among the smoke-smell of burned paper, Marguerite had felt something adjacent to relief — that even her secrets had been kept by faithful hands, that even her hidden self had been guarded. Petra had read her life for three years and stayed. Edmund had shared her blood for forty years and sold her.

There was no arithmetic in it that a reasonable person would accept. Marguerite had stopped requiring her heart to be reasonable somewhere between the doorstep at dawn and the dock at dawn, six days later, and she suspected she would never require it again.

At the top of the gangway, Nell turned. She did not wave. She raised one hand — flat, deliberate, the way one salutes a flag or lays flowers on a stone — and held it there until the crowd swallowed her.

Marguerite did not realize she was weeping until Petra returned and offered, wordlessly, a clean handkerchief ironed to knife-edge stiffness.

"Your brother's car just came onto the quay," Petra said.

* * *

Edmund crossed the dock the way he crossed every room he'd ever entered: as though the dock had been built for him and ought to feel flattered. His coat was new. His smile was old.

"Maggie." He said it warmly. He always said it warmly; warmth was his native language and lies merely its grammar. "Reeve tells me the little stowaway got a berth after all. Sentimental of you. Wasteful, but sentimental."

"Where are you going, Edmund?" She nodded past him, to the second-class gangway, where a man matching his description had already been seen embarking by one of Petra's contacts among the porters. Brussels, perhaps. Buenos Aires. Anywhere the newspapers' ink dried slowly.

"Away." He spread his hands, unrepentant, almost tender. "You'll publish your ledger, of course. You'll ruin me in every drawing room from here to Edinburgh, and I shall drink brandy in some café and read about it and laugh. You can't touch me where I'm going, Maggie. You must know that. I've always been the one who understood how the world actually works."

"Yes," Marguerite said. "That was always the tragedy of you, Edmund. You understood exactly how the world works, and so you never once tried to make it work differently." She stepped closer, and let him see — really see — what lived behind her composure. "Every woman you sold to the police, every fee you pocketed from a desperate girl's escape — I will say their names out loud until my voice gives out, and then I will write them. You will be free abroad, brother. You will simply never be welcome anywhere I am remembered. And I intend to be remembered."

For one moment — one flicker, quickly mastered — something moved behind his charm that might have been grief. Then it was gone, banked like a fire, and he lifted her hand and kissed the air above her knuckles with perfect theatrical grace.

"Give my regards to the cause," he said, and walked away down the quay, unhurried, into the smoke and the gull-cry and the grey, and was not arrested, and was not struck down, and was not sorry. That was the cost. Marguerite had known the cost when she chose it. Justice, she thought, watching his back diminish, was a country most of us emigrate to and never reach.

* * *

The *Adélaïde* gave three low notes and began to pull her great weight from the wall of the world.

Marguerite stood at the dock's edge with her hands empty and her future unwritten — no ticket, no passage, no plan beyond the afternoon — and felt, strangely, light. Reeve would come for her within the week. Let him. There were cells in Holloway that had held better women than her and would hold worse, and every one of them had made more noise from inside than out.

Beside her, Petra put her cap back on. Straightened it. And then, with the deliberation of a woman signing her name to something, took it off again.

"I've given notice," Petra said, to the water rather than to Marguerite. "Effective this morning. Mr. Hobbs at the garage has offered me work. Decent wages. He says I'm the best driver he's ever employed, which we both know is flattery, but flattering flattery."

"Hobbs pays half what I pay."

"I wasn't Hobbs's driver for the wages."

"No," Marguerite said. "You were mine for the letters."

Petra turned. Her face in the dockside light was bare as a stripped floorboard — everything showing, nothing sanded smooth. Three years of reading another woman's soul through her correspondence, and now the reader stood exposed in her turn, waiting to learn whether the reading had been forgiven or merely deferred.

"I read everything," Petra said. "From the first week. Every letter, every threat, every bill, every love note you pretended was correspondence about committee minutes. And I kept reading because—" Her jaw worked. "Because nobody else was protecting the whole of you. Only the parts people saw. Somebody had to guard the rest."

"And now?"

"Now there's a child on that ship with our name in her mouth, and a ledger in my boot with your brother's sins in its pages, and a cell in Holloway with your name on its door." Petra's voice did not shake, but it came close. "I don't want to be your driver, Mrs. Ashworth. Drivers get dismissed."

"What do you want to be?"

"Family," Petra said. "Chosen kind. The kind that stays."

Marguerite looked at her — at this woman who had arrived three years ago with clean nails and a guarded heart and had spent three years silently standing between Marguerite and the world — and understood that the choice had been made long ago, by both of them, in a hundred small hours neither had named. All that remained was the saying of it.

She extended her hand. Not to be kissed. To be held.

On the dock at Tilbury, in view of porters and policemen and the departing ship, in full light, Marguerite Ashworth took Petra Crane's hand and did not let go. Somewhere in the crowd a photographer raised a camera; let him. Let the papers print it. Let Edmund read of it in his café in whatever city harbored him, and choke gently on his brandy.

The *Adélaïde* slid past, growing smaller, carrying a stowaway of the heart toward a safer shore. The gulls wheeled. The tide ran.

And on the emptying dock, two women who owed each other nothing and had chosen each other for everything stood hand in hand, facing the grey water, facing whatever came next — the arrest, the trial, the long war — together, and unafraid, and home.
