## 1. The Weight of Lead

The type weighed more than Felix did when she was born. She knew this because her father had told her so, twice a year, on the days he took inventory, and she had never thought to question it until the year he died and the counting fell to her.

Now it was her habit too. She woke before the gulls, lit the lamp at the cutting bench, and counted.

Twelve cases of body type — long primer, pica, brevier, the small pearl they kept for almanac tables. Nine hundredweight of paper in the cellar, wrapped against damp in oilcloth, most of it bought honest through the customs house before the customs house turned into something else. Three presses: the old Columbian that Odile called Grandmother because she was temperamental and older than anyone living, the iron hand press for jobbing work, and the proof press that had been Thackeray's, its rollers worn smooth by his hands alone.

And the dies. Always last, always longest, the dies.

Felix unlocked the shallow drawer beneath her father's bench and lifted them out one at a time into the lamplight — steel punches and copper matrices, each cut by his own hand over thirty years at this bench. The alphabet he'd been commissioned to cut for the Cathedral of Saint Aubin, delivered and paid for and now standing in stone above their door. The ornamental fleurons nobody had ever ordered but he'd cut anyway on slow winter evenings, because his hands needed work the way other men's hands needed drink. And at the back, wrapped in flannel, the unfinished ones: a titanic display face he'd been carving when he died, eleven letters done out of twenty-six, the M still clamped in the vise with graver marks fresh as morning frost.

She counted them the way her father had taught her to count everything — not as property but as weight. What could be carried. What could not.

A case of type could be carried if you were desperate; twelve could not. Paper could be burned, which was sometimes the same as carried. Presses could not be carried at all. But a die was small as a coin and held a whole alphabet inside it, and her father used to say that whoever held the matrices held the language, and whoever held the language would never be conquered for long. She'd thought it was just a thing fathers said. Lately she'd begun to think it might be a thing soldiers understood too.

* * *

By mid-morning the rumor arrived the way rumors always did in Vellamare — wet, salt-stained, and half-garbled, brought up from the fish market on the shoulders of somebody's cousin who knew somebody's sergeant.

"They're coming," said Perrin, dropping two mackerel and a bundle of news onto the composing stone. He was their journeyman, nineteen, all elbows and convictions. "Requisition orders. Every press in the Quarter. They'll print nothing but proclamations from now on, or nothing at all."

"Which is it today?" Odile asked without looking up. She stood at Grandmother's press, locking a chase of almanac pages, her small quick hands moving with the economy of forty years. "Nothing, or proclamations?"

"The sergeant didn't say. The sergeant mostly said they'd shoot anyone who argued." Perrin grinned, because he was nineteen and the word *shoot* still sounded like a story. "Felix. Did you hear me? Requisition."

"I heard you."

"You're counting again." He nodded at her hands, which had gone on without her, sorting a distribution of sorts back into their cases, click and slide, click and slide. "You do that when you're scared. Your father did it too. Odile says he sorted type the night the plague ship came in, sorted it right through the bells."

"I'm not scared. I'm taking stock." She set the last sort home and closed the case. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

Yes, she wanted to say. Scared is a feeling. Stock is arithmetic. Her father had drilled it into her across ten years of inventory days: *Know your weight, girl. A shop that doesn't know what it can carry loses everything in the first panic. A shop that knows can save the important part.* She had assumed, at eight years old, that the important part meant the presses. It had taken her until now, at twenty-six, to understand he'd meant the dies, and maybe not even those — maybe he'd meant the hands, hers, the ones doing the counting.

"There's a difference," she said, "and I'll show it to you when there's something worth the difference. Go wash the fish smell off my stone."

Perrin went, laughing, and Felix stood a moment in the warm smell of ink and oil and looked at the shop the way you look at a face you love when you've begun to suspect you won't have it forever. The sign outside — a painted atlas, the world held open on an atlas's shoulders — creaked in the wind off the harbor. The window displayed their legal trade: almanacs, bills of lading, wedding invitations, a handsome broadside of tide tables. In the back room, behind the paper cellar, sat what paid better than all of it: the formes for pamphlets no licensed printer would touch. *On the Misgovernment of the Coastal Garrisons.* *An Account of Bread Prices in the Fourth District.* Unsigned, undated, set in a face cut by Thackeray himself so that no other shop could be blamed for it. Odile called it the night garden. Felix called it what it was: enough rope to hang every one of them, printed four hundred at a time.

Her father had cut that face knowing. That was the thing she kept returning to, turning like a coin under her thumb. He had known exactly what the type was for, and he'd cut it anyway, and then he'd died of a fever like an ordinary man and left her the bench and the debt and the habit of counting.

* * *

Odile found her in the cellar after the noon bell, checking the paper count against the ledger for the third time that week.

"It hasn't grown since yesterday," Odile said, settling onto the flour barrel with the sigh of a woman whose knees had opinions. She was sixty-one and looked fifty and moved like thirty, and she had run the Sign of the Atlas since before Felix was born, since she and Thackeray had — Felix had never gotten the whole story, only the shape of it, two young radicals with ink under their nails and a country going wrong around them.

"I know it hasn't grown."

"And you're counting it anyway."

"I'm counting it anyway."

Odile watched her fold the oilcloth back down over the top ream. Outside, muffled through stone, the city went about its business: cart wheels, a knife grinder's bell, someone singing the old bread song in the street, the one about the baker's daughter, which the government had banned in the spring and which everyone therefore now sang constantly.

"You heard the same rumor I did," Odile said. "Requisition orders, maybe within the month. Maybe within the week. If the army takes the presses, they take the night garden with it, and the night garden has names in it, Felix. Names go on scaffolds."

"I know what's in it."

"Do you? Then say it plainly. Say what we do."

Felix straightened. The cellar was cold and smelled of size and starch and the faint sweet rot of the harbor that got into everything in Vellamare. She made herself say it. "We break the night garden. Distribute the type. Melt what we can't hide. Burn the archives tonight, before any order comes."

"And the dies?"

The question landed where Odile had aimed it, which was always exactly center. Felix looked toward the stairs, toward the bench above, toward the drawer with its flannel-wrapped inheritance.

"The dies are my father's," she said.

"Yes."

"He cut the night garden face himself. He knew what it was for. If I melt his punches to keep his politics safe, I think—" She stopped. Started again, plainer. "I think he'd rather hang us both than see the letters melted."

Odile smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile; Odile's smiles rarely were. "Then we don't melt them. We carry them. Which brings me to the real arithmetic, girl, the one you've been circling all morning. Count it out loud for me. If the order comes tomorrow, what leaves this shop?"

"Two cases of type. The dies. The account books — no. Not the account books. The archives burn. Two cases, the dies, the cash box, and whatever paper fits in a handcart."

"And what stays?"

"The presses. The stock. The shop." Felix's voice caught on none of it, which surprised her. "Grandmother stays."

"Grandmother stays," Odile agreed softly. "I taught that press to kiss paper when your father was still cutting his first M. She stays, and I stay with her, if it comes to it." She said it the way she announced weather. Then she pushed off the barrel and stood, knees cracking, and squeezed Felix's shoulder once, hard, on her way to the stairs. "Finish your counting. And Felix — the boy's asking questions about the night garden again. Either let him set a page or tell him less. But stop letting him stand in the doorway looking hungry."

* * *

Evening came down gray off the water, and with it the new curfew bell — six o'clock now, moved forward again that week, the army tightening the city like a fist around something it meant to crush. Felix barred the door, shuttered the windows, and worked by lamplight at her father's bench, graver in hand, finishing what he had left unfinished. Eleven letters done. Tonight she cut the twelfth, an N, and her hand shook once near the end and she had to grind the mistake out and start the counter again, and she thought: *he never shook. Twenty-six letters and he never shook.*

But her father had never cut type with requisition orders coming. Her father had never lain awake listening to boots in the street below, counting them like reams of paper — how many men, how much weight, what could be carried past them and what could not.

She blew the copper dust from the N and held it to the lamp. It was good work. It was almost his work. Someday, she thought, if there was a someday, some child would learn letters from this face and never know whose hands had made it, and that was the whole point of the craft, wasn't it — that the words outlived everyone.

The lamp guttered. Oil was rationed now; even light had a count.

She was banking the fire, thinking of supper and of the archives that should be burned and hadn't been, when the knock came. Not a customer's knock. Not Perrin's. Three soft raps, low on the door, at an hour when no one knocked at all, and then — through the wood, barely louder than the wind — the sound of a child crying, small and feverish and trying very hard to be quiet about it.

Felix stood in the dark shop with her father's unfinished alphabet under her arm and listened, and every count she'd made that day rearranged itself at once, because here at last was something that had come in over the scales unmeasured, something that could be carried, and would have to be.
