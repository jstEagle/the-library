## 2. A Child at the Door

The curfew bell rang twice that night, and Felix counted the strikes the way her father had taught her to count everything: once for the fact of it, once for what it might mean.

Two bells meant the patrols were doubled. Two bells meant stay off the streets or be shot where you stood, and no inquest afterward, because inquests required paper and paper was what the regime had begun to fear most.

She sat alone in the shop with every lamp shuttered, working by feel. That was the trick of a blackout — you learned your bench by touch, learned to read a letter's face with your thumb, learned which drawer stuck and which slid true. The army had ordered all windows darkened after the first riots in the spring, and Odile had complied so punctually that their shop, the Sign of the Atlas, looked from the street like every other dead building on Ropewalk Lane. It was the best camouflage they had. A press that looked asleep was a press left standing.

Felix ran her hands over the type cases anyway, taking inventory in the dark. Twelve pounds of new lead. Three drawers of worn pica she'd been meaning to recast since autumn. Her father's gravers, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of his old bench, each one still sharp enough to open a vein. She did this most nights now — counting what could be carried. Her father used to do it aloud, tapping items with one finger: *lead, paper, dies, ink, the boy* — the boy being Felix, small then, sitting on a stool sorting quads. She'd inherited the habit without meaning to. Lately the list kept shortening. Lead, paper, dies, the child she didn't yet know existed.

Outside, Vellamare held its breath. No carts on the cobbles. No gulls even, though the harbor was two streets east; the birds had grown canny about gunfire. Somewhere beyond the fish market a dog barked three times and stopped, as if corrected.

Then came the knock.

Not a knock, precisely. More like a palm pressed flat against the door, once, softly — the sound a person makes when they have decided something terrible and are asking permission to be finished with it.

Felix went still. In the dark, stillness is a kind of armor.

The palm pressed again. And beneath it, faintly, she heard breathing that wasn't right — quick, wet, labored, too fast for an adult. A child's breathing. A child fighting its own lungs.

Every sensible instinct told her to stand motionless until whoever it was gave up and moved along. Sensible people survived curfews. But her hand was already on the bolt, because she had learned long ago that her body did not consult her sense before acting on behalf of someone small and afraid.

She opened the door six inches. Fog poured through the gap like something poured.

A woman stood on the step, hooded, soaked through, holding a bundle that squirmed and coughed. Behind her the lane was empty white. Felix could see nothing past her except fog and the dim suggestion of the harbor lamps, smothered under their blackout hoods.

"Please," the woman said, and her voice cracked on the word like dry wood. "Take him."

"Take him where? Who—"

"There's no time." The woman pushed the bundle toward the gap, and Felix took it without deciding to — her arms simply opened, the way arms do. He was heavier than she expected, hot as a stone left in the sun, and he was burning. Fever rolled off him in waves. His breath came in shallow, whistling pulls, and between them he made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, small and mechanical and heartbreaking.

"He's sick. He needs a physician, I can't—"

"You can." The woman's hood shifted back an inch, and Felix saw a face for perhaps two seconds: young, sharp-boned, gray with exhaustion, eyes so dark they seemed to have no bottom. There was a bruise along her jaw going yellow-green, days old, healing. "Keep him warm. Barley water if you have it. Don't send for anyone — no physicians, none. Do you understand me? No one."

"I don't understand any of this."

"You will." The woman reached into her coat and drew out a flat object wrapped in sacking and tied with cord, pressing it into Felix's free hand. It was heavy. Metal-heavy. Copper-heavy. "And this goes to no one either. Not yet. Not until—" She stopped herself, glancing down the fogged lane as if names might be listening. "When the time comes, you'll know. Or she'll tell you. She'll know what to do."

"She?"

But the woman wasn't listening anymore. She stepped back off the threshold into the fog, and Felix, absurdly, lunged forward — "Wait, your name, at least, how will I ever—" — and the woman turned her head just far enough for the light of nothing, the absolute black of the street, to catch her profile.

"Forget my face," she said. Not cruelly. Almost gently, the way you'd say it to a child you were leaving somewhere you hoped was kinder than wherever you were going. "Forget my face, and keep him safe, and we're even."

Then she was gone. Not dramatically — no running footsteps, no door slamming down the lane. Just gone, swallowed whole, the way fog takes things, so completely that within ten seconds Felix honestly could not say which way the lane ran. Left toward the market or right toward the customs house. Gone.

The feverish child coughed against her collarbone and shivered, and the decision made itself, the way the true ones always do.

She kicked the door shut, threw the bolt, and stood in the pitch-dark shop with a burning toddler in her arms and forty pounds of copper against her hip, feeling the shape of her ordinary life quietly come apart around her like a seam giving way.

* * *

Odile came down the stairs at half past midnight, drawn by the sound of a child crying — a thin, exhausted, intermittent wail, the cry of someone who has been crying for days and knows it isn't working but cannot stop.

Felix had done what she could. She'd lit one candle inside the iron stove room at the back, where the glow couldn't reach the windows, and set the boy near the warmth. Barley water, sweetened with the last of the honey. A blanket from the rag bin, clean enough. He'd taken a little of the barley water, gagged, taken more. Now he slept in a nest of blankets on the settle, breathing in that terrible whistling rhythm, his cheeks flushed scarlet, his small fists clenched even in sleep, as if whatever he'd been carried through had taught him never to fully let go.

Odile stopped in the doorway of the stove room. She was sixty-two and built like the presses she tended, all iron frame and stubborn leverage, and Felix had never once seen her look frightened. Not during the conscription raids. Not when they printed almanacs over seditious text and burned the proofs. Not when soldiers kicked the door in last March and found nothing because Odile had spent a sleepless week building a false back into the paper cupboard.

She looked frightened now.

"Whose is he?" Odile asked.

"I don't know. A woman. She wouldn't give a name."

"Describe her."

"Young. Dark-eyed. Bruised jaw, healing. She said—" Felix hesitated, hearing it again. "She said forget my face. And that when the time came, *she* would tell me what to do with this." She lifted the wrapped bundle from the bench. "She knew you, Odile. I think she meant you. *She'll know what to do.*"

Something moved across Odile's face — not recognition exactly, more like the shadow recognition casts. She took the bundle and untied the cord with fingers that weren't quite steady, peeling back the sacking.

Copper caught the stove-light and threw it back warm.

An engraved plate. Atlas-sized, heavy, cut with the fine dense linework of a map — coastlines, hatching, tiny lettered labels. Odile tilted it toward the candle, and Felix leaned in, reading the engraver's marks the way she read type: by habit, by love.

Her stomach dropped.

She knew that hand. Every printer knows the hands of their own shop the way musicians know voices. The swash on the title letters. The particular confidence of the coastline strokes, unbroken, drawn in single passes. The way the sea was textured with short parallel ticks instead of stippling, because her father had argued once, at length, that stippling was for cowards.

"That's Papa's cutting," Felix said. Her voice sounded very far away. "That's my father's hand."

"Yes," Odile said.

"Papa's been dead four years."

"I know."

"Odile. Look at the date plate." Felix's finger found it, bottom corner, cut small and neat: the month and year. Eleven months ago. Seven months after they buried him, after Odile had closed his workshop and sealed his tools in oilcloth, after Felix had cried herself out over the gravers she would never see sharpened again. Eleven months ago, someone had sat at a bench and cut copper in Thackeray's unmistakable hand.

Odile did not answer. She turned the plate slowly, tracing the coastline with one blunt fingertip, and Felix watched her master's face go from fright to something older and colder. Odile had grown up in Vellamare's printing wars; she knew what a map of the coast meant, what fortifications meant, why a map like this would be cut in secret, in the dark, by lamplight, by a hand pretending to be a dead man's.

Or not pretending.

"Felix," Odile said quietly. "Go and check the boy."

"He's sleeping. Odile, whose hand is this? Tell me whose hand this is."

For a long moment the only sounds were the wind worrying the shutters and the child's ragged whistle of breath. Odile set the plate face-down on the bench, deliberately, the way you turn over a card you aren't ready to show.

"It's Thackeray's hand," she said at last. "It's his hand exactly. Which means either someone alive knows his work well enough to forge it — or your father is not where we buried the coffin, girl. Either way—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to nearly nothing. "Either way, there are people who will burn this city block by block to keep this plate from reaching the water. And now that woman has put it in our keeping, with her son, in the middle of a curfew, three weeks before the garrison doubles."

Felix looked at the sleeping boy. His fever-flush had faded a shade toward mere pink. One fist had finally loosened in sleep, curled open like a fern.

"The checkpoints," Felix said. "If it has to reach the harbor—"

"Don't." Odile's voice cracked like a whip. "Don't say it tonight. Tonight we feed him and watch his fever and bar the door. Tomorrow—" She exhaled. "Tomorrow we decide who carries it."

But Felix was already looking at the date plate again, and at the swash on the title letter, and thinking of her father's finger tapping the inventory in the dark — *lead, paper, dies* — and understanding, with a cold spreading certainty, exactly who that list had always been for.

Upstairs, a patrol boot struck the cobbles outside, paused opposite the darkened windows, and moved on.

The boy coughed in his sleep. And on the bench, face-down, the atlas waited.
