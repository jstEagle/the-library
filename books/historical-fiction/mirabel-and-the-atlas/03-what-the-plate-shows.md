## 3. What the Plate Shows

Odile Marchand had been a printer for forty-one years, and in that time she had learned that the truth of any object lived in its weight. Paper lied by the ream. Ink lied by the barrel. But copper — copper told you everything. She turned the plate over in her hands and felt it the way she'd once felt her mother's face in the dark: by memory, by grief, by the stubborn geometry of what remained.

It was heavier than it looked. They always were.

"Light the second candle," she said.

Felix did, cupping the flame with one hand as though it might be seen from the street — an old habit, a dangerous one now, when the curfew patrols had taken to counting lit windows the way tax men counted chimneys. The shutters of the workroom were drawn. The press stood silent under its dust sheet like something buried alive. Outside, Vellamare was holding its breath between the evening guns and the morning ones, that thin black hour when the city belonged to nobody but the fog.

The boy slept in the warming drawer of the composing stone, swaddled in Odile's oldest shawl, his fever finally breaking into sweat. He smelled of camphor and salt. Every few minutes Felix drifted to him, touched his forehead with two fingers, and came back to the candle. She couldn't seem to stop doing it. Odile let her. There were things a body needed to do that the mind hadn't approved yet.

"Now," Odile said, and set the plate on the stone.

The copper caught the candlelight and threw it back in long, shallow waves, the way the harbor did at dusk. Engraved lines ran across its surface in a lattice so fine it looked, at first, like weather — like frost on glass, like the veins of a leaf. Then the eye settled, and the frost became a coastline.

Vellamare's coastline. All of it.

Odile had expected this much. A map was a map; smugglers dealt in maps the way priests dealt in sins. What she had not expected — what made her sit down slowly on the cutting bench, the old wood groaning under her exactly as it had groaned under Thackeray — was the date cut into the lower margin, small and neat as a confession:

*Cut in the year of the Governor's Peace, third month.*

Four years after they had buried Aldous Thackeray. Four years after she had stood at the graveside in the rain with Felix's cold hand in hers and watched the harbor swallow the coffin-boat, and had thought: *that is the end of the story, then.* Four years of setting type beside his daughter and saying nothing about how the shop felt emptier every season, because grief was not the kind of thing you said out loud in a trade that measured everything.

"He's dead," Felix said. Not a question. A fact she was gripping with both hands.

"Yes."

"This is dated *after*—"

"I can read, child."

Felix flinched at the sharpness. Odile almost apologized. Instead she bent closer to the plate, because the alternative was to look at the girl's face, and she was not ready for that yet.

*Study it,* she told herself. *Grieve later. You are sixty-three years old and you have survived three regimes by studying first and grieving later.*

She began at the top left corner, where a careful hand would begin, and the hand that had cut this plate was the most careful she had ever known.

Fort St. Aubin, rendered in section — not the pretty elevation printed in the official almanacs, but the guts of the thing: powder magazine here, marked with the tiny crossed hammers Thackeray used for anything explosive; the cistern; the garrison quarters with their sleeping bunks indicated by rows of minute dashes, forty-two dashes, which meant forty-two men, which meant someone had counted. Gun emplacements along the seawall, each barrel drawn with its arc of fire etched as a faint fan of lines, so fine you'd take them for shading until you understood they were killing ranges.

The customs house. The quay patrol routes, timed — there were little numerals beside each route, watch changes, hours. And threading through all of it, cut in a different depth of line so they nearly disappeared unless the light raked them just so: escape routes. Ways in and out of every fortification. Drainage channels. A gap in the palisade at the fish market gate with a measurement beside it precise enough to be an insult to whoever built the wall.

"It's an atlas," Felix whispered. "A whole atlas. Of the coast."

"One plate of one." Odile turned it over. The reverse bore the ghost of a title, struck off and burnished away but still legible under the candle's rake: *THE ATLAS OF THE FREE COAST* — and beneath it, in smaller letters, the mark she knew better than her own name in the dark.

Thackeray's device. The compass rose with the broken needle.

He had cut that device into his first apprentice piece at fourteen. He had stamped it into every die he ever finished. When he died — when they *said* he died — Odile had half expected the mark to appear on his headstone. It had not. Now she understood why. A man does not sign his own grave when he intends to keep working.

"He faked the drowning," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange, a typesetter's voice, reporting facts. "Or someone arranged it to look like one. Either way — he went under the water as Aldous Thackeray, printer, and came up somewhere else as something else. And he spent four years cutting this." She tapped the plate. "Coastal defenses. For the fleet. For the *revolution*, Felix. Your father was a conspirator, and a good one, and we are standing in the middle of whatever he built."

Silence. The candle flames leaned in a draft from under the door, straightened.

Then Felix said, "He didn't tell me."

And there it was — the thing Odile had been bracing against since the woman vanished into the fog with her *forget my face*. Not fear. Hurt. Felix stood with one hand flat on the warming drawer, feeling the boy breathe through the wood, and her face had gone young and hard at once, the way it did when the type wouldn't justify and the deadline was tomorrow.

"He didn't tell me he was alive. He let me bury him. He let me—" Her voice cracked and she mastered it, because she was Thackeray's daughter down to the bone and mastering it was also inheritance. "Every almanac I ever printed, I did it thinking I was keeping his shop warm for a ghost. And he was *cutting maps*."

"Or he was dead," Odile said quietly, "and someone who loved him finished his work in his hand. Hands can be taught, Felix. I taught yours."

"No." Felix shook her head before Odile finished speaking. "Look at the hatching on the magazine walls. Look at the way the numerals lean left on the descenders. That's the lazy wrist. That's *his* lazy wrist, he never corrected it, I've pulled proofs from his plates my whole life. This is him. Alive, or recently alive." She stopped. Breathed. "Which means whoever gave us this plate knows he's alive too. And chose us."

"Chose the shop," Odile corrected, and heard how hollow it sounded even as she said it.

But it was true, and it mattered. The woman had come to *this* door. Out of all the doors in Vellamare — the smugglers' doors, the pamphleteers' doors, the dozen houses Odile could name where sedition was kept in jars like preserved fruit — she had carried a feverish child and a seditious treasure to a print shop on a curfew night. That was not chance. That was a network making a decision. And networks that made decisions about you did not consult you first, and did not release you afterward.

Odile got up, joints announcing themselves, and took the plate to the window slit where the moonlight came through, because candlelight flattered and moonlight told the truth. Under the colder light the engraving seemed to move, the gun-arcs breathing like tide.

There was more. She had missed it, or her eyes had refused it. Along the bottom margin, worked into what looked like decorative wave-scroll, ran a line of micro-lettering no wider than a hair. She fetched Thackeray's old loupe from its hook — his loupe, still hanging where he'd left it, because none of them had ever dared move it — and pressed her eye to the glass.

Letters. Dozens of them. A message, cut letter by letter at a scale only another cutter would ever find.

She read it twice. Then a third time, lips moving.

"Felix."

"Mm?" Felix was checking the boy again.

"Come here. Bring the loupe your father's ghost is going to make me regret every kind thing I ever said about him."

Felix came. Odile put the loupe in her hand and guided it to the margin and watched the girl's face while she read, because some things you need witnesses for.

The message read: *If this reaches the fleet, tell them the tide tables are true and the third fort falls first. If it reaches Corvain instead, burn it and forget Vellamare ever loved me. — A.T.*

"*Corvain*," Felix repeated. The name dropped into the room like a piece of type slipping off the stick.

Inspector Corvain. Who audited their license every quarter with a courtesy that felt like a held knife. Who had known Aldous Thackeray, everyone said, back before the Governor's Peace — though nobody said more than that, because Inspector Corvain's past was a locked case and he kept the key in his glove.

"He knew," Felix said slowly. "Whoever cut this — my father — knew Corvain might get hold of it. He built the plate for two readers. The fleet and the hangman." She looked up, and her eyes had gone bright and terrible. "Odile. My father planned for *Corvain*. Which means my father knew what Corvain is. Or what he was."

Odile took the plate back before her hands could shake visibly. Sixty-three years old, three regimes survived, and tonight she felt like an apprentice again — holding a master's unfinished work, understanding just enough of it to be afraid.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not avoid. A plate like this was worth a fleet to the revolution. It was worth a gallows to the regime. And it sat on her composing stone in a shop that Inspector Corvain visited every quarter, whose next visit was — she cast back through the almanac of threats and rumors that was her real ledger —

Due Friday.

Two days.

Outside, far off toward the citadel, a bell rang once and stopped. Not the hour. Not the alarm. The signal the night-watch used when they'd found something they wanted to look at longer.

Odile blew out both candles and stood in the dark holding copper that weighed as much as a coffin and as much as a key, listening to a feverish child breathe and a grown girl refuse to cry, and thought about Aldous Thackeray walking out of the sea four years ago without telling anyone, and decided that if he was alive, she was going to kill him herself — right after she saved his map, his grandson-by-circumstance, and his daughter.

"Get some sleep," she said into the blackness. "Both of you. Tomorrow we count what can be carried."

She heard Felix go still.

"You mean run."

"I mean," Odile said, "that tomorrow we decide whether we're a print shop or a target. And either way, child — either way — Corvain walks through that door on Friday, and we will be ready for him, or we will be ash."

In the warming drawer, the boy sighed in his sleep, and turned toward the sound of Felix's voice before Felix had even spoken.
