## 4. Inspector Corvain Calls

The knock came at half past nine, three measured raps, and Felix knew before she opened the door that it was trouble, because honest callers in Vellamare knocked twice and dishonest ones counted.

She had been at her father's bench since dawn, working a new die for the almanac's harvest tables while the child slept in the drawer Odile had lined with felt. The fever had broken in the night — his skin cool now, his breathing even, his small fists curled around a wooden sort with the letter *a* that he refused to surrender even in sleep. She had let him keep it. There was no harm in a letter.

Three raps. She wiped her hands on her apron, glanced once at the type cabinet against the far wall — the deep bottom drawer where the child lay hidden beneath a false floor of lead pigs — and opened the door with what she hoped was a printer's boredom.

"Signoria Thackeray." The man on the step was tall, gray at the temples, wearing the charcoal coat of the Ministry of Information with its small brass seal at the collar. He held a leather folio under one arm as if it were a hymnal. "Inspector Corvain. Licensing audit."

"We were audited in the spring."

"And I audited you myself," he said. "Which is why I know your spring figures were immaculate. Immaculate figures deserve a second look."

Behind him, two soldiers stood in the street with the loose, patient posture of men who had done this many times and would do it many more. One of them shifted his weight, and his boot scraped the cobbles, and from the back room Felix heard — faintly, horribly — the child stir.

*He's asleep,* she told herself. *He's asleep, he's quiet, he's nothing.*

"Come in, then," she said, and stepped aside, and committed her first sin of the morning: she did not call out to Odile, who was upstairs grinding ink, because some animal part of her understood that the fewer witnesses to this visit, the better.

Corvain entered the way water enters a hull — slowly, finding every low place. He walked the length of the shop without touching anything, his gloved hands folded behind him, reading the room the way Felix read a galley proof: top to bottom, left to right, hunting errors. The press. The paper racks. The composing stone with its half-set almanac page. The bank of type cases along the wall, each labeled in her father's neat engraver's capitals.

"You've reorganized," he said.

"My father died. Things get moved when someone dies."

"So they do." He stopped at the bench.

Felix's stomach went cold. Of all the places in the shop, he had chosen the one place she could not bear him to stand — the scarred oak bench where Thackeray had cut dies for thirty years, where the gravers still lay in their roll of leather, each handle worn to the shape of his grip. Corvain set his folio down on it, deliberately, the way a man sets a boot on a fallen flag.

"A fine bench," he said. "Beech, is it? Old growth. They don't fell trees like this anymore."

"It cuts true," Felix said. "That's all I know about it."

"That's all anyone needs to know about anything, Signoria. That it cuts true." He smiled, and something in the smile snagged at her memory like a burr in cloth. She had seen that smile before. Not on him — she was almost certain she had never met him — but somewhere. A face at a funeral. A name spoken by her mother in a tone she'd never used for anyone else. *Corvain.* Her mother had said it once, late, after wine, looking out the window at the harbor, and her father had laughed in a way that wasn't laughing at all.

The inspector opened his folio and began, methodically, to compare the shop's license against reality: presses registered, presses present; typesetters registered, typesetters employed; output declared, output sampled. He pulled an almanac from the rack and leafed through it with genuine, unhurried interest, pausing at the tide tables.

"Your father cut these figures?"

"He did."

"They're beautiful work. You can tell a Thackeray die anywhere — the serifs have that little flare, like a wave breaking." He set the almanac down and looked at her directly for the first time. "You have his hand."

"I have his bench."

"Mm." He turned back to the type cases. "May I?"

"It's a licensing audit," Felix said. "Not an inventory."

"It's whatever the Ministry says it is, Signoria, which today happens to be both." And he drew open the first case — the 24-point roman — and ran one finger down the ranks of lead letters, tilting them into the light.

From the cabinet, three steps behind him, came a sound.

Small. Soft. The sound of a child turning over in sleep, or nearly waking, or dreaming — a breathy half-sigh, gone almost before it existed.

Corvain's finger stopped.

Felix's heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs, and then she did the thing she would think about for the rest of her life: she moved. Not toward the cabinet — toward the window, throwing the shutter wide so that morning light and street noise flooded in, a fishwife shouting prices, gulls screaming off the breakwater.

"There," she said loudly. "You hear that? Third time this week. The gulls get into the eaves and carry on like the whole quarter's on fire. Drives my master printer mad. She's been up there since dawn trying to scare them off and they've won every round."

Corvain looked at her. The pause stretched — one second, two, three — long enough for Felix to understand exactly how thin the line was, how much of her life hung on whether a tired inspector decided a sigh was a bird or a child.

"Gulls," he said.

"Vellamare gulls. They fear nothing and respect less."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Recognition, maybe — of the lie, of the liar, of the shape of the thing being protected. Then he closed the type case, gently, and stepped away from the cabinet, and Felix exhaled a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

But he wasn't finished. He crossed to the last case in the row — the one her father had kept locked, the one Odile had unlocked two nights ago with the key from the atlas plate's wrapping — and stood before it without opening it.

"This one's empty," he observed.

"It's being recut. The sorts are worn."

"All of them?"

"Enough of them."

He nodded slowly, and Felix watched him file it away, watched the folio close, watched him gather himself to leave — and then, at the door, he paused with his hand on the frame and said, without turning:

"I knew your mother. Before she married your father. Vellamare was smaller then, and kinder, and we were both young enough to be foolish about it." Now he turned, and his gray eyes were unreadable. "She was a remarkable woman. I'm sorry I never told her so while it mattered."

"She'd have been flattered, I'm sure."

"No," Corvain said quietly. "She'd have been careful. She was always careful. It's why she chose Thackeray over me — she could see what I'd become before I could." He settled his folio under his arm. "I'll want your spring figures again within the week, Signoria. Send them to the Ministry personally. Don't trust them to the post."

And he was gone, the soldiers falling in behind him, their boots fading down the street until only the gulls remained, screaming their innocence from the eaves.

* * *

Felix stood in the doorway until they turned the corner. Then she shut the door, shot the bolt, and crossed the shop in four strides to the cabinet.

The child was awake. He looked up at her from the felt-lined dark with enormous, fever-bright eyes, clutching his wooden *a*, and when she lifted him out he weighed almost nothing and trembled like a struck bell.

"You're all right," she whispered, into his hair. "You're all right, you're all right." She said it eleven times, because eleven was how many seconds she had stood between him and the inspector's finger, and each second deserved its own promise.

Odile came down the stairs two at a time, ink-black to the elbows, having heard everything through the floorboards. She took one look at Felix's face and asked no questions.

"The spring figures," she said instead. "He wants them within the week."

"Yes."

"Then we have a week to be gone." Odile looked at the burned-down candle stubs, the half-set almanac page, the beautiful, doomed shop that smelled of ink and oil and thirty years of her life. "The smuggler sails on the morning tide day after tomorrow. Three checkpoints between here and the quay. We plan tonight."

Felix nodded. But she stayed by the window a moment longer, watching the empty street where Corvain had stood, turning over the strange arithmetic of the morning: that the man who had come to destroy them had also, in his own unfathomable way, warned them. *Send them personally. Don't trust them to the post.*

Her mother's old suitor. Her father's unrepaid debtor. An enemy with a familiar face, and mercy in it somewhere, buried deep — if it was mercy at all, and not just a longer, crueler kind of game.

She looked down at the child, who had fallen asleep again against her shoulder, the letter *a* still locked in his fist.

"Hold on to that," she murmured. "It's going to matter."

Because somewhere across the city, Inspector Corvain was writing his report — and everything now depended on what he chose to put in it, and what he chose to leave out.
