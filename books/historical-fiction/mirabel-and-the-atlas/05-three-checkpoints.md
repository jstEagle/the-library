## 5. Three Checkpoints

Odile had run the Sign of the Atlas for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned one rule above the others: never argue with a Thackeray. It was a rule she broke that night, and broke it badly, and would spend the rest of her life being glad she had.

The shop was closed. Shutters barred, lamp turned low, the great press draped in its canvas like a body under a sheet. In the back room, over a map of Vellamare drawn in chalk on the top of the composing stone — chalk, because chalk could be wiped away with a sleeve in the three seconds it took boots to come down the stairs — Odile laid out the route.

Felix sat across from her with the child asleep against her shoulder. The boy was better. The fever had broken on the second night, and now he slept the heavy, boneless sleep of the recovering, one fist curled around a loose piece of nine-point type he'd been given to hold because it kept him quiet and it couldn't be swallowed. He drooled slightly on Felix's collar. She did not seem to notice.

That, more than anything, frightened Odile.

* * *

"Three checkpoints," Odile said, and tapped the chalk map. "Between this door and the *Meridian*'s berth at Salt Quay, there are three. The fish market gate, here. The customs house, here. And the quay patrol, here." She drew three crosses, hard enough to snap the chalk. "Each one deadlier than the last. The gate is soldiers — boys, mostly, bored, hungry, bribable. The customs house is clerks with lists. And the quay patrol answers directly to Corvain."

"And Corvain," Felix said quietly, "knows my face."

"Corvain knew your mother's face. Which is worse." Odile straightened. "Which is why I carry the plate."

The silence that followed had weight. Tam, who was mending a cracked roller by the stove, set down his knife. Marta, who had been sorting type into cases with the mechanical precision of thirty years' habit, let a sort sit unsorted between her fingers.

"Felix—"

"No." Felix shifted the child higher on her shoulder. Her voice stayed level, which Odile recognized as the most dangerous register her voice could reach. "No, you don't."

"I'm sixty years old, girl. I'm nobody's list. I've been stopped eleven times since the licensing edicts and waved through ten of them because I look like what I am — an old woman going about old woman's business. You're twenty-three, you're Thackeray's daughter, and Corvain has been asking after Thackeray's daughter since before you could read. If they open that basket and find the plate, they find me, and they find an old woman who will tell them nothing worth telling. If they find you—" She stopped. She made herself finish. "If they find you, they find everything. Your father's hand. Your mother's name. A thread they can pull all the way back to this room and everyone standing in it."

"That's why it should be me."

"That is exactly why it should not be you."

* * *

It went on like that for some time. Odile laid out arguments the way she locked up type — each one fitted, justified, solid. She was known at the fish market; she'd bought herring from the same widow for two decades. She had no papers worth forging because she had papers so dull no clerk had ever looked past the first line. She had carried seditious type through occupied streets during the last troubles, when Felix was in leading strings, and she had done it without losing so much as an em quad.

And Felix met every argument with the same maddening stillness, the child breathing against her neck, until finally Odile ran out of logic and said the thing underneath the logic:

"I am not letting you die in my shop's debt, girl. Your father cut that plate. He died for less than what's in it. I will not bury his daughter too."

"You're not burying anyone." Felix stood, carefully, and laid the sleeping boy in the blanket nest they'd made in the empty type cabinet — the tall one, the double-case cabinet with the false back Tam had built in an afternoon of sweating terror while Corvain's men drank coffee downstairs. She tucked the blanket with hands that were very steady, and when she turned around her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain.

"He left him at *my* door," Felix said. "Not yours. Mine. Whoever she was, she walked past the harbor master's office, past the garrison, past every other printer in Vellamare, and she chose the Atlas. She chose his bench. That plate is cut in my father's hand and it came to *me*, Odile. Not to the network. Not to the cause. To me. You can argue strategy all night and you'll be right about half of it, but you cannot argue inheritance."

"It's not an inheritance, it's a death warrant with pretty edges—"

"It's both!" Felix's voice cracked and she mastered it, jaw tight. "Everything worth anything in this city is both right now. You taught me that. You taught me that the almanacs pay for the pamphlets and the pamphlets are worth more than the almanacs, and you never once asked whether the arithmetic was safe. So don't ask me to be safe now."

Tam spoke from the stove, without looking up from his roller. "She's got you there, Odile."

"Stay out of it, Tam."

"She's got you there," he repeated placidly, and drew his knife along the leather again.

Marta set down her sort at last. "There's a middle road," she said, in the voice she used for settling disputes over whose kerning was at fault. "Nobody carries it alone. The plate goes in the fish basket — the false bottom, the one we used for the '19 pamphlets. Felix walks it through the gate because the widow Verel knows her face and trusts it. But Odile walks twenty paces behind, separate, carrying nothing, seeing everything. If the gate turns ugly, you're a witness, not a courier. If it goes clean, you meet her at the tannery lane and take stock."

Odile opened her mouth to object and found, to her considerable irritation, that she couldn't. It was good. It was the kind of plan she herself would have built, if her heart hadn't been shouting down her head.

"Twenty paces," she said at last. "And if I signal — I'll drop my scarf, left hand — you put that basket down and you walk away and you let them take it. Do you hear me? Lead can be recut. Paper can be reprinted. *You* cannot."

Felix looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes — grief, maybe, or gratitude, or the terrible arithmetic of a young woman realizing that the people who raised her intend to spend themselves on her behalf and finding she wants to forbid it and cannot.

"I hear you," Felix said. Which was not the same as agreeing, and they both knew it.

* * *

They worked through the small hours. Marta sewed the plate into the fish basket's false bottom with waxed thread, her stitches so fine they'd have shamed a surgeon. Tam ground out a page of almanac proofs to stuff around it — innocent paper, weather tables, tide charts, the kind of thing a soldier's eye slides off like rain off oilcloth. Odile herself forged nothing, printed nothing, committed nothing to ink that could hang them; instead she sat with the chalk map and memorized it, and then wiped it away with her apron, and then sat looking at her own empty hands.

At some point Felix came and stood beside her at the cold hearth.

"If I don't come back," Felix said, "the boy goes to Marta. She wanted children. She pretends she didn't, but she hums when she changes him."

"He's coming back with you."

"If I don't—"

"He's coming back with you," Odile said, "because I forbid you to die, and I have been forbidding printers to do foolish things for thirty-one years with a success rate I refuse to disclose."

Felix laughed — a short, startled sound, gone as fast as it came. Then she did something she hadn't done since she was small: she leaned her head against Odile's shoulder, just for a moment, the way she used to fall asleep waiting for her father to lock up the shop.

Odile let her. Thirty-one years, and she could count on one hand the times the girl had asked for comfort outright. This wasn't asking. This was worse. This was goodbye dressed as habit.

"The tide turns at dawn," Odile said. "We go at four."

* * *

At quarter to four the fog off the harbor had thickened to wool. Odile stood in the doorway of the Sign of the Atlas and watched Felix come down the steps with the fish basket on her hip, wrapped in a shawl, looking precisely like what she was pretending to be — a young woman running an errand before market, tired, unremarkable, invisible.

Twenty paces behind, Odile pulled her own shawl up and followed.

The streets were gray and dripping. Somewhere ahead, the fish market gate waited with its lanterns and its bored soldiers, and beyond it the customs house with its lists, and beyond that the quay where Inspector Corvain's patrol walked the fog like wolves walking a treeline.

Odile counted paces. At fifteen she noticed Felix had begun to hum — barely, under her breath, a scrap of something tuneless.

It took Odile three more paces to place it. A printing song. An old setter's rhythm, the beat you keep pulling sorts from the case, the one Thackeray used to drum on the bench with his knuckles while he worked.

The girl was carrying her father's map through a city full of soldiers, and keeping time to his song.

Odile adjusted her shawl, dropped back to twenty paces exactly, and followed her into the fog toward the fish market gate — where, even now, a lantern swung, and a soldier's voice called out: *"Halt. Basket inspection. All traffic, single file."*

All traffic. Not random. Ordered.

Someone had told them to look.
