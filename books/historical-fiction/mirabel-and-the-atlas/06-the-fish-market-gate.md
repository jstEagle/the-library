## 6. The Fish Market Gate

The basket smelled like a lie that had worked hard to become true.

Felix had watched Marta Voss pack it herself at the stall behind the tannery: a layer of herring packed in wet salt, then the false bottom — a board of pine no thicker than a hymnal, the copper plate sewn into oilcloth beneath it, and above it another layer of fish, and above *that* a scattering of shrimp gone soft on purpose, so the whole thing would reek of ordinary commerce, of a woman too busy to wash her baskets properly. The needlework holding the false bottom was Marta's own. She'd bitten the thread through twice without looking up from her work.

"Nobody," she'd said, "inspects a fishwife's basket. They inspect her papers, they inspect her face, and then they wave her through because the smell has already defeated them."

But Felix wasn't a fishwife. That was the flaw in the plan, and everyone at the stall had seen it and said nothing. Marta couldn't go — her husband was already under watch for the pamphlets he carried. Odile had argued for it until dawn, until her voice cracked, and Felix had put her hand flat on the bench where her father's dies still lay in their felt-lined drawer and said, in a voice she didn't recognize as her own:

"He cut it. I carry it."

So now Felix walked the last hundred yards to the fish market gate wearing Marta's second-best shawl and carrying a basket that weighed eleven pounds more than it should have, and every step of those hundred yards she kept time without meaning to — *one-two-three-four*, the pull-and-drop rhythm, the setter's beat her father used to drum on the bench — because her hands were shaking and the rhythm was the only thing that steadied them.

Behind her, twenty paces back exactly, a shape in a gray shawl moved through the fog. Odile. Following against all sense, against everything Felix had begged her not to do. If Felix was stopped, Odile was supposed to keep walking. That had been the whole argument. Instead Odile had simply said, "Twenty paces is not close enough to help you and not far enough to save me," which was Odile's way of admitting she intended to do neither.

The gate itself was two wagons wide, wedged between a chandler's warehouse and the long low wall of the market stalls. A lantern hung from a pole driven into the cobbles, swinging in the harbor wind, throwing light that never stayed where you wanted it. Three soldiers. A folding table. A sergeant with a ledger.

And at the table, checking a fish cart's papers with his boot propped on the wheel, a boy Felix had grown up with.

Tam Arrel. Sixteen when the conscription lists came through the Salt Quarter; seventeen now, though he still had the same cowlick at the crown that no amount of army barbering could kill. He'd been one of the children who hung around the print shop's open door in summer because Thackeray let them set their own names backwards in loose type and pull proofs of them. Tam's proof had hung on the shop wall for three years. TAM ARREL, printed wrong-reading, inked unevenly, treasured like a diploma.

*He doesn't know what I'm carrying,* Felix told herself. *He's a boy at a table with a ledger. He is not the regime. He is a boy.*

Her heart did not believe her, but she made it walk anyway.

The line ahead of her was short — curfew fog kept honest people home, and the dishonest were already through or already caught. An old woman with kindling. A man leading a mule. Then Felix, and the lantern swung, and the sergeant looked up.

"Papers."

She handed them over. Marta's cousin's market pass, doctored overnight by the best hands in Vellamare — hers. She had reset the date column herself at two in the morning, breathing through her mouth so her nerves wouldn't tremble the composing stick, and even now, even terrified, some cold professional corner of her mind noted with satisfaction that the kerning was flawless. If they held it to a lamp, the paper stock was right, the watermark was right, the stamp impression had been pulled from a cast Odile had made of a genuine seal.

It was a good forgery. It was also, she understood suddenly, completely beside the point, because the sergeant wasn't reading it. He was looking past it at her face.

"You're not Voss's cousin."

Here it was. Here was the moment she had rehearsed forty times in the dark, and her rehearsed voice came out steady, which surprised her more than anything else that morning.

"I'm his wife's girl. From the Salt Quarter side. Marta's laid up — her hands, the salt gets in the cracks." She held up her own palms, raw from the shop's alkali soap, honest-looking cracks that were, at least, entirely real. "Someone's got to move the catch or it rots and the family eats nothing. You can send me home if you want. I'll come back tomorrow and the day after, till someone lets a girl feed her brothers."

The sergeant grunted. It was not a warm grunt. But it was the grunt of a man doing arithmetic about how much he cared, and the sum came out low.

"Open the basket."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. Felix heard the fog dripping off the chandler's eaves. She heard her own pulse keeping her father's time.

Then Tam looked up from the fish cart.

"Ferryman's waiting on this cart, Sergeant, and he's got the tide against him. I've known these people since I was in short coats — half the quarter's come through here since dawn." He grinned at Felix, easy and unguarded, the grin of a boy who had once been prouder of his name printed backwards than of anything else in his life. "That's the printer's daughter, isn't it? Thackeray's girl? My mum still talks about your dad letting us mess up his type."

The sergeant's eyes went from Felix to Tam and back. Something shifted in them — not suspicion. Boredom resolving into permission.

"Thackeray. The printer who died." He handed the papers back. "Fine. Go on, girl. And tell your mother's cousin to wash her baskets."

"I'll tell her," Felix said, and her voice only shook now, which was safe, which read as gratitude.

She walked through the gate.

Ten steps. Fifteen. The lantern light slid off her shoulders. Twenty paces back, the gray shawl turned to follow her, and somewhere in the fog beyond the wall Odile was watching her daughter-by-nothing walk out the far side of a checkpoint carrying a map that could hang them all, and Felix allowed herself — one step, two — to feel it. Not triumph. Relief, vast and stupid, flooding her legs so fast she nearly stumbled.

That was when the rider came out of the fog on the quay road.

She heard him before she saw him — hooves on wet stone, too fast for curfew hours. A lieutenant, mud-spattered, dispatch case strapped to his thigh. He reined in at the gate so hard the horse skidded, and his voice carried across the whole market square, thin with exhaustion and authority.

"Sergeant. New orders, effective immediately. Every basket, every sack, every load — opened and searched. All traffic. No exceptions." He thrust a folded paper down at the sergeant. "They're moving something out of the city tonight. Paper. Copper. Could be disguised as anything. Search *everything*."

Felix did not stop walking. Stopping was the one thing she must never do. She kept her pace exactly, the pace of a tired girl with rotting herring, and she counted her steps away from the gate the way her father had counted his type — and behind her she heard the sergeant bellow:

"You there! The herring girl! Hold up!"

*One-two-three-four.* Her father's rhythm. Pull the sort, set the line. Her feet kept moving because stopping meant turning around meant facing the lantern meant the end of everything, and the lie her body told was simple and total: *a girl who has done nothing wrong does not hurry.*

"Hold UP, I said—"

"She's deaf with the cold, Sergeant!" Tam again, laughing, covering, God knew why covering — maybe just because he was sixteen and the sergeant was a nuisance and the morning was long. "HERRING GIRL! Turn round when you're spoken to!"

She turned. Of course she turned. She made her face empty and polite and slightly stupid, the face of a girl whose whole mind was on supper.

Behind her, at the table, the soldiers were already pulling the old woman's kindling apart stick by stick. The mule driver was unstrapping his panniers. The lieutenant sat his horse with his hand resting on his dispatch case, watching the line with dead, patient eyes, and Felix understood with terrible clarity that the search was not random, had never been random, that someone — Corvain, it would be Corvain — had told them what to look for, and that the only reason she was ten steps free instead of ten steps from ruin was a cowlicked boy's fond memory of her father's kindness.

Grace. Not skill. She had passed by grace, and grace had a ledger too, and someday it would present the bill.

"Bring it here," the sergeant called. "Basket on the table."

Felix walked back toward the lantern. Eleven pounds of copper and treason swung gently at her hip with each step, sewn under herring, under salt, under a pine board and Marta Voss's bitten thread, and twenty paces back in the fog the gray shawl had gone utterly still.

* * *

Later — much later, with her lungs still burning and the customs house clock striking seven across the water — Felix would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. Memory kept only three things, bright as freshly cast type.

The first: setting the basket on the table, and Tam, assigned to the search, whistling under his breath as he took the top layer of herring out — and freezing mid-whistle, because the smell hit him, and he wrinkled his nose and said, "Saints, Thackeray's girl, this is criminal," and the sergeant, downwind, stepped back and waved a gloved hand and said "Enough, enough, if she's smuggling anything it's plague" —

— and the second thing: Tam's hands going into the fish, lifting the second layer, his fingers an inch — one inch — above the false bottom, while the lieutenant on the horse leaned forward in the saddle to watch.

And the third thing, the thing that saved them all, arriving from nowhere, from everywhere, from the fog itself:

A bell. The market bell, then the church bells after it, then shouting from the direction of the customs house — fire in a granary, or a riot, or a rumor dressed as both, no one ever knew. The lieutenant's horse screamed and sidestepped. The sergeant shouted an order nobody obeyed. And Tam, seizing his moment the way boys seize moments, slammed the herring back into the basket with both hands, fish juice flying, shoved it across the table at her, and yelled over the chaos:

"Take your stinking fish and GO, woman!"

Felix took her basket and went.

She did not look back at the gate. She did not look at the gray shawl falling in beside her, twenty paces closing to five, to two, to Odile's breath at her ear, saying nothing, saying everything. She walked with her father's rhythm hammering in her blood — *one-two-three-four* — through the fog toward the customs house, the second checkpoint, the one with documents and desks and no boys who owed her family kindness.

Only later, at the tannery, when Marta unpacked the basket with shaking hands and found the plate untouched, did anyone speak of what had happened. Marta touched the oilcloth once, gently, the way you touch a sleeping child.

"And the next gate?" Odile asked quietly.

No one answered. Because they all knew the customs house kept ledgers, not graces — and somewhere in its ledgers, Inspector Corvain was already reading names.
