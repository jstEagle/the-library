## 7. The Customs House

The customs house had been a counting hall once, back when Vellamare traded in cloth and spice instead of suspicion. Now its vaulted ceiling echoed with boot heels, and the long marble counters where merchants had weighed their goods held instead a row of clerks behind iron grilles, stamping papers, shaking heads, waving people through or into the side rooms from which fewer came out than went in.

Felix joined the queue at the third counter with the fishmonger's basket on her hip and the plate riding flat against her spine, sewn into the false bottom Odile's nephew had stitched before dawn. The copper was warm from her body now. She'd stopped thinking of it as a plate hours ago. It was a second heartbeat, pressed between her shoulder blades.

The line moved in fits. A woman ahead of her argued about a permit for dried cod and lost the argument. Two soldiers stood at the end of the counter with rifles slung, not doing anything, which was somehow worse than if they'd been doing something.

*Papers.* That was all she needed to survive: papers. She had papers — Odile's real trade license, folded soft as cloth from handling, and a letter of consignment for the fish, written in Odile's hand because Odile could imitate any clerk in the city. The plan had been simple. Show the license, show the letter, look bored.

Then the soldier at the counter turned his stamp over in his fingers and said, "New orders since noon. Consignment letters need a district countersignature." He jerked his chin toward a placard nailed above the grille. *BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL COMMISSARIAT, ALL DOCUMENTS MUST BEAR THE SEAL AND SIGNATURE OF THE DISTRICT MAGISTRATE OR HIS DELEGATE.*

"You can get one at the magistrate's annex," the soldier added, with the flat kindness of a man who did not care whether you got one. "Two streets east. Come back tomorrow."

Tomorrow the ship would be gone.

"I have the countersignature," Felix said.

He looked up. "Where?"

"In my bag. My master sent me for it this morning." Her voice came out level, and some cold, clear part of her mind marveled at that. "It's a separate sheet."

"Let me see it."

She had nothing. She had a license, a fish letter, a child asleep against her chest under the shawl, a basket of herring going silver-eyed and stiff, and a copper map of every gun emplacement on the coast sewn into her spine.

And she had forty years of Thackeray craft in her hands.

"Here." She set the basket down, opened the satchel, made a show of rummaging — and let out a small, disgusted sound. "It's not here. He must have left it on the bench." She pressed her knuckles to her eyes like a girl on the edge of tears. "He'll flay me. This is the third time this month."

The soldier's face did something complicated. Sympathy and irritation fighting to a draw.

"He'll want it today," Felix said. "The shipment goes out tonight or the buyer walks. Look — I work at the Sign of the Atlas, off Tanner's Row. We're licensed printers. Give me an hour and I'll bring you the letter with the seal still wet, if that helps. Or—" She paused, and let the pause do the work. "Or stamp me through, and I'll come back after with the whole file. License, letter, countersignature, everything."

"We don't stamp anyone through without—"

"Sergeant." One of the idle soldiers had drifted over, older, with the creased neck of a man who had been standing in doorways for twenty years. He glanced at the license when Felix offered it up, and his eyebrows rose a fraction. "Atlas Press. They do the army almanacs. Tide tables, artillery corrections." He handed it back almost respectfully. "Half the garrison would be shooting at the wrong horizon without them."

"So it's true," Felix said, letting weariness flood in. "You've been getting them late. I keep telling my master the roads—"

"They've been late twice," the sergeant admitted, and there it was, the small grievance of a small man, and Felix stepped through the gap it opened like water through a cracked dike.

"Which is why he sent me today with everything signed and sealed, and why I'm standing here looking like an idiot because I left the one page that matters on the bench." She hoisted the basket. "An hour. I'll be back before your shift ends, and you can tell your corporal you personally fixed the artillery delays."

The younger soldier hesitated. The sergeant shrugged with magnificent indifference. "Almanac press. Let her go. If she doesn't come back we know where the shop is."

That was the joke of it, Felix thought, walking toward the doors with her heart slamming against the copper at her back. *We know where the shop is.* Everyone knew where the shop was. By nightfall, everyone would know where the shop *had been*.

She was three steps from the doors when the trumpets started.

Not alarm trumpets — procession trumpets, the brassy stutter of official arrival, and the customs house rearranged itself around the sound the way water rearranges around a dropped stone. Soldiers straightened. Clerks stood. Through the tall windows Felix saw the column forming in the square outside: mounted officers, a standard, and at the center of it, dismounting with the unhurried precision of a man who had never once hurried in his life, a figure in a grey inspector's coat.

Corvain.

Her lungs forgot their business. Of course he was here. Of course. Three checkpoints between her and the tide, and the regime had put its sharpest instrument at the middle one. Odile would have cursed in three languages. Felix simply turned her back to the window and became very interested in adjusting the shawl over the sleeping child, buying seconds, buying nothing.

"Wait."

A hand on her arm — the younger soldier. "Inspector wants document checks suspended until he's passed. Nobody in or out for a quarter hour."

"A quarter hour," Felix repeated faintly. The tide waited for no inspector. "And then?"

"And then normal processing resumes." He was already moving toward the doors. "Stay inside the line, please."

Inside the line. Inside while Corvain walked past close enough to touch, while the clock ate her morning in bites, while the ship's agent counted down minutes she could see burning behind her eyes. Felix set the basket down, leaned against a pillar, and let herself look — really look — at the customs house for the first time.

There, along the far wall: the old assay office, converted, but the fittings still in place. A proofing press, army surplus, used for printing seizure manifests and wanted bills. A cabinet of type beside it, government issue, the sorts worn shiny. And above the press, pinned by its corners, a fresh specimen sheet of the district magistrate's countersignature — printed that very week for the clerks' reference, because the regime trusted no one's memory, only paper.

*All documents must bear the seal and signature of the district magistrate or his delegate.*

His delegate. Any delegate. The order didn't say whose hand.

Felix had cut her first sort of type at seven years old, standing on her father's footstool, her small fists around the graver while his big ones steadied them. She had spent fifteen years learning what he called the honest lie of the trade — that every letter is a forgery of itself, that a B cut well enough will convince ten thousand readers it has always been a B. Her father used to hold a finished die up to the light and say, *Now it's true, little fish. Now it's true forever.*

The child stirred under the shawl. Felix kissed the damp crown of his head without deciding to.

She crossed the hall to the assay office the way a woman crosses to a public inkstand — briskly, with the expression of someone who has done a tedious thing a hundred times. No one stopped her. The soldiers were watching the doors; the clerks were watching the inspector. She took up a pen, dipped it in the government ink, and wrote the countersignature line beneath a fabricated consignment header, her wrist rolling into Magistrate Delorme's looping, self-satisfied capital D exactly as it appeared on the specimen sheet — except better, except *truer*, because she gave it the slight tremor of a hand signing its four-hundredth document of the day, and no living magistrate ever signed his four-hundredth document with a steady hand.

Then she blotted it, folded it into the fish letter, and walked back to the counter with the walk of a woman who has wasted enough of the morning.

"Countersignature," she said, laying it flat. "Delorme himself. You can see the seal impression where the wax caught."

The younger soldier studied it. Turned it over. Studied it again. Felix watched him not-find anything wrong and felt her father's ghost lean against her shoulder, warm and approving and appalled.

"That looks right," the sergeant said over his shoulder, already bored. "Stamp her."

The stamp came down. Red wax, red ink, the weight of the state pressing its approval onto her forgery, and Felix thanked them in a voice that only shook a little, gathered her basket, and turned for the doors—

—and the doors were open, and Inspector Corvain was coming through them.

Up close he was thinner than the portrait her mother had kept hidden in a drawer of sheet music, and greyer, and his eyes were the same: pale, patient, the color of shallow water over sand. He was scanning the hall out of habit rather than purpose, a man reading a room the way other men read weather. His gaze crossed the queue. Crossed Felix. Stopped.

Came back.

She kept moving. One step. Another. The basket swayed on her hip; the herring smelled like victory and rot. Behind her, she heard him say something low to the duty officer, and then his boots on the marble, unhurried, closing.

"Madame."

She turned. There was no help in it; turning away would have been worse. "Inspector."

"Your papers." He held out his hand, and it was not a request.

She gave him the stamped letter. He read it upside down first — she watched him do it, the way her father read proofs, hunting errors in the negative — and then turned it right side up, and she saw the exact moment the countersignature stopped being paperwork to him and became handwriting.

His thumb moved, very slightly, along the edge of the page. The gesture of a man feeling the grain of a lie.

"This is Delorme's hand," Corvain said.

"Yes, Inspector."

"It is a good imitation of Delorme's hand." His voice stayed conversational, almost gentle, pitched below the noise of the hall. "But Delorme signs with pressure on the downstroke. Whoever wrote this was trained in reverse — pressure on the upstroke, the way engravers sign plates." He raised his pale eyes. "I have seen that hand before. Many years ago. On tide tables, on artillery corrections, on a certain atlas of the coast that was supposed to have died with its cutter."

The hall went distant. Trumpets, boots, the clerk's stamp falling like a slow heartbeat — all of it behind glass. Felix held his gaze because running was impossible and fainting was fatal, and somewhere under the terror she found her father's bench-steadiness and set it between them like a blade laid flat on a table.

"I don't know what you mean, Inspector. My master signed that letter. I only carried it."

"Your master," Corvain said softly, "is dead."

"Odile Marchand is alive and well and very annoyed with me."

Something flickered across his face — not doubt, not quite. Recognition, maybe, of a fight he hadn't expected to enjoy. He lifted the letter toward the light, and Felix understood with terrible clarity that she was perhaps ninety seconds from irons, that the plate at her back might as well have been screaming, that the child under the shawl had begun, merciful silence be praised, to wake—

The windows blew in.

Not all of them — the tall panes over the eastern arch shattered inward in a glittering curtain, and with the glass came the sound: a wall of shouting from the square, deep and animal, hundreds of voices with nothing left to lose. Someone screamed *bread*. Someone screamed a word Felix hadn't heard shouted openly in years, the old word, the forbidden one, the name of the republic. A smoke pot arced through the broken window frame trailing grey, and the customs house dissolved.

Soldiers ran for the doors. Clerks ran under their counters. The duty officer was bellowing orders that no one could hear, and Corvain — Corvain turned toward the riot for one heartbeat, one professional heartbeat, because duty was the spine of him — and Felix ran.

Through the smoke. Through the falling glass. Out the western postern where the grain porters pushed through with their sacks and no one checked baskets, no one checked anything, the whole machine of the checkpoint seized and shrieking. She ran with the child pressed to her chest and the plate hammering against her spine and tears cutting clean lines through the ash on her face, and behind her, faint under the riot, she thought she heard her name — not *madame*, not *halt*, but her name, spoken the way a man speaks a word he swore he'd forgotten —

— and she did not stop.

The quay lay ahead through the thinning smoke. Beyond the last patrol line, beyond the grey water, a ship rode the morning tide with her canvas half-loosed, waiting.

Waiting for the plate. Waiting for her.

Behind her, across the roofs of Tanner's Row, a black column of smoke was beginning to rise from a street she loved, and Felix ran toward the water and away from it both at once, unable to look, afraid to know, knowing.
