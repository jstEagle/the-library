## 8. Fire at the Sign of the Atlas

*Odile*

She had always known the shop would die by fire. Every printer knew it. Paper and oil and lamp-black — a shop was a pyre waiting for its match, and in Vellamare the matches were issued by the state.

Odile sent the boy Perrin away at dusk with a lie about needing lamp oil from the Chandlers' Row, and when his footsteps faded she bolted the door behind him and stood a moment in the quiet, listening to the building breathe. Forty years of breathing. The press she'd learned on as an apprentice girl, its iron frame worn to a soft shine where ten thousand hands had gripped it. The type cabinets, each drawer labeled in her own cramped hand. The archive room at the back — twenty years of pamphlets, proofs, subscriber lists, correspondence. Names. Every name a sentence.

They would come for it tonight. She had known since Corvain's visit; a man like that did not leave without finding what he came for. He had lingered over Thackeray's bench too long, run his thumb along the grain of the wood like a man touching a scar. And this morning a soldier had walked past the window twice, slowly, memorizing it.

So Odile worked.

She started with the correspondence, feeding letters into the stove until the grate glowed orange and the chimney breathed sparks. Then the subscriber lists, page by page, each name vanishing in a curl of ash that smelled like burnt sugar. She worked methodically, without hurry, because haste was how mistakes were made, and because some part of her wanted to read each name once more before she killed it. Marta of the ropewalk. The two brothers who fished off the north jetty. A lieutenant whose loyalty had cost him everything. She said their names aloud as they burned, a kind of last rites.

The child's swaddling blanket was already gone — Felix had taken it, cut into strips and resewn around the copper plate beneath fish-gut rags. Good. The plate was on the water now, or nearly. Everything else could burn.

By midnight the archive was half a stove's worth of ash and the smoke was thick enough to taste. That was when she heard the boots — not one pair but many, the heavy cadenced tread of soldiers who did not care whether they were heard.

She did not run. There was nowhere left in the building worth running to, and besides, she wanted them to find her here, among the ashes of the evidence, so that whatever they took from this room they took from *her* hands only.

The door came down rather than opened — of course it did; subtlety was not the army's trade — and lamplight flooded in, and silhouettes, six or eight of them, rifles leveled at a sixty-year-old woman standing at a stove.

"Step away from the fire," said an officer.

"I'm warming my hands," Odile said. "It's cold in here. It's been cold in here all night."

He crossed the room, looked into the stove, into the drifts of ash she'd been sweeping across the floorboards since sundown, and she watched him understand exactly what he was looking at and exactly how little of it remained. His jaw tightened.

"Odile Marchand," he read from a paper. "Master printer. You are under arrest for sedition, conspiracy, and destruction of state property."

"Destruction?" She smiled at him, an old woman's smile, unhurried. "I burned nothing of the state's. Only mine. A lifetime of my own ink and paper. You're welcome to the rest." She gestured at the presses, the cabinets, the great wooden sign upstairs — an atlas borne on the shoulders of a carved figure, gilded once, flaking now. "Take the whole shop. It's yours. I'd only ask you to be careful with the type. Some of it is older than your regime."

The officer did not smile. "Where is Felix Thackeray?"

"Gone to bed. Gone to church. Gone wherever girls go these days." She folded her hands. "I raised her after her father died. Ask me anything you like about her, Captain, and I'll tell you a story. Whether it's true is another matter."

They bound her wrists with cord, not kindly, and marched her through her own shop past the bench where Thackeray had cut his last dies, past the cabinet where a feverish child had slept through an inspector's search three days ago. At the door she stopped and looked back once — not at the soldiers, not at the officer, but at the sign above the stair, the atlas on its bearer's shoulders.

"Hold still," she told it softly, as if it were a child being fitted for new boots. "You're going somewhere."

Then they put her in a cart, and the cart rolled toward the citadel, and behind her — she did not see it begin, but she felt it somehow, the way you feel weather coming in your knuckles — behind her, the Sign of the Atlas began to burn.

*Felix*

Felix saw the glow from the quay before she understood it.

She stood in the lee of the customs warehouse with the basket on her hip and the child asleep against her shoulder — he had finally stopped burning with fever that afternoon, thank every saint there wasn't — watching the fishing boats come in for the dawn market, and the sky over the north quarter of the city turned the color of a bruise. Orange at the center. Wrong, for that hour, for that direction. There was nothing in the north quarter that should be lit at all.

Then the bell in the harbor chapel began ringing, fast and ragged, the alarm pattern, and the fishermen stopped hauling and turned to look, and Felix's legs went out from under understanding before her mind caught up.

"No," she said. Just that. Just *no*, once, quietly, the way you say a word you don't believe.

The shop was four streets back from the water. From the quay you couldn't see it directly — you saw its light thrown against the low clouds, a stain spreading upward, pulsing. But she knew the shape of that stain. She had grown up under that roof; she knew which way the smoke would lean on a night wind from the east, and it leaned the way it leaned now.

She started walking. Not running — running would draw the patrol's eye, and the plate was sewn against her ribs and she could not be taken, could not, whatever else happened tonight the plate had to reach the ship — but walking fast, the child clutched tight, one hand pressing the basket flat against her body as if she could hold the whole world together with her arm.

A hand caught her elbow. The smuggler's agent — Marrec, gray-bearded, smelling of tar — pulled her back into the warehouse shadow.

"Not that way."

"That's my home. That's—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Odile. Odile stayed behind. She said she had things to destroy, she said she'd follow before midnight—"

"She stayed because she meant to be taken, girl, or she stayed because she ran out of time. Either way, if you walk up that street with that basket, everything she burned herself to protect goes into a soldier's pocket by morning." Marrec's grip was iron. "Look at me. Look at me. What did she send you here to do?"

Felix closed her eyes. Behind her eyelids the shop stood complete: the press, the cabinets, the smell of ink and lamp oil, Thackeray's bench with its grooves worn by his hands and then hers, the narrow stairs, the attic room where she'd slept under the rafters and listened to rain. All of it burning. All of it gone, or going, while she stood in the dark doing arithmetic with other people's courage.

"She sent me to carry the map," Felix said.

"Then carry it."

Across the harbor, the fire found more air. She could hear it now even at this distance — a low continuous roar beneath the bell, and beneath that, faintly, the sound that broke her: cheering. Soldiers' voices, whooping, somewhere near the blaze. They were letting it burn. In Vellamare, fires were fought within minutes; the whole city feared flame the way it feared flood. Nobody was fighting this one.

"They gave the order," she whispered. "They're just — standing there. Letting it go."

"A print shop full of seditious paper doesn't need an executioner. It needs a match and a blind eye." Marrec glanced at the child, sleeping through it all, his cheek damp against her neck. "And the old woman?"

"They'll take her to the citadel." Felix's throat was raw. "If she's alive."

"If she's alive, she's proud of you, and she'd box your ears for standing here weeping when the tide turns in ninety minutes."

That landed. It landed the way Odile's words always landed — blunt, unglamorous, true. Felix wiped her face with the back of her wrist, once, hard, and when she lowered her hand something had rearranged itself inside her. Not calm. Nothing so gentle as calm. It was more like the moment at the bench when a cut piece of type separates from the parent plate with a clean click, and there is no joining it back, and you look at the small bright letter in your palm and understand it can never again be anything except itself.

There was no going back. Not to the shop — the shop was light against the clouds now, a pillar of it. Not to the life before the woman in the fog, before *forget my face*. The regime had burned her house and taken her family, and it had done it believing that would end the matter. It had done the one thing guaranteed never to end it.

"They think fire finishes a press," she said. Her voice came out level. Almost surprised by its own levelness. "My father taught me better. You don't destroy type by burning it. Lead doesn't burn. You melt it down and cast it again. Every letter, over and over, forever."

Marrec studied her. "Is that a promise?"

"It's a plan."

The child stirred against her shoulder and made a small sound, half-asleep, and she shifted him higher and pressed her lips to his hair. Ash was beginning to fall on the quay — fine gray flakes drifting down out of the ruined sky, settling on the boats, the baskets, the black water. She caught one on her palm and watched it dissolve.

Somewhere behind her, out in the harbor dark, the smuggler's ship rode at anchor, riding low with cargo, waiting for the tide. Somewhere ahead, at the foot of the quay, the last checkpoint between her and the water — and Inspector Corvain, if he had any sense at all, would be standing there, because he had always had sense, and because he had loved her mother, and because tonight of all nights she could no longer afford to guess what that meant.

The chapel bell kept tolling. The tide was turning. Felix gathered the basket, gathered the child, gathered herself, and walked toward the light at the end of the quay — where a man in a gray coat stood waiting, hatless in the falling ash.
