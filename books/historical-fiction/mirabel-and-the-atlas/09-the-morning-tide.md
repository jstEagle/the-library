## 9. The Morning Tide

The quay smelled of tar and dying fish and smoke.

Felix kept her eyes forward, on the water, on the grey line where the harbor met a sky just beginning to think about light. Behind her, across the channel, the Sign of the Atlas still burned. She could feel it at her back like a hand pressed between her shoulder blades — heat that wasn't there, light that was only the first suggestion of dawn, but her body insisted otherwise. Her body had been insisting all night: *run, run, run.* She had not run. Running was for people with nothing left to carry.

She carried a fishmonger's basket gone sour with herring brine, a copper plate sewn into its false bottom, and a child.

Tam slept against her chest in the sling Odile had rigged from sailcloth, his fever finally broken sometime in the black hours before the fire, his breath coming small and even against her collarbone. Every few steps she bent her head as if adjusting the basket's handle and listened. Still breathing. Still hers, whatever anyone said about whose he'd been first.

The smuggler's ship rode at anchor past the last of the moorings — *Kestrel*, black-hulled, no flag, her captain supposedly a man who traded in salt and lies in equal measure. Somewhere aboard was an agent of the fleet. That was all Odile had told her, three days ago, in the candlelit back room with the atlas plate spread open between them like a wound. *Find the Kestrel. Find the man who asks about tides. Give him nothing until he says the word "Thackeray."*

Odile. Felix's throat closed and she made herself breathe through it. Odile had stayed behind. Odile had stood in the doorway of the burning shop with her sleeves rolled and her chin up, shoving Felix toward the water stairs, saying, *The archives burn or I do, girl, and only one of those is useful* — and then the patrol wagons had come, and Felix had run after all, down the stinking steps into a borrowed skiff, because there was no other way to make Odile's sacrifice mean anything.

There were soldiers on the quay ahead.

Two of them, lanterns swinging, standing at the foot of the customs pier where the harbor police kept their final table. Beyond them, the water. Beyond the water, everything.

Felix adjusted the basket on her hip and walked.

* * *

The checkpoint was a plank table, a brazier, a ledger, and a sergeant who looked like he'd been awake too long to be dangerous. He waved forward a fishwife ahead of her, tipped her basket, prodded at the herring with a stick, waved her on. Routine. The machinery of occupation running smooth as a press on its thousandth impression.

Then the lantern nearest the brazier turned, and Inspector Corvain stepped out of the dark.

Felix had seen him twice in her life before this week. Once at her mother's funeral, standing at the back in civilian black, hat in his hands, leaving before the prayers ended. Once outside the shop, years later, pausing to read the almanac broadsheet in the window with an expression she'd been too young to name and understood now: it was the look of a man reading a letter from someone dead.

He wore the regime's grey now, well-cut, and he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

"Name," said the sergeant.

"Felix Thackeray." No point lying. Corvain would know her face; he'd studied it across a licensing audit table two days ago while she lied to him with her heartbeat hammering in her ears and a feverish child folded up among the type cases behind her.

Something moved through the inspector's face — not surprise. Confirmation. He came to the table and set one gloved hand flat on the ledger, and the sergeant, reading some signal Felix couldn't see, suddenly found urgent business with the brazier.

"Thackeray." Corvain said it the way you'd test a coin against your teeth. "Your shop is burning."

"I can see it from here."

"Do you know how it started?"

"Lanterns are careless things."

A muscle worked in his jaw. Behind him the harbor lay flat and pewter-colored, and the *Kestrel*'s riding lights burned steady, patient, maddeningly far.

"You were seen leaving the customs house yesterday," Corvain said. "Through a riot you did not start and could not have predicted. With a basket. You were seen again this morning, before first light, on the water stairs with a skiff that belongs to a woman named Perrin, who will be questioned when we finish with the printers we took tonight." He paused. "You have had a very visible two days, Miss Thackeray, for someone carrying nothing."

The child shifted against her chest. Felix felt Tam's small hand curl into the fabric of the sling, and she thought, absurdly, of her father's bench, of the way he'd taught her to hold a graver — *let the tool do the work, Lixie, don't fight it, guide it* — and she guided her voice the same way. Flat. Even. A straight cut.

"If you're going to arrest me," she said, "do it. My arms are full and I'd rather not stand here being quoted at."

For a moment she thought he would laugh. What happened instead was worse and better: he looked at her the way Odile looked at a well-cut plate. Recognition of the hand.

"Your mother used to say something like that," he said quietly. "Not the words. The tone."

The quay seemed to tilt. Felix held still, the way you hold still when the ice under you makes its first sound.

"You knew my mother."

"I knew her." He drew off one glove, finger by finger, unhurried, and Felix understood that every second he spent drawing off that glove was a second the sergeant wasn't searching her basket. "Before your father. Before Vellamare decided what it was going to be. I was a clerk in the harbormaster's office and she sang in the choir at Saint Ferren's, and I was going to ask her father for permission, and then—" His mouth twisted. "And then a young type-cutter with ink on his knuckles walked into the chandlery where she bought her candles, and that was the end of it. She chose him inside a month. I've been choosing the losing side ever since."

"The regime isn't losing."

"No," Corvain agreed. "But I am."

He turned his head and looked out at the water, at the black hull riding the tide, and Felix watched him do arithmetic with his face — duty on one side of the ledger, memory on the other, and twenty years of interest accrued against him.

"There was a night in '41," he said. "Your father came to me. He'd cut something he shouldn't have — a plate, a map, work far above what the guild licensed him for — and the colonel's men were asking questions, and he came to me because I was the law and he thought the law might have one honest bone in it." Corvain's voice didn't change at all, which was how Felix knew it cost him. "I reported the inquiry closed. Unfounded. I put my seal on it. And your father walked out of my office and never spoke to me again, because he knew exactly what my silence had cost and exactly what it hadn't fixed, and he was right on both counts. I owed him a debt from that night. I have carried it through two promotions and one war and I have never once found a way to pay it."

"Inspector—"

"Your father is dead," Corvain said. "Your mentor is in a wagon headed for the Citadel. Your shop is ash. Whatever debt remains, I find myself—" he exhaled, and for one moment the grey uniform looked like a costume on him, "—unable to locate the creditor."

He picked up the sergeant's stick from the table, reached over, and lifted the corner of the cloth covering Felix's basket. Herring, brine, a false bottom he could not possibly have missed. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he set the stick down and pulled the cloth straight again.

"Tide turns in twenty minutes," he said. "The harbor patrol changes watch at the turn. There's a gap. It won't come again."

Felix's heart was doing something violent and stupid behind her ribs. "Why?"

"Because your mother once gave a cold clerk her scarf on the ferry and told him the city would be worth living in someday, and I have spent thirty years waiting to find out if she was right." He put his glove back on. "Also because I am tired, Miss Thackeray, and because when this ends — and it will end, sooner than they think — I would rather be remembered as the man who let one basket of herring pass than as another name on another list."

"And Odile Marchetti," Felix said. "The woman they took tonight. If I walk away and she hangs, I'll remember you too. Not kindly."

Something flickered behind his eyes — the first honest flinch she'd seen from him. "The prisoners go to the Citadel, not the gallows. Not yet. There's a process." He paused. "Processes can be slow. Slow processes lose paperwork. Paperwork, in my experience, burns remarkably easily when a building does." He glanced, deliberately, across the black water, at the glow where the Sign of the Atlas was collapsing into itself. "It seems Vellamare is full of fires lately. Anything might happen to a file."

Felix looked at him — this grey man, this enemy, this ghost from her mother's choir — and understood that mercy did not always arrive wearing the face you prayed for. Sometimes it arrived wearing the face you feared, and arrived late, and cost more than it should have, and you took it anyway because the tide would not wait and neither would the child sleeping against your heart.

"Thank you," she said. The words tasted strange. She'd earned them honestly for the first time in her life.

"Don't thank me." Corvain stepped back from the table and raised his voice into its official register, loud enough for the sergeant and the dark. "Nothing here. Fishwife with a basket. Move along." Then, lower, only for her, as she passed: "Tell whoever's waiting on that ship that the fortifications at Cape Merren changed garrison three weeks ago. The plate is already out of date by one battery. They'll want to know."

She stopped. Turned her head. "You knew what I was carrying."

"I've known since the audit, child. I've known since I saw your hands rest on your father's bench." He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, worn thin. "Go. Cut your letters. Build whatever it is she promised me the city would be."

* * *

The gap in the patrol was real. Felix walked the length of the customs pier with the dawn coming up behind the burning shop and the tide pulling at the pilings, and no one stopped her, and the not-being-stopped was the loudest thing she had ever heard.

A boat waited at the pier's end — a wherry, a boy at the oars, no questions. The *Kestrel* rose over them as they crossed, black and barnacled and beautiful, and a rope ladder came down, and Felix climbed it one-handed with a child strapped to her chest and a basket of rotting herring on her hip, and if the sailors watching thought anything of it, they had the good grace of their trade and said nothing.

On deck, amid the smell of salt and canvas, a man detached himself from the shadow of the mainmast. Grey-bearded, plain-coated, eyes like a man who had learned to count gunports at a glance.

"You're late," he said. "Tide's turning."

"I'm told the fortifications at Cape Merren changed garrison three weeks ago," Felix said. "One new battery. The plate's out of date."

The old man went very still. "Who told you that?"

"A man who loved my mother," Felix said, "and owed my father. He sends it as payment."

The agent looked at her for a long moment, then held out both hands, palms up, an altar waiting. "And the word?"

"Thackeray," Felix said.

She unbuttoned the false bottom of the basket with fingers that shook only now, now that it was done, now that it was safe to shake, and lifted out the copper plate — her father's lines, her father's coast, her father's secret cut into metal in a dead man's unmistakable hand. She laid it in the agent's palms. It weighed less than a loaf of bread. It weighed more than the whole shop going up in smoke across the water.

The agent turned it once in the growing light, and his breath caught — Felix saw it, the small involuntary catch of a man looking at the thing he'd crossed a war to find — and then he wrapped it in oilcloth with the tenderness of a priest handling relics.

"They'll sail on this," he said. "Everything changes now."

Behind him, the sun cleared the headland and struck the harbor full-on, and the smoke over Vellamare turned briefly gold, and Tam woke against her chest, blinking at the light, at the rigging, at the enormous moving world.

"Mama?" he said — the word still new, still half-accident, still the truest thing she owned.

"Yes, love," Felix said, holding him as the *Kestrel* took the tide. "We're going to build it."
