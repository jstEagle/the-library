## 10. First Letters

The punch was nearly done.

Felix held it to the window light of the new press house — the old customs house, requisitioned by the Republic and given, by decree of the Provisional Assembly, "to the printers of Vellamare, in perpetuity" — and turned it slowly the way her father had taught her to turn a cut: not looking at the letter itself but at the space around it, the counter, the white that made the black legible.

A capital F. The first letter of the first typeface of the free press.

She had been cutting it for eleven days. Odile would have said that was vanity. Odile was right; it was vanity, and Felix had decided she was entitled to exactly one act of vanity in the founding of a nation.

"Mama." Tam's voice came from the sorting bench, where he stood on a stool he was no longer allowed to need and refused to stop using. "This one's wrong."

He held up a sort between finger and thumb, the way she'd taught him, the way her father had taught her — never by the face, always by the sides, because the face is what prints and the body is what lives. He was six now, tall for it, with his mother's dark eyes and an expression he had learned entirely from Odile: skeptical, patient, faintly amused by the incompetence of adults.

"Which one?"

"The e. It's cracked." He squinted at it. "It'll fill with ink and print like a smudge and then someone will think the Republic can't spell."

Felix laughed before she could help it. "Where did you learn to talk like that?"

"You," he said. "And also Nana Odile. Mostly Nana Odile."

* * *

Odile had come back in March, three months after the Liberation, walking up from the harbor with one bag and no warning, thinner than Felix remembered, gray where she had once been iron-colored, and laughing before she'd even reached the door.

"Well," she'd said, standing in the wreckage of what had been the Sign of the Atlas, which was now a foundation and four scorched walls and Felix's new life half-built inside them. "They burned my shop. I intend to be insufferable about this for the rest of my days."

"Nana!" Tam had launched himself across the rubble and hit her at knee height, and she'd gone down laughing into the ash, and Felix had stood there with her hands over her mouth, unable to move, watching the two people she loved most in the world embrace in the ruins.

"They said—" Felix had managed. "The lists from Corvain's office said—"

"The lists said many things." Odile had gotten up, brushing herself off, and looked around at the ruin with the appraising eye she'd once turned on shipments of Dutch paper. "Most of them lies. Some of them true. I spent four years in a cell in the Citadel with a woman who could recite every pamphlet we ever printed from memory. She'd been arrested for owning three of them. She says hello, by the way. She wants a job."

And that had been that. That was how Odile did things. Grief, prison, liberation — processed, folded, put away, replaced immediately by work.

Now she came through the press house door with the morning, carrying bread and a newspaper — an actual newspaper, the *Vellamare Courier*, printed on the old Kestrel press they'd smuggled out piece by piece aboard fishing boats during the war years, hidden under herring and salt cod while Tam grew from a feverish toddler into a boy who could set type faster than men twice his age.

"Page one," Odile announced, slapping it down. "The Assembly votes Thursday on the press charter. And there's an editorial calling your father 'the martyr of the atlas.' They've got him wrong, of course. They make him sound solemn."

"He wasn't solemn?"

"He once engraved an obscene pun into the tail flourish of a capital Q and let it run through an entire almanac before anyone noticed." Odile set the bread down. "Solemn. Honestly."

* * *

Felix went back to the punch. The morning light moved across the bench — her father's bench, salvaged from the fire, its top scarred and scorched but true, trued again with her own planes until it cut flat as the day he'd bought it. Everything in this room had a history like that. The galley had come out of a drowned smuggler's lugger. The composing stone had been a doorstep in the Fish Quarter until the fishmongers' association donated it, ceremonially, with speeches. The Republic was built mostly of things that had survived something else.

That was the thought she kept returning to as she cut: that memory was not a thing you kept by hiding it. For five years she had believed otherwise. Through the war years — printing leaflets in cellars, teaching Tam his letters with movable type so that his alphabet smelled of ink — she had believed that remembering meant guarding, concealing, carrying the plate sewn into the lining, the name spoken only in whispers, the face deliberately forgotten because a woman at a door in the fog had asked it.

But look at the room. Every object here remembered out loud. The scorch marks on the bench were not hidden; they were pointed to, explained to visitors, explained to Tam. *Your grandfather's bench. It burned. We kept it anyway.*

"Forget my face," the woman had said. And Felix had forgotten her face — truly, helplessly, fog and fear had taken it — and it hadn't mattered at all. What mattered was the child, who was now sorting sorts beside her. What mattered was the plate, which had broken a blockade. What mattered was that Thackeray's hand, five years dead, was about to speak in public again, in metal, in the opening line of the first thing the free Vellamare would ever print.

She finished the counter. Held it up.

It was good. It was better than good — it was her father's letterforms with her own weight behind them, the way a daughter walks like her mother without ever deciding to. The serifs carried a little of his flare. The stress sat a little more upright than his, because she was a little more stubborn than him, or less, depending on whom you asked.

"What do you think?" she asked Tam.

He took it in both hands, careful, and studied it with the long seriousness he reserved for letters and nothing else.

"It looks like Grandfather's," he said. "But it looks like yours too." He frowned. "Is that allowed?"

"Yes," Felix said. Her throat was tight, which annoyed her; she made her voice steady anyway. "That's exactly what's allowed. That's the whole point of everything."

* * *

At noon they gathered — Odile, the exiled Citadel reciter whose name was Brann and who turned out to be a compositor of terrifying speed, the widow Perrot from the paper stall, two of the fishmongers who had walked her through the gate five years ago and still claimed credit for the entire revolution — to proof the first pull.

Felix had set the opening line the night before, alone, when the press house was empty and Tam asleep upstairs in the room that had once been the customs house's strongroom. She had set it in the new face, the face she was still cutting, the F and the R and the E done, the rest roughed but serviceable:

*Let it be known, in the city of Vellamare, that the press is free, and shall remain free, and that this freedom was paid for.*

Below it, smaller, in her father's recovered atlas face — the very sorts cut from the plate itself, cast and reworked, the map that had become a message become a monument:

*Cut by E. Thackeray, printer of this city. Finished by his daughter.*

Brann worked the press. The bed came around, the platen closed, and the sound it made — that deep wooden breath, the sound Felix had slept to as a child and nearly died for as a woman — filled the hall.

They pulled the sheet and hung it in the window facing the harbor, where the sun came through the paper and lit the words backward, ghost-white, readable from the street by anyone who cared to stand and read them reversed, which the children of the Fish Quarter promptly did, because children always do.

Tam stood at the window a long time, looking at it.

"Mama," he said finally. "There's going to be more, isn't there. More papers. Books. All of it."

"All of it," Felix said. "Every word anybody wants to say. Even the ones we hate."

"That last part sounds bad."

"That last part is the whole part." She crouched beside him. "That's why we cut the letters, love. So that everyone gets to say everything. Forever."

He considered this with six-year-old gravity, then nodded once, the way Odile nodded, the way verdicts are delivered, and went back to his sorting.

* * *

Odile found her at dusk at the bench, cleaning the gravers. The old woman sat down across from her without being asked, which was her way of saying a great deal.

"Corvain came by the Assembly today," she said.

Felix's hands stilled. "Did he."

"He's leaving. Taking a ship east. Says there's nothing left for him to inspect." Odile paused. "He left something for you at the clerk's desk. A package."

Inside the brown paper, wrapped in oilcloth against the sea air, was a small brass-bound case. Inside the case: a graver. Old, worn thin at the handle, its edge lovingly maintained. Felix knew it instantly — knew the balance of it in her hand before her mind caught up — her father's finest tool, the one missing from his kit after the fire, the one she had mourned along with everything else.

There was a note in a precise inspector's hand:

*I took this from the evidence room five years ago and told them it was destroyed. I owed him. Now I owe no one. Tell the boy his grandfather's steel cuts true. — C.*

Felix sat with it a long time. Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, and somewhere down the quay a crew was singing, off-key and unashamed, and the tide was coming in the way tides do, without permission asked or needed.

Odile watched her. "Well?" she said. "Are you going to cry, or are you going to cut something?"

Felix wiped her eyes once, briskly, and fit the graver to her palm. It settled home like a word into a line.

"Both," she said. "In that order."

She pulled a fresh blank toward her, and began.
