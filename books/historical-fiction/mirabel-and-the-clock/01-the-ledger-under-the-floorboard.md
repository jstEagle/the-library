## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

The fever ward smelled of carbolic and coal smoke and, beneath it all, the sweet-rot scent that Noor Montrose had learned never to name aloud. She moved down the row of iron cots with her lamp held high, checking the men whose breathing had gone shallow as the year itself.

December had come down hard on the garrison town. Frost ferned the tall windows of the hospital each morning, and the orderlies scraped it away with wooden paddles so the surgeons could see their work by daylight. The wind came off the river carrying the smell of the tanneries, and it found every gap in the old brickwork, whistling through the wards like something searching for a way in.

"Water," said the man in the third cot.

She gave him water, tipping the tin cup carefully against his cracked lips. Private Ellis, twenty-three years old, three days into typhoid, his mother's address written in his file in a clerk's careful hand. Noor had read that address so many times she knew it the way she knew her own—Rosemary Lane, Ashcombe, the county of Dorset. She had never been to Dorset. She suspected she would go there one day, if she lived long enough, to tell someone how their son had died. That was a debt she kept a private account of, along with all the others.

"You'll want to sleep now," she told him, though he was already sinking back.

At the far end of the ward, near the stove, Sergeant Bramley was awake and watching her with the fever-bright patience of a man who had decided not to die yet but was keeping his options open. He'd been at her elbow for two days asking small questions—how long she'd been nursing, whether the hospital was short of hands, whether she'd heard anything about transfers. Men asked such questions when they were afraid. She answered them gently and did not tell them what she knew, which was that the garrison's returns for November listed forty-one men admitted to this ward when she herself had counted fifty-eight beds filled.

Seventeen men, unrecorded. Seventeen names that existed nowhere but in her memory and in a notebook sewn into the lining of her skirt.

* * *

The notebook had begun two years ago, on a night she could still reconstruct in perfect detail, the way one remembers the exact weather of a funeral.

Her brother Thomas had been a sapper with the garrison company, a quiet man who mended bridges and wrote letters full of small jokes about the food. In the autumn of 1897 there had been an action upriver—a skirmish, the official dispatch called it, brief and regrettable, four wounded. Noor learned otherwise from a dying corporal who had been brought in with a wound in his belly and a horror in his face he could not swallow down. The corporal talked because the dying talk, and because Noor listened the way no one else in that ward ever seemed to: without flinching, without hurrying him toward comfort he didn't believe in.

There had been no skirmish. There had been a column caught in open ground, and orders given from behind it, and men shot at from directions their own artillery should have covered. And afterward there had been paperwork—clean, efficient, damning in its tidiness. The dead were entered as deserters. Deserters were struck from the rolls. Struck from the rolls, they became men who had never served, whose mothers received no pension and no letter, whose names appeared on no memorial. The dispatch reported four wounded because four wounds required treating, and the hospital had treated exactly four.

Thomas's name was not among them.

She had searched the ledgers for three nights, by lamp, while the ward slept. She found the admission entries for eleven men who had died of wounds in those weeks—entries in her own hand, some of them—and then she had gone to the casualty returns posted in the adjutant's office and found none of them there. Eleven men, erased between the bed and the book. Thomas was the twelfth; he had died before reaching the hospital, and so he was erased earlier, more thoroughly, at the source.

The officer who signed the corrected returns was Colonel Ashworth.

She remembered standing in that office with the ledger open under her hand, feeling nothing so much as cold—the cold of a well, of water that has stopped moving. Grief she had expected. This was different. This was the understanding that the world did not merely contain cruelty; it contained machinery for the disposal of evidence, and the machinery ran smoothly, and men were promoted for operating it.

That night she took a schoolboy's notebook from the hospital stores—there was always a surplus of stationery; requisitions were another thing the colonel understood—and began to write.

*Ellis, J., private. Died of fever, 14 December, 3 o'clock in the morning. His mother lives at Rosemary Lane, Ashcombe.*

Not the whole truth, not yet. She had learned caution the way soldiers learn drill, one repetition at a time. At first she recorded only what could not be disputed: names, dates, hours of death, the words of dying men taken down verbatim where they concerned matters beyond the ward. Later, as her nerve grew and her method refined itself, she added more—the discrepancies between admissions and returns, copied out in figures that would bear any audit; the dates of Ashworth's inspections, which coincided so neatly with corrections to the books; once, folded between pages, the original admission slip of a man whose return-entry claimed he had never been admitted at all.

She hid the notebook beneath a floorboard beside her cot in the nurses' quarters, in the gap where the joist met the wall, under a loose board she had loosened further herself with a chisel borrowed and returned. The floor was old pine, scarred by decades of boots. No one looked twice at it. No one looked twice at Noor Montrose either—that was the point of her, or so she had made herself. A competent nurse, neither pretty nor plain enough to be remarked upon, punctual, uncomplaining, with a face that gave away nothing because she had spent two years teaching it to give away nothing.

It was astonishing, she thought sometimes, how much rebellion a person could fit inside a life that looked like obedience.

* * *

On the twentieth of December, the returns came again.

She saw them carried through the corridor in the adjutant's leather folder, and she saw the adjutant himself stop at the ward door and count—not the men, she noted, but the beds marked with the black-edged cards that meant deceased. His lips moved faintly. He was doing arithmetic for someone above him.

That evening, three men died. She sat with each of them in turn, and each time, after, she washed her hands and waited until the ward was settled and the night orderly had gone down to the kitchens, and then she stood at the little desk in the duty room and wrote in her fair, round hand:

*Bramley, Sgt. R., died 20 Dec., 11:40 p.m., of his wounds. Asked after his regiment. Said: "Tell them I stayed."*

And below it, in smaller letters, the figure that mattered: *Ward strength at midnight, 54. Returns will show 39.*

She blotted the page, folded the notebook closed, and felt the familiar sensation settle over her—not satisfaction, which implied hope, but something sterner. The sensation of a debt being entered correctly.

Bramley had been one of her questioners, and in his last hour he had gripped her wrist with a strength that surprised them both and said, "Sister, they'll write me off. They did my brother same way." She had promised him nothing. She had only held his hand, and he had looked at her with eyes going dim and distant, and whatever he saw in her face had been enough, because he closed his eyes then and let go as a man lets go who believes his affairs are in order.

His affairs were in order. They were under the floorboard.

* * *

Christmas came and went almost unnoticed in the ward. Someone strung paper chains above the stove; the matron produced a ham and a case of oranges from sources she declined to explain; the men who could eat ate, and the men who couldn't pretended to, for form's sake. On Boxing Day the frost broke briefly and it rained, a thin grey rain that turned the yard to slop, and through the ward windows Noor watched the sentries at the gate huddle into their greatcoats and thought: *another winter, and the book grows longer.*

By the twenty-ninth the notebook held nineteen new names for the month. She had begun to wonder, in her coldest and most practical moments, how large a thing needed to grow before it stopped being a record and became a weapon. A notebook proved nothing to anyone who did not wish to be proven against. But a notebook plus a witness, plus an occasion—she did not finish the thought. Occasions were not things one arranged. They were things one survived long enough to meet.

On the thirtieth, the snow came again, heavy and windless, falling straight down past the lamplight like ash settling over the town. The ward was quiet. Even the worst of the fever cases had sunk into the exhausted sleep that meant the body was fighting rather than fleeing. Noor made her rounds, trimmed the lamps, and stood a moment at the window watching the snow bury the yard, the gun-carriages, the flagpole with its furled standard.

It was nearly ten when the knocking began.

Not at the ward door—at the main entrance, far down the corridor, a sound too soft and too irregular to be military. The night orderly had gone for coal. Noor untied her apron, took the lamp from its hook, and went to answer it, her footsteps loud in the empty hall, the snow-silence pressing at the glass doors on either side of her.

When she pulled the bolt and opened the great door, the cold hit her like a wall, and out of it—half-buried already in the drifts gathering on the step—stood a child.

A girl, seven years old perhaps, in a coat too thin for the season, her lips blue, her eyes enormous in a small white face. Snow had piled on her shoulders and in her hair. She said nothing. Behind her, footprints ran away across the yard toward the lane, already filling—one set, large, a man's stride, ending at the gate.

In her fist, clutched so tightly her knuckles had gone bloodless, she held a pocket watch on a broken chain.

Noor knelt, ignoring the wet soaking through her skirt, and lifted the child over the threshold. The girl weighed almost nothing. As Noor gathered her up, the watch swung free on its chain and struck the doorframe with a small, hard sound, and the child flinched and pressed it to her chest with both hands, and over her shoulder Noor saw the note pinned inside the coat collar, stitched there with rough black thread, the needle-marks crooked, the work of a man's hand done in haste:

*Forget the face.*
