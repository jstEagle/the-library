## 2. A Child at the Door

The frost came early that night, silvering the windowpanes of Ward C until the lamplight fractured into cold constellations. Noor Montrose moved down the rows of iron cots with a basin of ice water, pressing cloths to fevered foreheads, and the whole time she counted. It was what she did now, counting—the men who breathed, the men who would not see morning, and beneath both tallies, the quieter arithmetic that no one else in the garrison performed: the names that had been, and the names that officially were.

Private Hollis, third cot from the stove, was dreaming of home. She could tell by the way his lips moved—*Maggie, Maggie*—and by the slack peace in his face that the fever had not yet managed to burn away. She wrung the cloth and laid it across his brow.

"Drink," she murmured, lifting his head. "There's a good soldier."

He drank. Somewhere behind her, a man coughed the wet, tearing cough that meant the winter would keep him longer than the war had. The stove ticked. Wind worried at the shutters like a beggar at a kitchen door.

Noor had learned, in three years of this work, that the night hours belonged to no one—not to the surgeons, not to the orderlies, not even to Colonel Ashworth's clerks with their ledgers and their stamps. The night belonged to whoever stayed awake to claim it. So she claimed it. In the pocket of her apron rode a small book bound in grey cloth, its pages filled in her own careful hand: names, ranks, regiments, dates of death, and beside each one, in smaller script, the word *struck* or *omitted*, as though she were a clerk in some invisible ministry that answered to God alone.

Her brother's name was on the fourth page. Thomas Elias Montrose, Corporal, 2nd Battalion. Killed in action, they told her afterward—or rather, they told her nothing at all, because there was nothing official to tell. He had simply ceased to appear. Her mother wrote asking when Thomas might come home on leave, and Noor had sat with the letter unopened for two days before she could find words that were neither lie nor truth, because she possessed neither.

She finished her rounds at half past eleven and stood a moment at the ward door, looking out across the yard. Snow had begun again, fat flakes falling through the yellow cone of the lamp above the entrance, and beyond the gate the town lay hushed under it, chimneys smoking straight up into still air. A beautiful night, if you had no business out in it. Nobody sensible had business out in it.

Which was why, when she saw movement by the steps—a shape too small to be a drunkard, too upright to be an animal—her heart performed a strange double beat, dread arriving a full second before curiosity.

She took the lamp from its bracket and went out.

* * *

The child stood at the bottom of the hospital steps as though she had been set there, which in fact she had; the snow around her small boots showed the deep prints of a grown man leading up to the spot and none leading away but his own, long strides already filling with white. He had walked away quickly. He had not looked back. You could read all of it in the snow if you knew how, and Noor knew how.

The girl was perhaps seven, wrapped in a coat too large for her, brown wool gone grey at the seams, a scarf wound round her neck twice and knotted with the desperate thoroughness of someone who loved her. Her chin was tipped up, watching Noor come, and she did not cry. That was the first thing Noor noticed—that she did not cry—and it frightened her more than weeping would have.

"You're frozen through," Noor said, crouching to the child's level so the lamplight fell kind instead of looming. "Where is your mother, little one?"

The girl said nothing. Her eyes went past Noor's shoulder to the lit windows of the ward, then back, measuring. Children who measured like that had been taught to by hard lessons.

"Can you speak?"

"Yes," said the girl. Her voice was small and clear as a bell rung once.

"What's your name?"

A pause. Then, with the gravity of a child reciting something drilled into her: "I'm not to say. I'm to give you the note."

"Whose note?"

"He said you'd come out. He said the lady with the lamp always comes out." The girl fumbled at her collar and produced a folded paper, damp at the corners, held out in a mittened fist. "He made me learn it. He said if anyone but the lady asks me questions, I'm to say I'm lost and start crying, and they'll send me to the police, and the police will send me to the parish, and I mustn't go to the parish." She recited it flatly, a catechism. "But he said the lady with the lamp would know what to do."

Noor took the note. The handwriting, when she unfolded it beneath the lamp, stopped her breath—not because it was unfamiliar, but because it was not.

She had seen that hand before. Slanted leftward, the capitals over-ornamented, the *f* crossed with an impatient double stroke. She had seen it in a dispatch copied out for the ward records eight months ago. She had seen it in a signature she herself had written into the grey book, copying it exactly, so that the record would be true even if the ledger was not.

Lieutenant Ambrose Kettering. Eleventh of June. *Struck.*

Dead men did not write notes. And yet here was his hand, alive on the page, urgent, pressed hard enough into the paper to nearly tear it:

*Nurse—I am told my name will not survive the month. If you are reading this then neither may I. What I carry is worth more than my life and less safe than my grave. The child is Mirabel. Forget her face. Forget you saw me. Sew it into her coat and let the watch do the remembering. Burn this. Trust no clerk. —A.K.*

"Forget her face," Noor read aloud without meaning to, and the girl—Mirabel—watched her with those measuring eyes.

"He sewed it in while I slept," Mirabel said. She turned obediently, presenting the back of her coat like a child showing off a new dress, and pulled up the hem where the lining sagged. There, stitched flat against the wool between the layers, was a shape the size of two hands cupped together. Noor touched it through the fabric: metal, round, dense, cold even through the wool. A watch case, or something wearing a watch case's clothes.

"Come inside," Noor said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "You'll take your death out here."

"I'm not to go to the parish," Mirabel said, but she took Noor's hand anyway, her fingers little bars of ice inside the mitten.

* * *

The kitchen was empty at midnight except for one orderly asleep in the corner chair, and he could sleep through bombardment. Noor sat Mirabel on a stool by the banked range, wrapped her in a blanket that smelled of starch and carbolic, and put warm milk in front of her. The child drank it in small, disciplined sips, watching Noor over the rim of the cup the entire time.

The coat came apart easily once Noor found the beginning of the stitches. Whoever had done the sewing had done it in haste and in darkness, but with a soldier's neatness—tight running stitches, thread doubled, knots buried. Inside the slit lay a pocket watch, heavy, silver-cased, old-fashioned, the kind with a hinged inner cover. Engraved on the outer case, worn nearly smooth: *To A.K., from his father, 1881.*

Noor turned it over twice before she found it. The hinge of the inner cover had a twin—a second hinge, hairline-fine, along the case's lower edge, disguised by engraving so cleverly worked she'd have sworn it was solid metal. She pressed with her thumbnail. The false back sighed open.

Papers. Folded papers, thin as onion skin, packed tight as seeds in a pod. Three of them, four, their edges furred from years—no, months—of being carried against a living body, then weeks against a sleeping child's.

By the stove's dying glow Noor opened the first.

It was in Kettering's hand again. Not a note this time but a report—no, a copy of a report, dated the eleventh of May, marked *Confidential*, and it did not add up. She read it three times. Casualties returned: seven wounded, none killed. But she had been at the receiving end of that same engagement's aftermath; she remembered the wagons, she remembered the smell, she remembered writing names in the grey book that never appeared anywhere else. Seven wounded. She had counted more than thirty dead with her own hands, and among them—

Among them, in this very hand that now trembled in her fingers, the name she had copied on the fourth page of her own secret ledger. Not her brother's. Another. One of the struck.

The second paper was worse. It bore a signature she knew from proclamations nailed to the garrison notice board, from inspection reports, from the brass plaque outside headquarters: *J. Ashworth, Col.* It ordered, in language of immaculate bureaucratic courtesy, that the returns for the engagement be "reconciled to figures consistent with the honour of the service." Reconciled. As though the dead were accounts that had drifted out of balance.

The third paper Noor did not finish reading. Her eyes kept sliding off it, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. She refolded everything along its existing creases, slid it back into the watch's hollow, and closed the false cover, and for a moment she simply sat with the thing in her lap, feeling its weight as though it were not silver at all but lead, or iron, or something with a pulse.

Mirabel had fallen asleep sitting up, chin sunk into the blanket, milk cup tilted precariously in her loose grip. In sleep her face had lost its measuring look and become simply a child's face, chapped lips parted, lashes furred with melting snow still drying in the warmth. Forget her face, the note said. Too late. Faces were the one thing Noor had never managed to forget; it was why she kept the grey book at all. Every name in it had a face attached, and every face had someone somewhere waiting at a window.

She looked at the sleeping child and understood, with a cold clarity worse than fear, that the man who had left her on the steps had chosen Noor deliberately. Not randomly. The lady with the lamp—he had watched the hospital. He had known, somehow, that the nurse who lingered after rounds, who wrote letters for illiterate privates, who asked too gently and too often about the missing, would come out into the snow. Which meant Lieutenant Ambrose Kettering had been alive within these last days. Alive, and expecting not to remain so, and he had spent his final currency on a seven-year-old girl and a silver watch.

And it meant that whoever had erased his name would very soon discover that something else had escaped the erasure.

Outside, the wind rose and drove snow against the kitchen window in soft, persistent handfuls, like someone asking to be let in. Noor rose, blew out the candle, and stood a moment in the dark listening—to the orderly's snoring, to the stove's settling, to the child's slow breathing—and beneath all of it, faint and far off, the sound of hooves on the frozen road by the river. More than one horse. Moving fast toward the garrison, though the hour argued against any honest errand.

She tucked the watch into the bodice of her uniform, against her heart, where its cold case slowly warmed. Then she woke the orderly just enough to fetch blankets, and carried Mirabel, blanket and all, to the disused linen room off Ward C, where the shelves stood empty until Thursday and the door locked from the inside.

Tomorrow, she thought, laying the girl down. Tomorrow she would think clearly. Tomorrow she would decide.

But even as she spoke the words to herself, she knew the decision had already been made—in the snow, at the steps, the moment a dead man's handwriting had called her by her profession and trusted her with the only things he had left. She locked the linen room door from within, sat down on a folded sheet with the grey book open on her knees, and by the light of a match began to write: *Mirabel. Arrived 29th December, eleven o'clock at night. Brought nothing but a watch full of the truth.*

Below the window, the hoofbeats faded toward headquarters, and did not come back.
