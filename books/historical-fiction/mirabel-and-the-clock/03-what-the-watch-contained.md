## 3. What the Watch Contained

The hospital settled into its night sounds around them: the groan of fevered men, the hiss of the boiler, the wind worrying at the shutters like a beggar at a door. Noor sat in the dispensary with the watch open before her on a tray of instruments, and she did not touch it for a long moment, the way one does not touch a wound until one has decided to hurt.

Tobias Fenwick stood at the far side of the room with his coat still dusted in snow, holding his hat against his chest as though he were at a funeral. He had come when she sent for him — a boyish clerk of perhaps five-and-twenty, ink-stained fingers, a face too honest for the work it performed. She had chosen him precisely because he was honest; she would remember that choice later, and what it cost.

"You said it was urgent," he said. "You said it couldn't wait for morning."

"It can't." She turned the watch toward him. The case caught the lamplight, dull gold gone green at the seams. "Tell me what you see."

"A pocket watch. Fine make. Not army issue."

"Open the back. There's a catch under the hinge — press where the metal thins."

He hesitated, glanced at her once, and did it. The inner lid sprang free with a click that sounded obscenely loud in the quiet ward beyond. Beneath it, where a portrait or an inscription might live, lay a second compartment no deeper than two fingernails, and folded within it papers so thin they seemed cut from moth wings.

Tobias went very still.

"That's not possible," he said softly. "That mechanism — I've catalogued hundreds of watches for the quartermaster. None have—"

"This one belonged to a man who expected to be searched," Noor said. "Take them out. Carefully. They've been waiting a long time."

* * *

The documents were four in number, written in a small, disciplined hand, the letters upright as soldiers on parade. The ink had faded to the brown of dried blood. Tobias spread them across the dispensary table, weighted the corners with brass weights, and bent over them with his lamp pulled low, and for a long while neither of them spoke.

Noor knew the hand before she read a single word. She had seen it once before, in her own secret ledger, copied from a casualty roll she was never meant to see: Lieutenant Edmund Vale, struck from the rolls of the 3rd Battalion in October, listed as *deserted*, though every man in his company who survived Kessel Ridge knew better.

"Read it aloud," she said. "I want to hear it in a voice that isn't only mine."

Tobias cleared his throat. His voice cracked on the first line and steadied itself.

"'Memorandum for my own keeping, being the true account of what occurred at Kessel Ridge on the ninth of October last. Colonel Ashworth ordered the village of Saint-Aubin shelled after its surrender was accepted, on the grounds that resistance there justified the action. There was no resistance. I counted forty-one bodies in the church alone. Among them were women and children whose presence Ashworth had been informed of by the mayor himself, under flag of truce, two hours prior.'"

The boiler hissed. Somewhere down the ward a man called out for his mother, and a younger voice soothed him.

"The second page," Tobias said. His hands were not steady now. "It's a tally. Casualties returned as — as dysentery and typhoid, thirty-eight names, but here—" he tapped a column, "—here are the same thirty-eight marked *killed in action, Saint-Aubin*, with dates of death that fall before the sicknesses they supposedly died of. He moved the dead, Miss Montrose. Filed them under disease so the ridge wouldn't appear on any report."

"And the third?"

"Ashworth's own order. Signed. 'No survivors of the engagement are to appear in correspondence. The matter is closed.'" Tobias looked up, and in the lamplight his young face had aged ten years. "This is not carelessness. This is murder with a pen."

* * *

They worked until nearly three o'clock, piecing the fourth document together — a letter half-finished, addressed to no one, in which Vale wrote that he had entrusted the watch and its contents to a friend among the townspeople, and that if it ever reached London it must reach the War Office inspection, not the garrison command, because the garrison command *was* the crime. The letter broke off mid-sentence. Below it, in a different, shakier hand, someone had written a single line:

*They came for him at dawn. The child knows nothing. Forget the face.*

Noor read it four times. *The child knows nothing.* Except that the child knew something, or carried something, which amounted to the same thing in a town governed by Colonel Ashworth.

"She's seven," Noor said quietly. "Whoever left her at our door sewed a dead man's testimony into her coat and told God she knew nothing. And then told us to forget her face."

"Forgetting is the safest thing," Tobias said. It was not a threat; it was fear speaking, plain as hunger. "Miss Montrose — Noor. If this is what it appears to be, then Ashworth has spent three months ensuring it doesn't exist. Men have been reassigned for less. Men have *died* for less. We should burn it."

She looked at the papers spread under the weights, at the upright little letters marching across them, and she thought of her brother Thomas, whose name existed nowhere now but in her own hidden ledger — not on a stone, not on a roll, not in any mother's prayer book but hers. Thomas had died at Kessel Ridge. She had known it in her bones for months before she found the proof of it, and the proof, when it came, was a single struck-through line in a clerk's tidy column.

Thirty-eight names filed under dysentery. Forty-one bodies in a church.

"Burn it," she repeated. "Yes. That is exactly what a sensible woman would do."

"And you're not going to be sensible."

"I'm going to be accurate," Noor said. "There's a difference, Tobias, and it happens to be the whole difference between Ashworth and everyone he's destroyed."

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin, and with it came the sound Noor had learned to dread above all others in the garrison: boots in the corridor, more boots than routine required.

Sergeant Hale appeared in the dispensary doorway with two military policemen behind him, snow melting off their capes, their breath steaming. He was not a cruel man, Hale; he was an obedient one, which in this town amounted to the same thing.

"Colonel's compliments," he said. "There's been an irregularity in the October returns. A discrepancy in the ledgers. Colonel Ashworth wants every record in this hospital accounted for by noon, and he wants word of anyone — anyone at all — brought to the gates recently. Civilians. Children. Anyone."

"Children?" Noor kept her voice level, bored even. "What child would come here?"

"Deserters' brats turn up on church steps often enough," Hale said. "You'd know if one had. You'd have reported it."

"I report everything, Sergeant. It's my one virtue."

Hale studied her a moment longer than comfort allowed, then touched his cap and went. The MPs' boots receded down the corridor, checking wards, pulling drawers, opening cabinets that held nothing but laudanum and lint.

When they were gone, Tobias let out a breath that shook. "He knows. Somehow he already knows something is missing."

"He doesn't know what," Noor said. "If he knew what, we'd both be under arrest, and the child would be gone. He knows a shape. A hole in his tidy records." She folded the documents back along their old creases, slid them into the watch's hidden belly, and pressed the lid closed until it clicked. "Which means we have until he learns the difference between a hole and a name."

"What will you do?"

She looked out the frost-ferned window at the parade ground, where the morning bugle was calling men to muster, and beyond it at the colonel's quarters, chimneys smoking, flags snapping in the cold.

"First, Mirabel eats a proper breakfast and stops flinching when doors open. Then, Tobias Fenwick, you and I are going to study a map of this town, because somewhere between this hospital and that inspector arriving from London stand three checkpoints — and before the century turns, everything in this watch is going to cross all three."

Tobias stared at her. "You mean to go through them."

"I mean to go *past* them," Noor said, and slipped the watch into her apron pocket, over her heart, where it ticked faintly against her ribs like a second conscience.
