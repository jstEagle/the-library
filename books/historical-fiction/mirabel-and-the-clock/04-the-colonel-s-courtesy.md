## 4. The Colonel's Courtesy

Colonel Edmund Ashworth had always believed that a man could be read the way a ledger was read: by what was written, and more importantly, by what had been struck through. He sat in the hospital's cramped administrative room with his gloved hands folded on his knees, waiting for Nurse Montrose to bring the tea, and he permitted himself the small pleasure of surveying the room as though it were a field of battle already won.

The room smelled of carbolic and damp plaster. Frost ferned the corners of the window, and beyond the glass the parade ground lay white and silent under a sky the colour of unpolished pewter. Somewhere below, in the fever ward, men coughed in the ragged chorus that had become the town's winter music. Ashworth found the sound not unpleasant. It was the sound of his career being managed.

He had come, officially, to inspect. The War Office liked its colonels to be seen among the sick before the century turned; there would be photographs, perhaps, and a dispatch with phrases like *devoted attention* and *unflagging vigilance*. Unofficially, he had come because a clerk named Fenwick had gone pale at his desk three days ago, because a dead lieutenant's effects had been inventoried incomplete, and because somewhere in this building of lye and lamplight there existed a document that could unmake him.

He did not think of it as fear. He thought of it as tidying.

The door opened, and Nurse Montrose entered with a tray held level, her steps unhurried, her face composed into the particular blankness that good nurses wore like armour. She was younger than he'd expected from her reputation—thirty perhaps, or nearly—with dark hair pinned severely and eyes the colour of strong tea left to cool. She set the tray down without a tremor, poured for him first, and stood with her hands folded, waiting to be dismissed or used.

"Nurse Montrose," he said warmly. "Sit, please. I dislike conducting interviews across a gulf."

"I am on duty, Colonel."

"And I am asking you to sit. Duty bends to courtesy, in my experience. Courtesy is the oil that keeps an army from seizing." He smiled, and watched her weigh it, and watched her sit—straight-backed, hands in lap, a woman arranging herself rather than submitting. Good. He preferred opponents who believed themselves composed.

"You have served here how long?"

"Four years, sir."

"Four years." He stirred his tea though it needed no stirring, the spoon clicking against porcelain in the quiet. "Through the autumn campaign. Through the worst of the wounded coming down from the pass. You must have seen a great deal."

"I see what is brought to me, sir."

"A diplomatic answer." He laughed softly, inviting her to share the joke. She did not. "Let me be plainer, then. I am told you keep excellent records. Better than excellent. There are those in this garrison who say your ward book is the only honest document in the building."

He let that hang in the air between them, delicate as wire. Her expression did not change, but something behind her eyes went very still—the stillness of a creature hearing a trap close one tooth at a time.

"Honesty is a nurse's duty," she said.

"It is indeed. And yet honesty is such an inconvenient virtue in wartime. Men die with their papers unfinished. Reports arrive garbled. A name is misspelled, and so a widow writes to the wrong parish, and the wrong parish writes to me, and I must spend an afternoon of my life composing condolences for a woman whose husband never existed in the first place." He sipped. "You will forgive me if I have developed a certain... flexibility regarding names."

"Names matter to the dying, Colonel. They are often the last thing they say."

"What a curious thing to say." He set down his cup and studied her openly now, the way he might study a map of ground he intended to take. "Tell me about the night of the fourteenth of December. The cold snap. I understand a child was brought to your door."

"Yes, sir. An abandoned girl. She was half-frozen. We took her in."

"No note? No token? Nothing to say who left her?"

"Nothing, sir."

"Strange." He rose and walked to the window, presenting her with his back—a courtesy of sorts, or a test of it. In the glass's dull reflection he could see her seated figure, motionless, watchful. "A child does not walk through a garrison town at midnight alone. Someone carried her. Someone chose this door out of every door. And someone, I think, gave her something to carry too." He turned. "You would tell me, wouldn't you, if she had? As a matter of loyalty to the Crown?"

"I would tell you anything that concerned the safety of this hospital, sir."

"Ah." He smiled again, wider this time, and gestured toward the door. "Then you won't mind if my men look around while we talk. Captain Reyes has a talent for finding things that have been misfiled."

* * *

They searched the wards while he poured her a second cup.

Ashworth heard them moving through the long room below—the scrape of a cot dragged from a wall, the muffled complaint of a feverish private told to hold his tongue, drawers opening and closing with the rhythmical patience of men who were paid by thoroughness. He kept his voice low and pleasant throughout, a host making conversation while burglars worked his own house, which was, in a sense, precisely what was happening.

"The thing about power, Nurse Montrose," he said, resuming his chair, "is that everyone imagines it to be loud. Drums, banners, orders shouted across a square. But real power is quiet. It is knowing which drawer to open. It is a word spoken at the right desk in London. It is a man's whole future turning on whether I write one sentence or omit it." He leaned forward. "I could make your career this afternoon. Matron of the new convalescent wing—they're building it in the spring. Your own quarters. A salary that would let you buy your mother a house with a garden instead of whatever narrow rooms she keeps now."

Something flickered across her face at the word *mother*, quickly mastered, and he noted it with satisfaction. Everyone had a mother. Everyone had a lever.

"I am content where I am, Colonel."

"Content." He tasted the word with distaste. "Contentment is what the powerless call their cages. You are better than this place. You know it. Four years of other people's blood and bedpans, and for what? A commendation no one reads?" He spread his hands, magnanimous. "Loyalty, Nurse. That is all I ask. Loyalty freely given, and rewarded as it deserves. Tell me about the child's coat, and by February you will have keys to a wing of your own."

Below them, boots crossed the ward floor. A drawer slammed. Then footsteps on the stairs, and Captain Reyes appeared in the doorway, shaking snowmelt from his shoulders, and shook his head—a small, precise movement that meant *nothing found*.

Ashworth felt disappointment settle in him like cold lead, but he did not let it reach his face. Instead he laughed, warm and easy, and stood, and buttoned his coat as though the visit had been a pleasure entire.

"Well. No matter. A misunderstanding, I'm sure, and soon resolved." He took her hand—it was dry and steady as a rail—and pressed it briefly between his gloves. "You have remarkable composure, Nurse Montrose. I mean that as the highest compliment. Composure is rarer than courage, and worth more." At the door he paused, and looked back at her with genuine, almost paternal kindness. "One last thing. If a pocket watch should find its way to you—if some sentimental soul should leave it in your keeping—you would bring it to me at once. Not because I distrust you. Because things that belonged to dead men have a way of hurting the living, and I should hate to see you hurt."

"I understand, Colonel," said Noor Montrose.

And she lied.

She lied the way she poured tea—smoothly, evenly, without a ripple—and Ashworth saw it happen and could not prove it, and knew that she knew that he could not. For one strange moment he admired her, the way a chess player admires the opponent who has just seen ten moves past his blunder. Then admiration curdled into resolve. She had the watch. Or she knew where it slept. And she had looked at his offer of a wing, a salary, a garden, and priced it exactly at nothing.

In the corridor he stopped beside Captain Reyes and spoke without turning his head.

"She is the one. Watch her—not obviously; she'll smell obviousness. And the child. When the inspector arrives, I want that girl where I can see her, and the nurse where I can predict her." He pulled on his gloves, finger by finger. "New Year's Eve, Captain. By midnight I intend to have that watch back, this hospital quiet, and the century proclaimed over a clean ledger. See that it happens."

Reyes saluted. Down in the fever ward, the coughing chorus rose and fell, patient as tide, and Ashworth descended into it thinking of gardens, and ledgers, and the single loose thread in a grey coat that he would cut before it unravelled him.

Behind him, in the administrative room, a cup of untouched tea was going cold on the table—poured by his own hand, offered and refused, the first thing in a long time that had declined to be bought.
