## 5. Three Checkpoints

The frost came in through the ward windows like a visitor who would not be told to leave. It silvered the sills, feathered the lower panes, and turned each breath of the soldiers into small ghosts that hung above their blankets before dissolving. Noor Montrose moved down the row with a basin of ice-water, chipping it into rags for fevered brows, and all the while—beneath the work, beneath the steady rhythm of her hands—the map was drawing itself.

She had not chosen to think of escape. Escape had begun thinking of her.

It had started that morning, folding bandages, when she caught herself staring at the hospital gate through the dispensary glass: the wrought-iron arch, the sentry box, the corporal who stamped his boots against the cold at half-hour intervals like a clock with feet. She knew his face now. She knew the hour he took his tea from a flask, the way he read a folded newspaper propped against the wall, the angle at which he let the gate swing wide for the laundry cart and did not trouble himself to look inside it. These were not observations she had decided to make. They were simply there, accumulating, the way water accumulates behind a dam—quietly, patiently, under enormous pressure.

Three checkpoints stood between her and the truth. Three gates between Mirabel and whatever future a child could have in a town where Colonel Ashworth's men were already asking questions in shops and doorways about a girl in a grey coat.

*The hospital gate,* she thought, pressing a cold rag to Private Ellison's brow while he muttered at figures only he could see. *Then the river bridge. Then the railway barrier.*

She said nothing aloud. In the garrison, even one's own thoughts felt as though they ought to be whispered.

* * *

That evening, after the lamps were trimmed and the ward settled into its coughing chorus, Noor spread a map on the administrative table—not the official survey, which was locked in the adjutant's office, but a railway timetable with a strip map printed inside the cover, cheap and incomplete and therefore perfect, because no one would ever suspect a timetable of being anything but what it seemed.

She weighted the corners with an inkwell, a candlestick, a spoon, and studied it the way she had once studied anatomy plates: memorizing, committing, making the knowledge part of her body.

The hospital gate first. That was the easy one—or rather, the familiar one, which was a different thing entirely. The laundry cart left every second morning at seven, driven by Mrs. Okafor, a woman of vast shoulders and impenetrable silences, whose loyalty to the hospital ran deeper than any oath to the Crown but whose discretion could not be purchased, only respected. The sentry rarely searched the carts. *Rarely.* The word sat in Noor's chest like a splinter. Rarely was not never, and never was the only margin in which she and a seven-year-old child could afford to live.

Then the river bridge. Half a mile of cobbled road between the hospital wall and the water, lit by gas lamps that the Corporation had been promising to repair since October and had not. The bridge itself was narrow, guarded at the far end by a checkpoint that had grown from one bored private to four watchful ones in the space of a fortnight. New Year's Eve was coming, and with it the great proclamation ceremony—and with *that*, she had heard the orderlies whispering, an inspector from the War Office, come to see with his own eyes the clean books and orderly wards of Colonel Ashworth's command.

An inspector. A man whose whole profession was looking closely at things.

Ashworth would want the town spotless for him. Every record squared, every street swept, every loose thread cut. The checkpoints would tighten like a fist.

And then the railway barrier. The last gate. Beyond it, the platform, the London train at twenty minutes past eleven—a train that would carry whoever stood upon it out of the valley and into the new century, if they could reach it. The barrier was manned by military police now; it had been constables before Christmas. She had watched the change happen the way one watches weather shift: gradually, then all at once, and too late to do anything but adjust.

Noor set down the candlestick and pressed her palms flat against the table until the wood's cold bit into her skin. Three checkpoints. One child. One pocket watch sewn into a coat lining, heavier than any object its size had a right to be.

Her brother Thomas had died at a place whose name had been struck from the dispatches, in a battle that officially had not happened, among men who officially had never been there. If someone had smuggled the truth out—one person, one act of ordinary courage—would he be a name in a ledger now instead of an absence at the family table? She did not know. But she knew that she could not fold Mirabel's coat over the watch and hand the girl back to whatever fate waited outside the hospital walls. Not this time. Not again.

The door opened behind her, and she slid the timetable shut with a motion so smooth it frightened her—how quickly the lie became instinct, how easily the body learned deceit.

Tobias Fenwick stood in the doorway with a stack of requisition forms against his chest, his spectacles fogged from the cold corridor, looking exactly like what he appeared to be: a clerk, a mild man, a man made of ledgers and ink-stained cuffs.

"I've brought the December returns," he said. "You'll want to sign for the laudanum before the quarter closes."

"Set them down."

He set them down. He lingered. And in the lamplight Noor saw what she had been refusing to see for days: that his eyes went, once, quickly, to the place beneath the floorboard by the stove where she kept her private ledger of names. He looked away again at once, but a look is a door opened and closed, and you cannot pretend afterward that the room beyond does not exist.

"Tobias." Her voice was low. "How much do you know?"

He took off his spectacles, polished them on his cuff with unnecessary thoroughness, and put them back on. When he spoke, his voice was barely above the sound of the wind at the window.

"I know the casualty returns don't balance," he said. "They haven't balanced since September. I'm the one who copies them, Noor. Do you imagine I don't count?" He laughed, a thin, unhappy sound. "I count everything. It's my whole life. And everything is short by thirty-one."

Thirty-one. The number landed in the silent room like a stone into still water. Thirty-one men who had lived and fought and bled and been subtracted from existence with a clerk's neat stroke.

"The colonel thinks the discrepancy is an error," Tobias went on. "He asked me last week whether my arithmetic was failing. I told him arithmetic never fails, sir; only honesty does." His hands were trembling, and he clasped them together to hide it. "He laughed. God help me, he thought it was wit."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you've been carrying that child like a basket of gunpowder for nine days, and because the watch she came with—" he stopped, swallowed, "—the handwriting in those documents. I recognized it. Lieutenant Calloway. I filed his reports myself, back in October, before his name stopped appearing anywhere at all."

The stove ticked. Somewhere down the ward a man called out in his sleep and was answered by another voice, soothing, automatic.

"You should walk away," Noor said quietly. "You should sign your forms and keep your head down and survive this town. That's what a sensible man does."

"Yes," Tobias agreed. "I have considered it at length. I've drawn up a list of arguments for it. I'm very good at lists." He met her eyes at last, and behind the fogged spectacles there was something she had never seen in him before—not bravery, exactly; something smaller and more stubborn than bravery, the courage of a man who has counted everything and found himself short. "But my brother is on your ledger, Noor. Page two. You wrote his name in October and didn't know it was mine."

She had not known. She had written *Fenwick, J., private* in her careful hand between two other erased men, and the name had meant nothing to her, and everything to the mild clerk standing before her with trembling hands and requisition forms.

"The laundry cart leaves at seven on Thursday morning," she heard herself say. "It goes to the bridge. After the bridge there's the barrier, and the barrier is the hard part, and I don't yet know how we cross it—but I will by Thursday."

"*We*," Tobias repeated softly, testing the word.

"We."

He nodded once, the nod of a man signing something, and gathered up his unsigned papers. At the door he paused.

"There's one more thing," he said. "The inspector arrives tomorrow. Ashworth has ordered the wards prepared for viewing—all of them. Including the children's annex." His voice dropped. "He'll see her, Noor. He'll see her and he'll ask who she belongs to, and you'll need a better answer than any you've rehearsed."

The door closed behind him. Noor stood alone with the cold map and the ticking stove, and understood that the countdown had begun in earnest—that she had perhaps forty hours, no more, in which hope and terror would have to share the same narrow bed.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark she laid her hand flat against the floorboard by the stove, over the hidden ledger, over thirty-one names and one sleeping child, and felt—for the first time since the night Mirabel arrived—not alone.

* * *

She checked on Mirabel before going to her own cot, as she did every night now. The girl slept curled in the corner bed of the children's annex, the grey coat draped over her like a second blanket, one hand resting—as it always rested—on the place where the watch lay sewn flat against the wool.

As Noor tucked the blanket higher, Mirabel stirred. Her eyes opened halfway, dark and unafraid in the lamplight, and she said, in the small clear voice that never failed to undo Noor's composure:

"He said you'd come. Papa said the lady with the kind hands would take us to the train." The child's fingers tightened on the coat. "He said to tell you—it wasn't sickness that killed the others. Will you write that down? He said you'd know where to write it."

Noor's blood went cold and still, like the water in the basin at dawn.

"Mirabel," she said carefully, "what else did your papa tell you?"

The girl smiled sleepily, a gap-toothed smile, and pulled the coat closer, and began to speak.
