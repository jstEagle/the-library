## 6. Mirabel's Version

The lady with the kind hands asked me to tell it proper, from the beginning, and not to hurry. She said stories were like tea — they needed time to steep before you could drink them. I liked that. Nobody at home had ever said my words were worth waiting for.

I sat on the edge of her bed with my boots swinging, because I was not allowed to touch the floor of the fever ward without permission, and the watch lay between us in its nest of blanket. It looked smaller in the daytime. Smaller, but still heavy, the way secrets are heavy even when they are only made of brass.

"Start wherever you like," she said. "Wherever is truest."

I started with the smell. That was the truest thing I had.

* * *

Papa smelled of bootblack and woodsmoke and, underneath it, something sweetish like the inside of a violin case. He played the fiddle sometimes, badly, and Mama would put her hands over her ears and laugh until she had to sit down on the kitchen chair. He carried me to the hospital on the last night because he could not carry anything else anymore. His arms were shaking. I thought he was cold, but he wasn't cold. He was frightened. Frightened has a smell too — like pennies left in your pocket too long, like rain about to come down.

He walked fast through streets that were too quiet. In our town the streets were never quiet. There were always dogs and carts and Mrs. Pellowe shouting at her goat. But these streets were quiet, and Papa kept looking behind us, and his breath came out in little white flags like he was surrendering over and over to nobody.

"Papa, where are we going?"

"To a door," he said. "A good door."

I asked him what made a door good, and he thought about it properly, the way he thought about everything, which was slow as syrup but sure. "A good door," he said, "is one that opens for a child when nobody else will." Then he kissed the top of my head, and his lips were hot as a kettle, and that was the first time I knew he was sick. Not just frightened-sick. Sick-sick.

We stopped in a shadow by a wall, and he took off his coat and wrapped it round me even though he was shivering himself, and then he did a strange thing. He took out the watch — his papa's watch, the one with the dent shaped like a little moon — and he got out a needle from his kit, a curved needle like a fish hook, and he sewed the watch into the hem of my coat. Right into the wool. I watched him do it and I didn't say a word, because grown-ups doing needlework look so serious that it feels rude to interrupt.

"What are you sewing?" I whispered.

"Insurance," he said.

"Is that a kind of coat?"

He laughed then, a real laugh, short and surprised out of him, and it made me feel better than the coat did. "It's a kind of promise," he said. "You keep it safe, my Mirabel. You don't open it. You don't let anyone open it. And if anyone asks you where your papa is—" his voice went funny, like a step missing on a staircase— "you forget my face."

"Forget your face?"

"Forget it, sweetheart. Say you never had one. Faces get people found."

I told him I couldn't do that. I told him his face was my favorite face in all the world, better than the horse next door, better than the moon, and he pressed his hand flat over my mouth very gently, the way you'd hush a bird, and he said, "That is exactly why you must. The best hiding place for a treasure is in plain forgetting."

Then we walked again, and the door came, the good door, and it had light around it like butter, and he set me down and knocked three times, quick-quick-quick, and ran. He didn't even say goodbye. I used to be angry about that. Now I think goodbye would have been too heavy a thing for him to carry along with everything else.

* * *

But here is the part the papers in the watch don't know, the part only I have, because I was there, small and watching, the way children watch — sideways, always sideways, when nobody thinks we're looking.

Before we ever came to the garrison town, there were men at our house. Three of them, in grey coats, no buttons shining, the kind of men who stand like furniture. They talked to Papa in the yard while Mama pretended to boil water we didn't need so she'd have an excuse to listen. I sat on the step with my doll, and my doll's head had come off, and I was holding it up against the wind, and so my ear was empty and free, and ears are cleverer than people think.

They said a name over and over. Ashworth. They said it like it was a stone in their mouths they couldn't spit out. One of them, the tall one with the scarred lip, said: *You signed, Corporal. Your signature is on the morning report. All you must do now is sign nothing further. A man who signs nothing lives long.*

And Papa said — I can hear his voice now, low and even, like when he read psalms — "I already wrote what I wrote. It's written fair and it's written true, and true doesn't unwrite itself."

The scarred-lip man laughed, and the laugh was worse than shouting. "Corporals die of fevers," he said. "Fevers are very patriotic this time of year."

Mama dropped the lid of the pot. That clang went through the whole yard. And the men went away, walking slow, unhurried, the way cats walk away from things they know they'll come back for.

Afterward Papa dug in the garden at night. I saw him from my window, kneeling in the dark with a lantern turned down to a bead, wrapping papers in oilcloth and fitting them inside the watch's back — the back that opened with a trick, press the crown and twist left, the way you'd soothe a stubborn jar. He buried the box the watch came in under the gooseberry bush, which seemed to me a terrible waste of a nice box.

"Mama," I said, "why does Papa garden at night?"

"Gardens keep their own hours," she said, and her face was white, and she gave me extra bread with honey, which is how mothers say *be afraid* without saying it.

* * *

There were other things too. Things I told the nurse slowly, while she wrote them down in a little book of her own, her pen going scratch-scratch like a mouse in a wall.

I told her about the night the guns came to our valley, though I wasn't supposed to hear that either. I heard it because the baby next door cried all night and I slept in the pantry to be near the coolness, and sound comes into pantries like water. Men shouting orders in the dark. Not battle-shouting. Battle sounds like thunder breaking. This sounded like a man reading a list, very calm, while other men begged. And then a silence afterward that was so big it had a shape, like the hole where a tooth was.

I told her about the wagons. Grey wagons, more of them than I could count on two hands, going out full and coming back empty, and the men driving them wouldn't meet anybody's eye for a week after, and one of them — young, freckled, hardly older than my cousin — sat down in our yard and drank all our well water and cried without any noise, just shaking, and Papa sat beside him and put an arm round him and neither of them said one word, and that was the loudest conversation I ever heard.

And I told her the names. Because Papa, some evenings, when the lamp was low, would say names to himself like a prayer or a punishment. Names I learned the way children learn anything — without trying, without knowing it matters, which is how it sinks deepest. *Ellery Voss. Tam Beckett. The Hollis brothers, both of them. Old Sergeant Crane, who owed me a pipe.* I said them back to the nurse, all eleven that I could remember, and she wrote them, and when she finished writing she didn't move for a long moment, and then she touched the page flat with her whole hand, gentle, like she was keeping the names warm.

"There were twelve," she said quietly. "Your father's name makes twelve."

And that is when I understood, in the way you understand falling — suddenly and all the way down — that Papa was not coming back for me. That the fever they'd give him in the papers was the kind you catch from telling the truth. That the watch sewn into my coat was not insurance for a trip. It was insurance for a grave.

I did not cry. I want that known. I sat very straight on her bed and I swung my boots and I did not cry, because Papa said faces get people found, and I had decided my crying face was also mine, and they could not have that either.

The nurse — Noor, she said her name was, like a bell struck once — looked at me for a long time. Something changed in her face while she looked. It was like watching ice decide to become a river. Before, she had been careful, the way everyone is careful here, eyes down, voice soft, doors shut. After, there was a stillness in her that wasn't careful at all. It was the stillness of somebody who has stopped asking whether it is safe.

"Mirabel," she said. "Did your father ever say where the wagons went?"

"The valley past the mill," I said. "He called it the Hollow. He said the grass grows wrong there now. Too green."

She stood up so quickly the chair scraped. She went to the window and stood with her back to me, and through the frost ferns on the glass I could see the parade ground below, where soldiers were drilling for the New Year, wheeling and turning like clockwork toys, and somewhere beyond them a drum was practising its one proud sentence over and over.

"He counted them," she said, mostly to herself, mostly to the window. "He wrote them down and hid them in a child's coat. He sent her to the one door in this town that might not burn the paper." Her breath fogged the glass and wiped it clear again with the side of her fist, once, hard. "Twelve names. And he trusted a seven-year-old to carry what forty grown men were too afraid to hold."

I didn't understand all of it then. I understood enough. I slid off the bed and went and stood next to her, because standing next to sad people is most of what kindness is, and I took the hem of her apron and held it, the way I used to hold Mama's.

"They can't find it if they don't know I remember," I said. "Forget the face. That's what he said. So I forgot everybody's face except yours."

Noor looked down at me, and her eyes were wet but her jaw was set like a keel, and outside the drum went on beating, and somewhere across the town a clock ticked toward the end of the year, and she said:

"No one is forgetting your face, Mirabel. Not ever again. Now help me count the days we have left — and pray they are fewer than the ones he needs to find us first."
