## 7. The Bridge at Dusk

The last day of the old year came down grey as dishwater, and Noor woke before the bells with Mirabel's breath warm against her collarbone, where the girl had burrowed sometime in the small hours like a wren seeking shelter. Noor lay still a long while, listening to the hospital wake around them—the clatter of pails, the groan of men who dreamed of mud, the kettle beginning its low complaint in the scullery. Above all of it, beneath her floorboard, the ledger waited with its ninety-one names.

Today it would have to travel.

She had spent three nights planning it the way she once planned ward rotations: obsessively, on scraps of paper burned afterward in the stove. The hospital gate was the first wall. Private Hobbes had walked that post since October, a boy of nineteen with chilblains and a mother's photograph folded in his pay book—she knew this because soldiers told nurses everything, the way the drowning tell anyone standing on shore. He counted what passed through his gate because counting was the only power he had ever been given. But he did not count laundry carts, not when the garrison's linen had to reach the washerwomen by eight or the whole compound stank by noon.

So at half past seven, Noor knelt in the linen room with Mirabel and taught her to be still.

"You'll be under here." She lifted the top shelf of sheets, already damp and heavy, showing the hollow beneath where the soiled bundles sat loose. "It will smell of bleach and worse things. You mustn't sneeze, you mustn't cough, you mustn't laugh even if something is funny."

"What if something's funny?"

"Then you may think about it very hard and laugh tonight."

Mirabel considered this gravely. She was wearing the coat—the coat, with its sewn seam now empty, the watch riding instead in a bread tin tucked into Noor's market basket beneath a cloth of onions. They had agreed the child should carry nothing. Ashworth's men were looking for a girl with a watch; let them look. The coat itself was danger enough, but there had been no time, no money, no other garment in the stores small enough for her, and Noor had reasoned—coldly, in the way she was learning to reason now—that a coat was only evidence if someone knew it. And one person knew it. That thought she put away in the same drawer where she kept her brother's last letter, to be opened only when she could bear it.

"Hobbes," Mirabel said suddenly. "The man at the gate. Is he the one who looks?"

"He looks," Noor said. "But he doesn't see. There's a difference, and we're going to teach it to him."

* * *

The cart went through at twenty past eight.

Noor pushed it herself, having claimed the errand with a nurse's unassailable authority—*the washerwomen are shorthanded, I'll take it across myself, Sister*—and Hobbes stamped his feet against the cold and glanced at her face, at her basket, at nothing else. The cart rattled over the frozen ruts of the gateway. Under the wet sheets, Noor heard Mirabel breathing: small, deliberate, the measured breaths they had practiced, in for four counts, out for four, like a soldier keeping step. Ninety-one names, Noor thought. She is the ninety-second, and I will not write hers in a ledger. I will keep her where ledgers cannot reach.

Ten yards beyond the gate she stopped, set down the shafts, and made a great show of adjusting her collar while the sheets shifted and a small voice whispered, "Was that him? Did he look?"

"He looked."

"Did he *see*?"

"No." And then, unable to help herself, lifting the corner of a sheet to find two bright eyes in the dimness: "You did beautifully."

"I wasn't scared," said Mirabel, in the tone of a girl who had been terrified since November and had decided terror was simply weather.

They left the cart at the wash-house as arranged—one of the washerwomen, a gap-toothed woman named Mrs. Calloway who owed Noor for three quiet months of extra laudanum for her bad hip, asked no questions and took no payment, only pressed a hot bun into Mirabel's hands and said, "God go with you, missus," which was more benediction than any chaplain had managed all year.

The bridge was a mile east, across the town's crooked spine, and Noor walked it with the bun-eating child's hand in hers, two figures among dozens bound for the New Year's Eve markets. Dusk would come early—that was the plan's whole architecture. The river checkpoint manned itself thin at market hours; the sentries wanted their own suppers; the crowds gave cover the way snow gives cover to tracks. But between the wash-house and the water lay the long open street, and Noor felt every yard of it like a held note, her spine a plumb line, her face arranged into the mild boredom of a woman thinking about turnips.

The pocket watch rode against the onions, cold as a stone. Once—a woman jostled her, laughed an apology—Noor nearly dropped the basket. Once Mirabel tugged her hand and pointed at a Punch and Judy booth being assembled on the corner, and Noor felt the whole enterprise tremble on the hinge of a seven-year-old's whim, and then Mirabel lowered her finger again without asking, because she understood, in the way of children raised near fear, exactly how much asking cost.

* * *

The bridge at dusk was a country unto itself.

Lamps were being lit along both parapets, and the gaslight turned the crowd to amber—carters and fishwives, soldiers on leave, a barrel-organ grinding out something jaunty, gulls screaming over the offal barges below. The river ran black and quick beneath, carrying ice from upstream. On the far span, at the mouth of the bridge, stood the checkpoint: two sentries, a brazier, a barrier of rope and iron. Beyond it, the railway station, and the night train north.

Noor joined the press of people funneling toward the barrier and became, deliberately, ordinary. A nurse going home for the holiday. A tired aunt with her sister's child. She had rehearsed the sentence—*my niece, we're for my mother's in Harwick, yes, the six o'clock*—until it wore smooth as a river pebble.

The queue crept. The brazier spat. And then the taller of the two sentries turned toward the light, and Noor's blood went to ice water.

She knew him. Corporal Dane. He had been carried into the fever ward in September with a bullet through the shoulder, and she had sat with him through four nights of delirium, mopping his brow, holding the basin when his stomach rebelled, writing down—at his sobbing insistence—the names of the men of his section who hadn't come back. Names the rolls didn't carry. He didn't know about her ledger. But he knew her face, and faces were the one thing the plan could not disguise.

He saw her. She watched it happen: recognition moving across his features like cloud-shadow over a field. His eyes went to the child. To the coat. Noor saw him place it—the same way she had placed the handwriting in the dead officer's letter—and she saw, too, the arithmetic begin behind his eyes: a nurse, a girl, a coat described in orders posted that very morning at every post and gate, *missing child, report immediately*, reward unstated but surely generous.

The queue moved forward. Three people between them and the barrier. Two.

"Paper?" Dane said, and his voice was flat as slate.

Noor produced her hospital pass with fingers that did not shake, because she had ordered them not to and they were disciplined, her fingers, trained in the art of steadiness. Dane took it. Read it. Looked up.

And in the space of one heartbeat—one in which Noor felt the entire year tilt, in which she thought of the floorboard and the tin and the small warm weight of the hand in hers—Dane's gaze slid off her like rain off glass.

"Ward nurse, aren't you," he said. Loudly, bored, for the benefit of his companion. "Fever block. Sat with me when I had the fever. Good woman." He handed back the pass. "Family, is it? Going somewhere nice?"

"My niece," Noor said. "My mother's, in Harwick."

"Nice at New Year," said Dane, waving them through. "Next!"

* * *

They crossed.

Noor kept walking—past the barrier, past the brazier's orange circle, into the lamplight of the far bank—and only when the crowd thickened around a chestnut seller did she allow herself to stop, to lean against a wall, to breathe. Mirabel looked up at her.

"That man knew us."

"Yes."

"And he let us go anyway."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because he owed her nothing, Noor thought. Because he was a poor man in a rich man's army and had learned, as all the powerless learn, that the only currency that never devalues is what you owe your conscience. Because somewhere in the mud of some foreign field his friends' names had been scraped off the official record like frost off a windowpane, and though he couldn't have known it, she was carrying those names in a bread tin under some onions, and perhaps—perhaps—he had seen that too, in the way the powerless see each other across a crowded world.

"Because he remembered being cold," she said aloud, "and someone warmed him."

She bought chestnuts with the last of her small coin, and they ate them standing in the lamplight, sugar-burnt and steaming, and Mirabel smiled for the first time in days, a quick crooked smile like a crack of sun through January cloud. The station clock above them read quarter to six. The night train stood at the platform, breathing steam, doors open, lamps lit, going north.

Noor took the child's hand.

They had almost reached the barrier—almost, ten steps, five—when a hand closed on Noor's arm from behind, gentle and absolute, and a voice she trusted more than any voice left alive in the world said quietly at her ear:

"Don't run. It's Tobias. We need to talk—there's something wrong at the bridge ahead, and I don't think we have much time."
