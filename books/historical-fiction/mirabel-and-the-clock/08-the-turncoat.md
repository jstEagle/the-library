## 8. The Turncoat

The river checkpoint was called the Weir Gate, though no weir had stood there in living memory—only a squat stone tollhouse, a chain lowered across the water road, and two lanterns burning behind frosted glass like eyes that never blinked. Noor had passed it a hundred times in her nursing years, carrying requisitions and dead men's letters. She had never once thought of it as a wall. Now it was the only thing between her and everything.

She came down the towpath with Mirabel's small hand swallowed inside hers, the girl's breath puffing in white flags, the watch sewn against Noor's ribs where it lay warm as a second heart. Tobias walked on her other side, his clerk's coat buttoned to the throat, his boots making the careful sound of a man who had rehearsed this walk all day.

"The sentry at the chain is Corporal Voss," Tobias murmured. "I know him from the records office. He drinks. He owes me eleven shillings I have never asked for, which is better than money." A thin smile. "We go through as your brother's people—quartermaster's clerks moving hospital stores before the holiday. Nobody counts boxes on New Year's Eve."

"And if somebody does?"

"Then we are three fools out walking by the river," he said, "and fools are the one thing this garrison has never run short of."

Mirabel laughed—a small startled sound, quickly smothered, as though she had been taught already that laughter cost something. Noor squeezed her hand. *Half an hour*, she told herself. *The railway barrier next, then the inspector's train at midnight.* The plan sat in her mind like a house of cards in a drafty room, and she had learned not to look at it directly.

The river ran black and quick under the ice-fringed banks. Somewhere upstream a church was practicing its bells for tomorrow, and the notes came down the water broken and silver, a rehearsal of midnight. Mirabel tilted her head toward them.

"Is it true the new century comes with fireworks?"

"It comes whether anyone welcomes it or not," Noor said. "That is the nature of centuries."

"I'd like fireworks anyway."

"If we do what we must tonight, you shall see them from a better window than mine."

* * *

The chain was down. Corporal Voss stood under the tollhouse lamp picking at his gloves, and beside him—not in any plan Tobias had drawn—was a lieutenant of Ashworth's own company, young and pink-cheeked, stamping his feet against the cold with the impatience of a man kept from a party.

Tobias did not falter. That was the first thing Noor noticed, and the last thing she would ever stop noticing: he did not falter. His voice went smooth and bored, the voice of a man who had argued his way past a hundred gates.

"Quartermaster's stores, Lieutenant. Hospital linen and laudanum, if you want the inventory. Nurse Montrose will sign for it at the other end."

The lieutenant waved the papers away without reading them. But Voss looked up from beneath the brim of his cap, and his eyes went from Tobias to Noor to the child, and stayed on the child a moment too long. There was a folded paper in his glove, Noor saw it when he shifted his weight—the way the corner showed, cream against grey wool—and her blood went cold with a knowledge that arrived whole, without steps, the way grief arrives.

*Someone had already sold them.*

"Tobias," she said lightly, "the manifest."

He handed it over. Their fingers touched on the paper. And in that touch she felt it—something wrong in the pressure, an apology pressed into her skin like a seal into wax. She had held dying men's hands for six years. She knew what a goodbye felt like.

"Voss," Tobias said. "Eleven shillings."

"Aye," said Voss, and lifted the chain.

They were through. Ten yards along the water road, twenty, the tollhouse lanterns shrinking behind them, and Noor let herself breathe—let herself believe, for the length of one frozen breath, that the house of cards had held.

Then Tobias stopped walking.

"Lace your boot, nurse," he said, loud enough for the checkpoint to hear. "You've come loose."

And while Noor crouched, Mirabel's mittened hand in hers, Tobias turned back toward the light and walked to meet the lieutenant halfway across the ice-glazed stones, and Noor heard every word as clearly as she would hear bells at midnight.

"She's the one. The girl from the hospital door. The watch is on the woman—it's sewn in her coat, left side, under the arm. I'll want it understood I came forward freely. Pardon in writing. My name kept out of whatever follows."

*Tobias.*

The name made no sound in her mouth. She could not even shape it. Six years of shared tea gone cold on the night desk; the man who had helped her decipher a dead officer's hand by lamplight, who had traced the falsified columns with his finger and whispered, *God help us, Noor, it's all here*—walking now, hat in hand, selling a seven-year-old girl for a signature that promised he would not be punished for having helped her in the first place.

*Loyalty bought is loyalty sold.* Her mother had said it once about a neighbor who informed on strikers. Noor had thought it a hard old proverb. It was not a proverb. It was a weather report. It was simply what happened, everywhere, to everyone, when the price was right and the fear was deep enough.

The lieutenant's whistle cut the dark.

Noor did not think. Thinking was for afterward. She took Mirabel up under the arms—seven years old, light as a bundle of wet laundry—and ran for the reeds where the towpath dropped to a boat skiff some fisherman had hauled above the waterline, hull cracked, oars gone, useless except as shadow. Behind her: boots, shouts, the lanterns swinging wide, the world coming apart exactly as she had always known it would.

"Noor—" Mirabel's voice, small and fierce. "Noor, don't let go—"

"I won't. I won't, little one—"

But hands closed on the girl's coat from behind, gloved, certain, gentle in the way of men who had been ordered to be gentle, and the coat tore at the shoulder seam where the watch's twin stitches had once been, and then the grip was on Mirabel and not on Noor and there were too many of them, four, five, a wall of wool and lantern-light, and Noor was struck across the mouth—she tasted copper and snow—and thrown down among the reeds with the river roaring in her ears.

She heard Mirabel scream her name twice. Then a hand, almost kind, covered the child's mouth, and the third time the girl cried out it came muffled, and then there were voices saying *easy, easy, miss, no harm meant,* which was the language of every soldier doing a thing that would shame him by morning.

Noor crawled up the bank on her elbows because her legs would not answer. From the high ground she watched them carry her away—Mirabel rigid with terror, her arms reaching back over a soldier's shoulder, reaching toward the river, toward the reeds, toward her—with the watch's documents still safe against Noor's ribs and nothing else in the world safe at all.

At the tollhouse, Tobias Fenwick stood holding out his hand for the pardon paper. He did not watch the child carried off. He looked, instead, at the reeds where Noor had been—at the empty darkness where she had been—and she understood that he had known she would live. That was part of the bargain too, or he had told himself it was. *The woman escapes. Only the girl is given up.* Men were very good at carving bargains into shapes their souls could survive.

His collar was up. His shoulders were shaking. She noted that too, with a cruelty she did not choose and could not refuse: he got nothing else, so let him keep the shaking.

* * *

They were gone. The lanterns burned. The river ran. Somewhere upstream the church bells practiced still, patient, oblivious, counting toward a midnight that would not wait for grief.

Noor knelt in the frozen reeds until her knees went numb, and then she made herself stand, because standing was the first act of every nurse who had ever watched a patient die and been required, immediately, to save the next one. Blood came thinly from her split lip; she wiped it with the back of her glove. The watch pressed against her ribs, warm, intact, indifferent. Everything they had killed each other for tonight, and it was still here, beating its small brass heart under her coat.

Mirabel had told her, once, in the dark of the linen room: *Papa said if ever I'm frightened I should count the names. He said names are stronger than soldiers.* Four days ago Noor had believed that was a child's comfort, a talisman made of words.

Now she knew it was instructions.

Ashworth would keep the girl alive—leverage, insurance, a hostage against exactly the evidence Noor carried. Which meant Mirabel would be wherever Ashworth was tomorrow night. Where Ashworth was, the War Office inspector would be. The proclamation, the assembly, the midnight bell—all of it converging on one lit hall where a colonel meant to stand before his superiors with clean ledgers and a stolen century.

Noor put her hand flat against her ribs, over the hidden compartment, and spoke aloud to the black water, to the erased dead, to the girl being carried through the freezing town:

"Hold on till midnight, little one. I am coming to read the names."

Downstream, the bells began, softly, to practice again.
