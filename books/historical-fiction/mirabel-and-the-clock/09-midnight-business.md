## 9. Midnight Business

The garrison hall had been dressed for the century the way a corpse is dressed for burial: carefully, and by hands that did not love it.

Noor saw this from the servants' corridor, through the gap between a coat rack and a pillar of borrowed bunting. Below, the assembly floor gleamed with fresh wax, and the chandeliers had been polished until their light fell in hard, unkind coins across the shoulders of officers in dress uniform. Garlands of pine and holly climbed the pillars. A banner above the orchestra's dais read *1899* in gold thread, and beside it, waiting under a cloth like a patient not yet dead, hung the banner that would replace it at midnight. Someone had already carried in the new numeral. It leaned against the wall in the shadows where Noor stood — a great gilt *1900*, tall as a man, smelling of size and fresh paint.

She counted her heartbeats to keep them slow. Forty years old in three days, she thought absurdly, and I am hiding behind a Christmas garland with a stolen watch sewn into my bodice.

Not stolen. Recovered. There was a difference, and she intended to spend the rest of her life insisting upon it.

Below, Colonel Ashworth held court near the hearth. He wore his best uniform and his best face, which was to say the one he kept for superiors: warm at the eyes, modest about the mouth, a face built to be believed. The War Office inspector stood at his elbow — Mr. Calloway, grey-whiskered, grey-gloved, carrying his authority the way some men carry an umbrella they hope not to need. Ashworth was speaking; Noor could not hear the words over the string quartet, but she knew the shape of them. She had heard him give that speech in miniature over tea, all those weeks ago, while his men turned over her wards looking for a ledger. *A difficult year. A garrison can be proud of its losses as much as its victories.* He had said something like that once, and she had smiled and poured, and hated herself for how easily her hand stayed steady.

Tonight Mirabel was not hidden in a laundry cart or a hayloft. Tonight Mirabel stood at the edge of the assembly floor in a borrowed blue dress, holding the hand of Sergeant Doyle, who had been told — Noor had watched Ashworth tell him, an hour before, in a low voice with the door ajar — that the girl would remain within arm's reach until certain business concluded after midnight. The child looked small and terribly clean, scrubbed for the occasion, her hair braided with somebody's ribbon. Her chin was up. She had learned that trick in the fever ward, Noor thought. You hold your chin up when you are frightened, because looking down makes it worse.

Mirabel's eyes swept the room once, twice — searching, the way she always searched — and passed over the pillar where Noor stood without stopping. Good. That was good. A child who found what she searched for might show it in her face.

The clock above the gallery read quarter to twelve.

Noor pressed her palm flat against the watch beneath her bodice. It sat over her heart like a second heartbeat, ticking out of time with her own. Tobias had taught her the clasp's secret weeks ago — press the crown, turn left, and the case opened along a seam no eye would find — but she did not open it now. Not yet. There was an order to things. Her brother had been a soldier and had believed in order even when the order killed him; she had inherited the belief the way one inherits a scar.

*Walk past the last checkpoint in plain sight.* That had been her own plan, made in the days after the bridge, after Tobias, after the cold empty hour when she had knelt on the riverbank and understood exactly what she had lost and what she had left. The final checkpoint was not a barrier or a bridge. It was the assembly itself. Every guest admitted by invitation, every servant vouched for, every eye sharpened by champagne and consequence. And therefore, precisely because of it, blind. Nobody searched a nurse carrying a tray. Nobody looked twice at a woman whose work was invisible by design. Ashworth had spent a career making people invisible; tonight one of them would walk straight through the belly of his triumph.

She had come in with the hospital's catering detail, in her grey dress with her sleeves pinned back, carrying a tray of syllabub nobody had asked her for. Three sentries had looked at her. One had thanked her. She was still, officially, the woman who had lied smoothly to the colonel over tea. He trusted her lie more than he trusted his own men — that was the corruption of power, she thought; it teaches a man that everyone else is corruptible too.

The clock read ten to twelve.

* * *

Calloway moved away from Ashworth toward the punch table, and Noor made her decision the way she made all her decisions now: quickly, before fear could dress itself up as prudence.

She crossed the service door, set down her tray on a sideboard, unpinned her sleeves, and walked onto the assembly floor in her nurse's grey with the red cross at her collar. The crowd parted around her the way water parts around a piling — not hostility, merely the assumption that she had business elsewhere. She let it part her toward the inspector.

"Mr. Calloway." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Forgive the intrusion. Nurse Montrose, garrison hospital. I have documents relevant to your inspection. Casualty returns. For the autumn campaign."

Something flickered behind the grey whiskers — not surprise, she thought. Confirmation. "Documents," he said mildly. "From whom?"

"From Captain Elias Vane," she said, and said the name aloud for the first time in two years, and felt it leave her body like a splinter drawn. "And from forty-one other men whose names do not appear in any ledger in this building."

Across the room, Ashworth turned.

She never learned afterward what caught his attention — whether the inspector's posture changed, whether some instinct older than evidence reached him across the candlelight. But he turned, and he saw her, and she watched the warmth go out of his best face the way frost leaves a window. He crossed the floor unhurried. That was the terrible thing about him, Noor thought; he was never hurried, because hurry was for men with something to hide, and his whole art was hiding things in plain sight.

"Nurse Montrose." His voice was pleasant, pitched for the surrounding guests, who were already pretending not to listen. "You are attending tonight? How diligent."

"I was invited by my conscience, Colonel."

A ripple of laughter somewhere. Ashworth did not smile. "The inspector is fatigued from his journey. Whatever petition you imagine—"

"They are casualty returns," Calloway said quietly. "I will see them."

"The inspector will see nothing until—" Ashworth stopped himself, recalibrated, and Noor watched him do it, watched the machinery of his charm engage. "Gentlemen. Ladies. It is nearly midnight. Let us not darken a new century with clerical quarrels. Nurse, whatever grievance you carry, bring it to my office tomorrow, and I promise you a full hearing."

"You promised Captain Vane a hearing too," Noor said.

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. Several heads turned. Somewhere near the dais, the quartet faltered and resumed. And Mirabel, at the edge of the crowd, went very still, the way children go still when they hear a word that belongs to them.

Ashworth's eyes moved to the girl, then back to Noor, and for one unguarded instant she saw the true arithmetic behind his face: the watch, the child, the clerk who had sold the child and not the watch, the whole edifice of convenient omission balanced on a knife-edge of seconds. Then the charm reassembled itself, seamless as wax.

"Take her to my office," he said to the sergeant, not loudly. "Wait."

"No," Noor said, and her voice cracked across the assembly like a whip, and fifty faces swung toward her, and she understood that there was no longer any version of this night in which she walked away unheard. So be it. She had spent two years whispering names under floorboards. Her throat ached for volume.

"The colonel has ordered a massacre covered and forty-one men erased," she said clearly, to the room, to the chandeliers, to the gilt *1900* leaning against its wall. "He has falsified the returns so that London believes this garrison honorable. He is holding a seven-year-old child hostage to his reputation. And I hold the proof in my hand, and I am going to open it now, and every man here may choose which side of history to stand on while the bells ring."

Silence, total, ringing silence — the kind that precedes either catastrophe or truth.

Calloway extended one gloved hand. "Open it, Nurse."

* * *

She pressed the crown. She turned it left. The seam parted with a sound too small for anyone but her to hear, like a lock opening in a dream.

Inside lay the folded papers in Elias Vane's careful dead hand — the returns, the true counts, the orders countermanded and the orders obeyed — and beneath them, worn soft at the folds, the page she had written herself, forty-one names in her own ink, copied night after night from memory and confession and the feverish murmuring of dying men. She had sewn it into the watch case herself, three nights ago, with surgical thread, by candlelight, while Mirabel slept.

"Bells are striking," someone said stupidly, uselessly, as the first stroke rolled down from St. Aldric's tower and shivered the windows.

Noor lifted the papers high, and the chandelier light caught them, and she began to read.

"Private Thomas Alder. Private Samuel Beck. Captain Elias Vane." Her voice shook on his name and she let it shake. "Corporal James Dunn. Private Robert Fairweather—" her brother, and the room blurred, and she read through the blur because he deserved to be read through anything. The bells struck four. Five. Six. Ashworth was saying something — *this woman is deranged, gentlemen, seize her* — and no one moved, because Calloway had stepped between them, grey and immovable as a milestone, and because forty-one names take time to say and the room gave her the time.

Seven. Eight. Nine. Mirabel had broken free of Sergeant Doyle — she saw the child slip through the crowd like a minnow, blue dress flashing — and stood at the front, chin up, watching, listening for her father's name among the others.

Ten. Eleven.

"—and Private Willem Orr," Noor finished, as the twelfth bell struck midnight and the new century came in not with the colonel's proclamation but with the sound of a woman reading the dead back into the world.

Calloway took the papers from her hands as though they were communion. "Colonel Ashworth," he said, over the last resonance of the bells, "you will consider yourself relieved of command pending court-martial. Sergeant — the child stays with the nurse."

Ashworth looked around his assembly hall, at his garlands and his champagne and his guests already turning away from him, and Noor saw, finally, what he truly was: not a colossus, only a man standing in a room that had stopped believing him.

Then Mirabel's arms were around her waist, and the gilt *1900* still leaned against its wall, unwheeled, unproclaimed, and Noor held the child and the empty watch case together against her heart.

Outside, snow was beginning to fall on the year 1900 — and somewhere across town, a man named Tobias Fenwick was lighting a lamp, unaware that his pardon had just expired.
