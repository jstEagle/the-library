## 10. The Names Read Aloud

The bells had not finished striking when the world changed shape.

Noor stood in the great hall of the garrison assembly, her hand still resting on the open watch, and watched Colonel Ashworth's face perform a slow collapse—pride draining out of it the way water drains from a cracked basin, leaving only the grey sediment beneath. The inspector from the War Office, a spare man named Halloran with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a ledger himself, held the documents up to the gaslight and read them twice.

"Where did you get these?" he asked Noor, though his voice carried to every corner of the hall. The orchestra had stopped. Three hundred officers and their wives stood frozen among the bunting and the paper garlands that spelled 1900 in gilt letters.

"A dead man gave them to me," Noor said. "Through his daughter."

She nodded toward Mirabel, who sat on a folding chair near the door between two orderlies, small and solemn in her borrowed coat, watching everything with those dark, unblinking eyes. The girl did not cry. She had not cried since the men brought her back from Ashworth's quarters an hour before midnight—unchanged, unharmed, as though she were a pawn being moved back to its square rather than a hostage returned. Noor had gathered her up and felt the child's heart hammering like a bird against her own ribs, and had promised herself that whatever happened in the next hour, this heartbeat would go on.

Halloran turned to the colonel. "Colonel Ashworth. You will consider yourself relieved of command, effective immediately. Major Deverell—" a young officer at the side of the hall straightened as though struck "—you have temporary authority of the garrison. The court-martial convenes Monday." He paused, and something almost human crossed his dry face. "Happy New Year, gentlemen. I'm afraid it has started without some of us."

Ashworth said nothing at first. That was the thing Noor remembered afterward—not rage, not denial, but silence. He looked at her across the hall with an expression she could not name, something between curiosity and contempt, as if she were a specimen that had behaved contrary to its classification. Then he unbuckled his sword belt with steady hands, laid it on the refreshment table beside the punch bowl, and allowed himself to be walked out between two military policemen past the paper garlands and the cold champagne.

He did not look at Mirabel. Perhaps that was mercy. Perhaps he simply could not see children; Noor suspected he never had.

* * *

They kept her three days for questioning, which she did not mind. For three days she slept in a real bed, ate food someone else had cooked, and answered the same questions in different orders until Halloran seemed satisfied that she was exactly what she appeared: a nurse who had kept accounts no one asked her to keep.

On the third morning he spread her ledger—the little oilcloth-bound book she had carried through checkpoints and betrayals and a year of fevered nights—on the table between them.

"You understand these names must be verified," he said. "Cross-referenced against muster rolls, pay records, burial returns. Some may prove to be duplications. Errors."

"They are not errors," Noor said quietly.

"No," Halloran agreed, after a moment. "I don't suppose they are." He drew a fresh sheet of official stationery toward him. "But they will be entered properly now. Every one. Into the record, where they should have been all along." He looked up at her. "You wrote them down when no one else would. That is more than most soldiers managed this decade."

She thought of her brother then—Thomas Montrose, struck from the rolls of the 4th Battalion because a dead deserter made tidier arithmetic than a dead hero—and she had to turn her face toward the frosted window until her throat obeyed her again.

"His name goes first," she said when she could speak.

"It already has," said Halloran.

* * *

Tobias Fenwick came to see her on the last afternoon, escorted by a corporal who waited by the door with the patience of a man guarding something that had already surrendered. He looked smaller than she remembered, as though the winter had gotten inside him and stayed. His clerk's hands were folded on the table between them, knuckles white.

"I want you to know," he began, and stopped. Started again. "There is no version of what I did that I can explain to you that won't sound like what it was."

"Cowardice?"

"Yes." He didn't flinch from it. That, at least, she gave him. "They told me they'd hang me for the forgery charges—the ones Ashworth manufactured about my own office—and I believed them, because I wanted to believe anything except that I was going to die over a pocket watch." He laughed, a terrible dry sound. "And then the watch turned out to be worth dying over, and I'd already sold it. Isn't that always the arithmetic? You spend your courage buying your life, and then find out too late the price was wrong."

Noor looked at him for a long time. She had rehearsed hatred on the bridge, on the walk to the assembly, in the hours before midnight. She had imagined saying cruel things, precise things, the way she imagined writing letters she never sent. But sitting across from Tobias Fenwick in the grey January light, she found only a tired pity, cold and clean.

"The men whose names you traded away are dead either way," she said. "Mirabel is not. She'll grow up remembering that a man she trusted handed her to soldiers. That's the sentence you'll serve, Tobias. Not the dismissal. Her memory of you."

He closed his eyes. "Yes," he said. "That one."

They dismissed him the following week—stripped of post, denied pension, put on a boat south with a reference so bland it was an insult. Noor heard later that he took a position keeping books for a grain merchant in some provincial town, that he was competent and quiet and drank alone. She found she could not wish him worse. Wickedness, she decided, was rarer than weakness, and harder to carry.

* * *

The reading of the names took place on the fourteenth of January, in the same hall where the century had been proclaimed, though the garlands were gone now and the gilt numerals taken down and burned.

Major Deverell, acting under orders from the War Office, stood at a lectern with the restored casualty roll before him, and read. It took two hours. One hundred and eleven names, each spoken slowly, each followed by regiment, rank, date of death, and cause—*killed in action at Kessel Ford, contrary to prior report*—the last phrase repeated like a bell tolling until the hall learned its weight.

Noor sat in the second row with Mirabel on her lap, because chairs were scarce and the girl refused to sit anywhere else. Around them sat the families—some summoned from distant counties, some local, mothers and widows and one very old father who had traveled three days on a mail coach. When their name was read, they did not weep, mostly. They sat straighter. It was, Noor thought, the strangest arithmetic of all: how a word spoken aloud could return something to people who had been robbed of even grief, since grief requires a body, a grave, a fact, and Ashworth's omissions had left them nothing to mourn but a rumor.

When Thomas Montrose's name was read, Noor's hands tightened on Mirabel's small waist, and the girl reached up and patted her cheek once, gravely, as though administering medicine.

"That's your brother," Mirabel whispered. It was not a question. Children collect secrets the way other people collect coins.

"Yes," Noor whispered back.

"He sounds tall."

A laugh broke out of her, wet and sudden, into the middle of the solemnity, and several heads turned, and she did not care. "He was," she said. "Very."

* * *

Afterward there was the question of Mirabel, and it resolved itself with the quiet efficiency of paperwork finally done right. There were no relatives—the mother dead two years, the father erased and then un-erased, an aunt in Manchester who replied by letter that she had five children already and a husband with weak lungs. The War Office, embarrassed into generosity, offered the girl a place in an orphan asylum.

Noor wrote instead to Halloran. She requested guardianship. She listed her income, her character references (the matron of the fever ward vouched; even the stranger from the bridge, a bargeman named Cottle, sent a letter written in pencil), and she ended with a paragraph she rewrote four times:

*I am not a soft woman, and I do not offer her a soft life. But I am the person who knows what her father died carrying, and I am the person whose name he trusted with it, though we never met. That is a kind of family. I believe it is enough.*

Halloran approved it in nine days.

They walked home from the hospital on the last evening of January, through streets still crusted with old snow, Mirabel's mittened hand swallowed whole inside Noor's own. The town smelled of coal smoke and thawing ice, and somewhere a barrel organ played a tune slightly out of key, and the lamplighter was working his way down the boulevard ahead of them, touching flame to wick after wick until the dusk bloomed with small warm suns.

At the door of the narrow house Noor had rented—two rooms above a chandler's shop, with a view of nothing but sky and chimney pots—Mirabel stopped on the step and dug in her coat pocket. She produced the watch. It had been returned to her after the court-martial exhibits were photographed, its hidden compartment empty now, its secret spent, just a watch again.

"He'd want me to keep it wound," she said seriously. "Papa. So it doesn't forget the time."

"So it doesn't forget the time," Noor agreed.

Inside, the room was cold, and Noor built the fire while Mirabel explored every inch of it, opening cupboards, testing the bed, claiming the window seat as sovereign territory. When the fire caught and the room filled with orange light, the girl climbed onto Noor's lap and studied her face with the frank appraisal of the very young.

"You're sad," Mirabel announced.

"I'm finished," Noor said, and found it was true. "It's different."

Mirabel considered this. "Will you write more names?"

"No. All the names are in the book now. The real book, the big one." She touched the girl's hair, fine and brown, smelling of woodsmoke. "From now on I'm only going to write letters. And maybe, someday, a story."

"What kind of story?"

Noor looked out the window at the lamplit street, at the century lying ahead of them like unwritten pages, and pulled the blanket around them both.

"One about a nurse," she said, "and a clock that wouldn't stop."

Outside, the church bells began to ring the hour, and neither of them counted.
