## 1. The Weight of Water

The moon did not rise that night, and the company called it a blessing.

Juniper Lockridge stood at the rail of the *Meridian Grace* and watched the black water breathe beneath them. No moon meant no light for the pearl beds, which meant the oysters could not be seen from above—which meant the divers would have to find them by touch, by memory, by the particular stubbornness of hands that had learned the reef the way other children learned their letters. The foreman, Aldous Wren, stood at the wheelhouse door with his lantern shuttered to a slit, and his voice came across the deck soft as silt settling.

"Tide's turning in twenty minutes. Beds off the eastern spur. Quota's forty shell per head before slack water." He paused, and Juniper heard him smile, though it was too dark to see it. "And mind the drop-off. The sea keeps what she takes."

He said that every night. It was not a warning so much as an accounting—a reminder that the sea and the Meridian Trading Company kept their books together, and both were owed.

Juniper pulled on the diving dress without hurry. Twenty years of this had taught them that hurry was how men drowned: the hurried diver forgot to check his valves, forgot to count his line, forgot that the air in his helmet was borrowed and the ledger of breath always came due. So they worked slowly, deliberately, letting Tam Osei buckle the corselet while the boat rolled under them, letting the copper weight of the world settle onto their shoulders one piece at a time.

"Cold night for the deep beds," Tam murmured. He was older than Juniper by ten years and talked less than most, which was why they tolerated each other. "Wren could've waited for the morning tide."

"Wren doesn't wait." Juniper tested the air valve with their thumb. A whisper of pressure, steady. Good. "Waiting costs interest."

Tam huffed something that might have been a laugh. "Everything here costs interest."

That was true enough. Juniper's own account sat in the company office in a ledger bound in green calfskin, and they knew its figures the way they knew the lines of their own palm—better, really, because they had never been permitted to hold the book itself. Four hundred and twelve pounds, nine shillings, carried forward year after year like ballast. Their mother's debt, inherited whole at age seven along with her diving suit, her name entered in the indenture rolls, and a silence about her that everyone in the compound observed with the ease of long practice. Drowned, they said. Lost off the western shoals in the spring of '79, when the storm took three boats. The sea keeps what she takes.

No body. But then, the sea rarely returned what it took, and no one at Meridian ever seemed to grieve the way grieving people grieved. They grieved the way clerks grieve—a line drawn through a name, a page turned.

Juniper stepped onto the dive ladder and let the water take them.

* * *

Down was a country with its own laws.

At twenty feet the last grey memory of surface light died, and Juniper hung in a darkness so complete it pressed against the eyes like a thumb. They struck the signal once on their helmet—two taps, *I am well*—and felt the answering tap travel down the lifeline from Tam's hands on the boat above. Then they began to work.

They moved by touch and by the map they carried behind their eyes. Every diver built such a map; the good ones built them over years, laying down the reef in the mind's dark the way a blind man learns a room. The eastern spur ran out in a shelf of coral broken into terraces, and on the second terrace the pearl beds lay in their troughs of sand, the oysters clustered thick as coins spilled from a torn purse. Juniper's fingers read the bottom the way a clerk's fingers read a ledger: here, a healthy bed; there, a patch stripped bare by last month's greed; there, the pale scar of dynamite some fool had used years ago, before the company learned that dead reef paid no dividends.

Forty shell. Juniper filled the mesh bag at their hip methodically, feeling each oyster's weight, judging by heft alone whether it was worth the space. The work emptied the mind. That was the mercy of it, and the cruelty. Down here there was nothing but the next oyster, the pull of current, the small animal sounds of one's own breath cycling through the helmet—the sound of being alive, reduced to machinery.

It was on the third terrace, reaching into a crevice where the big ones hid, that Juniper's hand found the wrong thing.

Not coral. Not stone. Rope.

Old rope, weed-furred, knotted. And attached to the rope—Juniper's fingers walked along it with the terrible patience of habit—heavy wood. An iron ring. A length of chain gone to lace. It took a long moment for the shape to assemble itself in the dark, and when it did, Juniper went very still inside the helmet, so still that their own heartbeat sounded like someone knocking at a distant door.

An anchor stone. A small one, the kind skiffs carried. Still bent around the rotted stem-post of a boat that was mostly shadow now, mostly suggestion, its planks softened into the sediment of two decades.

Juniper knew that wreck. Not from any chart—they had never been permitted near the company charts—but from the map behind their eyes, because they had found it once before, eleven years ago, and had told no one, and had never dived this terrace again until tonight, because Wren had ordered the eastern spur and Wren did not accept refusal.

The western shoals, they said. She was lost off the western shoals.

But boats drift. Storms move things. Juniper had told themselves that for eleven years, the way you press a thumb against a splinter to keep it from working deeper. Boats drift, currents carry, and a wreck on the eastern spur proves nothing except that the sea is careless with what it keeps.

Their fingers lingered on the iron ring a moment longer than the work allowed. Then they withdrew, closed the bag, and tapped the helmet twice. *I am well.* The lie traveled up the line as smoothly as truth ever had.

* * *

The trouble started on the ascent.

Later, Juniper would reconstruct it piece by piece, the way they reconstructed everything—not to feel it, but to know it. The slack tide had come early; the current over the spur had reversed without warning, dragging the cold deep water up and across the beds in a wall. Such walls carried silt. Silt meant blindness. And somewhere in that blindness, Junifer's air line had snagged—on the wreck's standing rigging, perhaps, or on coral they had passed a hundred times—and the line had gone taut as a hangman's rope.

The first tug, they ignored. Lines tugged; that was their nature. The second tug lifted them half off the bottom, and the third swung them sideways into the terrace wall with a clang that rang through the helmet like a church bell.

Then the valve.

Juniper felt it before they understood it—a change in the breath, a thinness, the way bread thins when the baker stretches the dough too far. The snagged line had fouled the valve joint, wrenching it half-closed, and the air coming down to them was coming in a thread. In the dark, in the silt-blind black, thirty feet above the nearest help, Juniper Lockridge began to suffocate with the patience of the very experienced.

They did not panic. This was not courage; it was training, laid down in childhood by a woman whose face they could no longer fully summon. *Panic spends air,* she had said—or they believed she had said; the memory had worn thin as the rope on the wreck—*and spent air is spent life. Breathe slow. Think slow. The sea rewards the slow.*

Breathe slow. Hands to the valve joint. Feel the kink, feel the angle of the foul. Work it gently—wrenching would tear the hose and end everything at once. Count. One, two, three, four. The thread of air thinning further. Five, six. The edges of the dark going soft, going kind, going sweet, and knowing—that was the horror of experience—that sweetness was the first symptom of the end.

Seven. Eight. The joint gave a quarter-turn. Air rushed in, sudden as forgiveness, and Juniper hung in the black water and breathed like a man rescued from drowning who has forgotten how drowning works, and hated themselves for every greedy swallow.

They cleared the foul on the third attempt, freed the line, and made the surface with a bag of fifty-one shell and a story already composed: current, snag, delay, quota met. Fifty-one shell. More than quota. A good night, by the books.

* * *

Wren was waiting at the rail when they came up the ladder, and he was holding the tally book.

"You're late," he said. Not angry. Wren was almost never angry; anger was expenditure, and Aldous Wren wasted nothing. He was a dry, neat man, grey at the temples at forty-five, with small hands that were always either folded or writing. "Slack water came eight minutes ahead of the almanac. You cut your margin."

"The current fouled my line." Juniper pushed back the helmet, and the night air hit their wet face, cool and enormous after the copper prison. "Snagged on the spur. I cleared it."

Something flickered behind Wren's small eyes—the quick arithmetic he performed on every statement every diver made. Weighing the claim against the tide tables, against the character of the speaker, against whatever use the claim might serve. Juniper had watched him do it for twenty years and had long ago concluded that Wren believed nothing anyone said and disbelieved nothing either. He simply filed.

"Fouled line," he repeated. He wrote it in the tally book in his small exact hand, and Juniper watched the words go down—*delay, equipment fault, diver's report unverified*—and understood that even their survival tonight was now an entry pending audit. "You'll want to inspect your valve in the morning. Company property, that hose. Damage comes off your account."

"I know how it works."

"I know you know." Wren closed the book. And then he did the thing that unsettled Juniper more than any threat could have: he looked at them. Not at the diver, not at the debt, but at them, standing dripping on his deck, and for a moment his face did something almost gentle. "You dove the third terrace tonight. Nobody's worked the third terrace since Okonkwo retired."

"Maren Okonkwo dove the western beds."

"She dove wherever she was sent, same as you." Wren turned toward the wheelhouse, then paused, silhouetted against his shuttered lantern. "There's a new regulation coming with the new century. Inspections at the harbor, at customs, at the archive. The directors are nervous about—irregularities. Old accounts being reopened by troublemakers." His voice stayed mild as milk. "I'd hate to see anyone I've invested in mixed up with irregularities, Lockridge. You've got four hundred pounds left on your contract. At your rate, that's nine more years. Nine years is nothing, if you keep your head down and your mouth shut."

"I always do."

"Yes," Wren said softly. "You always do. That's why you're still alive, and why I intend to keep you that way."

He went into the wheelhouse and shut the door, and the boat turned for home.

* * *

The divers' quarters at Meridian compound were long sheds of salt-bleached timber, divided into cells barely wider than a bunk, and Juniper's cell held everything they owned: a spare shirt, a tin of lamp oil, a hairbrush with three bristles left, and a photograph wrapped in oilcloth that they had not unwrapped in six years because the last time they had unwrapped it, the face had refused to resolve, and the grief of that failure had lasted a month.

Tonight, dried and fed on the company's ration rice and salt fish, they sat on the bunk with the lamp turned low and let their hands remember the night instead of their mind. Hands remembered honestly. Hands said: *iron ring, rotted stem-post, eastern spur.* Hands said: *the valve fouled at the exact moment you touched that wreck, as if the sea herself objected to your visiting.*

Or—as if someone wanted the third terrace dived and wanted it known what lay there. The thought arrived uninvited and sat down like a guest. Wren had ordered the eastern spur. Wren, who checked tide tables against almanacs and divers' reports against tides, who wasted nothing and forgot nothing. Had Wren sent them to the third terrace on purpose? To what end? To test whether they would report the wreck? To confirm that Juniper, of all divers, would recognize—

No. That was the exhaustion talking. Wren was a clerk with a whip, not a conjurer. The eastern spur was simply the richest unworked bed within range, and rich beds were what kept the *Grace*'s manifests fat and Wren's superiors content.

Juniper unwrapped the oilcloth after all. They didn't decide to; their hands did it, the way hands do things at the end of nights that nearly ended.

The photograph was small, sepia going brown at the edges: a woman in a diving dress seated on a dock, helmet off, held under one arm like a captured moon. Her hair was braided tight for the water. Her face was turned three-quarters away, laughing at someone outside the frame, and it was precisely that turned-away quality that had defeated Juniper these last years. They could recall the laugh—deep, unhurried, a laugh that came from the chest of a woman who feared very little—but the face itself had become a shoreline seen in fog. There had been a scar, they thought, near the left eyebrow. Or was that Maren? Was that someone from the stories?

Her name was written on the back in pencil, faded: *Sereia Lockridge, Eastern Beds, 1878.*

One year before she was lost. One year before the green ledger opened and the debt began.

Juniper wrapped the photograph away again and lay back on the bunk and listened to the compound settle around them—the creak of drying racks, the far-off clang of the night watch, the sea working at the seawall with its patient, endless filing motion. Somewhere out past the wall, on the third terrace, an anchor stone held a dead boat to the bottom, and the boat held its secret, and the secret held its date, and the date was 1879, and no one living had ever explained to Juniper why a woman lost off the western shoals should leave her anchor east of the spur.

*The sea keeps what she takes.* But the sea had given this back, tonight, into Juniper's own hands, and the question was what the sea expected in return.

They slept badly, and woke before dawn to hammering on the shed door—Tam Osei, rain streaming off his hat, monsoon weather arriving weeks early, the whole sky come down at once.

"Get up," Tam said, and his voice was wrong, tight, urgent in a way Tam's voice never was. "There's a child at your door. Been standing there half the night by the look of her. Says—" He stopped, wiped the rain from his face, and looked almost embarrassed. "Says she's got something for you. A compass and a paper. Says her mother told her to find you."

Juniper stood very still in the doorway, rain beginning to soak their shoulders, the word *mother* ringing in the wet air like the bell-clang of a helmet against coral.

"Whose child?" they asked.

"That's the thing." Tam lowered his voice, glancing back toward the compound lane. "Nobody knows. She wasn't here yesterday. And Lockridge—" he hesitated, "—she's got your family look about her. Around the eyes."
