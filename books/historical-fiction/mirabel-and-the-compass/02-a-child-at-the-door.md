## 2. A Child at the Door

The monsoon came in off the strait like something owed.

Juniper heard it before they saw it—a sound like a thousand wet sails filling at once, the palm fronds along Company Row snapping upright, and then the sky simply let go. Rain hammered the corrugated roof of the divers' quarters hard enough to make speech useless, which suited Juniper fine. Speech was a currency they had learned early never to spend without cause.

They were mending a mask strap by the light of a guttering oil lamp when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a fist the size of a pear makes against wood.

Juniper went still, needle suspended mid-stitch. Nobody knocked on their door. In nineteen years at the Meridian compound, nobody had ever knocked on Juniper Lockridge's door—not friends, because Juniper had none worth the word; not company men, who entered as they pleased; not neighbors, who had long ago concluded that the quiet one who dove deeper than anyone and said less than anyone preferred it that way. A knock meant either a mistake or an emergency, and both were dangerous.

The knock came again. Then, faint beneath the rain's percussion, a voice—high, cracked, determined: "I know somebody's there. I can see the lamp."

Juniper set down the needle. They took up the boat knife instead, slid it into the sheath at their hip out of habit rather than intent, and opened the door a hand's width.

A child stood on the stoop, drowning where she stood.

She could not have been more than six, though hunger and rain made age impossible to read precisely. She was soaked past any help the weather could do her—hair plastered flat to her skull, a shift that had once been white clinging gray to narrow shoulders, bare feet blue-white and bleeding at one heel from what must have been a long walk over coral shingle. She held something against her chest the way a diver holds a last breath: arms crossed tight, chin down, protecting it.

Her eyes found Juniper's and did not flinch. That was the first strange thing. Children looked away from Juniper—adults too, often. This one stared as if she had been sent to stare, as if staring were the whole of her errand.

"Are you Juniper?" she asked. Her voice had the rounded vowels of the harbor quarter, but underneath them something else—something northern, island-borne, a lilt Juniper's body recognized before their mind could object.

"Yes," Juniper said, because lying to a half-drowned child seemed beneath even the company's standards, and stepped back to let her in.

* * *

She moved through the doorway like water finding its level—quick, silent, already scanning the single room with the practiced economy of someone who had learned to take inventory of exits. Juniper noted that. Noted also how she placed herself with her back to the wall, the lamp between herself and the door, the bundle still clutched to her chest.

"You're bleeding," Juniper said.

"It stopped." She lifted one foot to show, matter-of-fact, the crusted line across her heel. "I wrapped it in a leaf but the leaf came off."

Juniper fetched the basin, the rag, the tin of coconut oil kept for split lips and rope burns. Kneeling to tend the small foot, they felt the child watching them with an attention that had nothing childlike in its weight.

"You're the one from the deep boats," she said. It was not a question. "The woman at the fish stall said so. She said if I told you the sea sent me, you'd open the door."

"And if I hadn't?"

"She said you would."

There was no answer to that, so Juniper cleaned the cut in silence. The child bore it without wincing, which told Juniper something else about her history—she had been hurt before, and alone, and had learned that complaint changed nothing.

"I'm Sefa," she offered, after a while. "Sefa Vai. My—" and here her voice did something complicated, folding around a word and setting it down carefully elsewhere, "—the woman who kept me, she said to come here. She said your name three times so I wouldn't lose it. Juniper Lockridge. Juniper Lockridge. Juniper Lockridge." She recited it like a mooring knot, tied three times for safety. "Then she put me in the mail boat and told me to forget her face."

The rag went still in Juniper's hand.

"Forget her face," Sefa repeated, with the grave precision of a child quoting scripture she did not understand. "That's what she said. Forget the face that left you. She made me say it back. But I didn't." The defiance that rose in her small face then was total and ancient. "I remember it exactly. I practice every night so I don't lose it. So I can find her again."

Outside, the rain redoubled, drumming the roof like applause from a crowd that wanted blood. Juniper knelt there on the floorboards with a stranger's child, holding a rag, feeling something move in their chest that they had spent two decades bricking over—the particular ache of hearing one's own instruction spoken back in a smaller voice.

Because someone had said those words to Juniper once. Or so a shape without features suggested, deep below memory's safe waterline, where things sank that were never recovered. *Forget.* The word itself arrived with a taste attached—salt, and lamp smoke, and the specific terror of being small in a room where adults were deciding something.

"What's her name," Juniper heard themselves ask. "The woman who kept you."

Sefa's face closed like a hand closing. "I'm not supposed to tell anybody. If I tell, they'll find her." She hugged the bundle tighter. "But she knew you'd ask. She said you'd be the only one who'd understand why I can't."

Juniper sat back on their heels. Through the window, lightning printed the harbor in white for a half-second—the masts, the company crane, the black hills—and then took the image away again, leaving afterimage ghosts swimming.

"You're freezing," Juniper said, and got up to find the dry blanket.

* * *

It was while the child lay cocooned in wool, shivering through the first stage of warming, that she finally surrendered her cargo.

"He said to give it only to you," she said, sitting up. "The man on the mail boat. He wasn't kind, but he wasn't unkind either. He watched me the whole way like I might turn into something." She worked the bundle loose, layer by layer, with the care of someone unwrapping something that had been described to her as important in terms she only half believed. "He said if anyone stopped us, I was to throw it in the sea. He said the sea keeps secrets better than people do."

Inside the wrapping were three things.

The first was a brass compass, old, its case worn smooth at the edges by years of handling, its glass clouded at the rim with salt bloom. It was heavy in a way cheap instruments are not. Juniper held it and felt their own pulse knock against it, once, twice, and stop pretending to be calm.

They knew this compass.

Not this exact compass—they had never seen this compass—but *this compass*, the way one knows a face in a dream that turns out, years later, to have been real. Its weight, its size, the particular dark patina the brass took on in seawater air. Their hands remembered a compass like this being held above their head, out of reach, turned in laughing fingers while someone sang. The memory had no face. It had never been allowed a face. But it rose now all the same, whole and sudden, like a body coming up from depth too fast.

Juniper set it down on the table very carefully, the way one sets down something that might go off.

The second thing was paper. A single page, torn—not cut, torn—from a bound volume, the ragged left edge showing the ghost-prints of stitching. The paper was good stock, company stock, the heavy cream-colored sheets Juniper had seen only once a year at contract renewal, when the foreman laid them out and waited with patient menace for a mark. Someone had torn this from a ledger. Someone had torn it cleanly enough to keep the columns intact.

And the third thing was a note, folded twice, soft at the creases from handling.

Juniper unfolded it under the lamp.

The handwriting was cramped and quick, letters leaning forward as if hurrying somewhere:

*Forget the face that left her. Burn this after reading. The compass will tell you whose she is. The page will tell you whose fault. Trust the diver named Maren. Trust no one else. — You know my hand. You have always known my hand.*

Juniper read it twice.

Then a third time, slower, because the second reading had done something to the floor, which no longer seemed entirely level.

*You know my hand.*

It was true. That was the horror of it. Juniper did know the hand—the forward lean, the way the *f* descended into a long deliberate flourish, the numeral habit of crossing sevens. It was a hand Juniper had watched write their name into a book when they were seven years old. It was a hand that had written the indenture contract, the ration chits, the annual assessment that read, year after year, *Lockridge, J. — compliant. Silent. No dependents.*

It was the hand of Aldous Wren, foreman of the Meridian Trading Company.

* * *

The lamp hissed. Rain filled the silence the way water fills a drowned hold—from everywhere at once.

Juniper stood at the table for a long time without moving. Behind them, the child's breathing had gone slow and even, sleep taking her the way it takes children anywhere, indifferent to catastrophe. Juniper listened to it the way a diver listens for a second heartbeat in dark water—for confirmation they were not alone in the deep.

Wren's hand. Wren's writing. On a note warning Juniper against trusting everyone—which must mean, since he named himself nowhere, that Wren was not the one sending it. Which was worse, somehow. Which meant someone else wrote like Wren, or Wren wrote warnings against himself, or—

No. Juniper made themselves think it straight, the way they thought through a dive plan, one element at a time, no breath wasted on panic.

Someone had access to company ledgers. Someone had torn a page—one page, surgically, the way a surgeon takes a tumor—rather than steal the book. Someone knew the compound, knew the mail boats, knew the harbor quarter well enough to route a six-year-old through a fishmonger to Juniper's door. And someone knew Juniper's hand in the sense that mattered most: knew what Juniper had lost, knew the name Juniper was forbidden to carry, knew that a child delivered with a compass would undo twenty years of trained forgetting faster than any amount of gold or threats.

*The compass will tell you whose she is. The page will tell you whose fault.*

Juniper picked up the torn page and held it to the lamp.

Columns. Dates. Figures in a clerk's meticulous script, amounts in pounds and shillings, entries running down the page in the company's merciless tidy order. Most of it meant nothing without the surrounding book—it was a limb severed from a body. But near the top, boxed and initialed, was an entry type Juniper had never seen but understood instantly, the way one understands a reef shelf ending in open blue:

**WRITE-OFF — DIVER, FEMALE — CONTRACT VOID BY DEATH (DROWNED). REMAINING TERM SOLD. PROCEEDS ENTERED.**

Below it, a date. Juniper read the year and their knees informed them, quietly, that the table was now bearing their weight.

It was the year their mother went into the water and did not come out.

The year the company priest said a mass for her with no body to bury. The year the foreman of that day—an earlier foreman, Wren's predecessor—had pressed a coin into young Juniper's palm and said, *the sea takes what the sea is owed, girl-boy, best learn that now.* The year Juniper had learned that grief was a luxury itemized on no ledger, and had begun, methodically, to forget.

Except.

*Contract void by death. Remaining term sold.*

Sold. You did not sell the remaining term of a dead woman's contract. Dead women generated no revenue. Dead women's papers were burned at year's end with ceremony and rum. Unless the death was a fiction. Unless the woman had walked out of the water alive somewhere beyond the company's sightline, and her contract—the flesh and years of her, priced by the season—had passed to some other buyer, and the drowning had been entered in the books not as a tragedy but as an accounting convenience.

Juniper's mother had not drowned. Juniper's mother had been *written off*—converted, on paper, from a person into a profit, erased so thoroughly that even her own child had been issued a story instead of a truth.

The compass sat on the table between the note and the page, brass dull in the lamplight, its needle swinging in tiny restless arcs as if it too were searching for north and finding only this room, this night, this child asleep in a blanket that smelled of salt and old rope.

Juniper looked at the compass and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water closing overhead, that they were looking at their mother's.

And that whoever had sent it—whoever wrote in a foreman's hand and signed with a riddle—had known exactly what it would do.

Behind them, Sefa murmured in her sleep. "Don't go," she said, to no one, to the face she refused to forget. "Come back."

Juniper stood in the lamplight with the evidence of a crime in their hands and the century's last monsoon hammering the roof, and understood that the safe life was over—that it had perhaps been over since the moment three small fists had knocked on the door—and that tomorrow, at first light, they would have to decide what a person does with a truth that has been buried for twenty years under a company flag.

First, though, they would have to read the page properly. Every figure. Every initial. Because somewhere in those neat columns, Juniper was suddenly certain, was a name—and it would not be their mother's.

Names erased never appear where you need them. Only the numbers remain, and the numbers, unlike people, cannot be taught to forget.

Juniper drew the lamp closer and began to read.
