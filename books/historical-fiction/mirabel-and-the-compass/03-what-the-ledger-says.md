## 3. What the Ledger Says

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and the silence it left behind was worse than the storm. Juniper Lockridge sat at the scarred table in the single room they had lived in for nineteen years—a room the company owned, down to the nails in the floorboards—and turned a torn page of ledger paper over in their hands.

The child slept in the hammock strung in the corner, or pretended to. Sefa's breathing was too even for true sleep. Juniper knew that breathing; it was the breath of someone listening hard, the way divers breathed when the foreman walked the deck above them counting heads. Counting what belonged to him.

Juniper lit the lamp.

They did not light it often. Oil cost scrip, and scrip cost dives, and every dive took something out of you that the company never entered on its books. But some things required light, and this—this scrap of paper with its water-stained columns and its careful clerkish hand—was one of them.

The brass compass lay beside the lamp, throwing back the flame in a small warped arc. Juniper had not touched it again since the child arrived. They were afraid of it, though they would not have named the feeling as fear. It was too fine a thing to have come from nowhere, too heavy in the hand, too deliberate. Things like that did not wash ashore. Things like that were *sent*.

And the note. *Forget the face that left her.* The handwriting had done something to Juniper's chest when they first read it by lightning—something structural, like a rib cracking inward. They had folded the note away quickly, before Sefa could see their face change. Now it sat in their breast pocket, against their skin, warm as a coal that would not go out.

Because Juniper knew that hand. You do not spend your childhood being taught your letters by a woman who writes supply requisitions for the whole diving crew without learning how she crosses her t's—with a long flat stroke, impatient, like a cut. You do not forget the way she loops her g's, low and generous, like hooks meant to catch something falling.

Twenty years ago, that hand had written the letter that told Juniper their mother was dead.

*Forget the face that left her.*

Juniper unfolded the ledger page instead. That was safer ground. Paper about paper.

* * *

The page was torn from a bound register—the stubs of its binding still clung to the left edge like little torn tendons. The columns were ruled in faded red. Across the top, in the same familiar hand, a heading: **MERIDIAN TRADING CO. — DIVER CONTRACT REGISTER — ADJUSTMENTS & DISPOSITIONS**.

Juniper read the first column twice because the numbers refused to make sense the first time, and then a third time because they refused to make sense the second time, and then they made sense, and Juniper wished very much that they hadn't.

**Entry 44.** A diver's number—not a name, never a name; the register dealt in numbers the way the sea dealt in bodies. **Contract commenced:** a date. **Status: DECEASED — drowning, occupational. Date of record:** the ninth of March, 1879.

Below that, in smaller script, the disposition:

*Write-off approved. Contract value transferred per standing arrangement. Remains not recoverable. Family notified per policy. No further liability attaches to Company.*

No further liability attaches to Company. Juniper had read thousands of lines like that in nineteen years of ledgers—lines written by clerks who had never once put their faces under saltwater, who thought of the sea as an accounting problem with weather. *Remains not recoverable.* It was the phrase used when a body went down past the shelf and no one could be paid enough to fetch it. It meant grief, translated into ink.

But here was the thing that made Juniper's hands go still on the table's edge, both palms flat, pressing down as if the room itself might tilt:

The entry had been amended. Someone—different ink, different pressure, a hand that pressed harder, a man's hand surely—had come along afterward and added a notation in the margin, small and cramped, the kind of writing that does not want to be read:

*Contract sold. Proceeds to H.M. See folio 12.*

Sold. Not closed. Not mourned and filed. *Sold.* A drowned woman's contract—her years, her debt, her labor, the flesh-and-blood hours of her remaining life—had been sold onward, as if death were merely a change of ownership.

Drowned women did not work. Sold contracts implied a living seller and a living buyer, which implied—

Juniper sat back. The lamp flame stood perfectly straight in the dead air, and Juniper found they could not look away from it, because looking back down at the page meant letting the page be true.

*Her contract sold.*

There was a term for this. Juniper had heard it only once, years ago, in the dark between bunks, from an old diver named Pem who drank his scrip and talked too much on the nights before he was too sick to dive. He'd called them *paper drownings*. A diver too old, too injured, too troublesome—too expensive—declared lost at sea. No body, no inquiry, no widow's claim. The company wrote off the contract and quietly sold the remainder to another outfit up the coast, where the diver reappeared under a new number, working off a new debt, legally dead in the eyes of every registry that mattered. Pem had laughed when he said it, the laugh of a man telling a joke he needed to be funnier than it was, and then he'd said, *You didn't hear that from me,* and two winters later Pem himself went down on a routine scallop bed in calm water, alone, and came up as a line item.

Juniper had been fourteen. Juniper had said nothing. Saying nothing was the first skill the sea taught and the company perfected.

But this entry was not Pem's story. This entry was dated the ninth of March, 1879.

Juniper's mother had gone into the water—or failed to come up from it—in the spring of 1879.

* * *

The child moved in the hammock. Rope creaked. Juniper's hand shot out and covered the page, palm flat over Entry 44, the way you'd cover a flame against wind.

"I'm awake," Sefa said, in the small precise voice she used for everything, as if each word were a stone she had picked up and inspected before setting down. She climbed down—she was quick, monkey-quick, despite the bruise on her cheekbone going yellow now—and padded across the boards to stand at the table's edge. Her eyes went from the lamp to the compass to the covered page. "Is that my paper?"

"It is."

"You're reading it wrong-side up." She tilted her head. "That's how Mama read. Upside-down and backwards. She said numbers don't care which way you look at them, but people do."

Juniper's mouth was dry. "Your mama read ledgers?"

"She read everything. She read the big books at night when she thought I was asleep. She had beautiful writing." Sefa demonstrated in the air with one finger, sweeping loops. "Like birds sitting on a wire."

Birds on a wire. Long flat crossings. Hooks meant to catch things falling.

"And then one day," Sefa said, still in that inspecting voice, "a boat came, and she went down to it, and a man held my hand too tight, and she looked back and—" the finger stopped mid-loop, "—and I'm supposed to forget. I practice every day. I'm not good at it yet."

"Who told you to forget?"

"A man with a soft voice. He wasn't mean. He gave me sweets and said forgetting is a kindness, for me and for her." Sefa frowned, working at the memory like a knot. "But he said it wrong. He said *forget the face that left you*. And the paper says—" she leaned forward suddenly, jabbing a small finger at Juniper's breast pocket, at the folded note visible at its top, "—that. Forget the face that *left her*. Left *her*. Not left me. So either he lied, or somebody fixed it after, and fixing means it matters, and if it matters then forgetting is a lie, isn't it?"

Juniper stared at her.

Six years old. Trained to forget, and too clear-eyed to swallow the training. Juniper had needed twenty years and a storm-tossed child to ask the question this girl had worked out on her own in the space of a monsoon night.

"Yes," Juniper said. Their voice came out rusted. "Yes, it is."

Sefa nodded once, satisfied, as though a sum had checked. Then she pulled the crate over, climbed onto it, folded her arms on the table's edge, and waited.

Juniper uncovered the page.

* * *

Reading it aloud was different from reading it silently. Silently, the words stayed shapes, stay-able, deniable. Aloud, in the lamplight, in front of a witness, they became facts with weight and edges.

"Diver forty-four," Juniper read. "Contract commenced April, eighteen sixty-one." Eighteen years underwater before the end—that was a long service; most didn't last ten. "Status: deceased. Occupational drowning. Ninth of March, eighteen seventy-nine." Juniper's own voice sounded far away, like it was coming up through fathoms. "Remains not recoverable. Family notified per policy."

"My mama's name isn't in it," Sefa said. "Why isn't her name in it? Numbers aren't people."

"No," Juniper said. "Numbers are what the company uses instead of people."

"But whose number was she?" Sefa pressed, leaning in, and her eyes in the lamplight were dark and absolute, and Juniper understood with a lurch that this child would keep asking until someone answered, that she was constitutionally incapable of the strategic silences that had kept Juniper alive, and that some deep drowned part of Juniper loved her for it already.

Whose number. Juniper looked at the entry. Forty-four. And beneath the amended line, *Proceeds to H.M. See folio 12*, and beneath that, almost invisible unless the light raked across it sideways, a fainter mark—a pencil ghost, erased but not fully, the way things written by a hurried hand can be smudged but not destroyed:

*44 — M.L.*

Two letters. Initials. M.L.

Juniper's mother's name had been Mirabel Lune. Before marriage, before the sea took the Lune and left only the debt—Mirabel Lune, who dove the outer beds when the men wouldn't, who could hold her breath four minutes in cold water, who taught letters by lamplight and crossed her t's like cuts. M.L.

The room did not tilt, exactly. It was subtler than that, and worse: everything in the room stayed exactly where it was—the lamp, the compass, the sleeping storm outside the shutters—and it was Juniper who came loose from all of it, drifting, unmoored, the way a diver comes loose from the guide rope in bad visibility and knows, with perfect calm, that they may never find the bottom or the surface again.

Eighteen sixty-one to eighteen seventy-nine. Eighteen years of contract. Sold, not ended. *Family notified per policy.*

Juniper had been nine years old in the spring of 1879. Juniper remembered the notification: a foreman—not Wren, an earlier one, a gray man with a gray voice—standing at the door of the company barracks saying the words *lost at sea* and *the company extends its sympathies* and *your mother's debts pass to the estate*, which meant, since there was no estate, to the child. Nine years old, and the sympathy of the Meridian Trading Company had taken the form of an indenture signature pressed into wax while the tears were still wet on Juniper's face. They had told Juniper the water took her past the shelf. They had shown Juniper her dive bag, sodden, sand-heavy. They had buried an empty box because the priest insisted on a box.

They had never shown a body. Juniper had spent twenty years telling themself that was the sea's way. The sea keeps what it takes.

But the sea had kept nothing. The ledger said so. The ledger, in a hand Juniper knew better than their own heartbeat, said *write-off*, said *sold*, said *proceeds to H.M.*, and the pencil ghost said *M.L.*, and none of it—not one word—was the language of drowning. It was the language of paperwork. Of arrangement. Of a woman walked to a boat on a March morning while her daughter's hand was held too tight, and a man with a soft voice teaching a six-year-old—no. Teaching a *younger* child, another child, years later—to forget the face that left her.

Wait.

Juniper's mind snagged, tore, caught. Sefa was six. The entry was twenty years old. Whatever woman had walked away from Sefa on that boat, she could not have been the same woman the register declared drowned in 1879—unless the write-off was not one event but a method, a machine that ran for decades, grinding out paper drownings year upon year, and unless the woman in the boat was—

Was what? Alive? Dead twice? Was Juniper's mother the clerk whose handwriting sat on this page, recording other women's disappearances in her own careful loops, birds on a wire, while her own name waited further down the register in someone else's harder hand?

Or had the clerk been the one sold? Had Mirabel Lune sat at this desk, copying dispositions, understanding at last what the columns meant, and then one morning in March been walked to her own boat, and her own entry written up by the man whose initials were H.M.—and the same pen, the same patient hand, later used to notify her family per policy?

*Forget the face that left her.* Left *her*. As if the note's writer were speaking of themself. As if the writer were the face. As if a mother, forced to leave a child, had sent word through twenty years and one monsoon: do not remember me the way they told you to.

The lamp flame wavered—some flaw in the oil, some breath—and in its stutter the compass threw a crooked gleam across the page, right across Entry 44, like a finger pointing.

"Sefa," Juniper said. Very carefully. Keeping the voice level the way you keep a lantern level on a pitching deck. "The woman in the boat. The face you're supposed to forget. Tell me everything about it. Everything you remember, even the pieces that don't fit."

Sefa's brow creased. She was quiet a long moment, and Juniper watched her do the thing she had said she practiced daily—watched her reach for the forgetting and fail, thank every salt god in the deep, *fail*.

"She had a scarf," Sefa said slowly. "Red, with the color gone out of most of it. She held my face in both hands, like this—" she pressed her own small palms to her own cheeks, "—and she said a word I didn't know. She said it twice. Then the man pulled me back and the boat went, and I counted to a hundred, and it was gone." She lowered her hands. "I've been trying to say the word ever since. I think I only heard half of it."

"What half?"

"Bel," Sefa said. "Or maybe it was the whole word. Bel." She looked up, and her dark eyes were terribly steady. "Do you know it?"

Outside, the tide was turning. Juniper could hear it in the harbor's throat, that long slow exhale the water makes when it gives back what it has decided to return.

"Bel," Juniper repeated, and the syllable broke open in their mouth like a seed pod, spilling twenty years of carefully packed dark into the lamplight. *Mirabel.* Half a name, carried by a child across half a coastline, delivered to the exact door where it could do the most damage. This was not chance. Chance did not write in familiar hands. Chance did not tear pages precisely. Somebody had built this—had aimed this child at this room like a bolt fired through the storm.

Which meant somebody believed Juniper could be reached. Which meant somebody knew what Juniper had spent two decades making sure no one knew: that under the strategic silences and the flawless dives and the obedient blankness, some part of Juniper Lockridge had never once believed the sea took their mother.

The ground was gone. Juniper sat in the middle of the absence where the ground had been and found, strangely, that they could breathe there.

"Get your shoes," they heard themself say. "We can't stay past tomorrow. If this page is real, the company will already know it's missing—and they'll want to know who has it."

"Where are we going?" Sefa asked, already moving, obedient and unafraid, which frightened Juniper more than any question.

"To see an old woman who dives no longer," Juniper said, folding the ledger page along its torn edge and slipping it inside their shirt, next to the note, next to the heat of it all. "And who once swam beside a woman whose name began with the other half of *bel*."

Sefa paused at the door, one hand on the latch, and looked back at the compass lying in its pool of lamplight.

"That's hers, isn't it," she said. Not a question. "The face. The word. All of it. It's yours too."

Juniper pinched out the flame, and in the sudden dark answered honestly, for the first time in twenty years:

"Yes."
