## 4. The Foreman's Kindness

The knock came at dawn, and it came soft.

That was the first wrong thing. In twenty years of the Meridian compound, no one had ever knocked on Juniper's door softly. The company did not knock at all; it arrived. Foremen hammered, clerks pounded, the night watch simply opened doors that were never truly locked to begin with. A soft knock was a question, and questions from the Meridian Trading Company always had teeth behind them.

Juniper lay still on the cot and listened.

Behind the curtain strung across the alcove—strung only last night, in the small hours, after Juniper had lain awake cataloguing every sound the child made and finding each one too loud—came the slow, salt-sweet breathing of sleep. Sefa slept like the drowned, utterly, bonelessly, her fists curled near her face as though still holding a compass she'd been told to let go of. The brass compass itself was buried now beneath the third floorboard from the window, wrapped in oilcloth with the torn ledger page, and above the boards sat a clay pot of dried fish that smelled convincingly of nothing but fish.

Juniper rose without sound, crossed the room, and put one eye to the shutter gap.

Aldous Wren stood in the lane outside, alone, holding a canvas sack.

Juniper's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. Wren was the compound's foreman, which meant he was its weather: everything bent around him, nothing grew without his permission. He was not a large man. That had fooled people once, decades ago, before they understood that small men who never raised their voices had learned other ways to be heard. He wore his grey coat buttoned to the throat despite the wet heat, and his hands hung at his sides with a patience that looked almost gentle until you remembered where those hands had been—counting divers down the ladder, counting them back up, marking the difference in a book.

He knocked again, soft as rain.

Juniper considered the window at the rear, the drainage ditch beyond it, the mangroves. Considered how far a six-year-old could run before sunrise, barefoot, in mud, with a stranger's hand clamped over her mouth to keep her quiet. Not far enough. Not fast enough.

So Juniper did what twenty years had trained them to do. They smoothed their face into the shape the company preferred—attentive, empty, slightly tired—and opened the door.

"Foreman."

"Lockridge." Wren's voice was dry paper. His eyes went past Juniper's shoulder into the single room, unhurried, thorough, the way a man reads a ledger line by line. Juniper watched him read it: the cot, the cold lamp, the curtain over the alcove, the pot of fish. "You look like you didn't sleep."

"The monsoon kept me up."

"Mm." He held out the canvas sack. "Rice. Salt pork. Some of the good tea from the office stores—the leaves the clerks drink when they want to feel important. There's also a tin of condensed milk. I'm told children like it."

The world tilted very slightly, the way a boat tilts when something moves underneath it that you cannot see.

"I don't have children," Juniper said.

"No," Wren agreed. "You don't."

* * *

He stepped inside uninvited, because of course he did; the concept of invitation had died somewhere in this compound long before Juniper was born. He set the sack on the table, squared its corners precisely, and then—took off his hat, which he had never done in Juniper's presence in two decades. It was such an ordinary gesture of courtesy that it landed harder than any threat would have.

"You've been with us since you were six yourself," Wren said, turning the hat slowly in his hands. "Signed over by your mother's estate—or what the company recorded as her estate, which was a debt and a diving knife. Twenty years, Lockridge. Do you know how rare that is? Most indentures run out or drown or run. You've done none of those things. You're the steadiest diver I have." He looked up, and there was something in his face that in another man, another life, might have been called warmth. "I notice my steady ones. I protect them."

"Thank you, Foreman." The words came out flat and correct, the way coins come out of a purse counted beforehand.

"There have been developments this week." Wren set the hat down on the table beside the sack, aligning its brim with the table edge, and Juniper understood suddenly that every object in this room was being arranged into a sentence he intended to read later. "A boat came through the outer channel three nights ago during the storm. Small craft, no lights, running dark. The watch saw it and lost it in the rain. Yesterday morning, one of the drying-house women reported hearing a child crying near the north lanes. Last night someone left a lamp burning past curfew in this quarter."

Juniper kept their breathing even. Behind the curtain, Sefa slept on, and Juniper prayed to whatever governed drowned mothers and monsoons that the child would not stir, would not sigh, would not do the small snuffling thing she did when she dreamed.

"Troubled times make for strange reports," Juniper said.

"They do. And here is the trouble with them." Wren picked up the tin of condensed milk, turned it over, read its label as if it contained instructions relevant to the conversation. "Strange reports attract inspectors. Century-turn inspections, they're calling them—every checkpoint from the harbor to the customs house doubled, every manifest re-read, every name cross-checked against the old registers. The founders are anxious about their legacy going into the new century. Anxious men audit everything." He set the tin down. "Anxious men also ask why a diver with no family receives rations meant for dependents."

"I didn't ask for rations."

"No. You didn't. That's what makes this a kindness and not a transaction." He smiled, briefly, and it was the most frightening expression Juniper had ever seen him wear. "But kindness obligates, Lockridge. That's what kindness is *for*. So I'll say plainly what I'd otherwise spend all morning circling: there are troublemakers moving through the islands this season. People who dig up old papers, old grievances. People who tell divers that the company owes them things. If such people came to your door—if a child appeared at your door in the night, say, planted by such people to compromise my steadiest diver—you would bring her to me. Immediately. Because anything else looks like complicity, and complicity, at century's end, gets audited."

The silence stretched. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere across the compound the first shift bell rang, thin and far away, and neither of them moved toward it.

"And if no child came to my door?" Juniper asked.

Wren collected his hat. He settled it on his head with both hands, carefully, the way a priest settles a crown.

"Then eat well," he said. "Dive well. And know that I am watching out for you. It's what I do best."

* * *

He left. Juniper stood with a hand flat against the door and listened to his footsteps diminish down the lane—not hurrying, never hurrying, pausing once, twice, at intervals Juniper could map blindfolded: at the well, at the corner by the net-mender's shed, at the gate where the day's tally board hung. At each pause, Juniper knew, he was looking back. Reading the street. Reading the door. Reading the smoke that wasn't rising yet from a chimney because Juniper dared not light a stove with a child hidden three feet from the flue.

When the footsteps faded entirely, Juniper crossed to the alcove and drew back the curtain.

Sefa was awake. She had been awake for some time—her eyes said so, wide and dark and fixed on Juniper's face with the terrible patience of children who have already learned that adults lie by moving their mouths. She hadn't made a sound. Not through Wren's voice, not through the mention of her, not through the word *child* spoken twice in a room barely wider than a coffin.

"You heard," Juniper said quietly. It wasn't a question.

Sefa shook her head, then stopped, then nodded, then seemed to decide honesty was the safer current. "I heard a man giving you food."

"Yes."

"My mama said never take food from men who smile with their mouths only." She recited it like a lesson, flat and exact, and something behind Juniper's ribs cracked along an old seam. "She said it the last day. Before the boat."

Juniper crouched to her height. Up close the child smelled of salt and sleep and the tallow Juniper had rubbed into her chapped knuckles the night before. Her hair was still full of grit from wherever she had walked from. There was a bruise going yellow-green along her jaw that she had not explained and Juniper had not yet asked about, because asking was a door that swung both ways.

"Sefa. Listen to me carefully, because this part matters more than anything I will ever say to you." Juniper took both her small hands. "The man who came here—he knows about you. Maybe not your name, maybe not your face, but he knows a child exists somewhere in these lanes, and he is going to keep knowing until either he finds you or we make him stop looking."

"Are you going to give me to him?"

"No."

"You could." It was not an accusation. It was arithmetic, done in a six-year-old's voice. "One less mouth. One less trouble. He gave you rice for it."

The honesty of it went through Juniper like a harpoon. This was what the company made, eventually, out of everyone it touched: creatures who understood themselves as entries in a ledger, debits that could be cleared. Juniper had been that creature for twenty years and had thought the training was silence. It wasn't. The training was this—this child's calm certainty that she could be sold.

"Listen to me." Juniper tightened their grip, careful, careful, around those small salt-cracked fingers. "I was signed over to that man's company when I was exactly your age. Six. Someone wrote my name in a book and after that, everyone treated me like a thing that could be bought. I spent my whole life learning to be quiet so the book would leave me alone." Their voice did something they did not permit it to do—it roughened. "I am not writing your name in any book. Not his. No one's. Do you understand me?"

Sefa studied Juniper's face for a long moment, the way she studied everything, as though memorizing it against a future loss. Then she nodded once, businesslike, and asked the practical question, the diver's question, the question that proved she had been born into the same hard school:

"Then where do we hide?"

* * *

They hid her the way water hides itself in water.

Not under the floorboards—Wren's eyes had rested on that pot of fish too long, and a man who arranged a hat brim with the table edge noticed pots. Instead Juniper rebuilt the child's whole existence in a single morning, working with the speed of someone who has rehearsed disaster in the mind so many times the rehearsal has worn grooves.

The loft above the drying racks behind the compound's cooperage was accessed by a ladder no adult used, being too narrow for grown shoulders and too rickety for anyone with weight to spare. Juniper tested every rung, reinforced two with cord, laid sacking over the gaps in the slats so the light falling through would show nothing but dust. A jar of water. Hardtack. The tin of condensed milk—let Wren's own gift feed the secret it hunted; there was a justice in that Juniper intended to enjoy. A bucket, lidded. Instructions delivered in a whisper: no singing, no talking aloud, if anyone comes below, breathe through the mouth and slow.

"Why slow?" Sefa whispered.

"Because fast breathing sounds like fear, and fear sounds like a person. Slow breathing sounds like the building settling."

Sefa practiced. Three breaths, four, five—long and even, her small chest rising like a swell—and Juniper watched a six-year-old master in ninety seconds the discipline that had taken them years, and felt grief and pride arrive together, indistinguishable.

By midmorning the loft was empty-looking and the child was invisible inside it, and Juniper walked back across the compound wearing the face the company liked: attentive, empty, slightly tired.

* * *

The tally board at the gate had been updated overnight. New columns. Juniper read them while pretending to study the dive assignments: *Century Inspection — All Contracts Subject to Review — Report Irregular Persons to Foreman Wren.* Below that, in smaller print, the phrase that turned Juniper's mouth to chalk: *Registers 1860–1899 open to audit.*

Twenty years. Every name. Every contract. Every write-off.

And somewhere in those registers, if the page Sefa carried spoke true, was a woman declared drowned while her lungs still worked—a woman whose contract had been sold onward like a bolt of cloth, whose child had been entered into service the same week, whose name Juniper had been taught, gently, thoroughly, over years, to forget.

Juniper found Maren Okonkwo's granddaughter at the net sheds and passed along a message wrapped in nothing but ordinariness: *Tell your grandmother the tide's low Thursday, and I'll bring the tea leaves the clerks drink.* Whether Maren would understand the summons, whether she would dare answer it—unknown. But Maren had dove beside the woman whose name Juniper couldn't say, and there were debts between old divers that ran deeper than company books.

The afternoon brought the second visit.

Not Wren this time. Two of his tally-clerks, young men with ink-stained fingers and apologetic spines, going door to door through the north lanes with a list. *Census,* they said. *Century census. Names and numbers of all persons resident.* They reached Juniper's doorway at dusk, and Juniper answered the door with a lamp lit deliberately, generously, flooding the little room with honest light: cot, table, cold hearth, curtain over the alcove.

"Residents?" the elder clerk asked, pen ready.

"One. Juniper Lockridge. Diver, second class. You'll find me on your list already—I've been on it twenty years."

"We're verifying." He peered past Juniper's shoulder. Juniper let him peer, standing easy, arms loose, a tired diver at the end of a long day with nothing in the world to hide. The lamp threw their shadow big and placid against the wall. Behind the curtain: nothing. The alcove held firewood and a broken crate and forty years of a solitary life's clutter. Sefa herself was thirty feet away and fifteen feet up, breathing slow, sounding like a building settling.

"No dependents? No kin staying?"

"Kin," Juniper repeated, and heard their own voice come out with just the right grain of dry amusement. "The sea takes kin, clerk. It doesn't send them."

He wrote it down. They moved on. Their footsteps merged with the evening sounds of the compound, hammers and gulls and the shift bell, and Juniper closed the door and stood in the lamplight with their heart going like a hammer on a hull.

It had worked. Today it had worked.

But Wren had said *registers open to audit*, and Wren had given rations to a household with no dependents, and Wren aligned hat brims to table edges because he intended to come back and check whether anything had moved. Kindness here was a lantern shone into corners. The question was never whether he was watching. The question was what he'd already seen.

Juniper pinched out the lamp, climbed into the loft in the dark, and sat beside the small warm shape of the sleeping child, listening to the compound settle around them.

Tomorrow, the ledger page. Tomorrow, the compass. Tomorrow, finding out what Maren Okonkwo knew about a drowning that never happened—and whether an old diver's loyalty ran deeper than her fear.

Above the cooperage, Sefa breathed slow and even, like the building settling, and dreamed of a boat, and a woman's face she had been ordered to forget.
