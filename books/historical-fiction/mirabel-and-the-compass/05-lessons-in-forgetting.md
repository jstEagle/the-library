## 5. Lessons in Forgetting

Sefa counted things when she was scared. The woman who left her had taught her that.

*Count the waves until they stop being loud. Count to a hundred and your hands will stop shaking.* The woman's voice lived inside Sefa's head like a bird in a roof, sometimes quiet, sometimes not quiet at all.

Now she was counting floorboards.

The tall person — Juniper — had said *stay* and Sefa was good at staying. Staying was easy. Staying was like being furniture. She had been furniture before, in the room with the green door, for two whole days while men shouted on the dock about cargo and someone wept in a way that sounded like laughing but wasn't.

Forty-one boards from the door to the window. Forty-two if you counted the cracked one twice, which Sefa did, because the crack made it feel like it deserved extra.

She sat cross-legged on board thirty-seven and held the brass compass in both hands. It was warm now, warm from her pockets and her palms, and the needle swung in its little glass house, pointing and pointing at nothing she could see. North, Juniper had said. But north was just a word that meant *that way* to somebody else. To Sefa the needle pointed at the sea, which was everywhere here, so mostly it pointed at everything.

That seemed useless. She liked it anyway.

* * *

Here were the things Sefa remembered. She had made a list in her head, because lists stayed better than pictures did. Pictures faded like wet ink. Words you could say over and over and they kept their shape.

One: a face. A woman with skin the color of tea when you put too much milk in, which Sefa knew because once she'd spilled the whole cup trying to pour for herself and nobody had hit her, only sighed. The woman's eyes were dark and one of them had a little brown star in it, a fleck, like a freckle that got lost and swam up into her eye instead. Sefa had looked at that eye a long time. That was why she remembered it. You remembered things you looked at long enough. That was a rule she'd figured out all by herself.

Two: a boat. Not a big boat. A boat with paint coming off in curls, blue under gray under blue again, like the boat had gotten dressed in the dark three times. There was a name painted on the side but part of it had flaked away, so Sefa only ever saw the end: *—mira*. Or maybe *—miral*. She used to make up the beginning. *Alamira. Kamira. Samira.* She said them all in her head like they were different boats, and maybe somewhere they all existed, and maybe somewhere they were all going somewhere nice.

Three: hands. The woman's hands were rough on top and soft underneath, which Sefa knew because the woman had held her face once, both sides, like Sefa was something breakable and precious, like a bird egg or the glass house of a compass. Rough on top. Soft underneath. She could still feel it if she tried. Sometimes she tried too hard and then it went away faster, which didn't seem fair, but most things weren't.

Four: the words.

*Forget the face that left you.*

Those weren't the woman's words. Sefa knew that the way you know thunder isn't the rain, even though they come together. The woman's voice went up and down like walking over hills. This voice was flat, like a road, like the men on the dock, like the sound a shutter makes when it slams. A man's voice or a tired voice; Sefa couldn't tell which, and it bothered her more than almost anything, because how could she forget properly if she couldn't even remember what forgetting sounded like?

*Forget the face that left you. It will be easier.*

It hadn't been easier. It had been the hardest thing anyone had ever asked her, harder than carrying water, harder than being quiet during the bad night, harder than holding still while the doctor looked in her ears with his cold metal spoon.

And here was the worst thing, the thing Sefa told no one, the thing she kept down deep where even her list couldn't reach it:

She didn't want to forget.

Everyone said forgetting would be a kindness. Forget the face that left you. Forget the boat. Forget the rough-soft hands. Then you wouldn't be sad anymore. But when Sefa imagined not remembering — really tried to imagine it, the way you try to imagine the back of your own head — it felt like drowning. Not the scary kind of drowning from stories. The other kind, the slow kind, where the water comes up gentle and warm and you fall asleep and never wake up and everyone says what a peaceful way to go, and Sefa thought, no, thank you, she would rather be cold and awake and sad than asleep forever.

Sad meant the face had been real.

If she forgot, it would be like the face had never been real at all.

* * *

Juniper came back in the grey part of afternoon with bread and dried fish wrapped in paper, and moved around the small room doing the things people do — lighting the lamp, latching the shutter against the wind that was starting to push at the walls. Monsoon weather gathering itself out past the harbor like a big animal deciding whether to come in.

"Did you stay?" Juniper asked.

"Fifty-eight boards," Sefa said. "I mean forty-two. I counted some twice."

"That's staying."

"You said don't go out and don't look out the window and don't talk loud."

"All true."

"I did none of those." She hesitated. "I talked normal."

Something happened on Juniper's face then. It was quick, like a fish flashing under water, there and gone before you could name it. Sefa was good at faces. She watched this one carefully and decided it had been sad, but a strange kind of sad, the kind that has laughter buried somewhere inside it like a stone in a loaf of bread.

"Eat," Juniper said.

Sefa ate. Between bites she watched the compass on the table and thought about her plan.

Her plan was this: Juniper was clearly someone who knew about forgetting. Grown-ups always knew about forgetting; it was their favorite chore to assign. And Sefa had decided that if forgetting was a chore, then it was a chore you could practice, and if you practiced anything enough you either got good at it or you proved it couldn't be done, and either way you learned something true. The woman had taught her that too. *Try a thing a hundred ways,* she used to say, mending nets, fingers flying. *Ninety-nine of them won't work and then you'll know something.*

So after the bread, Sefa folded her hands in her lap the proper way, the way children do when they are about to be instructed, and said:

"Teach me."

Juniper looked up from the lamp. "Teach you what?"

"How to forget."

The lamp flame stood very still. Outside, the first fat drops of rain hit the roof like someone throwing gravel politely, one piece at a time, testing.

"It's a chore, isn't it?" Sefa went on, because Juniper hadn't said no yet and silence usually meant yes-waiting. "Forgetting. Like sweeping. If I practice every day I'll get better at it, and then I won't be sad, and then I'll be less trouble for you. That's what the man said. That it would be easier. He said easier like it was a gift he was giving me." She frowned. "But gifts are supposed to be things you want. So maybe it wasn't a gift. Maybe he just called it that."

Juniper had gone very still. Still in a different way than before — not busy-still, not thinking-still. Frozen-still. Like a rabbit Sefa had once seen on the road, deciding between running and hoping.

"Sefa," Juniper said finally, and the voice was careful, each word set down like a foot on ice. "Who told you to forget?"

"The man." She shrugged. "Or the voice. It was dark. I remember the dark part best. Dark and the smell of tar." Tar and rope and cold wood under her cheek — those details came back now, unasked, like cats wanting dinner. "He crouched down next to me. I could see his boots. Good boots. Company boots, with the little brass tips." She pointed at Juniper's own boots, which had the same brass tips, and watched another flash-cross-the-water expression move over Juniper's face, except this one wasn't sad at all.

"And the woman," Juniper said. "Your mother. What did she tell you? Before."

Sefa considered lying, briefly, the way children consider it — seriously, and then not. But lying to Juniper felt like lying to the sea; pointless, and the sea always found out.

"She didn't tell me to forget," Sefa said. "She told me to remember. She held my face — like this—" she put her own small rough-child hands on either side of her own cheeks and pressed, demonstrating, though it wasn't the same at all, "—and she said, *you hold on to me, little fish, whatever anybody says. You hold on tight.* And then she gave me the compass and pushed me toward the wharf and the last time I saw her she was standing on the boat and the wind was pulling her hair sideways and she was smiling, but it was the wrong kind of smile, the kind you make so the child doesn't cry."

The rain found its rhythm then, coming steady, filling the room with its sound so that the silence between Sefa and Juniper had walls around it, private.

"So you see," Sefa said, practical, folding her hands again. "I already tried it your way and it doesn't work. Forgetting. I practiced all morning. I sat right there and I said *forget the face* over and over, forty times, and I squeezed my eyes shut and everything, and do you know what happened?"

Juniper shook their head. Slowly.

"I remembered more." Sefa's voice cracked with the unfairness of it. "Every time I said forget, my brain said oh, *what* are we forgetting, let me get it out and look at it — and then there she was again, the eye with the star in it, the hands, the boat. It's backwards. It's completely backwards. Whoever invented forgetting made a mistake."

And then, because Juniper still hadn't spoken, still sat there like a person listening to an echo of something, Sefa said the thing she'd carried all day, the thing that had grown heavier since morning:

"You know how to forget. Don't you. I saw it in your face when you looked at the ledger page. You looked at it like you'd met it before. Like it owed you money." She leaned forward. "Somebody taught *you*. Didn't they. Somebody taught you the same chore, a long time ago, and you got good at it. Really good. Good as sweeping."

The rain drummed. The lamp flame leaned and straightened.

"You can tell me the trick," Sefa offered generously. "I won't use it."

* * *

Later — much later, after the lamp was turned low and Juniper lay on the pallet facing the wall, breathing in the pretend-sleep rhythm that fooled absolutely no one, least of all a six-year-old who had spent a season sleeping in doorways and learning which adult sounds were real — Sefa lay on her mat and did the opposite of forgetting.

She built the face.

This was her nighttime work, her secret craft. She closed her eyes and assembled the woman piece by piece, the way the boatwright by the green door had built his hulls, plank by plank, checking each seam.

Skin: milk-tea. Hair: black with salt-curls at the ends where it touched the collar, always escaping however she tied it, one curl hanging down the left side like a question mark. Hands: rough on top, soft underneath. Mouth: a line that wanted to smile and mostly lost the argument. Eyes: dark, dark, and in the left one — no. Right. Right one. The star.

There. Whole. Real.

*I'm holding on,* Sefa told her silently. *You said hold on tight and I am. Somebody gave me a chore and I'm not doing it. Don't tell.*

The compass sat on the table, and in the dark she couldn't see the needle, but she could feel it anyway, the way you could feel someone watching you across a room. Pointing at the sea. Pointing at everything. Pointing, maybe, at wherever the boat with the broken name had gone, and whoever was standing on it, hair sideways, wearing the wrong kind of smile.

Somewhere out in the storm, a door banged. Not the wind — doors banged differently when hands did it. Sefa heard footsteps in the lane, slow ones, deliberate ones, the kind that stopped outside buildings to listen, and she heard them stop.

She held very still, one hand curled around the compass chain, counting.

Footsteps. Three. Four. A pause, right below the shutter, long enough for ten waves to pass.

Then moving on — but slowly, unhurried, the walk of someone making a list of their own.

Sefa slid off her mat without a sound and crossed the forty-one boards to Juniper's pallet, and tugged the blanket-edge once, twice, the way she'd woken the woman a hundred mornings.

"Juniper," she whispered. "Someone's counting our windows."
