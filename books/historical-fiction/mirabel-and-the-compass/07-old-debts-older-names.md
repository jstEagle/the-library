## 7. Old Debts, Older Names

The retired divers lived on the wrong side of the breakwater, where the company's lanterns didn't bother to reach and the houses leaned together like old men sharing a secret. Juniper had not been down Rope Row in six years. There was no reason to go. Retired divers were the company's least favorite ghosts—people who had seen too much and lived too long, kept at a polite distance with a pension of salt fish and silence.

Juniper went at dusk, when the tide was out far enough to expose the flats and the smell of the drying racks hung thick enough to hide any other purpose. Sefa was tucked into the crawlspace beneath the floorboards of the hut, fed, warned twice not to make a sound unless the door spoke first. The child had looked up with those grave dark eyes and said, "If someone comes, I'm not here."

"Where are you?"

"Nowhere." A pause. "Like Mama."

Juniper hadn't corrected her. There was no correcting that kind of arithmetic.

Rope Row announced itself by sound before sight: the creak of nets, a dog with an opinion about strangers, the rhythmic knock of a woman splitting firewood somewhere behind the shacks. Juniper counted doors by memory—Maren Okonkwo had been third from the seaward end, twenty years ago, back when Juniper was small enough to be carried to the water's edge on Maren's shoulders while their mother laughed about something Juniper could no longer hear.

Third door still stood. Barely. The wood had gone gray as driftwood, patched with tin where the storms had taken bites. But the boat frame in the yard was new work, or new-ish, ribs steamed and lashed with the particular neatness of hands that refused to be done with the sea.

Juniper knocked the diver's knock—two, pause, one—and waited.

The firewood knocking stopped.

A long silence. Then: "That knock is twenty years dead. Who taught it to you?"

"You did," Juniper said. "Or someone with your hands."

The door opened on a chain, then all the way, and there she was: Maren Okonkwo, broader than memory, hair gone white at the temples but still braided tight for work though she'd not touched open water in years. Her eyes did what old divers' eyes did—went straight past the face to the way a person stood, the shoulders, the breath.

"Little Lockridge," she said. "You grew into her chin."

The words landed like a weight belt dropped on bare feet. Juniper kept still. That was the training. Keep still, keep breathing, let the moment pass over you like cold water.

"I need to talk," Juniper said.

"Then you'd better come in before the whole row sees you standing there looking like a debt collector's letter."

* * *

Inside smelled of lamp oil, tar, and dried kelp hung from the beams. Maren poured two cups of something bitter and hot without asking whether Juniper wanted it. Old divers didn't ask. They assessed and acted; asking was for people with time.

She sat across the table and set down her cup and said nothing. Waiting. Another old habit. Silence was a tool, and Juniper had spent a lifetime learning it was also a language—but sitting across from Maren Okonkwo was like sitting across from someone fluent in the same tongue, and Juniper found their careful quiet suddenly legible, readable, transparent.

"The ledger page," Juniper said finally, and put it on the table between them, folded soft as cloth now from handling.

Maren didn't touch it. She looked at it the way you look at a shark fin cutting the surface—at a distance, without moving anything you don't have to move.

"Where'd you get this?"

"A child brought it to my door. Six years old. She was told to forget the face that left her."

Maren's jaw worked once. "And whose face would that be?"

"I don't know yet. I know the page records a write-off. A diver declared drowned while her contract was still live. Sold onward. Dated—" Juniper turned it flat, tapped the line. "This year. The year my mother went under."

For the first time, Maren reached out. She drew the page toward her slowly, like pulling a splinter from her own flesh, and read it by the lamplight with her lips moving, though Juniper suspected she wasn't reading so much as remembering.

"Name's smudged," she said at last. "Ink bled. But look at the margin. See the little mark? Two strokes, like a gull's wing."

"I saw it."

"That's Wren's mark. He was a junior clerk even then, doing the founder's dirty arithmetic. Every write-off got the wing. Every one." She set the page down and pushed it away from herself, as if it burned at close range. "I dove beside your mother eleven years. You know that?"

"I was told you did. I was told—" Juniper stopped. Twenty years of strategic silence made even speaking feel like prying open a rusted hatch. "I was told she drowned. That the sea took her. That the company paid her debts against her contract and buried an empty coffin because the body never came up."

"And you believed it because believing it was safer than not."

"Yes."

Maren drank. Looked at the lamp instead of Juniper. When she spoke, her voice had gone low and flat, the voice divers used underwater, where panic kills.

"Your mother was the best of us. Could hold her breath longer than anyone, could read a current like a priest reads scripture. And she asked questions. That was her sin—not the diving, not the debt. She found out what happened to divers who got hurt bad, the ones too broken to work off their contracts. Found out they weren't dying free."

Juniper's hands had gone cold around the cup. "The write-offs."

"The write-offs. A diver gets crippled, or gets old, or asks too many questions—the company declares them drowned. No body, no burial costs, no widow's claim. Contract gets sold on to some plantation outfit upcoast, fresh paper, new name. The person walks onto a boat alive and comes off the books dead. And everyone on Rope Row watches the boat leave and says nothing, because we've all got kin on the ledgers, and saying something drowns them faster than the sea ever would."

"How many?"

"More than the storms took. More than I'll say out loud with walls this thin." Maren's gaze finally came up and met Juniper's, and there was something terrible in it—not guilt exactly, but its sediment, twenty years of silt settled over whatever she'd once felt. "I watched your mother's boat leave. That's what I've never said. Not drowned. *Departed.* At night, from the company dock, with three other written-off divers, chained below like cargo. I stood on the breakwater and watched the running lights go out past the point, and I went home, and I never told a soul. Including you."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the dog had found something to complain about again.

"Why tell me now?"

"Because you came through my door holding a piece of paper that should have been ash twenty years ago, with a child hiding somewhere and your face wearing the exact expression your mother wore the week before they took her." Maren leaned forward. "Because I am sixty-one years old, my heart beats like a cracked bell, and I have decided I would rather die loud than finish quiet. That's why."

* * *

They talked until the lamp needed trimming. Juniper laid it all out—the compass, the note, the checkpoints forming in their mind like a route charted for a dive: harbor, customs house, archive. Maren listened the way she must have listened to currents, interrupting only to ask the questions that mattered.

"You'll never get original paper past the archive. The archive is the company's heart; Wren guards it like his own chest cavity."

"I know. That's why the copy goes first, separate routes. If one sinks, the other floats."

"Mm. Your mother talked like that. Redundancy. She used to say the sea forgives nothing, so you carry everything twice." Maren rubbed her thumb along the rim of her cup, around and around. "You'll need more than copies, though. Copies can be called forgeries. You need someone with standing to receive it—someone who can't be bought, scared, or promoted."

"A magistrate."

"The magistrate himself is Meridian's fishing partner, so forget him. But his clerk—" Maren paused, and something almost like a smile moved through the weather of her face. "His clerk is a man named Tobias Fenn. Forty years ago, before either of us was born, he was a deckhand on a pearling lugger that went down off the shoals. My father pulled him out of the water. Fenn has never forgotten it, mostly because he reminds me every year with a gift of rum he thinks I don't notice is obligation dressed up as friendship."

"You trust him?"

"I trust the debt. Debts are the most honest things in this city—it's people who lie about them." She fixed Juniper with a hard look. "But hear me, Juniper Lockridge. If you bring this to Fenn, it stops being a secret and becomes a case. Cases have witnesses. Witnesses have names. Mine will be on that list, and my heart won't survive a company interrogation, and I know that going in."

"Then don't testify. Take the name and stay out of it."

"No." She said it fast, before her own fear could catch it. "No. I've been out of it for twenty years, and look what that bought. It bought empty coffins and sold children and you, standing in my kitchen with your mother's grief wearing your face like a hand-me-down coat." She stood, joints cracking like rigging, and went to a chest in the corner. From beneath folded oilskins she took a small packet wrapped in waxed cloth and set it on the table.

"What's that?"

"My own insurance. Names, dates, boats I saw leave. Twenty years of writing things down in the dark like a coward keeping a diary." She pushed it toward Juniper, then pulled it back, one finger still on it. "It stays with me. When your evidence reaches Fenn, I walk in after it. Understood? I'm not cargo to be smuggled. I'm crew."

Outside, the tide was turning; Juniper could feel it in the pressure behind the ears, an old inherited sense. Time to get back to Sefa, back to the hut, back behind the shutters before the patrol lamps swung down the row. Juniper rose, and Maren caught their wrist at the door—grip strong as a mooring line.

"One thing more. The compass the girl carried. Let me see it sometime. Because if it's the compass I think it is, your mother didn't just ask questions." Her voice dropped. "She was given gifts by the man who erased her. And that means the story is uglier than a write-off. It means somebody loved her and destroyed her anyway, and people do worse things to protect a love than a crime."

Juniper walked home along the flats in the last gray light, the ledger page against their chest, Maren's words circling like gulls overhead. Behind them, in the third house from the seaward end, a lamp stayed burning late—an old diver writing out her testimony in a steady hand, settling accounts.

Ahead, at the bend of the harbor road, a company lantern bobbed. A figure in a foreman's coat stood talking to the night watchman, and even at this distance, even in the dark, Juniper knew the set of those shoulders.

Wren was asking questions on Rope Row.

And the only house he hadn't yet come to was the one with a child hidden under its floorboards.
