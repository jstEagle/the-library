## 8. The Compass Points Inward

The lamp had burned down to a thumb's width of oil by the time Juniper finished copying the ledger page a third time, and still they could not stop looking at it.

Three copies now. One folded flat against their ribs, sewn into the lining of the old diving jacket that hung behind the door where no one but Juniper would ever think to look. One rolled tight as a wick and pushed into the hollow handle of the water cask, sealed with wax from the stub of candle Sefa had been using to make shadows on the wall. And the original—the original lay on the table under Juniper's palm, weighted at its corners with four river stones because the night wind kept trying to take it back.

*Write-off. Diver declared drowned. Contract sold.*

The words had not changed since the last time Juniper read them. They would not change tomorrow either. But reading them was like pressing on a bruise to check whether it still hurt, and it always did, and so Juniper pressed again.

Sefa slept in the corner, curled in the blanket Juniper had bought off the rag boat two winters ago, her breath going in and out with the small mechanical patience of a child who had learned that sleep was safer when no one could tell you were doing it. Even asleep she held the compass. Her fist was closed around it the way a mussel closes around a grain of sand—not for comfort, exactly, but because it had become part of her, or she of it.

Juniper crossed the room quietly and knelt beside her.

For a long moment they only watched her breathe. Then, gently, working one finger at a time, they loosened her grip.

She stirred. Murmured something—a word, maybe half a word—and turned her face into the blanket. Juniper waited until her breathing lengthened again before standing with the compass in hand.

It was heavier than it looked. Most brass things were; the sea taught you early that weight lies. But this one carried its weight strangely, all of it gathered in the lid, as though something more than hinges lived there. Juniper had turned it over a dozen times since the monsoon night—had watched the needle swing and settle, swing and settle, always finding north no matter how the room seemed to tilt—but they had never done what they were about to do now, which was sit down under the lamp with a knife and a steady hand and open the thing the way an evidence-taker opens a body.

Because the ledger page named dates. And the compass had come to Sefa through hands Juniper could not see. And in Meridian Harbor, things that traveled together usually belonged together.

The blade slid into the seam of the lid. For a moment nothing. Then something gave—a click, soft as a knuckle cracking—and the inner plate lifted free of the case on a hidden lip of brass, and Juniper stopped breathing.

Engraved inside the case, in the space the plate had covered, in a fine looping hand that had been filled with black enamel so it would never wear away:

*M.E.M. — from E.A.M. — "True in every weather."*

Juniper read it once. Read it again. The letters did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

M.E.M. Marisol Estrella Merrow. Their mother's name, which Juniper had not spoken aloud in nineteen years and which rose in them now like a diver coming up too fast, bringing the pressure with her. M for Marisol. E for Estrella. M for Merrow.

And E.A.M. Everyone in Meridian Harbor knew those initials. They were stamped on the warehouse doors, embossed on the contract ledgers, cast into the iron plaque above the company gate: *EDMUND AURELIUS MERIDIAN, FOUNDER.* Children used the letters in hopscotch. Divers spat after them, quietly, the way you spat after a shark's shadow.

The compass had not been stolen. It had not been lost or scavenged or passed down through some anonymous chain of the poor. It had been *given*. Given by Edmund Meridian to Marisol Merrow, engraved with both their names, sealed inside a case built to hide the giving—and then it had traveled, somehow, nineteen years through the dark, to arrive at the door of the child Marisol had left behind, in the hands of another child who had been told to forget the face that left her.

Juniper set the compass down on the table very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

* * *

They had been told, at six years old, standing on this same floor in this same room while rain came off the harbor in gray sheets: *Your mother drowned. The sea took her. The sea takes everyone eventually, and it is mercy to let it have what it takes.* A company man had said it—not Wren, someone before Wren, someone whose face Juniper had deliberately let erode—and he had said it kindly, the way Aldous Wren said everything kindly, and kindness in Meridian Harbor was a kind of rope: it was only ever offered so that it could be tied around something.

Juniper had believed it the way you believe the tide comes in. Not gladly. Not with understanding. But completely, because there was nothing else to stand on.

Now there was this: a brass compass with a hidden compartment, engraved with a founder's initials and a drowned woman's name, inscribed *true in every weather*, sitting on the table of a woman the same company had written off alive.

You did not give keepsakes to the drowned. You did not engrave pet phrases for women you intended to lose. Whatever had happened between Edmund Aurelius Meridian and Marisol Estrella Merrow, it had happened close enough that gifts were exchanged, and it had ended badly enough that the gift had been smuggled out of his orbit and into the dark, and the woman herself had been entered into a ledger as a loss to be written off, her contract sold as though she were cargo that had spoiled in transit.

Juniper thought: *He knew her. He knew us.* The thought arrived without heat, cold as ballast, and sank straight to the bottom of them.

Because that was the thing the ledger page alone had not done. The ledger page had made Juniper an instrument of a crime—evidence walking around on legs, useful, dangerous. But the compass made them something worse. It made them *known*. Somewhere in the fine house on the hill, a man had held their mother's name in his hands often enough to have it cut into metal. He had known her middle name. He had chosen a phrase for her. And then, whatever came after, he had signed—or permitted, or ordered—the paper that erased her, and gone on signing papers for nineteen years while her child dove his oyster beds for pennies against a debt that had never once gotten smaller.

Juniper picked the compass up again and turned it under the lamplight, hunting the outside of it now with new eyes. The brass was worn smooth at the hinge. There was a dent near the crown, old, salt-pitted. On the back, almost rubbed away, a maker's mark: a small anchor over the letter *V*, and beneath it a date.

1891.

Eight years ago. Long after the drowning. Which meant the compass had been in circulation, in use, *carried*, for eight years after Marisol Merrow went into the water and did not come out. Someone had kept it. Someone had carried it through eight years of weathers, true or otherwise, and then—what? Passed it to Sefa? Left it where a fleeing woman would find it and press it into her daughter's hands?

The note came back to Juniper then, unbidden, in that handwriting they had recognized and refused, until now, to name: *forget the face that left her.*

Not *her mother's face*. Not *my face*. *The face that left her.* Written by someone who knew exactly what they were asking, and how much it would cost, and asked anyway—because forgetting was armor, and the child would need armor, and the only person who could forge it was the person who understood the forging best.

Juniper's own hand. Or a hand so like it that the difference was a matter of ink.

They stood up. Walked to the window. The harbor lay below in its usual midnight arrangement of lamps and dark water, the company warehouses ranked along the quay like headstones, and somewhere out there, past the breakwater, the channel where the intake pipes ran down into the compound—the channel Juniper had swum as a girl, before they knew better than to swim anywhere the company could see.

Their mother had taught them to hold their breath. That was the memory that surfaced now, unasked, the way it always did when Juniper stood at this window: Marisol in the shallows, laughing, saying *the sea is only water until you panic, and then it is teeth.* Saying: *when you are afraid, count. Counting is a kind of silence you can carry underwater.*

Juniper counted. Got to forty-seven before the shaking stopped.

* * *

The problem, laid out plainly, was this: the ledger page proved a crime. The compass proved a relationship. And a crime committed by strangers could be prosecuted, but a crime committed between intimates—between a founder and a diver, between initials engraved in a secret compartment—was a story, and stories required witnesses, and every witness who might have stood in that gap between M.E.M. and E.A.M. was either dead, drowned, written off, or twenty years silent by training.

Except one.

Maren Okonkwo had dove beside Marisol Merrow. Maren had said so with the flat certainty of a woman handing over a knife: *I dove beside your mother, and I saw what the company does with divers it wants gone.* Maren had given a name—the magistrate's clerk, the owed favor. Maren had looked at the ledger page and had not flinched, which in Juniper's experience meant either great courage or great guilt, and with Maren it had always been impossible to say which ran deeper.

But Maren had not seen the compass yet.

Juniper wrapped it in the scrap of oilcloth they used for the dive knife and put it inside their shirt, against the skin, where it lay cold as a coin from a dead man's pocket. Then they took the lamp down to its last blue inch of flame and sat at the table again and forced themselves to do the arithmetic they had been avoiding.

If Edmund Meridian had loved their mother—if that word even applied to whatever moved between a man who owned the harbor and a woman the harbor owned—then the write-off was not merely greed. Greed was simple. Greed you could predict. But a write-off that began in intimacy ended wherever the man's fear or shame or rage had taken him, and men like Edmund Meridian did not leave loose threads. They left tidy ones. A drowned diver. A sold contract. An orphan indentured back into the same company, at the same beds, in the same waters—kept close, the way you keep a thing you're not finished with.

Or kept close the way you keep a debt to yourself. Juniper did not know which was worse. Both of them meant that every year of their life had unfolded inside the sightline of the man who had erased their mother, and neither of them meant anything Juniper could act on tonight.

What they could act on was this: the compass had to reach the clerk along with the page. A document accused. An object testified. Together they were a case; apart they were curiosities. And the clerk, whoever he was, owed Maren a favor—and favors in Meridian Harbor expired like milk, and the century was turning in eleven days, and Wren had already begun asking his gentle questions.

Juniper looked at Sefa sleeping in the corner, one hand opening and closing on empty air where the compass had been, searching for it even in dreams.

*I'm sorry,* Juniper did not say, because saying things was the one skill they had never been permitted to keep. *I'm taking the thing you carried across the whole dark to bring me. I know what it cost you to carry it. I know what it will cost me to give it away.*

Outside, far down the hill, a lantern moved along the quay road—slow, unhurried, stopping at intervals the way lanterns stopped when the man carrying them was speaking to people in doorways. Juniper watched it pass the boatwright's shed. Watched it pause at the widow Cardoso's steps. Watched it turn up the lane that led, eventually, to this street.

Wren was not sleeping either. Wren was making his rounds of kindnesses, and every door he knocked on was a mouth being opened, and sooner or later one of those mouths would say *yes, there's been a child seen at the Lockridge place, small, dark-haired, came in the storm.*

Juniper pinched out the lamp. In the dark, the compass pressed cold against their breastbone, and they thought: *he gave her this so she would always find her way back. And then he made sure there was nowhere to come back to.*

Tomorrow they would go to Maren. Tomorrow they would show her the engraving and watch her face do whatever it did, and they would ask her the question that had been forming in them all night, the question that tasted of harbor water:

*Did you know he knew her?*

But tonight—tonight the lantern on the quay road had stopped moving, directly below the window, and a voice was saying something pleasant to someone, and Juniper stood in the dark holding their mother's name in brass and listened to the sound of the net drawing closed.
