## 9. Wren Closes In

The Meridian Trading Company kept two clocks in its harbor office, and Aldous Wren trusted neither. The wall clock ran four minutes fast—a foreman's trick, inherited from his predecessor, designed to hurry the laborers out at closing and hurry them in again at dawn—and the brass regulator on his desk, a gift from the founder himself, ran three minutes slow for reasons no one had ever explained to anyone's satisfaction. Between them, Wren had learned to keep time in his own body. He did it now, standing at the window with his hands folded behind his back, counting the interval between one lantern crossing the quay and the next.

Seven seconds. Too long.

He turned back to his desk and drew the morning's papers toward him with the deliberate economy of a man who wasted nothing—not paper, not ink, not breath. The ration tallies. The dive rosters. The quarterly debt schedule, in which every diver in the company's employ was listed by name, by contract number, and by the precise figure that stood between them and freedom, a figure that had a way of growing rather than shrinking, like a tide that only came in.

Wren read figures the way other men read scripture: closely, and with an understanding that the words were not really the point.

The child was still missing.

Three days now. A girl of six or thereabouts, last seen—according to the statements he had collected—on the night of the monsoon, when the rain had come down so hard that half the quay had claimed to be asleep and the other half claimed to have seen nothing at all. The mother, one Vessa Kaine, contract number 1147, laundry and mending division, had failed to report for her shift the following morning. Her bunk was stripped. Her sea chest was gone. And her daughter, who should have been sleeping beside her, had vanished with her—or appeared to have.

*Appeared.* Wren set down the pen he had been holding without writing anything. Appearances were the company's native language. He had spent nineteen years learning to read beneath it.

Because here was the thing that troubled him, the splinter under the skin of an otherwise clean report: Vessa Kaine had left nothing else. No note in her own hand. No debts unpaid beyond the ordinary crushing ones. And she had left behind—this was the detail, this was the grain of sand in the works—the child's shoes. Both pairs. Small leather things, patched at the toes, sitting neatly by the door of an empty bunk as though their owner had been carried out barefoot into a monsoon.

Mothers did not leave shoes. Mothers took shoes. Wren had grown up poor enough to know that a pair of serviceable boots was worth more than most sentiments, and a mother who abandoned even the boots while taking the chest understood, on some level, that she was not coming back—which meant either flight or death, and neither explanation accounted for the neatness of the door.

Or for the third detail, which Wren had kept off every written report and locked instead inside the drawer of his own attention:

Juniper Lockridge's lamp had been lit past midnight that same night. Juniper Lockridge, who banked their flame at nine o'clock with the regularity of a tide table, whose habits were so fixed they might have been carved into the doorframe. Someone had seen the light—old Piet at the net-menders' bench, who saw everything and reported nothing, which made him useless and therefore honest. Past midnight, the lamp burned. And past midnight, if a woman were running through rain toward the only door on the quay she might dare to knock on—

Wren opened the ledger drawer and took out the page he had copied before returning the original to the archive, as procedure demanded. A routine audit flag. An entry dated twenty-two years back, pulled in the course of verifying the pre-1880 records slated for disposal. *Contract 0342 — write-off — drowned — balance transferred.* Ordinary. There were dozens like it in any given year. Divers drowned; it was the trade's whole arithmetic; the sea took its fee and the company recorded it.

Except that this particular write-off had been countersigned twice, once in the founder's hand and once in a hand Wren recognized because he had spent years studying it across a desk: the hand of the harbormaster who had died comfortably in bed six years later, having never once, in all his tenure, certified a drowning he had not personally witnessed.

Wren had checked. It was a habit of his. In twenty-two years of ledgers, the harbormaster had witnessed eleven drownings. Nine of the eleven had been real—he'd found the widows, the graves, the pension entries. Two had simply ceased, like lamps going out.

And one of those two had been named Mirabel Lockridge.

Wren closed the drawer softly, though there was no one to hear. He did not believe in ghosts, but he believed thoroughly in unfinished business, and unfinished business had a way of surfacing like a body in a harbor—weeks late, bloated with consequence, impossible to push back under.

He rose, took his coat from its hook, and settled it across his shoulders with the care of a man putting on armor he intended never to remove. On his desk, between the two untrustworthy clocks, lay a slip of paper bearing a single instruction in his own cramped script: *Pre-1880 burn scheduled. Confirm no outstanding claims against disposed records.*

Outstanding claims. He almost smiled at himself. Twenty-two years, and the dead woman's child was still on the company's books, still diving their waters, still banking a lamp at nine o'clock sharp. If there was a claim anywhere against the record of Mirabel Lockridge, it slept under Juniper Lockridge's roof—and something had woken it three nights ago in the rain.

He would go and ask, pleasantly. That was always the method. Pleasantness opened more doors than force, and doors, once opened, tended to stay open in the memory of the person through whom you'd walked.

The wall clock said he was already late. His body said he was exactly on time. Wren went out into the gray morning trusting his body, as he trusted it in all things, and gave no thought at all to the possibility that somewhere across the quay, someone else was also counting seconds between lanterns, and had been for days.

* * *

Juniper heard the knock at the neighbor's door first—the second knock, actually, because the first had come an hour earlier at the cobbler's, and the cobbler's wife had a voice that carried like gull-cry across the close-packed roofs of the divers' quarter.

"—no, no children here, Mr. Wren, we've none but our own and they're grown—"

Grown. Juniper pressed their shoulder harder against the wall beside the window, where the morning shadow kept them, and watched Sefa where she sat on the floor beneath the loft bed with a rag doll Maren had stitched for her, small hands moving the doll's arms in slow, silent pantomime. The girl had learned quickly what the game was. When Juniper said *still*, Sefa went still as sunken things go still. When Juniper said *quiet*, she folded her voice away somewhere behind her teeth and kept it there, hour upon hour, with a discipline that broke something open in Juniper's chest every time they witnessed it. Six years old. Six years old and already fluent in the only language the company respected.

Another door. Closer. The widow Anthea's, three houses down, and Wren's voice came through the thin morning air with the pleasantness of a man reading aloud from a book he had written himself.

"—small for six, I'd say. Dark-haired. Speaks little, they tell me." A pause. "You'd remember, surely, if a child had passed your door."

"I see nothing past my own washing line, Mr. Wren. You know that."

"And yet you see a great deal *from* it, Mistress Anthea. Which is why I ask." A soft sound that might have been laughter, shared between professionals. "If anything comes to you. Anything at all. You know where my door is, and you know the company remembers its friends."

Juniper's mouth had gone dry. They knew that tone. Every diver on the quay knew that tone—it was the tone Wren used when handing out a food ration in a hard month, the tone he'd used just days ago pressing a sack of rice into Juniper's arms with that terrible gentleness, *for the winter, Lockridge, you've earned it*. Kindness as a hook. Generosity as a line with a barb worked into it, cast wide over the whole quarter, waiting.

He wasn't searching for a runaway child. Or not only. He was teaching the street to watch itself—to make every neighbor a checkpoint, every washing line a customs post—so that the search would perform itself while he walked through it slowly, whistling.

Two doors down now. The rope-seller's. Juniper counted the seconds of the conversation the way Wren presumably counted lanterns: *fourteen, fifteen*—long, the rope-seller liked to talk—*sixteen, seventeen*—and then footsteps on packed mud, unhurried, coming on.

Juniper crossed the room without a sound, lifted the loose board beneath the floor loom, and looked at Sefa. The girl was already looking up at them, doll clutched to her chest.

"Still," Juniper breathed. "Quiet. Like the bottom."

Sefa nodded once, solemn as a burial, and slid down into the dark beneath the boards. Juniper laid the board back over her, dragged the loom's corner post a finger's width to seat it, scattered a handful of lint and thread-ends across the seam, and returned to the window-seat with a net-mending needle in their hand just as the knock came at their own door.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. Patient.

"Lockridge." Pleasant as rice. "It's Wren. Have you a moment?"

Juniper opened the door the width of a hand, blinking sleep into their face as though the morning had surprised them, though they had not slept and the morning had been rehearsed since dawn. "Foreman." Their voice came out flat and rough, exactly as intended. "Early for a call."

"Company business keeps early hours." Wren's eyes moved past them into the room with the smoothness of water finding a crack—over the cold hearth, the single bowl drying on the shelf, the made-up bunk, the mending basket. Everything in its place. One bowl. One bunk. Juniper had spent three days making the room into a lie so complete it hurt to stand inside it. "May I?"

"It's your quay, Foreman."

He stepped in, and the room seemed to shrink around him the way a tide-pool shrinks around a dropped stone. He did not sit. He picked up the mending basket's lid, weighed it in his hand, set it down. He looked at the hearth ash—at the cold ash, and here Juniper felt the first true prickle of fear, because the fire had been burning last night, and ash remembered—and then his gaze moved on, mercifully, to the window.

"Cold nights lately," he said. "I notice your lamp's been dark these past evenings. Unusual for you. You're a nine-o'clock man, Lockridge. Always struck me as a man who respects a schedule."

"The wind's been bad. Lamp smokes out the crack in the glass. Been meaning to mend it." Each sentence a coin paid out flat, giving nothing to bite.

"Mm." Wren turned. For a moment he regarded them directly, and his eyes were the color of harbor water over mud—nothing visible on the surface, everything suspended beneath. "I'll come to it, then. A child's gone missing. Vessa Kaine's girl. You'll have heard."

"Heard the talk." Shrug. Stitch. The needle moved in Juniper's hands with a life of its own; their mother had taught them to mend net before they could write, and the hands stayed steady even when the rest of them wanted to shake apart. "Sad business."

"Sad," Wren agreed. "The mother's gone too, of course. Run off, most likely. These laundry girls—" He let the sentence die with a small dismissive motion, testing whether Juniper would reach for the bait, defend the absent woman, reveal acquaintance. Juniper stitched. "What interests me," Wren continued, softer now, leaning slightly forward, "is the *timing*. A woman runs in a monsoon, the very night, as it happens, that certain old records are being reviewed for disposal. Coincidence is common on the water. But timing—" he smiled, briefly, thinly, "—timing is a kind of testimony. Don't you find?"

"I find the sea doesn't keep a calendar, Foreman."

"No," Wren said. "But I do."

He straightened, and for a moment he simply looked around the room again, slower this time, and Juniper understood with a cold, sinking clarity that the room had already told him whatever it was going to tell him—that the interview was theater, that the verdict had been reached somewhere behind those mud-colored eyes before the door ever opened, and that everything now was only the pleasant working-out of procedure. He paused at the hearth. He looked down at the ash a long moment.

"You know," he said, conversational, "when I was a young clerk, the founder told me something I've never had occasion to forget. He said: *Aldous, the ledger never lies, but it does get tired. Every few decades it needs cleaning. Old pages, old names, old griefs—sweep them out, and the new century starts with a clean book.*" He tapped the mantel once, lightly, with one finger. "We're burning the pre-1880 ledgers next week. Hygiene, officially. But between us—I've never thought much of hygiene as a reason to destroy a record. Have you?"

The question hung in the room like smoke. Beneath the floorboards, Juniper knew, Sefa was hearing every word and understanding none of it, and the mercy of that was almost unbearable.

"Haven't thought about it at all," Juniper said.

"No," Wren said. "You wouldn't. That's what I've always valued about you, Lockridge. Most men on this quay think too much, and the thinking makes them careless. You—" he moved to the door, opened it, and stood a moment framed in the gray light, "—you are the least careless person I employ. Which is why I'm sure that if a lost child were to turn up anywhere on this quay, you'd be the first to bring her to me. Wouldn't you."

It was not a question. Juniper held the needle steady and met the harbor-water eyes.

"If a child needed finding," they said, "I'd do right by her."

"I believe you would." Wren put on his hat. At the threshold he stopped, half-turned, and added, almost as an afterthought, almost kindly: "The burn is Thursday. After that, it won't matter what anyone remembers. Comforting, isn't it—how the century cleans up after itself?" And he touched his hat brim, and went out, and his footsteps receded down the quay with the unhurried patience of a man who has set a trap and can afford to wait for it to work.

Juniper stood unmoving until the footsteps faded entirely, until the gulls resumed their arguments, until the ordinary noise of the quarter came flooding back like blood into a numb limb. Then they knelt, lifted the board, and looked down into the dark.

Sefa looked back up at them, doll pressed to her chest, eyes enormous and dry. She had heard everything after all. Children always heard everything; that was the company's great mistake about them, and perhaps Wren's too.

"Is he gone?" she whispered.

"Yes."

"Was I quiet?"

Juniper reached down into the dark and lifted her out, and the girl wrapped her arms around their neck with the fierce, boneless trust of the very young, and Juniper held her and felt the truth of the morning settle over them like cold water filling a suit: *he knows*. Maybe not the shape of it, maybe not the compass or the page or the name Mirabel—but he knew the weight was here, in this room, under this roof, and he had given them until Thursday to prove otherwise.

Thursday was the burn. But tonight—tonight Wren would finish his round of doors, and tonight the quarter would be full of watching eyes bought with remembered kindnesses, and every hour Sefa slept beneath these floorboards was an hour borrowed at ruinous interest.

Tonight. It had to be tonight.

Juniper set the girl on her feet, took the brass compass from its hiding place in the flour tin, and pressed it into her small hands. "We're going to visit a friend," they said. "Maren. The salt house. Do you remember her? The laugh like thunder?"

Sefa nodded. Then, clutching the compass to her heart, she asked the question that undid Juniper entirely, asked it in the small clear voice of a child who had learned that questions must be spent carefully, like coins:

"Do we run now, or walk like we're not scared?"

Juniper looked at her—at this small, trained, unbroken survivor of a house of silences—and heard their own childhood speaking.

"We walk," Juniper said. "We walk like the water belongs to us. Because it does."

They banked the lamp, swept the ash clean of every trace, and stepped out together into the gray afternoon, two figures among many on the quay road, walking at the pace of people with nowhere particular to go—while behind them, at the far end of the harbor, a lantern was already being lit in the window of the company office, where Aldous Wren sat down at his desk between the two lying clocks and began, in his neat and patient hand, to draft the indenture papers for a six-year-old girl.

Dated, as it happened, tomorrow.
