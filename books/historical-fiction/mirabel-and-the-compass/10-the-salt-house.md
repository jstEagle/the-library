## 10. The Salt House

The salt house sat at the end of a plank road that the tide took back every evening, as if the sea were collecting rent on it twice a day. It was a low building of gray timber and corrugated iron, its walls furred white with mineral bloom, and it smelled of what it was: brine crystallized into stone, fish oil, the sweet-rot ghost of kelp heaps. Maren Okonkwo had kept it for eleven years, drying salt in wooden trays and selling it to the fish-curers at a price that let her eat and nothing more. Nobody from the Meridian Trading Company came out here. There was no ledger worth reading on the plank road, no debt worth chasing to a woman whose lungs had been ruined by twenty years of their water.

That was precisely why Juniper had chosen it.

They walked out at slack tide, Sefa's small hand swallowed inside Juniper's own, both of them dressed in the drab brown of dock laborers' children because a fugitive should look like nobody's concern. Sefa had not asked where they were going. She had learned already — three days in Juniper's care, and she had learned — that questions were things you saved up like coins, spending them only when the light was right.

"The floor will be sticky," Juniper said. "The salt gets into everything. Don't pick at the walls."

"Is Maren there now?"

"She's always there."

Sefa considered this. "Like a barnacle."

Juniper almost laughed. It came out wrong, half-strangled, but it came out, and Sefa glanced up with the fierce satisfaction of a child who has made an adult produce something true. "Yes," Juniper admitted. "Like a barnacle."

Maren was waiting in the doorway with a lamp turned low, and when she saw the two of them coming down the plank road against the bruised evening sky, she did something Juniper had never seen her do outside of memory: she pressed one broad hand flat over her heart, briefly, the way divers once greeted each other before surfacing from deep water. *Still alive. Still here.* Her mother had used that gesture. Juniper's hands remembered it even if Juniper had spent nineteen years refusing to.

"In," Maren said. "Wind's turning. I can smell rain behind the rain."

Inside, the salt house was one long room and a loft, lit by a single storm lantern hung from a beam. Trays of coarse salt glittered on trestles like fallen stars. Maren had laid a pallet by the little iron stove — an actual mattress, stuffed with something other than straw, and two blankets that smelled of camphor — and next to it a pot of rice porridge with dried shrimp, and a cup of black tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. Small mercies, laid out with the precision of a woman who understood exactly how rare they were.

"You'll sleep here tonight," Maren told Sefa, "and as many nights after as need be. You'll help me turn the trays in the morning. Salt doesn't care who turns it."

"Is that your job?" Sefa asked, already toeing off her too-large boots.

"It's my work. My job was diving, years ago, until the company decided my chest was finished." Maren said it without bitterness, the way one reports weather. "Now I make the sea give me salt instead of pearls. Slower money. Drier."

Sefa looked at the trays, then at Maren, then at Juniper, and Juniper watched the child perform the arithmetic that children of the harbor performed early: *this person is safe because this other person trusts her, and trust is the only currency that cannot be counterfeited.* Then Sefa climbed onto the pallet, pulled the blanket to her chin, and announced, "I'm going to eat all of it."

"You'd better," said Maren.

* * *

While Sefa ate, Juniper sat at Maren's table under the lantern with the ledger page flattened between two strips of driftwood glass, and began to copy it.

It was slow work, slower than Juniper had expected. The original was written in the cramped clerk-hand of the Meridian countinghouse, columns of figures marching down the page like ants crossing a rope bridge, and threaded through the numbers were the annotations that made the page a weapon rather than scrap:

*Contract No. 1149 — diver, female, "A. Lockridge" — declared lost at depth, 12 October 1880. Salvage value nil. Indenture transferred to Meridian & Associates per founder's instruction, consideration received, entry closed.*

*Entry closed.* As if a life were a door someone could shut from the outside. As if a drowning could be signed off in triplicate while the drowned woman was still breathing somewhere on the far side of a boat, sold north with her contract re-cut and her name struck through in red ink.

Juniper copied every figure. Every abbreviation. The clerk's initials — *H.V.B.*, the same H.V.B. whose neat hand appeared on Juniper's own indenture papers — the date, the marginal note about *consideration received*, even the coffee-colored stain in the corner that proved the page had lived on a working desk, that some clerk had set his mug down beside a human being's erasure and gone on with his afternoon. Evidence needed its accidents. A perfect forgery would convince no one; a stained page might.

"What are you doing?" Sefa had come to stand at Juniper's elbow, porridge bowl still in hand, watching the pen travel.

"Making another one."

"Why?"

"Because important things shouldn't live in only one place." Juniper dipped the pen. "If a pearl is kept in one shell and the shell breaks—"

"The pearl is lost," Sefa recited. Then, after a pause: "But you're making two shells."

"I'm making three, if I can. One stays with me. One goes somewhere the company can't reach. And the original—" Juniper hesitated, then decided the child had earned the truth, having carried the thing through a monsoon at six years old. "The original goes to a man whose job is making the company answer questions."

"A magistrate," Sefa said solemnly, pronouncing it *mag-i-strate*, four careful syllables.

"That's right."

She thought about that while she ate, scraping the bowl clean, and then she said, "My mama wrote the note. The one that said forget the face." She wasn't looking at Juniper; she was looking into the empty bowl, at her own reflection warped in the tin bottom. "I didn't forget. I practiced every night like you're supposed to practice washing behind your ears, and I couldn't do it. Am I bad at forgetting?"

The question went through Juniper like a hook through the palm.

"No," Juniper said. "You're good at remembering. That's different, and it's better, even when it hurts. Especially when it hurts."

"Maren says the company wants people to forget things."

"Maren is right."

"So remembering is fighting."

Juniper put down the pen. Somewhere outside, the first fat drops of the second rain hit the iron roof like knuckles. "Yes," Juniper said. "Remembering is fighting. The quietest kind there is."

Sefa nodded, satisfied, and carried her bowl to the wash basin with the exaggerated care of a child performing adulthood. Juniper watched her go and felt the strange double vision that had haunted them since the night of the monsoon: this child standing in front of them, and beneath her, translucent as a watermark, another child — a smaller Juniper, thirty years younger, being taught the same curriculum by colder teachers. *Don't look up. Don't ask. Forget the face.* They had been so proud of their silence. They had called it survival, and it was, and it had also been the company's finest product, manufactured in Juniper's own mouth, year after year, free of charge.

No longer. The pen scratched on. Two shells. Three.

* * *

Maren banked the stove and brought a second lamp to the table. "Show me again," she said. "The part about the transfer."

Juniper turned the glass strips aside. "Consideration received. Someone paid for her contract the day she 'drowned.' If she'd actually died, the contract dies with her — everyone knows that, it's why they fake drownings instead of just taking people. A dead diver owes nothing. A living diver sold to new owners owes everything." Juniper tapped the line of figures. "This is purchase money. This is my mother being bought."

"And the name."

"'A. Lockridge.'" Juniper's voice stayed level. Nineteen years of practice. "Amara Lockridge. That's what the parish record says, what little of it survived the fire at the mission hall. But everyone at the harbor called her—" Juniper stopped.

"Say it," Maren said quietly. "Not for me. For you."

But Juniper couldn't, not yet, not with the words still locked behind the old doors, and Maren didn't push. She had dove beside Amara Lockridge for nine seasons; she had held her at the rail the morning the company boats went out and only one came back. She knew better than anyone alive that some names have to be pried loose like pearls — gently, gently, or the flesh tears.

Instead she said, "I knew a clerk named H.V.B. once. Hollis Van Buren. Little man, ink-stained cuffs, used to come down to the quay to count heads off the boats. He counted us like sacks." She looked at the initials on the page. "He retired rich, I heard. Bought a house on the hill with windows all down the seaward side."

"Windows are useful," Juniper said. "To whoever's watching."

"To whoever's *paid*," Maren corrected. "There's a difference. Watchers watch for love of the thing. Watched men watch for wages, and wages can be outbid." She fixed Juniper with eyes the color of wet slate. "Which brings me to what I've been chewing on all day. You mean to carry this through the checkpoints yourself."

"I mean to try."

"You'll fail."

"Probably."

"Then don't carry it alone." Maren drew from her apron pocket a folded paper of her own — old, soft as cloth at the creases, written in a diver's blunt capitals. "Twenty-two years ago I signed a statement. Testimony, sworn before a harbor chaplain who owed me his brother's life. Names, dates, boats, write-offs — everything I saw in fifteen years of diving, everything we all saw and swallowed. The chaplain sealed it and died before he could deliver it, and I let it sit, because I was afraid, and fear is comfortable when you get used to it." She laid it on the table beside the ledger page, and the two documents seemed to lean toward each other like old shipmates. "My heart is bad. The doctor gave me a year, maybe two, and I'll tell you what I decided this morning while you were off playing courier: I'm done letting fear spend my last year for me."

Juniper stared at the paper. "You kept it twenty-two years."

"I kept *her* twenty-two years," Maren said. "The paper just came along with her."

Outside the rain arrived in earnest, hammering the iron roof, and the lantern flame bent and steadied, bent and steadied, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. Then Juniper reached across and covered Maren's wrist with one hand — the gesture of still-alive, still-here, passed down from mothers to children of the deep water — and Maren turned her hand over and gripped hard enough to mean it.

* * *

Sefa fell asleep mid-sentence, sitting up against the salt trays with a spare rag in her lap, having insisted she would help turn them "in five minutes." Juniper carried her to the pallet, and she weighed almost nothing, all bone and salt air, her hair smelling of the cheap soap Juniper had bought her at the chandlery. She murmured something in her sleep — a word, maybe two — and her hand curled around the brass compass, which she refused to surrender even unconscious, the way other children clutched dolls.

Juniper stood over her a while, doing nothing, being nowhere, simply present. It was a luxury they hadn't known they'd been denied.

Then Maren touched their shoulder and pointed at the window. "Your tide's turning, courier. You want to be across the flats before the water takes the plank road, unless you fancy swimming home."

Juniper packed the copies into an oilskin packet — one sewn into the lining of their coat at the shoulder seam, one rolled tight inside a hollow reed among Maren's fishing rods — and stood at the door with the rain roaring beyond it.

"Three days," Maren said. "Come back in three days or send word. If you don't, I'll assume the worst and act accordingly."

"Act accordingly how?"

Maren smiled, and it was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a woman who had once swum through a wrecked hull in the dark to cut a trapped diver free. "You'll find out."

Juniper crouched once more by the pallet. Sefa slept with her brow furrowed, chasing something through her dreams, and Juniper brushed the furrow smooth with one thumb and whispered, so quietly the rain nearly took it: "Forget nothing. Remember everything. I'll come back."

And then out into the dark, down the plank road, the packet riding against their heart like a second pulse —

— and stopped, halfway across the flats, because far behind them, up the harbor road, a lantern was moving. Not the bobbing lantern of a fisherman. A steady, official light, swinging in a deliberate arc, the way men swing lights when they are searching rather than walking. And below it, faint under the rain, voices — several, moving along the shore, calling to one another in the clipped cadence of the Meridian watch.

Heading for the plank road. Heading for the salt house.

Juniper turned around, heart slamming, and began to run back through the rising water.
