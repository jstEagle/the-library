## 11. Checkpoint Two: Customs House

The courier's coat smelled of someone else's sweat.

Juniper had bought it that morning from a ragman off Rope Street—three coppers and a lie about dressing for a funeral—and it hung on their narrow frame like a borrowed skin, which was precisely what it needed to be. The brass buttons had been polished with ash until they gleamed dully, honest-looking. The leather satchel across their chest bore the faded stamp of the *Palisade & Vane Courier Service*, its ink cracked with age, its strap worn soft by years of some other runner's shoulder. Juniper had walked the man's routes in their head all night: the customs house sat at the mouth of Meridian Row, and couriers crossed its threshold forty times a day carrying manifests, bills of lading, letters no one bothered to read. A courier was furniture. A courier was invisible.

Invisible was the only country left where Juniper could travel freely.

The copy rode against their ribs now, folded into quarters inside an envelope addressed in Maren's careful hand to *T. Ashcombe, Clerk, Office of the Magistrate, Harbor District*. Maren had written the name like she was carving it into stone. Beneath the envelope, wrapped in oilcloth and wedged between two genuine shipping manifests Juniper had lifted from the boatwright's scrap bin, lay the decoys: three blank pages bearing the Meridian Trading Company seal, stamped in proper violet wax, worthless as seawater but heavy with implication if anyone looked too long.

And beneath everything, sewn into the coat's lining where the ragman would never think to look and neither, Juniper prayed, would any guard—the original.

They had argued about it with Maren until the candles guttered. Splitting the evidence meant splitting the risk; carrying both meant losing both. But the original page, with its water-stained columns and its founder's initials in the margin, was the only thing that could hang a man. A copy could be called forgery. An original could be called murder.

"If they take you," Maren had said, pressing the needle through the lining with her scarred diver's hands, "you eat it."

"I'll what?"

"You heard me. Paper goes down easier than a person goes to prison." She had not smiled when she said it. Twenty years of complicity had burned the humor out of Maren Okonkwo, leaving only iron and salt. "Swallow it or chew it fine. Either way it never reaches Wren's desk."

Juniper had practiced the motion twice in the dark of the salt house while Sefa slept, dry-swallowing air, feeling absurd and terrified and ready.

Now the customs house rose ahead of them through the drizzle, a squat brick fortress straddling the harbor road like a tollbooth for the whole century. Its arched passage funneled every soul entering or leaving the docks—carters, sailors, fishwives, couriers—past a wooden counter where two guards in company gray checked papers with the bored efficiency of men who had done nothing else for years. Above the arch, carved into the lintel, the company motto: *THE SEA PROVIDES. THE COMPANY PROVIDES.*

*The sea provides,* Juniper thought, stepping into the queue behind a fruit cart. *It provided my mother, and look what they did with her.*

The line moved. Juniper counted breaths the way they'd once counted fathoms—one, two, three, down—keeping the rhythm slow, keeping the heart from galloping. Fear was a thing you could dive past if you didn't fight it. Panic made divers shoot for the surface and burst their lungs. Panic, Aldous Wren had told a class of indentured children twenty years ago, standing over them with his neat gray gloves, *panic is how the company loses property.* Juniper had learned the lesson so well they'd forgotten it was a lesson. Now they used it like a tool handed down from an enemy.

*One. Two. Three. Down.*

The cart ahead lurched forward. Oranges tumbled from its top layer and rolled across the wet cobblestones, and the carter swore and scrambled, and the guard at the counter waved him through without checking anything because chaos was more trouble than it was worth. Then it was Juniper's turn.

The counter guard was young, maybe twenty-five, with a wispy mustache he clearly believed was authoritative. He held out one hand without looking up from whatever he was reading—a penny dreadful, Juniper saw, its cover depicting a woman tied to train tracks.

"Papers."

Juniper produced the courier's pass, purchased along with the coat from a forger who worked out of a chandlery and charged according to how much trouble the document needed to survive. This one had cost a week of pearl money. It named one *J. Vale*, courier, Palisade & Vane, authorized for inter-district delivery. It was good work. It should have been good enough.

The guard glanced at it. Glanced again. His eyes came up.

"Vale," he said, tasting the name.

"Courier run," Juniper said, and pitched their voice low and flat, the voice of a man who had been awake since four and resented every hour of it. "Manifests for the harbor office. Nothing interesting."

"Open the satchel."

Here it was, then. Juniper had known it would come to this moment since before dawn; knowing did not stop the cold from climbing their spine like water filling a boot. They unhooked the strap slowly, clumsily, letting the satchel swing wide—letting the guard see the manifests first, the dull gray paper, the columns of tonnage and tariff. Boredom was armor. They laid the envelope on top, address side up, and watched the guard's gaze slide across *T. Ashcombe, Clerk* without catching.

He flipped the manifests. He prodded the oilcloth bundle, felt the stiff edges of the sealed pages within, and frowned—not suspiciously, but with the mild irritation of a man whose routine had developed a wrinkle.

"What's all this, then?"

"Company correspondence," Juniper said. "Sealed pages. I don't open them. That's the rule—you know how the company is about its seals."

Everyone knew how the company was about its seals. Tampering with a violet wax stamp was a flogging offense, and every guard at this checkpoint had seen it enforced at least once. It was the single most reliable law in the harbor district, and Juniper was betting their life that the young guard's fear of it outweighed his curiosity.

The bet nearly failed.

Because at that moment, the second guard—the older one, stationed at the far end of the counter, who had been half-asleep against the wall—straightened up and looked over. And something in his face changed.

"Hold on," the old guard said. He came around the counter with the unhurried gait of a man who had all day and enjoyed using it. "Hold on now. Let me see those papers."

The young guard stepped back, relieved to surrender the problem. The old guard took the courier's pass in fingers stained yellow with tobacco and turned it toward the lantern light, and Juniper stood very still and watched twenty years of careful silence prepare to come apart in a pair of stranger's hands.

"This seal," the old guard said. He tapped the Palisade & Vane stamp. "This outfit folded. What, two years back? Three?"

"Firm merged," Juniper said. "New owners kept the name on the passes. Cheaper than reprinting."

That was good. That was smooth. Juniper heard their own voice deliver it and marveled distantly at the calm of it, the way the body could go on lying while the mind screamed. But the old guard wasn't listening anymore. He had turned the pass over, and now he was looking at the reverse side, at the small embossed mark near the bottom edge that the forger had included for authenticity without understanding what he was including.

A second seal. Small, circular, pressed deep into the paper.

The Meridian Trading Company's mark. The founder's own device: a pearl cradled in a compass rose.

The forger had thrown it in free. *Makes it look official,* he'd said. *Nobody questions paper with the Meridian mark.*

Nobody except the men who worked under that mark every day of their lives.

"That's funny," the old guard said softly. "That's real funny. Because I processed the dissolution papers for Palisade & Vane myself, back in ninety-seven. And I remember—" he looked up, and his eyes were not bored anymore, they were bright and sharp and terrible— "I remember that when a firm dissolves, its seals get surrendered. All of them. To the customs registry." He turned the pass around slowly, displaying it. "So you want to tell me how a dead company's pass comes walking through my checkpoint wearing a live company's seal?"

The passage noise seemed to recede. The drizzle, the cart wheels, the shouting of the fishwives—all of it fell away, and there was only the counter, the lantern, the guard's face, and the envelope riding against Juniper's ribs like a second heartbeat. Behind them, Juniper felt the queue shifting, impatient bodies pressing closer, and understood with perfect clarity that in thirty seconds this man would call for the watch sergeant, and in five minutes there would be a room with no windows, and in ten minutes Aldous Wren would be sent for, because Wren was always sent for when the company's marks appeared where they shouldn't.

*Eat it,* Maren's voice said. *Paper goes down easier than a person goes to prison.*

But the original was sewn into the lining. The lining couldn't be eaten. The lining—

The lining could bleed.

Juniper moved before thinking, the way a diver moves when the current shifts—no decision, just response. Their hand went to their chest as if steadying the satchel, and in the same motion they coughed, deep and wet, doubling over the counter. The cough was magnificent. It was the cough of a man dying of the century itself, phlegmatic and sincere, and it bent them far enough forward that their forehead nearly touched the pass in the guard's hand.

"Beg pardon," Juniper wheezed. "Beg pardon—chest's been bad since the monsoon—"

"Stand back," the old guard said, recoiling. "Stand back from the counter."

"Water," Juniper rasped. "Please. It's the damp. Comes on sudden."

And here the harbor district's oldest truth arrived to save them, the truth that governed guards and smugglers alike: nobody wanted a sick man's breath on them. Not in December. Not with the fever-season rumors crawling through the docks like rats. The young guard had already retreated two full steps. The old guard held the pass at arm's length now, his face twisted between suspicion and self-preservation, and Juniper coughed again—harder, folding further, one hand slapping the counter for support—and let the fit carry them sideways, stumbling, catching themselves on the satchel strap, tearing it loose in the scramble.

The satchel hit the floor. The envelope skidded under the counter's shadow. The manifests scattered across the wet stones, and the oilcloth bundle slid free and thumped against the guard's boot, and the violet-sealed pages inside flashed in the lantern light.

For one suspended instant, everyone in the checkpoint stared at the fallen papers instead of the falling-apart courier.

Then the old guard did exactly what Juniper had gambled he would do. He looked at the company seal gleaming up at him from the oilcloth—violet wax, intact, official—and every question he'd been building died in his throat. You did not touch sealed company correspondence. You did not even pick it up carelessly. He had seen men flogged for less, and whatever curiosity had sharpened his face a moment ago now curdled into the particular fear of a small man holding something that belonged to much larger men.

"Pick that up," he said, stepping back. "Pick all of it up and clear my checkpoint."

Juniper gathered the papers with shaking hands—shaking was easy now, shaking was honest—and repacked the satchel with the exaggerated care of a man concussed by his own lungs. The envelope went in last, address up, boring as tax records. When they straightened, the old guard was still watching them, and there was something unresolved in his stare, some splinter of doubt working at him, and Juniper understood that the improvisation had saved the documents but not the day.

"Name?" the guard said quietly.

"Vale. J. Vale." Juniper met his eyes and gave him nothing else—not fear, not defiance, just the flat gray patience of a man with deliveries to make. "You can write it down if you like. I'll wait."

Something flickered across the guard's face. Men like him spent their lives being defied or groveled to; flatness unsettled them. He waved the pass vaguely. "Go on, then. Before I change my mind."

Juniper walked through the arch, under the carved motto, out onto the harbor road beyond, and did not run. Running was a confession. They walked at the pace of tired legs and aching lungs, past the fish market, past the ropewalk, past the great black hulls of the company ships, until the customs house was a smudge of brick behind them and the drizzle had soaked through the stolen coat to the skin.

Only then, in the lee of a shuttered sailmaker's loft, did they stop and put a hand against the wall and breathe.

Their hands would not steady. They watched them shake and thought, with strange detachment: *that is what twenty years of silence looks like when it finally moves.* The splinter of doubt in the old guard's eyes would find its way somewhere. Guards talked. Guards wrote things down. Somewhere in the customs registry there would be a notation by morning—*courier, J. Vale, irregular papers, released*—and notations had a way of traveling to desks, and desks had a way of belonging to Aldous Wren.

Which meant the clock had just started running faster.

Juniper peeled back the coat's lining with numb fingers and checked the original page. Dry. Intact. The water-stained columns legible, the founder's initials still sitting in the margin like a signature on a confession. Against their ribs, the copy for Ashcombe rode safe in its envelope. Both pieces of the evidence had passed through the mouth of the checkpoint, and the mouth had closed behind them empty.

But the original was still on their body, and their body was still known, and every hour they carried it was an hour the company might close its fist.

They rebuttoned the coat. They pulled the collar up against the rain. Ahead, through the gray curtain of the harbor afternoon, the magistrate's office glowed with early lamplight—but between here and there lay the widest, most exposed stretch of road in the district, and Juniper's legs were still trembling, and somewhere behind them a guard with a splinter of doubt was probably reaching for a pen.

*Not yet,* Juniper told themselves. *Not in daylight. Not while the roads are watched.*

Tonight, then. New Year's Eve. When the whole city would pour into the streets with lanterns and noise and fireworks, when every guard in the district would be pulled to crowd control, when a thousand strangers in masks and borrowed coats would make anonymity cheap as rainwater. Midnight was the deadline the company had set for itself—Wren wanted Sefa indentured and the ledgers clean before the century turned—and so midnight would have to be the deadline Juniper beat them to.

They turned toward the salt house to warn Maren, and had gone perhaps fifty paces when they saw the boy.

He was one of Wren's runners—the quick-legged children the foreman used for messages, quick-eyed too, and this one was leaning against a stack of crab pots across the street with the studied idleness of someone who had been paid to lean. His gaze slid off Juniper's face the way trained gazes did, casual as weather.

But as Juniper passed, the boy pushed off the pots and fell into step behind them, keeping distance, keeping pace.

Juniper did not turn around. They walked on through the drizzle, counting breaths—one, two, three, down—and began, silently, to plan.
