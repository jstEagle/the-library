## 13. Burn Notice

The notice went up on the company board on a Tuesday, nailed there by a boy too young to know what he was pinning to the world.

*By order of the Meridian Trading Company, all ledgers predating the year 1880 are hereby declared unsalvageable, having suffered water damage, mold, and vermin infestation during recent flooding of the lower archive. In the interest of public hygiene, said records will be destroyed by fire on Thursday last bell. Employees and residents are invited to witness.*

Juniper read it three times while the harbor wind snapped the paper against its nail. Around them, divers and lumpers and rope-makers drifted past the board with the practiced blindness of people who had learned long ago that reading company notices was a habit that got you noticed back.

*Hygiene.* Juniper turned the word over like a stone pulled from a riverbed, feeling its weight, its smooth deliberate shape. Someone had sat at a desk with good light and chosen that word. Not *destruction*. Not *disposal*. *Hygiene* — as though the paper itself were sick. As though forty years of names and debts and dates were an infection to be burned out of the body of the company before it could spread into the new century.

The date matched. Of course it matched. The ledger page sewn into Sefa's coat lining — the one Juniper had copied twice, once for the courier run through customs and once more after Wren's men came too close to the salt house — bore the year 1879. Whatever register held the original entry, whatever book recorded the write-off of a living diver named in a dead woman's hand, it lived below the 1880 line.

They were not burning old paper. They were burning the context. And a ledger page without its book was a tooth without a jaw — evidence, yes, but evidence that could be dismissed, doubted, called a forgery by any clerk who cared to sneer.

Juniper pressed their thumb against the notice until the paper dented, then walked back toward the drying sheds without letting their stride change. Twenty years of practice: carry the alarm inside where no one can see it.

* * *

Maren heard the news before Juniper reached her, which meant half the waterfront already knew and had decided, collectively, to shrug.

"Burning the old books." She sat outside the salt house on an upturned crate, hands wrapped around a chipped enamel mug, watching Sefa chase gulls along the tideline with the fierce, silent concentration of a child who had learned that loudness drew attention. "Company's calling it hygiene."

"You knew?"

"I read the same board you did, girl. I just read it faster." Maren sipped her tea. Her heart troubled her these days — she'd told Juniper as much in the flat, unafraid way she told most truths — and she moved like someone conserving fuel for a fire she hadn't lit yet. "Forty years I dove for them. You think this is the first time they've cleaned house? They burned a warehouse of contracts in '85 when the cholera came through. Called it quarantine. Half the town's dead from that season aren't in any book at all now. Just gone, like they never signed, never owed, never drowned."

Sefa came running back, sand in her hair, a gull feather held aloft like a trophy. She stopped when she saw the look Juniper and Maren wore, and something old moved behind her young face — the particular sadness of a child who has learned to read rooms before she learned her letters.

"Is it about my mama's paper?" she asked quietly.

Juniper crouched to her level. "It's about making sure nobody can say your mama's paper is a lie."

"Is it a lie?"

"No." Juniper held her eyes. They had promised each other truth, Juniper and this child, back in the first week, when truth was the only inheritance either of them had to give. "That's exactly why they're doing it."

Sefa considered this with terrible seriousness, then held out the feather. It seemed, in her economy of gestures, to mean something like: *here, so you have one thing that burns slower.* Juniper took it and tucked it behind their ear, and the small absurd tenderness of it cracked something open in their chest that they immediately sealed shut again. There would be time to feel things later. There was always supposed to be time later. Later was the lie the company sold by the pound.

"Maren." Juniper straightened. "The copies. Mine went through customs. Yours is still here?"

"Under the floor with the salt, wrapped in oilcloth. Where you left it." Maren's eyes narrowed. "You're thinking out loud again. I can hear it. What is it you're not saying?"

Juniper looked down the tideline. The company's warehouses stood in their gray ranks above the water, and at the far end, set apart behind its own wall and its own gate, stood the archive — the third checkpoint, the one Juniper had spent weeks believing they might never need to breach, because the original page in Sefa's keeping plus the copy through customs would be enough between them.

But the original page was a fragment. Torn from a larger book. A single line of a song, deniable.

And now the song itself was scheduled to become smoke on Thursday.

"The magistrate won't act on a torn page alone," Juniper said. "Not against the Meridians. Not with the founder's name on half the civic buildings in this town. He'll need to see the entry in its place. Before. After. The diver's contract number cross-referenced. The signature. He'll need the whole body of it, or the family lawyers will pick our one bone clean and hand it back to us as dust."

"So the archive."

"So the archive." Saying it aloud made it real in the way words do — heavy, cold, settling into the stomach like swallowed ballast. "Thursday, everything under 1880 burns publicly. Which means Wednesday night is the last night those books exist as anything but memory. And the archive gate—"

"Hasn't opened for you or me since the day we were indentured," Maren finished. "And Wren's been walking the quarter asking after a missing girl. You think he doesn't know what's in those books? You think this burn isn't *for* him?" She shook her head slowly. "He's not cleaning house, Juniper. He's destroying the crime scene before anyone thinks to cordon it."

* * *

Wednesday passed in the particular agony of a day that knows what tomorrow holds.

Juniper worked the shallows with the other divers, hauling shell and grit, and every hour the sun climbed felt like theft. Wren appeared on the staging platform at midday — crisp coat, kind smile, the foreman's kindness that Juniper had long ago learned to read the way sailors read weather. He watched the boats come in. He watched Juniper watch him. Neither of them waved.

In the afternoon, Juniper walked past the archive wall under the pretext of delivering a chit to the cooperage. The gate stood shut. Two guards at the arch, bored, armed. Above them, the company crest in weathered bronze — the meridian line through the globe, the motto worn nearly smooth: *ALL DEBTS FIND THEIR HOUR.* Juniper had walked beneath those words ten thousand times without ever hearing them. Now they sounded like a threat with excellent manners.

Through the gate's iron lattice, Juniper could see the yard beyond: crates being carried out and stacked in rows, ready for the morning pyre. Clerks moving among them with lists. One crate stood open near the wall, and even at distance Juniper could see the spines of the ledgers inside — leather gone green with damp, gilt lettering flaked to ghosts.

Somewhere in those stacks: a register. An entry. A woman declared drowned on a date Juniper now knew by heart, her contract sold onward in the same neat hand that had once written *gift* inside a brass compass case.

Juniper stood at the wall until the shadows lengthened, counting guards, measuring the interval of their circuits, memorizing which windows of the archive building showed lamplight and which showed nothing. It was hopeless and they knew it was hopeless and they kept counting anyway, because the alternative to counting was standing still, and standing still was how the company liked its people.

That evening, Sefa found them on the doorstep.

She didn't come in right away. She stood in the blue dusk holding something behind her back, and when Juniper raised an eyebrow she brought it around: the brass compass, polished until it shone, clutched in both hands like a bird she was afraid would fly.

"It pointed wrong today," she said. "I was on the rocks and it pointed at the water and I thought maybe it wanted me to go home, except I don't know where home is, so I asked it to point at you instead and it did." She held it out. "Maybe it's broken. Maybe broken things just pick somebody."

Juniper took the compass. Inside the case, hidden beneath the needle's dance, the engraving waited as it always waited: two sets of initials, joined by nothing so formal as a word. Their mother's. The founder's. Intimacy, stamped in brass, decades old.

*Sefa,* Juniper almost said. *There's something I have to try tomorrow and it might go wrong.* But the child's face was open and tired and trusting, and some promises were kinder broken than kept.

"Broken things picking somebody," Juniper said instead. "That's about the truest navigation there is."

Sefa smiled, satisfied, and went inside to where Maren had rice warming. Juniper stayed on the step a moment longer, turning the compass in their fingers, watching the needle swing and settle, swing and settle — never resting, because there was nowhere in this whole harbor it could honestly call north.

* * *

Thursday dawned windless, which was a mercy to the fire and an insult to everyone else.

By last bell the whole waterfront had turned out, drawn less by spectacle than by the gravity that public burnings exert on the guilty and the curious alike. The company had built the pyre in the yard behind the cooperage, visible from the quay, and stacked it high: forty years of ledgers, contract books, dive rosters, debt registers, pay sheets, all of it dragged into daylight for the first and final time. A company man in a clean apron moved along the stacks with a tin of lamp oil, applying it with the reverence of a priest anointing.

Juniper stood at the edge of the crowd with Sefa on their shoulders — visible, ordinary, exactly what a guilty person would never risk. That was the trick of it now. Hiding had become suspicion; only boldness bought safety. So Juniper stood in plain sight and let the crowd press warm around them and watched.

Aldous Wren presided. Of course he did. He stood on a low platform beside the pyre in his best coat, and he gave a short speech about progress, about the century turning, about the wisdom of leaving diseased paper behind and entering the new era with clean books. His voice carried beautifully. He had the gift of making destruction sound like housekeeping.

"These records served their hour," he said, and Juniper's skin crawled at the phrase, at hearing the company motto spoken aloud in Wren's reasonable voice, "and now we release them. The new age keeps new accounts."

Then they lit it.

Fire took the old ledgers the way fire takes anything that has waited a long time to burn — greedily, with a sound like applause. Heat rolled across the crowd and faces flinched back and then leaned in again, because human beings cannot help it. Pages lifted on the thermal currents, blackening as they rose, spiraling up past the warehouse roofs like a migration of scorched birds. Names went up in them. Divers' names. Mothers' names. Contract numbers that had been the whole legal substance of human lives, ascending as ash.

Juniper watched a page turn end over end in the smoke, and for one vertiginous moment imagined reading it — imagined their mother's name printed there in some clerk's careful hand, *declared lost at sea, contract settled,* the sea invoked by men who had never gotten their boots wet.

Beside their ear, Sefa whispered: "Which one was she?"

Juniper's arms tightened around the child's legs. "I don't know," they said, barely audible under the roar of the fire. "That's what they're taking. That's exactly what they're taking."

Because here was the arithmetic Juniper had done all through the night and done again now, watching, helpless, as the flames did their tidy work: the customs copy in the magistrate's potential reach was a fragment — real, damning in outline, but deniable. The original page was a fragment too. But the *book* — the register with the full entry, the surrounding pages, the pattern of write-offs repeated across years and divers, the weight of a system documented in its own handwriting — that book had been the difference between a claim and a case. Between a magistrate frowning and a magistrate signing warrants.

And it was going into the fire, crate by crate, and the crowd was warm, and the company man with the oil tin was smiling.

Except.

Juniper's eyes tracked the clerks working the stacks, and caught what they should have caught days ago: the clerks were not emptying the archive. They were emptying *part* of it. Every crate coming out through the gate bore the same chalk mark — a symbol Juniper recognized now, having seen it stenciled on archive boxes for years without curiosity. Pre-1880. Yes. But only the general registers. Only the volumes the company could afford to lose publicly.

There were books that would never see this fire. Juniper knew it the way a diver knows a current shifting under calm water. Companies like Meridian did not survive forty years by destroying their own leverage — they survived by destroying everyone else's proof while keeping their own. Somewhere behind that archive wall, in a locked room, in a founder's private cabinet, sat the duplicate registers. The real ones. The ones Odile Meridian's father had trusted to no furnace.

The fire cracked and collapsed inward. A shower of sparks went up. The crowd sighed — a sound almost like grief, almost like relief.

Wren stepped down from the platform and, passing along the crowd's edge, paused beside Juniper for exactly one moment. He did not look at them. He looked at the fire.

"A pity," he said mildly. "All that history. Still — hygiene is hygiene. Some things fester if you keep them."

"Some things," Juniper agreed, in the flattest voice they owned.

Wren smiled at the flames and moved on.

* * *

Night came, and the pyre smoked itself down to a red ruin that the tide-wind worried apart. Juniper stood at their window long after Sefa slept, the compass open in one palm, the needle hunting.

Despair had come first — honest, total, a black-water pressure on the chest. Let it drown me, Juniper had thought, watching the burn. Let it be over. Twenty years of silence and the silence wins; the books win; the sea gets credited with murders it never committed.

But despair, Juniper discovered, was a depth like any other. You could sink through it if you equalized. If you kept breathing. If you remembered what the body knew that the mind had been forced to forget.

And beneath the despair, when they sank through it, there was clarity.

The fragments weren't enough. The archive's locked room held the whole truth. And there was exactly one person alive with a key to that room who had recently received, anonymously, a copied page in her father's hand — a page Juniper themselves had slipped to her, gambling that conscience lives even in the enemy's house, gambling on the estranged daughter who had grown up with this very compass hanging from her childhood neck.

One person. One key. Two days, maybe three, before Wren connected the missing child to the missing page to the quiet diver who watched fires too carefully.

Juniper closed the compass. The needle stilled.

At dawn, they would write a note that named a place — the tidal flats at low tide, where bargains could be struck out of sight of both the company's windows and God's. And they would deliver it to the one address in this town where the enemy slept.

The archive was the last door. It was time to find out whose side the key stood on.
