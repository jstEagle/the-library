## 15. Bargain at Low Tide

The tidal flats at dawn were a country that belonged to no one. For a few hours each day the sea withdrew and left behind its ledger—mudflats scored with channels, stranded jellyfish gone to glass, the ribs of some old boat the company had written off decades ago and never bothered to burn. Juniper walked out onto it with their boots in hand, bare feet finding the cold silt, because the mud held prints and prints could be read. Barefoot prints belonged to clam-gatherers, to children, to anyone. Boots belonged to someone going somewhere on purpose.

They had chosen the hour and the ground carefully. Low tide meant no boats could come close; the flats' only approach was the long causeway from town, visible for half a mile. If Wren or his men came, Juniper would see them coming across all that naked distance, the way you see a wave building before it breaks.

Odile Meridian was already there.

She stood near the old wreck's ribs with her skirt hem darkened to the knee, her shoes abandoned somewhere behind her—a small surrender she had probably not planned and would not acknowledge. She looked smaller than she had in the lamplit alcove of her father's library, when she had read the copied page and gone white as paper salt. Out here, with the gray light coming up flat off the water, she was just a woman of thirty-odd years standing in mud, holding a parasol she clearly did not need.

"You found the place," Juniper said.

"I grew up on this coast." Odile's voice was clipped, precise, a voice trained in rooms where every sentence might be repeated back to someone powerful. "My father used to bring me down here to look at the tide pools. He said the sea was the company's first warehouse." She looked at the wreck ribs. "He never once mentioned what was stored in it."

Juniper stopped three paces away. Close enough to talk without raising a voice. Far enough that if this was a trap—and they had rehearsed the possibility every night since the anonymous page went under the Meridian door—they would not be taken in the first reach.

"You read it," Juniper said.

"I read it." Odile's jaw tightened. "The handwriting is my father's. The seal is my father's. The date—" She stopped herself, and Juniper watched her do what Juniper themselves had done a week ago over a lamplight table: arrive at a number and refuse to say it aloud, as if saying it made it true.

"Say the date," Juniper said quietly.

"March 1881." Her voice cracked on the last syllable like ice under a heel. "Two days after the *Meridian Star* returned short-handed. Two days after—" She pressed her lips together. "There was a diver. A woman. The entry says drowned, body unrecovered, contract voided by write-off, remaining debt transferred to sale of indenture." She finally turned to face Juniper fully. "You knew her."

It was not a question, but Juniper answered it anyway, because they had spent twenty years not answering it and the silence had grown teeth.

"She was my mother."

The words went out over the flats and nothing echoed. The mud drank sound the way it drank everything. But something changed in the air between them—the way the water changes pressure before a storm you cannot yet see.

Odile looked at them for a long moment. Then she sat down, abruptly, on the curved rib of the wreck, skirts pooling in the silt, a lady of the founder's house sitting in the mud like a fishwife. It was, Juniper thought, the most honest thing he'd seen anyone from the company do in twenty years.

"They told me she ran," Juniper said. "That divers who vanish are divers who flee their debts. That I shouldn't ask after her because asking marked me as ungrateful." They turned the brass compass over in their palm, its case worn smooth. "I learned not to ask. I learned it so well I forgot I'd ever wanted to."

"My father taught me a different lesson with the same result." Odile stared out at the waterline, far off now, a silver seam between mud and sky. "He taught me that some ledgers are not for reading. He kept a locked case in his study and when I was nine I opened it and he—"

She stopped. Recalibrated. Juniper recognized the maneuver: give them the shape of the wound without the wound itself.

"He convinced me I had imagined it," she finished. "For thirty years I have been grateful to him for convincing me." She laughed, short and bitter, a sound with no humor in it anywhere. "And then a stranger slips a page under my door and I open my father's register duplicate in the archive and find forty-one entries in his hand. Forty-one divers written off alive. Sold north, sold to the guano works, sold to God knows what. And my gratitude turns out to have been the lock on the case."

* * *

The tide was beginning its slow turn. Somewhere beneath the flats, the sea was gathering itself to come home, and both of them felt it without speaking of it: the clock under everything, ticking backward toward them.

"The archive," Juniper said. "Tell me what you can actually do."

"I can unlock it. My key opens the outer room. The register duplicates are in the iron cabinets—those take a second key, but the clerk who keeps it drinks, and the cabinet locks are old Meridian work; my father had me learn the mechanism when I was fourteen because he thought precision was charming in a daughter." Her mouth twisted. "I can have both open within a quarter hour of entering. What I cannot do is explain why I'm there if anyone asks. Which means whatever happens inside must happen fast and must happen clean."

"And your price."

Odile's chin came up. Here it was, then. Juniper had known since the slip of paper went under her door that nothing from inside that house came free.

"After the magistrate has seen it—after warrants issue, after it's done its work—you destroy the page. The original and the copy. All of it."

"No."

"You haven't even heard—"

"I heard." Juniper's voice stayed level, the voice of a diver conserving breath. "You want the entry naming your father erased. Everything else can survive; his name burns."

"I want my father's name out of history's hands," Odile said, and now her composure slipped enough to show the thing underneath, which was fear—not of the company but of memory itself, the same fear the company farmed in everyone it touched. "Do you know what will happen to me? Not the scandal. The scandal I can survive. But he is still alive, Lockridge. Old, yes, failing, yes, but alive, and he built an empire on making people forget, and if he learns his own daughter carried his crimes to a magistrate while he still draws breath—"

"He'll do what?" Juniper stepped closer, mud sucking faintly at their feet. "What does a dying man do to the daughter who betrayed him? Disinherit her? Write her off?" They let the word sit between them. "*Write her off?* That's the family trade, isn't it. When a person becomes inconvenient, you declare them gone and sell what's left."

"That is not fair."

"No," Juniper agreed. "Nothing about this is fair. Fairness drowned in 1881."

* * *

They argued the way the tide argues with the shore—in waves, retreating and returning, each pass wearing something away. Odile pointed out that destroying one page preserved the rest; the register duplicate held forty-one entries and the magistrate needed only evidence, not vengeance. Juniper pointed out that pages were not the point—that a record which could be edited by the guilty was not a record at all but a hostage, forever renegotiable, forever one bargain away from vanishing. That was how it had worked for twenty years. That was how *everything* worked here.

"You think I don't understand wanting it gone?" Juniper said at last. They held up the compass. "Inside this case there's an engraving. J.L. and E.M., cut into the brass. Your father gave this to my mother. Do you understand what that means? He knew her. She wasn't cargo to him, she was—" Their throat closed. They had not said any of this aloud before, not even to Maren. "Whatever she was to him, he wrote her off anyway. So no. I will not destroy one word of that page. Not for your safety. Not for mine. The whole reason we're standing in the mud at dawn is that people kept agreeing to let things disappear."

Odile was quiet for a long time. The first wavelets had begun to reach the far edge of the flats, thin sheets of water sliding up the silt and withdrawing, each one a little closer.

"If I help you and the page survives," she said finally, "my name may end up beside his in the magistrate's file. Daughter of the founder. Accessory after the fact. You understand I'm risking exactly what you're risking."

"Yes."

"And you won't trade. Even one page. Even his name."

"Not one letter."

Another silence, longer. Out on the horizon a fishing boat was coming around the headland, too far to matter, too close to ignore entirely. Juniper tracked it without turning their head, the old diver's habit of watching everything while appearing to watch nothing.

Then Odile laughed again—but differently this time, a real sound, startled out of her.

"You're just like him," she said. "Did you know that? The obstinacy. The refusal to move one inch off a position once taken. He used to negotiate contracts at the harbor office and men twice his size would leave having conceded things they swore they wouldn't." She shook her head. "God help me, I'm about to trust a diver because they remind me of my father."

"Don't trust me," Juniper said. "Trust the arithmetic. You can walk away right now and spend the rest of your life being grateful for things that aren't true. Or you can open two locks tonight and find out what actually happened, with witnesses and warrants and a magistrate to make it stick. One of those futures ends. The other one begins."

"The century turns tomorrow night," Odile murmured. "The archive closes at six for the holiday inventory. After that, no one enters until the second of January—and by then Wren will have moved whatever he wants moved. It has to be tomorrow evening. New Year's Eve. Everyone watching the harbor, the fireworks, the crowds." She looked up. "You realize what he'll be doing while the whole town counts down. Purging. Burning. He burned the pre-1880 ledgers publicly last week—for hygiene, they said. Tomorrow night he'll finish the job privately."

"Then we go tomorrow night."

"And the page?"

Juniper crouched, picked up a fragment of shell, and drew a line in the wet mud between them. Then a second line crossing it. Then a third.

"The original goes to the magistrate in my hand. The copy Maren holds goes to the clerk separately, by a different road, an hour later. If either of us is stopped, the other arrives." They looked up. "And after the warrants issue—after it's law and not paper—I'll put the original in the public registry myself. Sealed copies, witnessed, beyond anyone's reach, including yours. Including mine."

Odile studied the lines in the mud. The tide had reached them now; the nearest channel was filling, water threading silver through their drawn lines, erasing them slowly from the outside in.

"Witnessed copies," she said at last. "Sealed and public. And the magistrate sees everything—all forty-one entries, no exceptions." She exhaled, long and unsteady, the breath of a woman stepping off a ledge. "Then we have a bargain, Juniper Lockridge. God forgive us both."

"We don't need forgiveness," Juniper said. "We need the tide."

They shook on it—her hand fine-boned and cold, theirs scarred rope-and-coral hard—and Juniper thought: *this is the strangest contract of my life, and the first one I wrote myself.*

* * *

They gathered their things in the growing light. Odile retrieved her shoes from behind the wreck ribs and put them on with the resigned efficiency of someone re-entering her own life. At the foot of the causeway she paused.

"The child," she said, not turning around. "The one the page came with. Wren has been asking after her. Door to door. He told the neighbors he's looking for a lost little girl to reunite her with her family." Odile's shoulders were rigid. "I grew up listening to my father's men lie. I know what that sentence costs to say and what it's for. Get the girl somewhere Wren's kindness can't reach before tomorrow night. Because if he finds her first, there will be no midnight filing. There will be a new indenture signed at 11:59 and no one to stop it."

Ice went through Juniper that had nothing to do with the tide water now lapping at their ankles.

"She's hidden," Juniper said. "She's safe."

"People said that about my mother's ledgers," Odile said, "right up until the night they burned." She climbed the causeway toward town, a solitary figure growing smaller against the brightening sky, and did not look back.

Juniper stood alone on the flats as the sea came home around their ankles, cold rising past the knee, the compass heavy in their hand. They should run for the salt house now, this minute, check on Sefa with their own eyes—

But the tide was turning, and the flats flooded fast, and the long way around by the dunes would cost an hour, and Odile's warning was one hour old already, maybe two.

Juniper began to run.
