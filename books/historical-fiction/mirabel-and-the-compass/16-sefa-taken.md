## 16. Sefa Taken

The morning of the last day of the century began with gulls screaming over the drying racks, and Juniper woke in the salt house with Sefa's small foot pressed against their ribs and thought, absurdly, *this is what it would have been like.*

They had one full day of it. That was the bargain Juniper had made with themselves without ever speaking it aloud: one day where the ledger page stayed folded in oilcloth beneath the floorboards, where the compass stayed in the inner pocket, where the name *Meridian* stayed unspoken. Maren had gone out at dawn to buy rice and lamp oil and, if the fishmonger's stall was open, dried mackerel, because Sefa had eaten three pieces the night before and asked for more in a voice so small and so certain that Maren had laughed—a sound Juniper had not heard from her in twenty years.

One day of being simply what they were. A diver and a child. Nothing owed, nothing owed for.

Juniper lay still and watched the light come through the gaps in the salt-house boards, striping the girl's dark hair. She slept the way children sleep in places where they have decided they are safe: entirely, bonelessly, one arm flung over her eyes as if even in dreams she was refusing to look at something. Juniper knew that refusal. They had practiced it for two decades. It was the first thing the company taught you, before you ever touched the water: *the sea takes what it takes, and you look at nothing, and you say nothing, and you live.*

Sefa stirred. Her hand came down from her eyes and found the brass compass, which Juniper had let her keep under her pillow, and she curled around it the way other children curled around dolls.

"You're watching me," she said, without opening her eyes.

"I'm watching the light."

"You're watching me." She opened her eyes then, one at a time, and grinned. "Maren said we could go see the boats. The real ones. Not the company boats."

"After the rice," Juniper said. "After the racks are turned."

Sefa groaned with the full theatrical despair of the six-year-old, and got up anyway, and the morning went on.

* * *

They turned the racks together. It was work Juniper had done as a child, before the diving, before the debt deepened—carrying trays of wet salt crystal from the evaporation pans to the drying floors, turning each tray so the sun would not bake one side pale and leave the other gray. The salt house sat on the flats beyond the last of the company warehouses, close enough to smell the harbor but far enough that the foremen rarely bothered. Maren had dried her own salt here for years, sold it in small paper twists, and no one had ever thought it worth watching.

That was the mistake, Juniper would understand later. Nothing in Meridian's harbor was ever unwatched. It was only unwatched until it mattered.

Sefa worked beside them, her small hands scooping crystal with a seriousness that made the work look sacred. She had salt dust in her eyelashes. She had begun, in the last two days, to talk—not the fragments she'd arrived with, not the woman's face and the boat and the voice saying *forget*—but real talk, questions, observations, the running commentary of a child discovering that the world had things in it besides fear.

"The salt comes from the water," she said, "but the water doesn't miss it."

Juniper paused with a tray half-turned. "What?"

"When you take the salt out, the sea just makes more. Maren said so." Sefa looked up, squinting against the light. "So you can't really take anything away from the sea. It just makes more of it. So why do people drown for it?"

Juniper had no answer that was not the true one, and the true one was: *because the company taught us the sea's abundance belongs to them, and taught us to dive for their abundance until our ears bled, and wrote down what we earned in books we were never allowed to read.* They said, instead, "Because they were lied to about what the water owed them."

Sefa considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Were you lied to?"

"Yes."

"Did you believe it?"

"For a long time."

"And now?"

Juniper looked out at the flats, at the silver skin of water over the mud, at the harbor beyond with its masts and its cranes and its flag—the black trident on white—hanging slack in the windless morning. "Now I'm learning to stop."

Sefa nodded, satisfied, and went back to her salt, and Juniper stood there with a tray in their hands and felt something in their chest that had no name in any language the company permitted. It was not hope. Hope was a word for people with exits. It was closer to *appetite*—a sudden, physical hunger for a future, for the specific future in which this child ate breakfast in a kitchen Juniper paid for, in a house with a door Juniper owned, on a morning when no one knocked.

They would get less than four hours of it.

* * *

Maren came back from the market walking too fast.

Juniper saw it from thirty paces—the set of her shoulders, the way her eyes went to the flats behind her twice before she reached the salt house, the basket swinging in a grip too tight for rice. Twenty years of diving beside a woman teaches you her weather. Maren Okonkwo in a calm sea moved like syrup. Maren in this particular weather moved like a squall line.

"Company men," she said, before she was through the door. "Three of them. At the harbor road, asking at the boatwright's. Asking about a child."

Juniper's hands went still on the tray. "Wren?"

"Not him. His people. But they carry his voice, you can tell—the way they ask. Not *have you seen.* They ask *when did you last see.*" Maren set the basket down hard. "That's a man who already knows the answer."

The trays. Juniper looked at the trays of salt, at the neat rows, at the evidence of habitation—two bowls, a child's cup, the small straw mat Sefa slept on, folded but present. A place that had been a hiding place for two days and had become, without anyone deciding it, a home.

"We move her now," Juniper said. "The fishing families on the north spit—"

"The north spit road passes the harbor road. You'd be walking her past them." Maren was already moving, snatching the mat, the cup, sweeping the child's traces into the basket with the economy of a woman who had hidden things from foremen before and knew the choreography. "The flats. We go out on the flats and around by the channel, and we come at the spit from the water side. Low tide's in an hour. We can walk it."

Sefa stood in the doorway with the compass in both hands, watching them, and Juniper saw her do the thing that broke something open in Juniper's chest: she began, very quietly, to fold herself smaller. Shoulders in. Chin down. Eyes up and watchful. The posture of a child who had learned already that adults moving fast meant adults about to leave, or adults about to be angry, or adults about to be taken, and that the correct response was to become a thing too small to notice.

"Sefa." Juniper crouched. "Look at me."

She looked. Her eyes were the color of the harbor in December, and just as hard to see the bottom of.

"Nobody is leaving you. Do you understand? Whatever happens today, I am not leaving you, and Maren is not leaving you. We are going to walk somewhere, and it will be boring, and you will complain about it, and that is all it will be."

"Like the salt work?"

"Exactly like the salt work."

She almost smiled. Then, from the direction of the harbor road, thin with distance but unmistakable: a whistle. Two notes, then one. Juniper had heard that signal ten thousand times across twenty years of decks and docks—the foremen's call to gather, to line up, to be counted. It was the sound of being inventory.

"They're not coming here," Maren said. "They can't know—"

But even as she said it, Juniper saw them. Three figures on the harbor road, small with distance, and then a fourth, and then the fourth resolving into a shape Juniper knew the way you know a scar: broad-shouldered, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back, walking the flats' edge the way a man walks a garden he owns.

Aldous Wren had not sent his people.

Aldous Wren had come himself.

* * *

Later—much later, in the dark, in the cold, in the water—Juniper would take the morning apart piece by piece and find the moment where it all turned. They would try to find the choice they could have made differently. They would fail, because there wasn't one. That was the architecture of the company's power, and Juniper had always known it and had let themselves forget it for one salt-scented day: it did not defeat you with force. It arranged the world so that every door you could reach opened onto the same room.

Wren did not run. Wren never ran. He walked the last hundred yards across the flats at the pace of a man out for his health, his boots printing the wet mud, and his three men spread behind him in a line that was not quite a line—not a formation, nothing a magistrate could call an arrest, just three large men standing where a child might run.

"Master Lockridge." Wren's voice carried easily over the flats, mild as morning. "And Mistress Okonkwo. And—" he paused, and his eyes found Sefa, and Juniper watched him do it, watched him look at the girl the way an assessor looks at stock, "—and this would be the child."

"There's no child here," Maren said. "There's my salt house and my salt and my grandniece visiting from upcoast."

"From upcoast." Wren smiled. It was a good smile. It had been reassuring divers for thirty years. "Mistress Okonkwo, I have stood at your son's grave. I signed the allowance that kept you fed the winter after. I want you to hear that I take no pleasure in what has to happen next, and that it happens next regardless."

He took a folded paper from his coat. Juniper's whole body went tight, because they knew the paper stock—they had carried it, filed it, watched it nailed to company posts for twenty years. Notice of indenture assessment. The first document in the machinery that turned a person into a line in a ledger.

"She's six years old," Juniper said. Their voice came out low and even, and they were grateful to it, because everything under it was not even at all. It was a scream with a lid on it, and the lid was twenty years old and rattling.

"She's six years old and unclaimed," Wren said. "No kin registered. No guardian of record. Found wandering in a company district during a period of civil disorder." He said the words like a man reading a shipping manifest, and Juniper understood with a cold, sliding horror that he had rehearsed them, that the paper in his hand had been drafted days ago, that the machinery had been oiled and waiting since the night the child first knocked on a door in the rain. "The company assumes wardship of unclaimed minors under the Harbor Ordinance. She'll be housed, fed, schooled, and entered into apprenticeship at the age of eight. Diving, given her evident constitution."

"She has kin."

"Do you have papers saying so?"

"I am her kin."

"Are you." Wren looked at Juniper with something that was almost gentle, and the gentleness was the worst thing Juniper had ever seen, because it was the look of a man who had already checked. "Juniper Lockridge. Indentured diver, Meridian Trading Company, contract term twenty-two years, eleven remaining. You cannot claim guardianship of a stray cat. You are, yourself, property. It's in the books."

The flats were very quiet. The tide was out and the water stood far off, a bright line at the edge of the mud, and between Juniper and that line stood Wren and his three men, and the paper, and the whole gray weight of everything the company had ever written down.

Every silence Juniper had ever kept stood up inside them at once, and each one had a face.

The diver on the *Cormorant* who came up bleeding from the ear and begged them not to report it, and Juniper had said nothing, and the man had dove again the next week and not come up, and Juniper had said nothing then either, because the foreman was watching and the foreman's watching was survival. The night sounds from the overseer's quarters that everyone on the indenture row heard and no one named. The ledger page Sefa had brought, and the twenty years of other pages like it, and every diver who had ever surfaced from a write-off's shadow and looked at Juniper, and Juniper looking back, and both of them choosing the water's silence over the air's truth, because the water's silence kept you alive one more day.

*Say nothing and live.* It had worked. It had worked for twenty years, and now the silence had come due all at once, with interest, and the debt was a six-year-old girl standing in salt dust with a compass in her hands, watching the adults fail her the way adults had always failed everyone.

"No," Juniper said.

Wren's eyebrows rose a fraction. "I beg your pardon?"

"I said no." Juniper stepped in front of Sefa. It was not a plan. It was not strategy. It was the body moving before the trained mind could stop it, twenty years of strategic silence cracking down the middle like ice. "You will not have her. Write what you want. Bring what you want. She is not going into your books."

"Juniper." Wren's voice dropped, and now it was just the two of them, foreman to diver, the old intimacy of the deck. "I have watched you for twenty years. You are the best diver I have ever signed, and the smartest, and the quietest, and I have respected all three. Don't make me remember, on the last day of the century, that respect and usefulness are different things."

"Then remember this instead." Juniper's heart was slamming, and their voice did not shake, and they did not know where the voice was coming from, only that it sounded like someone older, someone drowned, someone whose name they had not said aloud in twenty years. "The century turns tonight. And everything your company wrote down in the old one is going to be read out loud in the new one."

Something moved behind Wren's eyes. It was there and gone in half a second, but Juniper had spent twenty years reading foremen's faces across the water, and they saw it clearly: not surprise. Confirmation. He knew about the ledger. He had known for days. The kindness, the ration, the warning about troublemakers—it had all been the same gesture, a man checking the water depth before he stepped in.

"That," Wren said softly, "was a very foolish thing to say in front of witnesses."

He turned to his men. "Take the child."

* * *

They did not take her easily, because Sefa fought.

Juniper would carry that too, into the water, into the dark: the image of a six-year-old girl going rigid and screaming, the compass flying from her hands and landing in the salt mud, her small fists and her teeth and her voice breaking on a word that was not a word, just a sound, the sound of a child being erased. One of the men—Juniper would remember his face, would find his face again, would make sure of it—picked her up under the arms like a sack of rice, and she kicked, and Maren grabbed the man's arm and was thrown down into the salt trays, and the trays scattered, and a year of Maren's careful work glittered across the mud like broken glass.

Juniper moved. They got two steps—one, two—before the second man's arm caught them across the chest, and the third took their other arm, and Juniper was strong from twenty years of hauling themselves up boat ladders with a hundred pounds of gear, but two men are two men, and they held.

Wren crouched in front of Sefa where she hung in the first man's grip, sobbing, and did something Juniper had not expected. He took the brass compass out of the mud. He wiped it clean with his thumb, unhurried, thorough, and held it up so she could see it.

"This is company property," he said to her, in the tone adults use to teach children letters. "It has a maker's mark. Do you see? Everything in this harbor has a maker's mark. Everything belongs to someone. You'll learn."

He put the compass in his own coat pocket.

He did not know what he had done. He could not have known. He held in his pocket the single object that could open the founder's own house, the engraved proof of intimacy between Aldous Meridian and a drowned diver, and he had taken it from a child's hands to teach her a lesson about ownership, and Juniper felt something turn over in their chest that was not fear anymore and was not rage either but was cold and patient and very, very clear.

"Master Lockridge." Wren straightened his coat. "You'll report to the compound office by nightfall for a conduct review. Failure to appear will be treated as breach of contract, and breach is settled at the intake channel, as you know." He paused. "I'd hate to lose my best diver. But the century's ending, and I'm told it's a time for settling accounts."

They took her across the flats toward the harbor road. She screamed for a long time. Then she stopped screaming, and that was worse. Juniper watched her go, held by men who did not let go, getting smaller against the gray sky, until the harbor warehouses swallowed her, and the sound of her stopped, and the flats were empty except for Maren on her knees in the ruined salt and the wind coming in off the water.

* * *

Maren was bleeding from the mouth where she'd bit her lip in the fall. She sat in the wreckage of her trays and did not cry, and Juniper knelt in the mud beside her, and for a long moment neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left that silence could protect.

"I told her nobody was leaving her," Juniper said. The words came out flat and dead. "I told her it would be like the salt work."

"Juniper—"

"Twenty years." Juniper's hands were shaking now, finally, the lid off, the scream arriving late and in the wrong key. "Twenty years I said nothing, and it bought me nothing, and it bought her nothing, and he walked across the flats and took her like taking a tray off a rack, and he *smiled*—"

"He smiled because smiling works." Maren took Juniper's face in both her hands, and her grip was a diver's grip, hard and sure. "Listen to me. Listen. He took her before midnight because after midnight she's a new century's child and the old papers rot. He's afraid. Wren does not walk the flats for health. He came himself because he is afraid of what you have, and a frightened man makes mistakes."

"He has the compass."

"Then you'll take it back." Maren's eyes were bright and hard. "And you'll take her. And you know exactly how to get inside that compound, because I watched your mother teach you, on this same shore, when you were smaller than that girl is now. The intake channel. The tide. The dark."

Juniper looked out at the water. The tide had turned; the bright line was coming back across the mud, slow and certain, filling the channel that ran under the compound wall, the channel every diver knew and no diver spoke of, the company's own artery, carrying seawater in to the holding tanks, and carrying, tonight, if the water permitted it, one diver who had finally run out of silence.

Nightfall. The conduct review. The century's last hours.

Juniper stood up, and the mud let them go, and the tide kept coming.
