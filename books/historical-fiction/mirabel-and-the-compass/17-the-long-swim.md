## 17. The Long Swim

The water took Juniper the way it always had—cold first at the ankles, then the wrists, then all at once when they slid off the mud shelf and let the tide close over their head like a door.

They had stripped to their diving shirt and trousers, boots left wedged in a rock on the flats where no one who wasn't looking would ever look. The ledger page was gone from them now—Maren carried one copy sewn into her coat lining, Odile Meridian had the other folded against her breast like a letter from a dead man—and what remained in Juniper's keeping was nothing but a knife, a shuttered lantern wrapped in oilcloth, and the brass compass hanging at their throat on its cord, riding cold against the sternum.

And Sefa's absence. That too they carried. It was heavier than everything else put together.

The intake channel began as a gash of black water between two walls of cut stone, wide enough for a skiff at high tide, running straight under the compound wall toward the holding tanks where the company kept its oysters before shucking. Every diver on the coast knew about it. Every diver had been taught, in the same breath as they were taught their letters and their knots, that the channel was company property, that the grating at its far end was company property, that anything found inside the channel belonged to the company, including a drowned child, including a drowned diver, including a diver who had drowned on purpose trying to slip past the wall at night.

Juniper had been eight years old, standing on this exact mud shelf, when a foreman with Wren's own patient voice had explained all of this to the new indentures. He had smiled while he said it. Juniper could still feel the smile, the way you can feel a scar's shape years after the wound has closed.

They trod water in the dark and breathed.

The trick—the whole of it, the only thing that mattered—was not to fight. The tide was coming in, which meant the channel was filling, which meant the current ran inward, toward the compound, carrying them if they let it carry them. Fighting the sea got divers killed faster than sharks did. Their mother had said that once—or someone had said that once; the memory came up out of Juniper like a pearl out of a shell, wet and whole and unasked-for—*the water is stronger than you will ever be, so stop arguing with it and start asking.*

Juniper asked. The water answered. The black stone walls began to slide past.

* * *

There was light ahead—not lantern light but the pale, sourceless glow that comes up out of deep water when the moon is behind cloud, the glow old divers called *the sea remembering the sky*. It turned the channel into a corridor of gray glass. Juniper dove under, opened their eyes against the sting of salt, and saw the walls going down farther than they should have, down into green-black nothing where the stones were furred with weed and the weed waved slow, like hair underwater, like a woman's long hair drifting in a current—

Their mother's hair, in the shallows off Diver's Point, the year before she vanished. Juniper had been small enough to stand chest-deep while she swam out beyond them, and her hair had spread on the surface like ink, like wings, like something holy, and she had called back to them—called what? Called *what?*

Nothing. The memory gave nothing. It never gave anything but the picture, the picture without the sound, twenty years of a silent film running behind Juniper's eyes.

*Later,* they told themselves, the way they had told themselves since childhood. *Breathe now. Grieve later. Later is a country you can reach by swimming.*

They surfaced again inside the channel proper, and the walls closed overhead, and the sky became a ribbon of starless gray no wider than a hand.

* * *

The first danger was the grate.

Every account agreed there was an iron grate across the channel where it passed beneath the wall—a grate to keep divers out, or in, depending on which side of it you were born on. But accounts were all Juniper had, and accounts were twenty years stale, and the company had spent those twenty years getting richer, and rich companies mended their fences.

So Juniper went under again and swam the last hundred yards blind along the wall, one hand trailing the stone, counting strokes, and at the count of ninety their fingers found iron.

Bars. Vertical, thick as a man's wrist, crusted with barnacle and weed but iron still, set into the channel floor and rising up out of sight into the dark above the waterline. Juniper hung in the cold water beside them and felt their heart begin its old diver's arithmetic: air remaining, distance remaining, strength remaining, and somewhere behind all of it, patience remaining, because panic spent air faster than work ever did.

They came up under the low curve of the channel roof, where the air pocket sat thick and salt-smelling between water and stone, and held the compass case to the bars and listened. Beyond the grate the channel widened—they could hear it, the echo opening out the way sound opens out when a passage enters a hall. The holding tanks lay somewhere in that darkness, and above the tanks, the pump house, and above the pump house, the compound yard, and somewhere above all of it, in a lit office with a lamp turned low, Aldous Wren was writing down whatever he intended to do to a six-year-old girl at midnight.

Juniper pressed their forehead to the cold iron and breathed once, twice, three times, and made themselves think instead of feel.

A grate this old, this fouled, in a channel this essential—the company could not simply remove it, because the channel fed the tanks and the tanks needed protection from more than divers. They needed protection from driftwood, from storm debris, from the great tangles of net and rope the monsoon washed down the coast every season. Which meant the grate could not be solid. Which meant—

Which meant there was a gap. There was always a gap. Water moved through it; things smaller than a skiff moved through it; and a diver who had learned their trade slipping between the legs of coral heads no wider than a coffin could move through it too.

They dove. They worked along the bars from the floor upward, feeling with both hands now, letting the barnacles take their skin as payment. At shoulder height above the seabed, between the third bar and the fourth, the crust fell away under their palms and their fingers found open water—a gap of perhaps two feet, choked with old rope and a snag of fishing net gone soft as cloth, but open, open all the way through.

Juniper backed off, filled their lungs until their ribs ached, and went through.

It was worse than any dive they had ever made in open sea, because the sea forgives mistakes and iron does not. The rope caught them at the hip. The net closed over their shoulders like hands. For three full seconds—one, two, three, the old count their mother's world had beaten into every diver's bones—Juniper was held fast in the dark with their air burning and the bars on either side of them like the teeth of something vast, and the animal part of them, the part that wanted to live, screamed at them to thrash.

The other part—the part that had been trained into them on the decks of company boats by men whose kindness was surveillance and whose silence was survival—that part said: *stop.* And Juniper stopped. Went limp as kelp. Let the incoming tide push them, inch by inch, through the tangle, the way a pearl eases out of an oyster, and then the net released them all at once and they were through, through, through, rising into open black water inside the wall, and their head broke the surface and the first breath they drew was so sweet it felt like theft.

They hung there in the dark, gasping quietly, laughing silently at nothing, alive.

The body remembers, they thought. The mind forgets, because forgetting keeps you safe, keeps you small, keeps you employable. But the body—the body had been keeping every lesson their mother ever paid for, in trust, all these years, waiting for the day its owner came to collect.

This was the day.

* * *

The holding tanks sat in a long shed open on the seaward side, and Juniper crossed the last of the channel underwater and came up among the pilings where the shadows were deepest. From there it was a matter of watching. The pump house stood forty feet off, lamplit, and inside it a man was doing whatever pump-men did at night—the rhythmic knock and sigh of the machinery covered him, and covered Juniper too, when the time came to move.

They waited through two cycles of the pumps. Then, in the trough of the noise, they crossed the open ground in a low run, wet feet silent on wet stone, and reached the shadowed corner of the pump house where the drainage sluice ran down toward the tanks. Above the sluice, a ladder. Above the ladder, a catwalk. Above the catwalk, the compound itself: the barracks, the counting house, the archive with its iron door and its single key.

Odile would be at that door within the hour. All Juniper had to do was be there too, alive, unseen, with a knife and a lantern and twenty years of inherited silence ready to break like a dam.

They climbed.

At the top of the ladder they paused, straddling the rail, and looked out over the compound for the first time from the inside—from the side of the wall they had been brought through as a child, weeping, clutching a stranger's hand, and had never once seen again except from the fields, from the boats, from the wrong side of every fence the company owned.

It looked smaller from here. That was the thing no one warned you about. Twenty years of fear had built the compound up into something vast in Juniper's mind, a fortress, a kingdom, the fixed center around which their whole constrained life had turned—and now, crouched on a catwalk with salt drying white on their arms, they looked down at the squat buildings and the muddy yard and the few dim lamps, and thought: *this is it? This is what I gave my whole life to being afraid of?*

Somewhere in that sprawl, Wren's office window glowed yellow. Juniper watched it the way sailors watch a storm front.

Then they heard voices below—two men crossing the yard toward the pump house, unhurried, talking—and Juniper flattened against the catwalk planks and became, in the old phrase every diver knew, part of the architecture. The footsteps passed. The pump house door opened and shut. The voices sank under the machinery's noise.

Juniper exhaled and moved along the catwalk, away from the pump house, toward the dark bulk of the archive wing.

* * *

Halfway there, crouched in the lee of a chimney stack, they allowed themselves thirty seconds. Thirty seconds was affordable. They took the compass cord in both hands and lifted the brass case, and even in this darkness they could feel it—the worn smoothness of the lid, the little catch that clicked, the engraving inside that named two people who should never have shared a gift. Their thumb found the catch and did not press it. Not yet. Pressing it out here, alone, was a luxury, and luxuries were for people with futures.

Instead they held it the way you hold a hand. *I'm coming,* they told the woman inside the metal, the woman whose face the water had taken and the ledgers had sold and whose name Juniper had not spoken aloud in twenty years because speaking it was the one thing the company could never quite forbid, and therefore the one thing Juniper had never dared. *Whatever happens tonight, I'm coming, and tomorrow I'll say it. Out loud. On the shore. Where everyone can hear.*

Below, in the yard, a bell rang once—the quarter hour, or a watch change, or some signal Juniper couldn't read—and a moment later the archive door swung open, spilling a blade of lamplight across the flagstones, and a figure stepped out into it.

Not a guard. Guards didn't move like that. This figure was slight, hooded, and stood for a heartbeat in the light with her chin lifted, listening, exactly the way Odile Meridian had stood on the tidal flats listening for Juniper's answer—like a woman deciding, once and finally, which side of her own name she belonged to.

Then she looked up—straight up, as though the dark itself had whispered to her—and found Juniper's eyes above her in the chimney's shadow.

She raised one hand. Not a wave. A warning: *stay.* She turned back toward the doorway, where a second figure had appeared—a man, broad, uniformed, lantern in hand—and Odile Meridian's voice drifted up across the yard, clear and cold and carrying, pitched for the whole compound to hear:

"You're late, Captain. My father's papers have been waiting an hour, and I don't wait twice."

The guard stammered something. The door stayed open. And Juniper understood, with the sudden vertigo of a diver breaking surface into weather they hadn't predicted, that the archive was standing empty behind that open door, that the key was in play, that the century had perhaps ninety minutes left in it—and that the next thing they did would have to be done *now*.
