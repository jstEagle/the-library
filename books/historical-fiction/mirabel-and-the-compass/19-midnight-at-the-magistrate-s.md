## 19. Midnight at the Magistrate's

The documents were against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth, bound with the cord I had cut from Sefa's ankle before Wren's men carried her away. Two pages and a testimony sworn in a salt-drying house by a woman whose heart beat like a tired drum. Everything I had stolen from the company, everything the company had stolen from me, pressed flat between my ribs where a diver learns to keep their breath.

The magistrate's court sat on Constitution Hill, four streets and one lifetime away from the harbor district. It was half past ten. The century would end whether I arrived or not.

I went out through Maren's back window, into an alley that smelled of fish heads and gunpowder, because every child on the waterfront had been cracking firecrackers since dusk and the smell would only thicken as midnight came. Above the rooftops, the sky was already wrong—orange flares rising over the plaza where they'd built the centenary bonfire, sparks raining down like the sea turned to embers.

I ran.

* * *

The first street was easy. The second was not.

New Year crowds in this city move like tides, and tonight the tide had come in drunk. Lanterns strung from balcony to balcony, paper dragons carried on poles above the heads of the crowd, drummers beating out the old year's death rattle. I pushed into it with my shoulder down, the way you push into current, and the crowd pushed back—not from malice but from sheer mass, hundreds of bodies all trying to be somewhere festive at once.

"Coming through," I said. "Coming through."

A man in a paper crown stepped on my heel. A woman carrying a roasted pig on a pole turned without looking and the pole caught me across the jaw. I tasted blood and kept moving. Somewhere behind me, past the drums, past the firecrackers, I heard a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat: the flat clap of company boots, two men walking in step, cutting through a crowd the way foremen cut through anything—by making people afraid to be in the way.

I didn't look back. Looking back costs time, and time was the whole of what I owned now. Eleven forty-something minutes of the nineteenth century, and then it was gone forever, and if the filings weren't in the clerk's hands when it went—

Don't count. Swim.

I cut left off the main thoroughfare into Tanner's Row, where the crowd thinned because the smell of the vats drove the celebrants elsewhere. My boots slapped wet cobbles. Behind me the boots stopped at the mouth of the row. A voice—Wren's voice, that reasonable, unhurried voice he used for telling widows their husbands' contracts had passed to the company—said something I couldn't make out. Then the boots started again, faster.

He knew. Of course he knew. A man who has spent twenty years reading ledgers can read a city too; he knew which door mattered tonight, and he was walking toward it at the pace of a man who has never once needed to run.

So I ran harder.

* * *

Constitution Hill rose out of the fog like the hull of a ship. The courthouse steps were lit with lanterns for the occasion—there was to be some ceremony at midnight, the mayor reading proclamations, the new century welcomed with speeches nobody would remember—and the steps were crowded with clerks and constables and families who had come to see the lamps.

At the top, doors. Beyond the doors, the filing office, and beyond that, the clerk: a narrow, gray man named Pellham whom Maren Okonkwo had described to me three days ago with the tenderness most women reserve for sons. *He owes me his life and he knows it. Give him the papers and say her name, and he will do the rest.*

Her name. I climbed the steps with the words stacked in my throat like ballast stones.

Halfway up, a hand closed on my collar.

Not Wren. One of his—big, breathing hard from Tanner's Row, his face shiny under the lanterns. "Miss Lockridge," he said, or meant to say; I had already dropped, the way you drop when a wave comes aboard, turning inside his grip so that his hand tore cloth instead of holding flesh. The crowd around us gasped and then, being a New Year crowd, decided it was entertainment and pressed closer to watch.

"Constable!" someone shouted cheerfully.

"No," I said, "not—" but the big man had recovered, and there was a second figure behind him coming up the steps two at a time, and the third figure, unhurried, gray-gloved, was Aldous Wren himself, ascending through the crowd as though the crowd were furniture.

I did the thing I had spent twenty years learning not to do. I opened my mouth in front of strangers and told the truth.

"This man is stealing a child," I shouted. "Meridian Trading Company. They took a six-year-old girl off Salt House Lane an hour ago. Ask him where she is."

It worked the way truth works in a crowd: badly, slowly, and then all at once. Faces turned toward Wren. A woman near the balustrade drew her children back. A constable detached himself from the door detail and came down the steps with the particular heavy dignity of a man who does not want trouble on the last night of the century.

"Foreman Wren," the constable said. "Is there a child missing, sir?"

"There is a runaway indenture," Wren said smoothly, "and a thief carrying stolen company property." His eyes found mine over the constable's shoulder, and there was nothing smooth in them at all. "Juniper Lockridge. Hand it over, and I will speak for leniency."

"The papers are filed with the magistrate or they aren't," I said. "There is no handover."

"Then there is an arrest."

The constable put a hand on my arm. And here is what I learned about the last hours of the nineteenth century, standing on those lantern-lit steps with a constable's grip on my sleeve and Wren's shadow falling across me: the old world still had strength enough to hold you, right up until midnight, right up until the very last stroke—but its grip had gone strange, uncertain, the grip of a drowning man who no longer believes in the water.

Because the crowd did not disperse. The crowd leaned in.

"I saw them take a child off Salt House Lane," called a voice—a boatwright's voice, Halvard's voice, the sympathetic boatwright from the harbor checkpoint who had stalled me weeks ago and evidently been doing his own arithmetic since. "Two men and the foreman's clerk. Carried her out like cargo."

"My cousin works the Meridian warehouse," another voice. "They've been burning ledgers a fortnight. Burning books, at Christmas."

Wren's composure performed perfectly, which is how I knew it had failed. "These are slanderers and debtors," he said. "Every word—"

The courthouse clock began to strike eleven.

And in the confusion of the bell—everyone glancing up, everyone counting—I twisted out of the constable's loosening fingers, took the remaining steps at a dead run, shouldered through the doors, and was inside.

* * *

The filing office smelled of ink, dust, and lamp oil. Pellham sat behind a counter scarred by fifty years of stamps, a narrow gray man exactly as advertised, sorting the year-end backlog with the serenity of a monk.

"Maren Okonkwo sent me," I said, gasping, and watched the serenity crack.

He looked at me a long moment. Then he rose, crossed to the double doors, shut them, and shot the bolt.

"You have something to file?" he asked.

I unwrapped the oilcloth on his counter. The ledger page—the original, torn from the founder's own register, recording the write-off of a living diver in the year my mother vanished, her contract sold onward while her death was entered in the books. The duplicate register page Odile had pulled from the archive with her father's key, confirming the practice across thirty years and nine names. And beneath them, Maren's sworn statement, signed with her mark beside her name because her hands could no longer hold a pen steady, witnessed by a harbor notary who owed Halvard money.

Pellham read the first page twice. When he reached the second, his lips moved. At the third, he sat down.

"Nine names," he said quietly.

"Nine divers declared drowned while alive. There may be more. The company burned its early registers this month—for hygiene."

"I heard." He looked up at me, and behind the grayness there was something older and harder, something that had been waiting twenty years for exactly this knock. "Maren Okonkwo dove the north shoals. She pulled me out of the channel flood of '81 when I was a fool with a boat. I have been waiting," he said, "to repay a debt the company prefers I forget."

"Then don't forget it tonight."

He was already pulling forms. "Filings accepted after hours require—" He stopped himself, waved his own regulation away like smoke. "Name of complainant."

I gave it. Juniper Lockridge. My voice did not shake, and I noticed that it didn't, and noticed myself noticing, and made myself keep speaking. Names of witnesses. Names of the accused: Aldous Wren, foreman. Edmund Meridian, founder. The nature of the complaint: fraudulent declaration of death; unlawful sale of persons under contract; abduction of a minor, Sefa, age six, taken from Salt House Lane this night.

When I wrote her name, my hand finally shook. Pellham waited. I steadied it against the counter and finished.

Outside, muffled by oak doors, I could hear Wren's voice arguing with the constable, rising, losing its smoothness. Boots on stone. A fist on the doors—once, twice.

"The office is closed for the holiday," Pellham called pleasantly, and did not move from his chair.

The pounding continued. Through the frosted glass of the transom, Wren's shadow moved, large and distorted, a giant in a doorway.

"How long," I whispered, "for warrants?"

"A magistrate must review and sign. Magistrate Osei attends the midnight ceremony—he'll return within the hour." Pellham glanced at the clock. Twenty to twelve. "If he returns. If he signs. If the company's counsel doesn't reach him first—they'll have runners at the ceremony too, Mr. Lockridge. This is not over at midnight. This begins at midnight."

"I know." I pressed my palm flat against the oilcloth wrapping, empty now, feeling my own heartbeat underneath. "But it begins with these papers in your hands instead of theirs. That's more than the last twenty years managed."

Another blow struck the doors, hard enough to shiver the lantern flames. Then a different sound: Wren's voice, low, giving orders, retreating. Not leaving. Repositioning. A man like Wren does not abandon a siege; he simply chooses a better wall.

"They'll go for Osei directly," Pellham said, following my thought. "Company counsel will find him at the ceremony, wine in hand, and fill his ear before he ever sees these pages. Unless—"

"Unless someone gets there first," I said. "Unless she does."

* * *

I came out of the courthouse through the side passage Pellham showed me, into the cold, and the world had gone mad in the twenty minutes I'd been inside.

The plaza below Constitution Hill was a sea of lanterns and noise. The bonfire had been lit—a tower of flame throwing sparks two hundred feet up, heat reaching us even at this distance. Fireworks were already going up from the breakwater, early ones, impatient ones, bursting green and gold over the black water. And everywhere the crowd, thicker than I had ever seen it, packed shoulder to shoulder from the steps to the harbor, waiting for midnight with the terrible patience of people who have waited all their lives for something to change.

Somewhere in that crowd: Wren and his men, hunting. Somewhere in that crowd: Magistrate Osei, at the ceremony dais, holding the whole night in the nib of his pen. And somewhere between the two, if Maren Okonkwo had kept her promise and her heart held, a hired chair was fighting its way uphill with a dying woman inside it who had decided, after twenty years of silence, that her testimony would outlive her.

I found the chair at the foot of the hill, stuck fast in the crowd like a nail in timber.

Two bearers, sweating despite the cold, trying to turn the chair sideways through a gap no wider than a coffin. Around them, revelers laughed and shoved and sang. And inside the chair, framed in the opening, wrapped in blankets against the sea damp, sat Maren Okonkwo, gray as ash and bright as a blade, watching the fireworks with an expression I had seen on divers' faces just before they went over the side.

I fought to her. Took the chair's frame in both hands.

"Let me through," I told the bearers. "She's expected. Magistrate Osei—sworn testimony. Move."

They might have argued. But Maren leaned out of the chair, seized my wrist with a grip that had hauled anchors out of mud, and said, in the voice that had commanded dive crews for thirty years, "Up the hill. Now. And if either of you drops me, I will haunt you into the next century personally, and I will be *insufferable*."

The bearers went like mules possessed. The crowd, seeing an old woman in state, parted with the superstitious courtesy crowds grant funerals and coronations. We climbed. The fireworks thickened—the display proper had begun now, shell after shell, the sky tearing open in red and white, each burst washing the plaza in light like lightning trapped in glass. Beneath us the crowd roared with each explosion, counting nothing yet, saving itself for midnight.

On the dais ahead, bunting and lamplight, the mayor's voice booming through a speaking trumpet, and behind him, seated in a row of dignitaries, a heavyset man in judicial black: Osei. And at the edge of the dais, whispering into the ear of the mayor's secretary, a sleek man I recognized from the customs house—company counsel, arrived ahead of us, already working.

"We're late," I said.

"We're alive," Maren said. "That's the same thing, mostly. Put me down by the steps and get me to the magistrate. You carry the story, child. I *am* the story. There's a difference, and tonight it matters."

The chair set down. I helped her out, felt how little of her there was, how much will. She stood straighter than she had any right to. The final minute of the year was coming—I could feel it the way divers feel the turn of tide, a pressure change, a shift in the whole body of the water.

We started up the dais steps together, her hand on my arm, the crowd's roar swelling around us, and across the plaza, cutting through it at the pace of a man who has never needed to run, came Aldous Wren, gray gloves, gray face, eyes fixed on the magistrate like a heading.

The clock began to strike.

Twelve strokes to divide one world from another. On the fourth stroke, we reached Osei's side, and Maren said, "Magistrate. Maren Okonkwo. You signed my husband's diving license in '74, and I have come to tell you what the Meridian Company wrote in their books"—the fifth stroke, the sixth, the crowd screaming the numbers now, TEN, ELEVEN—"while their divers were still breathing."

Osei looked at her. Looked at me. Looked past us both at Wren, arriving at the base of the dais, halted by the crush of the crowd at the exact moment the crowd chose to erupt—fireworks, bells, the century dying in glory, everyone on their feet, everyone shouting—and in that roaring, blind, glorious chaos, the twelfth stroke fell.

"Tell me," said Magistrate Osei, leaning close so I alone could hear him over the birth of 1900.

I opened my mouth, and spoke my mother's century's crimes into the new year's ear.

Behind us, at the foot of the dais, Wren was shouting for the constables—and the constables, every one of them, had turned to watch the fireworks.
