## 20. The Century Turns

**Juniper**

The fireworks began early.

They rose over the harbor in gold and green bursts, and the whole city seemed to tilt toward them—the crowd on the esplanade surging like a tide pulled by a moon made of light—and Juniper moved against it, shoulder-first, the oilcloth packet pressed flat beneath their coat where a heartbeat ought to be.

The packet was heavier than paper had any right to be. Twenty years of drowned women. A founder's signature repeated down the margin like a man practicing his own name. Juniper had carried pearls up from forty feet of black water with less fear than this.

*Left at the fishmonger's. Through the arch. The clerk's window faces the courtyard—Maren said the lamp burns green past midnight on filing nights.*

A rocket screamed overhead and the crowd gasped as one animal, and in that collective upward turning of ten thousand faces, Juniper slipped through the gap they left behind like water finding a crack in stone.

Behind them, farther back than comfort, a voice cut the crowd: *"Stop there! In the company of Meridian Trading, I invoke recovery of assets—"*

Wren. Of course Wren. Even now, even tonight, the foreman spoke the language of ledgers, as though a human being could be an *asset*, as though a child could be *recovered* like a misdelivered crate.

Juniper did not stop. Stopping was for people who had something to lose that hadn't already been taken from them twice.

The crowd thickened near the customs house steps, celebrants drunk on the century itself, waving paper lanterns that said 1900 in wobbling ink. Someone thrust a cup of arrack into Juniper's hand; they let it spill rather than slow. A woman kissed their cheek and wished them a new year of fortune, and Juniper thought: *I only need eleven more minutes of the old one.*

The magistrate's courtyard opened before them, ringed by iron lamps, and there—green glass glowing, exactly as Maren had promised—was the night clerk's window.

And between Juniper and the window, unhurried as a man taking an evening stroll, walked Aldous Wren.

He had come around the other side of the square. He must have known the streets better than any map could teach—he had spent thirty years learning which doors opened and which stayed shut, which silences were loyalty and which were fear. His coat was buttoned to the throat. His face wore its kindness like a mask worn backward.

"Lockridge." He spread his hands, and his voice carried the gentle reasonableness he used on divers at the end of a bad season. "Whatever you're carrying, it isn't worth what happens after. You've seen what we do to troublemakers' families. You *were* one."

"I was," Juniper said, and their own voice sounded strange to them—rusty, unpracticed, loud. "That's rather the point."

Wren stepped forward. Juniper stepped sideways, putting a lantern post between them, and behind Wren the crowd was spilling into the courtyard now, carried by fireworks and cheap wine and the great democratic joy of a date changing, and the clerk's window was three yards away, two—

A hired chair came through the arch at a dead run, its bearers shouting, and out of it, before it fully stopped, climbed Maren Okonkwo, gray-faced, one hand pressed to her chest, the other holding a folded statement sealed with wax she had clearly melted herself over a tavern candle.

"Witness deposition," she announced to the courtyard at large, in the voice of a woman who had once been heard across a quarter mile of open water, "for the magistrate. And if anyone lays a hand on me before it's filed, my heart will oblige you all with a scandal no newspaper can resist."

The bearers flanked her like honor guards. Wren turned toward her—turned, one fatal half-second—and Juniper ran the last two yards and put the packet through the green-lit window into the clerk's surprised hands.

"Filing," Juniper said. "Dated before midnight. It has to be dated *before* midnight."

The clerk looked at the packet. Looked at Wren, whose expression had gone from kindness to something older and barer. Looked at the clock above the courtyard arch, its hands crawling toward the top of the hour, toward the top of everything.

He stamped the docket.

"Received," he said, "the thirty-first of December, eighteen hundred ninety-nine, at"—he squinted at the clock—"eleven fifty-eight."

Somewhere beyond the rooftops, the first shell of the true display went up, and the whole sky held its breath.

***

**Maren**

Maren Okonkwo had kept her silence for twenty years, and so when it finally broke, it broke all at once, like a net giving way under too much catch.

She stood in the courtyard while the clerk read names aloud from the ledger page—names she knew, names she had dove beside, names that had been entered as *lost at sea* while their owners were sold south or sold east or simply sold—and she felt each one land in her chest like a stone dropped into deep water.

There it was: *Amara Lockridge. Write-off declared. Contract transferred.*

Not drowned. Never drowned. Sold, on a Tuesday, while her daughter mended nets and waited for her mother's boat.

Maren had been on that boat. Maren had seen the founder's agent count out coin. Maren had said nothing, because saying something meant the bottom of the sea, and the sea was generous but not forgiving.

Her heart stuttered—a familiar betrayal, the old muscle faltering like a tired rower—and she gripped the chair's arm and breathed through it, because she would not collapse until this was done. She had made a bargain with her own body on the ride over: *hold, old thing, hold until the ink is dry.*

"You're killing yourself for paper," Wren said quietly, near her elbow. He had not left. She almost admired him. "For words written by dead men about dead women. What will it change?"

"It will change whether they were dead men," Maren said, "or murderers. That has always mattered to somebody, Aldous. Just never to us."

Above the courtyard, the clock struck.

Not twelve—not yet—but the crowd counted anyway, roaring, because the century did not care about the accuracy of municipal clocks. Paper lanterns rose off the esplanade like a flock of small suns. Somewhere a band struck up a tune too fast for dancing.

And through the courtyard gates, borne on the shoulders of a customs porter who had demanded no payment and asked no questions, came Odile Meridian, carrying a key.

***

**Odile**

Odile had spent fifty-nine weeks of the old century hating her father efficiently, in the way one hates weather—thoroughly, privately, without expectation of effect.

The sixtieth week had undone her.

It was not the ledger page itself, though the page was damning enough, her father's hand flowing confident and beautiful across the record of a living woman converted into profit. It was the compass. Her compass. The brass case worn smooth by a child's thumb, the needle she had watched spin on her windowsill believing it pointed to adventure, when all along it had been a gift passed between her father and Amara Lockridge like a token exchanged over a card table—one more thing he took and gave away and took again.

She had loved that compass. She had loved him. Both loves had been aimed at things that were never what they seemed.

Now she stood in the magistrate's courtyard amid strangers and fireworks, and the clerk approached her with the deference owed to her name, and she said, before he could bow: "Don't. Not tonight. Tonight I'm just a witness with a key."

"The archive warrant," the clerk said, "issues at the turn of the hour. But the seal requires—" He hesitated, embarrassed. "It requires the family key, madam. Or a forced entry, which takes days your documents say you don't have."

Odile looked at the brass in her palm. It had opened doors her whole life without her ever choosing which ones.

"My father built a century on locked rooms," she said. "Consider this the rent coming due."

Across the courtyard, Juniper Lockridge was watching her—this diver with their careful face and their mother's eyes, though Odile suspected neither of them knew the second part yet. Juniper nodded to her, once, the smallest possible motion. Not gratitude. Something more binding than gratitude: acknowledgment. *You were in the water with us, whether you meant to be or not.*

The clock struck midnight.

The sound rolled over the harbor, and every ship in the roads answered it—bells, horns, steam whistles, the whole ragged chorus of the port announcing to the dark that the books had closed on a hundred years.

In the noise and the light, the magistrate himself appeared on the courthouse steps, an old man in evening dress interrupted at his own party, holding the docket the clerk had thrust upon him. He read for a long moment. The fireworks painted his face red, then white, then blue.

Then he raised one finger, and by some miracle the nearest whistles fell silent enough for his voice to carry:

"Warrants issue! Against the estate of Edmund Meridian and the officers of the Meridian Trading Company—for fraud, for false certification of death, for the unlawful sale of persons! Sealed premises to be opened under inspection at dawn!"

The courtyard erupted. Strangers embraced. A customs porter wept openly into his cap. Somewhere in the crush, Maren saw, Aldous Wren was no longer standing at all—he had simply become absent, the way certain men do when the architecture that held them upright is withdrawn, slipping backward into the crowd and out of history with the quiet efficiency of a man closing a ledger he can no longer balance.

Odile turned the key over in her hand one last time.

Then she dropped it into the clerk's palm, like paying a fare.

***

**Sefa**

Sefa did not understand most of the words, but she understood the lights.

She sat on Maren's salt-house cot with a blanket around her shoulders like a cape, because Maren had said important people got capes on important nights, and through the high salt-stained window she could see the whole sky doing its trick—blooming, falling, blooming again in colors that didn't have names yet, being born along with the year.

She had practiced her forgetting every day, the way she'd been told. *Forget the face that left you.* She had squeezed her eyes shut and pushed at the memory like pushing at a splinter. But the face wouldn't go. A woman with sea-salt in her laugh lines. Hands that smelled of rope. A voice saying *you are my north, little star, whatever else they tell you.*

And tonight, somehow, trying to forget it felt less like a chore and more like a choice—and she found she chose to keep it.

Footsteps on the stairs. The door opened, and Juniper filled it, soaked in crowd and gunpowder smoke, looking at her the way people look at a harbor light after a long crossing.

"They said," Sefa reported gravely, "that the lady with the gray hair gets to keep me. Pending." She wasn't sure what pending meant, except that Maren had said it gently, twice, and gentleness made words safe.

"Pending," Juniper confirmed, kneeling to her height. "Which means nobody takes you anywhere you don't agree to go. Not ever again."

"Not even the company?"

"*Especially* not the company."

Outside, the greatest burst yet went up, gold on gold, and the whole salt house lit up like the inside of a pearl. Sefa grabbed Juniper's sleeve and pointed at the window, demanding they look, because some things were too large to see alone.

And Juniper looked. And for the first time in the whole of their acquaintance, their face forgot how to be careful.

***

**Juniper**

Later—much later, when the fireworks had burned down to smoke and the crowd had thinned to stragglers singing off-key—Juniper stood alone in the courtyard where the clerk's green lamp still burned.

The docket was filed. The warrants were signed. Somewhere in the building, a magistrate's pen was moving through the small hours, converting twenty years of silence into entries in a newer book, and Juniper felt the strangest sensation: lighter, and heavier, both at once. As if a weight had not been lifted but finally *set down where it belonged.*

The old century's debts, formally entered into the new one's accounts. Let the new century figure out how to pay them.

From the doorway, Maren's voice: "The archive opens at dawn. Odile's key is with the clerk, and the inspectors sail at first light. There'll be names in that duplicate register that aren't on your page. Names whose children are still indentured." She paused. "Including, if I'm right, one entry that explains where your mother's contract was *sold to.*"

Juniper closed their hand around the brass compass in their pocket, feeling the worn case, the needle trembling toward a north that had nothing to do with direction anymore.

Twenty years ago, a boat had gone out empty and a ledger had lied, and a child had learned that silence was survival.

Tonight the child had spoken, and survived anyway, and dawn was coming with a key and a register and a list of names.

"Then we'd best not be late," Juniper said, and turned toward the water, where the first gray light of the first morning of the new century was already gathering.
