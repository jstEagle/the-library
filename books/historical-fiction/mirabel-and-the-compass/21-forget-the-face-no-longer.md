## 21. Forget the Face No Longer

The tide was going out when we came to the shore, and it took the light with it, pulling the last of the afternoon into long copper ribbons on the wet sand. Sefa walked ahead of me, her small feet printing the sand in a line of half-moons, and I let her lead because she wanted to and because my own legs had gone strange and heavy since the magistrate's door closed behind us yesterday.

Free. The word still didn't fit. It sat in my chest like a stone I hadn't learned to breathe around yet—no, not a stone. A pearl. Something that had grown around an old wound until it had weight and shape and worth. Twenty-two years of contract, dissolved with a stamp and the clerk's dry voice: *Juniper Lockridge, indenture void by order of Magistrate Halloran, this thirty-first day of December.* He'd said it twice because Wren's lawyers kept objecting, and the second time he'd looked at me over his spectacles as if to ask whether I understood what I'd just been handed.

I understood. What I didn't understand yet was what to do with my hands.

They had held rope and ledger and lantern and child. They had learned every silence the company taught. And now they were mine, and they didn't know their own work.

Sefa stopped where the sand went dark and firm, where the water had been that morning and would be again tonight. She turned in a slow circle, taking in the harbor road behind us, the salt house smoke rising thin from Maren's direction, the gray-green line of the channel markers bobbing out past the breakwater.

"Is this where you said?" she asked. "Where she went into the water?"

"This is where they *said*," I told her, and heard how my voice came out flat as slate. "There's a difference."

She considered that with the terrible seriousness of six-year-olds, who have not yet learned that some questions are better left drowned. Then she held out her hand for the compass.

I gave it to her. The brass was warm from my pocket, warm from my grip all the way down the harbor road, though there was no sun left to account for it. She turned it over twice, found the seam, and pressed the little catch the way Odile Meridian had shown us—the founder's daughter, standing in the wrecked archive with her father's key ring still swinging from her fist, saying *it opens like this; it always opened like this; I used to pretend it was mine.*

Inside, the engraving caught the last light: **M.L. — E.M., 1877**. Mirabel Lockridge. Edmund Meridian. Two initials joined by a date and a small stamped anchor between them, the company mark worn soft by twenty years of a dead woman's pocket or a living woman's flight—I would never know which, and I was learning, slowly, that never-knowing could be borne if you stopped pretending you'd ever be given the answer.

"Sefa." I crouched so we were eye to eye. Her hair was still stiff with salt from Maren's hasty washing, and there was a scratch on her cheek from the salt house door that Wren's men had broken open, and she had not cried once through any of it, and I loved her for it and hated that she'd needed the strength.

"Yes, Juniper?"

"I'm going to say something now. Out loud. You're going to hear it, and then it will be in the world, and it can't be taken back out of the world. Do you understand?"

Her eyes went wide and steady. "Like the ledger page."

"Exactly like the ledger page." I took a breath, and the breath hurt, which surprised me. Twenty years of holding a name behind my teeth and I'd thought the letting go would feel like relief. Instead it felt like a birth. "My mother's name was Mirabel Lockridge."

The wind took it. The gulls took it. The water took it, folding the syllables under and carrying them out toward the channel markers, and nothing happened. No foreman rose from the dunes. No pen scratched in a ledger. The century did not end again. The world simply received the name of Mirabel Lockridge and kept turning, and I stood there shaking like a diver surfacing too fast, because I had spent two decades believing that the name itself was dangerous—that to speak it was to surface without exhaling, that it would burst something vital in me.

It hadn't. I was still standing. The sea was still the sea.

"Say it back to me," I whispered.

"Mirabel Lockridge," Sefa said, clear as a bell over the flats, and the wind took that too, and I laughed—a cracked, startled sound that made her grin—and put my hand on her head.

"That's your grandmother's name. Do you know what a grandmother is?"

"A mama's mama." She frowned. "Or a papa's mama."

"Your papa's mama, maybe. Or your mama's mama. We don't know yet which side of the family you come from, but the magistrate's clerk is looking, and Maren says kinship claims move faster than company contracts now that warrants are out against Meridian's estate." I paused. "And if it turns out you're mine—by blood or by choosing—it's yours too. Mirabel. It can be your middle name, or your daughter's someday, or just a name you carry in your pocket like the compass. But nobody gets to write it off. Nobody gets to sell it. Not ever again."

* * *

We walked down to the waterline while the tide retreated further, leaving ribbons of weed and the pale curved shells Sefa liked to collect. This was the beach where they told me my mother drowned. I'd stood on it at seven years old while Aldous Wren—younger then, already smooth, already kind in that way that was worse than cruelty—had knelt and explained about riptides and heavy work and how sometimes the sea takes its own, and how the company would take care of me, and wouldn't it be better if I remembered her smiling instead of crying?

I'd nodded. I'd been trained to nod. And somewhere between that nod and this evening, I had built a wall of silence so complete that I'd nearly believed the story myself.

But the ledger page had said otherwise. *Write-off declared: subject alive at time of transfer. Contract sold north.* My mother hadn't drowned. She'd been sold, alive, breathing, probably fighting—and the compass engraved with her initials and the founder's meant she'd mattered to someone powerful enough to destroy the paper trail, powerful enough to make the whole harbor agree she was dead.

Whether she'd lived out her years up north, whether she'd searched for me, whether she'd died still clutching that brass case—all of it lay beyond any ledger I would ever read. Odile had promised to search the northern offices when the estate settled, and I believed her, the way you believe someone who has turned a key against her own blood. But belief wasn't the same as knowing, and I was done demanding certainty from a world that traded in erasure.

What I could do was stop erasing her myself.

"Sit," I told Sefa, and we sat together on the cold damp sand, our backs to the wind, facing the water.

"Tell me about her," Sefa said. "You said you would. When the bad men weren't chasing us anymore."

So I did.

"She had hands like mine," I began, and held one up so Sefa could see. "Wide palms. Callused here—" I touched the base of each finger, "—from rope and shell. Divers' hands. She could hold her breath four minutes, maybe five, and Maren says she once brought up a shell the size of a dinner plate from forty feet, just to show the new divers it could be done. Not to sell. To show."

"What color was her skin?"

"Brown like wet kelp," I said. "Darker in summer. She had scars on her arms from coral—here, and here, and one long one across her shoulder that she said was from a moray, but Maren swears it was from climbing the company fence to see me when I was a baby and they'd moved my cot to the other compound."

Sefa giggled, and the sound went out over the water and I let it.

"She laughed loud," I went on. "Not polite. Loud. Other divers used to say you could find her by the laugh before you saw her boat. And she sang—not hymns, not company songs, old diving songs her own mother taught her, songs about the moon pulling the water and the water pulling the pearls and the pearls pulling the diver down and down and down." I hummed a bar of one, surprising myself; the melody surfaced whole, untouched by twenty years of silence, living in some part of me no foreman had ever reached. Sefa leaned against my shoulder.

"Was she pretty?"

"Everyone said so. But that's not what I remember most." I watched a gull hang motionless over the flats. "What I remember is that she was *furious*. All the time, quietly furious, at the ledgers and the ration counts and the way they weighed our catches short. She taught me numbers before she taught me letters, so I could check their arithmetic myself. That's why I could read the write-off page, Sefa. That's why I knew what it meant. She gave me that. They erased her face from every record they kept, but they couldn't erase what she put *in* me."

I stopped. My throat had closed, and I let it stay closed a moment, breathing through it the way you breathe through a long dive—slow, even, trusting the body to know its work.

"And she loved me," I said at last, steadier. "Whatever happened after—whatever bargain Edmund Meridian forced on her, whatever choice she made with that compass in her hand—I know she loved me, because she fought the company for six years before they took me, and people don't fight like that for something they don't love."

"Do you think she forgot you? Like Sefa practices forgetting?"

The question went through me like cold water through cloth. I pulled Sefa closer.

"No," I said. "I think forgetting is a thing they do to us, not a thing we do. I think if she was alive up north, she remembered. I think remembering was all she had left of me, the same way it was all I had left of her. And now—" I turned the compass over in my hand, the engraving catching the last light, "—now we have more than memory. Now we have paper. Now the whole world has to say her name too, in courtrooms, in warrants, in the new ledgers they'll keep. Mirabel Lockridge. Alive at time of transfer. Never drowned. Never lost. *Taken*."

* * *

The sun went down while we sat there, and the first stars came out over the water, and neither of us moved. Somewhere behind us the harbor lamps were lighting, and somewhere beyond them Maren was resting her bad heart in front of her stove with Sefa's spare shirt drying on the rail, and somewhere beyond that Odile Meridian was drafting letters to northern offices in handwriting I was beginning to recognize as her father's mirror—elegant, forceful, impossible to ignore.

But none of that was here. Here there was only the water and the child and the name, spoken and spoken again until it stopped tasting like blood and started tasting like bread.

"Mirabel Lockridge," Sefa said once more, quietly, practicing, and patted the sand beside her as though inviting the name to sit down with us.

And I found, to my astonishment, that I was done mourning.

Not healed—healing would take years, and I intended to give it years, honestly, out loud, in daylight. But done with the mourning that had been assigned to me, the tidy grief the company had measured out like rations: one drowning, one orphan, one silence, closed file. That mourning was finished. What remained was mine to shape—grief and pride and fury braided together into something that could hold a child's hand and walk into an uncertain future without flinching.

I stood, and pulled Sefa up, and we walked back along the darkening beach toward the lights of the harbor, toward Maren's stove and tomorrow and whatever came next. At the top of the sand I stopped once and looked back at the water.

"Goodnight, Mama," I said. Just that. Just loud enough for the sea to hear.

Then I took Sefa's hand, and we went home—to Maren's home, our home now, the first home I had ever chosen rather than been assigned.

In the morning, the independent crew from the north would be at the docks, and Captain Voss would want an answer about the three berths we'd asked for, and there would be paperwork of the good kind, contracts of the free kind. In the morning, Sefa would need breakfast and Maren would need her medicine and Odile would send word about the northern search.

But that night, walking the harbor road with a child's hand in mine and a dead woman's compass in my pocket, I felt something I had not felt since I was seven years old.

I felt the future coming toward me like a tide—vast, unknowable, and entirely my own.
