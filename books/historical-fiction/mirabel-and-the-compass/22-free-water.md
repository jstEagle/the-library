## 22. Free Water

The magistrate's order came on a Tuesday, written in a clerk's careful hand on paper so thin Juniper could read the ghost of the next page through it. *The indenture of one J. Lockridge, diver, being found to have been procured under fraudulent circumstances, is hereby dissolved in full.* Dissolved. As if twenty years had been a spoonful of sugar in warm water, stirred until it vanished.

Juniper read it four times in the magistrate's anteroom while the clerk pretended not to watch. The ink was barely dry. Somewhere behind the closed door, voices argued about warrants and estates and the word *exhumation*, and none of it touched this thin sheet of paper that weighed more than any pearl Juniper had ever brought up from the dark.

"You'll want copies," the clerk said. He was the man Maren had named, the one who owed her a favor older than Juniper. He had gray at his temples now and ink stains permanently mapped across his knuckles, but his eyes were kind in the way of men who had spent their lives writing down what others wanted erased. "Three, at least. One for you, one for whoever employs you next, and one—" he hesitated, quill hovering, "—one for the grave, if you take my meaning. Paper burns. Memory is harder to sink."

Juniper took five.

Outside, the city still smelled of fireworks and spilled rum. The New Year crowds had thinned to stragglers sweeping ash and ribbon from the gutters, and the harbor beyond them lay flat and silver in the January light, as innocent as if it had never swallowed anyone at all. Juniper stood on the magistrate's steps with the order folded against their chest and breathed air that belonged to no company ledger, no foreman's tally, no debt inherited before memory began.

It should have felt like flying. It felt like standing at the edge of a dive, before the lungs burn and the ears seal—suspended, weightless, terrified.

*You are free,* said the paper.

*Then why do my hands still shake?*

* * *

Maren Okonkwo met them at the salt house with her bad heart propped up on two pillows and Sefa braiding her gray hair into a crown that would have scandalized every churchwarden in the district.

"Well," Maren said when Juniper came through the door. She looked Juniper over the way she'd once looked over green divers checking their gear—slowly, missing nothing. "Still breathing."

"Still breathing."

"Good. That's the hard part done." She patted the bench beside her. "Sit. We've business."

Sefa scrambled off the cot and attached herself to Juniper's arm like a barnacle, showing off the braid with the fierce pride of a six-year-old who had recently discovered she could make things happen with her own two hands. Her hair smelled of salt and soap. There was a scrape healing on her knee from the night Wren's men had taken her, and Juniper's chest still tightened whenever they saw it—the memory of that rage arriving whole and hot, the way it must have lived in their mother too.

"Business," Juniper said, sitting.

"The *Kestrel*." Maren named the ship the way other women named suitors. "Independent crew out of the northern ports. Pearl, salvage, whatever the water gives. No Meridian colors, no company contracts, no ledgers." She fixed Juniper with a look. "I wrote to the captain three weeks ago. Before the archive. Before I knew how any of this would end."

Juniper went very still. "Before—"

"I'm an old woman with a failing heart, child. I don't gamble. I *plan*." She tapped her sternum, where the heart knocked its uneven rhythm. "This thing of mine could stop tomorrow or in twenty years. But if it stops while I'm still owed testimony and still owed apologies, I'll come back and haunt every one of you. So I made arrangements. The captain owes me for a salvage job in 'eighty-one. I told him I'd send him the best diver on this coast." A pause, dry as driftwood. "He assumed I meant myself. I let him."

"Maren—"

"He's taking passengers north. Two of them." Her eyes went to Sefa, then back. "Pending kinship claims, the magistrate says. Well. I've filed one. Distant kin, my grandmother's line, close enough for a clerk with a conscience. And where I go, the girl goes. That's settled law now, thanks to certain documents delivered at considerable personal risk by a diver I could name." She raised an eyebrow. "Unless you object."

Juniper's throat had closed somewhere around *two of them*. They shook their head.

"Good." Maren leaned back against her pillows, satisfied, and for a moment the iron old woman looked simply tired—twenty years of complicity finally set down like a weight belt unbuckled. "The *Kestrel* sails on the morning tide, day after tomorrow. Which gives you one day to decide what you are, now that nobody else is deciding it for you." She smiled, small and crooked. "Take your time. You've got a whole century."

* * *

Juniper spent the day walking.

Not toward anything. Away—from the compound, from the drying racks, from the long shed where the company bell still hung, silent now, its rope coiled and confiscated as evidence. The streets were full of the strange aftermath of celebration: broken lantern paper, a child chasing a hoop, shopkeepers nailing up fresh calendars with the new year printed in bold black numerals. 1900. A number that looked like a doorway.

At the shoreline where the tide ran low, Juniper stopped.

Two days ago they had stood here and spoken a name aloud for the first time in twenty years. *Seraphine Lockridge.* It had come out cracked and half-drowned, and then again, clearer, and then a third time like a bell rung until the sound was true. The sea had taken the name and given back nothing but foam, which was right. Names weren't for the sea. They were for the living.

Juniper crouched and let the water touch their fingers. Cold. Honest. The same water that had taught them everything—how to hold breath, how to hold silence, how to hold grief without letting it pull you under. Every skill they owned, they'd learned in service of surviving a lie.

But skills didn't care whose hands held them. That was the thing the century was teaching, if it taught anything: inheritance was choice. Maren had chosen testimony with a failing heart. Odile Meridian had chosen her key over her father's name. Sefa, six years old, had chosen to carry a compass through a monsoon instead of forgetting the face that left her.

And Juniper Lockridge chose the water—not the company's water, fenced and tallied and owned, but the free kind, the kind that touched every shore and answered to no flag.

They stood, and walked back toward the salt house, and their stride had changed somehow. Lighter. Not because the past was gone—it would never be gone; it lived in the ledger lines and the scar tissue and the engraving inside a brass case—but because for the first time, the past walked *behind* them instead of ahead.

* * *

The *Kestrel* rode at anchor beyond the breakwater, a working schooner with patched sails and no pennant whatsoever, which to Juniper's eye was the most beautiful sight the harbor had ever offered. Her captain was a broad woman named Ilse Varga with sun-cracked hands and a voice like gravel poured slow, and she looked Juniper up and down on the deck with open skepticism.

"Maren says you're the best diver on this coast."

"Maren says a lot of things."

"She does. Usually true ones." Captain Varga spat thoughtfully over the rail. "I don't fly colors. I don't keep books for anyone but myself, and those books say what actually happened, which is more than most can claim. I pay shares, not wages. Shares mean when the water's generous, everyone eats. When she's stingy, everyone's hungry together." She shrugged. "It's a poor arrangement compared to a company contract. Company contracts guarantee you'll be cheated slowly and consistently. Out here we cheat ourselves honestly."

Juniper almost smiled. "Where do I sign?"

Varga produced a plain notebook, opened it to a clean page, and handed over a pencil stub. No seals. No witnesses. No clause binding body, breath, and future descendants unto perpetuity. Just a line, and a space beside it for a name.

Juniper looked at the blank space for a long moment.

Twenty years ago, someone had signed them into servitude with a flourish, probably without ever looking at their face. Now Juniper stood on free deck with a pencil stub and the entire unwritten century in front of them, and understood that this was the real ceremony—not the magistrate's order, not the warrants, not even the midnight bells. This. A name, given freely, to a page that would tell the truth about it.

They wrote *Juniper Lockridge* in careful letters, and beneath it, unasked, added: *diver.*

Varga read it, grunted approval, and snapped the book shut. "Crew mess at dawn. Bring your own knife." Then, softer, with a glance toward the harbor road where two figures were picking their way along the seawall: "That your family coming aboard?"

Juniper followed her gaze. Maren walked slowly, leaning on a cane and on Sefa, who carried the old woman's carpetbag with both arms and the gravity of a dockhand twice her size. They moved at the pace of the tide coming in—unhurried, inevitable, home.

"Yes," Juniper said. The word fit easily, like a key turned in a lock that had been waiting all these years. "That's my family."

* * *

They sailed on the morning tide.

Juniper stood at the bow as the harbor fell away behind them—the customs house with its new, embarrassed administration; the compound with its silent bell; the shoreline where a name had been spoken and a mourning finished. Odile Meridian had sent no farewell, only a package left at the salt house: her father's remaining correspondence, boxed and labeled in her precise hand, *for the magistrate, posthaste*, and beneath it a note of four words: *Some debts outlive founders.* Juniper had mailed it themselves, and felt nothing but a clean cold satisfaction, like deep water on a fevered brow.

The wind filled the patched sails. The coast unspooled northward, green hills giving way to gray cliffs, and the water beneath the hull shifted from the company's familiar silt-brown to something deeper, bluer, unclaimed.

Sefa stood beside Juniper at the rail, the brass compass cradled in both hands. She'd asked to hold it for the voyage, and Juniper had handed it over without hesitation—that was the point of it now. Not direction. Remembrance.

"Which way is north?" Sefa asked, watching the needle swing and settle.

"That way." Juniper pointed. "But that's not why we keep it."

"Why then?"

Juniper knelt so they were eye to eye, and opened the compass case, tilting it so the morning light caught the engraving inside—the two sets of initials, intertwined, a gift between a founder and a diver, evidence of intimacy and crime in equal measure. Sefa studied it solemnly.

"It belonged to your mother," Juniper said. "My mother. Seraphine Lockridge. She dove the way birds fly—like she'd been built for it. She laughed loud enough to scare fish. And she loved us enough to leave a trail of paper and brass through twenty years of silence, hoping someone brave enough would follow it." They closed the case gently. "We keep it so she stays found."

Sefa considered this with the terrible seriousness of the young. Then: "Will you teach me to dive?"

The question landed in Juniper's chest and bloomed there, terrifying and bright. Behind them, Maren dozed in a deck chair with her heart keeping its stubborn uneven time; ahead, the water ran blue and open to the horizon, holding pearls and wrecks and storms and years.

"When you're ready," Juniper said. "And I'll be beside you the whole way down."

Sefa grinned, gap-toothed, and turned back to the rail, and the compass needle trembled in its glass and held true.

Juniper rose and faced the open water—no flag above them, no ledger below, no name they hadn't chosen—and breathed in the free salt air, all the way down.
