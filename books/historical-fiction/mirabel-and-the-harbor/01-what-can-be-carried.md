## 1. What Can Be Carried

There were eleven things in the world that belonged to Ines Kavanagh, and he could name them in the dark.

He did so now, lying on his pallet in the divers' barracks while the fog pressed against the shutters like a hand testing a lock. He named them the way other men said prayers — not because he believed anything was listening, but because the naming itself was the belief. *A knife. A shell comb. Papers. A tin cup. A wool coat with one good sleeve. A length of fishing line, ten yards. Three hooks. A whetstone worn to the size of an egg. A second shirt, patched at both elbows. A pair of boots two sizes too large, stuffed with rags. And a river stone, smooth and gray, that was worth nothing at all.*

Eleven things. He had carried all of them across the bridge from his father's house four years ago, when he was thirteen and the Meridian Trading Company had bought his father's debt along with his father's silence, and he had carried every one of them since. Nothing added. Nothing lost. That was the whole of his discipline: not gain, but keeping. A boy who owned nothing had nothing taken from him. A boy who wanted nothing could not be made to want.

The knife first. It lived under the loose board by his head, wrapped in oilcloth, and he knew its weight the way he knew his own pulse. His father had given it to him the morning they signed the papers — had pressed it into his hands and closed the boy's fingers over it with his own broad diver's hands, cracked and salted white at the knuckles from thirty years of cold water. *"A man should have one thing the company didn't buy."* His father had said it quietly, without looking at the clerk waiting by the door with his pen uncapped. Then he had gone down to the harbor and Ines had not seen him weep, because Ines had never once seen his father weep, and this too was inheritance.

Then the comb. His mother's, cut from a single abalone shell, iridescent along the spine so that it caught whatever light it was given and made something better of it. She had died in the fever winter, three years before the indenture, and the comb was the only thing she had left him that wasn't grief. He kept it sewn into the lining of his coat, inside the good sleeve, where no inventory clerk would ever think to look. Sometimes, on the worst nights, he took it out and held it against his palm until the cold of it warmed, and he could almost hear her voice telling him to stand up straight, to eat slowly, to be careful with what he let people see.

*Be careful with what you let people see.* That had been her whole catechism, though she hadn't known she was preaching. His mother had been a harbor woman, born above the fish stalls, and she had understood something his father never had: that the company did not take by force what it could take by attention. A man who was noticed was a man who was weighed. A man who was weighed could be found wanting. So Ines had learned early to be light — light in his step through the compound yard, light in his voice in the counting room, light in his wants, his opinions, his face. The clerks called him a steady lad. The overseers called him reliable. Nobody called him anything that required remembering.

He was fifteen now, and useful, and unseen, and he told himself each night that these were the same thing.

The papers were third. They lay folded in a waxed packet beneath the floorboard beside the knife, and he did not touch them often, because touching them was like pressing on a bruise to check whether it still hurt. It always still hurt. Four pages in a clerk's neat hand: *Ines Kavanagh, son of Tomas Kavanagh, pearl diver, bound to the Meridian Trading Company for a term of seven years, in satisfaction of debts outstanding...* The debts were his father's — gear advanced, rations advanced, the funeral of a wife advanced against future hauls that had grown smaller every season as the beds thinned and the quotas rose. Seven years for numbers written in someone else's ledger. He had four years left. Two thousand days, give or take the ones the company would decide he hadn't earned.

He knew the papers by heart anyway. That was part of the discipline too. Know exactly what is held against you, hold exactly what can be held.

Beyond those three — the true trinity, the knife and comb and papers — the rest of the eleven were practical things, and he loved them less but trusted them more. The tin cup, dented, that hung from his belt and marked him as kitchen detail, which was the best detail there was, because kitchen meant food proximity and food proximity meant survival arithmetic. The coat. The line and hooks, for mussels off the pilings when the ration got thin, which it always got thin by late winter. The whetstone, the second shirt, the boots. And last the river stone, which he had picked up the day he crossed the bridge, from the middle of it, where the tide ran underneath loud enough to swallow a voice. He couldn't have said why. It was the only thing he owned that served no purpose, and maybe that was why he kept it — one object the ledger of his life could not account for. One small weight of his own choosing.

Outside, the foghorn sounded, long and low, and the whole compound seemed to settle around the sound like sediment.

Ines lay listening to the sounds that meant the world was still arranged correctly: the watchman's boots on the gravel path, twelve minutes past the hour; the creak of the drying sheds; somewhere far off, the mutter of divers' families in the lower town, cooking whatever the company store had sold them that day at whatever price the company store had decided. All of it held in place by the bridge — the long iron spine of it reaching south across the strait toward the mainland, toward markets and magistrates and everything else that existed in theory. In summer the bridge carried wagons and wire and orders. In winter the storms came and the company closed it, and the harbor became a closed fist, and everyone inside the fist learned what they were worth to the fingers holding them shut.

Six weeks, perhaps, until the closure. The equinox gales were already stacking up beyond the headland; he could feel them in the way the fog moved, in the ache the cold left in his old dive-cracked ears. When the bridge closed, the season ended. The pearls went out on the last wagon train or not at all. The ledgers would be balanced — balanced being the company's word for settled, and settled being the company's word for finished — and then the fist would close, and whatever happened inside it until spring would happen without witnesses.

He turned this thought over the way he turned the stone over in his pocket, and set it down again. There was no use carrying a dread too heavy to lift. That was the first rule of lightness: weigh your fears honestly, then leave the ones you cannot carry.

But sleep did not come easily tonight, and he was honest enough with himself to know why. Tomorrow was weighing day. The season's final haul, every shell opened under company eyes, every pearl passed across the scales into company hands, every diver standing in line with his season's work in a cloth bag and his season's hope in his throat. Ines had kitchen detail on weighing days, which meant he carried water and swept shells and stood in corners being useful and unseen while men twice his age watched strangers decide what their year had been worth.

And tomorrow Mr. Quill would preside.

The name arrived in his mind the way cold water arrives through a seam — gradually, then all at once. Quill. The company's enforcer, though no document used that word; the documents said *Superintendent of Compliance*, which was the company's way of making a wolf sound like a filing system. Ines had seen him perhaps a dozen times in four years, usually at a distance, always at moments when something in the compound's air changed pressure. Quill was not large. That was the unsettling thing about him. He was a dry, precise man with ink-stained fingers and a voice pitched so reasonably that men found themselves agreeing with him before they understood what they'd agreed to. He kept a small notebook, and he wrote in it constantly, in a script so fine it looked printed, and Ines had privately concluded that the notebook was the most dangerous object in the harbor. Guns took things from you quickly. Quill's notebook took things from you slowly, and gave you time to help.

Tomorrow Quill would stand beside the scales with his fine pen and his finer patience, and forty divers would hand over a year of their lungs.

Ines rolled onto his side and faced the wall, where a hairline crack ran from floorboard to rafter like a river on a map of nowhere. Around him the barracks breathed — fourteen boys and young men of the indenture, some asleep, some pretending, one weeping very quietly the way they all eventually did, and none of the others ever acknowledging it, because acknowledging it was a weight and nobody had room to carry anyone else's.

That was the bargain he had made with himself at thirteen: carry only yourself. Stay light. Stay small. Stay useful, because usefulness was armor, and stay invisible, because invisibility was the only country the company couldn't tax. Four years he had kept the bargain, and it had worked, and he had the eleven things and his health and his four remaining years to show for it, and if there was a hollowness somewhere underneath all that keeping, he had learned to call it safety and sleep near it anyway.

The foghorn sounded again. Somewhere below the compound, beneath the black water of the harbor, the oyster beds lay in the dark doing their slow secret work, wrapping grit in nacre, turning wounds into luster. His father had once explained it to him on a night much like this one: *the pearl is just the oyster's answer to pain, lad — it doesn't heal the wound, it only makes it beautiful.* His father had laughed when he said it, a short bitter sound, and Ines hadn't understood the laugh then.

He was beginning to.

Sleep took him finally, shallow and salt-flavored, full of the creak of scales and the scratch of a fine pen. He woke before the bell as always, dressed in the dark as always, and performed his inventory one last time by touch alone — knife, comb, papers, cup, coat, line, hooks, stone, stone, no — *knife, comb, papers, cup, coat, line, hooks, whetstone, shirt, boots, stone.* Eleven. All present. All carried.

Then the bell rang across the compound, and the fog split open briefly to show a bruised-colored dawn over the water, and Ines Kavanagh went out to be useful on weighing day, light of step and empty of expression, carrying everything he owned and showing nothing at all.

He did not yet know that by nightfall one of the eleven things would matter more than the other ten put together, and that it was not the knife.

It was the promise he hadn't made yet — the one already waiting for him, three years upstream, in the shape of a girl standing waist-deep in the shallows with her hand outstretched.
