## 3. Sefa of the Shallows

The counting room smelled of ink and cold tea, and Ines Kavanagh had been adding columns for so long that the numbers had begun to swim. That was the trouble with figures, his father used to say — they looked solid until you stared too long, and then they moved like fish. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye and let the ledger blur.

Outside the tall window, the harbor lay gray and patient under its lid of fog. Somewhere out there, beyond the breakwater, beyond the shallows where the water went from green to brown over the oyster beds, was the reef where he had almost died three years ago. He did not look at it. He never looked at it directly. It sat in his mind the way a stone sits in a shoe — felt with every step, acknowledged by none.

He was seventeen now, and he knew the weight of everything he owned. A knife with a horn handle, worn smooth. His mother's shell comb, wrapped in flannel in the bottom of his sea chest. The indenture papers that made him, on the company's books, worth eleven more years of labor. He could carry all of it in one basket. He had learned young that a man who owns little can be lost in a crowd, and a man who can be lost in a crowd lives longer. That was not cowardice. He had told himself it was wisdom so many times that he almost believed it.

The clerk across the table, old Mr. Fenwick, sniffed and turned a page. "You're drifting, boy. The Tevake account won't reconcile itself."

"No, sir." Ines bent back to the column. But the numbers kept moving like fish, and the fish led him down, as they always did, to the water.

* * *

It had been the last dive of the season, three springs past, when he was fourteen and still allowed in the boats because his father's lungs were going soft and the family needed every basket filled. The day had been beautiful in the way the harbor sometimes was — no fog, a high pale sun, the water so clear over the shallows that you could count the shells below like coins spilled on a floor.

His father had warned him about the deep channel. Every diver's father warned about the deep channel; it was practically a rite of passage, the warning, delivered with the same gravity as a blessing. *The current runs funny there when the tide turns. You feel it take your line, you drop the catch and come up.* And fourteen-year-old Ines had nodded the way fourteen-year-olds nod, which is to say with his ears and not his heart.

The morning's diving had gone well. He was proud of that season — prouder than he had ever been of anything since. His hands had learned the work without his mind's help: thumb along the shell seam, blade in, twist, free, into the basket, up for air. His body had become a tool the sea permitted to work in it, and there is a pride in that which no clerk's wage will ever buy. By midday his basket was heavy. By early afternoon it was heavier than it should have been, because he was greedy, because the beds were generous, because he wanted his father to see the weight of it and say something kind.

The tide turned while he was down.

He felt it first as a sound — a low hum in his chest, deeper than hearing, as though the whole harbor had cleared its throat. Then the water moved around him, not with him. It pushed sideways, steady and enormous, indifferent as a door closing. His line went taut and angled away from the boat above. He remembered thinking, with strange clarity, that the rope looked beautiful, a silver curve drawn through green light, and then remembering his father's voice, and then dropping the basket.

The catch scattered. Oysters tumbling down through the murk, shells catching what light remained, and some part of him grieved for them even as the current took him too — dragged him off the bottom, rolled him, and stuffed the channel's throat with foam. He tried to find up. There was no up. There was only pressure and the taste of iron and the rope sawing against his ankle where it had wrapped him, once, twice, and his own heartbeat loud as surf, faster than any surf, and then slower. Much slower. The cold stopped feeling like cold. It began to feel like rest.

He thought — and this he would carry all his life, the shame and wonder of it — he thought, *so this is how it ends, and nobody will even know which part of me is which when they find me.*

Then the water brightened.

Not much. Just a shifting of the green, a suggestion of a shape above him, dark against the lighter dark. He felt hands at his ankle — small hands, working fast at the rope — and then an arm hooked under his, across his chest, thin but iron-firm, and the world tilted, and he was rising. Rising through the cold layer, through the warm, into air that hit his lungs like a blow and then like mercy.

He vomited half the harbor onto the rocks of the skerry where she hauled him. He hung over the weed-slick stone and coughed and shook while she knelt beside him, wringing seawater out of her braid with both hands, watching him with an expression he would spend three years trying to describe and never quite manage. Not pity. Not relief, exactly. Something closer to accounting — as if she were tallying what the sea had taken and what she had gotten back, and finding the sum acceptable.

"You dropped your catch," Sefa said.

It was the first thing she ever said to him. Not *are you alive*, not *you fool*. She was thirteen, two years younger, daughter of Tevake the diver, and she had seen his line go wrong from her father's boat and jumped before anyone else had finished shouting. Her lips were blue. Her hands were shaking. She hid both by folding her arms and looking at him the way she would look at him forever after: levelly, from beneath wet brows, as though he were a problem she had solved and intended to keep solved.

"I owe you," he said. The words came out scraped raw. "Anything. Whatever you ever ask."

She considered this with the seriousness of a magistrate. The tide chuckled against the rocks. Far off, the boats were coming for them, oars knocking, men calling their names across the water — *Tevake! Kavanagh boy!* — but she didn't turn toward the sound.

"Remember it, then," she said. "Don't just say it. Remember it. People say things in the water they forget on land."

"I'll remember."

"Everyone says that too." But something in her face eased, a fraction, a hairline crack of warmth in the stone of her, and she held out her hand — diver's hand, already calloused at thirteen — and he took it, and that was the whole ceremony. No blood, no oath, no witness but the tide. Two children shaking hands on a rock while the harbor closed over the place where he had very nearly stopped existing.

* * *

"Ines." Fenwick's pen tapped the desk. "The column, boy. Are you ill?"

"No, sir." He blinked the harbor out of his eyes and found the figures waiting, obedient again, and wrote them down. But the memory stayed with him the way the stone stayed in the shoe, and he let it, because some stones you keep.

Three years, and she had never asked. That was the thing he marveled at, mending pens and tallying pearls in the counting room while the divers' season ground on outside. Three years of knowing Sefa Tevake — of hauling her father's baskets when Tevake's breath went bad, of walking her home along the shingle when the fog came in thick, of trading jokes across the drying racks where the nets hung like the rigging of drowned ships — and she had never once called in the debt. Other people collected. The company collected. Quill collected, with his soft voice and his lists. The whole harbor ran on debts the way the tide ran on the moon. But Sefa carried hers the way she carried everything: lightly, on those strong shoulders, without complaint and without spending.

Once, only once, she had come close. They had been sitting on the breakwater the summer before last, feet dangling over the swell, sharing a stolen apple cut into pieces with her knife. The lighthouse had been sweeping its beam through the fog like a slow arm, and she had watched it go around four times before speaking.

"If I asked," she said, not looking at him, "would you do it? Whatever it was. Even if it cost you?"

He had known the answer so long it hardly needed saying, but he said it anyway, because some things are vows only when repeated aloud. "Yes."

"And if it cost you everything?" She turned then, and her eyes in the lighthouse sweep were black and bright and terribly awake. "Your papers. Your place. Your invisibility — don't think I haven't noticed how careful you are, Ines Kavanagh, how quiet, how useful. You survive by being nothing. What if what I asked made you something?"

The beam swept past. In its wake the dark seemed thicker. He had laughed — a small, defensive sound — and said, "What could you possibly want that costs everything?"

And she had smiled and handed him the last piece of apple and said, "Nothing yet," and that had been the end of it, and he had slept badly that night anyway, and many nights after, turning the question over like a shell with something living inside it.

* * *

Now the fog was pressing at the counting room windows and the season was ending and somewhere out on the water Tevake was organizing men, though Ines didn't yet know how far it had gone — meetings in the net lofts, voices low, the word *strike* passed hand to hand like contraband. He knew only what the ledgers told him: catches falling, accounts disputed, tempers short. And he knew what the whole harbor knew, which was that Mr. Quill had arrived in autumn with his soft shoes and his patient eyes and had begun, quietly, to make lists.

Ines finished the column. Checked it. Signed it. Set the pen down precisely parallel to the ledger's edge, because precision was safety, because small neat things attracted no attention.

But his hand paused on the blotter, and he thought of a girl on a rock with blue lips and iron arms, and of a promise made in the water, where people say things they are supposed to remember on land.

*Remember it,* she had said. *Don't just say it.*

He blew out the lamp and went out into the fog, carrying nothing, owed everything, and did not yet know that within the week the orders would come down and the debt would finally come due — that the sea keeps its accounts, and so, sooner or later, does everyone who has ever been saved.
