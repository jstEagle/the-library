## 4. Orders Come Down

The counting room smelled of ink and whale oil and the particular staleness of paper that had been handled too often by too few hands. Ines had learned to read that smell the way other men read weather. It meant a long day. It meant Mr. Aldous Wren, the company's factor, would be in a temper before noon, and that the clerks would absorb his temper like blotting paper and pass it down to everyone beneath them, which was to say, to Ines.

He arrived at half past five, as he always did, before the lamps were lit, and set about the morning's drudgery: stoking the small iron stove, filling the inkwells, sharpening the four pens that answered to his care, sweeping yesterday's shavings from under the long desks where the tallymen sat. The compound was quiet in that hour. Beyond the shuttered windows he could hear the harbor breathing — the slap of water against pilings, the groan of the great crane on Meridian Pier as it swung in the wind off the strait. Somewhere out past the breakwater, the divers' boats were already moving, dark shapes against a sky the color of wet slate.

Ines paused at the window while the stove caught, and looked at those boats, and felt the particular ache he had learned not to name. Three years ago he had been on one of them. His father had been a diver then — a good one, before the lung fever took him — and Ines had spent his boyhood hauling baskets and mending nets and learning the water's moods from men who treated it like a temperamental relative. Now the boats belonged to the company, every hull and oar and sail of them, held against debts that never shrank no matter how many pearls came up from the beds, and the men who rowed them rowed because the alternative was hunger.

He had been twelve when his father died, thirteen when the indenture papers were signed — his mother's hand shaking so badly the clerk had to guide her through her own name. Fourteen now, or nearly. Old enough to be useful. Young enough to be unseen. That was the arrangement of his life, and for three years it had kept him fed.

The first clerk arrived at six. Then another. By seven the room had filled with its usual population of bent backs and scratching pens, and Ines had settled into his corner with the day's first stack of chits to sort — weights and tallies from the weighing house, ration dockets, repair invoices for the drying sheds. He worked with his head down, as he always did. Useful and unseen. The motto of his survival, though he'd never have said it aloud even to himself.

It was Mr. Wren's arrival at nine that changed the shape of the day.

Wren came in wet from the walk between buildings, his gray coat beaded with fog, and he did not go to his office as he usually did. Instead he stood at the head of the central desk, removed his hat, and addressed the room in the flat voice he used for matters of account.

"Clear your desks," he said. "Company business."

The scratching stopped. Eleven faces lifted. Ines lifted his own from the chits and went still, the way he'd learned to go still when something large moved overhead — a habit from his diving days, when sudden shadow meant shark or storm and the only wisdom was to become a stone.

"By order of the resident director," Wren continued, "and pursuant to clause fourteen of the harbor charter, the following persons are declared enemies of the company contract." He unfolded a paper. He did not read it quickly; he read it the way a man reads scripture, each word given its full weight. "Tevake of the Shallows. Rua Tevake, his brother. Maren Ostrova. Jem Halloway. The brothers Kessu. And the committee they serve, being all persons who have subscribed to the so-called divers' association formed last month without license or sanction."

The names went into the room like cold water into a bath. Ines heard them and understood them and felt nothing for the space of three heartbeats — that was the trick of shock, it bought you a little numbness first — and then the meaning arrived all at once.

Tevake. *Tevake of the Shallows.* The man who had taught him to hold his breath until his vision sparked. The man whose daughter —

No. He would not think of her. Not here. Not with eleven pairs of ears around him.

"The said persons are barred from company premises, company boats, and company stores," Wren went on, folding his paper. "Their rations stand withdrawn. Their vessels, being held against debt, are forfeit to seizure effective this hour. Any employee found communicating with the named parties or aiding them in any respect will be considered party to their offense." He put his hat back on. "Questions?"

Nobody had questions. Nobody ever had questions when Mr. Wren asked if there were questions. The silence was itself an answer, and Wren accepted it with a small nod, as though the room had voted him its approval.

"Quill handles the seizures," he added at the door, and left.

* * *

Fear, Ines thought later, watching it move through the compound, does not spread like fire. Fire leaps and roars and announces itself. Fear spreads like oil on water: silent, iridescent, finding every low place, covering everything with a thin film that changes the color of whatever lies beneath.

By midday it had reached the drying sheds, where the shell-openers worked. By one o'clock it had reached the cookhouse, where the woman who ladled out the midday stew — a broad, silent Kessu cousin who was not herself on the list but had married onto it — stood over her pot with her jaw set like a keel. By two, it had reached the pier.

Ines was sent down at two with a bundle of revised ration dockets, because the seizure orders required new numbers and new numbers required a runner, and runners were what Ines was for. He carried the dockets in a leather wallet slung across his chest and walked the length of the pier with his eyes down, counting planks the way he always counted planks, forty-two from the shore fastening to the crane footing, and it was at plank thirty-one that he saw them bringing in the boats.

They came in under tow, six of them, the seized vessels, hauled by the company launch with Quill standing in its stern. Ines knew the boats the way he knew his own hands. There was the *Kestrel*, old Jem Halloway's, her gunwale patched with copper where she'd struck the reef two winters back. There was the double-ender the Kessu brothers fished from, her sail still flaked down on the thwart. And there, third in line, riding light and proud even under tow — the *Miren*, Tevake's boat, built by Tevake's own hands from island timber, the boat Ines had been hauled into half-drowned three years ago, coughing seawater while Sefa knelt over him with her fists pressed to his chest.

They brought her alongside the company float and men went aboard her with inventory books, and Ines stood at plank thirty-one and watched strangers run their thumbs along the wood Tevake had planed, writing numbers in the margins of her life.

Quill stepped off the launch last. Ines had seen the enforcer perhaps a dozen times since arriving at the compound — a spare, dry man with a face like folded paper, who spoke seldom and softly and made you lean in to hear him, which Ines suspected was the whole point. Today Quill wore his gray oilskins and carried a short iron bar, not raised, not threatening, just carried, the way a carpenter carries a tool he expects to need.

The divers had come down to the beach. Of course they had. They stood in a loose crescent above the tideline — twenty men, a scattering of women, the older children — watching their livelihoods being measured and recorded. No one shouted. That was the terrible thing, Ines thought; shouting would almost have been a comfort. Instead they watched in the heavy way of people watching a funeral that has been scheduled without a body.

Tevake stood at the center of the crescent. He was a big man gone lean, with shoulders like a capstan and hair going gray at the temples, and he did not look at the men boarding his boat. He looked at Quill. Just looked, steadily, the way a lighthouse looks at a ship that is determined to wreck itself.

Quill felt it — Ines saw the moment he felt it, a slight turn of the folded-paper face — and crossed the float to the foot of the ramp, and stopped there, and regarded the crowd with the mild attention of a man inspecting stock.

"You've had the notice," Quill said. His voice carried further than it should have. Soft voices always do, over water. "Stores are closed to the listed names. Boats are company property as of this morning. If any listed person attempts to put out on a company vessel, that attempt will be treated as theft, and theft is a magistrate's matter."

"And when the bridge closes?" It was Maren Ostrova who spoke, an old woman with a voice like gravel in a pail. "Storm season's three weeks off, Quill. Bridge shuts, road goes under. You'll have us starve behind your wall?"

"The bridge," Quill said, "is not my department."

A sound moved through the crescent — not laughter, nothing so generous. Something lower and harder.

"The season's haul is owed," Tevake said then, and his voice was quiet too, quieter than Quill's, and it carried further still. "Forty-one days of diving. Weighed and entered. The company holds our pay against debts it invented. Now it takes the boats that were the collateral for work already done. You can write that in your ledger, Mr. Quill. Write it plain, because my children will read it someday."

For a moment — just a moment — Ines saw something flicker across Quill's face. Not shame. Ines doubted the man owned any. Something more like an accountant noting an entry he hadn't expected: *this one will be trouble.* Then it was gone, folded away with everything else.

"The ledgers," Quill said, "are accurate," and turned back up the ramp.

* * *

Ines delivered his dockets and walked back along the shore instead of the pier path, because walking along the shore let him pass the beach, and passing the beach let him see Sefa.

She was at the edge of the crescent, apart from it, the way she always stood slightly apart — a girl of sixteen with her father's capstan shoulders and her mother's narrow face, black hair cut short for diving, arms brown to the bicep where the wetsleeves ended. She was watching the *Miren* the way you watch a wound being dressed by someone else's hands.

Three years ago she had pulled him out of the green dark. Three years ago he had promised her anything, lying on the stones of this same beach with his lungs full of fire, and she had laughed at him — laughed! — and said, *then don't drown again, stupid boy,* as if that settled it. As if a promise could be paid off with good behavior. He had spent three years believing she'd forgotten. Lately, watching the strike committee meet in the net-loft after dark, watching her carry messages between houses with a basket on her hip like any erranding girl, he had begun to suspect she remembered everything, and was waiting to see what he was worth.

Now her father's name topped a list of enemies, and her family's boat was being measured for confiscation, and Ines Kavanagh — clerk's runner, diver's son, invisible man — stood forty feet away with company dockets slung against his chest and did not go to her.

He told himself it was prudence. He told himself the shore was watched, that Quill's men had eyes everywhere, that a word between them now would cost them both. All true. All cowardice dressed as sense. He walked the tideline with his head down and hated himself with a fluency that was becoming practiced, and it occurred to him, somewhere around the old fish-curing racks, that this was the second time in one week he had said nothing while the company took things from people who had saved his life. First Tevake's pay at the weighing house, shorted by a thumb on the scale while Ines stood witness and swallowed it. Now the boats.

Silence, he was beginning to understand, was not a neutral thing. Silence had a weight, and it accumulated, and one day the scales would tip and all of it would come due.

* * *

That evening the compound changed its routines, the way a body changes its breathing when it senses a predator. Extra lanterns at the gates. A second watch on the storehouse. The divers who remained unlisted — the ones who hadn't signed the association rolls, whether from fear or calculation — came to the evening meal late and ate with their eyes on their bowls, and the listed families did not come at all, having been barred from the cookhouse by notice nailed to the door in Mr. Wren's neat clerk-hand.

Ines ate alone in the corner of the runners' mess, and listened.

"...took the *Kestrel*'s sails too, not just the hull..."

"...Ostrova's got grandbabies, they can't mean to turn her out in the storm season..."

"They mean it. Wren drafted the notice himself, I filed the copy—"

"Hush, boy."

"—it's true, there's a second list. I saw the folder. 'Persons under observation.' Half the compound's in it."

Ines kept his eyes on his bowl. Under observation. He wondered idly, chewing, whether a runner who sorted chits and swept floors and had never once signed anything or said anything qualified for such a list. He decided he did not. Invisibility was his single talent, and he had honed it for three years, and no one had ever yet seen fit to observe him.

Then the door opened, and the mess went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with respect.

Quill came in without his oilskins, in his gray coat, and stood a moment just inside the doorway with his hands clasped behind him, surveying the room like a man taking inventory of a warehouse. He did not eat here. Everyone knew he did not eat here. Which meant he had come for some reason other than food, and the reason was standing at the far table talking to the hall steward: Mr. Wren, holding a folder.

They spoke for less than a minute. Wren pointed once — at the notice board by the kitchen door, where the enemy-of-contract list hung in its neat clerk-hand. Quill nodded, took the folder, and left, pausing only to pull the notice from the board and fold it into his pocket, as though the declaration were now official enough to travel by hand rather than by nail.

But as he turned, his gaze crossed the room. Crossed it slowly, table by table, the way a lamp beam crosses water. It passed over the clerks, passed over the stewards, passed over the runners—

—and stopped, briefly, on Ines.

Not recognition. Nothing so warm. Just the flat, weighing attention of a man confirming that everything in the room was where he expected it to be, including the smallest item in the inventory. Then the door shut behind him and the mess exhaled, and conversation restarted in a lower register, like an engine dropped to half speed.

Ines sat very still with his spoon halfway to his mouth.

He had been seen. Not known — seen. Catalogued. Entered, somewhere in that folded-paper mind, under some heading. *Runner, fourteen, diver's son, no associations, no value.* He should have been relieved. Being filed as worthless was the safest thing a person at Meridian Compound could be.

So why, he wondered, walking back through the fog to his bunk in the runners' quarters, why did it feel like the first touch of a hook?

Outside, the fog had come all the way up from the water and swallowed the compound whole. From the direction of the town beyond the walls — the divers' town, the strikers' town, the town that was about to learn what a storm season tasted like without stores or boats — he could hear a bell. Not the chapel bell and not the tide bell. The net-loft bell, rung slow, the way you ring it when the committee wants every house to hear.

Meeting tonight, then. While the company sharpened its knives, the other side was lighting lamps.

Ines stood a moment in the fog between the two worlds, the compound at his back with its new lanterns burning, the town ahead with its slow bell tolling through the murk. Three weeks until the bridge closed. Six weeks of storm season after that. Whatever was coming would arrive inside that window, and it would ask everyone to choose, and he had a sick certainty, standing there with the damp soaking through his jacket, that it would ask him first.

Behind him, a gate lantern guttered and steadied. Ahead, the bell rang on.

He went inside, and lay awake in the dark listening to it, and did not sleep until long after it stopped.
