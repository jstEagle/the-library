## 5. Last Crossing

The bridge had a voice, and for eleven years Ines Kavanagh had fallen asleep to it.

It was not a grand structure — the Meridian Company had built it cheap and rebuilt it cheaper, a spine of ironwood pilings and salt-scabbed chain that linked the harbor town to the mainland road across a channel the color of tarnished pewter. At low tide its planks groaned like an old man rising from prayer; at high tide the water slapped its underbelly in a rhythm that never quite became music but never quite stopped trying. Ines had been carried across it as a boy of nine, asleep on his father's shoulder, and every crossing since had been a small arithmetic of survival: what he was bringing over, what he was bringing back, who might be watching him carry either.

Now he stood at the town end of it and watched them take it apart.

Not destroyed — nothing so merciful as destruction. The company's workmen moved along the span with pry bars and rope, drawing up the center section plank by plank, coiling chain into barrels, stacking the whole season's worth of road onto a barge that sat low and patient in the channel. By nightfall there would be forty feet of open water where a crossing had been, and the harbor town would become an island, and everything on it — every diver, every striker, every grumbling wife and barefoot child — would be sealed inside with the winter.

"They do this every year," said old Maren beside him, her voice pitched to carry no further than his ear. She had come down to watch with the rest, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate. "You'd think after thirty winters they'd build it so it could stay."

"Storm swell takes the middle span," Ines said. He knew the company's reasons the way a clerk knows a ledger — by rote, without believing any of them. "The pilings can't hold against a full gale if the deck gives them leverage."

Maren made a sound in her throat that was not agreement. "The pilings can't hold," she repeated, tasting the words like bad shellfish. "That's what they told us when they cut rations. That's what they'll tell us when the boats don't come back in spring." She turned her eyes on him then, and Ines felt their weight settle on him like a hand. "You're the company's boy, aren't you. The one who keeps the tallies."

He did not flinch. Eleven years of practice had burned the flinch out of him. "I keep records, ma'am."

"You keep *their* records." But there was no venom in it, only a tired kind of curiosity, the way one might look at a bird that had learned to live inside a cage and call the bars home. "Well. Records won't cross that gap come dark, will they."

No, Ines thought. They won't. Nothing will. And he stood among the townspeople while the workmen sang their hauling songs and the last planks came up, and he understood, with a slow cold settling in his chest, that he was standing on the wrong side of the only door out of his life.

* * *

The order had come down three days before, folded into his evening tally book like a knife slipped between pages:

*Bridge closes Thursday, first light. Harbor station winter complement: two clerks, one enforcer, stores sufficient. Kavanagh remains through storm season to maintain ledgers. No exceptions.*

*No exceptions.* He had read the line four times, hoping it would soften on repetition. It didn't. Mr. Quill had signed beneath it with a single economical stroke, the way Quill did everything — as though excess motion were a moral failing.

Ines had gone to him that same evening, because going to him was what the old Ines would do: useful, reasonable, invisible even in appeal. Quill's office smelled of lamp oil and the dried fish he ate at his desk without ever appearing to chew. He listened to Ines's carefully prepared request — his mother's grave needed tending on the mainland side, his lungs were weak, the winter damp — with the expression of a man watching a tide chart.

"Your lungs survived the pearl beds," Quill said. "They'll survive a winter." He blotted the tally sheet Ines had brought him and slid it back. "Besides. You're the only soul in this town the divers still half-trust and the company fully trusts. That makes you worth more here than anywhere on the mainland. I'd have thought you'd understand value, Kavanagh. You count it all day."

There had been nothing to say to that. There was rarely anything to say to Quill, whose genius was to make every cruelty sound like bookkeeping. So Ines had bowed his head and thanked him — thanked him, God help him, the word coming out smooth and practiced as a coin — and walked back through the compound with the taste of his own cowardice thick in his mouth.

And now the bridge was coming apart plank by plank, and the taste hadn't faded.

* * *

Thursday night, the town gathered at the Meridian hall to hear the winter terms read aloud. Ines attended because attendance was expected of him; he stood at the back wall with his tally book under his arm like a shield, and he watched the faces of the people he had spent years learning to be unseen among.

Mr. Halloran read the terms from the platform. Winter ration: reduced by one-third, effective immediately, owing to "supply uncertainties during the closed season." Boat moorings: transferred to compound custody for the duration — every hull, every skiff, every dugout, chained and locked behind the company fence. Assembly of more than six persons: prohibited. Payment of the season's dive shares: deferred until spring assessment.

The hall went quiet in the way a sea goes quiet before weather. Ines had heard that silence before, in the counting room, when Tevake's pearls were weighed and the numbers came back short and Tevake's face did something complicated and terrible and then smoothed itself over, because a diver could not afford rage the way a company could afford patience.

It was Tevake himself who stood first. He filled the aisle like a mast fills a deck, gray-bearded, scarred to the elbow from years of working the beds with his hands because the company would not replace the gloves. When he spoke, his voice was not loud. It didn't need to be.

"My father dove these waters before your company had a name," he said to Halloran, and to Quill, who stood at the platform's edge with his hands folded. "My grandfather before him. We did not ask you to come. We did not ask you to weigh our breath and our bread. And we will not ask you twice to pay what is owed."

"The shares will be paid," Halloran began, "pending—"

"PENDING." Tevake let the word sit in the room like something dead. "Everything with you is pending. The gloves are pending. The medicine for the bends is pending. My daughter's school fee is pending. Well." He looked around the hall, at the faces, and Ines saw the moment the silence changed — saw it turn from fear into something with a spine. "Let this be pending too. Come spring, when the beds open, let your ledger show how many shells we bring up while our shares sit in your strongbox. Let that number be pending."

A murmur ran through the hall like current through water. Somewhere a woman began, softly, to clap, and others took it up, and Ines stood against the back wall with his heart slamming against his ribs and his face arranged into perfect blankness, and underneath the blankness two thoughts fought each other like eels in a net.

The first thought was: *this is dangerous, this is exactly the kind of thing I am supposed to be watching and reporting.*

The second thought was: *good.*

He had never let himself hear that second thought before. He stood very still and let it speak.

* * *

Afterward, in the blue hour before the lamps were trimmed, he climbed to the loft above the counting room where he slept, and he did the thing he always did when the world grew too large: he inventoried.

One knife, bone-handled, his father's. One shell comb, his mother's, pink as dawn on a calm day, the only thing he had kept from the life before the company bought his father's debt and then, four winters later, collected it in drowned flesh. One set of indenture papers, soft at the folds from handling, which bound him to the Meridian Trading Company for — he did the arithmetic without wanting to — five more years. One blanket. One lamp. One tally book, and a stub of pencil worn to the length of a thumb.

Eleven years ago, when he'd first been brought across the bridge, his whole life had fit in a basket. He had learned then the philosophy that had kept him breathing since: carry little, want little, be seen little. A heavy man could be caught. A heavy man could be owed. A man who owned nothing owned no one anything, and no one anything meant no one's enemy, and no one's enemy meant alive. Lightness was safety. Invisibility was armor. Every year he had shed a little more — his father's language first, then his grief, then his anger, packing them away or drowning them, until he was so light a thing that the wind of other people's lives barely bent him.

He sat on the edge of his cot with the comb in his palm and tried to remember when the armor had started to chafe.

It was the bridge, he decided. Or rather, the absence of it. All those years, the bridge had been his excuse — not an excuse he'd ever spoken aloud, but one that lived in him like a tenant. *Someday*, the tenant whispered, *when my term ends, I'll walk out across that span and not look back. Someday I'll be free, and freedom is a place on the other side of the water.* The bridge had made his patience possible. As long as it stood, invisible, he could tell himself he was waiting rather than hiding.

Now he watched it from the loft window, that black gap in the world, and understood that the excuse had gone with the planks. There was no someday across the water. There was only this island, this winter, these people — Tevake with his mast's voice, the women with their tired eyes, the children already thinning at the cheekbone as the rations bit. And somewhere among them, he knew, were men and women who had decided that pending was a word the company would learn to choke on.

Quill would want reports. Quill always wanted reports, delivered in his office with the lamp turned low and the dried fish smell thick, and Quill's questions were scalpels laid out in a neat row: *Who spoke? Who clapped first? Who left early? Who looked at whom?* For eleven years Ines had answered such questions with the clean, useless precision of a man who had mastered the art of reporting everything except what mattered.

But tonight, lying in the dark with the comb's teeth pressing faint lines into his palm, he thought about Tevake's daughter. Sefa. Three years gone now from the shallows where she had pulled him gasping back into the world of air, her arm iron under his chest, her voice furious with fear: *Breathe, you stupid beautiful fool, breathe!* He had promised her anything, that day, kneeling on the shingle with his lungs full of fire. Anything she ever asked. He had meant it with the whole reckless weight of a sixteen-year-old heart, and then he had done what he always did with heavy things — he had packed it away, lightened himself, let the promise sink to the bottom of him like a stone dropped off a boat.

Tevake was branded now. If the strike hardened — and it would harden; Ines had watched enough ledgers close to know what happened to deferred debts — then the list of enemies would grow, and names would be added, and he found himself praying, with a desperation that shamed him, that Sefa's name would somehow be overlooked. That she would stay in the safe country of the unremarkable, the way he had stayed there all his life.

But he had watched her at the meeting tonight. She had stood near the front, straight-backed, seventeen now and carrying her father's stubbornness in her chin, and when the clapping started she had not joined it. She had simply looked around the hall, slowly, the way a lighthouse looks — once around, steady, missing nothing — and Ines had known, with the certainty of a man reading his own death warrant in fine print, that Sefa of the Shallows was never going to be invisible. Not for anyone. Not for anything.

Which meant that whatever was coming for the strikers would come for her too.

Ines pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. Below him, through the floorboards, he could hear the counting room clock ticking, patient as Quill. Outside, the wind was rising off the channel, finding the gap where the bridge used to be, and playing it — a long, hollow note, like breath across the mouth of a bottle. The town's new sound. The sound of being sealed in.

*Carry little,* he reminded himself. *Want little. Be seen little.*

But the words had gone strange in his mouth, like coins from a country that no longer existed.

He was still awake when the knocking started — three floors down, at the compound's side gate, soft and urgent and wrong for that hour. He heard the gate scrape. Heard voices, low, and then Quill's lamp flaring yellow against the stairwell wall, and boots on stone.

Then silence again, long and total, and Ines lay in the dark doing the only arithmetic that had ever frightened him: counting the hours until morning, and wondering, with every beat of the clock below, what news could make a grown man knock on the enforcer's gate in the black of a sealed-in night — and whether, come the dawn, someone's name would be added to a list.

He did not yet know whose name it was. He would know soon enough.
