## 6. A Knock at Low Tide

The fog that morning had a texture to it, a wet wool weight that settled on the shoulders and refused to be shrugged off. Ines Kavanagh carried his ration tin through the compound yard the way he carried everything—close to the body, unremarkable, a thing no eye would snag on. The Meridian Trading Company's flag hung limp above the counting house, and beneath it the divers' families moved through their morning like people wading in deep water: slow, careful, watching the surface for what might rise.

It had been nine days since the bridge closed. Nine days since the road out to the mainland had vanished into storm-season mist, taking with it every easy lie Ines had built his life upon. He was stranded now among the very people he had been quietly paid to watch, and he had told himself this meant nothing—that when the bridge reopened in spring he would simply walk back across it, hand over whatever scraps of observation Mr. Quill required of him, and continue being useful and unseen until his indenture burned itself out year by patient year.

He believed it about as much as he believed the fog would lift before noon.

The ration line formed at the company store as it did every second day. Ines took his place near the back, third from a woman with a baby slung against her chest, and let his gaze drift over the crowd without landing anywhere. That was the trick of it. Look at everything, fix on nothing. A man who stared was a man remembered; a man whose eyes slid past you like weather was a man forgotten.

The store clerk—a pale inland man named Hobbes who counted pearls better than he counted kindness—dole out rice, salt fish, oil. The rations had been cut again. Everyone could do the arithmetic; nobody said it aloud. Tevake's name still sat at the top of the enemy list nailed to the counting-house door, and below it eleven more names, and below those a line of small print that read *all persons aiding or abetting shall be held to the same account.*

*Held to the same account.* Ines turned the phrase over in his head as he accepted his tin. It was a strange kind of mathematics, the company's. They weighed pearls to the grain and lives by the pound, and somehow both came out short.

He was halfway back across the yard when the child stepped into his path.

She was small—six, perhaps seven—with hair cut raggedly at her jaw and the salt-cracked hands of a diver's daughter. She stood directly before him in the mud, which children of the harbor town were taught never to do, and she looked up at him with an expression far too old for her face. Ines knew her distantly: Maren, one of Old Ansel's granddaughters, a family that kept to the eastern shacks where the fog thinned first.

"You're Kavanagh," she said. Not a question.

"I'm nobody," Ines said automatically, and hated the words even as they left his mouth.

Maren reached into the front of her coat and pulled out something wrapped in oilcloth, no bigger than a sardine tin. She pressed it into his free hand with the flat certainty of someone delivering a debt notice. Her fingers were cold as kelp.

"She said you'd know."

Then she was gone—ducked between the drying racks, vanished down the lane toward the water, quick as a gull stealing bread. The whole exchange had taken less time than a heartbeat. Nobody in the yard had looked twice. Being unseen was a skill Ines had spent ten years perfecting, and here was a child of six who wielded it like she'd been born to it.

His hand shook so badly around the parcel that he nearly dropped his ration tin.

* * *

He waited until he was behind his own door, shutter latched, lamp unlit, before he unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside lay a scrap of paper folded eight times into a hard little square, and around the paper, packed with the care of someone who understood scarcity, three dried mussels and a twist of kelp cord.

The paper was soft with damp, written on in pencil, in a hand that pressed too hard—the writing of someone who feared the words might not survive unless they were driven into the page.

*I know what you are. I know what you were to me. The caves under the Point, where we swam as girls. Come at ebb tide, alone, or don't come at all. You owe me what you promised, Ines Kavanagh, and I am calling it in.*

*S.*

There was no signature beyond that single letter. There didn't need to be. Ines sat on the edge of his bunk with the note trembling in both hands, and the room seemed to tilt slowly around him, the way the deck of a boat tilts when the swell comes from a direction you weren't braced for.

Sefa. Alive.

For three days after the orders came down—after Tevake's name went up on the counting-house door and the company boats were confiscated and the strike committee scattered like startled fish—he had allowed himself to believe the worst. Sefa's name had been on that list too, fifth from the top, right beneath her father's, and no one had seen her since. Quill's men had searched the eastern shacks. They had dragged the shallows. They had posted a reward that would have bought a family a winter's worth of flour. And the harbor, which could keep a secret the way an oyster keeps a pearl, had given up nothing.

Ines had told himself it was grief he felt, and only grief. But sitting there in the dark with her handwriting digging into his palms, he understood that what he'd been carrying these three days was something worse than grief. It was guilt wearing grief's coat. Because part of him—the part that had survived the Weighing House, the part that watched Quill shortchange Tevake and said nothing—had been relieved. If Sefa was dead, then the promise he'd made her three years ago on the shingle below the Point was a promise to the drowned, and promises to the drowned were debts no collector could call.

Now the collector was alive. Now she was calling it in.

And beneath the terror—beneath the cold, crawling knowledge of what Quill would do to him if he were caught, what *held to the same account* meant written in a company clerk's careful hand—there rose in Ines a feeling so fierce and shameful he could hardly look at it straight on.

Gratitude. Terrible gratitude. Because she was alive, and because she had come to *him*, out of everyone in that harbor, and because for the first time since the bridge closed, the trap he lived in had a door in it, and the door had her name on it.

* * *

He did not sleep that night. He lay on his bunk listening to the compound settle around him—the creak of timber, the distant clank of the winch housing, a night watchman's boots crossing the gravel on his rounds—and he turned the situation over the way a man turns a stone to see what lives underneath.

If he ignored the note, Sefa would die in the caves. There was no other ending to that story. Ebb tide came twice a day, but food came from the town, and the caves under the Point were flooded sea caverns reachable only by submerged passage—a place you could hide in for weeks but not live in forever. She had asked for what he promised. What she needed was bread, medicine, oil, information. What she needed was a person on the inside of the compound walls, and there was exactly one such person, and he had spent ten years making sure that person was invisible enough that no one would ever suspect him.

That was the horror of it, Ines thought, staring at the ceiling beams. His invisibility, the great achievement of his life, was suddenly worth something. All those years of being no one had prepared him for exactly this.

But if he went, and if he was seen—

His mind supplied the images unbidden. Quill's office with its green-shaded lamp and its ledger open to the indenture rolls. Quill's voice, courteous as a surgeon's probe: *Kavanagh. Sit down. Tell me about your evening.* Quill didn't shout. Quill didn't need to. The man had a way of asking questions that made you feel the answers being weighed, grain by grain, against your future. And the company held Ines's papers—held seven more years of his life in a drawer in that office—and a man whose whole existence is a document learns to fear fire and flood and any hand that might carry that document away.

They would void the indenture, yes. But not the merciful way. They'd transfer him inland, to the mines or the rail gangs, where diver's sons went to be forgotten. Or worse—they'd make an example, because examples were how companies governed towns like this one.

Against all of it, one image: Sefa at fifteen, hauling him out of the green dark after the dive bell had failed, her arms like iron cable around his chest, her voice furious with fear—*Breathe, you stupid boy, breathe*—and then, when he could breathe, quieter, almost shy: *You owe me. Remember that. Someday I'll ask.*

He had promised anything. He had meant it. He had been seventeen years old and full of seawater and gratitude, and he had meant it with every cell of his drowning, saved body.

Ines got up before dawn, dressed in the dark, and ate half his ration. Then, standing at the window with the last of the bread in his hand, he looked at it a long moment—and set it aside, apart from the rest, on the corner of the table where it couldn't be mistaken for ordinary food.

It wasn't much. Half a loaf. But it was a beginning, and beginnings, he knew from watching tides all his life, start smaller than anyone believes possible.

* * *

The next day he walked the Point.

He made a business of it—carried his dive basket as though heading to check the mussel beds, took the long path along the cliff where a man might plausibly go to be alone with his thoughts. Fog came off the water in slow ropes. Below him, the harbor lay gray and hushed, the confiscated boats riding empty at their moorings like gutted things, and beyond them the black teeth of the reef where the sea broke in long white sighs.

He had known these caves as a boy—or known of them. Every diving child knew of them. The flooded caverns beneath the Point where the old smugglers used to cache cargo, where the tide ran through passages no map recorded, where a swimmer who misjudged the ebb could be drawn into the rock and never found. Divers' children were warned away from them with the same stories that warned them off deep water and company property. Sefa had never been warned successfully. She had swum them at thirteen, she claimed, holding a candle stub in a waterproof pouch just to see what the darkness looked like from the inside.

*What did it look like?* he'd asked her once, on the shingle, mending nets in the sun.

*Like being swallowed by something that hasn't decided yet,* she'd said, laughing. *Like the harbor keeping a secret even from itself.*

And now she was living inside the harbor's secret, and the harbor was keeping it, and Ines stood on the cliff above the cave mouth with his heart going like a drum in a procession and tried to calculate the ebb.

The entrance was visible at low water—a black seam in the cliff base, half-drowned, breathing with the surge. From the outside it looked like nothing, a shadow in shadow. From the inside, if Sefa's old stories held true, it opened into chambers where a dozen people could stand dry above the high-tide line, where rainwater collected in stone basins, where a fire's light would never reach the world above.

A dozen people. He thought about that. She hadn't written *I* am hiding. She had sent a child, which meant a network, which meant families, which meant the caves held more than one fugitive from Quill's list. The scale of it pressed against his ribs. This wasn't a girl in hiding. This was a wound the company didn't know it had, hidden in its own flesh.

He crouched, pretending to inspect the mussel beds, and while he crouched he marked the seam of the cave mouth, counted the seconds between surges, noted where the rocks offered handholds on the approach. A diver's son learned to read water the way clerks read ledgers. The passage would be swimmable at slack low water, maybe ninety minutes of grace, no more. Cold enough to kill a man who lingered. Dark enough to swallow a lantern's worth of courage.

He would need food that didn't spoil. Oil for light. Something dry to wrap things in. And he would need to take none of it from his stores in any way that showed—which meant hunger, measured and deliberate, a hunger chosen rather than imposed. The thought should have frightened him. Instead he felt something he hadn't felt in years: purpose, sharp-edged and terrifying, like a knife finally taken out of its cloth.

"Ines."

He nearly fell off the crouch. The voice came from behind and below—from the water itself, from the black seam of the cave mouth, pitched low and carrying the way sound carries over water.

He turned. At the edge of the seam, half in shadow, stood a figure he knew better than his own reflection. Thinner than he remembered. Hair loose and salt-stiffened. Eyes that held the last of the evening light and gave nothing else away.

"Sefa," he breathed, and it came out like a prayer he'd stopped believing in.

"Don't come down." Her voice was flat, urgent. "Not tonight. Watchman changes at the compound in twenty minutes and you'll want to be behind your door. Tomorrow. Ebb tide, two hours after the noon bell. Swim the seam, keep left where it forks, follow the cord I'll have strung." She paused, and something moved through her expression—anger, exhaustion, relief, all fighting for the same narrow ground. "You got my note."

"Maren delivered it."

"Good girl." A ghost of pride crossed her face, then hardened again. "Bring food if you can bring it unseen. Bring yourself regardless. And Ines—" She stepped back into the dark as she spoke, so that only her voice remained, rising off the water. "Three years I've been waiting to collect. Don't make me wait a fourth day."

The seam swallowed her. The sea sighed against the rocks. And Ines Kavanagh knelt on the cliff above the flooded caves of the Point with his hands pressed flat against the cold stone, shaking—not with fear, or not only with fear, but with the terrible, unbearable gratitude of a man who has just been given back the one debt he never wanted forgiven.

Tomorrow, two hours after the noon bell, he would swim into the dark.

He only prayed he could carry enough to matter, and stay invisible long enough to survive the carrying.
