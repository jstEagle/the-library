## 7. Bread Under the Floor

The bread was small. That was what surprised him most, afterward — how small it was. A loaf no bigger than his two fists closed together, gray with barley and ash, the kind the company issued by weight and counted by weight and remembered by weight. When Ines lifted it from the ration shelf that first evening, tucking it beneath his shirt against his ribs, he expected to feel the weight of theft. What he felt instead was its lightness, as if the thing had already begun to disappear, as if it were made of fog from the harbor itself.

He walked back to his bunk in the divers' longhouse without looking left or right. He had learned long ago that looking was the first mistake. A man who looked around himself announced that there was something to see. A man who kept his eyes on the middle distance, on the mud between the buildings, on nothing at all — that man was furniture, and furniture was never stopped.

The compound at dusk smelled of coal smoke and brine and the sour steam rising off the drying sheds where the season's shells were bleached white as bone. Somewhere beyond the palisade a dog was barking at the tide, which came up the shingle every night like an animal smelling meat. Ines counted his steps. Forty-two from the ration house to the corner of the counting room. Thirty more along the covered walkway where the rain drums hung. He knew the distances of his life the way a blind man knows the walls of his own house.

In the longhouse, six men played cards by lamplight, their voices low, their laughter thinner than it had been a month ago. The strike had emptied the air out of everything. Rations cut twice since the orders came down; boats chained at the mooring rings; Tevake's name read aloud in the yard like a sentence being passed. The card players looked up when Ines entered, then looked down again. Nobody here watched anybody too closely anymore. Watching cost attention, and attention was a currency everyone was hoarding.

His bunk was in the last row, against the wall where the plaster had crumbled and you could put your finger through into the cold space between the rooms. He sat, unlaced his boots, and waited for the lamp hour to end.

* * *

The basket had been his father's idea, though his father had been dead four years and had never seen it built. It had been his father's habit, Ines supposed — the habit of double bottoms, of hidden seams, of a life lived one layer beneath the visible one. Divers learned it young: the sea keeps nothing where it seems to keep it. The pearl is never where the shell opens widest.

He had made the false bottom in the second winter of his indenture, for no reason he could name then. A shallow tray of driftwood slats, fitted eight inches above the true floor of the woven basket, lashed with cord boiled in wax so the knots would hold and swell. Above it he kept his working things: the knife, the wax earplugs, the leather nose-clip, a stone for sharpening. Below it, for three years, he had kept nothing at all. An empty room in a house nobody searched. It had seemed like enough just to know it was there — a door in a wall, even if it opened onto darkness.

Now he lifted the tray and laid the bread inside, and the emptiness under the floor of his basket ended forever.

It fit with room to spare. That was the obscene part. All that fear, all that heat climbing his throat as he'd stood in the ration line with his heart hammering like someone else's fist on a door — and the loaf settled into its hiding place as gently as a bird onto a branch. The basket looked exactly as it had always looked. The basket *was* exactly as it had always looked. He set it beneath the bunk frame, in the shadow where the wall met the floor, and lay down on his pallet, and did not sleep.

Sleep, he discovered, had become a country with a border he could no longer cross. His body lay in the dark and ran calculations without him. How much bread remained on the ration shelf — half a dozen loaves, maybe seven, counted that morning. How often the ration clerk took inventory — Sundays, after chapel bell, unless the weather turned and then irregularly. Whether the clerk weighed the loaves or merely counted them. Whether anyone had marked Ines Kavanagh's face among the forty faces that filed through that room each evening, and whether a face could be marked without the mind knowing it had done the marking.

Somewhere past midnight a man in the next bunk groaned in his sleep and turned, and Ines's whole body clenched around the silence like a fist around a moth.

* * *

The second loaf was harder than the first. Not because the taking was different — it was identical, step for step, the same shelf, the same angle of withdrawal, the same press of coarse crust against his stomach — but because now there was a precedent, and precedents have gravity. The first theft had been an accident that happened to be deliberate. The second was a character forming.

He told himself the arithmetic. One loaf, halved and quartered and wrapped in oilcloth, would feed Sefa for three days if she ate thin and drank water with it. Six weeks until spring tide. Six weeks was eighteen loaves. Eighteen loaves was eighteen evenings of walking out of the ration house with his pulse in his ears, eighteen nights of lying awake listening to the longhouse breathe, eighteen mornings of eating his own allotted share — half portions now, since the cuts — and feeling his body eat itself underneath him instead.

Because that was the part nobody warned you about, that theft from your own ration was still theft from yourself. The company fed him barely enough to work. Every ounce he carried out of the line was an ounce his arms didn't have for the hauling, his legs didn't have for the stairs up to the shell lofts. By the fourth day his hands shook slightly when he held them out flat, a fine tremor like the surface of water when something moves beneath it. By the sixth day he caught himself standing at the window of the counting room watching the mess boys carry the officers' supper and hating them with a purity of hatred that frightened him — not Quill, not the clerks who shortchanged Tevake, but boys carrying bread.

He started sleeping in snatches, an hour at a time, waking to check the basket. The basket was always there. The basket was always fine. He checked anyway, sliding his hand under the bunk frame into the dust, finding the weave, finding the weight, counting the loaves by touch the way his mother had once counted coins in a purse by feel in the dark of their old house before the company owned even the dark of it.

Three loaves. Four. Five.

On the fifth night, lying awake, he heard footsteps in the walkway outside and his body went rigid as driftwood, and the footsteps passed, and he understood that this was his life now: a man listening to other men walk.

* * *

There was a mirror in the washhouse, cracked diagonally, silvered with salt bloom so that whatever it showed came back to you weathered, half-drowned. Ines had not bothered with it in years. But one gray morning, washing his face in the tin basin, he caught his reflection and stopped.

The face looking back was doing something wrong with its eyes. They moved too much. They flicked toward the door, toward the window, toward the crack in the wall, in little darting circuits they had never followed before, and the rest of the face stayed carefully, deliberately still — still the way a pond goes still when it's trying not to show what swims below. He looked like a man performing calmness. And a man performing calmness was a man advertising that calmness required performance.

*You are doubled,* he thought. *There is the Ines who carries the basket, and there is the Ines who watches the Ines who carries the basket, and the second one is tired, and tired men make mistakes.*

He thought about the promise. Three years ago, gasping on the shingle with the harbor coming out of his lungs, Sefa's hands pressing his chest, her voice furious with relief — *you owe me, diver's son, you owe me anything I ever ask.* He had said yes with seawater still in his throat. He had meant it the way you mean things at fifteen: totally, and without any idea of what the words would weigh later, when they were asked to be paid.

She had asked for bread. For medicine, eventually, when the coughs went through the caves. For quinine, when the fever came — but the fever hadn't come yet, or hadn't come yet to him; the note had only said bread, and bandages, and the bitter bark if he could find it. She had asked for almost nothing. That was the unbearable arithmetic of it. Everything he was breaking himself to carry was less, all of it together, than what she had once carried for him out of the water — his own breathing, handed back.

* * *

The seventh loaf nearly ended it.

He had timed his visit to the ration house badly — or time had shifted under him, the way it did now, hours folding like wet paper — and he arrived to find the ration clerk still at his desk, doing figures by lamplight, while the last of the divers shuffled through the line ahead of him. Ines took his place at the end and stood with his heart going hard and slow, a diver's heartbeat, the deep beat that comes before a plunge, and watched the clerk hand out the loaves one by one.

The clerk was a narrow man named Pellwith who wore his sleeves buttoned at the wrist even in summer and who had never once, in all the years Ines had drawn rations, looked anyone full in the face. Tonight was no different. Loaf, tally-mark, nod. Loaf, tally-mark, nod.

Then Tevake's widow came through the line.

She had come to draw her dead husband's portion because the company, with its flawless bookkeeping, had not yet struck his name from the rolls — the fever had taken him in the caves, but paper travels slower than death — and Pellwith paused over the ledger for a long moment, pen hovering, and then tore off the week's chit and handed her the loaf and did not mark the column at all. Ines saw it. Saw the unmarked space where the tally should have gone, saw Pellwith's eyes slide away from his own kindness as though embarrassed by it.

And Ines understood, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, that the ledger was softer than he had believed. That columns went unmarked. That the machine that counted everything sometimes forgot to count, and that a missing loaf might live in a ledger's blind spot for days, weeks, forever — or might be found tomorrow by a Sunday inventory, by a bored inspector, by Pellwith himself remembering, in some sleepless hour, the boy with the shaking hands who always stood at the end of the line.

Loaf, tally-mark, nod. His turn. He took the bread, nodded, walked out into the blue evening with his cargo warm against his ribs, and did not begin to breathe properly until he reached the covered walkway, where the rain drums were sounding their hollow iron note in a wind off the water.

That night he wrapped the loaf in oilcloth and slid it under the floor of the basket and lay back and stared at the rafters, where the lamplight from the card game threw shadows that swelled and shrank like breathing things.

Seven loaves now. Seven small weights in a wooden room beneath his life.

* * *

Sunday came, and with it the chapel bell, and with the chapel bell the inventory.

Ines spent the hour of it splitting shell in the drying shed, alone, his knife moving through the nacre in long clean strokes while his mind stood in the ration house watching Pellwith's pen travel down the column. He imagined the pause. The pen lifting. The count run twice. *Forty-one drawn, forty issued.* He imagined Pellwith's buttoned wrists, the way such a man would handle a discrepancy — not with alarm but with a note, a memorandum passed upward, a small flag planted in the paperwork that would take days to climb the ladder to whoever read such flags.

Mr. Quill read such flags. Ines knew that the way he knew the tide tables. Quill missed nothing that touched the ledgers; it was said he read the shipping manifests for pleasure, that he could smell an error in a column of numbers the way a shark smells blood in water a mile off. And Quill had been watching him lately — or Ines had begun to believe he was, which amounted to the same thing — those pale unhurried eyes resting on him across the yard a half-second longer than courtesy required, that soft courteous voice saying *Kavanagh, isn't it? The useful one,* whenever their paths crossed on the shell dock.

The bell finished ringing. Down the shingle, gulls fought over something the tide had given up. Ines wiped his knife, stacked his split shells, and walked back across the compound with his face arranged into its usual blankness, its practiced invisibility, and found that the arrangement required effort now, that the mask had grown heavy, that a face worn too long in wind begins to crack like paint.

At the longhouse he knelt, as he did every night, ostensibly to mend the fraying cord on his nose-clip, and slid his hand beneath the bunk frame into the dark.

The basket was there. The false bottom was sealed. Seven loaves slept in their oilcloth like seven stones at the bottom of a well, and above them, in the world of lamplight and card games and counted things, the longhouse door opened behind him and let in the cold harbor air and the sound of boots.

Boots with a particular rhythm. Unhurried. Even. The stride of a man who has never once needed to hurry, because everything he hunts comes to him eventually on its own.

Ines kept his head bent over the basket, over the fraying cord, over the seven hidden loaves, and did not look up as the shadow fell across the floor beside him — long and narrow and patient as the shadow of a gallows at noon.

"Kavanagh." Mr. Quill's voice, mild as milk. "A word, when you're finished."
