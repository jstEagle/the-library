## 8. Quill Takes Notice

The summons came on a morning so gray the harbor seemed to have been erased, drawn over in pencil and then rubbed out. Ines was mending a net in the drying shed when the boy arrived — not the boy from the caves, not his boy, but a compound boy in a company smock, who stood at the doorway and said, in the flat voice they were trained to, "Mr. Quill will see you. The counting house. Now."

Not *please*. Not *at your convenience*. The company did not traffic in those words.

Ines set down the net. His hands, he noticed, were steady, and he took a strange comfort in that — the body keeping its own counsel, refusing to confess what the mind already knew. For six days he had been carrying bread out of his own allotment, tucking it into the false bottom of his dive basket, and for six days he had been waiting for exactly this. The summons was not a surprise. It was a tide. You did not fight it. You learned its schedule, and you were standing on high ground when it came.

He walked across the compound yard with his basket on his hip, empty now, the false bottom riding against the wicker like a held breath. Rain had come in the night and gone again, leaving the cobbles dark and gleaming, and the gulls sat hunched on the warehouse ridge like old men at a funeral, not bothering to cry out. The counting house stood at the center of everything, as it always had — the stone heart of the Meridian Trading Company, its windows tall and salt-fogged, its door of black oak that swallowed sound. Men went in loud and came out quiet. Some did not come out at all, but were simply spoken of afterward in the past tense, the way you spoke of boats lost beyond the bar.

The clerk at the door looked at him, looked at a list, and nodded him through.

Inside, the air was different. Drier. Warmer. It smelled of paper and lamp oil and, beneath that, something faintly sweet — the tallow of the good candles, the ones that burned for Mr. Quill alone. A fire was lit in the grate, though it was not cold. The fire, Ines understood, was not for warmth. It was a prop. Everything in this room was a prop.

Mr. Quill sat behind the great desk with a cup of tea steaming at his elbow, and he was reading.

That was the first cruelty, though Ines would not understand it as cruelty until later. Quill did not look up. He let Ines stand there, hat in hand — he had remembered the hat, had thought about the hat, had decided the hat made him look like a man with nothing to hide — while the enforcer turned a page with one long finger, unhurried, a man with all the time the company owned. The clock on the mantel ticked with the fat, wet sound of a clock that had never once been in a hurry.

"Kavanagh," Quill said at last, still reading. "Ines Kavanagh. Dive tender, second register, indentured at eleven years of age. Father lost to the bends, mother lost to the fever. No kin. No debts outside our ledger. No vices we've recorded." He looked up then, and his eyes were pale, the color of water over sand, and they were not unkind. That was the terrible thing about them. They were not unkind at all. "Sit down, Kavanagh. Tea?"

"I don't take tea, sir."

"More for me, then." Quill poured himself a second cup anyway, as if the offering had been a formality between gentlemen, and settled back. "You're wondering why you're here."

"No, sir."

That landed. He saw it land — a small flicker at the corner of Quill's mouth, not quite a smile, the way a fisherman's rod tip flickers when the fish has taken the hook without knowing it. "No," Quill repeated, tasting the word. "No, of course not. You've been wondering for six days. Since the third of the month, when the ration tally came up four loaves short in your block. Since the fourth, when it came up three short. Since the fifth." He turned a page on the desk before him — Ines had not even seen the ledger there, so quietly had it been waiting — and read aloud, in the tone of a man reciting poetry he admired. "Bread, four loaves. Bread, three loaves. Bread, four loaves. Bread, two loaves, and a twist of dried fish. You've a modest appetite for theft, Kavanagh. A careful one. Almost — I want to say *respectful*."

"I couldn't say, sir."

"No. You couldn't." Quill drank his tea. "That's what everyone says, in the end. I've sat where you're sitting — no, that's not true, I've never sat where you're sitting, I've only ever sat where I'm sitting, which is a great deal more comfortable. But I've heard *I couldn't say* from divers and clerks and one memorable cook, and I'll tell you what I've learned." He set the cup down and laced his fingers together, and for a moment the lamplight caught the fine scars on the backs of his hands, old ones, rope-burns perhaps, from some earlier life the company had sanded off him. "Everyone says *I couldn't say* right up until the moment they say something else entirely. It's only a question of how long they can afford the silence. Silence has a price, Kavanagh. Everything does. That's not cruelty — that's arithmetic. The company's whole genius is arithmetic."

Ines looked at the fire. He had learned long ago, in the weighing house, in the compound, in every room where power sat behind a desk, that you did not look at the man's eyes for too long. Eyes were doors, and doors invited entry. You looked at the fire, or the window, or the middle distance, and you kept your face the way the fog kept the harbor: present, but giving nothing away.

"May I ask a question, sir?"

"You may ask. Whether I answer is a separate transaction."

"The tally." He kept his voice level, dull, the voice of a man asked to account for tools. "Is it only bread that's short? Or other things?"

Quill's eyebrows rose a fraction, and Ines knew at once he had made an error — a small one, a fish-scale of an error, but Quill collected those the way other men collected coins. "You tell me," Quill said softly. "You're the one who'd know what's missing."

"I only meant — the divers grumble. About the rations generally. If it's only bread, that's one story. If it's flour and oil too, that's a different story." He shrugged, and made the shrug into a confession of smallness. "I'm not clever, sir. I just know what the men say."

"And what do the men say?"

"That the company shorted them first." He said it mildly, almost apologetically, and watched it work: Quill's gaze sharpened, and for a moment — only a moment — the enforcer's attention slid sideways, off Ines and toward the strike, toward Tevake's name, toward the whole festering question of the divers' grievances. It was like watching a big fish turn after a smaller, brighter one. "That's the talk, sir. I don't hold with it. I'm indentured. The company's been good to me. But I hear what's said."

"Mm." Quill was quiet a while. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the fog a buoy bell rang, once, patient as the tide. "The company shorted them first," he repeated. "You know what I think, Kavanagh? I think that's true. I think the company did short them first — shorted them in every way there is, and called it contract, and called it law, and the law agreed, because the law is a ledger too." He said it without heat, without grievance, the way a man might describe the weather, and that was the most frightening thing yet: that Quill saw it all, saw the whole rotten arithmetic of the place, and was not troubled by it in the least. "But here is what the divers don't understand, and what you, being cleverer than you pretend, already do. The company doesn't need to be right. It needs only to be *consistent*. A man who steals bread is a problem. A man who steals bread and is seen to go unpunished is a *doctrine*. Do you follow me?"

"I think so, sir."

"I don't believe you. But you will." Quill opened the ledger again and turned it around, so it faced Ines, and slid it across the desk with one finger. "Your block. Your allotment. Your name against the tally, six days running. Now." He folded his hands. "Tell me how it happened. Take your time. I have all morning, and you, I suspect, have a basket to get back to."

* * *

Ines had prepared a story. He had prepared it in the nights before, lying awake in his narrow bunk listening to the rain, rehearsing it the way a diver rehearses his breathing before a long descent: in, hold, out, slow, slow. The story was simple, because simple stories survive and complicated ones drown. He was hungry. He was growing — he was seventeen and still lengthening like a sapling — and the allotment was cut for a man, not a boy still growing, and he had been ashamed to ask the block steward for an increase because asking was a debt, and debts were how the company held you. So he had taken a little extra. Bread only. Never more than his own hunger. He was sorry, and he would work it back in hours, if the company would let him work it back.

It was a good story. It had shame in it, and shame was credible. It had a motive small enough to be true. It asked for punishment rather than mercy, which flattered the punisher.

He told it now, in a flat, steady voice, looking at the fire, and he told it the way his father had taught him to dive: never rush the descent, never fight the water, let the weight of you carry you down and trust the body to know what the mind is too frightened to remember. And the strange thing — the thing he would remember all his life — was that the lie, once it left him, felt almost like truth. He *was* hungry. He *had* been ashamed. The story was built out of true planks, and so it bore weight.

Quill listened without interrupting. That was worse than interruption. Interruption meant you had said something wrong; silence meant he was waiting for you to say something wrong, and would wait as long as it took, the way the sea waits for a seawall.

When it was done, Quill nodded slowly, as if appreciating a well-tied knot.

"Hunger," he said. "Growing boy. Shame." He said the words like a man laying out cards. "It's a good story, Kavanagh."

Ines's heart did not stop — he was past that, past the dramatics of fear — but it *turned over*, the way an engine turns over, and caught on nothing. "Sir?"

"A good story," Quill repeated. "I want you to know that I know that. Not because I can prove otherwise — I can't, not yet, and I don't say things I can't prove, that's the first rule of my trade — but because I want our relationship to rest on honest ground." He rose, unhurried, and came around the desk, and stood looking down into the fire with his back half to Ines, and Ines understood that the interview was not ending; it was changing rooms. "Here's what I think actually happened. I think a man — or a woman, or a child, it makes no difference to the ledger — is taking bread out of this compound and carrying it *somewhere*. Not eating it. Eating leaves crumbs and grease and a guilty face, and I've seen no guilty faces. This bread is going somewhere with a purpose. And this harbor, this season, has exactly one purpose that requires feeding in secret." He turned his head, and the pale eyes found Ines across the warm air. "You know where the divers went, Kavanagh. The ones who didn't sign the new terms. The ones whose names are on the list."

"I know what's said, sir. That they scattered. That some went over the hills before the bridge closed."

"Some did." Quill came back to the desk, but did not sit. He stood over Ines, and the fire was at his back, and his shadow fell across the ledger, across the tally, across Ines's own name written there in a clerk's careful hand. "And the rest went where scattered men always go in a harbor town. Down. There are caves under this harbor, Kavanagh. Old ones. Every diver's child knows them — you dive them at low tide on a dare, and one boy in ten doesn't come out, and the company posts no notice, because a drowned boy is nobody's liability." He leaned down, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, almost confiding, and it was worse than any shout. "I'm not angry with you. I want you to hear that. Anger is for men who've lost control of a situation. I have not lost control. I am *early*. Do you understand the difference? The bread is six days old. Six days is nothing. A man can live in a cave on rainwater and mussels for a season. But the season is long, and the tide is patient, and I am more patient than either. So here is what happens now."

He straightened. He poured the last of the tea, drank it standing, looking out the fogged window at the harbor he owned in every way that mattered.

"Nothing," he said. "Nothing happens now. You'll go back to your shed, and your net, and your ration, and I will not watch you — not visibly. No escort. No searches. You'll think you've passed, and I want you to think that, for a day or two, because a man who believes he's passed relaxes, and a relaxed man is *legible*." He set the cup down with a small click, precise as a signature. "And in a week, or two weeks, when the tally comes up short again — and it will, Kavanagh, because whatever you're feeding is still hungry — I will know it before you've cleared the yard. And then the arithmetic changes. Because then it's no longer theft. Theft is a flogging and a docked ration, and we'd both shake hands and be done. But feeding enemies of the contract — that's a different entry in the ledger. That one has a magistrate's signature under it, when the bridge reopens. Do you know what they do at spring Assizes to men who provision declared enemies?"

"I can imagine, sir."

"Don't imagine. Imagine is what got you into this. *Know*." Quill came around the desk and opened the black oak door, and the fog-gray light of the yard came in, and with it the smell of salt and tar and the ordinary world, and Ines felt it on his face like the first breath after a long dive. "You're a useful boy, Kavanagh. That's not flattery; it's an inventory. Useful, invisible, no kin. Men like you live long lives in this harbor. I'd hate to see the ledger waste one." He paused, and for a single unguarded moment something moved behind the pale eyes — not warmth, nothing so generous, but a kind of professional curiosity, the look of a man studying a knot he has not yet decided whether to cut or keep. "Go on, then. Mind the step."

* * *

Ines walked back across the yard with the basket on his hip and his face arranged into the dull, obedient blankness that had kept him alive for six years, and it was only when he reached the drying shed and set the basket down among the nets that his hands began to shake — not much, just a fine tremor, like the ground before a distant seaquake.

He had survived the hour. He understood that with a diver's cold clarity: he had survived *the hour*, the way you survive a dive, and the hour was not the sea. Quill had told him the truth, that was the horror of it — told him plainly, courteously, with tea and firelight, that he was already caught, that the only question was how much line the enforcer would pay out before the reel locked. *I am early.* The words sat in Ines's chest like swallowed ballast.

And yet.

And yet that night, when the compound lamps were doused and the fog came up off the water to swallow the masts, Ines knelt in the dark beside his bunk and lifted the loose plank, and took out the bread — two days' hoard, wrapped in oilcloth, still faintly warm from being carried against his body — and he did not put it in the basket. Not tomorrow. Not the day after. Quill was watching the basket; Quill had said so without saying it; the basket was a door the enforcer had already marked.

So Ines did the thing he had never done in six years of being useful and unseen. He changed the pattern. He waited for the change of tide, when the watch turned and the fog was thickest, and he went out over the compound wall with the bread strapped flat against his belly under his shirt, and he swam.

The water was black and cold as iron, and the current pulled at him like a hand that knew his name, and somewhere below him the caves opened in the dark like a held breath. He could not see them. He did not need to. He had been diving these waters since he was small enough to ride his father's shoulders, and his body knew the way the way a bird knows the sky.

He thought, as he swam, of Quill's voice, courteous and surgical: *a man who believes he's passed relaxes.*

Let him think I've relaxed, Ines thought, stroking into the dark. Let him think I'm legible.

The bread was going to the caves tonight. And everything, from tonight on, would be different.

The fog closed behind him like a page being turned.
