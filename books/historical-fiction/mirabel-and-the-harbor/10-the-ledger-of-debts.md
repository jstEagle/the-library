## 10. The Ledger of Debts

Sefa did not lead him deeper into the caves so much as she led him *through* them, the way a schoolmistress walks a latecomer past the front benches — making certain he saw everything, missed nothing, understood exactly how much he had not known.

The passage beyond the first chamber narrowed and then opened like a throat, and the dark changed quality. It was no longer the dead black of stone and water but a living dark, shot through with small amber lights. Oil lamps, Ines realized. Dozens of them. They hung from iron hooks driven into the rock at shoulder height, each with its own little halo of warmth, and beneath each lamp there was something that stopped his breath.

People.

Not Sefa alone, hiding in a hole with her grief and her fury. People — women mending nets by lamplight, their fingers moving with the automatic patience of those who have done the same work ten thousand times. Old men sitting on upturned crates, playing a game with pebbles on a slate. Children — three of them, four — chasing one another between the stalagmites on soft bare feet, shushed by mothers without any real conviction, because what harm could noise do here, where the sea itself was louder than anything human?

A girl no older than six sat cross-legged before a flat stone, arranging mussel shells by size, smallest to largest, with the terrible seriousness of a child who has been told there is nothing else to play with.

Ines stood dripping in the entrance, his stolen bread heavy in the oilcloth bundle against his chest, and felt the floor tilt under him though the stone was level.

"This is what you brought me to see," he said. His voice came out wrong, too loud, echoing off wet rock.

Sefa turned. In the lamplight her face was thinner than he remembered — the roundness gone from her cheeks, the bones standing out like the ribs of a beached boat — but her eyes were the same eyes that had looked down at him through green water three years ago, furious and unafraid.

"No," she said. "This is what I brought you to *account* for."

* * *

She walked him through it chamber by chamber, and she counted as she went, aloud, in the flat voice of a tally-keeper who has learned that numbers are the only weapon left to people with nothing.

"Eleven families," she said, ducking beneath a shelf of limestone furred white with mineral bloom. "Thirty-one souls when we came in. Twenty-eight now." She did not explain the missing three, and Ines did not ask, because he already knew: Tevake's cough had begun down here, everyone whispered it, the damp and the cold had gotten into the old man's chest before the caves ever had. The fever was only finishing what the company started.

"Here." She stopped at an alcove where rainwater ran in a thread down the wall, thin as a spider's silk, into a line of barrels set on their sides. Someone had channeled it with strips of tin hammered into gutters, patched with tar, patched again with cloth soaked in wax. "We drink what the mountain gives us. When it rains, we drink well. When it doesn't—" She shrugged. "When it doesn't, we learn thirst. There's a lesson in it. The company would charge us for the lesson if they could find a way."

Further in, a woman was rendering blubber over a low flame, and the smell hit Ines like a wall — fish oil and smoke and salt, the smell of poverty distilled to its essence. But the woman looked up as they passed and nodded to Sefa, and then, after a moment's hesitation, nodded to Ines too, and there was nothing in the nod but courtesy, which somehow was worse. He was a stranger here. He had come from the world of lamps that burned kerosene bought with company scrip, of bread baked in company ovens, of wages weighed out in a room with brass fittings, and these people — these *enemies*, these names on a list pinned to the counting-house door — greeted him politely because Sefa vouched for him, and because politeness was all they had left to give.

"Old Marama keeps the mussels," Sefa said. "The beds below the low-tide line, where Quill's men can't work the dredges. We harvest at slack water, in the dark, and we've lost two baskets to the current this season." She paused at a ledge where row after row of shells lay open and scraped clean, the meat long gone, the insides polished smooth by someone's patient thumb. The children wore strings of them. Not ornaments — buttons, tokens, currency in an economy of nothing.

"And the garden?" Ines asked, because ahead of them, impossibly, the passage widened into a chamber where light fell from a fissure in the ceiling far above — a coin of grey daylight, filtered through fog — and beneath it, in wooden boxes and broken dory planks filled with sand and guano scraped from the rocks, something green was growing.

Sefa almost smiled. Almost. "Seakale. Samphire. A little cress if we keep the roots wet. Old Marama again — she grew it on Tevake's boat deck, in a crate, for thirty years. Says a body needs green or the gums go bad." She touched one of the pale leaves with a tenderness that undid Ines more than any accusation. "It's not enough. Nothing is enough. But we are alive, and we are fed, and every mouthful is taken from the sea the company says belongs to it."

He stood beneath the fissure and let the grey light fall on him and understood, with a slow cold certainty, that he had been lied to in the most complete way possible: not by words, but by the shape of his own attention. For three years he had lived inside the compound walls and believed the walls contained the world. The strike, when it came, had reached him as ink on paper — *the divers' committee is declared in breach of contract; the following persons are stripped of license and ration* — names in a ledger, enemies in a column, abstractions to be managed by men like Mr. Quill with their careful voices and their surgical questions. He had read the list. He had seen Tevake's name at the top of it, and Sefa's father beneath it, and Sefa's own name below her father's like a footnote, a daughter inherited by the enmity as property is inherited by debt.

He had thought: *a handful of troublemakers.*

He stood now in a village. A village with gardens and gutters and children learning to count on mussel shells, hidden beneath the harbor of the town that employed him, starving by inches in the damp while forty yards overhead men walked the quay buying bread with Meridian scrip.

"Say it," Sefa said quietly. She had watched his face the whole time; she missed nothing, she never had. "Whatever you're thinking. Say it out loud. You owe the air that much."

"I thought there were five of you," Ines said. "Six. I thought—" He stopped. Started again, because she was right, and because lying to her here, in the green light and the smell of growing things, was a thing he found he could no longer do. "I thought you were a problem. That's how the counting room talks. Problems get solved. Costs get cut. I read your name on a list and I thought, *she's clever, she'll get out, she'll be fine.*" His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it. "I made you small so I wouldn't have to be afraid for you."

"Yes," Sefa said. No anger in it. That was the terrible thing — no anger at all, only the weary precision of someone confirming a figure already entered in the book. "You made us small. The whole town does it. Quill does it best of anyone — he calls us *the situation.* 'The situation in the harbor.' As if we were weather." She turned away and lifted one of the lamp hooks free, and the light swung and sent shadows wheeling across the walls like a flock of startled birds. "Come. There's someone you should meet before you eat anything. Everyone eats together here or no one eats."

* * *

They ate together in the largest chamber, twenty-eight souls seated on crates and stones around a spread of mussel broth and seakale and a single loaf of bread that had been carried in by some smuggler's kindness weeks ago and was now divided so finely that Ines received a slice thinner than paper. Before anyone ate, old Marama rose, leaning on a net-mending needle worn smooth as bone, and spoke a few words in the diving tongue — the liquid syllables Ines had heard all his life on the water and never understood — and the others bowed their heads.

"She thanks the sea and the mountain," Sefa murmured beside him. "And she asks forgiveness for taking the mussels from the beds. Even now. Even starving. My people ask permission of what feeds them." Her voice had an edge on it now, fine as a blade's. "Your company posts notices. Ours asks forgiveness. Notice the difference, Ines Kavanagh, next time you're copying out a manifest."

The broth was good — better than good, it was the finest thing he had eaten in a year, though he knew it was mostly salt water and patience — and he ate slowly, ashamed of his hunger and unable to master it. Around him the talk ran low and constant in two languages, and pieces of it reached him like flotsam: a child's fever that had broken; a lantern that needed wick; the spring tide, mentioned twice, always in the same careful tone people used for a thing too fragile to say loudly. An old diver named Ratu — Sefa's father's cousin, she said, though the word meant more than cousin — asked him questions in slow, courteous trade-English: how many men did Quill have now, were the boats still chained at the company dock, had the bridge closed yet. Ines answered honestly, each answer landing among them like a stone dropped in a well, and he watched them weigh what he said the way they'd weigh a pearl, turning it to the light.

And all the while he was doing sums he did not want to do. Thirty-one mouths come down to twenty-eight. Rainwater measured in barrelfuls. Mussel beds harvested in the dark by people whose night vision was worth more than their licenses had ever been. A garden the size of a tablecloth feeding a village. This was the enemy of the Meridian Trading Company. This was what the orders had come down against, what the rations were cut against, what Quill's patient, courteous machine ground slowly toward. Not a riot. Not even a strike, anymore. A grandmother's gutter made of tin scraps. A child sorting shells by size because size was the only category left to her.

He thought of the weighing house. Of Tevake standing with his cap in his hands while Quill shorted him a season's pearls with the smooth apology of a man discussing arithmetic, and of himself, Ines, standing at the edge of that room with his delivery docket and his useful, invisible silence, telling himself *it is not my debt.* He had built his whole life on that sentence. Carry little. Owe less. Be seen by no one and no one will collect from you.

But the debt had been collecting anyway. It sat across the fire from him now, in the person of a girl sharing her broth with a smaller child who was not hers. It hung on the walls in rows of scraped-clean shells. It slept in the chests of old men breathing the cave-damp that was killing them by fractions.

"Ines." Sefa's hand closed over his wrist, and he realized his spoon had stopped halfway to his mouth. "Where did you go?"

"Nowhere," he said. And then, because she deserved better than nowhere: "To the weighing house. In my head. The day Quill shorted your father." He set the spoon down. "I stood there and I watched it happen and I said nothing, and I told myself silence was safety. That's the whole of my philosophy, Sefa. That's everything I know how to be. Light enough to carry, quiet enough to keep. And I look at this—" his hand made a small helpless circle that took in the lamps, the garden, the children, the graves he hadn't been shown but knew must exist somewhere back in the deep passages— "and I understand that my philosophy is just another way of saying *let them starve quietly.*"

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark a baby cried and was hushed. Nobody at the fire had stopped talking, had stopped eating, had marked his confession as anything but a man speaking low to the girl beside him — and that, too, was a kind of shame, that his ruin of himself could happen invisibly, in character.

"Good," Sefa said.

He blinked. "Good?"

"You finally understand the size of it. That's not despair, Ines, that's *ballast.*" She leaned close, and the lamplight moved in her eyes. "A diver who doesn't know the weight of his stone drowns on the way down. Now you know. So the question isn't whether you're ashamed. Shame is cheap; everyone in this cave owns some. The question is what you carry up from the bottom."

* * *

Later — much later, when the fires had been banked to coals and the children slept in heaps under sailcloth like puppies — she showed him the last thing. She took his wrist again, wordless, and led him along a side passage where the lamplight thinned to embers, to a flat wall of stone as smooth as a slate.

Scratched into it, in lines fine as fishing gut, was a record. Dates. Names. Numbers. He had to put his eye close to see them by the faint glow of her shuttered lamp.

"The ledger," Sefa said. "Marama started it the day we came in. Every soul accounted. Every basket lost to the tide. Every rain that fell, every rain that didn't. Every name the company struck off its books." Her finger traced a column. "They think striking our names from the rolls unmakes us. Look."

He looked. Name upon name upon name, in a hand that had grown steadier as it went down the page, as if the writing itself had grown certain of its purpose. Tevake's whole family. Ratu's. The widow Lani and her sister. Boys he had known from the beach, girls he had seen carrying water, entire households he had passed a thousand times on the strand road without seeing them at all — and there, midway down, in the same patient script: **Sefa, daughter of Tevake.**

"They wrote us out," she said softly, "so we wrote ourselves in. That's all a ledger is, Ines. It's a way of insisting you exist. Your company has theirs — pearls in, wages out, debts owed, debts collected. Everything weighed, nothing remembered." Her finger came to rest on the last line, freshly scratched, the stone dust still pale in the groove. "Ours remembers."

And beneath the names, in larger letters, deliberate as a signature:

**WE ARE THE HARBOR.**

Ines stood before it a long time. The cave breathed around him — the slow tidal respiration of the flooded passages, air drawn in and pushed out through throats of stone, the whole hidden village rising and falling on the lungs of the sea. He thought about weight. About the knife and the comb and the folded papers he had carried for three years, measuring his life in portable things, keeping himself light enough never to be caught, never to be owed, never to be held.

His pockets had never felt so empty. His hands had never felt so full.

"Sefa," he said. His voice didn't sound like his own. "Tell me the rest. The plan — however half-broken it is. The boat, the tide, all of it. Whatever you need counted, weighed, carried, stolen, or lied about." He turned to face her, and found that the shame had not vanished; it had changed state, the way ice changes to water, finding its level, becoming something that flowed toward a purpose instead of pooling underfoot. "I'm done being invisible. Tell me what that costs, and I'll pay it in installments until it's paid."

Something moved behind her eyes — not trust, not yet, trust would take longer than a night and a bowl of broth — but consideration. The look of a tally-keeper deciding whether to enter a new name in the book.

"There is a boat," she said at last. "There was supposed to be a boat." And in the way her voice flattened on the words, Ines heard the sound of hope being reduced to arithmetic — and knew, before she said another word, that whatever number she gave him next, it would be the truest thing he'd heard all year, and the cruelest.
