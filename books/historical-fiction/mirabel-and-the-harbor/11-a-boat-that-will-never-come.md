## 11. A Boat That Will Never Come

The tide came back at four bells by the compound clock, which meant nothing down here except that the water rose and the air grew smaller. Ines had learned the cave's breathing in three visits: the long exhale of ebb when the passage stood open and cold wind poured out of the rock like something exhaling through a keyhole, and then the slow inhale, the sea climbing the throat of the entrance, swallowing the sound of the world above until there was only water against stone and the drip of condensation from a ceiling no one had ever seen.

He sat with his back against the wall where Sefa's people had wedged a plank into a natural shelf, making a bench, making furniture, making *home* out of a place the company's maps marked as worthless. That was the thing he kept failing to understand about them. They did not endure the cave. They inhabited it.

Sefa crouched at the waterline, filling a tin pail from a seep that ran fresh — rain caught somewhere in the hill's interior, filtered through limestone until it came out sweet. She filled it without looking at her hands, the way her father must have shucked oysters, the way Ines himself tied knots. The body knowing its work so well the mind was free to go elsewhere.

"Where does yours go?" she asked, without turning.

"My what?"

"Your mind. When you stare like that." She set the pail down. "You've been staring since you climbed through. I thought it was the swim. It isn't."

It was the arithmetic. He'd been doing it since the counting room, since the bridge closed, since the note folded inside a child's fist: *the caves, low tide, what you promised.* He'd done it walking, swimming, lying awake on the plank bench listening to forty people breathe in the dark. And every time he finished the sum, the answer came out worse.

"The bridge closes for how long?" he said.

"You know."

"I want to hear you say it."

She wiped her hands on her skirt and finally looked at him. Three years had changed her the way weather changes a headland — not softer, exactly, but worn into truer lines. Her hair was cut short now, diver-short, practical, and there was a scar along her jaw he didn't know the story of and understood she would tell him only if she decided to.

"Nineteen weeks," she said. "Maybe twenty if the storms run long. The road crews don't work in the wet. Nothing crosses until they do." She paused. "And even then, nothing crosses for us. You know that too."

* * *

They walked the length of the main cavern while she showed him, because showing was how Sefa argued — she had never once won an argument with words alone, and she knew it, and she built her cases out of things instead.

Here: the mussel beds they'd seeded on rocks below the low-tide line, ropes of them, harvested by boys who swam the passage on the ebb with knives in their teeth. Here: the rain-catch, a sailcloth funnel stitched by three mothers and slung beneath a crack in the roof, dripping into barrels that had once held company whale oil. Here: the lamp corner, where they hoarded oil like misers, burning one wick at a time, moving it room to room as the families moved, a single flame shared among thirty-one souls.

Thirty-one. He counted twice to be sure. Children, old men, two women heavy with child who would deliver their babies underground to the sound of the tide because the alternative was Quill's list.

"Tevake's wife is over there," Sefa said quietly, nodding toward a curtained alcove made of flour sacking. "She hasn't spoken in nine days. Not since the boats were burned."

Ines had seen the boats burn. From the counting room window, from the wrong side of the glass, watching the harbor fill with orange light while Mr. Quill stood beside him with his hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring fireworks. *Property of defaulting parties,* Quill had called them. Eleven hulls. Tevake had built two of them with his own hands, and Ines had watched him stand on the shore watching them go, and Ines had said nothing, because saying nothing was the whole architecture of his life, load-bearing silence, and he was only now learning what the building would cost to bring down.

"They think a boat is coming," he said.

"Some of them believe it. Some of them need to believe it. Those aren't the same thing but I've stopped correcting the difference." Sefa stopped walking and turned to face him fully, and in the lamplight her eyes were the flat gray-green of deep water over sand. "Sit down, Ines. I'm going to tell you about the boat."

* * *

His name was Captain Ellery Voss, and he ran a pearling lugger called the *Kindly Gull*, which Sefa swore was a joke he'd made once and then couldn't escape, the way a man can't escape a nickname or a debt.

"He owed my father," she said. "Not money. Worse than money. Nineteen years ago Tevake dove on a wrecked lugger in the channel during a squall and pulled Voss's brother out of the water. The brother lived another thirty years and died fat in Port Meridian, and Voss never forgot that the reason he got those thirty years was a diver who didn't ask what the salvage rights were first." She said it without romance, cataloguing facts. "So when the strike committee needed a way out for the families — just the ones most at risk, the named ones, the children — my father went to Voss and Voss said yes. One run. Before the bridge closed. Out past the point, down the coast to the mission station at Karu, where nobody asks questions because everybody there has something to be asked about."

"And?"

"And the bridge closed early." Her voice didn't change at all, and that was the worst part, the terrible levelness of it, grief compressed until it rang like struck metal. "The storm season came eight days ahead of the almanac. Voss had to make port before the bar went dangerous or lose his license and his lugger both. He waited until the last safe morning. We weren't ready. Two families hadn't come down from the ridge yet — the roads were already washing out — and my father wouldn't leave people behind, so we missed the window by *eight days*, Ines. Eight days and a stubborn old man's conscience."

She sat down on the plank bench across from him, knees nearly touching his, and put her face in her hands for exactly three seconds. Then she took them away again.

"So the boat is gone," Ines said.

"The boat is gone. Voss is gone. Karu might as well be the moon." She looked up. "Do you want to know what's left? Because this is the part where you do your sums, and I'd rather you did them looking at me."

* * *

What was left was the spring tide.

She explained it the way her father explained currents, with a stick and the cave floor for a chart. Twice a year, at the equinox springs, the tidal range grew extreme — the lows lower, the highs higher, the whole harbor breathing deeper than any other time in the year. On the lowest of those low waters, the reef gap at the harbor mouth — the one the company dredged for its own ships, the one that stood barely a fathom deep at ordinary ebb — opened up wide enough and calm enough, in fair weather, for a small boat to slip through without a pilot, without lights, without the company's permission.

"Voss was going to take us through the gap on a spring ebb," she said. "That was always the plan. The bridge closing was just the other half of it — no road, no riders, no magistrate, nobody chasing us because nobody could reach us. We'd have been gone before anyone counted heads."

"But the bridge also means—"

"It means no boat can come to us either. Yes." She laid the stick down. "Nothing sails out of this harbor in storm season. Nothing sails in. The fishing fleet is hauled out and chained. The company luggers are lashed at their moorings. Every hull in this harbor is either burned or locked, and the men who could handle a boat through the gap are all either on Quill's list or too frightened to be anything else." She let that sit. "Except."

"Except?"

"Except the spring tide doesn't care about the bridge. The spring tide comes whether the road is open or shut. Six weeks from now — maybe five, the almanac's been wrong twice this year — the water drops farther than it will drop again until next autumn. And if there is a boat, and a hand to steer it, and weather that holds for one night—" She spread her hands. "One night. That's the whole plan now. One night, one boat, one gap in the reef, one gap in the season."

"And where does the boat come from?"

"That," said Sefa, "is the question I've been asking the dark for six weeks."

* * *

He did the sums after all, later, alone, in the side chamber they'd given him near the lamp corner because he refused the alcove with the curtain — he wasn't family, he kept saying, and they kept not arguing with him, which was its own kind of verdict.

Six weeks. He wrote it in his head the way he wrote figures in the counting room, clean columns, no sentiment. Thirty-nine days of food at current ration, if the mussels held and the fever stayed away and no more mouths came down from the hills. Thirty-nine days against forty-two. A deficit of three days, and deficits compounded; he'd spent four years learning that in Mr. Aldous's ledgers. People ate more in the cold. Children ate more when they were growing. Fear itself consumed calories — he was living proof, hollowed out these two weeks from carrying bread under floorboards and lies across tables.

Then the other column. Quill. Quill who interviewed men courteously and remembered everything. Quill who had already noticed missing rations and had, Ines was almost certain, noticed *him*. Quill who had all the patience of a man paid by the year and all the cruelty of a man bored by it. What were the odds Quill found the caves in six weeks? Ines turned the number over and couldn't fix it, and the inability to fix it frightened him more than any figure he'd ever entered in a book. In the counting room, everything balanced or it didn't. Down here the variables breathed and multiplied.

And the third column, the one he kept trying not to write: even if they survived six weeks, even if the food stretched and Quill stayed blind — where was the boat? Who, in a harbor where every hull was chained and every sailor watched, would risk hanging to carry thirty-one enemies of the Meridian Trading Company through a reef gap in the dark?

Nobody he could name. Nobody yet.

He sat in the dark with the single shared flame guttering somewhere beyond the curtain of sacking, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, what Sefa had actually told him tonight. Not a plan. An equation with two unknowns and a deadline. Hope, downsized to arithmetic — and arithmetic was honest, and honesty was unbearable, and he loved her for refusing to dress it up in anything warmer.

* * *

He found her at the waterline near dawn, or what passed for dawn down here — the hour when the ebb began and the passage exhaled and the air stirred faintly, bringing with it the smell of the living harbor, salt and kelp and tar, the smell of the world going on without them.

"You're leaving," she said. It wasn't a question. Low tide was his window back, and they both kept the tide's calendar now.

"For today."

"For today." She hugged her knees, chin resting on them, watching the black water drop inch by inch off the rocks. "I keep thinking about what my father says. Said." The correction landed between them like a dropped tool. "He says the sea gives you nothing on your schedule. You take what it offers, when it offers, or you drown waiting for better terms." She glanced at him. "I used to think that was about diving."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's about everything. The bridge closed on its schedule, not ours. The spring tide will come on its schedule, not ours. Everything that matters arrives on the water's timetable and never on ours, and the only choice left to a person is whether to be ready when it does." She unfolded herself and stood, close enough that he could see the exhaustion under her defiance, the hunger under the exhaustion. "So here's what I need from you, Ines Kavanagh. Not bread. Not medicine, though God knows we need both. I need you to find me a boat. Or a skipper. Or a miracle with a keel. Six weeks. Whatever you are inside that compound, whoever you can reach, whatever strings you can pull with those soft counting-room hands of yours — pull them. Because if the tide turns and we're still in this hole, we don't get another chance until next autumn, and none of us—" her voice cracked, once, briefly, and she mastered it, "—none of us are sure we'll be alive to see next autumn."

The water was dropping fast now. His window was opening, and dread came with it, the same dread that rode him home every time: the swim out, the compound, Quill's gray eyes, the long performance of being invisible.

"Sefa." He said it before he could stop himself. "If I can't find a boat—"

"Then you'll find one anyway." She almost smiled. It was the smile from three years ago, from the shallows, from the moment her hands closed on his collar and hauled him back into the world. "Because you promised me anything I ever asked. And I am asking."

He slid into the cold water and swam out through the throat of the cave into the gray morning harbor, and the last thing he heard, fading behind him, was the tide beginning to turn — and the first thing he saw, when his head broke the surface inside the breakwater, was a lantern moving along the quay where no lantern should be at that hour, swinging in a slow, patient arc, the way a man swings a light when he is searching.

Quill's men were out early. And they were counting something.
