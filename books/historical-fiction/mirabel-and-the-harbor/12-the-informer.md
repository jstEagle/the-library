## 12. The Informer

The caves had a way of teaching you their moods, and by the third week Ines had learned to read them the way he'd once read the compound's corridors — by sound, by draft, by the small betrayals of light. The main chamber breathed with the tide. Twice a day it exhaled through the submerged passage, and the water in the lower pool sank with a long, wet sigh, exposing shelves of rock furred with mussels, and then it returned, patient as a creditor. Between those breaths there was a stillness so complete you could hear a child's heartbeat three chambers away.

He had learned to count the people of the caves the way he counted everything now: what could be carried, what could be lost.

Eleven adults. Four children. Old Marama, who had dived until her lungs gave out and now sat wrapped in sailcloth near the freshwater seep, sorting shellfish by touch because her eyes had gone the color of fog. The Ferro brothers, who kept a fire going in a stone ring that vented through a crack in the ceiling — a risk Sefa hated but permitted, because raw mussel was turning the children's gums soft and grey. Two widows whose husbands' names were on the strike committee's list beside Tevake's. And the boy, Tam, fourteen maybe, all elbows and silence, who had come in over the rocks one night in the second week and never said where from.

Ines did the arithmetic every night without meaning to. Eleven mouths against the bread under his floor at home, against the quinine gone, against the rainwater caught in the hollows of the upper chamber where the roof wept even on dry days. It was not enough. It had never been enough. But hunger, he was learning, was a thing the body negotiated with, the way a diver negotiated with depth — you went down anyway, and you came up if you were lucky, and you didn't think about the ones who hadn't.

Tonight the fire was low and the children were asleep in a heap like pups, and Sefa sat across from him sharpening a fishhook on a whetstone she kept in a pouch at her waist. The sound was small and rhythmic, steel on stone, and Ines found his eyes closing to it.

"You sleep like a gull," she said without looking up. "One eye open."

"One eye is all I've got left."

"Then keep it open." She tested the hook's point against her thumb. "The tide's wrong tonight. High slack past midnight. If anyone's coming, they'll come on the ebb, when the passage thins out."

"Who would come?"

She looked at him then, and the firelight moved in her face the way it moved on water. "That's the question, isn't it."

* * *

It started, as these things do, with something too small to name.

A week earlier, Ines had noticed that the mussels on the middle shelf had been stripped bare — picked clean in a pattern no forager among them used. They took from the edges inward, always, a habit of caution bred into people who'd grown up rationing a reef. This had been taken from the center out, greedily, quickly, by someone who knew they wouldn't be back.

He'd said nothing. He was good at saying nothing; it was the one trade the Meridian Trading Company had certified him in.

Then the rainwater. The hollows in the upper chamber held perhaps two days' drinking for all of them, and twice now Ines had come up to find them lower than the weather accounted for. Sefa had marked the levels with a charred stick, lines scratched into the stone like the tally marks in a counting room, and the lines told a story she didn't like: water going where no thirst among them explained.

And then, three nights ago, the lantern.

They'd heard it first — boots on the cliff path above the cave mouth, the particular cadence of men who walked in step because they'd been trained to. Quill's search parties had swept the harbor headlands twice since the bridge closed, methodical as an audit, finding nothing. But this time the boots stopped. This time there was a voice, low and unhurried, giving directions, and the direction it gave was *down*, toward the shelf of black rock below the old pilings where the sea entered the hill.

Sefa had killed the fire with a sweep of wet sacking and put the children in the far chamber behind the curtain of hanging kelp, and they had all stood in the dark listening to the sea and the boots and the creak of a boat being run down the shingle.

The lantern light had come through the submerged passage.

Not much of it. A greenish smear, refracted through six feet of seawater, wavering along the ceiling of the lower chamber like the ghost of a jellyfish. But it was unmistakable — a hand-lantern held underwater or near it, someone on the outside probing the cave mouth, and the light reaching in, reaching *in*, close enough that Ines could see his own hands when he raised them, pale shapes in the dark.

Within a lantern's reach. That was how close they had come. One more degree of angle, one fewer foot of tide, and the light would have found the pool, the shelves, the eleven and the four.

The boat had withdrawn after twenty minutes. The boots had climbed away. Nobody breathed fully again until dawn.

Now, sitting across the dead fire from Sefa, Ines heard himself say what he had been not-saying for three days.

"It wasn't luck."

"I know."

"The company doesn't probe cave mouths on a guess. Quill doesn't spend men and boats on a guess. Someone told him this stretch of coast was worth looking at." He heard his own voice go flat, the counting-room voice, the voice that made lists. "Someone inside."

Sefa set down the hook and the whetstone. Around the chamber, the sleepers shifted in their sailcloth; one of the widows coughed, softly, and was still.

"You've been watching us," she said. Not accusing. Weighing.

"I watch everything. It's how I stayed alive in that place." He rubbed his face. His hands smelled of salt and mussel and the tallow they used for the fire, and underneath it all, faintly, the paper-and-ink smell of the counting room that never washed off. "I watched the rations disappear from my own allotment for six weeks before anyone noticed. I know what watching costs. And I'm telling you, Sefa — the mussels, the water, and now the lantern. Three things don't make a coincidence. They make a ledger."

"Then show me the ledger."

So he did. He laid it out the way Mr. Voss had taught him to lay out accounts, item against item, and hated himself for how well he did it: the stripped mussels, taken center-out by someone in a hurry; the waterline marks, falling faster than eleven mouths and four children could explain; the timing of the search party, which had come not on the spring tides when any sensible searcher would look, but on the neaps, when the passage ran deep and dangerous — the timing of someone who knew the cave was occupied but not exactly where, and wanted to flush them rather than find them.

"And one more thing," Ines said quietly. "Tam."

The name dropped into the dark like a stone into a well.

"He came in over the rocks in the second week," Ines went on. "No pack. No story. He sleeps nearest the entrance chamber. He's the only one who goes out at low water 'to check the pools,' and he comes back with nothing. He eats less than anyone here and gets thinner slower than anyone here." He made himself say the last part plainly, because dressing it up would be a lie. "I've seen hungry boys in the compound, Sefa. Hungry boys get thin fast or they die. Boys who aren't hungry but pretend to be — those are the ones Quill buys."

The fire lay dead between them, its charred heart ticking as it cooled. From the far chamber came the small, ragged breathing of children.

"You could be wrong," Sefa said.

"Yes."

"If you're right, and you say nothing, we all hang together when the bridge opens. If you're wrong, and I turn a starving boy out onto those rocks in winter, then I've done Quill's work for him, and I'd sooner drown." She picked up the hook again, turned it over, set it down. "So we don't act on your ledger yet. We test it."

"How?"

"The way you test anything in a counting house." Her eyes caught the last grey light from the entrance chamber. "You feed it a false figure and see where it turns up."

* * *

The false figure was a date.

Sefa constructed it the way her father had once constructed stories for the customs men — simple, specific, and just interesting enough to be worth selling. At the evening meal, in front of everyone, she announced that the Ferros had found an old skiff hull wedged in a side chamber, half-rotted but patchable, and that if the weather held they might launch from the shingle below the north point within the fortnight. She named the cove. She named a night. She let hope move through the cave like warmth through cold water, and she watched faces while she did it, and she made sure Ines watched too.

Ines watched Tam.

The boy sat with his bowl of broth and did not smile, which was itself unremarkable — Tam never smiled. But Ines noted the way his eyes went to the entrance chamber at the words *north point*, quick as a fish darting at bait, and away again. Noted that he finished his broth that night, all of it, for the first time in a week. Noted that he slept badly, turning, turning, a boy carrying something heavy in the place where other boys carried nothing at all.

Two nights later, on the ebb, Tam went out to "check the pools."

He was gone longer than the pools required. When he came back his trousers were wet above the knee, higher than any pool on that shelf reached, and there was a fresh scrape on his palm he didn't explain, and he would not meet Ines's eyes, which meant nothing by itself because Tam never met anyone's eyes — except that night he met Sefa's, once, briefly, and looked away the way a man looks away from a debt.

On the fourth morning after the false date was spoken aloud, the search party came back.

This time they didn't probe the cave mouth. They went straight to the north point — boats, grapnels, eight men with lanterns and a coil of line, combing the shingle below the cliffs where no skiff existed, where nothing existed but rock and kelp and the bones of an old wreck. Ines and Sefa watched from the high chamber's vent crack, pressed shoulder to shoulder in the cold draught, as Quill himself stood in the bow of the lead boat, hatless, patient, directing his men with two fingers the way a man directs a search he believes will succeed.

They searched the north point all day. They found nothing, because there was nothing to find. But they had come because they'd been told to come, and they had been told within four days of the lie being spoken inside a cave that only twelve living people knew about.

"Twelve," Sefa said that night, when the boats were gone and the fire was rebuilt and the fear in the chamber had settled into something harder and colder than fear. "There are twelve of us. And one of us is Quill's pen."

Nobody spoke. The widows looked at each other. Old Marama's blind hands went still over her shells. The Ferro brothers stared into the fire with the expressions of men doing sums they didn't want to finish.

Ines felt the cave contract around him. This was the thing he had never understood until now, the thing the compound had never taught him because the compound ran on the opposite principle: trust was not a wall you had or didn't have. It was a ration. You measured it out, you accounted for it, and when it ran short, people began to starve — not for food but for the belief that the person beside you wasn't selling your name.

He looked at Tam. The boy sat apart, as always, near the entrance chamber, hunched over his knees. He was watching the fire, and his face in the firelight was just a boy's face, sharp-boned and chapped with salt wind, and Ines thought: *he's fourteen, and someone owns him the way the company owns me, and whatever he's carrying, he didn't pick it out of a shop.*

But he also thought: *the north point. Eight men and a boat, because of what passed through that boy's hands.*

Sefa rose. She didn't raise her voice; she never needed to.

"No one leaves the caves," she said. "No one goes to the pools alone. No word passes the entrance chamber that isn't true, and nothing true passes at all. Until I know whose mouth Quill is reading from, we seal ourselves in." Her gaze crossed the chamber, slow as a sweep of light, and rested nowhere and everywhere. "Whoever it is — I want you to hear me. Whatever they hold over you, whatever they've promised you, it's a lie with a price tag. Come to me before the next tide, and we'll deal with it together. After that—" she let it hang, "—after that, you're not one of us anymore."

The fire ticked. The tide turned below, and the great pool began its long exhale, and somewhere in the dark of the passage the sea muttered against the rocks like a creditor who knows the account is coming due.

Ines did not sleep. He lay in his corner watching the entrance chamber, where Tam lay curled with his back to the room, shoulders shaking in a rhythm too steady to be weeping — the rhythm of a boy doing arithmetic, the same terrible arithmetic Ines had been doing since he was nine years old: *what can be carried, what must be paid, who can be saved.*

Near dawn, Tam rose silently, took the empty water gourd, and slipped toward the entrance chamber and the ebbing tide beyond it.

Ines rose and followed him into the dark.
