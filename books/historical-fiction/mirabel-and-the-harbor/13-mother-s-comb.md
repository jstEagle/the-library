## 13. Mother's Comb

The fever came on the ninth day of the rain, and it came the way fog comes off the harbor — not all at once but everywhere at once, so that no one could say afterward where it had begun.

Ines woke in his corner of the second chamber to the sound of breathing that was not breathing. It was Old Maren first, the grandmother who mended nets by touch alone because her eyes had gone the way of her teeth, and her breath had taken on a whistling catch, a small torn sound at the top of each inhale, like wind through a split reed. By noon it was her daughter Ana. By dusk it was three children from the far pool, and the boy Tam, and the old diver Hoku whose chest had been ruined by thirty years of cold water and who now could not lie flat without drowning in his own air.

They had no medicine. They had rainwater caught in sailcloth funnels, mussels pried from the dark rocks, a sack of flour going gray with damp, and the particular stubbornness of people who had already survived everything the company thought should have finished them. But fever did not care about stubbornness. Fever was patient as Quill. Fever waited.

It was Sefa who said the word, kneeling beside Maren with her palm flat against the old woman's burning forehead, and her voice when she said it was very quiet, the way it went quiet before she dove.

*Quinine.*

Ines knew the word the way he knew the word *indenture* — from the compound, from the ledger room where he copied columns for Mr. Aldous Vane. The Meridian Trading Company stocked quinine the way it stocked rope and lamp oil: as an instrument. Divers who fell sick were dosed just enough to be returned to the water. The tin boxes sat in the dispensary cabinet behind the counting room, each box stamped MERIDIAN — PROPERTY in letters that had been printed, Ines now understood, with the same certainty as the letters stamped across his own life.

"There is none here," Sefa said, though everyone in the chamber already knew it. She said it anyway, because saying a thing aloud was how she made it real, and making it real was how she decided what to do about it. "There is none here, and Maren has maybe four days."

"Five," whispered Maren, without opening her eyes. "I've outlasted worse than a fever. I outlasted your grandfather's cooking."

Someone laughed, a thin broken sound, and someone else began to cry, and Ines stood against the wall in the dark with his hands empty at his sides and felt the old arithmetic begin its work inside him, patient and pitiless: *what can be carried, what must be paid, who can be saved.*

* * *

He had carried few things in his life. That had always been the discipline. His mother had taught him before she died, or perhaps the company had taught him and she had only repeated the lesson with more love in it: a man who owns nothing cannot be robbed; a man attached to nothing cannot be threatened. He had kept himself down to the weight of a pocket. A knife, honed thin. A shell comb, pink as dawn water, that had been his mother's and hers before her, carved from a single piece of conch by hands two generations dead. And the folded papers that owned him, which he carried not because they were precious but because forgetting them would kill him faster than any knife.

Three things. For twenty-two years, three things, and even that had felt like too much some nights, lying awake in the compound barracks listening to the foghorn count out the hours like a creditor.

The knife he needed. The papers — those were a debt, and debts were not keepsakes. But the comb. The comb was the last warm thing. When he held it in the dark and ran his thumb along the teeth, worn smooth by his mother's hair and her mother's before her, he could smell for one moment a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere in the world except inside his skull: tar and bread and salt, and her humming, low, half a song. It weighed almost nothing. It had survived two crossings of the harbor, one fire, and every year of his indenture because he had never once let anyone see it, never once mentioned it, had hidden it in the seam of his mattress the way other men hid knives.

He had told himself, in the caves these past weeks, that he had already given up everything portable that mattered. The bread under the floorboards. The ration allotment. The sleep. The name he'd made for himself among the strikers as a company man, a watcher, a pair of eyes — that reputation, burned away chapter by chapter since the note at low tide. He had believed he was nearly empty.

But he had not touched the comb. He had moved it twice since coming into the caves, from his coat's inner pocket to the false bottom of the dive basket and back again, and both times his hands had shaken slightly, and both times he had told himself it was the cold.

Now he stood in the entrance chamber watching the tide drain out through the submerged throat of the cave, and he took the comb from his pocket, and he understood that the shaking had never once been the cold.

* * *

The smuggler came on the third night of the fever, if you could call him a smuggler, which Sefa refused to.

"He's a vulture," she said. "Vultures serve a purpose. That doesn't make them friends."

His name was Corvo Brine, though no one believed that was his name, and he came through the reef gap in a flat-bottomed lugger with no lights and no papers, the kind of boat that existed in the space between the company's laws and the sea's. He traded with the cave families the way gulls trade with fishermen: opportunistically, without sentiment, taking dried fish and salvaged copper and once, memorably, a wedding ring, and leaving in return flour, lamp oil, news from beyond the bridge, and lies priced accordingly.

Ines found him in the outer passage where the boats sometimes lay up at slack water, a squat man wrapped in an oilskin the color of old kelp, smoking a pipe that smelled like burning rope. He looked at Ines the way he looked at everything — weighing, pricing, finding the flaw.

"The Kavanagh boy," he said. "The invisible one. They say you can walk through the compound like smoke through a keyhole."

"They say a lot of things."

"They do." He drew on the pipe, and the ember lit the wet hollows of his face from below, so that he seemed to be lit from some fire burning underneath the world. "And lately they say something new. They say there's fever in the caves. They say the Meridian's counting room would pay good money to know exactly which families are weak enough to drag out. And they say—" he smiled, showing teeth stained the brown of tea left standing, "—they say the company man has changed sides. Which makes you either a fool or the most valuable thing in this harbor, and in my experience the two are often the same animal."

"I need quinine."

"Mm." Corvo Brine examined the glowing bowl of his pipe as though the answer might be written there. "Quinine. Do you know what quinine costs a man like me? It isn't the coin. Coin is easy; coin is the least of it. It's the risk. The dispensary cabinet sits behind the counting room, behind a lock, behind a clerk who counts the tins the way monks count rosaries. To get quinine I must either buy it at ruinous price from a corrupt source — and corrupt sources are rare this season, the company's got everyone frightened honest — or I must take it, and taking it means a rope, eventually, when the bridge opens and the magistrate comes with his little book." He looked up. "So. What are you offering?"

Ines had come prepared to bargain. He had spent the day cataloguing himself the way he had once catalogued his life: what can be carried, what must be paid. He had labor, though labor meant nothing to a man who worked alone. He had knowledge of the compound, though knowledge was dangerous to sell and Corvo Brine struck him as a man careful enough to know it. He had the dive basket with its false bottom, which was worth perhaps a meal.

And he had, folded in oilcloth against his chest, the comb.

He had told himself all afternoon that he would open negotiations elsewhere. That he would offer the basket first, then the labor, then his word, and only surrender the comb as a last extremity, wrung from him by necessity. That was the plan. That was the sensible sequence, the economical sequence, the sequence a man followed who had learned at nine years old that everything warm gets taken anyway, so best to hold it close until the last possible moment.

But standing in the dripping passage with the tide turning somewhere below them and a fevered child crying faintly back in the deep chambers — a sound like a kitten, thin and endless — Ines reached inside his coat and brought out the oilcloth packet, and unwrapped it, and held the comb up between two fingers where the smuggler could see it.

Corvo Brine went still. Even his pipe stopped moving. And after a long moment he leaned forward, slowly, the way men lean toward pearls, and took the comb, and turned it in the light of the storm lantern hanging from a spar overhead.

"Well," he said softly. "Well, well. Conch. Old carving. Look at the work on this — see the wave pattern cut into the spine? That's islander work, mainland work, older than the company, older than the bridge. This is a bride-price piece, boy. Or near enough." His thumb moved along the teeth, and Ines felt the touch in his own scalp, in his mother's ghost-hair. "Where did a company boy get a thing like this?"

"It was my mother's."

"And hers?"

"Before her."

Corvo Brine grunted. He turned the comb once more, and Ines watched the light run along the pink curve of it, dawn-colored, kitchen-smelling, and something in his chest tore with a small clean sound like the fever-breath in Old Maren's lungs.

"It's not enough," Brine said. "Not alone. Not for enough quinine to break a fever in five people, which is what you're asking for, because I can hear the coughing from here and I've got ears older than your whole village. I'll need the basket too, and whatever's in it, and—" he paused, savoring it, "—and a favor, unnamed, callable within the year. From a man who walks through the compound like smoke."

"No favors," Ines said.

"Then no quinine." Brine shrugged, vast inside the oilskin, and began to turn away. "Fever takes the old ones first. Then the children. You've got time to reconsider before the tide comes back. Not much. But some."

Ines stood in the dripping dark and hated him with a hatred so clean and total it was almost restful, and knew, with the clarity that had been coming to him more and more since the caves, since Sefa, since the note at low tide, that the hatred changed nothing. That it had never changed anything. That he could hate Quill and hate the company and hate this kelp-wrapped vulture until the end of the world, and Old Maren would still die on schedule unless the tin boxes crossed the water tonight.

"Take it," he said. "All of it. The comb, the basket, everything. No favor."

Brine turned back. The smile was different now — smaller, and for the first time not entirely unpleasant, the smile of a man who has won more cleanly than he expected to and finds it slightly embarrassing. "You're sure. It's a fine piece. Things like this don't come back."

"I know," Ines said. "That's why it's enough."

* * *

Sefa met him at the inner pool with a lamp, and saw his face, and knew.

"What did it cost?"

"Quinine," he said. "Enough. Five tins, and the powder besides. It's in the boat; I'll carry it through on the ebb."

"Ines." Her voice was gentle in the way that hurt most, because gentleness from Sefa was rare as quinine and always arrived attached to truth. "What did it cost?"

He looked at the lamp instead of her. The flame stood perfectly still in the dead air of the cave, a small gold tear suspended above the wick, and he thought: *that is what it looked like. That is what it will always look like, whenever I am foolish enough to remember.* Dawn-pink. Kitchen-smelling. Two generations of women's hands, and then a smuggler's thumb.

"My mother's comb."

Sefa said nothing for a long moment. The water lapped at the rim of the pool with a sound like slow applause. Somewhere deeper in, a child coughed, and stopped, and coughed again.

"You could have asked me first," she said finally. "We would have found another way. We could have—"

"There wasn't another way. There was a sequence, and I ran through it too fast, and I'm glad I did." He heard his own voice come out level and was vaguely surprised by it, the way you're surprised by calm water over a wreck. "Do you know what I was doing all afternoon, before I went out there? I was bargaining with myself. I was telling myself: offer the basket, offer the labor, hold the comb back, hold it, hold it, hold it until the last possible moment — because that's the rule, that's the whole law I've lived by since I was nine years old. Keep the warm things. Carry light. Hold the warm things until the sea takes them anyway." He looked at her then. "But the sea doesn't take them, Sefa. That's the lie. The sea doesn't take anything. *I* take them, or I keep them, and keeping them means Maren dies with her boots on and the fever works through the children one by one while I sit in the dark smelling a kitchen that doesn't exist."

Sefa reached out and took his hand — not gently, Sefa did nothing gently, but firmly, the grip of a woman pulling a drowning man, which was how they had begun and how they continued, always, everything between them shaped like that first rescue.

"It was your mother's," she said.

"It was my mother's," he agreed. "And my mother taught me the lesson wrong. Or I learned it wrong. Either way—" He exhaled, long and shaking, grief and relief arriving together in one breath, twins, inseparable. "Either way, it bought five lives tonight. That's better than it ever bought me. It bought me mornings. It bought me remembering. Five lives beats remembering."

"That's a lie," Sefa said quietly. "You'll want the remembering back. Some night when it's cold, and there's nothing to do with your hands, you'll reach for that pocket and it will be empty, and you'll wish you'd let Maren argue with death a little longer, because Maren would have argued, she'd have enjoyed it." Her grip tightened. "But you'll also know — that same night, the same minute — that you'd do it again. And that's the difference between us and them, Ines. Quill keeps everything. He's never given up a single warm thing in his life. Look what it made him."

* * *

The quinine worked the way mercy works: slowly, invisibly, and only afterward could anyone believe it had happened at all. They dosed Maren first, and Hoku, and then the children, bitter powder stirred into rainwater with a strip of dried fish to kill the taste, and by the third morning the whistling tear-sound in Maren's breathing had softened, and by the fifth she was sitting up demanding to know who had been touching her mending, because whoever it was had no feel for knots whatsoever.

Ines slept. Truly slept, for the first time since the fever began, ten hours in his corner with the drip of the cave walls keeping time above him, and dreamed — this was the strange part — not of the comb, not of the kitchen, but of the compound dispensary: the tin boxes ranked behind glass, MERIDIAN — PROPERTY, and his own hand reaching through the glass as easily as smoke through a keyhole, and waking he understood that the dream was not memory but forecast. Sooner or later the caves would need more than one smuggler's errand. Sooner or later he would have to go back through the compound that owned him, past the counting room, past the dispensary, past—

Past Mr. Quill, who was waiting.

He knew it the way animals know weather. It was in the silence of the compound these last weeks, in the too-casual questions of the ration clerk, in the sense of being watched that had followed him since the interview, courteous and surgical, weeks ago. Quill did not strike quickly. Quill waited for the tide to turn, and Ines could feel, in his bones and in the ache of his empty pockets, that the tide was turning now. The informer boy covered. The false channels running. A man does not spend that much patience without presenting the bill.

On the sixth morning, climbing the cliff path to the compound through fog so thick it seemed to be poured rather than fallen, Ines found a slip of paper tucked into the crack of the gatepost, folded once, unsigned, in handwriting he recognized the way you recognize a voice in the dark.

*My office. Eleven o'clock. Come hungry, Kavanagh. I'm told you're good at that.*

Below it, in smaller script, precise as a ledger line:

*I have something you want. And you have something I want. Shall we discuss terms?*

Ines stood in the fog with the paper in his hand and thought of a pink comb traveling east in a lugger's hold, sold onward, onward, to some stranger's drawer in some town he would never see. He thought of tin boxes behind glass. He thought of freedom papers, and what they might be written on.

Eleven o'clock, then. He folded the note into his empty pocket, where it weighed more than the comb ever had, and walked in through the gate.
