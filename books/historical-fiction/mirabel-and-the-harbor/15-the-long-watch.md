## 15. The Long Watch

The compound bell tolled nine, and Ines Kavanagh sat at his desk in the counting room with a blank sheet of company paper before him and a pen that would not move.

He had been given the room to himself. That was the first strange thing. Mr. Quill had done it deliberately, of course — everything Quill did was deliberate the way a snare was deliberate — clearing the clerks out with a flick of two fingers and saying, *Take the evening, Kavanagh. Think it over properly. A man should think in comfort.* Then he had set a lamp on the desk, trimmed its wick with his thumbnail, and left, and the door had closed behind him with the softness of a hand over a mouth.

Think it over properly.

The offer sat on the desk beside the lamp like a second sheet of paper. Inform on the caves. Names, numbers, the location of the seaward entrance if he knew it, which they suspected he did. In exchange: his freedom papers, signed and sealed, before the bridge reopened. Refuse, and when the magistrate came across the causeway in six weeks' time, he would hang beside them — beside *her* — as an enemy of the contract, a thief of company rations, a man who had carried bread under a false floor for a season while the ledgers went hungry.

Six weeks. The spring tide. The same arithmetic that governed Sefa's hope governed his ruin. It had a certain tidiness that Ines thought Quill would appreciate.

He picked up the pen. Put it down. Picked it up again.

Outside, the harbor breathed. He could hear it through the shuttered window — the long exhale of water against the pilings, the creak of the moored company boats riding high and empty because their divers were in the caves eating mussels off the rocks, and the fog coming in off the strait in slow gray tongues that tasted of salt and iron. Rain had begun somewhere out over the water; he could hear it approaching the town like a crowd approaching down a street, a soft millions-of-feet sound, and then it arrived against the roof and shut the world away.

Ines dipped the pen and began to write.

*To Mr. Quill,* he wrote. *Sir —*

He stopped.

It was not that he did not know what such a letter would say. That was the horror of it, the thing he kept discovering about himself tonight and wishing he could undiscover: he knew exactly how to write it. He had spent ten years learning the grammar of survival in this place, and the grammar of survival included sentences like *I regret to inform you* and *I have considered your generous offer* and *the entrance lies below the split rock at the north ebb.* He could write them cleanly. His hand would not even shake. That was what ten years of being useful and unseen had built in him — a man whose hand did not shake when he wrote his own damnation in a fair, legible clerk's script.

He looked at the words *To Mr. Quill, Sir* sitting at the top of the page, small and wet and black, and he understood that the letter was already half-written whether he put another word down or not. Every day he had stayed silent in the weighing house, every shortchanged diver he had watched walk out poorer without a word from him, every ration slip he had signed honestly while stealing beneath the honest signature — all of it was drafting. All of it was ink. Quill's letter had been writing itself since Ines was eleven years old and learned that the boy who made himself small got fed.

He struck through the line. Once, twice, until the paper nearly tore.

*No,* he wrote instead, large, across the middle of the sheet, and then felt foolish, because Quill would never see this page, and then felt worse than foolish, because some part of him had wanted to write it so that someone someday might see it. *No* — one word, enormous, childish, the whole of his argument reduced to a mark a child could make with a burnt stick on a wall.

He set the ruined sheet aside. There was plenty of paper. The company was generous with paper; it was people it rationed.

Rain thickened against the roof. Somewhere down in the barracks a door banged, and men's voices rose and fell — the night watch changing, boots on wet gravel, a laugh cut short by the weather. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a world that intended to go on exactly as it was, tide after tide, ledger after ledger, until the pearls ran out or the divers did. Ines listened to it the way a man listens to the house he grew up in after deciding to leave: hearing every board, every hinge, memorizing nothing because he already knew it all, and knowing that knowing it would not make staying survivable.

He took a fresh sheet.

This one was harder. This one his hand did shake over, though there was no one to see.

*Sefa.*

Two letters. He had written only the name and already the room seemed smaller, warmer, closer — as if the word itself gave off heat. Three years ago she had pulled him out of the green dark with her hands under his arms, and he had been so busy coughing up the sea that he had promised her anything, anything at all, with the extravagant stupidity of a boy who has just learned he is allowed to keep breathing. She had laughed at him. She had said, *Then carry my basket home, that's all I'll ever ask.* And he had carried it, and it had weighed nothing, and somehow in three years the basket had become bread stolen from his own mouth, and medicine bought with his mother's comb, and a lie told well enough to survive an hour but not a week, and now this night, this desk, this pen hovering over her name like a gull over a fish it hasn't the courage to take.

What do you write to someone you have promised everything, when what you have is almost nothing?

*Dear Sefa. If you are reading this, I have chosen wrong.*

He wrote it and stared at it. Chosen wrong. As if choice were a road with signposts. As if there were a version of the next six weeks where both of them walked out clean. Quill's offer had no version like that. The offer was built the way the weighing house scales were built — true only from one side. If he informed, Sefa drowned in the caves or swung from the customs-house beam when the boats brought her out. If he refused, they hung them together, and the company lost nothing but two names off a list, and the pearls kept coming, because there were always more divers, more sons, more children indentured young enough to learn that silence was safety.

Unless there weren't. Unless the list ran out.

He had been turning that thought over for three days now, like a stone in the pocket, feeling its weight change as it wore smooth. Tevake had said it once, drunk on nothing stronger than rainwater and rage, standing in the rain outside the weigh shed: *They can't dive for us, boy. Remember that when you're counting their coins. Every pearl down there comes up on the back of a man who can choose, any morning, to stay down.* Ines had filed the words away then, the way he filed everything — carefully, invisibly, where they couldn't hurt him. Tonight they would not stay filed.

He wrote on.

*I am sorry about the comb. You never saw it but it was my mother's, and I traded it for quinine, and I would trade it again, and I want you to know that I know the difference between those two sentences. I know the difference now between carrying a thing and being carried by it. My mother carried me across the strait when I was four and I don't remember her face, only the weight of her arms, only that I was held. That is what I have been trying to do for you and the others and doing badly. Held is not the same as hidden. I spent ten years learning to be hidden. You are teaching me, too late and exactly in time, to be held.*

He read it back and his ears burned. It was too much. It was not enough. It said everything except the thing, and the thing was: *I will not write Quill's letter. I will stand at the cave mouth at spring tide and hold the passage alone if I must, and if they take me, let this be what remains of me — not the ledgers, not the ration slips, but this.*

But he could not send it anywhere. That was the shape of the night: two letters, neither deliverable. One addressed to a man who already knew everything he needed to know and was merely waiting for Ines to confirm his own smallness on paper. One addressed to a woman hiding in the dark under the harbor who could not read by lantern-light without spending oil she did not have. Paper, Ines thought bitterly, dipping the pen again. The company drowns the world in paper and not a sheet of it goes where a heart needs it.

* * *

Midnight came without ceremony. The compound bell did not toll the hour; the night watch kept its own time, and the rain swallowed what little sound remained. Ines banked the lamp low and sat in the brown half-dark, and the counting room became the whole world, and the whole world became very simple.

On one side of the desk: the compound. Walls, bells, ledgers, the smell of ink and lamp oil and the boiled fish the mess served on Thursdays. Ten years of it. His bunk with its blanket gone thin at the shoulder from turning. The false bottom of his dive basket, empty now, waiting. Mr. Quill's voice saying *a man should think in comfort*, courteous as a scalpel. He knew every inch of this side the way he knew his own hands, and he hated it the way you hate your own hands when they've done what they were trained to do.

On the other side: the caves. Rainwater dripping into tin cans. Mussels pried off the rocks at low tide by children who had never seen a schoolroom. Old Maren's fever broken by the quinine that had cost a mother's face carved in shell. Sefa's fury, which was love wearing armor, telling him *you waited, you waited, you waited* — and underneath the fury, the night she'd fallen asleep mid-sentence against the cave wall and he had sat watching the lantern flame steady her breathing, and understood that he would burn the whole company down for that rhythm of breath and call it a fair trade.

Two worlds, and he was the only bridge still standing between them, and both sides were pulling.

He got up and walked. The counting room was twelve paces end to end; he walked it, and walked it, and the floorboards spoke under him in the old language of the building — settle, shift, endure — and past the shuttered window the storm worked itself up from grumbling to shouting. Wind found the gap in the casement and pushed cold air through, smelling of kelp and open water, smelling of the other side, and Ines stood in it with his eyes closed and let it move through his hair like a hand checking that he was real.

When he came back to the desk, he did something he had not planned.

He turned Quill's ruined sheet over — the one with *No* written huge across it — and on the blank back he began to draft, not letters, but arithmetic. Because that was what the night had come down to, and he might as well be honest about it. Hope downsized to numbers.

*Spring tide:* he wrote. *Night of the 14th. High water at 11:40 by the almanac. Reef gap passable perhaps ninety minutes either side.*

*Quill's men:* eleven on the compound roster. Four needed to hold the wharf and the storehouse even if every diver in the caves escaped. Seven he could bring against the caves — if he believed the date. If the boy told him the date. If the boy told him the date Quill wanted to hear rather than the date that was true.*

There it was. The thread he had been refusing to look at directly for two days, the one with the starving boy on the end of it, the informer with Quill's thumb on his neck, selling scraps of the caves' life to buy his own. Ines had covered for him once already — had fed the leak a falsehood small enough to seem like carelessness — and had lain awake afterward wondering if mercy had just been another word for maneuvering. But the stone turned again in his pocket, and showed a new face: the boy was not a traitor, the boy was a rations ledger with legs, hunger conscripted by fear. And a channel, once found, runs both ways. Whatever flowed toward Quill could be made to flow with stones in it. False tides. False dates. A whole counterfeit river of information, and Quill drinking from it because Quill trusted fear to make liars honest.

If it worked, seven men would spend spring-tide night watching an empty cove in the rain while a borrowed skiff slipped the reef gap on the far side of the point.

If it failed—

He did not finish the sentence. Instead he wrote: *If it fails, they hang us all together, and the last thing we will have shown each other is that none of us died informing.* He looked at that line a long time. It was not comfort. It was not hope, exactly. It was closer to a keel — the heavy fixed thing laid along the bottom of a boat so that the wind cannot simply flip it over. You did not sail on the keel. You sailed because of it.

The lamp burned low. He trimmed it, and the light steadied, and for a moment the flame stood perfectly still in its glass, and Ines watched it and felt the night reach its center — the still point, the turn of the tide, the moment when the water has gone as far as it will go out and hangs balanced, neither ebbing nor flooding, before it commits.

He committed.

He took Quill's sheet — *No* facing up — folded it once, twice, and slid it into the stove's cold ash-box, not burning it yet, but putting it where burning would be easy. Let it wait for a match and a moment. Then he took Sefa's letter, the one that said too much and not enough, and folded it small, and did not put it in the stove. He put it inside his shirt, against his chest, where the paper warmed to his skin. Not because he would ever give it to her. Because a man walking a plank should know what he carries, and he would rather carry her name than Quill's.

Then he sat down one last time, and on a fresh sheet, in his best clerk's hand — the fair, legible, ten-years-trained script that the company prized — he wrote a third document. Not a letter. A list.

*Bread, remaining: four loaves.* *Quinine: exhausted.* *Lamp oil in caves: eight days.* *Skiff: borrowed, at the smelter slip, oars wrapped in sacking.* *Boy: ours now, whether he knows it or not.* *Days until spring tide: nineteen.*

Nineteen days. He read the number until it stopped being a number and became a distance — a stretch of dark water to be crossed one stroke at a time, with Quill's patience on one shore and the magistrate's warrant on the other, and nowhere to land but through.

He signed nothing. He folded the list into his pocket beside nothing else, because he owned nothing else worth carrying, and blew out the lamp.

And in the dark, as his eyes adjusted and the counting room resolved itself into shapes he had known for ten years, Ines heard — faint, beneath the storm, beneath the rain's millions of feet — the bell buoy off the reef gap tolling with the swell. One note. Another. Steady as a pulse, marking the passage he would have to hold open, the water Sefa would have to cross, the exact seam of the harbor where his two worlds met.

He lay on the counting room floor with his coat for a pillow, because going to his bunk meant passing the night watch and their questions, and he was done spending energy on small lies. The floor was hard. The storm shouted. Nineteen days.

He had just begun, near dawn, to drift — finally, treacherously, into the shallow edge of sleep — when the knock came. Soft. Twice. The particular softness of a fist that has learned to knock without being heard.

At this hour, there was only one person it could be.

Ines was on his feet with his heart going like a diver's hammering chest, crossing the dark room, hand already on the bolt — and thinking, with terrible clarity, *either the caves are burning, or Quill knows everything.*
