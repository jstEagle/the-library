## 16. Tevake's Grave

The fever broke its pattern on the ninth night.

Until then it had moved through the caves the way the tide did — rising and falling on schedule, taking a little of everyone, giving most of them back. Old Maru had sweated through it and lived. The twins, Peli and Ano, had burned for three days and come out the other side thin as eels but laughing again by the fourth. Ines had learned to read the fever's rhythm the way he'd once read weather off the water: when the shivering came, pile the blankets; when the heat broke, get water into them before the thirst turned to sickness; when they slept, you slept too, because there was no telling how long the mercy would last.

Tevake had followed the pattern for eight nights. On the ninth, he stopped following anything at all.

Ines was sitting against the cave wall nearest the sick alcove, keeping the watch he'd taken over from Sefa so she could sleep, when he noticed that the old diver's breathing had gone quiet in the wrong way. Not the slow, laboring bellows-sound of the past week, each breath dragged up from somewhere deep as the pearl beds. This was quieter than that. Quieter than it should have been. He leaned forward with the lamp, and the flame bent toward the man as if leaning in to listen, and Ines understood, in the way you understand things in the dark hours — completely and all at once and without any hope of being wrong — that Tevake was no longer breathing at all.

He sat very still for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere deeper in the cave system, where the families were bedded down along the dry shelf above the flood line, someone coughed, and someone murmured comfort, and the ordinary sounds of the living went on without interruption, because no one else knew yet.

Ines looked at Tevake's face. It was calmer than Ines had ever seen it in life — and that was the thing that undid him, more than the stillness, more than the gray color creeping up from the man's neck like fog coming over the harbor wall. Because Ines had watched this man stand in the Weighing House two seasons ago and be cheated out of half a season's diving with nothing but arithmetic and a smile, and Tevake had not been calm then. He had gone rigid as a snapped line, jaw working, hands opening and closing at his sides, and Ines had stood three feet away with his eyes fixed on the floorboards and said nothing, and hated himself for it every day since. That anger had been the truest thing about Tevake. The company had taken his pearls and his boats and finally his name, branding him an enemy of contract, and none of it had managed to take the anger until now.

Now the fever had done what Quill never could. And his face was calm as still water, and it looked wrong on him, like a mask made by someone who had only heard Tevake described.

"Tevake," Ines said softly. There was no reason to say it. He said it anyway. Then he rose, joints stiff from cold stone, and went to wake Sefa.

She was sleeping curled against the far wall with her back to the passage, one arm under her head, and even asleep her face held a kind of readiness, as if she could be moving within two seconds of waking and often needed to be. Ines crouched beside her and touched her shoulder.

"Sefa."

Her eyes opened instantly, fully awake, already searching his face for what he'd come to say. She read it there before he spoke. He watched her understand. He watched her close her eyes again, just briefly — one long breath, drawn in and let out slow — and when she opened them she was already getting up, already the daughter of a diver, already moving toward the alcove with the lamp lifted high.

They stood together over him. Neither spoke for a while. The cave breathed around them — that slow tidal respiration of dripping water and distant surge, the sound the flooded passages made when the sea shifted its weight somewhere beyond the stone.

"He kept asking for the water," Sefa said at last. Her voice was steady, which cost her something; Ines could see what it cost, in the set of her throat. "Last night. He kept saying *put me in the water*, like I wasn't listening properly. Like I was a child who hadn't understood the instruction." A breath. "So that's how we bury him. In the water. At low tide, when we can reach the outer ledge. His people always went back to the water. All of us do, eventually."

"All right," Ines said. "Low tide is—" He worked it out automatically, the arithmetic of tides now stitched into him as thoroughly as the company's ledgers ever were. "Four hours. A little better than four."

"Then we have four hours." She turned to him, and the lamp threw her shadow huge against the wet stone behind her, a giant with a small exhausted face. "Wake the others. Tell them gently. The children first — tell the mothers, let them decide how much the children hear." She paused. "And Ines — no one goes near the east passage tonight. If Quill's informer is watching for movement, grief makes people careless. We bury him quietly or we don't bury him at all."

*If Quill's informer.* The words landed in Ines's chest and stayed there, cold as harbor water. They both knew by now that there was one — that someone inside these walls had been selling the shape of their lives to the enforcer piece by piece, a search party's lantern coming within arm's reach of the cave mouth a fortnight past. They didn't know who. Trust, in the caves, had become something you measured out in spoonfuls, like the quinine, like the rainwater caught in the tarps.

"I'll be careful," he said.

"I know," Sefa said. "That's the trouble with you. You're always careful." But there was no edge in it tonight. Grief had burned the edges off everything, leaving only the plain surfaces underneath.

* * *

They buried Tevake at the turn of the tide.

It took all of them. The passage to the outer ledge ran waist-deep at low water, a drowned throat of rock that the divers' children had swum since they could walk and that Ines had navigated twice now with his heart hammering and the ceiling pressing down like a held breath. They wrapped Tevake in the sailcloth from the ruined skiff — the boat that was supposed to carry them all out before the bridge closed, the boat whose absence had turned six weeks of hiding into a sentence — and weighted the wrapping with stones from the ledge itself, so that he would go down among his own ground. Old Maru insisted on carrying the head end, though his arms shook. Nobody argued with him.

Sefa had spent part of those four hours doing something Ines didn't understand until he saw it: she had gone through her father's few possessions — a broken shell knife, a cord with three worn pearl buttons, a scrap of oilcloth — and chosen one thing. A single pearl, small and misshapen, the kind the Weighing House would dock a diver's pay for bringing up. Baroque, flawed, worthless by the company's arithmetic. She closed her father's hand around it.

"For the road," she said, to no one and everyone. "Let him arrive rich, wherever he's going. Let him arrive with proof he was here."

The ledge opened onto black air and the sound of the sea working the reef in the dark. No storm tonight — the sky had cleared after days of rain, and the stars were out in their brutal multitudes, and the water below the ledge lay flat and shining, holding the stars the way a mirror holds a face. It would have been beautiful if beauty hadn't felt like an intrusion. Ines stood at the edge with the others, eleven of them in a ragged line, and thought: *this is the same water. The same harbor water I've crossed a thousand times on company business, carrying company cargo, invisible.* It didn't look the same anymore. Nothing did.

Maru spoke the old words — half in the divers' language, half in the harbor creole, and Ines understood perhaps a third of it, but the third he understood was enough: the sea gave, the sea took back, the sea keeps what it loves. Then Sefa stepped forward to the lip of the ledge, and two men lifted the sailcloth bundle between them, and for a moment it hung there against the stars, and Ines found he was holding his breath as though the whole world were underwater.

They let him go. The splash was soft. The sea folded over him without ceremony, which seemed right — the sea doesn't make speeches either — and the ripples spread out in widening rings that caught the starlight and broke it apart, and then the water smoothed itself, and Tevake was gone into the keeping of the thing that had fed his family for four generations and killed him in the end by way of a fever caught hiding in the dark from men who called themselves a trading company.

Nobody spoke on the way back through the passage. The water seemed colder going in than coming out, or maybe Ines was just colder now, all the way through, in some place the fever couldn't reach.

* * *

Afterward, when the others had drifted back to their shelves and blankets, Ines found Sefa sitting alone at the boundary where the dry floor met the dark mouth of the lower passage. She wasn't crying. He had learned by now that she rationed her crying the way the caves rationed everything — carefully, privately, at hours when it wouldn't cost the group anything. He sat down beside her, leaving a hand's width of stone between them, and said nothing, because sometimes the only useful thing a person can carry is silence offered freely.

The tide was turning. He could hear it — the faint change in the voice of the flooded galleries below, the sea drawing breath for the flood.

"My father used to say the harbor keeps accounts," Sefa said eventually. Her voice was roughened, sanded down. "Not the company. The harbor itself. Everything you take, everything you're given, everything you owe — it all goes in the book, and the book never closes, and sooner or later you settle up." She turned her head and looked at him, and in the dim reflected light her eyes were very clear and very tired. "You've been settling my account for six weeks, Ines Kavanagh. Bread under your floor. Medicine traded for your mother's comb. Your own rations cut in half while you lie to Quill's face and come back here smelling of fear and lamp oil. I never asked for any of that."

"You asked," he said. "The note asked."

"The note asked for what you promised." She shook her head slowly. "A promise made by a half-drowned boy to the girl who pulled him out of the water isn't a debt, Ines. It's gratitude wearing a coat. I could have named anything that day and you'd have sworn to it — you were too busy not dying to think about what you were agreeing to." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "I named nothing. Remember? I said, *one day I'll ask.* And then when I finally sent the note, I didn't ask you to burn anything down. I asked for bread. For quinine. For a lamp wick and news of whether my father was alive. Small things. Things a careful man could carry."

"And now?" His own voice came out strange to him, scraped thin.

"Now Tevake is in the water," she said. "And the spring tide is twelve days away. And Quill has an informer somewhere inside these walls, and a fake date in his pocket that you fed him, and enough men to fill a cove twice over. And the bridge opens in the spring, and when it opens, the magistrate comes with it, and every person on that enemy list answers for the strike." She drew her knees up and put her arms around them, making herself small in a way he had never seen her do, and hated seeing, and understood completely. "So I'm not going to ask you what you promised. I'm done with that question. It was the wrong question from the beginning."

The water below the ledge whispered. Somewhere in the upper galleries a child cried in its sleep and was hushed.

"The right question," Sefa said, "is who you intend to be. Not what you owe me. Not what the company owns. Not what Quill threatened or what your papers say. Those are all things other people wrote about you." She unfolded herself and turned to face him fully, and took his chin in her hand — a diver's grip, firm, unhesitating, the way she'd gripped him three years ago hauling his drowning body up the swim ladder — and made him look at her. "Who do you intend to be, Ines? The man who survives? Or the man who shows up on the ledger?"

He wanted to answer quickly. He was, by nature and training and six years of indenture, a fast answerer — the compound taught you that, taught you to give the expected word before the question finished forming, because hesitation read as guilt. But she was holding his face and waiting, and something in him refused to do it, refused to hand her the rehearsed thing, and so he sat in the silence with the question, and let it be heavy, and let it work on him the way water works on stone.

He thought about the Weighing House, and Tevake's rigid jaw, and his own eyes on the floorboards. He thought about his indenture papers folded in oilcloth against his chest — the document that said, in effect, that Ines Kavanagh was a quantity of labor belonging to another man's enterprise, portable, countable, weighable. He thought about his mother's comb, gone now to a smuggler in exchange for fever medicine, and how the loss of it had hurt less than he feared and freed him more than he expected. He thought about the boy — the informer, the starving child Quill had by the throat — and how Ines had covered for him instead of exposing him, and how that had felt like the first honest act of his adult life, and how dishonest it had looked from the outside.

"I intend," he said slowly, "to be the man who is seen."

Sefa's hand dropped away. She waited.

"Not safe," he said. "Not clever. Not the useful invisible boy in the corner of the counting room. Every plan I've made since I came here has been a plan to survive — mine, yours, everyone's — and survival is just another way of staying hidden. Quill counts on it. The whole company counts on it. They built the harbor so nobody would ever have to look at anybody, and everybody got very good at not looking back." He heard his own voice steadying, hardening, finding a floor beneath it. "Twelve days. When the spring tide comes, I hold the cave mouth, and your boat gets out, and if Quill comes back early — he will, he's already suspicious — then he finds me standing in the open with a lamp in my hand. Not behind a door. Not under a floor. Standing."

"That might mean your life," Sefa said quietly.

"It might." He looked toward the black mouth of the passage, where the sea breathed. "I spent six years learning what can be carried. Knife, comb, papers. Light things. Portable things. And the whole time the heaviest thing in the world was sitting right on my chest, and it was this — knowing what's right and choosing the lighter load instead." He turned back to her. "I'm putting it down. Whatever it costs."

For a long moment Sefa said nothing. Then she reached out and took his hand — not the diver's grip this time, but something gentler, her fingers lacing through his, warm against the cave-cold — and pressed it once, hard, the way you press a knot to test whether it will hold.

"Then listen," she said, "because if we're done hiding, we do this properly. Twelve days is enough time — barely — but only if the informer problem dies for good and only if the false-tide trick holds. Quill committed men to the wrong cove once. He won't forgive himself, and he won't be fooled the same way twice. Which means the next thing we feed him has to be true enough to taste real." Her eyes had changed — grief still in them, banked now, burning steady instead of wild — and Ines recognized the look, because he'd seen it on her father's face across a crowded Weighing House, the day the strike was voted. It was the look of a person deciding.

"And there's one more thing," she said. "The skipper who was supposed to take us out — Rell, out of the salt yards. His boat was confiscated, but Rell himself is still in town, and he still owes my father a debt older than you are. If anyone can find a skiff the company doesn't know exists, it's him. Someone has to get word to him before spring tide. Someone with a reason to walk the streets without suspicion." She held his gaze. "Someone the company still trusts."

Ines felt the shape of it settle over him — the errand, the risk, the walking-back-into-the-lion's-mouth of it — and found, to his own surprise, that he wasn't afraid. Or rather, that the fear was there but had stopped mattering, the way the weight of the water stops mattering once you commit to the dive and tip forward into it.

"When?" he said.

"Tomorrow night," Sefa said. "There's a market day. Half the compound will be at the stalls." She squeezed his hand once more and let go, and looked past him toward the sea-dark of the passage, toward the grave they'd made of the open water. "Say goodbye to him properly in your own way tonight. Tomorrow, we start building the thing he died waiting for."

Above the ledge, the tide had turned, and the sea was coming in.
