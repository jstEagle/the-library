## 17. Burned Edge

I followed the boy the way I had learned to follow everything in my life — at a distance, without a shadow of my own, letting the fog do half the work and the noise of the harbor the rest.

His name, I would learn later, was Pilo. He was perhaps thirteen, maybe fourteen, though hunger had a way of erasing years from a face the way water erased letters from paper. I had watched him for three nights now, ever since the search party came within a lantern's reach of the cave mouth and Sefa's eyes had gone flat as slate when she said, *Someone is selling us.*

Three nights, and each night Pilo did the same thing. He slipped out of the caves at slack water, swam the short channel to the shingle beach below the company's coal shed, wrung out his shirt with hands that shook — from cold, I thought at first, only cold — and waited by the breakwater until a man came.

The man was not Quill. It was one of Quill's watchers, a soft-bellied clerk named Hobb whose job was to count smoke from chimneys and note who bought what at which stall. Hobb would appear out of the fog like something the tide refused, hand over a twist of paper or a heel of bread, and Pilo would take it with both hands, the way you take communion, the way you take charity when charity is also a hook.

Then Pilo would swim back, and I would lie among the rocks with the cold coming up through my chest into my teeth, and I would think about what to do with what I knew.

* * *

The trouble was that I already understood him. That was the whole trouble.

You cannot hate a thing you recognize. I had been useful and unseen for eleven years, since my father drowned and the Meridian Trading Company folded his debt onto my shoulders like a coat I would never grow out of. I knew exactly how it worked — how they never had to threaten you outright, how they simply arranged the world so that your survival ran through their fingers, and then they waited to see what you would do when the fingers began to close.

Quill had done this before. I knew his methods the way a diver knows currents. He did not buy informers with coin; he bought them with need, and he made sure the need was bottomless. Somewhere there was a ledger page with Pilo's family on it — a mother's debt, a sister's ration chit, some small arithmetic of ruin that Quill held in his long white hands and turned over whenever he wanted a page read aloud.

And the pages Pilo read were about us. About the caves. About how many mouths, how much rainwater left in the barrels, whether the fever had burned through the low chamber yet, whether Tevake still lived.

Tevake was dead now. We had buried him at low tide two mornings past, wrapped in sailcloth because there was nothing else, sunk in the green dark beyond the second reef where the company's divers would find him someday and know, and say nothing. Sefa had stood in the shallows afterward with water to her waist and asked me the question I still carried like a stone: not *what did you promise me*, but *who do you intend to be*.

I intended, I thought, watching Pilo shiver on the breakwater while Hobb counted something into his palm, to be someone who did not feed children to machines. Even if the child was the leak in our hull.

* * *

The third night, I did not stay among the rocks. I crossed the channel behind him, silent, and came up out of the water on the shingle ten feet away, and said his name.

He spun, and what was in his face was not guilt. It was the pure animal terror of a creature caught between two sets of teeth. He looked past me toward the compound lights, then back at me, calculating — and I saw him decide that I could be run from, that I might not chase, that drowning boys learn early which risks are survivable.

"Don't," I said quietly. "If you run, Hobb hears the splash. If Hobb hears the splash, Hobb writes it down. And if Hobb writes it down, Mr. Quill asks why a cave boy was meeting his watcher at midnight, and then you are not useful anymore. You understand? Useful is the only thing keeping you alive right now. Don't spend it running from me."

He stopped. The fog moved around us both. Somewhere out in the harbor, a buoy bell clanked its idiot rhythm, counting waves no one else would ever tally.

"You're Kavanagh," he said at last. His voice cracked in the middle like green wood. "The indentured one. She — Sefa pulled you out. Everyone knows that story."

"Everyone knows stories," I said. "Nobody knows what happened after."

"I didn't want to—" He swallowed. His hands were still shaking, and now I could see it wasn't only cold. There was a rawness at his knuckles, the kind you get from working rope or scrubbing decks, and beneath the rags of his shirt his ribs stood out like the frames of an unfinished boat. "They have my mam's chit. She owes them nine months' ration debt from when the diving failed two seasons back. Nine months, Kavanagh. If she can't work it off, they put her in the company house. You know what the company house is."

I knew. Every child in the harbor knew. It was where the company kept people who owed more than they could dive, and it was called a house the way a trap is called a home.

"And Quill said if I told him what was in the caves, he'd tear the chit up." Pilo's voice dropped to almost nothing. "He tore half of it up in front of me. With scissors. Slow. He said the other half came due every time I brought him something true."

"What have you given him?"

"Numbers. How many of us. That the fever came." His chin trembled. "That Tevake was dying. I didn't know he'd die. I didn't—"

"He died of the fever," I said. "Not of you. But Quill will use what you gave him either way, and next time what you give him might sink all of us. Do you understand the difference?"

He nodded, or something in him nodded; his whole body did, a small shuddering agreement.

"So here is what happens now." I crouched down so we were eye to eye, the way you speak to a frightened animal, the way Sefa once spoke to me on the deck of a rescue boat with her hands still burning from the rope burn of pulling me aboard. "You go back inside. You tell no one. Not Sefa, not anyone — because if they know, they'll look at you sideways forever, and a person who is looked at sideways makes mistakes, and mistakes get us killed. From tonight, everything you carry to Hobb comes from me. Nothing else. Nothing true unless I make it true."

"Why?" he whispered. "Why would you—"

"Because someone covered for me once," I said, "when covering for me cost them something. And because Quill thinks he owns a boy inside our walls, and a thing a man thinks he owns is the easiest thing in the world to turn against him."

* * *

I went back through the flooded passage with the tide lifting me like a slow green hand, and I lay awake in the low chamber listening to the drip of water and the breathing of thirty-odd people who trusted me, and I turned the thing over and over the way you turn a shell to see if the light comes through.

Mercy, I thought. But mercy that was also strategy, and strategy that was also mercy, and no way to say anymore where one ended and the other began. Maybe they had always been the same animal. Maybe justice didn't live in magistrates and ledgers and contract law at all. Maybe it lived in the small illegal country of people deciding, night after night, not to hand each other over.

Sefa found me at dawn, sitting at the cave mouth with my knees drawn up, watching the first gray light come down through the water like something poured.

"You didn't sleep," she said. Not a question. She lowered herself beside me, and for a while neither of us spoke. The tide was turning; you could feel the change in the water's mood even before it moved.

"There's a leak," I said finally. "I've found it. It's handled."

Her head came around slowly. In the half-light her eyes were the color of deep water over sand — you couldn't see the bottom, but you knew there was one. "Handled," she repeated. "How?"

"Trust me."

"That's a large request, Ines Kavanagh. Last man who asked me to trust him was a skipper with a boat that never came."

"This isn't a boat," I said. "It's better. A boat only goes one direction." I told her — not the boy's name, never the name, but the shape of it. The chit. The scissors. The nine months of debt wearing a child's face. I watched her jaw tighten and loosen, tighten and loosen, grief and fury taking turns at the wheel.

"We should put him out," she said at last. "Turn him loose on the mainland side. It's kinder than what Quill will do when he's used up."

"It's also a body washing up somewhere with our secrets in his pockets. No." I looked at her. "We use him. Quill built himself a window into this place. Fine. Windows work both directions, Sefa. From tonight, everything he sees through that glass is what we choose to show him."

She was quiet a long moment. Out in the harbor, dawn was doing its slow work on the fog, turning it from gray to pearl, and somewhere above us — muffled, ordinary, obscene — the compound bell rang the hour.

"You've changed," she said. It wasn't a compliment and it wasn't an accusation. It sounded almost like wonder, which from Sefa was both.

"No," I said. "I've just stopped pretending the old way worked."

* * *

The false information had to be built like a pearl — layer on layer, each one thin and true enough to be believed.

That was the craft of it, I discovered. Quill would smell a lie that came gift-wrapped. So we fed Pilo things that were almost true. We let him see the stores being shifted — actually shifted, sacks of dried mussel meat and rain-barrels rolled to the far chamber, so that any physical check would confirm it — while the story attached to the shifting was ours. We let him count heads that included shadows, families who doubled back through the connecting passage to be counted twice. We gave him a sickness that had already passed, described with such precision that Hobb's reports must have read like scripture: the low chamber, the fever, the weakness, the *desperation*.

Desperation was the load-bearing word. Everything we built rested on it, because desperation was what Quill expected to find and therefore what he would not question. A hunted man believes what flatters his hunt. Give a wolf tracks going east, and he does not wonder why the tracks smell faintly of lamp oil.

Pilo learned fast. That was the hardest part to watch and the strangest to respect — the way a starving boy, handed a purpose instead of a leash, straightened by degrees like a plant turned toward light. He learned to report numbers without his voice shaking. He learned to add small false details of his own invention, misdirections I hadn't taught him, and when he showed me one — a fabricated quarrel between two families, planted to explain why stores had moved — I felt something turn over in my chest that took me a moment to name.

It was pride. God help me, it was pride.

"You're good at this," I told him one night on the shingle, fog beading in his hair.

"My mam says nobody from the flats is good at anything except surviving." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse.

"Your mam's wrong," I said. "Surviving is just the apprenticeship. What you do after — that's the trade." I hesitated, then reached into my shirt and pressed something into his hand. Bread, real wheat bread from my own ration, hoarded across three days of my own hollow stomach. "Eat. All of it. An instrument that's starving plays false notes."

He ate it in the dark, too fast, and cried a little while he did, silently, the way harbor children cry — without sound, without permission, without expecting anyone to notice. I looked out at the black water and pretended not to see, because dignity was the last ration we hadn't stolen from him, and I meant to keep it intact.

* * *

The test came sooner than I wanted.

Six nights into the new arrangement, Quill moved. We heard it from Pilo, breathless, arriving at the cave mouth an hour ahead of schedule with the news still wet on him: Quill had committed men to sweep the eastern bluffs — the wrong end of the harbor entirely — based on a report that the cave community was preparing to abandon the flooded chambers and shelter in the old lime kilns above the cliff road.

Which was a story we had planted four days earlier. Through Pilo. Which meant it had gone up the chain — Hobb, the day book, Quill's long white hands turning the pages — and come back down as boots and lanterns and a search party marching east into the fog after a ghost we had built for them out of a hungry boy's borrowed words.

It had *worked*. The machinery had taken our fuel and burned it and asked for more.

I sat with Sefa in the low chamber while the news settled over the cave community like warm silt — relief, then something more dangerous, laughter, actual laughter, old Marta laughing until she had to hold her ribs, and someone starting a song, badly, and others joining. Sefa did not laugh. She watched me across the fire-lamp with that deep-water gaze, and after a while she came and sat close, shoulder to shoulder, the way divers sit when the sea has earned their silence.

"You understand what you've started," she said quietly, under the singing.

"A lie?"

"A war," she said. "A small one. Fought with truth bent instead of iron swung. Quill doesn't forgive being made a fool, Ines. When he finds out — and he'll find out, they always find out — every bit of it lands on that boy first. Then on you. Then on us." Her voice was steady, but her hand, resting on the stone between us, had closed into a fist. "Are you ready for what that costs?"

I thought of my indenture papers folded in their oilskin, sleeping against my chest like a second heartbeat. I thought of the comb I'd traded, of Tevake sinking into the green, of a boy crying without sound over bread.

"No," I said honestly. "But I'm done paying Quill's prices instead of mine."

Before she could answer, Pilo came down the passage fast — too fast, stumbling on the wet rock, and the look on his face killed the singing in the far chamber the way a snuffed wick kills a flame.

"They're back," he said. "Quill's men — they turned around. Halfway to the kilns, they turned around, all of them, and came back to the compound. And Kavanagh—" His voice broke clean through. "Hobb sent word up. Quill wants you. Tomorrow morning. The weighing house. He said—" Pilo swallowed. "He said to tell you he's *counted the flour*."

The fire-lamp hissed. Somewhere above us, through forty feet of rock and black water, the compound bell rang the hour, patient as a debt.

Quill had counted the flour. Which meant somewhere in the arithmetic of missing rations, my small thefts had finally summed to a number he could not ignore — or worse, a number he had ignored on purpose, week after week, the way a fisherman ignores the line until the fish is tired.

He hadn't been hunting the caves at all, I realized, cold spreading through me like the tide coming in.

He had been hunting me.

* * *

That night I did not sleep. I sat at the cave mouth and watched the spring-tide moon build itself out of the sea, huge and white and indifferent, six days from full — six days from the only window Sefa and her people would ever get — and I turned the boy's question over in my hands like a shell held to the light.

Who do you intend to be.

Tomorrow, in the weighing house, under those courteous surgical eyes, I would have to be a liar again — the best liar of my life, because Quill had spent weeks listening to my lies and I had spent those same weeks teaching him, without knowing it, to trust the wrong ones. One slip and everything burned: the boy, the channel, the false tide, all of it.

I touched the oilskin packet over my heart, and for the first time it did not feel like a tether.

It felt like kindling.
