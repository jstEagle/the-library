## 18. False Tide

The boy's name was Pell, and he weighed almost nothing when Ines pressed him against the seawall in the blue hour before dawn, close enough to count the sores at the corners of his mouth and smell the brackish cave-water still dried in the creases of his neck.

"You'll do exactly what I tell you," Ines said, "and nothing else. Not one word more than I give you. Can you hold that?"

Pell nodded. His eyes were enormous in his starved face — a diver's son's eyes, used to looking up through water at light that might be sky or might only be surface. He had been selling scraps of truth to Quill for bread, and the shame of it had carved hollows under his cheekbones deeper than hunger had. When Sefa had proposed what they now meant to do — *use him, use the leak, feed the snake until it chokes* — Ines had expected her to flinch from it. She hadn't. She had looked at the boy the way her father must have looked at a stubborn oyster: something hard to open, but worth opening.

"Say it back to me," Ines said.

"The boats go Thursday." Pell's voice was a whisper with salt in it. "The north cove, under the old rendering shed. Before first light. The skipper's name is Voss."

"And who told you?"

"A girl in the caves. Sefa's cousin." The lie came smooth as oiled rope. They had built it together over three nights, Ines and Sefa, sanding every edge of it down, testing it for the places a lie catches light. A lie about a boat needed a name, a place, a tide. Quill would check the tide tables. That was the whole art of it — build your falsehood on true bones, and let the true bones carry it.

Ines gripped the boy's shoulder once, felt the knob of bone beneath the cloth, and let him go into the dark toward the compound gate where Quill's man took the morning reports.

Then Ines stood alone against the seawall and discovered that his hands were shaking — not from fear, or not only from fear. There was something underneath it, something that frightened him more than Quill ever could. It felt like the moment on a dive when you stop fighting the current and let it take you, trusting the reef to break its own path around you. For eleven years he had fought every current by never entering the water at all. Now he was steering one.

*The hunted becoming the architects*, he thought, and the phrase arrived whole, like a pearl already round in his palm.

* * *

Quill kept an office above the weighing house, and its window looked out on everything: the compound yard, the drying racks, the long gray spine of the bridge closed for the season like a drawbridge raised against the world. Ines passed beneath that window twice a day for years and had learned to walk in the blind spot of it — the narrow seam where the eaves' shadow met the wall. Invisible. Useful. Unseen.

Now he walked straight down the center of the yard in full light, because a man feeding false information to the company's enforcer could not afford to look like a man sneaking anywhere. He carried his dive basket with its innocent false bottom, empty this time, and he knocked on Quill's door before Quill could send for him.

That was the audacity of it. That was the part that made his heart slam against his ribs like a fish in a bucket. You did not come to Mr. Quill. Mr. Quill came to you, courteous and surgical, and took apart your week joint by joint. Coming to him uninvited was a move no invisible man would make — which was precisely why Ines made it.

"Kavanagh." Quill sat behind a desk too large for the room, hands folded, spectacles catching the harbor light. He was a thin man made of angles and patience, the kind of man who could wait out a tide. "This is unusual."

"I have something to trade," Ines said. "For my freedom papers."

He watched the words land. Quill didn't move — Quill never moved first — but something behind the spectacles sharpened, the way a hook sharpens on stone.

"You refused a generous offer three weeks ago."

"I've had time to think." Ines let his gaze drop, just enough, a performed weariness. "I've been thinking about Tevake. About how it ends for all of us if the bridge opens with things unresolved. I don't want to hang beside people I—" he caught himself, rerouted, "*—worked beside.*"

It was dangerous, that stumble. He'd rehearsed it a hundred times and still it came out wrong, and for one terrible second he saw Quill's stillness change texture, the way water changes texture before a shark comes through. Then Quill unfolded his hands.

"What do you know?"

"Thursday," Ines said. "Before first light. North cove, under the old rendering shed. There's a skipper named Voss coming in past the point — he ran rum last season, he owes someone in the caves a debt. They've been hoarding oil for torches. Buying silence from the boy who sweeps the counting room floor." He laid it out flat and unhurried, each piece placed like a stone in a causeway. All of it true except the day, the place, and the boat. "You taught me that information has a price, Mr. Quill. This is mine."

Quill was quiet long enough that Ines heard the harbor itself — the knock of hulls, a gull arguing with the wind, the deep breath of water filling the caves below the town.

"You understand," Quill said at last, mildly, terribly, "that if this is bait, I will not simply hang you. I will take the caves apart stone by stone first, and everyone inside them, and then I will hang you last, so you can hear it being done."

"I know," Ines said. And because the best lies are mostly confession, he added, quietly: "I've thought about that too. Every night since I decided to come here. But I've also thought about my papers, and about what happens to a man who spends his whole life being useful to a company that would burn him for warmth without noticing the smell." He met Quill's eyes. "You want them out of the caves. So do I. We only differ on what happens after they're out."

Something flickered across Quill's face that was almost respect — the recognition hunters have for prey that turns. "Thursday," he repeated. "North cove."

"Voss won't wait past slack tide. If you want them, you'll need your men in position by four."

"They will be." Quill rose, and Ines understood the audience was over. At the door, Quill's voice followed him, soft as a knife being sheathed: "Kavanagh. Men who sell their friends to me always come back to sell me to someone else. I'll remember which hand held the knife."

Ines went out through the light of the yard, legs humming, lungs burning as though he'd swum the passage twice. He did not exhale fully until he reached the seawall. Then he leaned on the cold stone and laughed — a small, cracked sound, nothing like joy, entirely like relief — because he had just handed the most patient man in the harbor a perfect lie wrapped in a true skin, and the man had swallowed it whole, and Ines was still breathing.

* * *

He swam out at the next ebb, through the drowned throat of the passage where the rock pressed close and the cold found every seam of him, and surfaced in the candlelit dark of the main cavern where the exiles lived among the drip and echo. Sefa was waiting at the shelf edge, as she always was, arms crossed, hair bound back with a strip of sailcloth. She read his face before his feet touched stone.

"He took it."

"He took all of it." Ines climbed out, teeth chattering, grinning like a madman. "Thursday. Four o'clock. Every man he can pull off the walls goes to the north cove to catch a boat called *Voss* that doesn't exist, crewed by a smuggler who is probably asleep in a tavern two hundred miles south."

Sefa didn't smile. She crossed to the far wall where the escape plan lived — a charcoal map of the harbor drawn on slate, tides marked in her dead father's careful hand, the real route inked along the southern channel through the reef gap. Six nights from now. Spring tide. Storm-swollen water that no company patrol would dare, but that a diver's daughter could read the way other people read scripture.

"Six nights," she said. "And we spend them knowing he'll figure it out the moment the north cove stays empty past dawn. One night's grace, Ines. Maybe two."

"Two nights is a lifetime in that office." Ines wrung out his shirt. "You should have seen him. He thanked me with his eyes. He thinks I've broken." He paused, and the grin faded into something steadier. "He said he'd remember which hand held the knife."

"Was he wrong?"

Ines looked at her. Candlelight moved on the wet stone behind her, on the huddled shapes of the divers' families, on the little mounds of shell and rainwater jars — a whole village living inside the harbor's ribs, waiting for a sea that would open for one night and close again forever.

"No," he said. "But he thinks the knife is pointed at the caves. Let him hold it that way right up until the blade turns."

Sefa studied him a long moment, and then — rare as spring tide itself — she smiled, quick and fierce, gone as soon as it came. "My father would have liked you," she said. "He'd have hated half of what you're doing. But he'd have liked you."

From somewhere deeper in the caverns, Pell's voice echoed faintly — he was back among them now, fed and forgiven and teaching the smaller children a diving song, his betrayal laundered into usefulness. The informer turned instrument. Mercy as strategy. Ines was beginning to understand that everything in the harbor worked this way: pearls, tides, lies, love. Everything valuable was formed around an irritation, layer by patient layer, until it shone.

* * *

The six days that followed were the longest of Ines's life, and he spent them performing normalcy like a play he'd rehearsed in his sleep. He delivered rations. He signed the tally sheets. He walked beneath Quill's window in the blind spot and then, deliberately, out of it. Twice more he was summoned to the weighing house, and twice more he gave up pieces of the plan — true fragments soldered to false ones: the oil stores, the signal lamp, a child's blanket counted as cargo. Each offering made the Thursday story fatter, more believable, more expensive to disbelieve. Quill wrote everything in his ledger with a pen that scratched like a crab on sand, and Ines learned to watch the pen instead of the man, because the pen hesitated less.

On the fifth day, Ines noticed the extra boots.

They were stacked outside the barracks in the gray morning — a dozen pairs, freshly greased, and beside them the long canvas wraps of the company's breach-loading guns, which had not left their rack since the strike began. His stomach dropped through the floor of him. *Four o'clock tomorrow,* he reminded himself. *The north cove.* They were preparing for the wrong place, the wrong hour, the wrong fight. It was working. It was actually working, and the knowledge should have tasted like triumph; instead it tasted like copper, because among those boots he recognized a pair he'd helped grease himself in another life, belonging to a guard named Ostrick who once slipped him an extra biscuit and asked nothing for it.

Tomorrow those men would stand in the rain at the north cove watching empty water, while a skiff slipped south through the reef gap carrying everyone Ines had come to love. And the day after, Quill would come for him with the ledger in one hand and something heavier in the other.

He stood there long enough that a passing tally clerk gave him a strange look, and Ines smoothed his face into nothing, into the useful blankness he'd worn for eleven years, and walked on. But that night, lying in his cot listening to the storm build over the harbor, he understood that the shaking had come back into his hands and would not leave. Not fear, exactly. Something larger. The feeling of a man who has stepped off the known reef into open channel, and feels the whole ocean deciding, beneath him, which way to turn.

One night of grace. One borrowed skiff. One gap in the reef wide enough for hope and narrow enough for ruin.

And somewhere across the compound, in a lit window above the weighing house, Mr. Quill was checking the tide tables for the north cove — and finding, perhaps, at last, the small discrepancy that patient men always find.

The storm arrived early.
