## 19. Spring Tide

The sea had been rehearsing for three days.

Ines felt it first in the floor of the counting room, a tremor too small to be anything but the ocean turning over in its sleep, and then in his teeth, and then in the way the gulls abandoned the harbor entirely on the second morning, wheeling out over the headland like a handful of thrown paper. By the third day the barometer in Mr. Halloran's office had fallen so far that the clerk tapped it twice with a fingernail, as if it were lying, and Quill stood at the window a long time with his hands clasped behind him, watching the water go the color of gunmetal.

"Spring tide," Quill said, not to anyone in particular.

"Yes, sir," said Halloran.

"And a storm behind it." Quill's reflection smiled faintly in the glass. "A generous night. Everything moving at once."

Ines kept his eyes on the ledger in front of him and wrote a number he would later have no memory of writing. *Everything moving at once.* The words went through him like a fishhook finding flesh beneath scales. He had spent six weeks learning to read this man's silences the way Sefa could read the sea, and what he heard now was not suspicion. It was patience reaching its natural end.

That evening, when the lamps were lit and the rain began in earnest — not the polite rain of the season but the great slanting sheets that came off the open water sideways — Ines carried his dive basket down through the compound as he had done a hundred times before, and no one looked at him twice. That was the whole of his life's work, those six weeks and all the years before them: to be a man no one looked at twice. He had been useful and unseen since he was nine years old, when the company took his father's debt and made it his own. He had survived by weighing less than the air around him.

Tonight the basket held rope, a signal lamp, oil, and the last of the bread. Tonight he intended to be seen by everyone.

* * *

The caves breathed with the tide.

He had learned their rhythm across six weeks of visits — the long inhale at ebb when the lower passage drained and you could walk upright through the throat of black rock, the exhale at flood when the sea came back and turned the tunnels into organs, booming and sighing in registers that lived in the chest rather than the ear. But tonight was spring tide, and spring tide drowned all the old arithmetic. The passage he needed would be open for perhaps forty minutes, and the sea would take it back without negotiation.

He reached the entrance as the light failed completely. Rain hammered the shingle; the swell came in long, rolling black shoulders that broke against the rocks with a sound like doors slamming in an empty house. Somewhere beyond the reef gap — invisible now, marked only in his memory by two white scars of surf — the borrowed skiff waited with old Maren at her tiller, riding out the storm on a mooring Tevake's people had set years ago for nights exactly like this one, though they had never imagined this use.

Inside, the caves were full.

Not just Sefa's small fire-circle anymore. Word had moved through the harbor town the way water moves through sand, and now there were eleven of them: divers' wives, two old men who hadn't dived in a decade, the boy Piri with his bruised face and his guilt he still couldn't meet anyone's eyes over, a baby who slept through thunder as if she'd already decided the world was not worth waking for. They sat among the mussels racks and the rainwater jars, and when Ines came up out of the flooded passage, dripping, hauling the basket, they rose the way a congregation rises.

Sefa pushed through them to him. She was thinner than the sea had any right to leave her, all wrist and collarbone, but her eyes were the same eyes that had found him drowning three years ago in the gray churn off the pearl beds — eyes that did not ask whether you were worth saving, only how.

"The lamp?" she said.

"Full. The wick's doubled." He lifted it out. "Maren will show a light at the gap when she comes in. One flash, then dark, then two. If we see anything else—"

"We won't mistake it." She took the lamp from him, and for a moment her hand closed over his, wet and strong. "You're coming with us."

It was not a question, which meant she had decided, which meant he would have to break it to her here, in front of everyone, or not at all.

"One seat," he said quietly. "Maybe two if the sea is kind, and the sea is not kind tonight. Eleven people, Sefa. The skiff holds six, seven in calm water. Someone has to stay and hold the cave mouth while the rest go, and make sure Quill's men are watching the wrong cove until the last possible minute. If they come early—" He stopped. Her face had already understood everything; he watched it understand. "If they come early, someone has to be standing where they'll look first."

"No."

"Sefa."

"*No.*" It cracked out of her like a whip over water, and the baby woke and began to cry, and Sefa lowered her voice to something worse than shouting, something level and terrible. "You promised me anything I ever asked. I am asking. Get in the boat."

"You're asking me to leave your mother and Piri and old Rano to hold this rock alone if the search party turns around. You know what Quill does to people he finds in these caves. You've seen what he does." He kept his voice low, steady, the voice of the counting room, and hated himself for using it. "I'm the one he watches. I'm the one whose absence he'll forgive longest, because he thinks he owns me. Every minute I stand at that entrance is a minute bought for the boat."

She stared at him. Around them the cave boomed and exhaled, and the firelight moved on eleven faces, and none of them spoke, because they all knew the arithmetic too. Hope downsized to numbers — that had been the whole of their six weeks. Six weeks of rainwater and mussels and counting heads against seats.

"If you die in there," Sefa said finally, "I will dive down to wherever they put you and drag you back up myself, and then I will kill you properly."

"That's fair," Ines said.

* * *

They went in shifts.

The passage was open — barely. The water stood waist-high where it should have been ankle-deep, and the current pulled toward the sea with a hand like a drowning man's grip. Ines went first with the rope, anchoring it inside the mouth, and then they came through one at a time: Rano, ancient and cursing magnificently; Sefa's mother with the baby strapped to her chest in oilcloth; the two old men; the others, six in all, gasping and streaming in the rain on the shingle outside, blinking at the storm as if it were a country they'd been exiled from.

Seven. The skiff would carry seven, badly, in a sea like this, if Maren was the sailor the divers swore she'd been before the company took her husband's boat.

Three remained inside: Sefa, Piri, and Ines. Sefa refused to go before the others were away. Piri refused to go anywhere near the front of the cave, and Ines did not press him; the boy's betrayal had been purchased with hunger and his mother's safety, and he had paid it back these last days by carrying false word to Quill's men about a breakout at the north cove, tomorrow's tide, a dozen strikers with a stolen longboat. Quill had committed eight men and a lantern patrol to the north shore. Eight men, on the wrong side of the harbor, believing a lie fed to them by a starving child.

For now.

Ines stood at the cave mouth with the signal lamp unlit at his feet and watched the sea perform its fury. The reef gap was a rumor of paler black in the general blackness, two hundred yards out, and every few minutes the swell rose up through it and collapsed in a smothered roar, and spray came down on the shingle like thrown gravel. He had never seen the water so large. It seemed less like weather than like a change of mind in the world itself — the ocean deciding, after all these patient centuries, to come ashore and collect.

Behind him, deep in the rock, the tide was climbing. He could hear it in the lower galleries, a rising whisper, the sea taking back the rooms it had lent.

*One flash, then dark, then two.*

* * *

The waiting broke him open in strange ways.

He thought of his mother's comb, traded weeks ago for quinine, and found he could not remember its weight anymore, only the click of its teeth against his thumbnail when he was small and nervous. He thought of the indenture papers folded in his shirt, dry against his skin, that thin legal skin the company had grown over his whole life. He thought of the Weighing House, of Tevake's face when the scale tipped wrong and nobody said anything, of himself saying nothing, of the long education in silence that had brought him to this rock in the rain with a lamp and a rope and nothing else to his name.

He had spent his whole life being portable. Knife, comb, papers. A man who could be picked up and moved like furniture.

Standing at the mouth of the sea's own house, watching the storm, Ines understood suddenly and completely that he was done being portable. Whatever happened in the next hour, he would not fit in a basket again.

A light flickered far out past the gap.

His heart slammed once, hard, and then discipline caught it. He counted. The light came again — one flash, long, then darkness, then two quick sparks like struck flints.

*Maren.*

He lit the lamp with hands that shook only a little, shielded it with his body, and swung it twice in answer. Out in the chaos, almost immediately, a shape resolved: the skiff coming in through the gap on a following sea, running before the swell with her sail half-furled, Maren at the tiller leaning back against the storm like a woman arguing with God and winning on points.

"Go!" Ines turned and shouted into the cave. "Now! Rope's anchored!"

Sefa came out of the dark with the last of the bundles lashed to her back, and Piri behind her, white-faced, and they fought their way along the rope through the flooded passage as the water climbed past their waists, past their chests, the sea roaring in behind them like applause. Ines hauled them both up onto the shingle. The skiff surged in close, Maren screaming something the wind ate alive, hands reaching, and Sefa turned at the waterline and grabbed the front of his shirt.

"Come *now*. There's room. There's always room, we'll make room—"

"I have to keep the light burning until they're clear of the gap," he said. "And I have to be here when Quill realizes the north cove is empty. If I'm gone, he tears the harbor apart looking, and he'll find Maren's mooring, and he'll find the trail back to everyone who helped. I'm the story he already believes. Let me finish telling it."

Her grip tightened until the seams strained. Rain ran down her face; he couldn't tell it from anything else. "You said you'd stop choosing to disappear."

"I'm not disappearing." He covered her hand with his own. "I'm standing in the most visible place on this coast. That's different. That's the whole point."

For one moment longer she held him, and the storm held all of them, and then she kissed him once, hard, salt and fury, and let go, and waded out, and hands pulled her over the gunwale, and the skiff swung away into the dark with seven lives aboard and the reef gap swallowing it.

Ines stood alone on the shingle and raised the lamp high, and burned its light steady over the water until the last pale suggestion of the sail vanished into the rain, and then a little longer, because letting go was a thing he intended to practice.

* * *

He was still holding it when the lanterns came.

Two of them, bobbing along the cliff path from the compound — not eight men bound for the north cove, but a pair, moving fast, cutting straight for the caves. His stomach dropped through the rock. *Piri's lie has a short shelf,* he thought, and then: *Quill didn't send them to the north cove at all. Or he sent men there and kept his own eyes here.* Either way, the arithmetic had changed.

He killed the lamp. Darkness fell on him like a lid.

There was nowhere to go. The return passage was under three feet of climbing seawater now and rising; swimming it in this surge would be a death performed slowly and publicly. The shingle offered boulders, shadows, nothing more. Ines wedged himself and the dead lamp into the cleft between two stones and made himself small, made himself unseen, the oldest skill he owned — and found, even as he did it, that the skill had rotted in his hands. Six weeks ago hiding had been a philosophy. Now it was just a delay.

The lanterns grew. Voices came with them, shredded by wind, and then reassembled as the wind turned: Quill's voice, unmistakable, courteous even at a shout, the voice of a man reading terms aloud.

"—checked the north cove, sir, nothing, not a bootprint—"

"Of course there isn't." Quill stepped onto the shingle twenty feet away, and Ines saw him entire in the lantern-glow: coat plastered to his frame, hat gone, gray hair streaming, utterly unbothered, a man attending the weather as he might attend a funeral he'd expected for years. "The boy told us what we were meant to hear. Someone taught him his lines very well." He turned his head slowly, surveying the black mouth of the caves, the boiling tide, the storm. "Someone clever. Someone who's been carrying bread in a dive basket and thinking I wasn't counting."

Ines's blood went to ice water. *He knew. He's known for weeks.*

Quill walked toward the cave mouth, unhurried, boots crunching, and stopped at the edge of the flooded passage, where the sea was pouring in and out with each enormous breath of the storm. He stood there a long moment, listening to the rock breathe.

"They're gone, aren't they," he said conversationally, to the darkness, to the rain, to the man crouched ten feet behind him whom he had not yet turned to look at. "Out through the gap, on a night like this. Bold. Foolish. Probably successful — Maren Voss always was the better sailor in this family." He sighed, a small, satisfied sound. "Well. Boats can be chased. Cargo can be seized at any port from here to the capital. But the one thing I cannot pursue—" and now he turned, and the lantern-light found Ines's face in the cleft between the stones, and Quill smiled the smile of a man closing a ledger, "—is a debt. And yours, Master Kavanagh, has just come due."

He extended one hand, palm up, patient as the tide.

"The papers, if you please. And we'll walk back together, quietly, like reasonable men. Or—" he glanced at the drowned passage, at the storm, at the small hard space where Ines crouched, and his smile widened by a single degree, "—we can settle accounts here."

Ines's hand went, without his deciding it, to his shirt. To the folded paper that was his name, his wages, his legal existence, nineteen years of careful invisibility pressed flat and dry against his heart.

The sea roared. The lamp lay cold at his side, full of oil, full of light, waiting to be asked what it was for.
