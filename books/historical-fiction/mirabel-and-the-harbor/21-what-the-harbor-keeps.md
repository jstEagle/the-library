## 21. What the Harbor Keeps

They took him at the water's edge, in the gray hour when the storm had spent itself and the harbor lay flat as poured pewter.

Ines did not run. He had thought, in the cave, holding the burning papers, that he might run — that when the flame died and the skiff was through the reef gap, some animal part of him would bolt for the rocks and swim until his lungs gave out. But his legs decided otherwise, or perhaps it was not his legs at all but something older and quieter in him, the same thing that had kept him useful and unseen for eleven years: the understanding that running was a kind of lying, and he had promised himself, in the light of that small fire, that he was done with lying.

So he stood on the shingle with the tide licking his boots and watched the skiff's lantern dwindle toward the channel, a spark, then a smudge, then nothing — and behind him he heard the boots on the stone, four men, maybe five, and Mr. Quill's voice, level as a ledger line, saying, "There he is."

They beat him on the harbor stones. Not at once; first Quill walked around him in a slow half-circle, hands clasped behind his back, studying him the way he had once studied a cracked scale in the weighing house, deciding whether it was worth repairing or better to simply replace. Ines's face was swollen where a fist had already found it, and through the one eye that still opened properly he watched the water, because the water was where she had gone, and he found he could bear almost anything so long as he kept looking at it.

"You understand," Quill said, conversationally, "that I had men at the north cove. All night. In that rain." He said it not with anger but with the mild bewilderment of a man whose arithmetic has failed him. "Every boat. Every lantern. And she goes out through the reef gap in a borrowed skiff with no light at all." He stopped in front of Ines. "You fed me a false tide."

Ines said nothing. His mouth was full of blood and the taste of it was strangely clean, like iron, like the taste of a pearl shell's inner lip.

"I want you to know," Quill went on, "that this was well done. I mean that. In eleven years I have watched you carry baskets and keep your mouth shut and be exactly what the company needed you to be, and I confess I never once thought to count you among the dangers." He leaned close, and his breath smelled of tea and something medicinal. "That was my error. It will not be repeated."

Then he nodded, once, and stepped back, and the men did what men do.

Ines kept his eyes on the water the whole time. He thought about the way Sefa had gripped his wrist in the cave mouth, salt-crusted and shaking with cold, and said, *You could come. There's room. There's always room.* And he thought about how he had said no — not bravely, not with a speech, just *no*, the way you say it when you have finally stopped being two people — and how she had not argued, because she had known, maybe longer than he had, that the boy who owed her his life was going to pay it back standing on this shore, not fleeing from it.

The last thing he saw before the world went white was the harbor, keeping its secrets, flat and silver and full of morning.

* * *

The cell was below the weighing house, which he found — in the lucid intervals between the pain — a fitting joke. He had spent years in that room above, watching Quill shortchange Tevake and write down numbers that were lies dressed in neat figures, and now he lay beneath it in the dark and listened to the floorboards creak with the weight of the company's business going on without him.

The cell had been a salt store once. The walls still wept brine, and in the damp corners there grew a soft white bloom that glimmered faintly when his eyes adjusted, so that in the worst hours of the night he could pretend the room was full of very small, very patient stars. He named them, in the beginning. Later he was too tired for naming.

His ribs had taken the worst of it. Breathing was a negotiation conducted in installments. His left hand had been stamped on — deliberately, he understood, because Quill's men were not brutes but professionals, and professionals know which injuries heal and which ones teach — and for three days he could not close it at all. He lay on the stone floor with his ruined hand cradled against his chest and his mother's absence all around him, because the comb was gone, traded for quinine, and the papers were gone, burned in a signal lamp, and the knife was gone, confiscated with everything else, and he took inventory the way he had always taken inventory and found the list empty.

He had built his whole life on being light. Nothing to carry, nothing to lose, nothing anyone could take.

Now there was nothing to carry, and he had never felt heavier, and he had never felt freer.

* * *

They came to question him on the fourth day. Not Quill — Quill, he gathered from the snatches of talk he heard through the floor, was occupied elsewhere, and occupied badly. It was a clerk, a soft-bellied man named Hollis who had once weighed Ines's baskets and never once looked at his face, and a company physician whose hands smelled of carbolic.

"Name," said Hollis, though he knew it.

"Ines Kavanagh."

"Indenture number."

"None," Ines said. "Burned."

The physician, to his credit, looked away. Hollis wrote something down with a small dry scratch, as though the pen itself disapproved.

"You will appreciate," Hollis said, "that the magistrate arrives with the bridge. Nine days, perhaps ten, once the weather clears. You will be held until then. What happens after depends very much on—" he consulted his papers, though there was nothing in them about this, "—on your cooperation. The company is prepared to be generous with men who assist in the recovery of stolen property. There were rations taken. There was a skiff. There is the matter of the girl."

"The girl," Ines said, "is at sea."

"The girl," Hollis said, "is a matter of contract. Her father signed. The debt passes to kin. If she is at sea, she is a fugitive, and fugitives are recovered. You could shorten that recovery considerably."

Ines looked at him — with the one eye that worked, slowly, the way you look at something you are memorizing in order to describe it later — and said nothing at all. He was very good at saying nothing. He had been, all his life. But it was different now, and Hollis must have felt the difference, because the clerk's own eyes dropped first.

They left him the physician's bandages and a bucket and a blanket that smelled of mildew, and after the door shut Ines lay back and laughed, or made the sound that was the nearest his ribs would allow to laughing, because *the debt passes to kin* — as if Tevake's debt were pearls, as if a man's life could be weighed and shortchanged like a basket at the scales, as if Quill had not spent eleven years doing exactly that and calling it business.

The harbor kept things. That was what he had learned, growing up in it. It kept drowned men and dropped knives and the names of the dead, and it gave them back only when it chose, at low tide, in its own time. Let it keep him a while. The magistrate was coming with the bridge, and the magistrate would find the harbor had been keeping other things too — a season of shortchanged divers, a list of enemies, a boy in a cell below the scales — and harbors, sooner or later, give everything back.

* * *

On the sixth day, he heard the singing.

It came at dawn, thin through the stone, from the direction of the water. He knew the song — every child in the harbor town knew it — the launching song the divers sang when a new boat first touched the water, the one with the long rising line at the end that was meant to carry out past the reef and tell the sea who was coming. But they were not launching anything. They were singing it standing still, on the shingle, in the gray light, dozens of voices, and Ines understood with a certainty that needed no evidence: they were singing it out to sea. To her. Telling the water who had come through, and telling the company, in the only language the company could not confiscate, that they knew.

He dragged himself to the barred grate high in the wall — it took him most of an hour, and cost him what breathing he had — and through it he could see a sliver of the harbor, gray water, gray sky, and on the shingle the dark shapes of the divers, standing in a line, not diving. The company's boats sat at their moorings with no one in them. Beyond, on the staging, the pearl baskets hung empty.

He watched until the song ended, and the line of divers did not disperse. They simply stood. And the harbor, flat and patient, stood with them.

* * *

The ration cuts came on the eighth day, and Ines felt a savage, aching joy.

He heard it in the voices above — Hollis and another clerk, their footsteps crossing the floorboards overhead, their talk no longer neat but frayed at the edges. The divers would not go down. The divers' families would not scavenge the shallows. The company had threatened, and the threat had been met with that same standing line on the shingle, and now the stores were being counted, and the counting was not going the way the counting had always gone.

"Eleven days to the bridge," one voice said. "We cannot hold the season's contract on what's in the stores. If the magistrate can't restore order—"

"The magistrate," said the other voice, and there was something in it Ines had never heard from a company man before, something small and frightened, "will want to know why there is no season."

Ines lay back on the stone floor, under his small patient stars, and let the pain in his ribs be what it was, and smiled at the salt-bloomed ceiling of the room where he had once watched a man get robbed in broad daylight and said nothing.

They had thought he was the one who kept things for them — the baskets, the numbers, the silence. But it was the harbor that kept things. It kept everything: every shortchanged diver, every confiscated boat, every name on every list. And now the harbor was giving it all back at once, through the only power the company had never learned to weigh — the simple, immovable fact that pearls came out of the water, and the water belonged to the people who went down into it, and they had decided, all of them together, to stay on the shore.

* * *

On the ninth night, Quill came down to the cell.

Ines heard the boots before he saw the lantern — that measured, unhurried tread he had known for eleven years, the tread of a man who had never once needed to hurry because the whole town hurried on his account. The light came through the bars and laid its long stripes across the floor, and Quill stood on the other side of them, and for the first time since Ines had known him, Mr. Quill looked tired.

Not beaten. Tired. There was a difference, and Ines, studying him through the working eye, catalogued it the way he had once catalogued everything: the collar slightly askew, the hands — always clasped, always behind the back — now hanging at his sides, the face carrying a new line between the brows that had not been there in the autumn.

"You should know," Quill said, "that the girl reached open water. A trawler out of the northern ports picked up her signal smoke two days past. She is beyond my reach, and beyond the company's, and she will stay there." He said it flatly, without ceremony, and Ines understood that this was the closest thing to a gift Quill knew how to give: information, accurately rendered. "You should also know that I have filed my report. It states that the escape was accomplished through the negligence of company personnel. It does not mention you by name."

Ines said nothing. The lantern flame stood very still between them.

"It will not matter," Quill went on, and now the tiredness in his voice had something under it, something that might, in a different man, have been called respect. "The magistrate will have my reports and the company's ledger, and the ledger says you were never indentured at all — the papers being destroyed — and a magistrate cannot hang a man the law does not recognize. They will find another charge. They always do. But I thought you should know the shape of it before he arrives." He paused. "You cost me a season, Kavanagh. A whole season. I have been in this harbor nineteen years, and no one has ever cost me a season."

Ines looked at him through the bars, through the lantern light, with his broken hand curled in his lap and his ribs strapped and his face half a mask of bruise, and he found that what he felt was not triumph. It was something quieter and stranger: the feeling of a debt being paid in full, and a new ledger opening, blank, in which nothing had yet been written.

"The divers will dive again," Ines said. His voice came out rusted, half its old size. "When the terms are fair. Not before."

Quill regarded him for a long moment. "You sound very sure."

"I'm sure of what the harbor keeps," Ines said. "It keeps everything. And it gives it back."

Quill stood a moment longer, the lantern trembling very slightly in his hand — and Ines would remember, all his life, that small tremble, the only honest thing he had ever seen the man do — and then the enforcer turned and climbed the stairs, and the light went with him, and the cell filled up again with its small patient stars.

* * *

He slept, after that, the first real sleep since the cave. And in the morning he heard the bridge.

Not opening — not yet. But the storm had broken, and from the sliver of harbor visible through the grate he could see men on the far span, small as gulls, working the winches, and he knew what it meant: three days, perhaps four, and the road would be open, and with the road would come the magistrate, and the magistrate would come down to the weighing house and ask his questions, and above him the company's clerks were already sharpening their figures and rehearsing their grief.

But that was not what made Ines press his forehead against the cold stone beneath the grate and hold there, breathing in installments, while the morning light crept across the cell floor.

It was the sound from the shingle. The divers were there again, in their line, and this time they were not singing the launching song. They were singing the other one — the old one, the mourning-and-return song, the one sung for men lost to the water and for men coming home from it, the one whose long last line was meant to reach a listener across any distance the sea could make.

And they were singing it facing the weighing house.

He could not see their faces from below. He did not need to. He lay on the stone floor of the cell beneath the scales, in the room where the company had weighed everything it owned and most of what it didn't, and listened to the harbor giving back what it kept, and he understood that in three days, when the bridge opened and the magistrate's boots came down those stairs, he would not climb them alone.

Somewhere north, past the reef gap and the gray water, Sefa was breathing open air. And here, in the fog-bound town that had never once seen him, the whole harbor had learned his name.

The boots on the stairs, when they came, would be many.
