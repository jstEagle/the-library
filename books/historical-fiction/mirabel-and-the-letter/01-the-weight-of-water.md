## 1. The Weight of Water

The sea at Meridian Outpost had two moods, and Paloma Voss knew both of them better than he knew his own face. In the morning it was a sheet of hammered tin, flat and indifferent, holding the light without giving anything back. By afternoon it turned generous — green-gold, warm as bathwater, the kind of water that made you forget it could kill you. The Company's ledgers recorded neither mood. The ledgers only recorded what came up out of it.

Paloma came up out of it now, gasping, one hand on the gunwale of the diving skiff, and held up his catch for old Tamu to tally.

"Six," Tamu said, not looking up from his slate. "One cracked. Four and a half."

"Five," Paloma said. "The crack is hairline."

"The crack," said Tamu, who had been tallying pearl divers since before Paloma was born and intended to go on doing it long after everyone currently breathing was dead, "is four and a half."

There was no arguing with Tamu's arithmetic, in the same way there was no arguing with the tide or with the interest rate on the Voss family debt, which was to say you could argue all you liked and the numbers would remain exactly where they were, serene and immovable, like stones at the bottom of a lagoon. Paloma dropped back into the water rather than waste breath on it. Below, the oyster beds spread out along the reef shelf like a field of grey mouths, all of them slightly open, all of them waiting. He worked them the way his father had taught him — thumb under the lip, gentle, patient, take what opens and leave what won't — though his father had been dead eleven years now and the debt he'd left behind was very much alive, compounding quarterly at a rate Paloma had never once managed to calculate correctly.

He didn't need to calculate it. Renwick did that for him.

Aldous Renwick, factor of the Meridian Trading Company's Pearl Station Nine, was the only man in the archipelago who could tell Paloma to his face exactly how much he owed, and did so every quarter-day with the mild regret of a physician delivering an unwelcome diagnosis. He would unfold the ledger, run one soft finger down the column, and say something like, "Ah. Well. Progress, of a sort," and then he would look at Paloma over the tops of his spectacles with such genuine, unfeigned kindness that Paloma always went away feeling grateful, which was its own particular trick, and a very good one.

That was the thing about Mr. Renwick that people missed, if they were visitors: he was kind. He had sponsored the Voss account personally after Paloma's father drowned, had advanced them seed-oil and rice through two bad seasons, had once — Paloma remembered this with a clarity that still embarrassed him — carried seven-year-old Paloma on his shoulders during the fire festival so the boy could see over the crowd. Kindness, on Station Nine, was not a quality. It was currency. And Renwick was rich in it, the way a moneylender is rich in coins, which is to say: he kept it where he could count it.

Paloma surfaced again, spat salt, and handed up five more.

* * *

The hierarchy of Station Nine was a thing nobody had ever written down, because writing things down was for the Company and the Company preferred its arrangements unwritten, but every soul on the island could have recited it in their sleep. At the top, the factor's white house on the hill with its veranda and its rain cisterns and its flag. Below that, the clerks and the overseers, men with ink stains and soft hands. Below that, the boat crews, who at least owned their own boats. Then the free divers from the mainland villages, who sold by weight. And at the bottom, below even the goats, who at least belonged to somebody —

— the indentured. Forty-one souls bound to the Company by debts inherited, incurred, or invented. Paloma had been born into the third category and graduated early into the first.

It wasn't cruelty, exactly. That was what took some getting used to, when he was younger. He had expected cruelty, the way you expect it in the stories Sefina's grandmother told, with whips and chains and villains twirling mustaches. What he found instead was a kind of enormous, well-organized indifference. Nobody hated Paloma Voss. Nobody thought about Paloma Voss at all. The overseer called him "boy" not from contempt but from genuine inability to distinguish him from the other eleven divers of similar build. The cook gave him the same ration as everyone else and the same expression, which was none. When the Company ship *Coriolis* came in twice a year, its officers looked straight through him on the dock, their gazes sliding off him like rain off oilskin, and settled on things that mattered: crates, manifests, the factor's white house on the hill.

For a long time — most of his life, really — Paloma had decided this was fine.

Better than fine. There was a shelter in it. A man who was invisible couldn't be blamed, couldn't be fined, couldn't be noticed when the notice would cost him. When the typhoon of six years ago took the roof off the drying shed, nobody asked the indentured divers how they'd slept. When the fever came through and took old Maro and two of the Lelani brothers, nobody from the Company sent so much as a question down the hill, and if that stung, it also meant that when Paloma sat alone on the breakwater eating his evening rice, no one watched him do it, and no one remarked on it, and the sea said nothing either, and there was a whole species of peace available in that, if you were willing to call it peace.

History, Paloma had concluded by the age of maybe twelve, was something that happened to other people. It happened up the hill, in the factor's house, in the dispatches that went out on the *Coriolis*. It happened across the water, in the war the mainlanders muttered about — some quarrel between the crown and the trading combines, or between the combines and each other; nobody on Station Nine had ever gotten the same version twice. It happened in the capital, wherever exactly that was, in rooms full of men whose names Paloma wouldn't have recognized and who, he was fairly certain, could not have found Station Nine on a chart if you paid them.

Down here, there was only weather, work, and debt. Weather changed. Work ended at sundown. Debt, alone, was eternal. It was, he sometimes thought while treading water above the beds, almost restful — knowing your whole life was already written down somewhere, in someone else's hand, in a book you'd never be allowed to open.

* * *

Except once. Once, someone had put a different kind of book in his hands.

Captain Elias Thorne had come to Station Nine the way bad weather comes — announced by others, explained by no one. A naval man, retired or exiled or both, given some half-official commission to survey the outer channels, and lodged — because the Company house was full and because Renwick enjoyed collecting interesting men the way other collectors collect shells — in the small room behind the cooperage. Paloma was fourteen and hauling barrels past the cooperage twice a day, and one afternoon the captain was sitting outside with a chart weighted down by four stones and a frown on his face like a man doing arithmetic against his will.

"You," Thorne said, without looking up. "Boy. Can you read?"

"No, sir."

"Hm." The captain studied him for a moment with eyes the color of shallow water over sand. "Can you carry a number in your head? Tally a line without losing it?"

"Yes, sir."

"Well, then." He patted the step beside him. "Halfway there. Sit."

What followed was two years of stolen hours. Thorne taught him letters scratched in coral dust, then in charcoal on barrel staves, then — extravagantly — in real ink on real paper, pages torn from an old navigation manual that Thorne claimed he'd outgrown. He was a strange teacher, impatient and delighted in alternating measure, prone to saying "No, no — *look* at it" as though the alphabet might behave better if properly regarded. But he never treated the lessons as charity, and he never treated Paloma as furniture. When Paloma recited the tide tables back to him without error, Thorne laughed — a short, surprised bark, as if the sound had escaped custody — and said, "Well. They've wasted you, haven't they."

Nobody had ever said anything of the kind to Paloma before. He hadn't known what to do with it, so he'd done nothing, which was his habit, and the words had sunk into him anyway, deep, below the reach of any ledger.

And then one morning the cooperage room was empty. No farewell that Paloma ever heard of — or perhaps there had been one, and it simply hadn't occurred to anyone that a diver's son would want telling. The *Meridian Rose* had taken on water and stores, the flag had gone up the factor's pole, and Captain Thorne had sailed east, toward the war Paloma never asked about, because asking felt like presumption, and presumption, on Station Nine, was the one debt you couldn't pay down.

He still had three of the practice pages, folded small and wrapped in waxcloth at the bottom of his diving kit, where no overseer would ever think to look, because no overseer would ever think to look at anything of his. Letters, mostly. One page was the word *lagoon*, copied forty times until Thorne pronounced it "nearly respectable."

Some nights, after the lamps were out, Paloma would read it to himself in the dark. Not aloud. Just moving his lips, tracing the letters with his finger, the way you'd touch a scar to confirm it's yours.

* * *

On the evening of the day described above — a Tuesday, for those who keep track of such things, though Station Nine officially did not — Paloma climbed the path from the drying beach with his tally slate and his aching shoulders, and stopped at the top because the light was doing something worth stopping for. The sun was going down behind the factor's hill in great molten sheets, and the whole lagoon had turned the color of the inside of a shell, and for a moment — he allowed himself the moment, it was nearly free — he let himself imagine being a man who could paint, or write, or otherwise make a record of a thing like this, instead of merely surviving it.

Then he went down the other side to the indenture quarters, ate his rice, and listened to Sefina complain about her mother's matchmaking ambitions with the fluent outrage of a girl who has rehearsed the complaint many times and improved it at each performance.

"—and I said to her, Mother, he has *eyebrows like caterpillars in an argument*, and she said, Sefina, eyebrows are not everything, and I said, Mother, neither is a boat—" She broke off and squinted at him. "You're not listening."

"I'm listening."

"You're listening with your mouth. Your eyes are out diving." She leaned close, lowering her voice to a stage whisper that carried three huts in every direction. "Is it the quota? Did Tamu cheat you again?"

"Tamu never cheats anyone. Tamu is the closest thing we have to a god, and gods don't cheat, they just decree."

That got the laugh he wanted, and she shoved his shoulder, and the evening went on the way evenings went: lamps, gossip, the sound of the reef working at itself in the dark, patient as a creditor.

He was asleep — genuinely asleep, sunk past dreaming — when the shouting started.

It came from the direction of the tide flats, thin and urgent, a voice he didn't recognize, and beneath it a commotion of running feet and someone calling for lanterns. Paloma lay still in the hot dark, listening the way you learn to listen here: cataloguing, weighing, deciding whether it concerned him. Almost nothing that happened on Station Nine concerned him. That was the arrangement. That was the shelter.

But the voice on the flats called out again, and this time he caught the shape of the words — *help, help me, in the name of God, someone* — and beneath the words something else, something wrong, a wet rattling edge to the sound that raised the hair on his arms.

Beside him, Sefina's brother groaned and pulled his mat over his head. Down the row, nobody moved. The indentured had learned long ago that after dark, trouble belongs to other people.

Paloma lay there another ten heartbeats, doing the arithmetic of invisibility, arriving at the sum he always arrived at.

Then he got up, took the lamp, and went out toward the flats to see what the sea had brought in.
