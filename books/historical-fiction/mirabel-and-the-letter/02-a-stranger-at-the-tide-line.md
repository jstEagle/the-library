## 2. A Stranger at the Tide Line

The tide went out on the eastern flats the way it always did—slowly, grudgingly, like a rich man parting with coins he felt he'd already given twice. Paloma Voss walked the flats because walking them was his job, and his job was the one thing in his life that never surprised him.

He carried a bamboo basket and a long iron pick, and he looked for pearl oysters the way other men looked for lost buttons: patiently, without hope of glory. The Meridian Trading Company paid him four annas a day for this, minus what it deducted for his food, his lodging, his lamp oil, and a mysterious line item called *administrative consideration* that appeared on every ledger page and had never once been explained to anyone. His father's debt was eleven years old now. By Renwick's arithmetic—and Renwick's arithmetic was the only arithmetic that mattered on Kestrel Cay—he had perhaps six more years of diving before the number reached zero, assuming no storms, no sickness, and no further administrative considerations.

Paloma had made his peace with all of it around age fourteen, which was roughly when he'd realized that being invisible was not a punishment but a kind of weather. The Company saw him the way it saw the tide: useful, predictable, beneath notice. The overseers shouted at him without hating him. The clerks recorded his catch without seeing him. Even the gulls treated him as furniture.

There were worse fates. He'd thought about this carefully, lying in the divers' quarters listening to Sefina snore like a woman arguing with God. A man who was noticed could be blamed. A man who was seen could be wanted for something. Paloma wanted nothing, so nobody wanted anything from him except oysters, and oysters couldn't talk.

The morning was doing that thing it did before the monsoon season properly arrived—the air thick as broth, the light coming down sideways through haze, everything smelling faintly of hot metal and old fish. Paloma was twenty minutes along the flats, having pried loose three decent shell and one that turned out to be just a rock wearing an ambitious disguise, when he saw the shape.

At first he took it for driftwood. Then for a dead turtle; they washed up sometimes after storms far out at sea, and the Company docked the cost of their removal against the divers' wages, which Paloma considered a special kind of genius. He walked toward it anyway, because a dead turtle meant paperwork, and paperwork meant the clerk Osei would want to know the exact time of finding, and Osei trusted only Paloma to report things accurately, since everyone else rounded numbers the way other people rounded corners.

It was not a turtle.

It was a man, face-down in six inches of water, and he was moving—one arm dragging through the wet sand in slow strokes, like a swimmer who had forgotten how swimming worked but remembered it was important.

Paloma dropped the basket. For a moment he simply stood there, because in nineteen years of living on Kestrel Cay, the sea had delivered him shells, fish, occasional wreckage, and once an entire ship's figurehead—a wooden woman with a serene smile whom Sefina had named Mrs. Pemberton and installed outside her family's hut—but never a person. History, Paloma had always understood, happened elsewhere. It happened in ports, in capitals, in the war Captain Thorne had sailed off to fight. It arrived here only as prices and rumors, filtered through Renwick's ledgers until it was safe and dry. It did not wash up on the flats at low tide, breathing.

"Hello," Paloma said, and then felt immediately foolish, because the man was clearly past hello.

He got hands under the stranger's arms and dragged him up onto the drier sand above the tideline. The man was young—younger than Paloma, maybe—with salt-crusted lips and skin scraped raw across both shoulders, and he wore nothing but a soaked shirt and trousers that had once been good ones. No boots. Boots, Paloma knew, were the first thing the sea took, along with hats, dignity, and anything in pockets.

Except this man still had something. There was a cord around his neck, and hanging from it, pressed flat against his chest by the weight of his own body, an oilskin-wrapped packet the size of a prayer book, tied with waxed string.

The man's eyes opened. They were the color of weak tea, and they found Paloma's face with an effort that seemed to cost him something physical.

"You're—" he rasped. Water came up with the word. "Not Company."

"I'm Company," Paloma said honestly. "I'm afraid. I dive for them. But I'm mostly me."

Something that might have been a laugh moved through the man's chest and died there. His hand rose—trembling, fingers white at the tips—and closed around the oilskin packet, and he tried to lift it, and failed, and tried again.

"Take it," he whispered.

Paloma looked at the packet. He looked at the sea, flat and innocent as a lied-about child. Somewhere behind him, back at the outpost, the morning bell would be ringing soon, and Osei would be marking him late, and late meant a deduction, and deductions were the Company's favorite form of conversation.

"Take it," the man said again, and this time there was something underneath the whisper—not strength exactly, but direction, the way a dying fire can still point you toward the woodpile. "They must never see it. The Company. Never. Swallow it first. Burn it first. Anything. But it goes to—" A cough tore through him, long and wet. "It has to go. You understand? It *has to go.*"

"To where?" Paloma asked. "To whom?"

But the man wasn't answering questions anymore. His hand slipped off the packet and lay open on the sand, palm up, in a gesture that looked almost like asking. His breathing had gone shallow and quick, each breath a small argument he was losing.

Paloma knelt. He should run for help—that was the correct sequence of events, the one any reasonable person would follow. There was a medic at the outpost, a retired whaler named Brannigan whose medical philosophy consisted chiefly of rum. There was Renwick, who would want to know everything, who would ask gentle, smiling questions, who would write it all down in a ledger under some heading Paloma couldn't imagine yet.

The man's hand twitched toward the packet again.

Paloma untied the cord from his neck.

Underneath, the oilskin was warm from the man's body, which seemed wrong somehow, indecent, like touching someone's heartbeat without permission. The packet was heavier than it looked. Paloma tucked it inside his shirt, against his own skin, and it sat there like a second heart, beating slightly out of time with his.

"Is there anyone?" Paloma asked. "Anyone I should tell? Anyone waiting?"

The man's tea-colored eyes had drifted upward, fixed on the haze where the sky ought to have been. His lips moved. Paloma leaned close, close enough to smell brine and blood.

"He said you'd come," the courier breathed. "He said—send it to the diver's son. He said the diver's son keeps his word."

Then the breathing stopped arguing and simply stopped, between one wave's withdrawal and the next, quietly, the way a lamp goes out when the oil runs true to the bottom.

* * *

Paloma sat beside the body for a long time. Long enough for the tide to turn and begin its slow return, licking at the man's heels like an apologetic dog. Long enough for his own thoughts to stop circling and start landing, one at a time, each one heavier than the last.

*Diver's son.*

There was only one diver's son on Kestrel Cay who had ever been promised anything by anyone. And there was only one man alive—or dead, or whatever he was—who called Paloma that instead of his name, who had sat cross-legged on the breakwater with a stick and taught a ten-year-old to draw letters in the sand, laughing whenever Paloma confused his b's and d's. *"Don't worry, little fish,"* Thorne used to say. *"Letters are just hooks. Once you learn to bait them, you'll catch whole worlds."*

Captain Elias Thorne had taught him to read. Captain Elias Thorne had sailed away to the war two years ago. And Captain Elias Thorne had been executed—officially, publicly, with a notice nailed to the Company house door that Renwick himself had read aloud, hat in hand, voice appropriately grave—for treason against the crown.

Paloma had attended the reading the way everyone had attended it: standing in the heat, feeling sad in a distant, ceremonial way, the way you feel sad about a storm that hits someone else's island. That was what history was. Something that happened to other people, in other places, and arrived here only as notices on doors.

The notice had said Thorne was hanged in the spring of the year.

Paloma pulled the packet out from his shirt with hands that did not entirely feel like his own. The oilskin was tied with waxed string, knotted in the sailor's fashion Thorne had shown him once, idly, the way he showed him everything—as if knowledge were sweets to be handed out freely to anyone who stood still long enough. Paloma picked the knot loose.

Inside was a letter, folded twice, and beneath the letter a thinner sheaf of papers he did not look at yet because his courage was a finite resource and currently running low. He unfolded the letter.

The handwriting hit him before the words did. He knew that handwriting the way he knew his own reflection—the strong forward lean of it, the g's that looped too far below the line like divers going deep, the little flourish on the capital E that Thorne had once admitted, drunk on festival rum, was completely invented and served no purpose whatsoever except vanity.

*My dear boy,* the letter began.

And then, lower on the page, the date. Paloma read the date three times. He held the paper closer. He held it farther away. He checked the date against the execution notice in his memory, which he could recite nearly verbatim because Renwick had read it slowly and the outpost had nothing else to think about for a month.

The date on the letter was three months after the day Elias Thorne had officially ceased to exist.

Paloma became aware, distantly, that he was sitting in rising water. The tide was coming in around him, soaking his trousers, indifferent. Somewhere out on the water a fishing boat was ringing its bell, and the sound was so ordinary, so aggressively ordinary, that it made the world feel like a trick.

Dead men did not write letters. Executed traitors did not send couriers across open sea to drown on the flats within sight of the place they'd learned a boy's name. History did not happen here.

And yet the paper was real in his hands, warped with seawater, ink slightly feathered at the edges. And the dead courier lay behind him with his palm still open to the sky. And against Paloma's ribs, where the packet had rested, his own heart was pounding in a rhythm he didn't recognize—quick, irregular, awake.

He looked down at the letter again. *My dear boy.* Two words, and already the comfortable shape of his life—the invisibility, the weather of being unnoticed, the six remaining years of debt and administrative considerations—had developed a crack straight down the middle, and through the crack he could hear something vast and cold moving.

Paloma folded the letter along its original creases. He wrapped the papers, retied the string with the sailor's knot, and slid the whole packet into the waterproof roll at the bottom of his diving kit, beneath his spare mask and the lucky cowrie shell Sefina had given him when they were children. Then he stood, picked up his basket, and began to walk back toward the outpost, three oysters rattling in the bottom, trying to arrange his face into the expression of a man who had found nothing all morning but shell.

Behind him, the tide crept up the flats and covered the place where the stranger had lain, smoothing the sand over, erasing him with the patience of a clerk.

By nightfall, Paloma knew, the Company would ask why a body had been found and who had found it. By nightfall, Renwick would be asking his gentle, smiling questions.

Paloma just hoped his hands would stop shaking before anyone thought to look at them.
