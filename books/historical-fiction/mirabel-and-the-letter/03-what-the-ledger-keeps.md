## 3. What the Ledger Keeps

The lamp in Paloma's quarters was a stub of tallow he'd won off Dario at cards, and it burned the way Dario did everything—greedily, unevenly, and with a strong smell of something slightly wrong. Paloma didn't mind. He had learned to read by worse light: a candle-end smuggled under a blanket, a sliver of moon through a shutter, once, memorably, the glow of a cookfire while Thorne held a page up and traced the letters with a finger rough as coral.

*Boy,* Thorne had said, *letters are the only cargo nobody can take off you.*

Paloma sat on his bunk with the oilskin packet in his lap and did not open it for a long time. He told himself this was caution. It was partly caution. Mostly it was that a man can carry a thing easily right up until the moment he understands what it weighs.

Outside, the outpost settled into its night noises—the creak of the drying sheds, someone's dog complaining about the injustice of dogs, Sefina's grandmother singing three notes of a lullaby before deciding, audibly, that no one deserved it. The ordinary sounds of a place where nothing had ever happened. Where nothing was supposed to happen ever again.

He broke the seal.

The handwriting hit him first. Before any word of it, before sense, there was simply the hand itself—that forward-leaning script, the *t*'s crossed like a man in a hurry to be somewhere, the little hook on the final letter of every name, as if each name were being flung rather than written. Paloma had spent four years of his childhood copying that hand. He had been praised for it, once, which was how he'd learned that praise from an officer of the Meridian Trading Company was a currency accepted almost nowhere.

*Paloma.*

Just his name first. Alone on the line. As if Thorne knew—because of course Thorne knew everything, Thorne had always known everything, including, apparently, how not to be dead—that the boy would need a moment.

*If you are reading this, then Marek reached you or failed to, and either way I am sorry for what I have put on your shoulders. I told you once that history happens elsewhere. I lied, or I was ignorant, and both shame me. History is happening here. It has been happening under our feet the whole time, in the ledgers, in the holds, in the polite arithmetic of men who count pearls and never divers.*

*Listen carefully, because I will say it plainly: the Company ships arms in the pearl consignments. Gun crates beneath the shell baskets, sealed in wax and listed as "ballast stone." The shipments out of this very outpost. Renwick signs for them. Renwick has always signed for them.*

Paloma read it three times. The third time his lips moved and no sound came out, which was just as well, because the sound would have been a laugh, and it would have been the wrong kind entirely.

Renwick.

Aldous Renwick, who kept sweets in his desk drawer for the factor's children and remembered the names of divers' babies. Renwick, who had "sponsored" the Voss family's indenture after Papa drowned—which Paloma now understood, with a slow cold turning in his stomach, meant Renwick had *purchased* them, the way one purchases a boat with good bones and a leaky hull. Renwick, who had once looked at nine-year-old Paloma's copybook and said, warmly, *ah, we have a scholar*, and given him extra rations, and Paloma had walked home floating on that sentence for a week.

The arms went out under the pearls. The man who counted the pearls was the kindly man with the sweets.

*There is more,* Thorne wrote, *and it is worse, and you do not need all of it yet. What you need is this: the governor must see these pages before the monsoon closes the sea. After that, the Company has nine months of privacy, and men like Renwick use privacy the way divers use air—sparingly, and only until the job is done. I am gathering the proof myself. Do not look for me. If you look for me, you will find what they want you to find.*

*I made you a promise once, that your letters would always find their way home. This is mine coming home. Carry it as far as it needs to go, and then, boy—put it down. You were never meant to be a courier. You were meant to be free of all this, and I intend, stubbornly, to see it happen.*

*—E.T.*

P.S. *Your penmanship was always better than mine. Don't let anyone tell you otherwise, especially me.*

Paloma sat very still. The tallow lamp gutted and recovered. Somewhere outside, Dario was losing at cards again, judging by the volume of his outrage.

A dead man had written him a letter. Not dead. Branded dead, executed on paper two years ago—there had even been a notice nailed to the Company board, and Renwick himself had taken it down gently, folding it like laundry, saying *a fine officer, lost to the war*. And now the dead man was out there gathering proof against the Company, and he had sent it home to a pearl diver's son who owed the Company eleven years of labor and whose hands, thank God, had finally stopped shaking.

Paloma folded the pages along their original creases, wrapped them back in the oilskin, and did not put them in his diving kit after all. He put them inside the hollowed gourd where he kept his mother's things—a comb missing teeth, a shell she'd painted with a fish, a strip of cloth embroidered with a word in her island dialect that he had never learned to read because she'd died—or left; the records said *left*—before she could teach him.

*Mirabel.* That had been her word for him. He didn't know what it meant. Nobody left alive seemed to know. It was the one ledger entry in his life with no column for value.

He blew out the lamp and lay in the dark doing arithmetic of his own: nine days' rations, one letter, two hundred miles of monsoon water, and a promise made by a boy too young to know what promises cost. The numbers did not come out kind. They never did. But for the first time in nineteen years, Paloma Voss noticed that the sum included *him*—that he was not merely the page on which other people's figures were entered.

It should have felt like freedom. Mostly it felt like vertigo.

* * *

Three hundred paces away, in the factor's house where the lamps burned whale oil because tallow smelled common, Aldous Renwick performed his nightly devotions.

They took the form of a ledger.

He sat at his desk with a glass of arrack he would not finish—he never finished it; discipline was the difference between a factor and a drunkard—and he turned the day's entries with the satisfaction of a man turning soil. Forty-one baskets of shell graded and weighed. Six hundred and twelve pearls above trade size. Two divers treated for ear-bleeds, cost of laudanum noted. One body recovered from the tide line, unidentified, cause of drowning presumed.

That last entry he read twice.

Drowning was the honest death out here. The sea took its tithe and asked no questions, and a drowned stranger was a sad fact, not a problem. Except that the stranger had been found by Voss's boy—the quiet one, the literate one, the one Renwick had sponsored out of what he genuinely believed was kindness and what the ledger recorded, accurately, as an investment. A diver who could read notices and keep tallies was worth two ordinary divers. Renwick had never pretended otherwise to himself. Self-deception, he felt, was the vice of gentlemen, and he was not a gentleman; he was useful, which was better.

But the boy had looked strange today. Renwick had watched him across the sorting floor—watching people was half a factor's work, and the other half was making them glad he had—and the boy had that particular stillness Renwick associated with men carrying more than their weight rating allowed.

And Marek's boat had gone down three days ago, alone, on a run that should have taken one day.

Renwick sipped his arrack and set down the glass and opened a different book—not the trading ledger but a slim private volume bound in grey calfskin, in which he kept figures of another kind. Not money. Money was simple. This book held probabilities.

*Marek: 1 unit, unreliable since the northern run. Disposition: resolved.*
*Letter, hypothetical: unknown quantity.*
*Voss, P.: exposure risk, low-moderate. Value: high. Cost of replacement: moderate. Sentiment: nil.*

He paused over that last line and permitted himself a small honesty, the way a man permits himself one sweet from the drawer. He had liked the boy. Liking was pleasant and cheap, like feeding birds, and he had never once confused it with obligation. When the arithmetic required it—and arithmetic always eventually required it—the birds flew off, or didn't, and the drawer locked again.

The war had taught him this much: empires were not built by villains. Villains were inefficient. Empires were built by careful men doing correct sums while everyone else was busy feeling things. Captain Thorne had been a feeling man, and look where feelings had gotten him—executed, mourned by the help, and no loss to the Company whatsoever.

Except.

Renwick turned the grey book closed and pressed his palm flat upon it. There was the matter of the northern run, and the matter of what Marek might have seen before his boat went down, and the matter—one could never quite dismiss it—of Thorne's body, which Renwick had never personally verified, because verifying corpses in wartime was expensive and the paperwork had been so conveniently complete.

He rose, took up his lantern, and stood a moment at the window looking out at the dark rows of the divers' quarters, sleeping under their palm thatch like rows of shells on a grading table. So many units. So little noise. That was the miracle of the archipelago, really—people who had nothing dreamed small, and small dreams made no sound.

Tomorrow, he thought, he would begin his gentle, smiling questions. Starting, perhaps, with the scholars.

In the dark, Paloma's gourd sat on its shelf holding a dead man's letter and a mother's unreadable word, and between those two weights, the boy slept badly, dreaming of tide lines that erased nothing at all.
