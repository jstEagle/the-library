## 5. Monsoon Season, Monsoon Rules

The Company announced the last ship with a bell and a board.

The bell was rung by the factor's clerk at dawn, three strokes, the same as for a death in the indenture rolls, which everyone agreed was about right. The board was nailed to the countinghouse door before the tide had finished going out, and by the time Paloma walked past it with his diving kit on his shoulder, half the outpost had already gathered to read what most of them could not read: *THE MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY REGRETS TO INFORM ALL PERSONS THAT THE FINAL VESSEL OF THE SEASON, THE FORTUNATE STAR, WILL DEPART ON THE NINTH DAY HEREAFTER. NO FURTHER PASSAGE UNTIL THE MONSOON PASSES. THE COMPANY THANKS YOU FOR YOUR PATIENCE.*

"Patience," said Old Tavita, who had been waiting eleven years for passage she could not afford. "That's a kind word for it."

"They could have said *endurance*," offered her grandson, who was fourteen and had recently discovered opinions.

"They could have said *suffering*," said Tavita, "but then they'd have to admit they know we do it."

Paloma did not stop to listen. He had learned long ago that the trick to being invisible was to never slow down near anything interesting. A man who paused at a notice board was a man with an opinion; a man with an opinion was a man with a name; and a man with a name was a man the Company could find when it wanted someone. So he kept walking, eyes forward, heart doing something complicated against his ribs that he chose not to examine.

Nine days. The *Fortunate Star* would carry the season's pearls to the mainland, carry the ledgers, carry Renwick's reports — and after it left, the monsoon would come down like a lid closing over the whole archipelago, and nothing would move for four months. Nothing except whatever the Company wanted moved, quietly, under pearl shipments, in the dark of the water where no one looked because no one like Paloma was ever supposed to be able to read.

He had read the letter eleven times now. He knew it nearly by heart, though every night he copied it again anyway, in his own careful hand, the letters Thorne had taught him, and hid the copy inside the hollow float of his spare diving kit where even the rats hadn't thought to look. It was becoming less like paper and more like something growing inside him. He wasn't sure yet whether that made him braver or just more afraid.

* * *

Renwick began searching on the third day, and he did it so gently that it took Paloma two days to understand it was happening at all.

It started with tea. Aldous Renwick came down to the divers' quarters himself — the factor, in person, holding his own teapot, which was roughly equivalent to the governor arriving barefoot — and went mat to mat asking after everyone's health, their families, their sleep. He sat on Tavita's mat and admired her weaving. He gave her grandson a sweet biscuit and listened gravely to his opinions. He asked Paloma about his shoulder, which had been stiff since the cold snap, and pressed it with kind fingers, and said, "You'd tell me if anything troubled you, wouldn't you, boy? Anything at all. That's what I'm here for."

"Yes, sir," said Paloma, who had been telling this man nothing but comfortable lies since the night a dead courier washed up at his feet.

The searches themselves were almost polite. Things were moved — a bundle of nets shifted two fingers' width, a chest of shell tools opened and closed with its latch turned back the wrong way, the smell of lamp oil where no lamp had been filled. Once Paloma came back from the shallows to find his own diving kit repacked so neatly it was wrong; he packed it badly, always had, knots in the wrong places, and someone had re-tied them properly. Properly was the word that frightened him. Only one person on the outpost tied knots like a navy man pretending he wasn't one.

They didn't find the letter. Of course they didn't; it was in the hollow float, and the float hung among forty identical floats, and to a Company eye one diver's spare gear was exactly as interesting as another's. But knowing you are being searched is its own kind of drowning. You can hold your breath only so long before your body starts making decisions without you.

On the fifth day, Paloma caught himself checking the tide line twice before dawn, and realized he had stopped sleeping, and understood that if he went on like this he would do something stupid, loudly, in front of everyone. Which was precisely how the Company collected people.

* * *

Sefina cornered him at the washing rocks on the sixth day, which was fitting, because Sefina had been cornering him at the washing rocks since they were six years old and she had decided he needed saving from something.

"You're dying," she announced, wringing out a shirt with the violence of a woman who had already diagnosed him and found him guilty of concealment.

"I'm tired."

"You're tired the way a man is tired when he's been awake listening for footsteps." She snapped the shirt flat. "You dive worse than you did last season. Yesterday you came up early and you lied about why — you said the light, and the light was perfect, I was on the boat, Paloma, I have *eyes*. And you flinch when the clerk rings the bell. You used to love that bell. You used to say it meant dinner."

"It does mean dinner."

"Not at dawn it doesn't." She folded the shirt, set it down, and turned to face him fully, arms crossed, in the stance she had perfected somewhere around age nine and never retired. "Here is what I know. A stranger died on the flats three weeks ago and you were the one who found him, because you're always the one who finds things, it's your curse. Since then you've been carrying something. Not on you — you're too careful for that — but *in* you, the way you carry a cramp from deep water, all clenched up where nobody can rub it out."

Paloma looked at the water. The water offered no assistance whatsoever.

"Sef—"

"Don't 'Sef' me. I've known you since you were a wet little eel of a boy who cried when the gulls stole his bread. I watched you learn your letters off a naval captain like it was candy. I am the only person on this rock who knows you can *read*, because you trusted me with that, once, and I have kept it for ten years." Her voice dropped, and gentled, which was somehow worse. "So trust me with the rest. Whatever it is. I promise you my silence is better built than yours."

And there it was — the thing he had been avoiding since the courier died. Not the Company. Not Renwick's soft hands and softer questions. This: that keeping a secret alone was its own kind of lie, told daily, to the person who had shared every meal of his life. He had told himself he was protecting her. Standing there, he admitted what it actually was. He was protecting himself — from having to watch her face change when she knew what he was carrying, and what it would cost.

Trust, it turned out, was not a feeling. It was a decision you made with your stomach clenched and your mouth dry, and then lived inside afterward.

"The letter," he said. His voice came out rough. "Thorne's alive."

Sefina did not gasp. She went very still, the way she went still over a pearl bed when she spotted something worth the dive, and he understood that she had known — not the fact itself, but that there was a fact, some enormous one, and that it had been eating him alive. Stillness was her way of saying *go on, I will not startle it.*

"He's alive, and he's branded a traitor, and the letter says the Company runs guns under the pearls. Under our pearls. Under—" His throat closed. "Under shipments my mother asked about. Before she left."

"*Left,*" Sefina repeated softly, and he heard her hear it — the word turning over, showing its other side.

"That's not the worst of it. The worst is I promised him. Years ago, the night before his last voyage, I promised I'd carry any letter he ever sent, anywhere, no matter what. I was fourteen. I thought promises were like the tide — they just go out and come back and nothing changes." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "The ship leaves in three days. If the letter doesn't reach Governor Ives before the monsoon, it reaches no one, and Thorne dies for real this time, and everything he bled for sits in a hollow float until the rats find it."

Sefina was quiet for a long moment. Out on the flats, a heron stabbed at something small and unlucky.

"All right," she said.

"All right?"

"All right. You'll not carry it yourself — you're being watched, Paloma, don't argue, you tie your knots differently when you're frightened, I've been watching your knots too. But the pearl shipment goes aboard the *Fortunate Star* in barrels, sealed by the clerk, weighed by the clerk, and *never once* opened between here and the mainland, because opening a sealed pearl barrel mid-voyage is theft and the crew knows it." She picked up her basket, businesslike, already solving him. "Barrels get packed Thursday. I sort the small lots. Nobody watches me — I'm a girl with good eyes and no opinions, same trick you use."

Paloma stared at her. "You'd hide a traitor's letter inside Company property. In front of the clerk."

"I'd hide it inside a *pearl shipment*," she corrected. "Which is different, because the Company trusts its pearls more than it trusts its people, and that, my friend, is the funniest thing on this entire rock." Then the humor went out of her face, carefully, like a lamp being shuttered, and what was underneath was older and steadier than he had ever let himself see. "You carried this alone for three weeks because you thought that was courage. It isn't. Courage is handing it to someone and letting them help. Now — show me the letter's hiding place, and then forget I ever knew it."

He showed her. And standing there among the floats while she committed the arrangement to memory with two quick glances, Paloma felt the cramp in his chest loosen for the first time since the tide line — not gone, nothing was gone, but shared now, halved at least, the way a load halves when two people lift instead of one.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me yet." She shouldered her basket and nodded toward the countinghouse, where, at the top of the steps, Aldous Renwick stood watching the washing rocks with his teacup in his hand and his head tilted, pleasant as Sunday.

He was looking directly at them.

"Thursday," Sefina murmured, not turning her head, smiling her best empty smile. "Pray he stays curious about someone else until Thursday."
