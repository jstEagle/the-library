## 6. The Governor's Shadow

The news came, as most news did on Meridian Cay, by way of the laundry line.

Sefina heard it first—she always did, because the governor's own linens had been sent ahead from the *Resolute* to be washed in the outpost's hot springs, and the laundresses read everything that arrived folded in paper. By midmorning the whole island knew: Lady Ives, Colonial Governor of the Archipelago, was coming to audit the Meridian Trading Company's books, and she would land at the Cay on Thursday with two clerks, a maid, and a trunk full of ledgers of her own.

Paloma learned it secondhand, standing waist-deep in the shallows while Sefina wrung out a sheet and delivered it like a verdict.

"Thursday," she said. "Three days."

"Three days," he agreed, and then, because he could not help himself: "She'll want to see the pearl works. They always do. Governors come to see pearls the way priests come to see miracles—they don't believe until they've touched one."

"And Renwick will stand beside her the whole time," Sefina said, "smiling his Sunday smile, telling her exactly which questions not to ask." She snapped the sheet flat against the wind. "You know what an audit is, Paloma? It's a man showing you a locked room and calling it open."

He knew. He'd known since the letter had come apart under his fingers three nights ago, its contents now copied twice over into the vault behind his eyes where Thorne had taught him to keep things—*the mind is a warehouse, Mirabel; nothing gets in or out without your signature*. The Company smuggled arms under pearl weights. Renwick signed the manifests. And somewhere beyond the horizon, Captain Elias Thorne—executed, officially, two years past, with a cannon salute and everything—was alive and waiting for proof to reach the one person on these islands with the power to do something about it.

Lady Ives.

*Deliver it to her,* Thorne had written, *or deliver it to no one.*

Three days.

"I keep thinking," Paloma said, watching a crab sidle along the tide line as if it too were being watched, "that if I just walked up to her on the beach and handed it over, that would be the end of it. Simple."

"Simple," Sefina repeated.

"Like handing someone a coconut."

"Paloma." She lowered the sheet. "Renwick has men watching the shore road day and night. He searched the divers' quarters Tuesday while we were at the washing rocks—he went through my mother's trunk and left everything crooked because he thinks none of us can tell a straight line from a crooked one. If you walk up to Lady Ives holding anything at all, he will see it. He watches her the way a fisherman watches a float. Not because he loves her. Because she tells him when the water moves."

"So I do nothing."

"So you do nothing *obviously*." She wrung the sheet again, hard, as though strangling it might strangle the problem. "Governors don't take letters from strange boys on beaches. But governors—" and here a small, dangerous light kindled in Sefina's face, the same light she got when haggling, when winning, when doing anything at all with her whole clever heart— "*do* take gifts."

Paloma stared at her. Then at his hands, which were diver's hands, scarred and salted, and which had pulled a black pearl out of the deep last spring that the countinghouse had valued at more than his father's entire debt.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard the plan yet."

"It doesn't matter. That pearl is Company property. Every shell on this reef is Company property down to the sand it sleeps in. If I'm found with it—"

"If you're found with *a letter*, they hang you. If you're found with a pearl—" She shrugged, folding the sheet into a crisp white square. "You're a diver caught with a pretty stone. It's a flogging and a fine. And you won't be found with it, because it will already be in the Governor's dispatch case, wrapped in silk, with a note."

"A note."

"One line. In your best hand. The hand a certain captain taught you." Her voice gentled by a degree. "You said yourself she audits books. Give her a page worth auditing."

The wind came off the reef and pushed the smell of brine and drying linen over them both. Paloma thought about the promise—the real one, made years ago on a moonlit dock by a boy who hadn't understood what *no matter where* could cost. He thought about his mother, whose face he was beginning to lose, whose name kept surfacing in the ledger entries he'd memorized. He thought about Thursday.

"All right," he said. "Tell me the plan."

* * *

The plan, it developed, depended on vanity, bureaucracy, and Mrs. Okonkwo.

Mrs. Okonkwo kept the guest quarters and had done so through four factors and one brief, chaotic occupation by a rival concern, and she possessed the outpost's only supply of proper beeswax for polishing floors, which she guarded like a dragon. She also owed Sefina's mother a favor going back fifteen years, involving a wedding, a stolen pig, and details Paloma had never been permitted to learn.

By Wednesday evening, Mrs. Okonkwo had arranged two things: that the Governor's chambers would need fresh polishing after the sea voyage (they would not, but Lady Ives need never know), and that the boy who polished them would be Paloma Voss, who was small, quiet, and famously invisible.

"Invisible," Mrs. Okonkwo told him, pressing the wax tin into his hands like a sacrament, "is the most useful thing a person can be in this world. I have made an entire life of it. Don't waste it on honesty."

The second thing was harder. The black pearl sat in the countinghouse strongroom, catalogued, weighed, entered in the ledger under Renwick's initials. Getting it out meant getting into the strongroom, and getting into the strongroom meant—

"Me," said Sefina.

"*No.*"

"Renwick takes his supper at seven. The junior clerk, Tamu, takes his supper at half past, and Tamu has loved me since we were nine years old, which means at half past six he will be so busy rehearsing how to say good evening to me that he will forget keys exist." She said it lightly, but Paloma saw her knuckles whiten around her basket handle. "Ten minutes. You go in through the shell room, I hand you the key through the vent, you take the pearl, you leave the ledger entry alone—we are thieves, Paloma, not idiots—and I lock it behind you."

"And if Renwick comes back early?"

"Then I scream, you run, and we find out together what happens next." She met his eyes. "You taught me to swim in that lagoon when I was six and terrified. You held my head above water the whole way across and you didn't let go even when I clawed you. So." A breath. "I'm not letting go either. Take the key."

The strongroom smelled of ink, camphor, and old money. Paloma found the tray of unset stones by feel, in the dark, his diver's fingers reading surfaces the way other men read print. The black pearl lay third from the left, round as a moon and dark as the bottom of the sea, the kind of pearl that happened maybe once a generation and made grown merchants forget their manners.

He took it. He locked the drawer. His hands shook only a little, and only afterward, in the alley behind the shell room, where Sefina stood waiting with her heart presumably going as hard as his.

"Well?" she whispered.

"It's done."

"Then Thursday," she said, "we make history touch somebody else for once."

* * *

Lady Ives came ashore at ten o'clock Thursday morning in a launch flying the Crown's colors, and Paloma saw her first from the water, hanging off the side of a dive boat with the rest of the crew, pretending to sort shells.

She was smaller than he'd imagined—a compact woman in dove-grey, with a clerk on either side and an expression that took in the washing rocks, the drying racks, the divers, the countinghouse steps where Aldous Renwick waited in his best coat, all of it, in one long unhurried sweep, the way Paloma himself looked at a reef before diving. Reading the bottom before trusting it.

Renwick bowed. Lady Ives let him. Her handshake, even at forty yards, looked like a formality performed on a suspect.

Through the afternoon, the audit proceeded exactly as Sefina predicted. Renwick hovered. The clerks were shown ledgers—*his* ledgers, selected, pruned. Lady Ives asked questions in a voice pitched too low to carry across the compound, and Renwick answered each one before the echo of the last had died.

At five o'clock, Mrs. Okonkwo's arrangement bore fruit: word came that the Governor wished to inspect the pearl works before supper, and that the factor would be detained reviewing shipping schedules with the harbormaster—detained, Paloma noted, by a scheduling conflict that Mrs. Okonkwo may or may not have manufactured through channels he preferred not to imagine.

The pearl works were loud, wet, and dim. Perfect.

Paloma was stationed at the sorting table when she came through, flanked by clerks, her grey dress somehow untouched by the spray. She paused at his table because protocol required her to pause at every table, and he saw her glance flick over him—registering a diver's son, an indentured boy, furniture—exactly the look everyone gave him, the look he had spent nineteen years perfecting the art of receiving.

He opened his hand.

On his palm, in a scrap of silk, the black pearl drank the lamplight.

Something changed in Lady Ives's face. Not greed—something colder and better than greed. Appraisal. Her fingers hovered, did not touch. "That," she said quietly, "should be in a vault."

"It should be in the right hands, ma'am." His voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "It was pulled from the north reef last spring. There's no entry for it in any ledger on this island."

A silence. Behind her, one clerk shifted. Outside, gulls argued over the midden.

"That sounds like an accusation," she said.

"It sounds like a gift, ma'am. From someone who was taught his letters by a dead man." He set the silk bundle on the table between them and stepped back, hands open, empty, visible. "There's more where it came from. But not here, and not tonight, and not anywhere Mr. Renwick can count."

For a moment—one heartbeat, two—she studied him with those reef-reading eyes, and Paloma understood he was being measured for depth, for current, for whether he was worth diving for.

Then Lady Ives picked up the pearl, drew a dispatch case from under her arm, and tucked the silk inside it with the easy motion of a woman filing correspondence.

"The pearl works close at sundown, I understand," she said, aloud, briskly, for the clerks. "Good workmanship here." And then, softer, only for him, without looking at him: "My ship sails for the mainland Saturday dawn. There is a boy who brings the officers their coffee at first bell. He is deaf, and very trustworthy, and he is used to carrying things."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Mr.—?"

"Voss, ma'am. Paloma Voss."

"Mr. Voss. When the right hands ask you what else there is, you had better remember all of it." She turned back to her clerks. "Shall we see the drying racks?"

They moved on. The lamps hissed. Paloma stood at his sorting table with his heart slamming against his ribs like weather against a hull, and did not allow himself to smile, because Renwick had appeared at the far door of the pearl works and was scanning the room, teacupless, smiling his pleasant Sunday smile.

His gaze crossed Paloma's table. Paused. Moved on.

But Friday morning, when Paloma reported to the sorting shed, Tamu the junior clerk was waiting for him, pale as a landed fish, with two Company guards at his shoulders.

"They say the strongroom was opened Wednesday night," Tamu whispered, miserable, not meeting his eyes. "Paloma—Renwick wants every diver counted at morning muster. All of us. He says—" Tamu swallowed. "He says someone's been stealing, and he already knows who."
