## 7. Everything the Company Owns

Aldous Renwick had a gift, and the gift was this: he could look at any object in the world and see what it cost.

The brass lamp on his desk: four shillings, plus freight. The inkwell, chipped at the lip: eleven pence, and he'd written to complain about it twice. The pearl shipment currently sealed in his counting room—eleven crates, forty-two hundred marks of graded nacre—represented roughly the annual revenue of a mid-sized plantation, and every pearl in it had passed through Renwick's own gloved hands with the tenderness other men reserved for their children.

Which was why he noticed immediately that one crate weighed wrong.

Not by much. A few ounces. The sort of discrepancy a lesser man would wave away as damp wood or a clerk's sloppy tallying. But Renwick had spent thirty years learning that the world did not make small mistakes out of kindness, and so he opened the crate himself, alone, after midnight, with the shutters latched against the rising wind.

The pearls lay in their straw like eggs in a nest. Beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn into a false seam some diver had stitched with more patience than sense, sat a letter.

Renwick read it twice.

The first time, he read it the way an accountant reads: names, dates, ship manifests, amounts. The second time, he read it the way a man reads his own death warrant, which is to say very carefully indeed.

*Thorne.* Alive. Gathering evidence. Manifests from three years of shipments, copied in a hand Renwick knew intimately because he had taught that hand its numbers when the boy was fourteen and quick with figures and, Renwick had thought at the time, worth cultivating.

Paloma Voss.

Renwick set the letter down and looked at his lamp for a long moment. Four shillings, plus freight.

Now. There were two roads here, and Renwick was honest enough with himself—he prided himself on that honesty—to see both clearly. Road one: report the theft to the mainland office, let the Company's investigators come, watch them pull this island apart thread by thread until everything Renwick had built, everything he had *protected*, came apart with it. Investigations were floods. They did not care whose house they drowned.

Road two was simpler. Road two required only that the evidence cease to exist, and that someone else be holding something incriminating when the searching started. Someone already suspected. Someone already indebted. Someone whose family, conveniently, had a history.

Renwick took the letter to the iron stove and fed it to the fire piece by piece, watching each corner curl and blacken, and felt nothing whatsoever, except perhaps a mild professional regret about the stitching. It had been fine work. He would have liked to know who taught the boy needlework.

Then he unlocked the bottom drawer of his desk—the one that never opened, the one whose key lived on the same ring as the key to the counting room—and took out the ledger.

Not the public ledger. The *other* one. Every factor kept one; it was practically a professional qualification. In it lived the accounts that did not appear anywhere else: the payments that weren't recorded, the cargo that wasn't weighed, the people who had asked questions and what asking questions had cost them.

He turned to the Voss entries. He knew them well enough—he'd entered most of them himself—but tonight he read them differently, reading backward down the columns like a man retracing his steps through a dark house.

Debt of Tomas Voss, pearl diver, deceased. Assumed by widow, Marisol Voss. Debt serviced by labor. Standard.

And then, seven years ago, an entry he had made himself and not thought of since:

*M. Voss — inquiry re: manifest, brig *Cormorant*. Debt transferred to son. Account closed.*

Closed. Not paid. Not discharged. Closed the way you close a door on a room where something has been buried.

Marisol Voss had not run off with a boatman, as the outpost's gossips had decided within a fortnight and never revisited, because gossip prefers a story with a woman's fault in it. She had walked into this office with a question about the *Cormorant*'s manifest—a question about crates too heavy for pearls—and she had asked it of Aldous Renwick, and Aldous Renwick had done what the Company trained him to do, which was arithmetic.

One widow, silenced. One debt, transferred to a child old enough to dive. One problem, solved for less than the cost of the lamp on his desk.

Renwick closed the ledger. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; it was the one thing about himself he had never had cause to doubt.

Outside, the wind was picking up out of the southeast, early this year, and the palms along the compound wall bent and whispered like a congregation told bad news. Nine days until the last ship. Plenty of time.

He rang for the guards.

* * *

They came for Paloma at dawn, which he supposed was considerate of them, since dawn was when the divers were already awake and could all watch.

Two guards, then four, wading through the tide flats where he knelt checking his diving kit, and Sefina's voice behind him going sharp with alarm before he even understood. Then hands on his arms—not rough, exactly; the Company's men were rarely rough without instruction, and Renwick's instructions were always precise—and the weight of them pulling him upright while his mask straps dangled from his fingers and the morning went strange and tilted around him.

"Paloma Voss," said the taller guard, in the flat reciting voice of a man reading a list, "you are detained on suspicion of smuggling."

The word took a moment to find him. Smuggling. Him. As if they'd named the crime of another person entirely—as if history, having finally noticed his existence, had reached out and mislabeled him.

"Smuggling what?" he heard himself say, and was almost proud of how level it came out.

"That'll be determined," said the guard.

They marched him up through the compound past the drying racks, past the cooperage, past the line of divers standing motionless in the half-light with their masks pushed up on their foreheads, watching. Old Iosefa crossed his arms. Young Nali spat, elaborately, into the surf. Nobody spoke, but nobody looked away either, and Paloma found that he could not stop cataloguing their faces, because it occurred to him with terrible clarity that this might be the last time he saw any of them as anything but a memory.

Sefina broke ranks. Of course she did. She got one hand on his sleeve before a guard turned her back with a forearm across her chest, gentle as moving furniture.

"Say nothing," she said fiercely. "You hear me? Say *nothing* until—"

Until. Until what, she didn't say. There was no until. There was only the counting room ahead, its windows shuttered, and the figure waiting inside it.

Renwick sat behind his desk with the lamp lit against the grey morning, and he gestured Paloma into the chair opposite with something that might have been kindness in a different light, in a different life.

"Sit down, lad." He folded his hands. "I'm going to give you one chance, and I want you to understand that I take no pleasure in any of this."

Paloma sat. His heart was going like a drum in a procession, but his mind had gone strangely clear and cold, the way water does at depth. *Say nothing,* Sefina had said. But there was one thing he needed to know more than freedom, and he had carried the question for seven years without ever once daring to say it aloud, and now, with nothing left to lose, he found the words came easily.

"My mother," Paloma said. "What did you do to my mother?"

Something flickered across Renwick's face—there and gone, fast, like a fish under the surface. Not guilt. Paloma would have recognized guilt. This was closer to annoyance, the irritation of a man whose tidy accounts have been disturbed.

"I don't know what you mean," Renwick said.

"The *Cormorant*. Seven years ago. She came here and asked about the manifest, and you wrote it in your little book, and then—" Paloma's voice cracked, and he hated it, hated that grief still lived in his body alongside the rage, hated that they arrived together like weather. "She didn't leave. Did she. She never left this island."

"You've been reading stories," Renwick said mildly. "Captain Thorne tells lovely ones, I'm sure."

"She asked about crates too heavy for pearls." Paloma leaned forward. "Just like the letter says. Just like Thorne wrote. She figured it out first, didn't she? Before anyone. Before Thorne. And you—"

"And I what?" For the first time, the mildness dropped away, and Paloma saw the thing underneath, the cold clean machinery of the man. "Be careful, boy. You are nineteen years old and you owe this Company more than you will earn in ten lifetimes. Your mother made a similar error of judgment. Asking questions about cargo that isn't yours is a form of theft, and theft has a price. You're paying hers now. You've been paying it since you were twelve."

The words landed one by one, each with its own weight, and Paloma understood that Renwick meant them as mercy—an explanation, a courtesy, the way you tell a patient what the disease is called. That was somehow the worst part. Not cruelty. Arithmetic. His mother's whole life reduced to a line in a drawer, *account closed*, and the balance forwarded to her son.

He wanted to scream. What he did instead was worse, or better: he laughed. One short, cracked sound that seemed genuinely to startle Renwick, who had clearly prepared for weeping, for begging, for anything but laughter.

"You should burn your book," Paloma said. "Ledgers keep things alive. That's the whole point of them. Everything the Company owns, everything it owes—you write it down so it can't be forgotten. Well. My mother's in one of your books now, Mr. Renwick. So am I. And books get found."

For a moment neither of them moved. The lamp flame stood perfectly straight between them, and outside, faint under the wind, came the sound of the garrison bell beginning to ring, and the muffled voices of men gathering.

Renwick recovered first. He always would; that was his nature. He rose, straightened his coat, and pressed the bell-pull twice with the air of a man closing out a day's business.

"Take him to the ship," he said to the guards in the doorway. "Hold him below. If the governor asks, he's a smuggler caught with contraband—Thorne's confederate, nothing more." He paused at the window, watching the storm clouds stack themselves along the southern horizon like cargo waiting on a dock, and added, almost to himself, in the tone of a man noting a figure in a margin:

"He's his mother's son after all. Asking questions right up to the end."

They bound Paloma's wrists—not tightly; the Company was rarely wasteful with rope—and walked him down through the rain's first scattered drops toward the harbor, where the Company ship rode at anchor, black-hulled and patient, its hold open like a mouth.

And as the gangplank swayed beneath his bare feet, Paloma lifted his head and saw, far out past the reef where no ship had any business being, a sail running hard before the rising wind. Running for the islands. Running ahead of the monsoon.

He couldn't have said why his heart lurched—hope is a foolish thing to spend in chains—but it did, all the same.
