## 8. The Diving Bell

They kept me in the ship's hold, which was a kindness of a peculiar sort, because it meant I could hear the water.

Not the open sea—the harbor water, slapping against the hull in the slow rhythm I'd known since before I could walk. A pearl diver's son learns the sea's moods the way other children learn their mothers' faces. The water outside was saying what it always said in late monsoon season: *soon*. Soon the wind would turn. Soon the sky would go the color of a bruise and stay that way for weeks. Soon no ship would leave this archipelago at all.

I lay on a cot meant for a much smaller man and listened to the sea count down.

They had taken my boots, my knife, my diving kit—everything except the one thing they wanted most, which was the letter, and the letter wasn't on me at all. It was somewhere out in the world doing its work, and every hour I spent lying on this cot was an hour it traveled farther from Renwick's reach. I held onto that thought the way you hold onto a breathline when the current drags: not comfortably, but absolutely.

The hold smelled of bilgewater and old cargo. Somewhere above me, boots crossed the deck in patterns I came to know by heart. The sailors walked like sailors. The marines Renwick had brought aboard walked like men pretending the sea hadn't beaten them. And once or twice each day, there came a step I knew better than any other—measured, unhurried, the tread of a man who had never once in his life hurried because hurrying implied things could go wrong without his permission.

Aldous Renwick, coming to look at his prisoner. Or perhaps just to be seen looking. With Renwick it was often difficult to tell where the arithmetic ended and the man began, if indeed there had ever been a line between them.

* * *

He came on the third evening, carrying a lantern and a covered dish, and he set both down with the care of a man arranging pieces on a board.

"Paloma." He said my name the way he always had—warmly, familiarly, as though we were still the factor who sponsored my family's debt and the diver's son who brought him good pearls. That warmth had been real once. I believed that still, even now. It made everything worse, not better. "You look thin. Prison food is an insult to cooking everywhere. I've brought you something from my own table."

"You've brought me something from your own trap," I said. "But thank you for the plate."

Something flickered across his face—not anger. Renwick didn't do anger; anger was inefficient. It looked more like disappointment, the disappointment of an accountant finding an error in a ledger he'd trusted. "I taught your father to keep accounts," he said, sitting down on the crate across from me. "Did you know that? Your mother brought him to my office, this frightened boy from the eastern atolls who couldn't write his own name, and I sat with him evenings after work. Two years. By the end he could balance a shipment manifest better than men I'd paid a salary for twenty years."

"I didn't know that."

"He was proud of it. He used to say—" Renwick stopped himself, and for a moment the mask slipped far enough that I saw something almost human underneath. Then he smoothed it over, the way you smooth a bedsheet, and continued. "Paloma. Let us not waste each other's time with theater. You took a document from Lady Ives's dispatch case."

"I gave a gift to Lady Ives," I said. "The dispatch case was her business."

"The document was Captain Elias Thorne's. Thorne is a traitor under sentence of death, and whoever aids him shares his sentence." He opened the covered dish. Roast fish, rice, a wedge of lime. The smell made my mouth water and I hated it for that. "Eat. I want to talk while you eat. People think more clearly when they're eating, and less heroically."

"That's very specific."

"It's very true." He waited until I'd taken a bite—it seemed foolish not to; a drowned diver is no use to anyone—and then he folded his hands and became, visibly, the kindly factor again. It was like watching a man put on a coat. "Here is what I can offer. You recant. You tell Lady Ives, formally, that you placed the paper in her case under coercion—that Thorne's agents forced you, that you feared for your life. You give us what you know of where Thorne keeps himself. In exchange: your indenture is paid in full. Not voided—*paid*, by me personally, as a gesture of the Company's regard. You walk off this ship a free man with money in your pocket, and in five years nobody on this outpost will remember your name at all."

I chewed. I swallowed. I thought about my father balancing manifests by lamplight, and my mother, whose debt records Renwick had gone through looking for leverage and found instead a grave.

"You went into her file," I said.

Renwick's hands did not move. But something behind his eyes closed, quietly, like a shop shutter at the end of the day. "Whatever you read there—"

"She asked about the arms shipments." My voice came out steadier than I expected. "She dove for you for eleven years and she asked one question about what else was going into those crates, and you—" I stopped, because the next part needed air, and breathing felt suddenly like work. "My whole life I thought she left. That she chose the mainland over us. I built myself around that. Do you understand? Every morning I got up and worked your pearls and told myself, *at least she's free, wherever she is.*"

Renwick said nothing. He had the discipline of a man who knows silence is cheaper than apology.

"And she was dead," I said. "Or worse than dead, whatever your ledgers call it. She was a line item. She was a rounding error."

"Paloma—"

"No." I set down the fork. My hand was shaking, but the voice held. "You asked what I know of where Thorne keeps himself. Here is what I know: I know you're afraid of him, and I know why. I know that a letter written in his hand reached a colonial governor despite every agent you have, and I know that frightens you more than the letter itself. Because if he can send letters from the grave, Mr. Renwick, then so can everyone else you've ever silenced. So can she."

For the first time since I'd known him, Aldous Renwick looked small. It lasted only a moment—he gathered himself like a man retrieving dropped coins—but I saw it, and I would keep seeing it for the rest of my life.

"You are making a catastrophic error," he said quietly, standing. "Thorne will not save you. The governor will not save you. When the monsoon passes, this ship sails, and the mainland has ways of making men forget their convictions. Think about it. I'll return tomorrow."

He collected the lantern. He left the fish, which was either courtesy or strategy, and I ate it anyway, because spite needs fuel.

* * *

That night the storm found us early.

It came up the channel like something with intent, and the *Meridian's Pride* began to roll at anchor, groaning in a language all ships share. I lay listening, mapping the sounds the way I'd map a reef. Hull. Chain. Chain again—a long metallic complaint, taut and straining, rising from the bow.

And underneath it, fainter, threaded through the wind: voices.

I knew those voices. You don't spend nineteen years among forty divers and not know them the way you know weather. Old Manu's rumble. The twins, Feru and Feri, whispering in unison the way they did everything. And cutting through it, sharp and low and utterly unmistakable:

Sefina.

I pressed my ear to the hull. Foolish, probably. Certainly undignified. I did it anyway.

"—third link from the crown, that's the one I showed you. Saw through the pin, don't cut the chain, a cut chain tells them everything and a sawn pin tells them nothing until it's too late—"

Feru's murmur. Feri's answering murmur. Manu: "*Boy's right, the pin's the trick of it.*"

They were on the water. In this. In dugouts, in the dark, in the leading edge of a monsoon—to get at an anchor chain, on a Company ship, for me.

I should have been terrified. Part of me was. But another part, some deeper chamber of me that I'd only lately discovered I possessed, was laughing. Because here was the joke of it, the beautiful, ridiculous joke: Aldous Renwick had spent three days explaining to me how invisible I was. How forgettable. How easily the world would close over me like water over a dropped pearl. And he had never once considered that invisibility was not only his weapon. It was ours. Forty divers who weighed cargo and cleaned holds and were looked through daily by every factor and clerk and marine on this outpost—who could sit in a boat beside a warship's anchor chain and be seen by no one at all.

We had been invisible our whole lives. Tonight we chose to be.

I heard the pin go—I swear I heard it, a small bright *tick* carried up through the hull like a coin dropped in church—and then the wind arrived in earnest, and the *Meridian's Pride* began to drag.

* * *

What happened next happened quickly and badly and gloriously. The anchor, its heart sawn through, parted company with its dignity somewhere in the third hour of the blow, and the ship swung and slid and grounded herself with a shudder I felt in my teeth, hard aground in the shallows where no tide this season would float her. Above, chaos: shouted orders, running feet, a lantern falling and rolling, someone crying that the cable had failed, someone else crying that cables don't fail, someone else crying that both of them should shut up and start bailing.

I sat on my cot in the dark hold and grinned like an idiot at absolutely no one.

Because I heard the other thing too, the thing the crew was too panicked to notice. Out past the grounding screech of the hull, threading through the storm: dugout paddles. Dozens of them, quick and practiced, fading away toward shore. And beneath the wind, if you knew how to listen—forty divers had taught me how to listen—you could catch the sound of people laughing.

By morning the storm owned the sky entire, the ship lay broken-backed on the bar, and the transport to the mainland was, by any honest reckoning, cancelled.

Which meant that when the hatch at the top of the ladder banged open and daylight poured down—gray, violent, rain-lashed daylight—and a silhouette stood framed against it holding a cutlass and wearing the uniform of a colonial marine, I was the calmest man on that ship.

"Name," demanded the silhouette.

I stood. I stretched. I thought about my mother, and about a promise, and about forty invisible people in leaking boats.

"Mirabel," I said. "My name is Mirabel. And I believe you're looking for me."
