## 10. Mirabel

The governor's writ arrived on the third morning after the storm, and it was read aloud in the drying yard because there was no building left on the outpost with a roof that didn't leak.

Lady Ives stood on the packing table herself—she refused the crate Sefina dragged over for her, saying her boots had survived worse than fish guts—and read the document in a clear voice that carried all the way to the tide line. Every indenture held by the Meridian Trading Company on the archipelago: voided. Every debt recorded against a diver's name: struck through, as if a great pen had come down from the mainland and crossed them out one by one.

Paloma listened with his hands very still at his sides. He had spent nineteen years watching numbers move in other people's ledgers—numbers that said he owed, that his father had owed, that his mother had owed—and now a woman he'd met exactly twice was reading those numbers into nothing.

The divers were quiet in the way they were quiet about everything important. Old Maru wept without any change in his expression, tears running down into his beard while his face stayed perfectly composed, the way a man cries when he has practiced not crying for decades. Sefina grabbed Paloma's arm and dug her nails in and did not let go for the whole reading.

When it was done, Lady Ives folded the writ and looked out at them—at the divers, the sorters, the children running between the racks of shell—and said only, "You were never the debt. The debt was theirs." Then she stepped down off the table and went back to work, because governors who arrive with marines during monsoon season do not have time for speeches.

* * *

Renwick went out on the first ship after the storm, under guard, in irons that Paloma suspected had been borrowed from the very smuggling cargo Renwick had once shipped in pearl crates. He did not look at anyone as he boarded. But at the gangway he stopped—one moment, half a heartbeat—and Paloma, standing close enough to see it, understood that the factor was looking at nothing at all. Not at the outpost. Not at the people. At the arithmetic of what he had lost, running the sum one last time and finding, for the first time in his life, that it did not balance.

Sefina spat into the water after the ship was gone. Several others did too. It was, Paloma thought, the most honest eulogy the man would ever get.

* * *

Thorne found him at the reef flat two days later, at low tide, when the whole world went shallow and silver and you could walk a mile out over sand that usually lived under six feet of sea.

"You walk like your mother," Thorne said, by way of greeting.

"I walk like someone who knows where the stonefish live."

"That too." The captain lowered himself onto a coral head with the careful stiffness of a man whose body had been broken in more places than the war had officially reported. His scarred cheek caught the light. Up close, alive, he was smaller than the ghost Paloma had carried for two years, and somehow more real for it. "Ives sails for the mainland tomorrow. I sail with her. There's a tribunal, and then there's whatever comes after tribunals, which is usually more tribunals." He turned the black pearl over in his fingers—the one Paloma had given the governor, returned now, its chain snapped in the storm. "I could use someone at my side who reads fast, dives deeper than any man I've met, and has already proven he can put a letter where it needs to go."

Paloma watched the water move across the flats. A ray went gliding over the sand below them, a shadow pretending to be a bird.

"You taught me letters," Paloma said. "Do you remember the first word?"

"Of course."

"Liar."

"It was *pearl*," Thorne said, wounded. "I have the slate marks burned into my memory. You wrote it eleven times before you believed me that the *a* went the other way."

"The second word was *debt*. Third was *owed*. Fourth was *indenture*, which took me a week." Paloma smiled, but it came out crooked. "You taught me to read inside a ledger, Captain. Everything I know how to say, I learned how to say in their handwriting. I think—" He stopped. Started again. "I think if I stand next to you, I'll spend the rest of my life being the boy who delivered your letter. And it was a good letter. It was the best thing I'll ever carry. But it was yours."

Thorne was quiet for a long time. Out past the reef, the monsoon was dying; the clouds had gone from iron to mere slate, and there was even, unbelievably, a stripe of gold along the western horizon like something healing.

"When you were fourteen," Thorne said finally, "you asked me why I kept sailing. Do you remember what I told you?"

"Because history doesn't happen to men who stay home."

"And do you believe it yet?"

"I believe history happens everywhere," Paloma said. "It happened here. It was happening the whole time, in the ledgers, in the debt books, in whatever they wrote down about my mother when she asked her questions. I just wasn't allowed to read it." He looked up. "So no. I'm not coming to stand at your side. I'm going to find out what else is written down. All of it. Every archive, every warehouse manifest, every island where a woman disappeared and got called an abandonment. I want to read the whole ugly book."

Thorne studied him for a long moment, and then—he laughed. Not much. Just enough.

"Your mother used to say you'd be trouble someday," he said. "She said it the way other people say *blessing*."

"She called me Mirabel."

"I know. I heard her. A hundred times, on this exact flat, when you were small enough to ride on her shoulders." Thorne pressed the black pearl into Paloma's palm and closed his fingers around it. "It means *looked-for*, in the old tongue. Did you know that? Not *found*. Looked-for. She chose it on purpose, I think. Mothers do that."

* * *

The ship was called the *Kindly Tide*, which everyone agreed was either a joke or a prayer, and she was taking on water casks and passengers at the main quay. Sefina walked him there with a sack of dried fish she insisted he take—"for dignity, so you board like a person with provisions and not like a stowaway"—and neither of them talked for most of the way, because some conversations are finished before they're started.

At the gangway she stopped.

"If you find anything about her," she said, "you send it to me. Not a summary. The actual paper. I want to hold it."

"You'll get the actual paper."

"And if you find out she's—" Sefina's jaw worked. She was the bravest person Paloma had ever known, and she still couldn't finish the sentence, so he finished it for her.

"Then you'll get that too. Whatever it is. Truth doesn't get to be picky anymore."

Sefina hugged him hard enough to crack a rib, or so he claimed loudly enough that three sailors laughed, and then she pushed him toward the gangway before he could see her face properly, which was her way of being kind.

Old Maru pressed something into his hand at the rail: a small, worn cowrie on a cord. "For the deep," he said. "It remembers the way home even when you don't."

And then the lines came off, and the *Kindly Tide* leaned into the channel, and the outpost got smaller behind them—the drying racks, the shattered quay, the reef flat where a dead man had once taught a debt-boy his letters—all of it shrinking into a green smudge, then a rumor, then gone.

Paloma stood at the stern rail until the sun went down. In his pocket: a cowrie, a black pearl, and a letter of introduction in Lady Ives's own hand, addressed to an archivist in the capital whose name Thorne had given him with the warning that the man was "difficult, brilliant, and possibly bribable, in ascending order."

A sailor beside him asked his name for the manifest.

Paloma looked at the water. Nineteen years of being Voss, son of a debt, a number in a column, a boy nobody bothered to notice. Behind him, all of it. Ahead of him, warehouses full of paper, islands full of silence, and somewhere in all of it—maybe—a woman who had once lifted her son above the tide line and given him a word that meant *looked-for*.

"Mirabel," he said. "Just Mirabel."

The sailor wrote it down without a second glance, and that, Paloma thought, grinning at the darkening sea, was the whole miracle of it: for the first time in his life, his name was his own to give.

The *Kindly Tide* ran east through the night, and ahead of her, waiting in the archives of the capital like a fuse waiting for a flame, lay everything the Company had buried.

He was coming to dig it up.
