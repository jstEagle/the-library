## 1. Salt and Ink

The ink was the first thing Yara Okafor touched each morning, and the last thing she touched at night, and in six years she had learned to read a day's fortune in its behavior. Thick ink meant humidity coming off the water, which meant rain by evening, which meant the harbor master would be foul-tempered and the tally clerks slow. Thin, watery ink meant someone had been diluting the pot again to stretch the supply, and she would have to say nothing about it because saying something made you noticed.

Being unnoticed was her profession, though no one at the Meridian Trading Company would have described it that way. On the company rolls she was a clerk, third class, indentured — six years served of seven — competent with figures, unremarkable in every particular. She had built that unremarkableness the way other people built houses: plank by plank, season by season, until it could hold the weight of everything she kept inside it.

She arrived at the counting house at half past six, an hour before anyone else, because arriving early looked like diligence and also let her open the ledgers herself, first, before any other eyes had been on them. The room smelled of salt worked deep into the timber, and of lamp oil, and of the tamarind sweets old Nwosu kept hidden in his desk drawer as if the whole floor didn't know. Nine desks in rows, windows facing the harbor, and above the door a painted motto in letters gone brown with age: ACCURACY IS HONOR.

Yara had thought about that motto more than most. Accuracy, in her experience, was not honor. It was a knife that cut whoever held it.

She lit the lamps, set out the day's ledger — inbound shipments, the barque *Sable Ann* due from the northern beds — and began the small ritual of erasure that constituted her real work. Not forgery; nothing so dramatic. Just tidiness. A shipment of shell recorded as arriving on the fourteenth when it had come on the eleventh. A weight adjusted here, a duty recalculated there. Small mercies of arithmetic that kept the company's books smooth and, folded inside that smoothness like a seed in fruit, kept certain other numbers invisible: the extra mouths fed in a settlement that did not exist, the rice bought with coin earned on paper that said she earned less than she did.

Her mother called it sweeping footprints. Her uncle Emeka called it living like a crab — sideways, always, never straight at anything. Yara thought of it as simply being careful, the way a diver is careful about air. You did not think of the surface every second. You trusted your discipline, and you counted.

By seven the other clerks drifted in — Patel first, always, nodding to her without really seeing her, then the twins from the customs annex, then Nwosu wheezing up the stairs with his coat over his arm despite the heat. Mr. Calloway came last, at eight sharp, smelling of bay rum and coffee, his boots striking the boards in a rhythm Yara could have set a clock by.

"Okafor." He never waited for her to look up. "The Sable Ann manifest. I want it reconciled before noon."

"It will be, sir."

"And the duty schedule for the northern beds. The assessor wants clean columns this time."

"Clean columns," she repeated, which was a sentence that meant different things to different listeners, and Calloway passed on to his office without a glance back. That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of her life: he asked for things that could be made true by adjustment, and she made them true, and neither of them ever used a word plainer than *reconciled*.

She bent over the Sable Ann's papers. Pearls, forty-two pounds declared. Shell, three hundred. Crew of nineteen, all listed, all accounted for. Somewhere in those columns, if she went looking — and she had learned not to go looking — there would be the gap where a second cargo rode: the goods that came ashore at night at the company's private jetty, the crates that never met an assessor's eye. She knew they existed the way you know a house has a cellar. You lived on the upper floors and kept your eyes off the trapdoor.

At ten o'clock she carried the reconciled manifest to the harbor office, walking the long way around the fish market because the long way passed the well where Orchard women drew water, and though she never stopped, never spoke, never let her eyes rest longer than courtesy allowed, she could count them. Four women this morning. All present. All safe. Mama Adaeze was not among them — she came later in the week, market days only, wrapped so thoroughly in indigo that even her neighbors in the shantytown sometimes mistook her for a stranger — but Kofi might be near the boats. Sixteen-year-old boys were always near boats.

He wasn't. She felt the small cold drop of that, the way you feel a stair missing in the dark, and told herself it was nothing. Boys wandered. Boys fished. Kofi had been restless all season, chafing against the Orchard's tight radius the way a kite chafes against string, and restlessness was not danger. Restlessness was just youth with nowhere legal to put itself.

Still, her hand checked the satchel at her hip without her deciding to — the satchel where she kept the passbook that said she was Yara Okafor, clerk, indentured, registered, legitimate — and finding it there she felt the day steady again.

That was the trick of it, she thought, climbing the harbor office steps into the smell of tar and rope. Fear was manageable if you gave it a routine. Check the satchel. Count the women at the well. Know where everyone slept. A life built on concealment was still a life, and hers had shelves in it, and lamplight, and the small pleasures of neat figures agreeing with one another.

She handed the manifest across the counter. The harbor clerk stamped it without reading it, because nobody read anything, because nobody had ever bothered to look closely at anything on this coast, and Yara walked back to the counting house under a sky the color of tin, feeling the particular satisfaction of a morning in which nothing had happened.

Nothing happening was, she understood, the most expensive commodity on the pearling coast. She paid for it daily, in vigilance, and considered it money well spent.

* * *

The Orchard did not appear on any map.

It lay east of the harbor, past the rendering yards, where the mangroves thickened and the ground went soft and the city's attention simply gave out — a scatter of shelters built from driftwood, salvaged planking, corrugated iron rusted to the color of dried blood. Forty-odd families, maybe more; no one counted, because counting was how people got found. They were the unregistered: laborers who had jumped ship or been put ashore when their contracts soured, divers whose passes had expired, families who had crossed water on vessels that never appeared in any log. The colonial office called them irregulars. The company called them a nuisance. Among themselves, they called it the Orchard because the first family to settle there had planted breadfruit and guava among the stumps, and because a thing with a gentle name is harder to hate and harder, too, to hunt.

Yara went at dusk, twice a week, carrying rice and oil in a basket under fish and greens, taking the fisherman's path along the creek where the mud hid her footprints by morning. She had done it so many times the route ran through her body like a prayer: past the rendering yard wall, left at the dead casuarina, down to the water, along the stakes, up through the mangrove roots. Twenty minutes if the tide was right.

Tonight the tide was right, and the guava trees were heavy, and the smell of woodsmoke and frying onions rose to meet her before she saw the first roofline. Children ran the lanes between the shelters — barefoot, quick, loud in the way children are allowed to be loud when no official is listening. An old man mended a net by lamplight. Someone was singing, low, a working song from a coast none of these children had ever seen.

"You're late." Emeka materialized beside her the way he always did, out of shadow, a lean gray-templed man with a fisherman's forearms and a magistrate's eyes. "I was about to send Kwame after you."

"The harbor office was slow."

"The harbor office is always slow. That's why we chose you for errands and not me." He took the basket from her, weighing it with a professional hand. "Rice is short."

"Rice is short everywhere. I'll bring double Friday."

They walked together down the lane toward her mother's shelter, and Yara let herself do what she only permitted herself here: she looked openly. At the cooking fires. At the laundry strung between poles. At the little school that met in the evenings, where a woman named Grace taught letters to children who officially did not exist, chalk on a board propped against a boat hull. It was, Emeka liked to say, a whole town pretending to be weather. Here and gone. Unremarked upon.

Adaeze was outside her shelter, grinding pepper, and the sound of the stone was so familiar that Yara's chest tightened with a love that had nowhere safe to announce itself. Her mother looked up, and her face did the thing it always did — the quick flicker of joy mastered into calm, because joy expressed too loudly was a kind of testimony.

"You've eaten?" Adaeze asked.

"At the house. Mama, sit, I'll finish that."

"I'm not an invalid." But she moved aside, and Yara knelt at the grinding stone, and for a while they worked in the comfortable silence of people who share a language beyond words. Around them the Orchard settled into evening: fires banked, songs fading, the tide breathing in the dark beyond the mangroves.

"Kofi's not back yet," Adaeze said at last, lightly, too lightly.

Yara's hands went still on the stone. "Back from where?"

"He goes fishing. With the Adeyemi boys."

"In the dark?"

"It's cooler in the dark. And the fish—" Adaeze stopped herself, and Yara heard the stop, heard the shape of the sentence her mother had decided not to finish. *And the fish are better where the patrols don't watch.* Fishing needed no license if you fished for yourself. But the reefs where the good fish ran were the same reefs where the pearl beds ran, and the pearl beds were watched, and a boy in the water at night was a boy who could be taken, questioned, made to talk.

"He's careful," Adaeze said.

"He's sixteen."

"He's sixteen and he watches you carry this family on your back and he wants to be a man about it." Her mother's voice stayed level, but her hands had stopped moving too. "I know my son, Yara. I know what he's thinking before he thinks it."

Yara looked toward the dark line of the creek, where the lanterns of the patrol boat sometimes slid past like slow yellow eyes. "Tell him the water can wait. Tell him we're fine."

"We're fine," Adaeze agreed, in the tone of a woman reciting a fact she had decided to believe. "We're fine, and next year your indenture ends, and we'll find a way to make it legal, all of us, some way that hasn't occurred to anyone yet." She resumed grinding. The stone turned. The pepper's bite rose sharp and clean into the smoke. "Go on, then. Before the light's fully gone. And Yara—"

"Mm?"

"Walk the long way home. Past the point. I don't like the creek path lately. There have been strangers asking the fishermen about tides and channels, and I don't like it."

Yara stood, brushing the pepper from her skirt. "What kind of strangers?"

"Official-looking. One of them had a book."

Everyone had books. Ledgers, manifests, tide tables. But something in her mother's voice put a hairline crack through the evening's comfort, and Yara found herself cataloguing, automatically, the week's small anomalies: the harbor clerk stamping without reading, yes, but also the new noticeboard outside the customs annex, papered over before she'd reached it. The way Nwosu had asked, idly, whether she'd ever been interviewed by a government survey. The way Calloway had seemed, these past days, almost cheerful.

"I'll walk the long way," she said.

She kissed her mother's cheek, tasted salt and pepper and woodsmoke, and went down through the mangroves with the basket light on her arm and the tide whispering at the stakes. Behind her the Orchard folded itself back into darkness, a town becoming weather again, and ahead of her the city glittered along the harbor front, all its lamps lit, all its records waiting.

She was halfway along the point path, the company's clock tower just visible against the last of the light, when she saw the figure standing at the seawall.

A woman. Alone, which was unusual; dressed plainly but in clothes that fit too well for a local, a straw hat against the dying sun, and in her hands — open, consulted, annotated in a small quick hand — a book. Not a tide table. Not a ledger. The pages were ruled in columns, and the woman was writing in them while she looked, unhurried, thorough, at the harbor, at the fish market, at the shanty edge where the city's attention frayed into mangrove.

Then the woman turned and looked directly at the point path. Directly, Yara would have sworn afterward, at her — a long, level, considering look, the look of someone counting something — before she closed her book, tucked it under her arm, and walked away toward the customs annex with the stride of a person who had business with the government.

Yara stood in the darkening with her heart going hard against her ribs, telling herself it was nothing. A surveyor. A missionary. Anyone.

But the word her mother had used sat in her ear like a burr, and would not brush out.

*A book.*
