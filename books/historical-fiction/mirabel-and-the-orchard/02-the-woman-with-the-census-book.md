## 2. The Woman with the Census Book

The woman came back on a Tuesday, which Yara decided was the worst possible day for it. Tuesdays were ledger days — the long afternoons when Mr. Calloway retreated to his inner office with the port authority's correspondence and left the counting house to her, and she could work in the particular silence she liked best: the scratch of her own pen, the tick of the brass clock, the sea breathing against the pilings below the floorboards.

She was halfway through reconciling the April manifest of the *Violet Crane* when the shadow crossed the window.

Not a ship's rigging shadow, not a gull. A person's shadow, moving slowly along the quay in front of the Meridian Trading Company's counting house, pausing at each doorway as though reading something written there. Yara looked up from her columns and saw her again — the same woman from the point path, the same grey travelling dress gone limp with heat, the same book under her arm.

This time Yara had a better view, and a longer one, and what she saw did not comfort her.

The woman stopped at the door of the chandlery next door. She spoke to the chandler's boy, who pointed. She wrote something down. She moved on to the rope-walk, where she spoke to three men sitting in the shade, and two of them pointed — toward the harbor master's office, toward the boarding houses, toward, at one moment, the very window behind which Yara sat. The third man shrugged in the elaborate way of a man being asked questions he had no interest in answering.

Then the woman came to the counting house door, and Yara had four seconds in which to decide what her face would do.

She made it do nothing. Six years of practice went into that nothing: the clerk's face, mild and faintly bored, the face of a person whose whole attention belongs to figures. She kept her eyes on the *Violet Crane*'s manifest as the bell over the door turned, and she listened to the footsteps cross the floor — unhurried, deliberate, the steps of someone who counted her paces the way Yara counted barrels.

"Good morning," said the woman. "I'm looking for whoever keeps the household registers."

Yara looked up as if surprised, which was itself a small performance; she had known the woman was coming since the shadow touched the glass.

"The port authority keeps those," she said. "Two streets up. The blue shutters."

"I've been to the port authority." The woman smiled. It was a nice smile, even, unremarkable, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "They were very helpful about ships. Less helpful about people. I'm told the trading companies keep their own rosters of employees and their dependents. For insurance purposes."

"We keep employment rolls," Yara said. "Names, wages, terms of indenture. Nothing about households."

"May I see them?"

"They're company property." Yara let a beat pass, then added the softening note, the clerkly courtesy that made refusal sound like procedure rather than fear. "Mr. Calloway would have to authorize it. He's in until five, if you'd like to leave your name."

The woman took from her pocket a card instead of speaking, and set it on the counter between them with two fingers, the way one lays down a playing card one intends to win with.

**MIRABEL HALE. CENSUS COMMISSIONER. OFFICE OF THE COLONIAL SECRETARY.**

Yara read it twice. The paper was good stock, the printing crisp, the ink still sharp at the edges. New cards. Recently printed. A commission that had arrived within the month.

"You're taking a census?" Yara said. "Of the port?"

"Of everyone in it." Mirabel Hale looked around the counting house with an appraiser's eye — the ledgers ranked behind their grille, the sample pearls in their velvet tray, the long counter worn pale by sixty years of elbows. "Population, dwellings, occupations, dependents. Every soul accounted for and every soul assigned a name and a place. That's the purpose of a census, isn't it? To make certain no one is invisible."

Something cold walked up Yara's spine and stood there.

"That will take you all year," she said, and heard with relief how flat her voice stayed.

"It will take me as long as it takes." Mrs. Hale picked up her card, considered it, and left it lying on the counter after all. "Keep it. You may be able to tell me who else I should speak to. You strike me as a person who knows this port thoroughly."

"I know ledgers," Yara said. "Ledgers are simpler than people."

Mrs. Hale laughed — a real laugh, short and warm, gone almost before it arrived — and for one unguarded instant her face was younger, tired, human. Then it closed again like a fan.

"Ledgers," she said, "are exactly what I'll want to talk to you about eventually. Not today." She paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and turned back with the air of a woman remembering a courtesy. "What did you say your name was?"

"I didn't say."

"No," Mrs. Hale agreed pleasantly. "You didn't. But the employment roll will say, won't it? Yara Okafor. Clerk. Six years' service." She smiled her even smile. "I read the noticeboard outside the customs annex. It lists the companies and their senior local staff. You were on it."

Yara said nothing. There was nothing to say that would not be a second lie stacked on a first, and she could feel already that this woman collected lies the way some collectors collected shells — patiently, by the shore, one at a time.

"Good day, Miss Okafor. I expect we'll speak properly soon."

The bell turned over. The footsteps receded along the quay, pausing, Yara did not need to look to know, at the fish market, at the mission hall, at every doorway where a person might be found and counted.

She sat for a long while without writing anything. The clock ticked. The *Violet Crane* waited in her columns, forty-one crates lighter than its manifest claimed, and she thought, with a sensation like a stone dropping through still water: *a census counts everyone. That is the entire purpose of a census.*

And somewhere north of the tanneries, past the lime kilns, in a fold of scrubland the maps marked only as *waste ground*, there were eleven souls the census had never been allowed to count, and every one of them had spent six years being grateful for it.

* * *

At noon she climbed the outside stair to the flat roof of the counting house, where the company stored its empty water casks, and ate her rice in the shade of the awning with the harbor spread out below her. From here she could see most of the port at once, laid out like a diagram: the customs annex, the mission, the market sheds, the long curve of the beach where the dhows careened, the jetty where the mail steamer would call in ten days. And beyond the tannery smoke, if she stood at the western rail and leaned, the green smudge of trees that hid everything that mattered.

Mrs. Hale was below, working the market sheds. Yara watched her move from stall to stall with her book open, and what unsettled her most — more than the card, more than the name she had produced from a noticeboard — was *how* the woman asked her questions. Yara had seen officials before. Officials swept through like weather: loud, brief, interested in bribes or cargo, gone by nightfall. This woman did not sweep. She *lingered*. She wrote things down. When the fish seller answered, she asked a second question, and when he answered that, she asked a third, and Yara, watching from thirty feet above, could see the fish seller's shoulders change — could see him go from amused to puzzled to careful, the way men did when they realized the conversation was not the conversation they'd thought it was.

Nobody had ever bothered to ask the port's poor a third question. That was the whole architecture of Yara's life: built on the universal official conviction that the poor, the brown, the unregistered were beneath the dignity of inquiry. Six years of safety resting on other people's laziness.

And now here was a woman who was not lazy, walking stall to stall with a book, asking third questions.

Yara finished her rice without tasting it and went back down.

* * *

Calloway emerged from his inner office at half past four, smelling of gin masked with clove lozenges, and found her balancing the *Violet Crane* column.

"Finished?" he said.

"Balanced to within two crates," Yara said. "I'll correct it tomorrow."

"Correct it tonight." He dropped into the chair across from her desk, which he never did unless something was wrong, and laced his fingers over his waistcoat. "There's a woman going round the port. Census commissioner, out of the Colonial Secretary's office. Name of Hale."

"She came here today," Yara said. "She wanted the household registers."

Something flickered across Calloway's face, quick as a fish turning. "And you told her?"

"That we keep employment rolls only. That you'd have to authorize anything further."

"Good. Good girl." He said it automatically, the way he said it about balanced columns, and Yara felt the familiar small disgust at herself for receiving it as praise. He was quiet a moment, turning his signet ring. "Did she say why now? Why this year?"

"The cards are newly printed. I'd guess the commission is new."

"Mm." Calloway looked out the window at the darkening quay, and Yara watched him think, and did not like any of what she saw there. Calloway was not a clever man, but he was a careful one, and careful men with smuggling debts were men who noticed when governments started counting. "A new administration wants numbers," he said finally. "Numbers mean assessments, assessments mean levies, levies mean—"

"Paperwork," Yara said.

"Revenue," Calloway corrected, with the ghost of a smile. "But paperwork too. A great deal of paperwork." He stood, brushed his trousers, and paused at the door of his office. "Okafor. If that woman comes back — and she will come back — you're to send for me before you answer anything. Anything at all. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"And Okafor—" He hesitated, and for a moment the mask of the supervisor slipped and Yara saw underneath it something she recognized from her own mirror on bad nights: fear. "Whatever you know of our arrangements, whatever appears in those books — a census is not an audit, but it can become one. Keep the books clean-looking. That's all I ask. Clean-looking."

He went into his office and shut the door.

Yara sat alone in the counting house with the lamps lit and the tide knocking softly at the pilings, and she understood, with terrible clarity, that the danger had just changed shape. All morning she had feared the census woman because of what a count might find in the Orchard — eleven names, her mother's voice, Kofi laughing at the water tanks, her father's grave beneath the frangipani. Now she saw that the same count might reach into the counting house, into the *Violet Crane*'s missing forty-one crates, into six years of entries she herself had copied in her own neat hand at Calloway's direction, entries that existed nowhere but in these books and in her memory.

One woman with a book. Two secrets, and both of them hers.

She locked the ledgers in their cabinet, wound down the clock, and put out the lamps. On the counter where Mrs. Hale had left it, the card still lay, white as a sail in the lamplight. **CENSUS COMMISSIONER. OFFICE OF THE COLONIAL SECRETARY.** Yara should leave it. Leaving it was the honest clerk's response; leaving it said, *I am nobody, I have nothing to hide, come and ask your third questions of someone else.*

She left it.

She was three steps from the door before she turned back, took the card off the counter, and slid it into the pocket of her skirt — telling herself, as the lock clicked behind her and the evening came down warm and salted over the harbor, that she only wanted to know the woman's full title, in case she needed to report her.

She knew the full title already. She had read it twice.

She walked home the long way, past the fish market and the mission hall, retracing Mrs. Hale's afternoon in reverse, and at the mouth of the lane above the tanneries she stopped, as she always stopped, to check the road behind her. Empty. The port settling into its evening noises, cicadas and crockery and a gramophone wheezing somewhere, the ordinary sounds of ordinary days.

Except that tomorrow, she was nearly certain, the woman with the book would find the blue shutters of the port authority inadequate and start asking about the waste ground north of the lime kilns. And Yara would have to decide, before that happened, whether to warn her mother — and how to warn her of a thing that had no shape yet, only a feeling, cold and patient, walking the quay with a book under its arm, counting.
