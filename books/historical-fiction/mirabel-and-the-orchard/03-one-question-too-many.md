## 3. One Question Too Many

The interview notice arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside the morning's correspondence between an invoice for copra and a complaint about rusted chain. It was written on Colonial Office paper, which was how I knew it mattered before I read a word of it. Company paper was cheap and grey; government paper had a watermark you could only see against the light, like a bruise showing through skin.

*Miss Yara Okafor, clerk, Meridian Trading Company, is requested to attend the office of the port census at ten o'clock Thursday, for the purpose of verifying household rosters.*

Requested. As though one could decline.

I set it down and finished my tea, because Mr. Calloway disliked clerks who fidgeted, and because the first rule of my six years at Meridian Trading had never changed: *be boring*. Boring clerks were not followed home. Boring clerks were not remembered in reports. A boring clerk could stand in a room full of officials and be as invisible as furniture.

But that night, walking the long way to the Orchard along the drainage channel where the mud smelled of salt and rot, I turned the notice over in my mind the way you'd turn a coin you suspected was shaved. Household rosters. The census taker — Hale, the woman from the counting house window — was not merely counting ships and heads. She was asking about households. And a household implied a home, and a home implied an address, and an address was exactly the thing my family did not have, not on any paper anywhere in this port.

I reached the Orchard after dark, as always, entering through the gap behind the salt-drying racks where the palisade had rotted years ago and no one had repaired it precisely because everyone knew it was there. Inside, the settlement breathed its usual evening breath: smoke from cookfires, a baby crying two lanes over, the click of dominoes, someone singing low in Igbo, off-key, unbothered. Forty-odd families living on land that did not exist on any map, paying rent to no one, registered nowhere. Six years I had walked into this place and out of it without leaving a ripple.

My mother looked up from her mending when I came through the door.

"They've sent for me," I said. "The census."

Adaeze Okafor did not stop sewing. That was her gift and her armor: nothing ever showed in her hands. "What will you tell them?"

"What there is to tell. That I lodge alone above the counting house. That I have no household." I sat across from her. The single lamp threw our shadows tall against the wall, so that we looked like two women much taller than ourselves, women who might own things.

"And Kofi?"

"Kofi doesn't exist," I said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it.

My brother was out — diving again, though he claimed he wasn't, though the wet suit hung behind the water tank still dripping. My uncle Emeka was at the fisherman's shed playing cards, losing cheerfully. Only my mother and I and the lamp and the lie.

"You'll do fine," Adaeze said at last. "You always do fine." Which was either faith or a warning; with her, it was usually both.

* * *

The census office occupied the old customs annex near the north quay, a building the company had once used for storing hemp and now lent to the government at a rate Calloway described as patriotic. I arrived at ten o'clock precisely, wearing my second-best dress and my clerk's expression: attentive, slightly tired, entirely empty.

Mirabel Hale was not what I expected. I had imagined, from the glimpse through the counting house window, some grey functionary, ink-stained and dutiful. The woman who rose to greet me was younger than I'd guessed — thirty perhaps, maybe less — with a directness in her gaze that made the room feel suddenly smaller. Her hair was pinned with mathematical precision. On the table beside her lay the census book itself: a fat ledger bound in blue cloth, its pages ruled into columns. Beside it, a teapot, two cups, a plate of biscuits.

"Miss Okafor." She pronounced it correctly — *Oka-for*, the emphasis where it belonged — which startled me more than any question could have. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. Tea?"

"Thank you, no."

"More for me, then." She poured herself a cup without ceremony and sat, opening the blue book. "This shouldn't take long. You're listed in the company rolls as a clerk of six years' standing. Impressive tenure for an indentured position."

"The company is good to me."

"I'm sure." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile. "Now. The port census requires verification of residential arrangements. You lodge at the company compound, correct? Room above the eastern counting house?"

"That's right."

"Sole occupant?"

"Yes."

"No family in the port? Parents, siblings?"

"No." The word came out smooth as oiled wood. Six years of practice. "My family remained in the interior. I correspond with them occasionally, but they're settled there. This—" I gestured vaguely at the walls, the harbor beyond the window, the masts swaying like reeds— "this is just my work."

She wrote something. I watched her pen move and could not read it upside down, which annoyed me; I could read most clerks' handwriting upside down, had built a small career on the talent. Hers was deliberately cramped, deliberately hers.

"The interior," she said. "Which district?"

"The northern districts. Beyond the mission stations." Far enough to be vague. Vague enough to be far.

"Mm." She turned a page. "And your father? What work did he do?"

"He was a farmer." Also true, once, in another country, before the sea took him. Before the sea took everything and gave us this coast instead.

"A farmer. And he brought you to the port for the indenture?"

"My mother arranged it after his death. The company's agent offered terms. It was a good opportunity." Every sentence polished, every sentence rehearsed across six years of dockside inspections and harbor-master queries and the occasional drunk constable with too many questions. I had given these answers so many times they had worn smooth as river stones. I could say them asleep. Sometimes I suspected I did.

Miss Hale sipped her tea. Set down the cup. Turned back three pages in the blue book, running her finger down a column, and asked her next question in the same mild voice she'd used for all the others:

"Can you tell me who Emeka Okonkwo is?"

The room did not change. The light did not shift. The harbor went on creaking outside the window, and the teapot went on cooling, and Mirabel Hale went on looking at me with those level grey eyes. But inside my chest something dropped — the specific, sickening drop of a floorboard giving way in the dark, that half-second of free fall before your body understands there is nothing beneath it.

Emeka. My mother's brother. My uncle, who slept forty minutes' walk from this room, whose name appeared on no document in this entire colony except possibly a debt record at a card table by the fisherman's shed.

She had found him somewhere. A patrol list, a church register, a witness statement — it didn't matter how. What mattered was that she had found the thread, and was holding it out to me, and watching to see whether I would take it.

"I'm sorry?" I said. "I don't know the name."

"No?" She tilted her head slightly, a scholar encountering a difficult text. "He gave your family name. Okonkwo. Okafor. I assumed a relation. These coastal communities often keep close kin ties even across—"

"We're a common name," I said. "Okafor. Half the laborers on the copra boats are Okafor something. If I investigated every one, I'd never get the rosters done." I smiled. I felt the smile arrive on my face like weather, automatic, well-fitted. "Is he on your census list? If he's a resident, I could check the company rolls. We track most of the dock workers."

It was a good recovery. It was, in fact, an excellent recovery — offering help, redirecting toward paperwork, burying the name in the general population where it belonged. Any inspector would have accepted it. Most inspectors would have been grateful.

Mirabel Hale closed the blue book with both hands, gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child.

"Thank you, Miss Okafor. That won't be necessary." She stood. "One more thing, and then I'll release you. The census includes a voluntary section on literacy. Clerks are presumed literate, of course, but I like to note languages. What do you read?"

"English. Arithmetic." I paused, weighing, and gave her the small true thing because it cost nothing and bought credibility: "Some Portuguese. The shipping manifests from the southern trade come through in it sometimes."

"And at home? Growing up — what language did your household speak?"

There it was again. *Household.* Twice now she had used the word, twice aimed it like a lamp into a corner.

"Igbo," I said. "Yoruba. Whatever the neighbors spoke. Children collect languages like shells."

"Mm." She gathered her pen, her ink bottle, squared the blue book on the table until its edges aligned perfectly with the edge of the wood. "Well. Thank you for your time. You've been very helpful."

"Have I?" I hadn't meant to say it aloud. It slipped out with an edge I hadn't authorized, and I saw her look up sharply, and for one bare moment neither of us performed anything at all. Two women in a hot room, each knowing more than she'd said, each knowing the other knew.

Then she smiled — warm, open, utterly unreadable — and extended her hand. "I hope so. I do hate asking people questions when the answers don't matter. Good day, Miss Okafor."

* * *

I walked back to the counting house by the long route, along the seawall, letting the wind off the water scour my face. My hands were steady. My hands were always steady; that was the first thing the indenture had taught me, steadier than penmanship, steadier than arithmetic. But beneath the steadiness my thoughts ran in circles like a crab in a bucket.

*Who told her?*

Not Calloway. If Calloway knew about Emeka, he wouldn't waste the knowledge on a census taker — he'd hoard it, spend it later, at a better price. Not the patrols; if the patrols had Emeka's name, Emeka would already be in a cell. Someone else, then. Someone in the Orchard itself, or near it. The fisherman whose boathouse backed onto the palisade. The midwife who traveled between settlements. Or simply a bad night, a loud argument, a fire seen from the ridge road — evidence accreting the way silt accretes, grain by grain, until one day there's a bank where there was only water.

Or — and this thought stopped me at the rail, both hands flat on sun-warmed stone — or she had gotten it from *me*. From the half-second too long it took me to say *I don't know the name*. From the smile arriving a beat late. From whatever it is that leaks out of a person when the floor drops, however well you've trained yourself, however smooth the oiled wood of your voice.

Six years I had been invisible. Six years of being boring, of being furniture, of answers worn smooth as river stones. And in eleven minutes, a woman with a teapot had put a hairline crack down the middle of all of it, and I couldn't even point to the moment it happened.

Back at the counting house, the afternoon ledgers waited in their neat stacks, columns of figures marching obediently toward their totals. Mr. Calloway's door stood half open; he was on the telephone, his voice a pleasant murmur, saying *yes* and *of course* and *you may rely on it*. I sat at my desk, dipped my pen, and began copying freight entries in my careful hand.

Halfway down the page I realized I had entered the same shipment twice — the *Vesta*, pearl shell, four hundred weight, consigned south — and crossed it out, and sat looking at the blot for longer than a mistake deserved.

That evening I did not take the long way to the Orchard. I took the short way, cutting through the fish market with its reek of brine and blood, moving faster than caution strictly allowed. Because there was a woman going door to door through the port with a blue book full of names, one of them Emeka's, and she had looked at me the way an appraiser looks at a stone — turning it, weighing it, deciding what it was worth.

I came through the gap behind the salt racks at dusk and found my uncle sitting on the step of my mother's house, whittling a piece of driftwood into nothing in particular.

"Yara." He smiled his easy smile. "You're early. Good news?"

"There's a census taker," I said. "She has your name, Uncle. She knows it's connected to ours."

The knife kept moving. But slower now. Behind him, through the open door, I heard my mother set down her sewing, and in the lane beyond, my brother's laugh, coming home through the dark with the smell of the reef still on him.

"She asked me one question too many," I said. "And I answered it wrong."

Emeka looked past me, toward the harbor lights beginning to prickle along the water, and his face — for the first time since I'd known him — showed no smile at all.

"Then," he said quietly, "we have very little time."
