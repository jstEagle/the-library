## 4. The Orchard at Dusk

The path to the Orchard began where the paved road gave up, at a drainage culvert behind the salt warehouses, and it was less a road than an argument the ground had won against everyone who tried to cross it. Yara walked it in her work boots, skirts pinned up, a basket of rice and dried fish balanced on one hip. She had made this walk six hundred times. She could have done it blind, in rain, with patrols at her back—had done all three—but tonight she walked it slowly, rehearsing.

*A census taker has come to the port.* That was the safe way to say it. *A woman with a book, asking questions.* Safe words for an unsafe thing. Her mother would understand anyway. Adaeze Okafor had never needed a thing explained twice; she had needed things survived once, and after that she simply knew them, the way the body knows weather coming in its joints.

The culvert smelled of brine and rotting palm fronds. Beyond it the mangroves opened into the tangle of paths the Orchard people kept swept bare so their feet would leave no prints worth reading. Yara stepped onto the packed earth and felt the day's mask loosen, as it always did—not falling away, because she was never entirely without it, even here, but loosening like a knot worked at with patient fingers.

Six years. Six years of walking this path with her shoulders down and her face arranged into clerk's boredom, carrying baskets that looked like a daughter visiting family and nothing more. The trick was not secrecy. Anyone could hide. The trick was being *unremarkable*—being the woman whose name appeared correctly on the company rolls, whose handwriting filled ledgers in beautiful copperplate, who had no story anyone would bother to learn. She had built a life out of being forgettable, and she had been proud of it, and tonight, for the first time, pride sat in her stomach like a stone.

Because someone had come asking questions. And Yara did not yet know what kind of questions, or how many, or whether any of them had already landed somewhere near the truth.

The Orchard announced itself by sound before sight: a goat complaining, a child laughing high up in the branches of the old mango tree that gave the place its borrowed name, the thin sweet smoke of cooking fires. It was not a village. A village had a shape on a map. The Orchard was what happened when forty families refused, year after year, to have a shape—to fold themselves into the folds of the land, houses of salvaged timber and woven palm set back from every path, laundry strung low, no light showing after the harbor bell rang eight. The settlement had stood eleven years, invisible in plain sight, and every person in it understood the arithmetic of their survival: *be nothing, be counted nowhere, be no one.*

Yara passed Mama Esi's cookfire and got the nod—the small chin-lift that meant *seen, known, silent*—and turned down the last path to the house her father had built before she was born. Timber frame, palm roof, a floor of rammed earth swept daily until it gleamed like poured coffee. Her mother was outside, kneeling at the edge of the yard with her hands in a bed of okra seedlings, though the light was nearly gone. Adaeze gardened the way other women prayed.

"You're late," her mother said, without turning around.

"The tide was against me." Yara set down the basket. "Mother."

"Now you use my name in that voice." Adaeze sat back on her heels, wiped her hands on her wrapper, and looked at her daughter with the flat patience that had frightened Yara's childhood friends and comforted Yara all her life. "Sit. Tell me what the tide brought."

Kofi came out of the house then, sixteen and suddenly taller than Yara remembered him being a month ago, all wrists and collarbones and that new careful handsomeness boys his age wore like borrowed clothes. He had salt dried white along his hairline. Yara's eyes caught on it and held, and something cold slid down her spine, but she put it away. Not now. Now there was the other thing.

"A census woman came to the port," she said. "Three days ago. Hale is her name. She's conducting the count for the Colonial Office—households, occupations, births. Everything."

Adaeze went very still. Stillness was her language; she spoke it fluently. "They have counted us before."

"Not like this. Before, they counted ships and bales and heads at the gate, whatever the company chose to give them. This woman is going door to door herself. She asked Mr. Calloway why the harbor registers list four hundred laborers but the port quarter shows only two hundred residents." Yara heard her own voice go thin and steadied it. "She asked about the east flats. Our flats, Mother. Nobody asks about our flats."

The fire cracked. Somewhere down the lane a child was being called to supper, the call rising and falling in a language the census books had no column for.

Adaeze rose, brushed earth from her knees with two precise strokes, and said, "Emeka should hear this."

Her uncle was already at the doorway. He was often already at doorways when he was wanted; Emeka moved through the Orchard like current through water, present everywhere, visible nowhere. He came out and lowered himself onto the bench, folding his long body with the unhurried care of a man who had learned that hurry attracted eyes.

"So," he said. "It begins again."

"It hasn't begun," Yara said. "It may be nothing. A new official wanting to look diligent."

"Nothing," Emeka repeated, tasting the word. "Your mother said the same word in 'ninety-two, when the new harbor master arrived. And in 'eighty-eight, when they burned the fish camp on the point. Nothing is the word we say while we are still deciding which way to run."

"We are not running," Adaeze said.

And there it was, laid on the swept-earth floor between them like a knife someone had set down handle toward no one. Yara sat on her hands so they wouldn't move.

Emeka turned to his sister-in-law with the gentleness he saved only for her, the gentleness that was itself a form of argument. "Adaeze. Listen to me as your brother, not as your grief. If this woman finds you—if she writes your names in her book—then the book becomes a weapon that can be carried anywhere. To the magistrate. To the deportation office. You know what they do with unregistered persons now. There is a ship being fitted for exactly that cargo; I've heard the stevedores talk. They will not burn a shantytown this time. They will *empty* it, and put the people in it on water, and send them where the records say they were born—which is nowhere, which is wherever the ship decides."

"I know what ships do," Adaeze said quietly. "I buried my husband because of what ships do."

"Then let the graves keep him!" Emeka's voice cracked open, and Yara flinched; she had not heard her uncle raise his voice in five years. "Adaeze, I am begging you. Scatter. Tonight if we must. Mama Esi has cousins upriver. I know a fisherman at the north spit who owes me his life twice over. We take the children first, the old ones next, we melt into the coast the way we always have—we have *always* been able to disappear, that is the whole art of us—and in a season, when this census fever passes, we regroup—"

"And leave him?" Adaeze said.

Not loudly. That was the terrible thing about her mother: she spent volume like money, rarely, and what she bought with silence cost more than anything shouted. The yard went quiet enough that Yara could hear the okra leaves moving in the evening wind.

"He is under the frangipani," Adaeze continued, "beside his mother, beside the two we lost before they learned to walk. Eleven years I have swept that ground every morning. Every morning, Emeka. When the patrol boats pass on the water, I stand in the garden with my hands full of leaves, and I am not afraid, because the dead do not run and neither does the woman who tends them." She looked at her brother-in-law, and her eyes were dry and enormous. "If we scatter, we scatter as ghosts. Ghosts have no graves. Ghosts have nothing to sweep. I will not make my children into ghosts to save their bodies."

"Better a ghost than a cargo manifest," Emeka said, harsh again, and Yara saw that he was not angry—he was afraid, and his fear had nowhere to go except into sharpness. "You think I love the running? I have run since before this girl was born. But I am alive, sister. Running is the price. The grave will still be here. The ground does not grieve."

"The ground does not grieve," Adaeze agreed. "I do."

Kofi spoke for the first time. "What does the woman look like?"

Everyone turned. He had gone to the doorway and was standing half in shadow, arms crossed, and Yara saw—noting it the way she noted discrepancies in ledgers, automatically, filing it away—that his forearms were corded with new muscle, and there was a raw line across his left palm that was not from farm work.

"Small," Yara said. "Gray-eyed. Younger than her manner. She carries a leather census book and she writes standing up, like she doesn't trust chairs." She hesitated, then gave them the true weight of it. "She watched the counting house for an hour before she introduced herself. She counted *us*, Kofi. The clerks. She wrote down how many of us there were before anyone told her."

Emeka grunted. "That's not a clerk's habit."

"No," Yara said. "It isn't."

Adaeze crossed the yard and took her daughter's hands. Her palms were rough as bark and warm as bread. "You will be careful," she said. It was not quite a question and not quite an instruction; it was the oldest prayer their family owned. "You live inside their walls, among their papers. If this woman comes to you with questions, you will be stone. You will be the most boring woman in that port. You will tell her about ink and freight and nothing else, and you will bring me word of everything she asks, because a question asked twice is a net being cast."

"Yes, Mother."

"And you—" Adaeze turned to Kofi, and something in her voice shifted, some thread of doubt entering it, quick as a fish. "Where were you today? The nets needed mending and you were not here."

"Out," Kofi said. "Walking."

"Walking." His mother repeated the word the way Yara might repeat a figure that didn't sum. "With salt in your hair."

"It's hot. I sweat."

Yara watched her mother hold her son's gaze and watched Kofi hold it back, and thought: *he has learned to lie to her, and she knows it, and neither of them will say so in front of me.* The family's whole survival lived inside such silences, stacked like ballast. But tonight the silences felt heavier than usual, and Yara found herself cataloguing them with a clerk's cold precision: her uncle's fear, her mother's rootedness, her brother's salt-crusted hair and cut palms and the coins she had glimpsed once, weeks ago, hidden in his sleeping mat—coins too many for a boy who mended nets.

Later, when the meal was eaten and Emeka had gone down the lane to spread the word quietly, house to house, the way oil spreads through cloth, Yara helped her mother wash the bowls. The fire had burned to embers. Through the gaps in the timber wall, the harbor lights showed far off, pretty and meaningless, the world of paper and ledgers reduced to decoration.

"She will come here eventually," Adaeze said, handing Yara a bowl. "This census woman. Sooner or later they always come."

"Then we'll be ready."

"There is no ready." Her mother took the bowl back, dried it, set it in the stack. "There is only before and after. Yara." She paused, and in the ember-light her face was suddenly older, suddenly the face of a woman who had crossed an ocean in the hold of a ship and walked out of it and built a life on ground that belonged to no one and would admit to owning her less. "If it comes to choosing—if it ever comes to choosing between the living and the ground—you choose the living. Do you hear me? Promise me. Your father is dead and he does not need you. Kofi is alive and he does."

"I promise," Yara said, and meant it the way you mean things at nineteen, wholly and without understanding the bill that would later arrive.

She slept that night on her father's mat, in her father's house, listening to the Orchard breathe around her—goats and children and the sea working at the reef—and just before dawn she woke to a sound she knew too well: footsteps on the swept path, unhurried, stopping.

Through the wall gap she saw a lantern swing low over the earth, and a voice—Mama Esi's, pitched carefully casual—saying, "No one lives past here, miss. Just gardens. Old gardens."

And a woman's voice answered, pleasant, gray, unmistakable even through timber and darkness:

"How curious. Someone has swept these gardens very recently indeed."
