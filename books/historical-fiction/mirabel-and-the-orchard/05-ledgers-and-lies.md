## 5. Ledgers and Lies

The counting house smelled of camphor and old paper, and by half past seven on Monday morning Yara Okafor had already sharpened six pencils to needles and set them in a row beside her blotter like soldiers awaiting orders. Routine was a kind of armor. She had learned that in her first year at Meridian Trading, when she was seventeen and so frightened of being noticed that she arrived an hour early every day just to prove she belonged. Six years later the habit remained: ink her hands before anyone could ask why they were stained; numbers before names; silence before speech.

She unlocked the ledger cabinet with the small brass key that lived on a ribbon beneath her collar, and she began where she always began—with the *Kestrel*, back from Palawan with a cargo manifest of mother-of-pearl, tortoiseshell, and one crate marked "miscellaneous marine specimens" that everyone understood to mean dried sea cucumber and no one asked about.

Except that this morning, when Yara opened the receiving book to enter the *Kestrel*'s figures against the customs declaration, the columns refused to sit together.

She ran her finger down the line twice, then a third time, as if repetition might shame the numbers into agreement. The manifest said four hundred and twelve taels of shell. The customs paper said three hundred and eighty. The difference—thirty-two taels, roughly the value of a fisherman's boat, or a year of Kofi's schooling if such a thing were even possible for a boy with no registered name—sat between the two figures like a stone in a shoe.

Yara sat very still.

In six years she had learned that discrepancies came in two species. There were honest ones—clerks' errors, wet ink, a figure transposed in haste—and there were the other kind, which were never called anything at all because calling them something made them real. She had been trained, gently and without ever being told she was being trained, to recognize the second kind and to make them disappear. A rounding here. A "spoilage at sea" entry there. The harbor took its toll of everything; who would audit the water?

But those corrections had always come to her already digested, already softened by Mr. Calloway's red pencil, arriving on her desk as neat annotations in his cramped hand. This was different. This was raw. This was a discrepancy sitting in front of her, undisguised, waiting for *her* to decide what it would become.

She looked up at the long room—the rows of desks, the ceiling fans turning their slow, useless circles, the tall windows through which the harbor glittered like spilled coin. Old Nwosu was hunched over his correspondence across the aisle. The two junior clerks were arguing in whispers about a cricket match. Somewhere upstairs, behind the door marked PRIVATE, Mr. Calloway was drinking his morning coffee and reading shipping telegrams.

Yara did what any careful clerk would do. She wrote the customs figure into the receiving book, entered the discrepancy in the margin in her smallest hand as *pending verification*, and moved on to the next ship.

By noon there were three more.

* * *

The pattern, once seen, could not be unseen. It was like noticing a limp in a familiar gait.

The *Sable Ann*, in from Thursday Island: manifest 290 taels, declared 261. The *Providence Belle*: a crate of shell entered as damaged and written down to nearly nothing, though the damage report attached to the file described only "minor moisture exposure." And then the strangest one—a shipment dated three weeks past aboard a lugger called the *Merry Widow*, which Yara knew for a fact had never left the harbor, because she had watched it rotting at its moorings off Tanjong Pier all season, its rigging hung with fishing nets gone grey as cobwebs. The *Merry Widow* had nonetheless, according to the books, carried sixty taels of pearl shell to Singapore and returned with a clean bill of lading.

Sixty taels of pearls that had sailed nowhere, sold to no one, and vanished into a column of figures that balanced perfectly because someone had wanted it to balance.

Yara closed the book and pressed her palms flat against the cover as though holding down something that might otherwise escape.

She thought: I will not touch this. She thought: this is not my affair; clerks who see too much become witnesses, and witnesses have a way of disappearing into deportation lists. She thought of her mother's hands, cracked from mending nets in the Orchard, and of Kofi's laugh, quick and bright as a struck match, and of how much silence was required to keep all of them safe. Silence was her trade. She had built a life inside it the way a snail builds a shell—slowly, secretly, out of her own substance.

And yet her pencil hovered.

Because here was the thing about discrepancies of the second kind: they did not simply exist. They required maintenance. Someone had to keep feeding them, adjusting them, keeping them hidden from auditors and inspectors and census takers with pointed questions. And the books for the last quarter were due to be closed and submitted to the company's London office in eleven days.

Eleven days. Yara looked at the margin note in her own handwriting—*pending verification*—and understood, with the cold clarity of a diver surfacing too fast, that pending verification meant *pending me*. That the red pencil had not appeared because Mr. Calloway trusted her less than usual. It had not appeared at all.

He wanted to see what she would do on her own.

* * *

At half past four, the private door opened and Calloway descended the iron staircase with the unhurried tread of a man who owned the stairs and everything they touched. He was a spare, dry man of fifty-odd years, with a face like folded paper and a voice pitched so low that people leaned toward him without realizing they were doing it. He stopped at Yara's desk and stood looking down at the open ledgers for a moment longer than courtesy required.

"Miss Okafor."

"Mr. Calloway."

"I understand you've noted some irregularities in the quarter's receipts."

Yara kept her eyes on the page, on the tidy column of her own figures. "A few variances between manifests and customs declarations. I've flagged them for verification."

"Mm." He turned the receiving book toward himself with one finger, slowly, the way a man might turn a card he suspected was marked. His eye traveled down the entries. Yara watched him read her marginal notes—the *pending verification*s, the careful question marks—and she watched his face do nothing at all, which was somehow worse than any expression.

"The *Kestrel*," he said. "Thirty-two taels short. And the *Sable Ann*. And—" his finger paused on the *Merry Widow* line, resting there lightly, almost fondly, "—this one. A ghost shipment. How interesting that you found it."

"It didn't balance," Yara said. "I find what doesn't balance. That's my work."

"Yes." Calloway straightened. He smiled, and the smile reached perhaps as far as his mouth and no further. "It is your work, Miss Okafor. Very good work. The best balancing I've seen in this office in twenty years—which is why I'm giving you the quarter-end reconciliation. Yours alone. No need to trouble Nwosu with it; his eyes are failing and he fusses. You'll close the books by the twenty-eighth, and you'll present them to me ready for London."

"Of course, sir."

"And Miss Okafor." He had already begun to turn away; now he paused, and the pause had weight, like a hand set gently on the back of the neck. "When you reconcile these figures, remember what reconciliation means. The books must tell one story. A single, coherent story, from first entry to last. Whatever the manifests say, whatever the customs men say, whatever stray thoughts pass through a clerk's head—the finished book tells *one* story. Do you take my meaning?"

Yara felt the words settle over her like fine mesh sinking through water. "I take your meaning, sir."

"Splendid." He tapped the *Merry Widow* entry once with his fingernail, a small dry click. "You have a gift for seeing what doesn't balance. See that you develop an equal gift for making it balance. The company rewards loyalty generously."

He went back up the iron stairs. The private door closed. Around the room, nothing changed—the fans turned, the juniors argued about cricket, Nwosu licked his thumb and turned a page—but Yara felt the air of the counting house thicken around her, close and warm and inescapable, like the hold of a ship.

* * *

That evening she did not go straight to the Orchard.

She told herself it was prudence—two visits in one week invited notice, and notice was death—but the truth was simpler and uglier: she could not yet look her mother in the face while carrying what she carried. So instead she walked the long way home along the seawall, past the customs sheds and the chandlers' yards, and let the evening wind off the water try, without success, to blow her mind clear.

One story, Calloway had said. The books must tell one story.

And what story would that be? Not the true one—that much was certain now. The true story involved pearl shell that left the harbor undeclared and reappeared, somewhere beyond the horizon, as money in accounts no auditor would ever see. It involved a lugger that smuggled nothing but its own fiction. It involved a company that had, for years, been paying duties on phantom cargoes while pocketing the value of real ones, and a supervisor whose red pencil had been erasing the difference so smoothly that no one—not London, not the customs house, not a single clerk in this room—had ever been given cause to look.

Until now. Until a census taker named Mirabel Hale had begun asking questions no official bothered to ask, and the Colonial Office had grown curious enough to send its people sniffing along the wharves, and suddenly the company's comfortable fiction needed new custodians.

Calloway wasn't asking her to hide the truth. He was asking her to *own* it. To write it in her hand, close the books under her name, stand as the author of the lie when—if—the day of reckoning came. Clerks who forged entries could be surrendered. Clerks who merely suspected could claim ignorance forever. He was transferring the crime from his ledger to hers, and dressing it as promotion.

Yara stood at the seawall rail and watched the lights of the harbor come up one by one, reflected and doubled in the blackening water, until the whole port seemed to burn gently above and below the surface.

There was a version of herself, she admitted, that would do it. That version was tired. That version had spent six years learning that survival meant complicity in a hundred small ways—signing forms that erased people, filing papers that pretended the Orchard did not exist, speaking English in the office and pretending she had no other tongue. What was one more false column? The company held her family's existence in its fist like a pearl too valuable to display. One word from Calloway and the patrols would walk the mangrove paths at dawn. She had known this for six years without letting herself know it, the way one knows the sea is rising while standing on a beach.

But another version of her—the one that had copied her father's old account book by firelight until she could write numbers in her sleep, the one who believed that a ledger was a form of truth-telling or it was nothing—looked at the ghost of the *Merry Widow* and felt something harden in her chest.

Her father had kept honest books. In the free years, before the registration laws tightened and the world contracted, Samuel Okafor had kept accounts for a trading post up the coast, and he used to tell her: *Numbers are the one place a poor man can't be lied to, if he learns to read them.* He had died unregistered, unmourned by any government, buried in the Orchard under a wooden marker with no official existence whatsoever—and his daughter could still read numbers better than any man in the counting house.

Calloway wanted her gift for seeing imbalance. Fine. Let him have it.

What he did not yet know—and what Yara was only beginning, cautiously, guiltily, to suspect—was that a woman who could see exactly how a lie was constructed also knew precisely where its seams lay. And seams could be unpicked. Or copied. Or, someday, shown to someone with the authority to pull.

She was not ready to think about copying pages. She put the thought down carefully, the way one sets down a live coal, and told herself she would decide later whether to pick it up again.

* * *

The Orchard received her after dark, as it always did, without questions. Lantern light leaked through gaps in the plank walls; somewhere a baby cried and was hushed; the smell of woodsmoke and frying plantain mingled with the salt marsh. Adaeze was outside their lean-to, mending a net by lantern, and Kofi sat cross-legged nearby cleaning a lamp glass, and for ten minutes Yara let herself simply be among them, answering her mother's questions about whether she was eating properly, laughing at Kofi's impression of the harbormaster's wheeze.

Then Kofi went to fetch water, and Adaeze looked up from her netting with the direct gaze that had always undressed Yara's silences, even across an ocean of years.

"You're carrying something," her mother said. "You've been carrying it since you walked in. Out with it, or don't—but don't sit there leaking."

Yara opened her mouth to deny it, and found she couldn't. Not here. Not to the one person whose safety was the entire architecture of her silence.

"There are problems at the company," she said quietly. "In the books. Bad ones. And Mr. Calloway has put them in my hands."

Adaeze's needle stilled. "Bad how?"

"Bad in a way that could reach us. If I do what he wants, I'm safe, and we're safe, and I'll be a liar in ink that can't be unsaid. If I refuse—" Yara looked at the lantern, at the moth battering softly against its glass. "Mother, I don't know what happens if I refuse. But I'm beginning to fear I'm going to learn."

For a long moment Adaeze said nothing. Then she reached over and took her daughter's hand—the clerk's hand, ink-stained and soft-palmed, so unlike her own—and held it in both of hers, rough and warm.

"Your father used to say a ledger is a promise written down," she said. "Every number is somebody's bread, somebody's boat, somebody's life. He'd have liked you, this girl of mine. God rest him, he'd have liked you too much." Her grip tightened. "Do what keeps you alive, Yara. Do you hear me? Whatever keeps you and your brother breathing, you do it, and you let God and your father judge the rest."

Yara nodded, because it was easier than saying what she actually thought, which was that judgment had already begun, and that the judge was a neat-handed woman at a desk in the counting house, writing *pending verification* in the margins of a lie.

She walked back through the mangrove dark toward the town lamps, and behind her the Orchard dissolved into shadow, invisible again, unregistered, safe—for now.

In eleven days the books were due. In nine, the census taker would come to interview the harbor offices. Yara counted the days the way she counted everything now—inwardly, precisely, in columns that refused, no matter how she arranged them, to balance.

And in the morning, when she unlocked the ledger cabinet and saw the *Merry Widow*'s ghost sailing serenely through its impossible voyage, she caught herself doing something she had never done in six years of careful service: she lingered over the page a moment too long, memorizing it, before she dipped her pen.
