## 6. Calloway's Offer

The summons came on a Tuesday, folded inside a routine requisition slip: *Mr. Calloway requests the junior clerk attend his office at half past five. After hours. Discretion.*

Yara read it twice, then a third time, and felt the words rearrange themselves into something colder with each pass. Clerks were not summoned after hours. Clerks were corrected in the open, at their desks, within earshot of the ledgers and the other pens, because shame was a management tool like any other. A private meeting was different. A private meeting meant there was something to say that could not survive witnesses.

She finished her column of figures anyway. That was the discipline that had kept her alive six years: do the ordinary thing thoroughly, and let no one see your hands shake. She blotted the page, squared it under the paperweight, and only then allowed herself to look out the window at the harbor, where the light had gone the color of old brass.

*He knows.* The thought arrived without permission and refused to leave. *He knows something. The question is how much.*

She thought about running—not far, just home, just to warn them—but running would confirm whatever he knew, and so she did the harder thing. She wound her hair tight, straightened her collar, and walked down the corridor to the office at the end of the building, where the floorboards changed from company pine to imported mahogany, as if money itself grew thicker underfoot.

Calloway's door was open. He was standing at the window with his back to her, hands clasped behind him, watching the last light come off the water. He did not turn when she knocked on the frame.

"Miss Okafor." His voice was mild. It was always mild. In six years she had never once heard him raise it, which was precisely why everyone in the counting house lowered theirs around him. "Thank you for staying. Sit, please."

There were two chairs before his desk. She took the one farther from the window.

He let the silence run long enough to be felt, then turned and settled behind his desk, arranging his cuffs, aligning a single sheet of paper so its edge ran parallel to the desk's edge. Everything about the man was aligned. Yara had always suspected that if you cut Calloway open you would find ruled lines inside him instead of organs.

"Do you know," he said, "how long I have been in this port?"

"Eleven years, sir. You mentioned it at the—"

"At the Christmas dinner, yes." A thin smile. "Eleven years. And in eleven years I have learned one thing above all others about this coast." He laced his fingers together on the desktop. "Everyone here is hiding something. The harbor master hides his drinking. The customs men hide their percentages. The merchants hide their second books. It is not a flaw, Miss Okafor. It is the local climate, like the humidity." He tilted his head. "One learns to dress for it."

Yara kept her face still. Inside, her pulse was climbing toward her throat.

"I mention this," he went on, "because I want you to understand that I bear no ill will toward hidden things. I am not a moralist. Morality is expensive, and I have never been able to justify the expense." He slid the sheet of paper across the desk toward her. "But occasionally a hidden thing becomes inconvenient. And then it must be dealt with."

She looked down at the paper. It was a census form—the new ones, the ones that had begun circulating with Mirabel Hale's arrival. Household rosters. Names, origins, registration numbers. And written across the top in Calloway's small, precise hand: *Orchard district — est. 40–60 souls — unregistered.*

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts.

"You've made a mistake, sir," she said, and heard her own voice come out level, almost bored, and marveled at it from a great distance. "I don't know anything about that district. There's nothing out past the salt flats but fishing huts."

"Of course there isn't." He said it gently, the way one humors a child who has insisted the dog ate the letter. "Nothing but fishing huts. Let us proceed on that basis, since we both enjoy it." He drew a second document from the drawer—not a census form this time but a ledger page, and even upside down she recognized the columns, recognized her own neat figures from three weeks ago. "Now. Do you recall the *Sable Anne*?"

"The *Sable Anne* came in light in March. Cargo short. Pearls short." She recited it flatly, because refusing to know would be worse than knowing. "The discrepancy was logged."

"It was logged," Calloway agreed, "and then it was buried, because that is what we do here. We bury things." He tapped the ledger page. "Except the burial has not held. The Colonial Office has sent someone. A census taker, they call her. Hale." He pronounced the name with faint distaste, as though it were a foreign word he declined to learn properly. "And Miss Hale has been asking questions in places questions are not usually asked. She has been walking the shoreline. She has been seen—" he paused, savoring it, "- speaking with fishermen. Fishermen, Miss Okafor, are the most unreliable witnesses on God's earth, except for one thing. They see everything."

The room was very quiet. Through the wall she could hear the distant clatter of the day shift closing out, the ordinary sounds of an ordinary evening, belonging to some other life.

"What does this have to do with me?" she asked.

"Everything." He rose and moved to the window again, hands behind his back, and spoke to her reflection in the darkening glass rather than to her directly. Which she understood—it was easier for people to threaten reflections. "Six years ago you arrived on an indenture contract with a clerical aptitude rating that I personally approved. Bright girl, quick pen, no family listed, no attachments. Ideal." He turned his head slightly. "You have been ideal. But ideal people have one weakness, and it is this: they have too much to lose by being anything else."

"I don't understand what you're asking me to do."

"Yes, you do." He came back to the desk and sat, folding his hands, and now at last he looked at her plainly, and his eyes were not unkind, and that was the worst part. Unkindness she could have fought. What he offered instead was courtesy, patient and reasonable as arithmetic. "Miss Hale will complete her household surveys within the fortnight. When she does, her findings go to the Resident Magistrate, and the Magistrate's men go where her findings point. Now—I cannot stop her inquiry. No one can; she has Colonial Office backing, which makes her effectively fireproof. But inquiries require *interpretation*, and interpretation happens in offices like mine. A census taker writes 'unregistered settlement, east of the salt flats.' An interested party reads that note and writes 'no settlement found; informant mistaken.' Do you see? Paper is soft. It takes whatever shape you press into it."

"You're asking me to falsify government records."

"I'm asking you to correct them." The smile again, thinner. "You have a talent for correction. Your ledgers are the cleanest in this house. Which brings me to my other request, the smaller one. The *Sable Anne*'s shortfall, and two others like it—nineteen hundred pounds of pearl debt sitting in our books like a stone in a shoe. I need those entries adjusted. Moved, reclassified, amortized into freight losses. Nothing you haven't done a dozen times in miniature. Nothing anyone outside this room will ever need to think about again."

"And if I say no?"

Calloway exhaled through his nose, almost sadly, and picked up the census form with its damning notation at the top. He held it up between two fingers, considering it, the way a man considers a bird he might release or keep caged.

"If you say no, then I will have no reason to file an amendment to this report. And unamended reports travel. They reach the Magistrate, and the Magistrate sends constables, and constables are not careful men—they do not distinguish between the registered and the merely present. Deportation ships are being scheduled as we speak; you may have read the notices. There is a vessel called the *Coromandel*. I'm told her holds fill quickly." He set the paper down and slid it back into his drawer, closing it softly, a man putting away a tool he hoped never to use again. "I should hate that. Genuinely. You are the best clerk this office has produced, and I am told—" his voice dropped into something almost tender, "- that the settlement east of the salt flats keeps its graves well. Graves are important. Roots are important. It would be a pity to pull up roots."

The word *graves* went through her like a nail. Her father lay under a frangipani tree with a fisherman's cross at his head, a cross no constable would pause over and no census would ever record, and Calloway had never been within a mile of that place, and yet he had found it, or imagined it, or simply guessed—and the guess was accurate, because men like Calloway did not need facts. They needed only leverage, and leverage grew wild along this coast like salt grass.

"How long," she heard herself ask, "have you known?"

"That there are people living beyond the registry? Years." He shrugged. "That they are *your* people—that is more recent. Three weeks, perhaps. Since Miss Hale began asking after a woman named Adaeze." He watched her carefully as he said the name, and Yara understood that this, too, was a test, and that her stillness now mattered more than anything she had done in six years of stillness.

She gave him nothing. Not a flicker. Somewhere inside, a door closed quietly, and behind that door something began to burn—slowly, without smoke.

"Adaeze is a common name," she said.

"It is indeed." He smiled, satisfied, and Yara realized with a cold drop that her denial had given him exactly what he wanted: confirmation that the name landed. He had not needed her to confess. He had needed only to watch her hear it. "Well. I won't keep you. Think it over—there is no hurry, the *Coromandel* is three weeks out, and I am a patient man. But not infinitely patient." He stood, and extended his hand, courteous to the last, and she took it because refusing would cost more than touching him. His palm was dry and cool, like paper. "You'll find, Miss Okafor, that most bargains look monstrous from a distance and quite reasonable up close. I'm merely offering you proximity."

She walked out of the mahogany-floored office with her spine straight and her ledger hand steady, and she did not let herself tremble until she reached the street, until the harbor wind hit her face and the gulls cried down their idiot news, and then she stood in the shadow of the customs shed and shook for a full minute, silently, like a woman in a soundproofed room.

They knew. Or he knew enough. Either way the Orchard's invisibility—the whole architecture of six careful years—was already cracked, and cracks in that kind of wall do not close. They spread.

She should go to them tonight. Warn her mother, argue with Emeka, look at Kofi's sleeping face and memorize it while it was still a face that slept without fear. But the tide was wrong for the back path and the patrols had grown thick since the census began, and going tonight might deliver her straight into the net Calloway had just described.

So instead she did what she had always done with unbearable things. She carried them home to her narrow room above the chandler's shop, lit her lamp, opened her private notebook—the one no supervisor ever saw—and wrote, in her flawless clerk's hand:

*He knows. He thinks he knows everything. He does not know about the copied pages. He does not know what I keep.*

She looked at that last line for a long time. It was true, and it was nearly enough, and it was nowhere near enough.

Then she blew out the lamp and sat in the dark listening to the harbor, and somewhere out on the black water she could swear she heard the moan of a ship coming in—one she could not yet name—carrying, perhaps, the woman with the census book, or the law, or nothing at all. Only one thing was certain: she had three weeks, and every hour of them had just become borrowed.

Tomorrow, she would find a way to reach the Orchard. Tomorrow, she would find out what Mirabel Hale actually wanted.

Tonight, she practiced saying no to a dead man's memory, and failed.
