## 7. What Mirabel Saw

The counting house smelled of salt and ink, and Mirabel Hale had learned in six days that both smells were lies.

She sat at the borrowed desk by the window—the one the Meridian Trading Company had offered her with such unctuous courtesy—and wrote numbers into a census ledger that would never be read by anyone who believed them. Around her, clerks scratched away at their own books, and the harbor light came through the glass in long dusty blades, and every man in the room performed the small theater of a person with nothing to hide.

Mirabel had been performing longer than any of them.

Her commission lay folded in the lining of her trunk at the Harbour Rest Hotel: three pages on Colonial Office letterhead, signed by a deputy under-secretary whose name she was not permitted to speak aloud unless she was standing in front of a magistrate or a corpse. *Miss M. Hale, appointed to conduct an enumeration of persons resident within the port district, with such supplementary inquiries as may be directed.* The phrase *supplementary inquiries* was doing more work than any clerk in this building had ever done, and it had been drafted deliberately vague so that if Mirabel was caught prying where prying was not wanted, the Company's lawyers would find nothing but a census taker being thorough.

Three years the Colonial Office had been receiving reports about Meridian. Pearls leaving the port on ships that carried no pearls. Tonnage that existed only in ledgers. Insurance claims against cargoes that had never been loaded, premiums collected, losses mourned, and somewhere in the arithmetic—Mirabel was certain of it now—a debt being serviced to people who did not appear on any manifest. The Office suspected smuggling. The Office suspected worse. What the Office lacked was paper: the actual, physical, damning columns of figures, kept in some office like this one, written in some clerk's neat hand.

So they had sent a woman with a census book, because no one guards against a woman with a census book. She was furniture. She was weather. Men answered her questions the way they answered questions about rainfall—with boredom, and therefore with truth.

Except the Okafor woman.

Mirabel dipped her pen and looked across the room without appearing to look. Yara Okafor sat at the third desk from the door, transcribing shipping entries in a hand so precise it looked printed. Six years indentured to Meridian, according to the company's own roster—which itself was interesting, because the indenture papers Mirabel had requested from the registrar's office had taken nine days to arrive, and when they came, one page bore the soft rectangular shadow of something that had been pasted over and removed. A correction. Companies did not correct indenture records out of kindness.

The woman was careful in the way that only frightened people are careful. She never arrived first and never left last; she drew exactly the attention her position required and not a grain more. When Mirabel had interviewed her four days ago about household rosters, Yara had lied about a name—smoothly, instantly, without the flicker most liars produce—and then had gone very still afterward, in the way of someone listening for the trap to close. Mirabel had let the moment pass. You did not spring a trap before you knew what it held.

But she had seen it. And Yara Okafor knew she had seen it, and that knowledge sat between them now like a coin on a table neither would reach for.

* * *

In the evenings, Mirabel worked.

Not the census—the census was a daytime fiction—but the real file, which lived in her memory and in a ciphered notebook disguised as a book of hymns. Each night at the Harbour Rest she added what the day had yielded, and each night the picture grew sharper and uglier.

The shipping schedules were the first thread. In the census interviews, she asked dockworkers and pilots not how many people lived in their households but where they had been on particular dates, and men who thought they were answering dull questions told her, incidentally, that the *Sable Ann* had been lightered at the outer anchorage on the ninth of March, that the *Coromandel* had taken on cargo at night twice since January, that boats went out from the Company's private jetty when no cargo was scheduled to go out at all. Pearl shell was heavy, wet, and smelly; it could not be conjured. Either those shipments were phantom, or they were carrying something else entirely, and the Company's insurance filings said phantom.

The second thread was money. Mirabel had spent two evenings in the public reading room of the customs house with back issues of the shipping gazette, cross-referencing freight declarations against the Company's published accounts, and the shortfall—carefully spread, carefully laundered through legitimate voyages—came to a sum that made her set down her pencil. Someone owed someone else an enormous amount of money, and Meridian was paying it off by inventing pearls.

And debts like that were never owed to banks. Banks forgave or foreclosed. This kind of debt had other remedies.

Which brought her to the third thread, the one she turned over nightly like a stone she couldn't stop lifting: the Orchard.

Everyone in the port knew there were people living beyond the palisade road—unregistered laborers, absconded indentures, families who had slipped through every net the colony strung. Everyone knew and no one saw them, because seeing them meant paperwork, and paperwork meant expense, or deportation, or trouble. The previous censuses had simply recorded nothing. Blank lots. Mirabel had walked the edge of the settlement twice at dusk, staying on the public track, and had counted rooflines, cooking fires, washing lines. Forty dwellings at least. Perhaps sixty. Children. Goats. A graveyard on the slope with wooden markers worn smooth by rain.

A whole parish of invisible people, and the Company's clerks stepped around them as though stepping around water.

It was the graveyard that had undone her, though she'd have been hard-pressed to explain that in a dispatch. She had stood on the public track looking at those little worn crosses and cairns and understood suddenly that these were not squatters passing through. These were people who had decided to stay—who had buried their dead in colonial ground and meant to be buried beside them—and the colony's entire machinery was arranged so that they could be erased with a signature. The census she was ostensibly conducting could do that erasing. Her book, filled in honestly, would put names to roofs, and names to roofs would put the Coromandel's holds to families.

She had not yet decided what an honest census was worth. That decision, she suspected, was going to cost her something. Everything honest did.

* * *

On the seventh day, Mr. Calloway invited her to inspect the Company's record room.

He was all solicitude, Calloway—silver-haired, soft-spoken, a man who had spent forty years making cruelty sound like bookkeeping. He walked her personally down the aisle of bound volumes and oilcloth-wrapped ledgers, gesturing like a curator. "You're welcome to whatever the enumeration requires, Miss Hale. The Company believes in good order." He tapped a shelf of shipping registers. "Everything here is open to the Crown."

Everything here is curated, Mirabel thought, smiling. But she thanked him, and he left her alone among the records for two hours, which was itself informative—they wanted her to be seen among the records, wanted the census to be visibly satisfied, wanted her report to say *thorough, cooperative, nothing amiss.*

She used the time well. She found the register for the *Sable Ann*, March, and read the cargo entry: pearl shell, 214 chests, valued at a figure that would have required the seabed outside the harbor to be paved with oysters. She found the insurance rider filed the same week. She copied both into the hymnbook in her cipher, her pen steady, her pulse less so.

And she found something she had not been looking for. Tucked inside the cover of a personnel register, a loose slip of paper—an old indenture assignment sheet, superseded, misfiled or hidden. On it, in faded ink: a family name. Okafor. Four names listed together, father deceased, status column annotated in a clerk's shorthand she recognized because she'd seen its twin on Yara's own file: *irregular.*

Someone had once tried to make the family visible, and the attempt had been buried inside another book like a body in a wall.

Mirabel closed the register and stood for a moment in the lamplit aisle, listening to the building settle. Then she returned everything precisely where it had been, because a witness who takes things becomes a thief, and she could not afford to become anything but boring.

* * *

It rained the following afternoon, a warm drumming rain that emptied the streets and gave the counting house the drowsy air of a church during a sermon. Most of the clerks had drifted toward the doorway to watch the water come off the eaves. Yara Okafor stayed at her desk.

Mirabel gathered her census satchel, crossed the room, and set a question on the corner of Yara's desk the way one sets down a cup. "One more household to confirm before the week's out," she said pleasantly. "The harbor district's nearly done."

Yara's pen paused—not a flinch, just a pause, a professional recalibration. "I can't help you there, miss. I keep company records."

"Of course." Mirabel watched the rain instead of the woman, which loosened people wonderfully. "Strange thing about this port. I've enumerated two hundred households, and I've yet to meet anyone who knows anything about anyone. Sailors who don't know sailors. Neighbors who don't know neighbors." She turned the page of her book as if checking something. "A town built entirely out of strangers. Very convenient for some, I'd imagine."

"Convenient for whom, miss?"

"For whoever needs people not to see each other." Mirabel smiled at the window. "Or perhaps I'm romantic. Rain does that to me." She shouldered the satchel. "Good evening, Miss Okafor."

She felt the woman's eyes on her back all the way to the door, and noted, with the cold satisfaction that was the nearest thing she allowed herself to joy, that the pen had not resumed scratching until the door had shut.

* * *

At the hotel, she opened the hymnbook and forced herself to write the paragraph she had been avoiding for three nights.

*The clerk O. is not a conspirator. Assessment:* —and here her pen had actually hovered, because assessment was supposed to be a science and this was turning into something with a pulse in it— *Assessment: coerced. The Company holds something. Family, most likely. The irregular annotation in the personnel register, the false name offered in interview, the vigilance—these are not the habits of a partner in fraud but of a hostage keeping house for her captors. If the Company is pressed, it will spend her first. They always spend the closest hostage first; hostages near at hand are cheaper than lawyers.*

She read it back and disliked herself for writing it, because sympathy was a leak in an investigation, and leaks sank investigators. She had seen it happen to better officers than herself—a man in Zanzibar who had fallen in love with the plight of everyone he interviewed and ended up discredited, dismissed, and drunk. Purpose was a discipline. Sympathy was a weather you worked in, not a place you lived.

But she also knew—knew with the certainty of thirty-nine years of watching institutions eat people—that Yara Okafor was the case. Not the ledgers, not the ships, not even Calloway with his curatorial smile. The ledgers proved the crime; the woman proved the method. Meridian survived on invisible people. It employed them, hid them, threatened them, and would eventually feed them to the Coromandel's hold or the magistrate's dock, whichever was quieter. If Mirabel took the Company down without taking the woman out of its hands, she would have won a victory and lost the war in the same motion.

So: the woman was not merely evidence. The woman was the objective.

She turned to a fresh page and began, in cipher, to plan—not how to catch Yara Okafor lying, but how to make lying to her unnecessary. There was a difference between an informant and an ally, and the difference was trust, and trust could not be summoned; it had to be earned in installments, paid in risk.

First installment: she would stop pretending not to know about the settlement beyond the palisade road. Next time they spoke, she would let Yara see that she had seen the rooflines, the fires, the graves—and had written none of it down. A census page left blank on purpose was either treachery or mercy, and letting the woman wonder which was worth ten interrogations.

Second installment: the berth. Mirabel had heard the talk at the customs house that morning—the *Coromandel* was fitting out for the northern run, and the harbor master's office had let slip, in the way offices let slip things they want known, that unregistered persons would be sailing on her whether they booked passage or not. Three weeks. Every family beyond the palisade road had three weeks to become a name on a list or cargo in a hold. Whoever controlled a single legitimate berth controlled a life, and the Company knew it, and would use it.

If Mirabel could get ahead of that arithmetic—if she could place one name, one documented, defensible, bulletproof name—she would have bought something no interrogation could purchase.

She blew out the lamp and sat in the dark listening to the rain, and admitted the thing she had been circling for a week: she liked the woman. Liked her steadiness, her terrible competent handwriting, the way she lied with the composure of someone protecting something rather than stealing something. Liking made the work dangerous. Liking meant that when the moment came—and it would come, in a room with Calloway, or at a dock fence, or over a burning book—Mirabel might hesitate between the mission and the person, and hesitation was the one luxury an investigator could never afford.

Very well, then. She would refuse to choose. She would build a case in which saving the woman and breaking the Company were the same act, and she would make the arithmetic work, because arithmetic was the language this port understood.

Outside, the rain thickened toward monsoon. Somewhere south along the coast, the *Coromandel* was taking on coal.

Three weeks, Mirabel thought. Enough time to save everyone, or enough time to learn precisely why that was impossible.

She picked up her pen and began to draft the telegram she hoped she'd never send.
