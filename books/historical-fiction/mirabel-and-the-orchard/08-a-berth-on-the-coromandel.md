## 8. A Berth on the Coromandel

The news came the way bad news always came to Meridian Trading: not shouted, but written. A single sheet of government stock, pinned to the harbor notice board by a constable who yawned through the task, and then transcribed by Mr. Calloway's own hand into the company circular that arrived at Yara's desk before the ink on the original had dried.

*By order of the Port Commissioner, all persons of unregistered status within the municipal bounds are to present themselves for embarkation aboard the steamship Coromandel, scheduled to depart on the twenty-third instant. Unregistered persons remaining after said date will be subject to detention and removal.*

Yara read it twice, standing in the aisle between the filing cabinets, her thumb pressed flat against the word *removal* as though she could smudge it out of existence.

Three weeks.

She had known it was coming — had felt it gathering for months the way one feels weather behind the hills — but knowing and reading were different animals entirely. Knowing was a weight carried alone at night. Reading was public. The circular would go to every counting house on the waterfront, every warehouse foreman, every patrol sergeant. By evening, the whole port would know that the clock had started, and men who had spent years pretending not to see the Orchard would suddenly find their eyes very sharp indeed.

"Okafor." Calloway stood in his office doorway, arms folded, watching her with the mild expression he wore like a coat. "The Ashcombe manifests. Today, please."

"Yes, sir."

Her voice was steady. She was proud of that, in the small cold way one is proud of anything when everything else is falling. She sat down, pulled the manifests toward her, and worked with her pen moving in its neat, disciplined strokes while inside her chest something ran in circles like a trapped bird.

Three weeks. Twenty-one days. Her mother. Kofi, sixteen years old and still growing into his shoulders. Uncle Emeka with his bad knee and his stubborn mouth. Nine souls in the Orchard who were kin to her by blood or by the long loyalty of shared hiding, and beyond them perhaps forty more whose faces she knew from dusk visits and market glances.

One ship. And no berths for any of them.

* * *

At midday she walked to the harbor office under pretense of delivering correspondence, which was even true — she carried a sealed packet against her ribs like a shield. The harbor office occupied the ground floor of the customs house, all teak and brass and ceiling fans turning with the lazy authority of the empire itself, and there, pasted beside the window where tickets were sold, hung the passenger schedule for the Coromandel.

She read it standing very still, letting the fan's breeze move the damp hair at her temples.

The Coromandel carried three classes of passage. First class, aft, with promenade deck privileges: fully booked, and irrelevant. Second class: twelve berths remaining, each requiring a certificate of registration or a sponsor's bond executed before two witnesses. Third class — the steerage berths, iron frames stacked four high in the bowels of the ship — thirty-one places remaining, and here the requirement was printed in smaller type but with no less force: *documented passengers only. No unregistered persons may be embarked under any regulation or dispensation whatsoever.*

No unregistered persons. The words did not soften with repetition. They hardened.

Yara turned away from the window and found herself face to face with the booking clerk, a pale, sweating Englishman named Prewitt whom she knew slightly, because everyone who did business with Meridian knew everyone else, in the way of small ports where secrets were currency and everyone counted everyone else's coins.

"Miss Okafor." He nodded at the packet under her arm. "Company business?"

"Manifests for Mr. Calloway." She smiled the smile that opened doors, the clerk's smile, unremarkable and forgettable. "A terrible business, this Coromandel affair."

"Terrible," Prewitt agreed readily. "Half the harbor's in a panic. We've sold eleven berths since Tuesday. Steerage especially — gone like ice in July." He lowered his voice with the relish of a man handing over gossip rather than grief. "Between us, there'll be nothing left but the dregs within the fortnight. Everyone with a paper wants off before the monsoon shuts the roads anyway."

"Everyone with a paper," Yara repeated.

Prewitt caught himself, as men do when they remember whom they're talking to — not an Englishwoman, not quite a person with papers either, though her indenture gave her a strange half-status, a registered number attached to a registered employer. His eyes flicked to the color of her hands and then away, embarrassed.

"Well," he said. "You'd know better than most how it goes. Company people have their own arrangements, I expect."

He said it carelessly, kindly even, and it passed over her like wind. But later — walking back along the quay with the packet delivered and her mind running hot — she would take the sentence out and turn it over. *Company people have their own arrangements.*

It was true. It was horribly, precisely true. The Meridian Trading Company held bonded labor, yes, but it also held influence: berths reserved for staff transfers, passages booked in bulk and cancelled at leisure, a standing arrangement with the shipping line that went back decades. Every year, a handful of such berths changed hands — given as favors, sold quietly, used to reward the useful and punish the troublesome.

And once a year, exactly one berth remained unallocated after the staff lists closed. The widow's berth, the clerks called it, because old Mr. Harrow had endowed it decades ago for the passage home of some long-dead supervisor's widow, and when no widow claimed it the berth rolled forward, year upon year, a small orphan slot in the great ledger of the sea.

Yara had processed the paperwork for the widow's berth twice in her six years at Meridian. She knew its number. She knew the form. She knew that it required only a company endorsement — Calloway's signature — and a named passenger with documents enough to satisfy a boarding officer.

Documents enough. A named passenger.

She stopped at the rail overlooking the water and let the arithmetic finish itself, cruel and clean.

One berth. One name. Fourteen days until the lists closed, if Prewitt's gossip held. And a family of nine — nine who were hers, never mind the other forty — among whom exactly one could be saved.

Hope arrived in her chest not as warmth but as arithmetic. That was the thing no one told you about hope in a place like this: it came in numbers, and the numbers never added up to mercy. One seat. Many lives. And somewhere behind the sum, waiting with his fountain pen and his pleasant voice, Mr. Calloway, who would want something for that signature. Who always wanted something.

She gripped the rail until her knuckles ached, watching a lighter loaded with copra slide out toward the anchorage, and made herself think it plainly, because thinking it plainly was the only way to survive the thinking:

Someone was going on that ship. And someone was not.

* * *

That night she took the long way to the Orchard, looping through the cassia groves behind the salt pans, doubling back twice out of habit, though habit felt thinner now, less like craft and more like ritual. The patrols had not yet changed their routes — that would come, she knew, within days of the circular reaching the garrison — but already the air of the port had shifted. At the corner by the rice mill, two constables stood talking, and one of them gestured broadly toward the eastern flats, toward the tangle of tin roofs and cooking smoke that the official maps marked as *marsh, uninhabited*.

In the Orchard, the news had arrived ahead of her, as news does among people who live by listening. Adaeze met her at the doorway of the lean-to, and behind her mother's shoulder Yara could see the whole settlement gathered around Emeka's fire — the Okafor cousins, the Mensah brothers, old Mama Efua with her grandchild asleep across her lap — all of them wearing the same expression: the look of people doing sums they cannot make come out right.

"You've seen it," Yara said.

"The fisherman brought word at noon." Adaeze's voice was level, but her hands, twisting the edge of her wrapper, betrayed her. "Twenty-three days. Your uncle says twenty-two, counting tonight."

"It's fourteen that matters. The berth lists close before the ship sails. After that, no paper in this port buys a place on her."

Emeka looked up from the fire. He had been sharpening a machete, pointlessly, the way he sharpened things when his mind needed somewhere to sit. "Then we don't wait for their ship," he said. "We go north, tonight, before the patrols wake up. The mangrove route to the fishing villages. I've said it before—"

"And I've answered you before." Adaeze did not raise her voice; she never raised her voice; she simply became immovable, like a tree deciding not to fall. "Your brother is buried here, Emeka. My husband is buried here. Six years I have tended that grave, and I will not run north like an animal while his bones lie in ground the company means to bulldoze."

"They mean to do worse than bulldoze it if we stay."

"Then they will find me standing on it."

The silence after that was total. Even the fire seemed to burn more quietly. Yara stood in the doorway with the harbor office figures still sharp in her memory — thirty-one steerage berths, documented passengers only — and understood with a clarity that felt like illness that her mother would rather be deported standing than flee kneeling, and that this pride, this beautiful, ruinous rootedness, would kill them all unless someone in this firelight did arithmetic instead.

"I can get a berth," Yara said.

Every head turned. Mama Efua stirred awake; the child on her lap whimpered and settled.

"One berth," Yara went on, and her own voice sounded far away to her, calm and terrible. "Through the company. Legitimate — real papers, real passage, no bribes to trip over. But only one. The lists close in fourteen days, and I must give them a name."

"A name." Emeka set down the machete slowly. "Whose name?"

"That," Yara said, "is why I've come."

What followed lasted perhaps ten minutes and would live in her memory for years, worn smooth by retelling and yet never softening. It was not a fight. Fights have winners. This was something worse: a family trying to hand each other death as politely as passing bread.

Adaeze spoke first, and she did not hesitate. "Kofi."

Kofi, sixteen, sitting cross-legged at the fire's edge, went white. "Mother—"

"You are young. You are strong. You have your father's hands and you will use them in some honest trade across the water, and you will write to us—" her voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture in stone— "and you will live. That is what a berth is for. That is what it is *for*, Yara. Not for old women. For sons."

"I'm not sending you onto the flats alone," Kofi said fiercely, and Yara heard in his voice the boy he had been and the man he was becoming, arguing with each other inside him. "I won't go. Give it to Mama."

"Mama Efua needs medicine we can't buy here," Adaeze said, wheeling on him. "Give it to Mama Efua, then—"

"Adaeze." Emeka's voice cut low. "Efua has daughters in the northern settlements. She has somewhere to scatter to. You don't."

"Neither do you."

"I'm not asking."

"You're not offering, either," Adaeze said. "That's the trouble with you, Emeka. You argue for scattering and then stand like a fence post whenever anyone asks who goes first."

Old Mama Efua lifted her head from her grandchild's hair and said, in her thin dry voice, "My berth went down with the ship that brought me, child. I don't need another one going the wrong way." And she laughed, softly, so as not to wake the boy, and no one laughed with her.

They argued in circles. They argued with tenderness, which is the most exhausting kind of argument, because no one can be angry and everyone bleeds. Each proposed a name and then dismantled it. Adaeze proposed Kofi; Kofi refused and proposed Adaeze; Emeka proposed nobody, including himself, and said the berth should go to whichever of the Mensah brothers had the weakest lungs; the Mensah brothers said the berth should go to the Okafors, who had fed half the Orchard through the bad season two years past. Around and around, generosity chasing its own tail, until Yara stood up and put an end to it because she could not bear one more minute of hearing the people she loved compete to be abandoned.

"No one decides tonight," she said. "The lists close in fourteen days. I'll bring word when I know the terms — and there *will* be terms, understand that. Nothing at Meridian comes free, least of all a signature." She looked around the fire, at each face in turn, memorizing them against whatever was coming. "But hear me now. When I come back with the answer, there will be a name on that form. One name. And whoever it is will board that ship, if I have to carry them up the gangway myself."

Nobody argued. That was the worst of it. They simply nodded, one by one, the way people nod at a doctor, and began to drift away into the dark between the lean-tos, and Yara stayed by the dying fire with her mother, neither speaking, both listening to the crickets and the far-off sound of the sea rehearsing the monsoon.

"Six years you've kept us safe with your careful little silences," Adaeze said at last. "Now the silence is ending, and you're the one holding the pen." She touched Yara's cheek, briefly, roughly, the way she had when Yara was small. "Whatever you decide, daughter — decide fast. Hope is heavy cargo. Don't carry it longer than you must."

* * *

She was halfway back along the salt-pan road, the moon laying her shadow long and thin across the causeway, when she saw the lantern.

It stood at the junction where the Orchard path met the company track, held by a figure in a light traveling dress utterly wrong for the hour and the terrain, and beside the lantern, held open against the breeze, a book — a large ruled volume that even at fifty paces Yara recognized by its shape, having seen its twin in every government office on the coast.

The census book.

Mirabel Hale closed it as Yara approached, unhurried, and inclined her head as though they had arranged to meet, as though a woman carrying questions through the marshes at midnight were the most natural thing in the world.

"Miss Okafor." Her voice was pleasant, faintly amused, and completely unreadable. "Late for the harbor office, aren't we?"

"The causeway floods at high water," Yara said. "I was checking the depth."

"At midnight."

"The tide keeps no office hours, miss."

Mirabel smiled then — a small, genuine, unsettling smile — and lifted the lantern so its light fell full on Yara's face, studying her the way one studies a ledger entry that refuses to balance.

"No," she agreed. "It doesn't, does it? Neither, I find, do the interesting things in this port." She turned the lantern aside, releasing Yara's face from its glare, and glanced eastward, toward the dark scatter of roofs and smoke, and Yara's heart stopped and started again in the space of that glance. "Good night, Miss Okafor. Mind the mud. And mind, too—" the smile deepened by a degree— "the company you keep. This port is about to become a very small place for people without papers. I'd hate to see anyone... misfiled."

She walked on toward the town, lantern swaying, census book tucked under her arm like a hymnal, leaving Yara standing on the causeway with the tide whispering at the pilings and one thought ringing louder than the water:

Fourteen days. One berth. One name.

And now, perhaps, one witness too many who had seen exactly where the clerk went when the lamps went out.
