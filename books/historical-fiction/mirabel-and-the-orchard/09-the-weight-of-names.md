## 9. The Weight of Names

The council convened after dark, as all important things in the Orchard did.

Yara arrived with a lantern turned low and a paper folded into eighths in her pocket, and by the time she ducked through the curtain of Adaeze's room—the largest space the family had, which was to say barely large at all—she had already rehearsed her opening three times. None of the rehearsals survived contact with the sight of them all waiting for her.

Her mother sat on the low stool nearest the cooking fire, hands folded in her lap like a woman in church. Kofi sprawled against the far wall, too tall for the room, knees up, trying to look bored and failing. Uncle Emeka stood by the window slit, though there was nothing to see but the black shapes of tin roofs and the orange smear of the harbor lights beyond. He always stood when something mattered. He said it kept his blood moving.

The paper in Yara's pocket weighed more than paper should.

"You found it," Adaeze said. Not a question. News traveled through the Orchard faster than light traveled over water, and the berth on the *Coromandel* had been news since noon.

"I found it." Yara set the lantern on the floor between them so its light made a small island, and they were all gathered on its shore. "One berth. Documented passage, booked through company channels—Calloway's channels, which means it comes with his smell on it, but it's legitimate. It's real. The clerk at the shipping office checked the manifest twice." She had watched him do it, had held her breath through both checks, had walked out onto the quay afterward and gripped a mooring post until her knuckles went white.

"And it leaves when?" Emeka asked.

"Three weeks. With the deportation sailing."

Kofi's head came up off the wall. "So it's not a berth. It's an escape."

"It's a berth," Yara said firmly, "and it's one. One seat on a ship carrying four hundred people we love out of this harbor, and one name gets written on the ticket, and that name is what I came here to settle tonight."

She said it briskly. She had meant to say it briskly. But the fire popped, and her mother's hands tightened in her lap, and the briskness died somewhere between Yara's chest and her mouth.

"Then let's settle it," Emeka said. "Quickly, and like grown people. Adaeze. You are the eldest of us. The ticket is yours."

"No," said Adaeze.

It should have been simple. That was the cruelty of it. A single berth, a single decision, arithmetic any child could do: four souls, one seat, choose. Yara had spent six years doing the Meridian Company's arithmetic—tonnage against tariff, freight against fine—and she had believed, walking up the hill with the paper burning its hole through her skirt, that this sum would be harder only because the numbers were people.

She had not counted on refusal.

"The ticket is yours," Emeka said again.

"My son is sixteen," Adaeze said. Her voice was level, the voice she used for settling disputes between neighbors, for telling the fishmonger his scales were crooked, for every occasion on which softness would be mistaken for surrender. "He has a whole life in him that hasn't been spent yet. Mine has been spent exactly where I wanted to spend it."

"On a tin roof beside your husband's grave," Emeka said. "Forgive me, sister. That is not spending. That is being spent."

The fire cracked again. Outside, somewhere down the lanes of the Orchard, a baby cried and was hushed, and the hushing was itself a reminder—there were children here, dozens of them, whole families living unregistered in the spaces between the census lines, and every one of them would be on the *Coromandel*'s lists if the patrols came before the monsoon broke.

"Mama," Yara began, "if you took the berth—"

"I heard you the first time, Yara." Adaeze did not raise her voice. She never raised her voice; she lowered everything else around it instead. "I am not taking anything. Sit down. All this standing and pacing—you'd think none of you ever ate a meal indoors."

Yara sat. Kofi had never actually gotten up, but he sat straighter, which was the same gesture in his grammar.

"There is no argument to be had about me," Adaeze continued. "I will tell you why, and then we will speak about Kofi, who is the actual question in this room." She unfolded her hands and laid them palm-down on her knees, a woman planting something. "Your father is buried forty paces from this wall. I dug part of that grave myself. Every morning I put water on it because he died thirsty and I have never forgiven the day for that. If I leave, I leave him to strangers' rain. And worse—" her eyes moved from Emeka to Yara, steady, unhurried, "—worse, I teach my daughter that a person can be pulled up by the roots whenever the weather turns, and call it survival. Your father crossed an ocean to plant us. I will not be the one to dig us back up."

"He planted you to live," Emeka said quietly. "Not to be buried alongside him."

"He planted us *here*. Living *here*. And here is where I stay until staying kills me or the world improves." She said it without drama, the way she said *the rice is ready* or *the roof leaks in the northeast corner*, and that was the most frightening thing about it. There was no arguing with a woman who stated facts.

Yara looked at Kofi, hoping—irrationally, shamefully—that he would side with their mother, that he would refuse too, that the mathematics might collapse entirely into a stalemate she would never have to solve.

Kofi was looking at the floor.

"Then it's Kofi," Emeka said. "Boy. Sixteen years old. Strong as a mast. The ticket is yours."

"No," said Kofi.

The word landed in the room like a dropped plate.

"It isn't mine," he said to the floorboards. "It's Mama's."

"She just refused it."

"Then it sits empty." He looked up finally, and Yara saw that his jaw was set in the exact shape of his mother's, that stubbornness in this family was apparently inherited along the maternal line like eye color. "You don't put a sixteen-year-old boy on a boat alone across the ocean. Even the company clerks know that. They separate children under twelve, not—"

"You're nearly seventeen."

"I'm sixteen," Kofi said, "and I'm not going anywhere without her, and she's not going anywhere, so write whoever you want on the ticket, Yara, it won't make anyone get on the ship." His voice cracked on the last words, and he hated that—it flashed across his face, the fury of a boy whose body kept betraying him into childhood—and he pushed himself up off the wall and stood, too tall for the room, filling it.

"Sit down," Adaeze said.

"I'm standing."

"You'll sit when your legs learn some sense." But there was no iron in it. Yara watched her mother look at her son—watched her take in the height of him, the new breadth of his shoulders, the shadow of a beard he was vain about—and Yara understood that Adaeze was seeing the same terrible thing Yara was seeing: that Kofi had grown past the age where mothers could decide for him, and that neither of them had noticed the exact moment it happened, and now the moment was gone.

"Sixteen years I've kept this boy fed on nothing," Adaeze said, mostly to herself. "Sixteen years of hiding him from every list in this city. And now the one legal door that opens, everyone stands in front of it arguing."

"That's what doors are for," Emeka said. "Arguing in front of."

That startled a laugh out of Kofi—a short, wet one—and Yara felt the room shift a degree, pressure vented but not gone.

Emeka turned from the window. He had been silent longer than was natural for him, and Yara knew why. Emeka argued the way other men breathed; silence from him was not calm but cost.

"Well, uncle?" Yara said. "You've given away both tickets now. Who's left?"

"No one," Emeka said.

"What?"

"I name no one." He came away from the window and crouched by the fire, old man's joints cracking, though Emeka was fifty-three and pretended otherwise. "You want to know what I've learned in fifty years? Names are how they catch us. Every list, every register, every census book—paper is the net, girl. Paper is how they turned free people into cargo. And you're asking me to help you decide which name goes on the company's paper." He spat, ceremonially, into the fire. "I'll not do it."

"The ticket is real," Yara said. "It's legitimate passage. It's the opposite of a net."

"It's a door in a trap that opens once." Emeka looked up at her, firelight in the deep seams of his face. "And it opens onto a country none of us has ever seen, where our name means nothing and no one will know what we were to each other. You want to send one of us into that alone? Choose which one of us gets to be lonely for the rest of their life?" He shook his head slowly. "I've been the one alone. In the mines, before we came to the coast. I know what a single survivor carries. I wouldn't wish the ticket on a dog."

The fire settled. Somewhere outside, the tide was coming in, that vast slow breathing beneath everything the Orchard was built on.

"So that's it," Yara said. Her own voice sounded strange to her, flattened, as if it were coming from across the water. "Mama refuses. Kofi refuses. You refuse. Four names and nobody will hold still long enough to be written down."

"Devotion," Emeka said, "is mostly refusal. Anyone can give a thing away. Refusing takes character."

"It takes stupidity," Yara snapped, and the word came out sharper than she intended, edged with six years of careful silence and one week of Calloway's smile, and she saw all three of them flinch slightly, because Yara did not snap, Yara was the smooth one, the even one, the daughter who never spilled anything. "Forgive me. Forgive me, but listen to me. This is not a game. The *Coromandel* sails in three weeks whether we argue or not, and after it sails there is no other ship, and after the census taker finishes her rounds there may not even be an Orchard. I did not climb this hill tonight to watch a family meeting turn into a competition over who loves whom enough to die politely in place."

Silence. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

Adaeze spoke first, gently. "Then tell us, daughter. Whose name is on the paper?"

And there it was. The question she had carried up the hill folded in eighths, the question that had been riding in her pocket all day like a stone, and every road away from it was blocked now by the bodies of the people she loved, all of them refusing, all of them brave in the specific unbearable way of this family, and Yara opened her mouth—

—to say Kofi's name. She felt it rising in her. He was young, he was strong, he could start again, he could carry the family forward the way seeds carry a tree; and it was right, it was obviously right, it was the answer any clear-eyed stranger would give—

—and she couldn't. The name simply would not cross her lips. Because saying it meant choosing, and choosing meant that Adaeze stayed behind the fence at the dock, and Emeka stayed behind the patrol lines, and the whole weight of survival settled onto a sixteen-year-old boy who still slept with his boots on in case of raids, and Yara—who could falsify a ledger without her pen trembling, who had lied to a government official's face two days ago and been praised for her neatness—could not say one true sentence to save them.

"I can't," she said.

"Yara—"

"I can't choose. Not tonight. Not out loud." Her hand closed over the folded paper in her pocket, crushing it. "If I say a name, it becomes real, and then it can't be taken back, and I—I need one night. One night where no one is decided yet."

Adaeze rose from the stool, crossed the small room, and took her daughter's face in both hands the way she had when Yara was small enough to lift.

"Child," she said. "You have been deciding things for this family since you were twelve years old, quietly, so the rest of us wouldn't feel the weight. Tonight you can't lift it. So put it down. Just for one night." She kissed Yara's forehead. "We will still be here tomorrow. That is the one thing about this family that has never once failed—we are still here."

Yara nodded, because speaking would have broken something.

*

She left late, the lantern trimmed to a coal, the crushed paper still in her pocket. Emeka walked her to the edge of the settlement, through the lanes where the tin roofs caught starlight, past the graves on the rise where her father lay under forty paces of earth and her mother's morning water.

At the boundary—the fallen fig tree that marked where the Orchard ended and the lawful city began—Emeka stopped her with a hand on her arm.

"One night," he said. "But hear an old man before you sleep. Whatever you decide, decide it soon, and don't tell that shipping clerk or that supervisor of yours anything before you do. Men like Calloway count on our arguments lasting longer than their patience."

"I know how Calloway works."

"You know how he works on ledgers." Emeka's grip tightened. "I mean how he works on people. He gives you time the way a fisherman gives line—he lets you run so he can feel exactly where you are." He released her arm. "Go home. Sleep. And Yara—whatever name you write on that ticket, write it in ink you can live inside."

She walked down toward the harbor road, toward the counting house and the narrow rented room that was all her registered life amounted to, and behind her the Orchard settled into its breathing dark.

In her pocket, the paper had gone soft with the heat of her hand. She didn't take it out. She didn't need to. She knew what it said, and she knew, with a certainty that frightened her more than Calloway ever had, that tomorrow morning she would sit down at her desk in the counting house, dip her pen, and begin making entries that had nothing to do with berths at all—because there was another way to buy a future than one seat on one ship, and it would cost her soul in installments, and she had already started paying.

The harbor lights burned ahead of her like a ledger waiting to be balanced.

She picked up her pace and did not look back.
