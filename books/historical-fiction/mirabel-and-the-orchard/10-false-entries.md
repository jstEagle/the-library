## 10. False Entries

The counting house smelled of lamp oil and old paper after dark, when the clerks had gone home and the harbor noise had thinned to the slap of water against pilings. Yara had asked Calloway for the key to the back room herself—had asked it in her flattest voice, the one that said *this is nothing, I am nothing, look through me*—and he had given it to her with a smile that sat on his face like a hat on a hook.

"Take whatever time you need," he'd said. "You're thorough. That's why I trust you."

*Trust.* The word had gone down like a fishbone.

Now she sat alone at the long table with the company's true ledger open before her and a fresh book beside it, its pages white as teeth. On the desk lay three entries in Mr. Calloway's own hand, written on scraps in his careless scrawl: dates, ship names, tonnages of shell. None of them matched anything real. The *Violet Crane* had never sailed in March. The *Sparrowhawk* had carried rice, not pearl shell—and certainly not four hundred weights of it, not from a lagoon where Meridian held no license.

Her job was to make the lies look born, not built.

She dipped her pen. Her hand was steady. That was the worst of it—that after six years of keeping other men's numbers straight, her hand no longer knew how to tremble on command.

---

She began with the date column, because dates were bones. If the dates were right, everything hung on them would stand up under casual eyes. She worked backward from the quarter's end, spacing the false shipments the way real ones spaced themselves—two close together after a monsoon delay, then a gap, then a run of small entries that looked like a diver crew finding its rhythm. She knew the rhythm. She had copied it faithfully for six years, entry by entry, the honest arithmetic of a dishonest trade.

The ink dried glossy in the lamplight. She blew across the page out of habit.

*Four hundred weight of pearl shell, ex Sparrowhawk, landed 14th, entered per manifest.*

There was no manifest. There was only Calloway's scrap of paper, which she had burned in the stove an hour ago, watching the curl of it go black, thinking: *now there is no proof I was told, and no proof I wasn't.*

That was the trick of it, she was learning. Forgery was not mostly ink. It was mostly the careful arrangement of things that could not be proven either way.

She wrote until her wrist ached. Around midnight she stopped, laid down the pen, and pressed her palms flat against the table as if testing whether the wood itself had gone crooked. Outside, a night bird called from the mangroves, three notes descending, like something being counted down.

Six years she had kept herself clean—not clean, exactly; no one on this coast was clean—but *unmarked*. Unnoticed. A woman whose name appeared in no column anyone read twice. She had thought that was safety. Now she understood it had only been a smaller cage with better lighting.

Because here was the thing she could not stop turning over, the stone in her shoe all day: Calloway had not needed to threaten her. Not really. He had named the Orchard once, softly, over tea he'd poured himself, and that single word had done more work than any shouted ultimatum. He knew. He had always known. Every year she had walked the long way round through the fish market, every year she had carried plantains wrapped in cloth as if cloth made them invisible, every year Adaeze had stood in the doorway of the lean-to scanning the road—Calloway had known, and let them live, the way a man lets a field stand because he may want to harvest it later.

Tonight was harvest.

And she was doing the reaping herself, with her own neat hand, because the alternative was soldiers in the mangroves and her mother's name on a deportation list.

*So you are not forced,* said a cold little voice that sounded uncomfortably like Emeka. *Forced men drag their feet. You are writing beautifully.*

She hated him for it. She hated that he was right.

---

She worked three more nights.

By the second night she had developed a system, because systems were how she survived everything: do the hardest entry first, while the mind was fresh; check each figure against the pattern of the true books, not against memory; never write a lie larger than the truth could plausibly hold. Calloway wanted the smuggling debts buried—wanted them converted into ordinary losses, storm damage, spoilage, shell sold below market to buyers who existed only in her ink. He wanted, in short, for the company's books to tell a story in which Meridian Trading was merely unlucky.

Yara was making them unlucky. It was almost funny. Almost.

On the third night, near the bottom of the first false page, she caught herself admiring the work.

That was the moment she put the pen down and walked to the window and stood there breathing harbor air until the admiration died. Because that was how it started—she had seen it in clerks older than her, men who began by balancing books and ended by believing the balance was a kind of virtue. The hand got proud. The hand forgot what it was holding.

Her father had kept accounts too, back before the registration laws tightened and the family's name stopped existing on paper. He used to say figures were the only honest language, because a number could not flatter you. She wondered what he would say if he could see his daughter now, fluent in the dishonest dialect, writing lies so clean they looked like scripture.

*He would say: keep your brother alive,* she answered herself. *Then say the rest.*

She went back to the table.

---

It was past two in the morning on the fourth night when she finished the last entry and closed the false book and sat very still, listening to the building settle around her—the tick of the lamp, the groan of shelving taking the night's damp, somewhere far off a drunk singing half a song to no one.

Done. Or done enough. Calloway would review it tomorrow, run his finger down the columns, find nothing to finger. The debts would dissolve into weather and bad luck, and Meridian Trading would present the Colonial Office a face as smooth as a pearl, and the Orchard would go on breathing in the mangroves a while longer.

A while. That was all any of this bought. She understood that too. Forged books were not freedom; they were a loan against a future reckoning, and the interest compounded daily.

She should go home. She should sleep.

Instead she sat there, looking at the true ledger.

It lay closed at her elbow, bound in green leather gone soft at the corners, the company crest stamped in gold that had dulled to the color of old honey. Inside it were six years of real entries—and among them, scattered through the spring months, the original record of the *Sparrowhawk*'s actual cargo. Rice. And a line item, small and easy to miss, that Yara had found on her second night and had not been able to stop seeing since: a payment, routed through the Singapore office, to a name that meant nothing and a purpose listed as "harbor fees, various."

Harbor fees did not get paid to private names in Singapore. Harbor fees did not get paid at all, not like this, not quietly, not in amounts that matched, almost exactly, the bribes a customs officer might expect to look away from four hundred weights of unlicensed shell.

Someone above Calloway was being paid. Which meant someone above Calloway knew. Which meant the rot went up the mast higher than Yara had imagined—and higher rot was, possibly, rot that someone else might want exposed.

She hadn't fully admitted that thought to herself yet. It had arrived sideways, the way important thoughts do, disguised as idle curiosity. But her hands had already acted on it, because hands sometimes know before heads do.

She opened the true ledger to the spring pages. She took a fresh sheet of the thin copying paper clerks used for duplicates, laid it flat, and began to trace.

Not copy—trace. A copy could be mistaken for theft; a tracing, done quickly, imperfectly, was just a clerk's rough memorandum, the kind anyone might make. She took the *Sparrowhawk* entry, the Singapore payment, the two matching lines from March, pressing hard enough to transfer a ghost of the figures onto her sheet but not so hard as to tear. Her heart was going fast, faster than it had gone all week of forgery, and she noted the irony without enjoying it: forging lies had felt like duty. Copying truth felt like crime.

Why? she asked herself, and the honest answer rose up ugly: because the forgery was for her family, and this was for something else—for some version of events in which Yara Okafor was not merely a woman who saved her own skin by dirtying her hands. Some version in which the hands stayed dirty but pointed, afterward, at the men who had dirtied them first.

Insurance, she told herself. That was all. A sensible precaution. If Calloway ever turned on her—if the bargain soured, if the Orchard was betrayed anyway—then a record of what the books had really said might be the only thing standing between her family and the holds of the Coromandel.

That was the story she told herself, and it was even true.

But it was not the whole truth, and she knew that too, folding the tracing into quarters and then eighths, small enough to disappear into the lining of her sleeve. The whole truth was that she had spent six years being invisible, and invisibility, she was discovering, is its own kind of hunger. Somewhere under the fear, under the self-disgust, under the neat rows of beautiful lies, there was a small hot coal of a different feeling entirely—one she did not have a safe word for yet.

She banked the lamp. She locked the back room. She slipped the folded paper inside her bodice, against her skin, where it warmed immediately and stayed warm, like a secret with a pulse.

---

The walk home took her along the seawall, as it always did, past the customs sheds and the chandlers and the long low warehouse where the Coromandel's agents had painted their notice on the wall in letters a child could read: SAILING IN NINETEEN DAYS. DOCUMENTED PASSENGERS ONLY. Berths limited.

Nineteen days. She had counted them against the forged columns the way other people counted rosaries. Nineteen days of lies, purchased one berth, maybe, through a company connection Calloway dangled like bait on a hook—and after that, whatever came after.

The tide was out. Mud flats gleamed under a half-moon, and the mangroves stood in the shallows like a congregation with its heads bowed. Somewhere in there, past the third channel, behind the screen of roots, the Orchard was sleeping: her mother, Kofi, Emeka, forty other souls who did not exist. She could see nothing of them. That had always been the point. Tonight it felt less like safety than like drowning slowly, in place.

She was nearly at the turn for the fish market when she saw the light.

A lamp burning in the customs office window—at this hour?—and a figure moving behind the frosted glass, unhurried, methodical. Not a patrolman; patrols moved in twos and hurried. This person moved the way Yara herself moved through a ledger: reading.

She stopped in the shadow of the chandler's awning and watched, and after a while the figure came to the door, stepped out, and stood a moment on the customs office steps, closing a leather portfolio with a soft clap that carried across the water.

Even at this distance, even by half-moon light, Yara knew the silhouette. The straight back. The way the woman stood as though the ground had been arranged for her convenience.

Mirabel Hale.

The census taker, leaving the customs office at two in the morning with a portfolio full of who-knew-what, moving through the sleeping port like a question that had learned to walk.

Yara pressed herself deeper into the shadow, one hand rising without instruction to the spot beneath her bodice where the folded tracing lay against her heart. Mirabel Hale paused at the foot of the steps. Turned her head. Looked down the seawall—directly, it seemed, at the chandler's awning, at the darkness where Yara stood—for three seconds, five, long enough for the tide to sigh twice against the mud.

Then she turned away and walked off toward the European quarter, and the lamplight took her, and let her go.

Yara did not move for a long time. When she finally did, her legs were stiff and her mouth was dry, and the thought that followed her home, step by step, all the way to her narrow room, was simple and cold:

Census takers do not visit customs offices at midnight.

And census takers, she suspected, did not send notes the next morning inviting a trading-company clerk to take tea.
