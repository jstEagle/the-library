## 11. Tea with the Enemy

The invitation came on cream card stock, delivered to the counting house by a boy who smelled of salt and government ink.

*Miss Mirabel Hale requests the pleasure of Miss Yara Okafor's company for tea, Thursday at four, rooms above the Customs Office.*

Calloway read it over her shoulder without touching it. "Charm offensive," he said. "They all do it before the real work starts. Be pleasant. Be dull. Tell her nothing she can write down." He tapped the card once with his forefinger, a small proprietary gesture, as though the invitation were a shipment he was approving. "You're a clerk, Okafor. Remember what that means. You count things. You don't *know* things."

Yara said, "Yes, Mr. Calloway," in the voice she used for weather reports and shipping manifests, and put the card in her apron pocket, where it lay against her hip like a coin she hadn't decided whether to spend.

She spent Wednesday evening deciding what to wear, which was absurd, and she knew it was absurd, and she did it anyway. In the end she chose the grey dress with the high collar—the one that made her look like exactly what she was supposed to be: a clerk, respectable, unremarkable, a woman whose most dangerous secret was a preference for strong tea. Armor, Adaeze would have called it. Her mother had a saying: *You dress the way you want the world to argue with you.* The world could not argue with grey wool.

Thursday came down hot and glassy off the harbor. At four o'clock precisely—she would not be early, early was eager; she would not be late, late was insolent—Yara climbed the stairs above the Customs Office and knocked.

"Come in."

The rooms were nothing like she'd expected. She had imagined government quarters: bare, official, a flag in the corner. Instead there were books everywhere, stacked on chairs and window ledges and the floor in leaning towers, and a jar of seashells on the desk between an inkwell and a tin of biscuits, and a map of the harbor pinned to the wall with so many colored threads running from it that it looked less like a map than a wound being stitched shut. One thread—red, thin as a hair—ran from the eastern shallows to a point on the waterfront Yara knew very well. Meridian's warehouse three.

Mirabel Hale stood at the window with her back half-turned, and for a moment Yara simply looked at her. She had seen the census taker only at a distance before, or across a desk: a tall woman, thirty perhaps, with ink-stained fingers and the kind of stillness that most people mistook for patience. Up close there was something else in the stillness. A held breath. A woman listening even when no one spoke.

"You found me," Mirabel said, turning. "I wasn't sure you'd come."

"The invitation had the weight of an order."

"It had the weight of an invitation." Mirabel smiled—a quick, dry thing. "But I take your point. Sit, please. The kettle's been muttering for ten minutes and I refuse to apologize for the state of the room. I'm told chaos frightens clerks."

"I keep ledgers," Yara said, taking the chair offered. "Chaos is just numbers waiting to be counted."

Something flickered across Mirabel's face—approval, quickly banked. She poured tea from a brown pot into two mismatched cups. "Milk?"

"A little."

"Sugar?"

"No, thank you."

"Of course not," Mirabel murmured, almost to herself, and Yara felt the first small cold touch of the afternoon: *of course not*, as if Mirabel had already catalogued her, already filed her under some heading Yara couldn't read.

For a while they talked about nothing, and both of them knew they were talking about nothing, and both of them let it happen anyway. The heat. The monsoon season coming early this year, the fishermen said. The price of copra. Whether the bakery on Cooper Street had changed hands or merely changed its bread. Mirabel asked each question lightly, set each one down like a stone on a table, and Yara answered each one honestly, because honest answers about nothing cost nothing.

Then Mirabel set down her cup and said, "Tell me about the harbor at night."

"The harbor at night?"

"What it looks like. You must see it sometimes, walking home."

"I take the main road," Yara said. "Lamps the whole way."

"Mm." Mirabel turned her cup slowly on its saucer, once, twice. "I ask because I've been walking at night myself. Census work keeps strange hours—you'd be surprised how many people are only home after dark. Fishermen. Divers. People who work when the officials sleep." She looked up. "There's a stretch east of the breakwater where the mangroves come down close to the water. Do you know it?"

Yara held her cup steady. Six years of practice went into holding the cup steady. "I know the breakwater."

"There are lights out there sometimes. Small ones. Cook-fires, I'd guess. And last Tuesday I counted eleven boats drawn up among the roots where no licensed fisherman keeps a boat." Mirabel's voice stayed pleasant, conversational, a woman discussing tides. "Eleven boats make a settlement. Settlements have names. The fishermen call it something—they won't say it to me, but they say it. Their mouths do a little dance around the word." She smiled again, and this time the smile didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Do you know what they call it, Miss Okafor?"

"No."

"They call it the Orchard."

The word sat on the table between them like a third guest.

Yara drank her tea. It had gone lukewarm and tasted faintly of tin. Somewhere below them a cart rattled past, and a gull screamed once and quit, and her heart went on beating with the flat diligence of a machine.

"I've heard the word," she said. "Orchards grow fruit. This is a pearling coast."

"So it is." Mirabel leaned back. "And yet when I entered 'Orchard' into my household register and asked the port authority how many souls resided there, I was told, very smoothly, by a very smooth man, that no such place existed. That the land east of the breakwater is tidal marsh, uninhabited, unsuitable." She paused. "He was lying. I've become rather good at recognizing it. Lying has a texture. Most people think it's in the eyes—it isn't, it's in the hands. Liars hold their cups too steadily."

Yara looked down and realized her knuckles were white around the porcelain. She loosened them, finger by finger, deliberately, letting Mirabel see her do it—because a liar caught would snatch the cup away, but a woman amused by a foolish observation would simply relax.

"You're saying the port authority lies to you," Yara said. "That seems like a complaint for your superiors."

"My superiors," Mirabel said, "are part of why I'm asking you instead."

The room got very quiet. Outside, the harbor light changed as a cloud crossed the sun, and the red thread on the map seemed to darken.

"Miss Hale," Yara said carefully, "why me? I'm a clerk. I copy figures for a trading company. I don't know anything about marshland or boats or—" she gestured vaguely eastward, refusing to look eastward— "whatever it is you're describing."

"Don't you?" Mirabel rose, went to the map, and stood before it with her hands clasped behind her back. "Six years ago a family arrived on this coast. Father, mother, a daughter of nineteen, a son of ten. They came on the *Vesuvia*, listed as domestic labor, and they stepped off the ship and into the city and were never entered in any register again. No tax roll. No census. No church record. Nothing." She turned. "The father is dead now—I found the burial entry, or rather I found the absence of one, which took longer. But the mother lives. The daughter lives. The son would be sixteen now." Her eyes came up, dark and level. "The daughter's name is Yara Okafor. She works as a clerk for the Meridian Trading Company. She is sitting in my room drinking my tea."

The silence stretched until Yara could hear her own pulse in it.

Every instinct she owned screamed at once, a whole flock of birds going up out of the mangroves. Deny. Deny everything. Calloway's voice in her ear: *be dull*. But dullness wouldn't cover this; dullness had never been built to cover this. And some other voice, older and quieter, underneath the fear, said: *she knows. She already knows. What is left to protect by lying?*

Only everything. Only her mother's house among the mangrove roots, her brother's life, the graves.

"That's a remarkable story," Yara said.

"Yes," Mirabel agreed. "It is."

"But it isn't mine. My father was a stevedore. He's buried in the municipal cemetery—section four, you can look it up. My mother keeps rooms on Lantern Row. My brother apprentices with a carpenter. We are registered, Miss Hale. We have papers. I carry mine." She reached into her apron and drew out the folded document, laid it on the table between the teacups, and opened it flat with two fingers. "You may examine it as closely as you like."

It was a good forgery. She had paid a great deal for it six years ago, and it had survived two inspections. Mirabel picked it up and read it slowly, front and back, and Yara watched her read and understood suddenly that she was watching a woman decide something—and that the decision had nothing to do with the paper.

Mirabel set it down. "Section four," she said. "Yes. I visited section four. There's a stone there with the right name and the wrong dates, and the ground around it has been disturbed more recently than the dates allow, and the sexton, when I asked him who had paid for the plot, remembered a woman in a grey dress with a high collar." She looked at Yara's collar. "He had a very good memory for collars."

"He should get better work."

"He should." Mirabel almost laughed—almost; it caught somewhere and died. She sat back down across the table, and for a moment the census taker's armor slipped, and Yara saw beneath it something tired and human and strangely kind. "Why are you doing this?" Mirabel asked quietly. "Not the lie itself—I understand the lie, I'd probably tell the same one in your place. I mean: why lie *to me*? You must know by now I'm not the danger here. Three weeks ago the deportation schedule appeared on the customs board. The *Coromandel* sails within the month, and every unregistered soul on this coast goes on it, or goes into the sea trying not to. Whoever is hiding those people needs help. Real help. And I am offering—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening, as if she'd said one word more than her instructions allowed.

"Offering what?" Yara asked.

"Tea," Mirabel said finally. "I'm offering tea. Everything else would be improper."

They looked at each other across the ruined pretense of the afternoon, and Yara felt something dangerous move in her chest—not trust, nothing so clean as trust, but the ache of wanting it. Two women at a small table, each holding a knife behind her back, each half-hoping the other would put hers down first.

"Your tea is cold," Yara said.

"So it is." Mirabel refilled both cups from the pot anyway, though the pot had gone bitter, and they drank it, and neither of them pretended it was hospitality anymore. It was something else. A truce with no terms. An opening move acknowledged by both players.

At the door, Mirabel said, "Thank you for coming, Miss Okafor. And for the document. It's very fine work."

"Is that a compliment or a threat?"

"It's an observation." Mirabel held the door open. The stairwell air rushed up, hot and salted. "I'll return the paper tomorrow. Unexamined further. Some things are better left unexamined—for now." She paused. "Give my regards to your mother. Lantern Row, wasn't it?"

Yara walked down the stairs with her spine straight and her mind roaring. Out into the street, past the customs board where the *Coromandel*'s sailing notice hung in fresh black paint, past the warehouse where Calloway would be waiting to hear how dull she had been.

She gave him dull. She reported the tea in five flat sentences, the weather, the biscuits, the census forms, and Calloway nodded and dismissed her, satisfied, and went back to his figures.

Only that night, walking the long way home along the lamp-lit main road, did Yara let herself understand what Mirabel had actually done. Not accused her. Not arrested her. Not written a single word in that register. She had shown Yara the map with its red thread, named the Orchard aloud, dismantled the forged grave and the forged address piece by piece—and then folded it all away and poured more tea.

*I know,* the afternoon had said. *Now we both know I know. Your move.*

And beneath it, quieter, almost gentle, almost a plea: *Trust me. I cannot ask you to trust me. But trust me.*

Yara stopped at the corner of Lantern Row, where the lamps ended and the dark began, and stood a long moment looking east toward the mangroves she could not see. Then she turned toward her mother's real rooms and walked into the dark, and behind her, in a room above the Customs Office, a woman with ink-stained fingers wrote nothing at all in her register—and pinned a new thread to the map.

Grey wool. Running from the Customs Office to the eastern shore.
