## 12. Kofi's Secret

He did not come home.

Yara told herself it was nothing, at first. Boys wandered. Sixteen-year-old boys especially wandered, and Kofi had been restless all season, chafing against the boathouse dark, against the rule that he be unseen before dawn and unseen after dusk. He had begun to argue with their mother—not loudly, never loudly in that house where loudness could kill you, but in the low, stubborn register he had inherited from their father, the voice that said *I am here* without saying anything at all.

So when the lamp burned down to its last inch of oil and the doorway stayed empty, Yara folded his blanket flat and told Adaeze he was sleeping at Uncle Emeka's.

Adaeze looked at her the way mothers look at daughters who lie badly.

"He is not there," her mother said. "I sent word this morning."

The words went through Yara like cold water through cloth. She kept her face still—that was her gift, stillness, six years of practicing it in front of men who would have sold her for the price of a stamp—but inside, everything began to move at once.

"Maybe he's with the fishermen," she said. "On the water."

"At night?" Adaeze's hands had gone to each other, knuckle over knuckle, the way they did when she was praying without moving her lips. "Yara. The boy knows the curfew better than any of us. The boy has never once—"

"I'll find him."

"You'll do no such thing. Not in the dark. Not alone."

"Then I'll do it in the morning," Yara said, "and by then it may not matter."

Her mother looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the wind was coming up off the strait, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet, the whole sky holding its breath the way the coast did before monsoon. Adaeze closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had been decided in them, some old arithmetic done quickly and in grief.

"Take the lantern," she said. "And take the short knife under the third floorboard. And come back, Yara. Whatever you find out there—come back."

* * *

She went first to the fisherman's boathouse where the family had sheltered after the raid, a lean-to of salt-bleached planks at the mangrove edge belonging to an old man called Tomas who took no money and asked no questions, which on this coast made him either a saint or a smuggler, and possibly both.

Emeka was there, awake, sitting on an overturned crate in the dark like a man who had been expecting bad news for years and was almost relieved when it arrived.

"He's not here," Yara said.

"No."

"Uncle. Where is he?"

Emeka rubbed his jaw. In the lantern light his face looked carved from something harder than flesh. "I told him not to," he said. "You understand that first. I told him, and I told him again, and then I stopped telling him because a boy who won't hear can't be talked to—only waited after." He reached into the gap between two planks and drew out a bundle wrapped in sacking, and set it on the crate between them.

Yara untied it.

Inside: pearls. Not many. Eleven, twelve of them, loose in a twist of cotton, small and grey-pink, the color of dawn on water. Shell-diving pearls, uneven, imperfect, worth perhaps a fraction of what the Meridian Trading Company paid its divers—and worth more than Yara earned in a year.

The floor seemed to tilt. She put her hand on the crate.

"Where did he get these."

"The reef past the second point. The dead ground, the company divers call it—the reefs too far out for the licensed boats, too deep for daylight work, too close to the patrol lane for anyone with sense." Emeka's voice was flat. "There are free divers who work it at night. Boys mostly. Boys who don't weigh enough to sink and are stupid enough to think that means they can't drown."

"When?"

"Three weeks now, maybe four. He goes out with a man called Ravi, whose cousin keeps a boat. Two hours before slack tide, back before the moon sets. He's been selling to a middleman on Fish Gut Lane—a Chinese pearl-broker, honest as those things go—who doesn't ask where the shells come from, because asking is expensive." Emeka looked at her, and in his eyes was something she had never seen there before: fear wearing shame like a coat. "He's buying passage, Yara. On a coastal trader out of the northern anchorage. Fourteen hundred rupees, deck class, no papers asked. He's got eleven hundred."

Eleven hundred rupees. Yara thought of the ledger pages she had copied and hidden, the neat columns of her own handwriting turning lies into truth, and felt sick—not at Kofi, but at the world that had taught a sixteen-year-old to hold his breath past the point of sense because paper was the only country that would have him.

"He didn't tell me," she said.

"He didn't tell anyone. That was the point." Emeka leaned forward. "He wanted to walk onto that ship with money in his fist and take the burden off your shoulders. Off your mother's. He said—" and here the old man's voice caught, just slightly, just once—"he said, *One of us should be able to give something instead of taking.*"

Yara sat down on the sand floor. The lantern flame bent in the wind coming off the water.

"But tonight," Emeka said quietly. "Tonight he didn't come back from the reef."

* * *

They found Ravi's cousin's boat pulled up above the tideline, hull dry, nets stinking in the dark—which meant the boat hadn't gone out tonight, which meant Kofi had gone out some other way, alone, or with someone Emeka didn't know, which was worse.

Or he hadn't gone to the reef at all.

Yara stood at the waterline with the lantern held high, letting its light swing across the black water, and tried to think like her brother instead of feeling like his sister. Feeling would drown her. Thinking might keep him alive.

Kofi was careful. That was the thing nobody understood about him, because careful quiet boys get mistaken for timid ones. He had survived six years in the Orchard by being exact—about tides, about patrol schedules, about the exact weight of risk against reward. He would not go out alone. He would not go out in weather that was already turning. Which meant—

Which meant he had gone somewhere else. Somewhere he thought was safer than the sea.

She turned back toward the town, and as she turned, the lantern light caught something snagged on the roots of the mangroves: cloth. A shirt, soaked, torn at the shoulder seam. She knew the tear. She had mended that seam herself, twice, with thread the color of the shirt because colored thread was cheaper than matching thread.

The shirt was Kofi's.

For a moment the world narrowed to that wet cloth in her hands, and Yara Okafor, who had not wept since her father's funeral, stood in the mangrove mud and let one breath shake all the way through her.

Then she noticed the smell on the fabric. Not brine. Not blood.

Lamp oil. And under it, faint but unmistakable, the resin-and-rag smell of the Meridian counting house.

* * *

She ran.

Through the dark streets, past the shuttered godowns, along Lantern Row where the lamps ended, the knife heavy in her sash, the wet shirt bundled under her arm like evidence in a case only she could read. Her mind was laying it out even as her legs pumped: Kofi had been near the counting house. Tonight. Why? Not to steal pearls—he'd sold those. To steal something else. Paper. Records. Because Kofi listened the way she listened, and Kofi knew what she knew, or part of it—that Calloway had them by the throat, that the ledgers were the rope, and that a boy with eleven hundred rupees and a ticket to the northern anchorage had decided the best gift he could leave behind him was a fire.

Or a theft. Something Calloway couldn't use.

The counting house rose ahead of her, dark against the darker sky, its shutters latched, its windows blind. She circled it once, twice, heart slamming. No smoke. No broken glass. No watchman stirring.

Then she saw the light.

Not in the counting house. Beyond it, down the alley between the warehouse and the seawall—a thin yellow line under a door, the kind of light a single shielded lamp makes, the kind of light someone uses when they want to see without being seen.

She put her hand on the door. It gave. Unlocked—or left so.

Inside was the company's shell store: sacks of unsorted shell, the reek of rotting nacre, and, kneeling among them with a shielded lantern and a diver's knife between his teeth, alive, breathing, furious, terrified—

Kofi.

The relief hit her like a wave and knocked every thought out of her head. She crossed the room in three strides and grabbed his arm and hauled him up, and for a moment neither of them spoke, both of them frozen, listening for footsteps that did not come.

"You're supposed to be on the reef," she whispered finally.

"You're supposed to be home." His eyes went to the bundle under her arm and widened. "That's my shirt."

"It was in the mangroves. Stinking of lamp oil. Do you know what I thought?" Her whisper cracked. "Do you know what I spent the last hour thinking?"

"I'm sorry." He said it fast, the way boys say sorry, meaning it completely and understanding none of why. "The shirt was a mistake, I cut myself on the oysters coming in over the wall and I couldn't wear it through the streets—"

"You've been diving." It wasn't a question. "The dead ground. At night. Alone."

"Not alone. Not usually." His chin came up, and she saw their father in it, that same stubborn lift, that same *I am here*. "Ravi's cousin wouldn't run me anymore after the raid scared everyone. So I found another way. And I'm close now, Yara. Eleven hundred and forty. Another month and I don't need the berth you're killing yourself to arrange. Another month and nobody has to choose anybody."

"And if a patrol boat finds you on the dead ground? If your lungs give out at forty feet? If the reef takes you the way it took Pa's cousin—"

"The reef won't."

"The reef doesn't care!" The whisper tore itself out of her louder than she meant, and they both went still again, two statues in a room full of dead shell. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Kofi. There are things you don't know. Things about the company, about what Calloway has on us, about what's coming. You diving at night isn't brave. It's a lit match carried through a powder store."

"Then tell me the things." He stepped closer. In the shielded lamplight his face was very young and very old at once. "Tell me everything, because I'm done being protected. Ma protects me. You protect me. Uncle Emeka protects me. Everybody protecting everybody until nobody tells anybody anything and we all drown separately." His hand came up, and it was shaking, and he let her see it shake. "I found something tonight, Yara. Down here, in the shell store. That's why I came back for the shirt—no. No, that's a lie. I came back because I couldn't sleep knowing what I'd seen."

"What did you see?"

Kofi reached into the sack beside him and drew out a wooden crate, small, the kind the company used for graded pearls bound for the assay office. The lid had been pried loose and nailed back crooked. Inside, packed in bran: pearls. Dozens of them. Graded, tagged, wrapped in paper slips bearing the Meridian mark—and beneath the top layer, when Kofi tipped the crate toward the lamp, a false bottom, and beneath the false bottom, bars. Grey metal, dull, stamped with marks that meant nothing to Yara and everything to anyone who knew coin.

Smuggled silver. Hidden in the shell store. In the company's own house.

"Calloway's books balance because this balances them," Kofi whispered. "Pearls going out one way, silver coming in the other, and the difference buried in shell nobody counts. I've been diving for scraps off the dead ground while the real treasure sits fifty feet from my sister's desk." He closed the lid gently, the way you close a coffin. "I wasn't going to take it. I was going to remember it. But Yara—if the patrols sweep this street tomorrow, they find it on us, or they find it and Calloway buries us with it. Either way—"

Footsteps. Outside. More than one pair, unhurried, the unhurried tread of men who own the ground they walk on.

Kofi snuffed the lamp. In the sudden dark, Yara's hand found her brother's wrist and gripped it, and her mind, that cold clerk's mind that had saved them a hundred times, laid out the map of the building in one flash: shell store, loading door, the drain-cut behind the seawall, the mangrove path.

The footsteps stopped outside the door.

A key turned in the lock—slowly, from the outside, by someone who had locked this door behind himself and was now unlocking it, which meant whoever was coming in already knew the room was empty, and was wrong.

In the blackness, Kofi breathed one word into his sister's ear:

"Calloway."
