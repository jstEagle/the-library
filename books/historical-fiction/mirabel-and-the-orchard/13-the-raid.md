## 13. The Raid

The tip came on a Tuesday, though Yara would not learn that until much later. What she learned first was the sound: boots on packed earth at an hour when no honest boots moved, and then the whistle—two short notes, one long—the signal Emeka had drilled into all of them since she was a child small enough to hide under her mother's skirts.

She was not in the Orchard when it happened. She was in the counting house, sharpening a pencil that did not need sharpening, because Calloway had asked her to stay late and his asking had begun to feel like a hand on the back of her neck.

The pencil point snapped.

"Something wrong, Okafor?" Calloway stood in the doorway of his office with a cup of tea he never drank, steam rising past his face like a veil. He had the look of a man listening to music only he could hear.

"No, sir."

"You flinched."

"The pencil broke."

"Ah." He set the cup down on her desk without being invited to, which was itself a kind of message. "You know what I admire about you? You never ask questions you don't already know the answers to. Most people can't help themselves. They ask and ask, hoping the world will lie to them kindly." He smiled. "You just wait for the world to tell the truth. It always does, eventually. Usually at the worst possible moment."

He went back into his office. Yara sat very still and listened to the harbor sounds through the window—the winches, the gulls, the flat slap of water against pilings—and tried to hear beneath them anything that might be wrong. But the port told her nothing. The port never told her anything until it was too late.

* * *

It was old Mama Efua who reached her, an hour before closing, shuffling into the counting house lobby with a basket of dried fish she had no business selling to clerks. She had sold fish to the Meridian building perhaps four times in six years, always on days when something needed saying that could not be written down.

Yara met her at the counter. "How much for the lot?"

Efua's eyes were wet-rimmed and steady. "Three shillings. My grandson says there's a buyer at the boathouse tonight who pays better than you do." A pause, deliberate as a dropped anchor. "He says the whole family should come see. Tonight. Before the tide turns."

Yara counted out three shillings with fingers that wanted to shake and did not. "Tell your grandson I'll come after work."

"Come quick," Efua said, loud enough for the room, and shuffled out.

Calloway's door stayed closed. Yara stared at it for a long moment, hating how much she could read from a rectangle of wood. Then she gathered her things, left the falsified ledger open on her desk exactly as he liked it left, and walked out into the heat of the evening with the fish wrapped in brown paper like a gift she could not eat.

* * *

The boathouse belonged to Tomas Reyes, a fisherman who owed the Okafor family more than money—a debt from the year of the fever, when Adaeze had nursed his wife through it with bark teas and stubborn hands while the registered doctors refused to cross into the unregistered quarter. Tomas kept a lamp burning low in the back room where nets were mended, and by the time Yara arrived, pushing through the hanging strips of oiled canvas, the whole family was there: Adaeze seated straight-backed on a crate as if it were a throne; Kofi crouched by the wall, salt-crusted to the elbows, still smelling of the sea he wasn't supposed to touch; Emeka standing at the crack of the door, watching the dark water.

"They came at dusk," Emeka said without turning. "Six of them. Port authority, but moving like soldiers. They walked the eastern edge of the settlement first—past the well, past the old chapel—and they had paper in their hands. Lists."

"How many names?" Adaeze asked.

"More than ours." He finally turned. His face in the lamplight looked older than it had a week ago, carved rather than aged. "They had the layout of the place, Yara. Every lane. Every house. Somebody drew them a map."

The words landed in Yara's stomach like swallowed stones. She thought of Calloway's tea going cold untouched. She thought of the anonymous tips that moved through colonial ports like weather—no one saw them form, everyone felt them arrive.

"It's him," Kofi said. "It has to be."

"It doesn't have to be anyone," Yara said. "It could be a neighbor. It could be—"

"A map, Yara." Emeka's voice was quiet, which was worse than shouting. Quiet was what Emeka did when he had stopped arguing and started grieving. "A map of lanes that exist on no official document. Someone gave them the shape of our lives."

Adaeze raised one hand, and the room went silent the way it always did, the way it had since Yara was a girl—her mother's hand a small flag of absolute authority. "Emeka is right that we cannot go back tonight. Perhaps not tomorrow either. We will stay here, with Tomas, until we understand what they know." Her eyes found Yara's. "What do they know?"

"I don't know yet."

"But you will find out." It was not a question. Adaeze did not ask questions she expected would go unanswered. "You sit inside their house, daughter. You hold their pen. Find out whether this is a warning shot or the beginning of the end, and find out quickly."

Kofi shifted against the wall. "I could go back tonight. Check the house. If they've marked the doors—"

"You will not," Adaeze said.

"If they take the house, they take everything. Father's letters. The—"

"*You will not.*" And there it was, under the iron: fear, naked and maternal, the fear of a woman who had already buried a husband and would not bury a son. Kofi subsided. In the lamplight Yara saw the dive-bruises on his forearms, the rope burns on his palms, and said nothing about them, and hated herself for the silence, and kept it anyway. One catastrophe at a time.

Tomas appeared with bread and hard cheese and a jug of watered wine, and they ate sitting on netting crates in a fisherman's boathouse like fugitives, because that was what they were now, and the terrible thing—the thing Yara would carry for years—was how practiced it felt. How smoothly they made themselves small. Six years of invisibility, and the muscles of hiding had grown so strong that using them again was almost a comfort.

That was the moment she understood what Calloway had done to them. Not the raid itself—that was only weather—but this: he had taught her family to feel at home in flight.

* * *

She went back to the port before dawn, walking the long way along the seawall so she could watch the light come up gray and indifferent over the water. The Coromandel lay at anchor in the outer roads, a black shape with white superstructure, and even at this distance Yara could imagine the berths stacked inside it like drawers, each one waiting for a name.

At the counting house, the answer was waiting too.

There were two constables at the gate—not unusual, except that they were talking to the gatekeeper and glancing toward the company offices, and one of them held a folded paper the way men hold papers they have been told to hold. Inside, Calloway was already at his desk, and when Yara passed his open door he said, pleasantly, "Okafor. A word."

She went in. He gestured her to the chair across from his desk, and for a while he simply looked at her, the way a man looks at a horse he is deciding whether to buy.

"There was a regrettable incident last night," he said. "A sweep of some irregular settlements east of the harbor. Unregistered persons, you understand. The authorities are quite serious about it now that the Coromandel's schedule is fixed." He laced his fingers over his waistcoat. "Naturally, the company cooperates fully with such operations. We provided certain... cartographic assistance."

"Did you," Yara said.

"We did." He watched her face with genuine interest, the way a boy watches an insect. "And I'll confess something else, since we're being colleagues about it: the tip was anonymous, but the map was mine. Drawn from memory, I'm afraid—I've never actually visited the place. One does one's best." He unfolded a newspaper on his desk, smoothed it flat. "Now. Here is what happened next, and I want you to attend carefully, because it concerns you directly. The sweep found almost nothing. A few empty houses. Some cold cooking fires. Your people scattered like quail, Okafor. Except—" he turned the newspaper around, and there, in the third column of the shipping page, was a paragraph she would read a thousand times in the years to come—

*"One man detained at the eastern edge of the settlement, giving the name Emeka Okonkwo, claimed to be awaiting a fishing boat. Held pending verification of status."*

"He's at the harbor lockup," Calloway said. "And by Friday he'll be on a list, and by the time the Coromandel sails he'll be on the Coromandel, unless someone intervenes on his behalf. Someone with standing. Someone the authorities trust." He leaned back. "Someone, say, whose employer is willing to vouch for him—as a favor. Companies can do that, you know. Vouching. It's remarkable what a signature can accomplish when it wants to."

Yara kept her hands flat on her knees. Under the desk, out of sight, her nails pressed crescents into her palms. "What do you want, Mr. Calloway?"

"Want?" He laughed softly. "I want what I've always wanted. Accuracy. The census taker, Miss Hale, has been collecting statements from dockworkers. Statements about shipments. About cargo that weighed less than its manifests claimed. She has been writing these statements in a little green book, and she keeps the book in her rooms at the Pier Hotel, and she has been under the impression that no one at this company knows about it." He picked up his cold tea and drank it, grimaced, set it down. "I want that book's contents discredited before they reach the Colonial Office. And you, Okafor, are going to help me, because you are going to tell me everything she's asked you, everything you've told her, and every piece of paper you've ever copied or carried or dreamed about. And when you've done that, I'll sign my name to a letter that puts your uncle on a registered berth instead of a deportation hold."

"And if I don't?"

"Then Friday comes, and Friday is very punctual." He smiled, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen, because it was kind. "You're not a coward, Okafor. I've watched you for six years. You're the opposite—you're the person who stays calm so everyone else can panic. So stay calm now. This isn't cruelty. It's arithmetic. One old man against one career. Even you can do that sum."

She stood. Her legs held, which surprised her. "I'll need until tomorrow."

"Take until noon," Calloway said, returning to his newspaper. "Noon is when the lockup takes its morning count. After that, names start becoming numbers."

* * *

She found Mirabel Hale on the seawall, of all places, standing at the rail with the wind pulling at her plain gray dress, watching the Coromandel as if she could read its passenger manifest from the water. Up close, Yara noticed things she had trained herself not to notice before: the ink stain on Mirabel's right middle finger that never quite washed off; the way her eyes moved constantly, cataloguing, filing; the small green book in her coat pocket, its corner visible like a threat.

"Miss Okafor." Mirabel didn't turn. "You're up early."

"So are you."

"I sleep badly in this climate. The heat, the noise, the general atmosphere of things unsaid." Now she turned, and her gaze was direct and unhurried. "There was a raid last night."

"So I heard."

"One man detained. Emeka Okonkwo." Mirabel said the name gently, the way one sets down something breakable. "The interesting thing about the raid is the map. The patrols had a map of lanes that appear in no survey, no tax roll, no record anywhere in this colony's archives. Which means someone drew it recently, from knowledge, and gave it away." She paused. "Which means someone chose to make the invisible visible. That's a rare thing, Miss Okafor. Most people guard their secrets like water in a drought. To spend one—to spend *someone else's*—takes a particular kind of desperation or a particular kind of malice. Sometimes both."

The wind came off the harbor and pushed between them, smelling of brine and tar and the far, clean nothing of open sea.

"Why are you telling me this?" Yara asked.

"Because you're the only person in this port who looks at me the way you do." Mirabel's voice stayed level, almost mild. "Everyone else looks at me and sees a clerk with a census book. You look at me and see a loaded weapon. People only do that when they know what weapons are for." She took the green book from her pocket, held it a moment, put it back. "If there were a family—any family—scattered somewhere east of the harbor, I would want them to know that a detained man named Emeka has until Friday. And I would want them to know that I keep accurate records. Accurate records have saved more lives than brave speeches ever have. Good day, Miss Okafor."

She walked away along the seawall, gray dress fading into gray morning, and Yara stood at the rail doing the arithmetic Calloway had assigned her—one old man against one career—and finding that the sum would not resolve. Because Calloway had left something out of his equation, and Mirabel had just shown her what it was: a third term, a woman with a green book and a memory for names, standing between the two sides of the ledger like a blade laid flat across the scale.

Friday was coming. Noon was coming. And Yara Okafor, who had spent six years perfecting the art of being unseen, understood at last that the only way out was through the one thing she had never dared to do.

She would have to be seen.
