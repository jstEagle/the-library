## 14. Bargaining Chips

The note came inside the morning's post, folded once and sealed with no wax at all—a liberty no proper gentleman would take, which was precisely the point. *Mr. Calloway requests Miss Okafor's presence at his residence. Eight o'clock. Come alone.*

Yara read it twice at her desk in the counting house while around her the clerks scratched and murmured and the ceiling fan pushed the heat from one corner of the room to another like a servant who had given up. Eight o'clock. After dark. At his home, not his office. Alone.

Every detail of it said: this is not business. Every detail of it said: this is leverage being spent.

She set the note down and squared its edges against the desk's lip, because her hands wanted something orderly to do, and then she looked across the room to where Calloway's own chair stood empty, its leather still holding the shape of him. He had left an hour ago without telling anyone where he was going. She knew where he had been going. Everyone in the port who mattered knew by now that Emeka Okafor sat in a cell at the harbor patrol house, charged with nothing yet—*pending inquiry*, the charge sheet said, as if inquiry were a place you could be kept overnight—and that inquiries, in this city, lasted exactly as long as someone wished them to last.

Six years Yara had kept her family off paper. Six years of silence worn smooth as sea glass. And now her uncle sat behind bars because she had hesitated one day too long.

*Friday,* Calloway had said. Noon.

It was Wednesday.

* * *

Calloway's house stood on the ridge above the harbor, white-walled and blind-shuttered, with a garden that had been arranged rather than grown—croton hedges cut to a ruler's edge, a fountain that trickled on a schedule regardless of drought. A boy took her parasol at the door without meeting her eyes. A houseman led her through rooms that smelled of furniture polish and old gin, past a dining table laid for one, into a study where the shutters were closed against the evening and a single lamp burned green over the desk.

Calloway stood at the window with a glass in his hand, looking out through the slats at nothing, or at the harbor lights below, or at some private arithmetic only he could see. He did not turn when she entered. He let her stand for a full ten seconds before he spoke, and she understood that too was deliberate—that he was showing her the shape of his patience, the way a man shows a watch chain.

"Miss Okafor." Now he turned, and smiled, and the smile reached everything except his eyes, which stayed flat and pale as two coins. "You'll take a drink?"

"No, thank you."

"Of course not. Sit anyway."

She sat. The chair was deeper than it looked; she felt herself sink into it, and hated the small animal comfort of it.

He settled behind the desk, but loosely, one ankle crossed over his knee, the glass balanced on the armrest. For a moment he simply regarded her, and she made herself regard him back, and between them the lamp hissed faintly like something cooking.

"You've been busy," he said. "Copying pages out of my books."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "I imagine you'd say that." He drank, set the glass down, turned it slowly by its base. "Here is my difficulty, Miss Okafor. I have a ledger problem—you know about the ledger problem, you've been balancing it beautifully—and I have a census problem, and now I have a Mirabel Hale problem, and all three of these problems have recently begun to talk to each other. Do you know what I mean when I say they talk to each other?"

"I'm sure I couldn't say."

"She visited your tea table yesterday." He watched her face with the mild attention of a man watching weather come in. "Two hours. The housegirl told me. Housegirls tell me everything; it's why I pay them so little and remember their names." A thin pause. "You gave her tea and biscuits and told her nothing, I hope."

"I told her what I tell everyone. Household rosters. Port schedules."

"Mm." He rose, went to the shelf behind him, and drew out a folder—not the company folder, not anything with Meridian stamped on it, but a cheap government folio, buff-colored, dog-eared at the corners. He laid it on the desk between them without opening it, the way a card player lays down a card he doesn't need to look at.

"Do you know what this is? This is the detention report on one Emeka Okafor. Uncle, I believe. Mother's brother. Lives—" he opened the folder at last, delicately, and read aloud as though from a menu, "—*lived*, until Tuesday, in the settlement locally designated the Orchard. Unregistered. All of you, unregistered, going back—let me see—six years, at least. Your mother. Your brother. You, Miss Okafor, though you've been careful enough about yourself that we'll never prove it, will we. No matter." He closed the folder. "The point is, I know. I have known for some time. I have known since before you ever balanced a single false entry for me, and you should sit with that a while, because I want you to understand that every kindness I've shown you has been priced in advance."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs a clock struck the half hour, unhurried, indifferent.

"What do you want?" Yara asked. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"What I've always wanted. Silence, and clean books, and Friday noon to pass unremarked." He leaned forward. "But there's a new item on the bill, I'm afraid, and it's this: the woman Hale keeps papers. Notes, copies, correspondence with the Colonial Office—I've seen the envelopes, they're not subtle—statements from fishermen, dockhands, God knows who else. A whole little case, building itself brick by brick in a rented bungalow with lace curtains. If that case reaches the Governor, Meridian falls, and when Meridian falls, do you know who falls with it?" He didn't wait for her answer. "Every name in those books. Every clerk who touched them. Including a clever girl who forged entries in her own unmistakable hand, entries that will look very much less like coercion and very much more like ambition once I've finished explaining them."

"And if I refuse?"

"If you refuse, Emeka goes on the Coromandel. Not in a berth—in the hold, in irons, listed as a stowaway taken at the Orchard after the tip-off that I did not send, officially. Deportation of an unregistered man caught hiding in a raid. It writes itself, Miss Okafor. Patrols love a tidy story." He said it without heat, without cruelty even, and that was the worst of it—the gentleness of a man discussing freight. "Or perhaps he simply stays in his cell until the inquiry concludes. Inquiries are slow. Cells are damp. Your uncle is sixty-one and has a chest that fills with water in wet weather, if family letters can be believed."

"They can't be," Yara said. "You've never seen a family letter."

"I've seen enough." He spread his hands, an open gesture, almost warm. "So. Here is what I'm asking, and I want you to hear how reasonable it is. You go to the Hale woman. You are frightened, penitent, grateful—she already half-believes you're a victim, God help her, she has that softness clerks learn to smell. She'll show you what she has; women like her always do, they mistake confession for conversion. And then you bring it to me, or better, you burn it—burn it in her own grate, let her watch, let her understand that the port protects its own. In exchange: Emeka walks free tomorrow morning, charges dissolved, no record. In exchange: your family stays invisible until the Coromandel sails, and the berth you've been circling stays available. In exchange: you keep your hands clean, your position, your future." He picked up the glass again. "Everyone keeps what they most want. That's all a bargain ever is."

Yara looked at the buff folder lying shut on the desk. At the man behind it, comfortable as a landlord. At the green lamp burning its small dishonest light.

Her uncle's chest, filling with water. Kofi's sixteen-year-old shoulders. Her mother's spine going straighter every time the world pressed on it. And somewhere across the town, a woman with lace curtains and government envelopes, keeping faith with a version of events that could save them all—if it survived long enough to be heard.

"Yes," Yara said.

Calloway paused, glass halfway to his mouth.

"Yes," she said again, and let her shoulders drop, and let six years of practice put a tremor into her voice that she did not feel. "I'll do it. But not tonight. She expects me—she trusts me a little, but if I move too fast she'll smell it. Give me until Thursday night. I'll come to you with the papers, or word that they're destroyed. Friday noon passes however you need it to pass."

For a long moment he studied her, and she bore it, the way one bears a searchlight, by being exactly what it expects to find: a tired, frightened girl doing arithmetic with the only currency she had left.

"Thursday," he said at last. "Midnight. If you're late, the price goes up." He rose, opened the study door, and became, in the space of a breath, gracious again—walked her to the hall himself, called for her parasol, held the front door as though she were a guest worth having. "Miss Okafor. You're making the right choice. Families are the only thing worth anything in this world. Everything else is paper."

"Everything else is paper," she repeated, and meant something entirely different by it, and walked out into the dark with her heart going like a hammer in a hull.

* * *

She did not go straight back to the counting house quarters. She walked the long way, along the harbor road, past the moored lights of the patrol boat that guarded the cells, and stopped where the fence met the seawall, close enough to see the small high window of the blockhouse glow yellow.

Emeka was in there. Sixty-one years old, hands ruined from thirty years of diving before the reefs were closed to men like him, a chest that filled with water in the wet season—which was true, whatever she'd said to Calloway, true as scripture, because she had listened to him breathe every monsoon of her childhood, that wet bellows sound, like the sea trying to get in.

And she had just promised to hand a man's ruin to the one person trying to build a case against it.

She gripped the fence rail. Below her, black water worked at the pilings, patient, taking everything eventually.

Because here was the truth she could not say in that lamplit study: she meant to keep her word to Calloway exactly as far as it bought her uncle's freedom, and not one inch further. She would walk into Mirabel Hale's bungalow tomorrow with her hat in her hands and her guilt arranged becomingly, and she would ask to see the papers—and then she would decide, in that room, with those papers in reach, whose side the truth was actually on.

It might be Calloway's. She was honest enough with herself to admit the possibility. If the evidence was thin, if the Colonial Office would file it and forget it, if Emeka rotted through a wet season waiting for justice to finish its tea—then blood was blood, and truth was a luxury imported like everything else in this port.

And if the evidence was strong—

If it was strong, then she would do the thing she was already, horribly, beginning to see the shape of. Something that would free her uncle without handing Calloway a weapon. Something dangerous. Something that required her to trust the enemy with the only thing she had left.

She turned from the fence and walked on, past the customs shed, past the shuttered chandlers, and saw ahead of her, at the corner by the ice works, a figure standing under the streetlamp with a document case in her hand, as though she had been walking this same road and stopped—as though she had known, somehow, that a girl would come by this way tonight needing to be found.

Mirabel Hale stepped out of the lamplight, and said quietly:

"Miss Okafor. We need to talk about your uncle."
