## 15. What Yara Knows

The note came tucked inside the census book itself.

Yara found it on her desk at the counting house, folded into quarters, no name on the outside—only a pressed sprig of jasmine, the kind that grew wild along the seawall behind the customs sheds. *The harbor court garden. Four o'clock. The bench under the banyan. No one watches a woman reading in a garden.* Beneath it, in Mirabel Hale's small, upright hand: **I know what Calloway asked of you. I know what he is holding over you. I will not ask again.**

Yara read it three times, then held the corner of it to the lamp flame and watched it curl into ash in the brass dish she kept for sealing wax. The jasmine she dropped into her tea, because wasting something sweet felt like a worse sin than burning evidence.

Four o'clock. She had until four o'clock to decide what kind of fool to be.

* * *

She spent the morning doing exactly what Mr. Calloway expected of her, because that was the discipline that had kept six years of secrets intact: when the ground shakes, you keep writing. She entered the fictitious cargo of the *Selene Maris* into the shipping register in her clean hand, dated it back eleven days, initialed it, blotted it, closed the book. Somewhere in the hold of the *Coromandel* or the belly of the customs archive, that entry would sit like a seed, waiting to grow into whatever lie needed growing.

Then she took off her clerk's collar, folded it into her satchel, and walked out through the yard without looking at anyone.

The harbor court garden was a narrow strip of civic vanity between the courthouse and the sea wall—frangipani, a banyan whose roots had cracked the paving, two benches facing the water where clerks ate their lunches and nobody said anything worth overhearing. At ten past four, Mirabel Hale sat on the northern bench with a book open on her knee and her hat brim tilted against the sun, and she did not look up when Yara sat down at the far end of the same bench, which was its own kind of message: *we are strangers; we have never met.*

"You got my note," Mirabel said quietly, eyes still on her page.

"I burned your note."

"I imagined you would." A pause. The tide was going out, dragging the smell of mud and salt across the wall. "You're punctual for someone who didn't come."

Yara looked straight ahead at the gray-green water. Her hands were steady in her lap, and she marveled at them, those traitorous competent hands that could forge an entry at eight in the morning and then carry themselves here, to this bench, to this woman, as if they belonged to someone braver.

"If I tell you what I know," Yara said, "and you do nothing with it—or worse, nothing *can* be done with it—then Calloway wins either way. He'll have my family's location from his own sources, and he'll have my confession besides."

"That's true."

"It doesn't frighten you, saying that?"

"No," Mirabel said. "It should frighten you. And if it does, and you speak anyway, then I'll know everything else you say is true."

There it was again—that flat, unhurried honesty that made Yara feel like a locked door being politely asked to open itself. She had rehearsed a dozen versions of caution on the walk over. She found she had left all of them in the street.

"The company ships pearls out in double bottoms," Yara said. "Declared cargo goes through customs clean. Undeclared weight travels in the ballast compartments of the same hulls—the *Selene Maris*, the *Cormorant*, twice last year the *Adelaide Rose*. Calloway books it as sand and stone. When the debts from the smuggling runs came due—there were losses, bribes that cost more than the take—he couldn't hide them in the ledgers anymore, not with the new audit schedules coming from the Colonial Office. So he needs the census."

Mirabel turned a page she hadn't read. "Go on."

"He wants unregistered persons written into the rolls as registered labor—phantom households, phantom wages, so the payroll tallies match the books he's already falsified. My handwriting does the work. It always does the work. Nobody questions the indentured girl's figures; questioning them would mean admitting he'd let an indentured girl keep his books for six years." Yara heard her own voice go dry and precise and hated how much it sounded like the counting house. "In exchange, he keeps silent about the Orchard."

"And the Orchard is?"

"My family. My mother. My brother. My uncle. Nine others who came off the labor ships and stepped off the records the same day they stepped onto shore. Unregistered means invisible. Invisible means alive. Until three weeks ago, invisible meant safe."

"Until the Coromandel," Mirabel said.

"Until the Coromandel." The name sat in the air between them like weather. "Deportation ship. Three weeks, they say, though the monsoon may argue with the schedule. Only documented passengers can book berths, and there is one berth—one—that Calloway's connections can make legitimate. He offered it to me like a man offering a starving woman one loaf. Choose who lives visibly. Choose who sails and who stays to be swept."

Mirabel closed her book at last. In the slanted light Yara saw her properly for the first time since the tea—not the census taker with her patient, cataloguing gaze but something wearier underneath, a woman who had been carrying her own arithmetic and finding it came out ugly too.

"You've chosen?" Mirabel asked.

"I can't." The words came out smaller than Yara intended. "My mother won't leave my father's grave. Kofi says the berth belongs to her. She's already signed it to him—I found the transfer paper hidden in the rice tin two nights ago, signed in her careful schoolroom hand. Sixteen years old and he thinks he's fooling everyone, diving the night reefs to buy his own passage so no one has to choose at all. My uncle Emeka—" Her voice caught, and she steadied it the way she'd been taught to steady everything, with silence. "My uncle was taken in the raid last week. Calloway's raid. His price for letting Emeka go was Mirabel Hale's evidence, destroyed."

The silence after that lasted long enough for a fishing boat to cross the whole gap between the breakwater and the quay.

"So you know about the raid," Mirabel said at last. "You know it wasn't chance."

"Calloway told me himself. An anonymous tip, he called it. He wanted me to understand that the Orchard is only invisible as long as he finds it convenient." Yara reached into her satchel, and her hands were finally, finally shaking. "He also wanted me to destroy your collected papers. I told him yes. I bought three days with a promise I never meant to keep."

And then she did the thing she had crossed the whole town to do and had not believed, until this moment, that she would actually do: she took out the copied ledger page.

She had made it nine days ago, in the blue hour before dawn, pressing hard enough to take a true copy through thin paper, copying the entry that showed the *Selene Maris* departing with forty chests of declared pearl shell and returning, per the manifest, light—and the second column, in Calloway's own initials, showing payment received from a buyer in a port the company officially did not trade with. Insurance, she had told herself at the time, not admitting what it was insurance *against*, or whom.

"Page forty-one of the shipping register, duplicated in Calloway's private book, which lives behind the third panel of his office cabinet. The panel sticks; lift and push. There are eleven more pages like it. I can tell you every date, every hull, every figure, because I entered half of them myself and memorized the rest out of self-disgust." She held the paper out. "It implicates me. Every line of it passes through my hand. If you use it, you use me with it. That's not a condition. It's just the shape of the thing."

Mirabel did not take the page at once. She looked at it the way she had once looked at the census roll—with the particular attention of a person who understood that paper was never just paper, that a name written down could shelter a family or sign a death warrant depending on whose hand held the pen.

"Do you know what I am?" she asked.

"A census taker who asks too many questions."

"I work for the Colonial Office. Office of Trade Inspection, out of the capital. The census is cover—it gives me a reason to knock on every door in this port and write down what I find without anyone asking why a woman would care." She took the ledger page now, carefully, by its edges. "Six months I've been building a case against Meridian Trading. Six months, and I had suspicion, pattern, rumor. What I did not have was the inside of the books. Do you understand what you've just handed me? Not the page. The *witness*. The one person in this port whose testimony turns paper into proof."

"I understand what I've handed you," Yara said. "A woman with no protection and a family with less."

"Yes. That too." Mirabel folded the page into her glove, and for a moment her composure slipped—not much, just enough for Yara to see the effort beneath it, the cost of the calm. "I want to be honest with you, because you were honest first, and honesty seems to be the currency we've decided on. I suspected you weeks ago. The way you answered my questions—too smoothly, too completely. People hiding something small hesitate. People hiding something large perform. You performed beautifully, and I hated watching it, because I knew what performance under that much pressure costs." She paused. "I also knew that whoever Calloway leaned on would be leaning back alone unless someone stood beside her. So I let your denial stand, and I waited, and I'm not proud of the waiting."

"You could have waited until we were both hanged."

"I nearly did." Mirabel looked out at the water. "There's a version of my job where I protect the case and not the people in it. I've watched men do that version their whole careers. They get promoted." She turned back, and her eyes were direct and tired and, Yara thought, not unkind—which was somehow harder to bear than kindness. "I don't intend to be promoted."

The relief hit Yara sideways, the way grief does—she had braced for exposure, for the cold procedure of giving evidence, and instead here was this stranger on a public bench saying *I see you, and I am not going to spend you*. Her throat tightened. She kept her eyes on the frangipani tree and breathed until the tightness passed, because a clerk learns early that the face is the last document you surrender.

"What happens now?" she asked.

"Now I take your page to the capital, by hand, tonight's mail boat, under seal. Now you go back to the counting house tomorrow morning and forge nothing further—write only true entries, however bad they look, because true entries can't be used against us and false ones can. Can you do that? Calloway will notice."

"Calloway will notice within a day."

"Good. Let him notice. A cornered man makes mistakes, and mistakes are evidence." Mirabel stood, smoothing her skirt, becoming once more the plain census taker whom no one troubled to watch. "Three days, perhaps five, before the inspectors arrive from the capital. Stay close to your family. Keep Emeka's name ready—we'll need his detention documented, time, place, witnesses. And Yara—" She hesitated, and for the first time Yara saw her search for a word and not find it immediately. "What you did just now, on this bench, knowing what it cost you—that was the bravest thing I've witnessed in eleven years of this work. I won't waste it."

Yara nodded, because speech was beyond her, and watched Mirabel walk away down the cracked paving with her book under her arm, unremarkable, unhurried, a woman carrying a revolution folded inside a glove.

* * *

She was halfway back along the sea wall, the wind picking salt off the water and throwing it against her face, when she saw the boy running toward her from the direction of the fish market—one of the Orchard's children, Sami, barefoot, breathless, his shirt dark with spray.

"*Nkechi*—" he gasped, using the old endearment, the secret name, which meant the news was Orchard news and urgent. "The boathouse. The monsoon surge came early—the whole front wall went, and the water's rising fast, Mama says there's an hour maybe—"

"Where is she? Where's Kofi?"

"Moving the stores to the high ground behind the lime kiln. But Nkechi—" Sami grabbed her sleeve, and his eyes were wide with more than flood. "Kofi isn't there. He went out before dawn with the diving gear and he hasn't come back, and Old Baptiste says the patrol cutter's been circling the reef passage since midmorning, slow, like it's *looking* for something."

The wind rose. Out past the breakwater, the sky over the open water had gone the color of bruised fruit, and the first real rain of the monsoon came stalking in across the sea like something with intent.

Yara stood on the sea wall with her family's hour measured out behind her and her brother somewhere under a graying sea, and understood that every door she had opened today was now blowing wide in the storm—all at once, all in the same wind.
