## 16. Storm Warning

The rain began at dawn on a Tuesday, and by noon it had erased the harbor.

Yara stood at the counting house window and watched the sea swallow its own edges. The masts of the anchored ships went soft and grey, then vanished altogether, as if the port were being rubbed out of existence one vessel at a time. Rain hammered the corrugated roof in sheets. The gutters overflowed. Somewhere down the quay, a crate of dried fish broke loose from its moorings and drifted past like a coffin.

"The Coromandel won't sail in this," said Odili, the junior clerk, peering over her shoulder. "Not through the straits. Not with a full passenger list."

"No," Yara said. "She won't."

She felt the delay settle into her chest like a stone dropped into still water. Three days—maybe four, maybe five. Every day the ship sat at anchor was a day the berth stayed empty, and a day her family stayed hidden in a boathouse that was itself only a plank wall and a prayer away from the flooding creek. But it was more than that. Delay meant time, and time, she was learning, did not belong to her. Time belonged to Calloway.

Behind her, Mr. Calloway's office door stood closed. He had not come out since eight o'clock. She could see his shadow moving against the frosted glass—pacing, stopping, pacing again—and every pass of that silhouette tightened something in her throat. For six years she had read this building the way sailors read weather. She knew what the closed door meant. She knew what the pacing meant.

He had gone to the magistrate's clerk on Saturday. She didn't know how she knew; she simply did, the way you know a storm is coming before the first drop falls—from the smell of the air, from the behavior of birds, from the particular quality of a man's silence when he passes your desk without looking at you.

Calloway had passed her desk twice that morning without looking at her at all.

* * *

The boathouse leaked.

It had seemed a refuge when they'd fled the raid—a fisherman's shed on stilts above the tidal creek, reachable only at low water, invisible from the patrol road. Old Bassey, who owned it and owed Emeka a debt older than any of them, had asked no questions. He'd handed over the key and a tin lamp and said, "Don't burn the place down," which Yara now recognized as less an instruction than a prophecy.

The monsoon found them on the second night. Rain came sideways off the creek, driving through gaps in the planking, pooling under the sleeping mats. Adaeze moved through the dark with the lamp held high, directing Kofi to wedge oilcloth where the roof wept, her voice low and steady in a way that made Yara think of their father, of hands steadying a boat in rough water.

"It's just rain," Adaeze said. "Rain we can survive."

But Emeka, sitting against the wall with his wrists still ringed by the memory of manacles—he had been released after two days, released too easily, Yara thought, the way a cat releases a mouse to watch it run—Emeka shook his head.

"Rain is not what I fear," he said. "A flooded creek cuts us off from the road. If patrols come at high tide now, there's nowhere to go but into the water." He looked at Yara. "How many days?"

"Three. Maybe five."

"And the ship?"

"Delayed. Anchored past the point."

Emeka nodded slowly, and the nod frightened her more than any argument he had ever made. It was the nod of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"We should scatter tonight," he said. "Before the water rises. Walk out separately, north along the mangrove track, and meet at—"

"No." Adaeze set the lamp down with a small, final click. "We are not walking anywhere in a monsoon with half the settlement already watched. We stay together. We stay dry. We wait for the ship."

"The ship may not come, sister."

"The ship will come." Her mother's voice did not rise. It never needed to. "And when it comes, my son will be on it. Until then, we are a family under one roof, even if the roof leaks. That is worth more than dry ground."

Kofi, seventeen now, sat sharpening his diving knife on a whetstone, and the small rhythmic sound filled the silence like a heartbeat. He had not spoken since Yara arrived. He kept looking at the rain, then at the creek, then at the rain again, and Yara understood that part of him—the reckless, drowning-pride part of him—was calculating whether he could dive in storm water. Whether the reefs would give up one more shell to a boy foolish enough to ask.

"Stop that," she said quietly.

"I'm maintaining my knife."

"You're planning a suicide." She crossed the room and took the whetstone from under his hand. "The water's brown with silt. You couldn't see two feet down. The currents in the channel have reversed with the storm surge. And if the patrol boats are anywhere, they're in the lee of the point, waiting for exactly one brave idiot."

Kofi's jaw worked. He was sixteen going on forty, all shoulders and stubbornness, and he had their father's face in a way that sometimes made it hard for Yara to breathe. "I'm not going to sit in a leaking box and wait to be deported."

"You're not going to be deported. There's a berth."

"One berth." He said it flatly, and the flatness cut. "For four people. Mama already signed it to me—I saw the paper, Yara, don't pretend I don't know—and I am not taking it while the rest of you—" His voice cracked and he hated it, she could see him hating it. "I'll buy my own way or none of us goes."

"That isn't how love works," Adaeze said from across the room, without turning around. "Love is not a debt you repay. Sit down, Kofi."

He sat. The rain filled the silence between them, drumming on the roof like impatient fingers.

Later, when the others slept, Yara lay awake listening to the creek rise. The water lapped at the stilt pilings with a sound like slow applause. She counted what remained: three days, perhaps. One berth. A forged ledger in Calloway's counting house bearing her handwriting. A copied page sewn into the lining of her coat. A census taker whose motives she still couldn't map. And somewhere beyond the rain, a warrant—or the beginning of one—moving through the magistrate's offices with her name on it.

Every door narrowing at once, she thought. And behind each door, the same small room.

* * *

Mirabel Hale came to the boathouse on the fourth day of rain, and she came alone, and she came at low tide, wading the last fifty yards with her skirts hitched to her knees and her census book wrapped in oilskin held above her head like an offering.

Yara saw her coming from the doorway and did not move to help. Some instinct told her that watching Mirabel Hale struggle through floodwater was information—that a woman who would ruin her boots to deliver a message was telling you precisely what kind of message it was.

"You shouldn't be here," Yara said, when the woman reached the stilt ladder.

"Probably not." Mirabel climbed anyway, breathless, her hair plastered to her forehead, and stood dripping on the boards with the oilskin bundle hugged to her chest. Up close she looked younger than she ever did in the counting house, and tireder. "May I?"

Adaeze rose from the corner, wary. Emeka did not rise at all; he simply opened his eyes, which was somehow more intimidating. Kofi put his hand near his knife, and Yara caught the movement and stilled it with a glance.

"They're my family," Yara said. "You've guessed that for weeks. Say it plainly so we can stop pretending."

"Your mother, your brother, your uncle." Mirabel nodded to each in turn, unflinching. "Yes. I've known since the third interview. Since you flinched at the name Okafor on the roster." She unwrapped the oilskin—not the census book inside, Yara saw, but a leather folio, official-looking, stamped with the Colonial Office seal. "I haven't come to take names. I've come because I have very little time to tell you something, and you need to hear it from me before you hear it from a constable."

The rain drummed. Nobody breathed.

"Calloway filed a complaint on Saturday," Mirabel said. "Alleging falsification of company records by a subordinate clerk. He's named you. He's provided samples of the entries—your entries—and sworn statements from two dock foremen saying you handled the shipments in question. The magistrate's clerk owes Calloway money, so the paperwork is moving faster than paperwork usually moves." She paused. "An arrest order could be signed as early as tomorrow. Day after, certainly."

The words landed one at a time, like stones dropped from a height. Yara heard herself say, "Then I should run."

"If you run, he wins. If you run, you confirm everything, and worse—you leave your family here with no one inside the walls." Mirabel's eyes were steady and grey and quite without pity, which Yara had come to understand was the same thing as mercy, properly applied. "Listen to me. I have spent four months building a case against Meridian Trading. Smuggling, bonded-goods fraud, bribery of customs officers—the ledgers you've been forced to balance are not your shame, Miss Okafor. They are my evidence. Every false entry in your handwriting is a confession in *his*. But I cannot move until I have everything, and I am days short. Perhaps hours."

"So I'm bait," Yara said. "Or a lever. Or a witness. Everyone keeps deciding what I am."

Mirabel flinched—actually flinched, a small motion around the eyes—and for a moment the official mask slipped and Yara saw beneath it: a woman exhausted, isolated, carrying a case no one in the Colonial Office had wanted to authorize, trusting exactly one person in this entire port and knowing that trust might be misplaced.

"You are the only person who has chosen correctly at every turn," Mirabel said quietly, "while being given nothing but terrible choices. That is not the same as being used. But I understand why it feels that way, and I am sorry for it."

Emeka laughed once, short and hard. "Colonial mercy. I've seen how it's applied."

"Yes," Mirabel said, turning to him without heat. "So have I. From the inside. Which is why I'm standing in a flood rather than sending a constable." She looked back at Yara. "The Coromandel is delayed three days, four at most. When the weather breaks, she sails. Your brother's berth stands—it's booked, it's paid, it's legitimate, and no magistrate in this territory will unbook a documented passage, not even for Calloway. That door is closed to him. The other doors—" she touched the folio— "are the ones I intend to close myself, before they close on you."

"How?" Adaeze asked. It was the first word she'd spoken, and her voice carried the whole question: *how does a woman like you stop a machine like that.*

"Because Calloway made a mistake," Mirabel said. "He thinks I'm a census taker with a notebook full of household counts. He thinks the Colonial Office sent a clerical nobody to count fishing families. He has therefore spent his energy threatening you instead of hiding from me." A thin, sharp smile. "Men who underestimate clerical nobodies tend to hand them their evidence."

Kofi spoke from the shadows. "And if the storm doesn't break? If the ship waits a week? Will your case wait too?"

Mirabel hesitated. It was a small hesitation, but Yara had built six years on reading small things, and she read this one completely.

"No," Mirabel admitted. "If the arrest order comes before the ship sails, I can stall the magistrate for a day, perhaps two. After that, Miss Okafor, you will be in a cell, and I will be fighting for you with one hand tied." She met Yara's eyes. "Which means the next few days decide everything. Yours, mine, your family's. I need you to hold. Can you hold?"

The creek slapped against the pilings. The lamp guttered. Yara thought of the ledger page sewn into her coat lining, of the neat, damning rows of her own handwriting, of her mother's face at the graves of her father, of a berth on a delayed ship, of every narrowing door.

"I've been holding for six years," she said. "Three more days is nothing."

Mirabel nodded, rewrapped the folio, and turned toward the ladder. Then she stopped.

"One more thing." She did not look back. "When the order is signed—and it will be signed—they'll come for you at the counting house. First thing in the morning, most likely. Don't be early to work that day." A pause. "Or do. I can't tell you which. But whatever you do in those last hours, Miss Okafor—do it with your eyes open."

She climbed down into the brown water and waded away, and the rain took her, figure by figure, until there was nothing left on the creek but grey water and the sound of a storm that would not end.

Adaeze put her arm through her daughter's. Behind them, Kofi stared at the flooded creek with the expression of a boy measuring the distance to the far shore.

Three days, Yara thought. One berth. One arrest order moving through the wet streets toward her name.

Whatever she did in those last hours—she would have to choose it soon.
