## 17. The Choice

The rain came at dawn, and with it a constable's whistle somewhere near the harbor road, thin and far away, like something heard underwater. Yara lay awake in the boathouse listening to the water slap the hulls and counted what she had left: two days, perhaps three, before Calloway's papers against her were signed and stamped. The monsoon had bought her time, but time was not the same as mercy.

She rose quietly so as not to wake Adaeze, who slept curled on a fisherman's cot with her back to the wall, the posture of a woman who had learned decades ago that sleep must be portable. Kofi was already gone — out before light, though Yara had forbidden it, out to the reefs or the fish markets or wherever a sixteen-year-old went when he was trying to become useful faster than the world would allow.

Yara stood at the boathouse door and watched the rain stitch itself into the sea. Somewhere across the harbor, on the Coromandel's manifest, one name waited in a berth she had bought with forged ink: *A. Okafor.* Her mother's name, because Yara could not bring herself to write any other, and because Adaeze was the center without which none of them held together. That had always been the arithmetic of the family. You protected the center first.

Except that the center had other ideas.

* * *

She found the transfer paper folded inside her mother's prayer cloth, where Adaeze kept the things that mattered — a dried sprig from the orchard they'd once had, a photograph gone soft at the creases, and now this. Yara unfolded it standing in the grey half-light and read it twice before the words would sit still.

The berth had been reassigned. In Adaeze's careful hand, in the handwriting Yara had inherited her own neatness from, the line read: *K. Okafor.*

Below it, the signature. Below the signature, the date — yesterday, while Yara had been at the counting house forging entries for Calloway, her mother had walked down to the shipping office in the rain, an unregistered woman moving through a city that did not permit her to exist, and had signed her son's life onto a ship.

"You went out," Yara said when Adaeze woke. "You went out *alone*."

"I went out as nobody," Adaeze said simply. "Nobody is invisible. You taught me that." She sat up, smoothing the blanket over her knees. "The clerk didn't look at me twice. Old women carrying papers are furniture."

"Mother—"

"Don't." Adaeze said it gently, but the gentleness had iron under it. "Sit down, Yara. I'm not going to argue with you. I'm going to tell you how it is, and then you're going to stop looking at me as if I've stolen something."

Yara sat.

"I buried your father here," Adaeze said. "Under the mango tree in the Orchard, with the graves of eleven other people whose names no government ever wrote down. I told myself I would never leave him. And I meant it. I mean it still." Her hands were quiet in her lap. "But a grave does not need me. It has him. What needs me is that boy, and he is diving reefs at night to buy passage he will never afford, because he has decided his life is a debt he owes this family. He is sixteen. He thinks he is made of iron. He will get himself drowned or detained, and either way the world will take him from me without my permission."

"He can't go alone—"

"He won't be alone. He'll be alive, and free, and writing us letters from some port where his face is just a face." Adaeze reached forward and took Yara's chin in her hand, the way she had when Yara was small enough to be lifted. "You have spent six years making yourself small so the rest of us could stay whole. I have watched you do it. I let you do it, God forgive me. But I am not going to stand on my husband's grave and let you choose between your mother and your brother. That choice will break you. So I made it for you."

"You signed my berth away—"

"I gave it to the person who can still be saved *easily*," Adaeze said. "You are not easy to save, my daughter. You never have been. You save everyone else and call it nothing." She released Yara's chin and looked out at the rain. "The Coromandel leaves when the storm breaks. Two days. Maybe less. Tell him tonight."

"I can't."

"You can. You will. Because if you don't, I will, and I will tell him it was your gift, and he will carry it badly, the way young men carry gifts that cost too much. Better it comes from you." A pause, and then, softer: "He listens to you. He pretends he doesn't. But he listens."

* * *

Kofi came back at midday, soaked to the ribs, salt-crusted, with a sack of oysters he'd pried off the pilings and a look of exhausted triumph that broke Yara's heart before she'd said a word. He set the sack down and wrung out his shirt, and she watched him — really watched him, the way you watch something you are about to lose — the long limbs he hadn't grown into, the scrape along his forearm from the reef, the eyes that were their father's eyes and no one else's.

"We need to talk," she said.

"I sold the oysters. Twelve coppers. It's not much but—"

"Kofi. Sit down."

He sat. She put the transfer paper on the table between them and turned it so he could read it, and watched his face move through confusion, disbelief, and then a dawning, terrified joy — the joy of a boy who wanted something so badly he'd stopped letting himself want it.

"She signed it over? To *me*?" His voice cracked in the middle of it. "No. No — she can't — she has to go, she's the one who—" He pushed the paper back across the table as if it burned. "Tell her to change it back. Tell her I'll dive, I'll make the money, I'll—"

"The money doesn't exist, Kofi. Not in two days. Not legally. Not safely." Yara kept her voice level, which cost her everything. "And there may not be a safe anything after tomorrow. Calloway has moved against me. When they come, they come for all of us. One berth. That's what we have. One."

"Then *you* take it." He said it fast, the words tumbling. "You've done everything for us, you've earned it, you—"

"I'm indentured, Kofi. My name is in Meridian's books. I couldn't board that ship if I swam to it." Which was true, and also not the reason, and she saw him see that it was not the reason, because he knew her better than anyone living.

"It should be Mama," he said, quieter now. "It should always have been Mama."

"Mama decided otherwise." Yara took the paper back and held it flat between them, his name in their mother's handwriting, the ink slightly blurred where the rain had touched it. "This is not a decision, little brother. This is a gift. There's a difference. A decision you argue with. A gift you accept."

He stared at it. His jaw worked. And then, in a voice very young: "I don't want to go without you. Either of you."

"I know."

"That's not fair."

"No," Yara said. "It isn't."

* * *

They told Emeka that evening. He received the news standing at the boathouse door with his arms crossed, and for a long time he said nothing at all, which from Emeka was a speech.

"So the girl buys a berth with company lies," he said finally, "and the mother gives it to the boy, and the boy argues, and everyone looks to me to bless it." He shook his head slowly. "Your father would have hated every part of this. He hated ships. He used to say the sea takes the young and gives back nothing but weather."

"And yet here we are," Adaeze said.

"Here we are." Emeka looked at Kofi, and something moved behind his gruffness — Yara had never been able to read her uncle easily, but she caught it now, plain as lamplight: fear. He was afraid for the boy, and hiding it the way men of his generation hid everything, inside silence and crossed arms. "You'll write," he said. It was not a question.

"Every month," Kofi said.

"You'll write even when there's nothing to say. Especially then. Loneliness eats men who only write good news." He uncrossed his arms and gripped Kofi's shoulder once, hard, and let go before anything could soften further. "Go pack. Take nothing you can't carry running. That's the only packing advice worth giving."

* * *

The storm broke on the second morning, the way storms do — all at once, the rain slackening into a fine silver drizzle, the wind swinging round, the harbor coming up out of the murk like something developing in a darkroom. The Coromandel sat at anchor beyond the mole, black-hulled, breathing smoke, and the dockside had transformed overnight into a machinery of departures: porters, gangplanks, officials with clipboards, families pressed against the fence that separated the permitted world from the permitted world's cargo.

Yara walked Kofi to the fence. Adaeze stayed behind — she had said she could not do it, that she would say goodbye at the boathouse and trust the rest to God, and Yara had not argued, because she understood: some farewells you survive by not attending them. Emeka walked them as far as the market stalls and then stopped, pretending interest in a tray of hooks, his back very straight.

At the fence, an official checked Kofi's paper, glanced up at his face, checked the paper again, and waved him through to the holding yard. Beyond the barrier, steerage passengers milled with their bundles. Kofi turned back.

"You'll fix it," he said. "All of it. The ledgers, the arrest, Mama, everything. You'll fix it and then you'll come find me. Promise me."

"Kofi—"

"*Promise me.*"

And because a lie told at a dock fence to a frightened boy is a kind of mercy, Yara said, "I promise," and hated herself and loved herself for it in the same breath.

He hugged her through the bars, hard enough to hurt, his face pressed into her shoulder, smelling of salt and reef and sixteen years. Then he pulled back, picked up his bundle, and walked toward the gangplank without looking back — because looking back was the thing that broke people, and he was his mother's son.

Yara watched until the rail swallowed him. She watched the smoke thicken. She watched the tugboats nose the great hull away from the mole, and she stood at the fence long after the crowd had dispersed, her fingers laced through the iron bars, holding on to nothing.

Behind her, boots on wet planking. Measured. Deliberate. She did not turn around; she knew the stride, and her stomach dropped before her mind caught up.

"Miss Okafor." Calloway's voice, pleasant as ever, courteous as a drawn blade. "I've been looking for you. There are matters at the office we really must conclude — today, I'm afraid."

Yara closed her eyes. In the harbor, the Coromandel's smoke was dissolving into cloud, taking her brother south with it, and somewhere in her coat pocket, folded against her heart, was the copied ledger page she had sworn to destroy.

She turned around.

"Calloway," she said. "Let's finish it."
