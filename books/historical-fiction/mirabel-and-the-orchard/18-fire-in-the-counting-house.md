## 18. Fire in the Counting House

The storm broke at dusk, and by midnight the counting house smelled of wet paper and lamp oil.

Yara stood in the doorway with rain running off her hem into a dark pool on the floorboards, watching Mr. Calloway move down the aisle of shelves like a man inspecting his own coffin. He had sent for her an hour after the monsoon arrived—sent a boy through flooded streets with a note that said only *Come now. Bring nothing.* She had come, because refusing would have told him everything, and because she had already decided what she meant to do, and it required being here.

"You're soaked," Calloway said without turning.

"It's raining."

"It is." He lifted a ledger from its shelf—the second of the falsified volumes, the one with her handwriting marching across its pages like ants toward a cliff—and weighed it in his hand as though judging a melon. "Do you know what water does to ink like this? Given time, given damp, it blooms. The words go soft. They bleed into one another until no magistrate could say who wrote them or when." He set the book back precisely where it had stood. "I've been thinking about water all evening."

Yara said nothing. Her heart was a fist inside her ribs, but her face, six years of practice, gave him the flat courtesy of a clerk awaiting instruction.

"The Colonial Office men arrived on the *Sparrowhawk* this afternoon," he went on. "Two of them. Very quiet, very polite. They've taken rooms at the Palafox Hotel." He turned then, and in the lamplight his face had the gray sheen of old tallow. "Miss Hale's doing, I imagine. Or yours. It hardly matters now which. What matters is that they will come here tomorrow morning with a warrant, and they will find these books—" he touched the shelf the way a man touches a wound, "—and in these books there are entries in your hand that will hang me, and entries in my hand that will drown you. We are tied together, Miss Okafor, tighter than any two people in this port, and I have been trying all evening to decide whether to cut you loose or let you pull me under."

"And which did you decide?"

He smiled. It was the worst thing she had ever seen him do, worse than the threats, worse than Emeka's name spoken like a coin slapped onto a table. Because the smile was almost kind.

"I decided," he said, "that you are going to help me burn them."

* * *

She made him say it twice.

Not because she hadn't heard—because she needed the shape of it in the air, needed to hear a man of ledgers propose the destruction of ledgers, needed to know how far the rot had gone before she put her match to it.

"Every volume touching the eastern routes," Calloway said, pacing now, energized by his own audacity. "Seven years of shipping manifests, customs declarations, the insurance filings. A fire in the counting house—stoves, lamp oil, a clerk working late in monsoon season, God rest her. Tragic. Unavoidable. And with the books gone, there is nothing for the Office men to read. Nothing to connect the Meridian line to the pearl shipments that never carried pearls. Nothing to connect you to anything at all." He stopped before her. "You walk out of this clean, Miss Okafor. Your uncle walks out of the lockup clean. Your family stays invisible. Isn't invisibility what you've wanted all along?"

It was. That was the obscenity of it. Every careful silence, every smoothed-over roster, every year of being nobody—it had all been for exactly this, and now he was offering it back to her as payment for her soul's last coin.

"And Mirabel Hale?" Yara asked. "What happens to her?"

Calloway shrugged. "A census taker loses her quarry. She'll be reassigned. Women like that always are."

*The orchard*, Yara thought. *The graves. Kofi on the Coromandel in nine days. My mother's hands in the soil.* All of it preserved by fire, all of it purchased with the same lie she had been telling since she was nineteen years old: *we were never here.*

And beside it, another voice—Mirabel's voice, low and even over tea neither of them drank: *Someone has to write the truth down somewhere, Miss Okafor, or the next family starts hiding from nothing at all.*

"When?" Yara asked.

"Now," said Calloway. "Tonight, while the streets are drowned and every constable in the port is sheltering under an awning wishing he were elsewhere. You'll strike the lamps yourself—you're the clerk, your presence explains itself if anyone asks. I'll be at the Palafox Hotel having dinner where forty people can swear to seeing me." He was already pulling on his coat. "Oil is in the storeroom. The stove grate opens wide enough for the smaller volumes; the rest goes on the floor. Start with the eastern routes and work outward. By dawn there should be nothing left but ash and a very sad story."

He paused at the door, rain roaring beyond it, and looked back at her with something that in a better man might have been regret.

"For what it's worth," he said, "you were the best clerk I ever had."

Then he was gone into the dark, and Yara Okafor stood alone in the counting house with seven years of lies stacked to the ceiling and a box of matches in her pocket that she had brought before he ever asked.

* * *

She did not begin with the eastern routes.

She began, instead, by walking the aisles slowly, the way she had walked them a thousand evenings, trailing her fingers along the spines. Here was her first year's work, aged seventeen, the letters still tentative. Here was the flood season of '95, when she had worked three nights straight and Calloway had given her half a day off and called her *the most reliable pair of hands in the port*. Here, in the third aisle, was the volume where the falsifications began—her own neat figures bent to carry weight that was never theirs, pearls that never existed crossing water that never bore them, debts dissolved into phantom cargo. She had copied the key pages weeks ago, folded them against her breast, and carried them to Mirabel Hale like a wound finally opened. Those pages were safe. Whatever happened tonight, the truth existed somewhere outside this room.

That was the thought that steadied her hand.

Because here was the thing Calloway had never understood, the thing men like him never understood about the people they used: she had not spent six years protecting the Orchard out of love of lying. She had lied because the truth was dangerous only so long as it lived in the wrong houses—in company books, in government registers, in the mouths of men who traded other people's existence like currency. The lie was not hers. The lie was the building she stood in.

Calloway wanted a fire that erased everything. Yara had decided, somewhere between the boathouse and the dock fence and the long drowning hours since, that she would give him a fire that erased only him.

She opened the storeroom and found the lamp oil where he said it would be, two tins of it, generous, prepared. She carried them to the center aisle. She fetched the eastern-route volumes first—all seven years, every manifest, every declaration, every page bearing her handwriting and his—because those must burn, wholly and certainly, whatever else survived. She stacked them in a low tower beside the oil. Then, methodically, working outward as instructed, she gathered the insurance filings, the customs duplicates, the correspondence files marked with the Meridian seal.

But between armfuls, quietly, without letting herself think too long about any single choice—for thinking was where cowardice lived—she pulled certain volumes free and set them apart in the far corner behind the iron strongbox: the wage rolls, the indenture registries, the shipping logs of legitimate cargo, the ordinary honest record of ordinary honest trade. The evidence of everything else the company had ever done. Enough remained, if anyone cared to look, to show what the Meridian had been before men like Calloway got hold of it—and, more importantly, enough to show exactly which years and which routes had gone rotten, by their absence. A hole in a record is also a record. Let the Office men read the gap. Gaps speak.

When the pile was built she poured the oil over it, slow and thorough, and the smell rose up thick and chemical, drowning even the rain-smell of the room.

Then she sat down on Calloway's own stool, behind Calloway's own desk, and waited.

* * *

He came back at half past one.

She heard him before she saw him—a lantern bobbing past the shutters, the scrape of his shoes on the veranda boards, the small curse as the door stuck in the swollen wet air. He entered shaking rain from his hat, and stopped.

"Why aren't they burning?"

"I was waiting for you," Yara said. "You'll want to see it start. Men always do."

Something eased in his shoulders—he read her stillness as obedience, the same error he had made for six years—and he came down the aisle, lantern held high, looking at the oil-blackened stack with the satisfaction of a man viewing his own cleverness. "Good. Good. Strike the lamp, douse it here, and step outside. Thirty seconds is all it needs. The whole street will be alight by the time—"

"Mr. Calloway," Yara said, "do you know what I learned, working for you?"

He glanced at her, irritated. "This isn't the hour for sentiment."

"It's exactly the hour for it. It's the only hour I'll get." She rose from the stool. "I learned that a record is a weapon, and whoever holds it decides whom it cuts. You held mine for six years. The Orchard's. My mother's. My brother's. You never once thought of them as people whose lives you were keeping in those books—you thought of them as entries. Debits. Risks to be managed." She took the matches from her pocket and held them where he could see them. "Tonight I'm holding yours."

Understanding moved across his face slowly, like weather. "Miss Okafor. Whatever you think you're doing—"

"The eastern routes burn," she said. "All of it. The smuggling, the forged clearances, the debts you buried in phantom cargo. That fire happens whether you consent or not, because I am done being your instrument in either direction—I won't save your books and I won't save your reputation. But understand what else is happening, Mr. Calloway. The wage rolls don't burn. The indenture registries don't burn. The honest trade doesn't burn. When the Office men arrive—and they are arriving—they will find a company with seven clean years of legitimate business and one rotten corridor running straight through the middle of it, and at the end of that corridor they will find you, standing in the rain with no explanation for why you came back to a burning building you claimed to have left an hour ago."

His face went white around the mouth. "You'd hang yourself with them. Your handwriting is in those volumes. You forged the entries."

"Yes," Yara said. "I did. And I will say so, freely, to whoever asks. I forged them because you threatened to deliver my family to the deportation ships. That is called coercion, and there are two investigators at the Palafox Hotel right now who have been waiting three weeks to hear exactly that sentence." She struck the first match against the box. The flame stood up small and steady between them. "You taught me the books, Mr. Calloway. Every entry balances. Tonight, mine does."

He lunged—not for her, for the oil tins, to hurl them clear, to salvage something—and she stepped back and dropped the match.

The oil took with a sound like a gasp, a soft whump that became a roar in the space of two heartbeats, and heat shoved them both backward down the aisle. Calloway stumbled against the shelving, arms crossed before his face, shouting something the fire ate whole. Yara retreated to the door and stood in it, rain streaming past her shoulders, and watched seven years of lies go up.

It was beautiful. That was the terrible, liberating truth of it. The flames ran along the spines and the leather cracked and curled and the pages fanned open like startled birds, and all those careful false numbers—her numbers, his orders—turned to light and then to black lace and then to nothing. Somewhere in the blaze a shelf collapsed. Sparks climbed toward the rafters and the smoke rolled black against the ceiling beams, and Yara felt something leave her body that had been sitting in her chest for six years, some coiled tenant finally paying its rent and going.

She was not free yet. She knew that. There would be questions, a cell perhaps, a magistrate, her own confession laid out in her own damning handwriting. She had burned her way into the story instead of out of it. But standing in that doorway, soaked and lit orange to the chin, she felt—for the first time since she was nineteen years old and first learned to keep silent—*visible*. Entirely, ruinously visible, and alive.

"Come away from there!" Calloway was coughing, dragging himself toward the side window, his coat smoking. "You've killed us both, you mad—"

"No," Yara said. "Only the books."

And that was when the lanterns appeared at the end of the lane.

Four of them, five, moving fast through the rain—constables, and behind them two figures in traveling coats who did not run but walked with the unhurried certainty of men who had already read every document worth reading. One of them carried a warrant case under his arm. The other carried an umbrella, and held it over a woman walking beside him, a woman with a census book tucked beneath her coat to keep it dry, a woman who had told Yara three days ago, in a boathouse smelling of fish and tar: *When the fire comes—and I believe it will come—be seen doing the right thing, not merely the necessary thing.*

Mirabel Hale reached the doorway and stopped beside Yara, close enough that their shoulders touched. For a moment neither spoke. The counting house roared behind them.

"You left the wage rolls," Mirabel said at last. Her eyes were bright, reflecting fire. "In the corner, behind the strongbox."

"Somebody has to write the truth down somewhere," Yara said.

Mirabel's breath caught—once, quickly, the way a person catches a falling glass—and then the investigators were pushing past them both into the smoke, and two constables were hauling Mr. Calloway out the side window by his armpits while he shouted about arson and ungrateful clerks and his rights as a subject of the Crown, and none of it mattered, none of it penetrated, because the books were burning and burning and burning, and Yara Okafor stood in the rain and let them.

They took her statement at the guardhouse at four in the morning, her hair still wet, her voice level, while ash drifted down outside the window like gray snow. She confessed to everything: the forgery, the coercion, the fire. She signed her name at the bottom of the page—her true name, spelled correctly, witnessed properly—and watched the clerk file it, and thought: *there. An honest entry, at last.*

They did not tell her what would happen next. They locked the cell door softly, almost apologetically, and their footsteps faded, and Yara lay back on the narrow bunk and listened to the rain easing off toward dawn.

Nine days until the Coromandel sailed. Her brother had a berth. Her mother had graves. Her uncle was in a cell two floors above her, detained and frightened and alive. And somewhere in this same building, in a room she could not see, a woman with a census book was arguing with men from the Colonial Office about what should become of a clerk named Yara Okafor—who had burned down her own history to light the way out of it.

Yara closed her eyes. She was not afraid. That surprised her more than anything.

Whatever came through that door in the morning—indenture, deportation, the dock, the dark—she had already written the only entry that mattered, in fire, in her own hand, and it balanced.
