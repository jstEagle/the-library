## 21. Letters Across Water

The letter came on a Tuesday, folded inside a canvas mail sack that smelled of tar and someone else's tobacco. Yara knew the handwriting before the harbor clerk set it on her desk — Kofi had always written his letters tall and leaning, like a boy running downhill.

She did not open it at once. She finished entering the morning's manifests into the court ledger, because that was the discipline she had learned in the counting house and the discipline she refused to unlearn: finish the page before you let your heart off its leash. But her pen slowed. The numbers blurred at their edges. The last entry of the column read *schooner *Vigilant*, in ballast*, and she wrote it twice by mistake and struck it through with a single clean line, because even her errors were going to be honest ones now.

Then she opened the letter.

*Dear Yara,* it began, in English so careful you could hear him sounding out each word.

*I am writing from Port Callao, which is further than I have ever been and smells like fish and iron and rain that comes sideways. The ship is called the Albatross and I am signed on as a deckhand, which means I haul rope until my hands are ruined and then I eat like a horse and sleep like a stone. The first mate says I have good sea legs. I did not tell him I got them diving at night off the Orchard while you thought I was asleep. Maybe now you will forgive me for that.*

Yara laughed out loud, alone in the records room, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand.

*The water here is cold. Not like home. I miss the warm water. I miss the reefs. I miss Mama's pepper soup so much I dreamed about it three nights running and woke up chewing my blanket. Tell her the cook on this ship is an Englishman who thinks salt is a spice and I may not survive the voyage back.*

*Tell Emeka I asked about him in every port. The sailors know his name. They say if anyone can talk his way out of a holding cell it is Emeka Okafor, and they are right. Write to me when you hear anything. I check the shipping office every time we dock like a dog checking a fence.*

*I keep thinking about the day at the dock fence. You held my face through the bars and you said, "Go and come back." That is all. Two words. I have said them to myself a hundred times since, when the work is hard or the water is cold. Go and come back. It is like a rope I hold in the dark.*

*Tell Mama the money is coming. Not much yet. Enough.*

*Your brother,
Kofi*

P.S. *Mama will ask if I am eating. Tell her yes. Tell her the truth only if she promises not to send me a jar of dried peppers through the post again. The last one broke and my bunk smelled like home for a week and the mate made fun of me until he tried some.*

Yara read it twice more, then folded it along its original creases and put it in the drawer where she kept things that mattered: Mirabel's registration papers, the deed to the house on Cordwainer Lane, the small brass key to the counting-house door she had never returned and could not bring herself to throw away.

Outside the window, the harbor lay flat and bright under the late-season sun. Somewhere beyond the breakwater, the Coromandel was two months gone, carrying her brother toward whatever a freed life looked like when it was built from nothing but a berth and a name on a manifest. Somewhere closer, behind the new house on Cordwainer Lane, her mother was probably on her knees in the dirt.

That was where Yara went when the court adjourned at noon.

* * *

The house was small and square and utterly ordinary, which made it miraculous. A registered dwelling. A number on a municipal roll. A door that locked from the inside without anyone needing to hide what was inside. Adaeze had painted it white with blue trim, and she had hung a curtain in the front window that she opened every morning at dawn and closed every evening at dusk with the punctuality of a lighthouse keeper, as if visibility itself were the point now. We are here, the curtain said. Count us.

Behind the house, where there had been nothing in September but weeds and broken brick, there was now a garden.

Not a garden, exactly. An orchard — or the stubborn beginning of one. Six saplings stood in a rough row along the back wall: two mango, one guava, a lime, and two others whose names Adaeze would only say were "a surprise." Their leaves were still young and absurdly green against the whitewashed wall. Stakes and twine held them upright against the wind off the water. Between them, Adaeze had planted okra and peppers and a scatter of greens, because a woman who had spent six years feeding a family from hidden gardens did not stop planting food just because she was planting trees.

"You're tracking mud," Adaeze said, without turning around. She was on her knees at the base of the lime tree, pressing soil down with both hands the way she pressed everything — firmly, as though the world might try to get up and leave otherwise.

"The whole town tracks mud."

"Not the whole town. Only people who walk fast because they're happy." She sat back on her heels and looked up at her daughter, squinting against the sun. "Letter?"

"How did you—"

"You walked here from the court in twenty minutes instead of forty. And you're smiling like an idiot."

"Kofi's well. He has sea legs. He hates the cook." Yara crouched beside her and took the letter from her satchel. "He wants you to know he's eating."

"I'll be the judge of that when I see his collarbones." But Adaeze read the letter slowly, twice, her lips moving over the English, and when she reached the part about the rope in the dark — *go and come back* — she went very still, and then she folded the letter and handed it back and said, "Peppers. I'm sending peppers. He can suffer."

"He asked you not to."

"He asked me not to because he loves them. That boy has never once asked me for something he didn't want." She picked up her trowel. "Dig here. If you've come to sit with your mother, you can work while you sit."

So Yara dug, and the afternoon went soft around them. This was the hour she loved most now, the one no ledger had ever taught her: the hour when the light came long and gold off the harbor, and the smell of turned earth rose up, and her mother hummed a song in Igbo that Yara half-remembered from a village she'd been too young to leave willingly. Her father had loved this song. Her father was under a tree in the Orchard, under a grave that could not be moved, and her mother had chosen to stay near the Orchard rather than move farther out — close enough to visit, far enough to be legal. Every Sunday Adaeze walked to the settlement with flowers she grew herself, and every Sunday the patrolmen nodded to her now, because she was on a register, because her name existed in ink, because a woman named Mirabel Hale had written it there in her own hand and paid for it with her career.

"Mama," Yara said, resting on the trowel. "Do you ever think about him? About Papa — about whether he'd have wanted this? The staying. The trees."

Adaeze considered it with the seriousness she gave all questions, which was why Yara rarely asked easy ones.

"Your father planted a lime tree behind our house the year you were born," she said. "It died in the dry season and he planted another. That one lived thirty years. He used to say a man plants a tree knowing he'll never sit in its shade, and that's how you know a planting is honest." She patted the soil around the sapling. "I'm not planting these for shade. I'm planting them so that whoever lives in this house after us — whoever we don't even know yet — looks out the window and thinks, *someone was here who meant to stay.*" She glanced at Yara. "You especially. You spent six years meaning to leave. Or pretending you might. Now you don't have to pretend anything."

"I wasn't pretending to leave. I was pretending we weren't here at all."

"And now?"

Yara looked at her hands. There was dirt in the creases, real dirt, garden dirt, and she found she didn't mind it. In the counting house she had washed her hands a dozen times a day, scrubbing at ink, scrubbing at the feeling of the forged entries. Here the dirt was honest. It came from her mother's ground.

"Now I write my name on things," she said.

Adaeze smiled and went back to the lime tree, humming.

* * *

The harbor court kept its records in a long room above the customs shed, in ledgers that predated Yara by forty years and smelled of it. The magistrate was a heavy, wheezing man named Prosser who had taken her on partly out of pity, partly because the scandal at Meridian Trading had left the port short of anyone who could keep a book straight, and mostly — though he would never admit it — because Mirabel Hale had written him a letter that began, *"You will find no clerk on this coast more exacting than Yara Okafor, and none whose word I would sooner trust,"* which, coming from a woman who had just torched her own career, carried a certain weight.

Exactness was the whole of the job, and Yara loved it with a fierceness that surprised her. Cargo in, cargo out. Fees assessed, fees paid. Disputes between fishermen arbitrated and recorded. Salvage claims. Berth allocations. Every entry dated, initialed, and left exactly as true as she could make it. Where a figure was disputed, she noted the dispute. Where a record was missing, she wrote *missing* in her small, severe hand and hunted it down. Mr. Calloway, awaiting trial in the colonial jail, had once told her that a ledger was a weapon. He hadn't understood, she thought now, that a ledger could also be a promise. *This happened. I saw it. I will not say otherwise.*

"You've corrected the Farrow account again," Prosser grumbled one morning, tapping the page with his pipe. "Third time."

"Because it's wrong a third time. He's under-declaring his catch by a third."

"He's a poor man with a sick wife."

"He's a liar with a sick wife. Put him on the payment plan. His honor will survive; the court's won't."

Prosser glared at her, and then, because the glare had nowhere to go — because she was right, and everyone in the building knew she was right — he waved his pipe at the door and said, "Get out of my office, Mrs.— Miss Okafor. Before I remember I'm supposed to be intimidating."

She gathered her pages, hiding her smile until she was in the corridor.

There were other changes, smaller ones, that she catalogued the way she once catalogued dangers. The fruit seller at the corner market saved her the bruised mangoes. Children in the lane outside the court called out *good morning, miss* and meant it. A woman from the Orchard — old Nkiru, who had survived the raid by hiding in a fish barrel and never quite lost the habit of flinching — stopped flinching when Yara passed. One evening Nkirua pressed a bundle of dried fish into her hands and said, "For the house. For your mother. We heard you burned their books." As if arson were a kindness. As if, among the people of the Orchard, it was.

And every few days, a letter arrived from the Colonial Office in the capital. Yara knew the handwriting on those envelopes too — she had watched Mirabel Hale fill out census forms with the same unhurried precision — but she did not know what the letters said, because Mirabel did not send them to Yara. She sent them to the court. To Prosser. Testimony, Yara gathered. Statements for the tribunal reviewing the Meridian case, and the case against Calloway, and — though this part Yara had pieced together from fragments and Prosser's carefully blank silences — the matter of Yara's own forged entries, which had not been forgiven, only postponed, pending the outcome.

Her crime sat in a file somewhere in the capital, patient as tide. She had burned the ledgers to free her family and damn the company, and the law did not care that the fire had been a kind of truth-telling. Fire was fire. Forgery was forgery. Some mornings she woke with her heart hammering, certain the constable was already on the stairs.

But the letters kept coming, and the constable did not.

* * *

On the last Saturday of the month, Adaeze hosted what was becoming a tradition: dinner for anyone from the Orchard who cared to come, eaten off mismatched plates in a house that legally existed. Emeka had been released in October — his detention quietly converted into a fine that Mirabel's testimony had gotten reduced to almost nothing — and he presided over these dinners like a man restored to a throne he'd never officially lost. He told the story of the fish barrel every single time, improving it with each telling, until by now he himself had hidden in the barrel and Nkiru had fought off four constables with an oar.

"You were in the boathouse," Yara said, ladling soup. "Nkiru was in the barrel. You've swapped stories."

"A story belongs to whoever tells it best," Emeka said serenely. "This is also the law of the sea."

Around the table: Adaeze, radiant in her good dress. Old Nkiru. The Adeyemi family, registered now too, their papers framed on their own wall two streets over. A fisherman named Bosun who had sheltered the family during the raid and pretended afterward it had been nothing. Fifteen people in a room meant for eight, and the noise of it carried out the open window into the street, where no one came to silence it, because there was nothing to silence anymore. They were counted. They were, for the first time in six years, allowed to be loud.

Afterward, washing dishes with her mother, Yara said, "Emeka's talking about going back to trading. Small runs. Legitimate."

"Emeka has been 'talking about' legitimate trading since before you were born. It's how he rests."

"He seems lighter, though. Since the release."

"We all do." Adaeze handed her a plate to dry. "Even you. You laugh now. Did you know that? Your father used to say you laughed so rarely that when you did, it was like finding money in an old coat." She paused, dish towel still. "Mirabel used to say something similar. About you."

Yara went still. "When did you speak with Mirabel?"

"At Sunday services. Three weeks ago. She sat in the back, as usual. Afterward she asked after you, and I told her you were well, and working, and that you laugh now." Adaeze resumed drying, elaborately casual. "She said she was glad. She said—" her mother's voice softened, took on an imitation so gentle it was clearly affectionate, "—she said, 'Tell her the tribunal sits on the ninth. Tell her not to be afraid. Tell her I have handled worse than this.' Then she bought one of my limes and overpaid terribly and left."

"The ninth." Yara set the plate down. "That's—"

"Twelve days. Yes." Adaeze turned to face her, and her hands found her daughter's shoulders, the way they had at the dock fence, the way they seemed to find her at every moment the world narrowed. "You are not going to spend twelve days drowning. You're going to work your ledger and plant nothing and eat my soup and sleep. Do you hear me? Whatever happens at that tribunal, you did the hardest thing already. Everything after is paperwork."

"Easy for you to say. It isn't your forgery on trial."

"No," Adaeze agreed. "It's my daughter. Which is worse." She kissed Yara's forehead, once, firmly, like stamping a document. "Soup tomorrow. Both bowls."

* * *

That night Yara sat at the table in the lamplight with Kofi's letter open before her and a blank sheet of paper in front of her, and for a long time she wrote nothing.

What did you say to a brother across an ocean? That the guava tree had taken. That Emeka was insufferable and healed. That the harbor court smelled of mildew and justice in roughly equal parts. That she had a tribunal in twelve days and was more afraid of it than she had been of the burning, because the burning had been hers to choose and the tribunal was not.

She wrote all of it. It took four pages. At the bottom, she hesitated, and then added:

*Mirabel is still here. She gave up her posting, you know — they recalled her to the capital and she refused, or delayed, or something she won't explain. She sits in the back of church and buys limes she doesn't need and writes letters to the tribunal that I am apparently not allowed to read. Mama says she asks after me every time. I don't know what to do with that. All last year I watched her through a counting-house window and decided she was a danger. Now I watch for her at church and decide nothing, because deciding anything feels like closing a door.*

*The boat season ends soon. Everyone says she'll be on the last boat out. I keep telling myself that's fine. People leave ports. It's what ports are for.*

*Go and come back,
Yara*

She sealed it, addressed it to the shipping agent in Port Callao, and set it by the door for the morning packet.

Then she blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the house settle — the registered, legal, curtain-hung, lime-shaded house — and to the harbor beyond it, where the masts knocked softly together like people murmuring in their sleep.

Twelve days. On the ninth, a tribunal. And somewhere between now and then, a woman who had traded her career for a stranger's family would either sail away forever, or —

Yara climbed the stairs, and did not finish the sentence. Some entries, she had learned, were best left open until the facts came in.

But the next morning, walking to the court past the market stalls, she heard it before she saw it: a familiar voice, unhurried and precise, asking the fruit seller — of all people — a question in that tone that had once made a whole port nervous.

"...and when did the Coromandel's replacement vessel arrive? No — don't guess. You keep excellent accounts. I can wait."

Yara stopped in the middle of the lane, her ledger bag swinging against her hip, and turned.

Mirabel Hale stood at the fruit stall with her notebook open, hat slightly crooked, entirely unchanged and completely changed — and she was looking directly at Yara, as if she had known, the whole time, exactly where in the crowd she would find her.
