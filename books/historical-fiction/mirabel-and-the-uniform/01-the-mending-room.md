## 1. The Mending Room

The lamp on the worktable had begun to smoke again, and Mirabel Joubert did not have time to trim its wick.

She knew this the way she knew most things in her life: by what she could not afford. The oil was nearly gone — a quarter of a bottle, maybe less, scraped from the bottom of a tin that had cost more than she liked to remember — and a smoking wick burned faster than a clean one. So she let it smoke, and the little room above the tailor shop filled with a haze that smelled of scorched paraffin and wool fibers and the cold seeping through the window glass, and she bent closer over the sleeve of an officer's greatcoat and kept sewing.

The coat belonged to a Captain Vasseur, or had, until last month. Now it belonged to whoever the quartermaster's office said it belonged to, which was the way of things in a republic that was shedding officers like a dog sheds a winter coat. The tear ran along the left forearm, four inches of split seam where something sharp had caught it — wire, Mirabel guessed, or the edge of a door slammed in haste. There was no blood in the lining. She checked anyway. She always checked. A uniform told you more honestly than a face ever would, and these days the faces coming into the shop were doing their best to tell her nothing at all.

She set her needle, drew the thread through, and listened to the house.

It was past nine. Below her, the shop was dark, the shutters drawn over windows that had once displayed men's suits cut by her father's hands — good suits, honest suits, the kind a clerk saved three years to buy. Now the display window held nothing but dust and a paper sign reading CLOSED FOR ALTERATIONS, which was true in a way her father would have found funny and her mother would have found sad, and both of them dead now, so the joke landed nowhere.

Above her, on the third floor, Colette's bed creaked twice and went still. The girl was fifteen and slept like a cat in a sunbeam, all elbows and sudden twitching dreams, but she'd been quiet tonight. Too quiet, Mirabel thought, and then corrected herself: too quiet meant asleep, and asleep meant safe, and she should be grateful instead of suspicious. But suspicion had become a domestic skill in the Joubert household, practiced as regularly as darning, and Mirabel could no more stop taking inventory of small sounds than she could stop counting stitches.

The wind came off the river in gusts that rattled the loose pane in the kitchen window. The pipes knocked — the city's water pressure failing again, another service dying quietly without anyone announcing its funeral. Somewhere two streets over, a tram bell rang at an hour when no trams ran, and stopped mid-ring, as if the driver had thought better of it.

And then, beneath all of it: boots on the exterior stair. Heavy, uneven, dragging slightly on the left.

Mirabel put down the coat.

* * *

Theo came through the door the way he always came through doors after a run — sideways, apologetic, already reaching back for the latch as though the night might follow him in. The lamplight caught him and Mirabel felt the familiar lurch, the one that started in her chest and ended somewhere behind her teeth: he looked older than twenty-four. He looked older every week.

Frost had whitened the shoulders of his courier coat and turned his eyebrows to pale stubble. His scarf was frozen stiff around his throat. And his hands —

"Your hands," she said.

"They'll thaw."

"Theo." She took his wrist before he could object and pulled him toward the stove, where the coals she'd banked that morning still held a dull orange heart. He let himself be led, which was itself an admission; Theo Joubert resisted help the way other men resisted confession. "Cold water first," she said, though there wasn't any, really, except what stood in the basin from washing up, and so she used that, working his fingers gently in the icy water because everyone knew you didn't warm frostbitten flesh fast, everyone knew it, and everyone who lived in this city now had learned at least one thing about the body's limits that they'd never wanted to know.

He hissed through his teeth but didn't pull away.

"Where?" she asked.

"North road. Then the river office."

"In this?"

"It was scheduled."

"Schedules," Mirabel said, and heard her own voice come out sharper than she'd intended, carrying a year of accumulated grievance in two syllables. She softened it. "Sit. Don't argue. I'll make tea."

"There's no tea."

"There's hot water and there's the end of the barley. It's tea if we're polite about it."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where smiles began when they still bothered to visit him. He sat on the stool by the stove, cradling his reddening hands, while she set the chipped kettle over the coals. Behind him, slung across his shoulder on its strap, hung the satchel.

It was grey canvas, government-issue, with a brass clasp gone green with age and a wax seal pressed flat against the flap like a scar. She had seen it a hundred times. She had never once seen him open it, or set it down anywhere but within arm's reach, or sleep without it hooked over the bedpost like a faithful dog. Tonight it sat against his hip and he sat very deliberately angled so that his body half-covered it, and he did this, Mirabel noticed, without appearing to notice he was doing it at all.

"How is it out there?" she asked, because it was easier than asking anything else.

"Worse." He considered. "Quieter, too. Those aren't usually friends. The cafés on the boulevard are dark. Half of them, anyway. The other half are full of men talking with their hands."

"Talking about what?"

"About whether they'll still be paid on Friday." He flexed his fingers in the basin water, wincing. "There were posters going up near the Ministry when I passed. I didn't stop to read them. You can tell a lot by how fast the paste-men work. These ones were hurrying."

The kettle began its low gathering hum. Mirabel got down three cups — three, always three, even when Colette was asleep, because a household that set out only two cups had started telling itself something — and spooned the last of the barley into the pot.

"You ate?"

"I ate."

"Liar. There's bread. Yesterday's, and it's gone hard, but I can soften it on the stove plate."

"Mira."

"Don't 'Mira' me. Sit there."

She toasted the bread slices until they steamed and gave them to him with the tea, and he ate, because he was honest underneath everything else and hunger always outvoted pride. She watched him eat and thought, not for the first time, that he had their father's jaw and none of their father's ease, that worry had carved lines into him early the way rain carves gullies into soft ground. He'd been nineteen when their parents died — old enough to inherit grief, young enough to inherit everything else along with it: the shop's debts, the lease, the fifteen-year-old ward nobody else had claimed. He had done it without complaint, which Mirabel had learned was not the same as doing it without cost.

"The coat's nearly done," she said. "Vasseur's. They'll send someone Thursday."

"Vasseur resigned. Didn't you hear?"

"He resigned, or he left?"

Theo looked at her over the rim of his cup. That was the trouble with them, sometimes — they understood each other too well, so that a question could be asked without being asked, and answered without being answered.

"Left," he said. "They don't resign anymore. Resigning implies there's something to resign from."

* * *

The floorboard in the hall creaked at the third spot, the one that sang a particular note, and both of them turned toward the doorway at exactly the same moment, which made Colette laugh.

"You're awake," Theo said.

"You're loud. You sound like a horse falling downstairs."

"I do not."

"You do. Mira, tell him he sounds like a horse."

"She agrees with me," Theo said.

Colette came in pulling her blanket around her shoulders like a mantle of state, her dark hair unbraided and wild, her feet stuffed into Mirabel's old felt slippers that swallowed her whole. Fifteen going on forty, Mirabel thought — the war had taken her parents at the border when she was eleven and had left behind a child who'd grown watchful and quick and funny in self-defense, the way plants grow sideways toward light. She kissed the top of Colette's head as she passed, and Colette allowed it with the long-suffering dignity of her age, and stole a piece of toast while doing so.

"There's barley tea," Mirabel said.

"There's barley water and we all know it." Colette dropped onto the bench beside the stove, tucking her feet under her. "I heard the boards. I was awake anyway. I couldn't sleep — Madame Roux's dog has been barking since midnight at absolutely nothing, which means either a burglar or the artillery range again."

"No artillery tonight. Too far north."

"You always say that."

"And I've been right so far."

"That's not how it works, though, is it." Colette said it lightly, the way she said most dangerous things, and reached for the toast again, and Mirabel watched her niece-sister-daughter-in-every-way-but-paper chew and thought: *she shouldn't know the word artillery sits in her mouth so comfortably.* But children here didn't get to not-know things anymore. Knowing was distributed equally now, like ration cards.

"Theo's hands froze," Mirabel said, partly to change the subject and partly to recruit.

Colette looked at his fingers, pink and stiff around the cup, and her face did something complicated — a flicker of fear quickly folded into practicality. "You should dip them in flour paste. Grandmother Roux swore by it."

"Her grandmother swore by leeches," Theo said.

"For other things."

Mirabel almost smiled. Almost. The room had gone warm around the stove's small radius, and the three of them had arranged themselves in their usual geometry — Theo at the stool, Colette on the bench, Mirabel standing because she was always standing, always moving, mending something even now, picking up the officer's coat and folding it over her arm. Outside the window the wind pushed at the glass like a beggar at a door.

"The satchel," Colette said.

Mirabel felt Theo go still. It was a small thing, a stillness lasting half a second, but she'd spent twenty years learning to read him the way she read cloth — by grain and tension, by where the weave pulled thin.

"What about it?" Theo said.

"You brought it home. On a rest day." Colette shrugged inside her blanket. "You never bring it home unless it's a rest day, and tomorrow isn't one, because you said so at dinner. So why is it here?"

The kettle ticked as it cooled. The lamp smoked. Mirabel kept her eyes on the coat in her arms and waited, because she wanted to hear the answer too and would rather die than ask.

"Because the office is closed tomorrow," Theo said. "Quartermaster's orders. Fuel shortage."

"You could have locked it in the courier vault."

"The vault's full."

"The vault is never full. Luc's brother works the vault and he says—"

"*Colette.*"

It came out of Theo harder than he'd meant it — they all heard that, the way a snapped thread is louder than a whole one — and Colette flinched back into her blanket, and the room changed temperature. Theo closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them he was Theo again, gentle, exhausted, sorry. "It's just papers," he said. "Routine dispatches. Nothing anyone needs to lose sleep over. Go back to bed, little one. It's late."

Colette looked at him a moment longer than a child looks at an adult — one of those long, level looks that reminded Mirabel the girl was keeping her own accounts now, had been since the border, and that some part of her had stopped believing adults at eleven years old and had never fully renewed the subscription. But she unfolded herself, said goodnight in a voice carefully stripped of everything, and padded out. Her door clicked shut. The third board sang.

The brothers-and-sister silence stretched.

"She asks questions," Mirabel said quietly.

"She's fifteen."

"She asks questions like someone collecting evidence."

Theo rubbed his thawing hands over his face. In the lamplight the shadows under his eyes looked bruised. "Everyone collects evidence now, Mira. It's the national pastime. We mended uniforms before the war and we'll mend them after it, whatever *after* turns out to mean, and meanwhile everyone collects evidence and keeps their heads down and their doors shut."

"Is that what you're doing? Keeping your head down?" She nodded at the satchel. "That seal is diplomatic wax. I may mend clothes for a living, Theo, but I'm not stupid. That's not routine dispatch weight. Routine dispatches go out in the morning and come back empty. That bag hasn't been empty in weeks. I've watched you carry it. It pulls your shoulder down on the left side, and you keep shifting the strap, and you've started sleeping with your back to the wall."

For a long moment he said nothing. The stove settled with a soft collapse of coals. Down in the street, far off, someone was walking home — footsteps ringing on frozen cobblestones, unhurried, ordinary, the last ordinary sound of the night.

"Theo," she said. "What's in the bag?"

He looked at her then, and she saw him weigh it — saw the exact mechanics of the weighing, the way she'd seen him weigh whether to tell Colette their parents weren't coming back, whether to tell her the shop couldn't pay the coal bill, whether to tell her anything true at all. It was his oldest habit and his most exhausting one: protection by omission, love administered like a bitter medicine in carefully measured doses.

"Not yet," he said finally.

"When, then?"

"Soon. I promise. Not tonight. Tonight my hands hurt and the barley tea is terrible and I want—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I want one more evening where it's just us, and the stove, and a coat that needs mending. Is that so much?"

Mirabel looked at him — at the frost melting in his hair, at the satchel pressed against his hip like a second heartbeat — and she thought about the seam she'd sewn that afternoon, four inches of torn wool, and how a tear always looked smaller from the outside than from within the lining, and how you never truly knew a garment until you turned it wrong-side out.

"One more evening," she agreed.

He exhaled. She picked up her needle.

But later, much later, when Theo was asleep in the next room with the satchel's strap looped over the bedpost and Colette breathed slow and even behind her door, Mirabel sat alone at the worktable with the lamp burning low, and she found herself thinking about the seal on the grey canvas. Diplomatic wax. The crest pressed into it was the republic's — the same crest that had been on her father's letterhead once, back when the shop cut suits for men who mattered — and she thought about how wax was made to hold things shut, and how every seal in history had eventually been broken by somebody curious enough, or frightened enough, or desperate enough.

Outside, the wind changed direction, coming up from the river now, carrying the smell of ice and smoke and the faint industrial groan of the docks where, though she did not yet know it, ships were already being counted.

Mirabel trimmed the wick at last. The flame steadied. She threaded a fresh needle, and sewed.
