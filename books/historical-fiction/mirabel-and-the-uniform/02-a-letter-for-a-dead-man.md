## 2. A Letter for a Dead Man

The letter came on a Thursday, which was the day Mirabel took in the officers' collars, and it came the way all terrible things come in a city that has learned to dread its own post — folded into an ordinary canvas satchel, stamped with wax gone soft from someone's pocket, and handed over without ceremony by a boy whose boots were too big for him and whose eyes were too old for his face.

"Joubert?" he said.

"That's us," said Colette, who was fourteen and answered doors as if she owned them.

Mirabel wiped her hands on her apron and came to the threshold of the mending room, where she could see past the girl's shoulder to the street: wet cobbles, a lamplighter making his slow rounds against a sky the color of dishwater, two soldiers at the corner pretending not to watch the door. The boy held out a single envelope on a flattened palm, the way one offers bread to a horse that might bite.

"For Minister Aubry," he said. "But the house is yours now, isn't it? The registry says so."

"The registry says what it says," said Mirabel, and took it.

She did not look at it until the door was shut and bolted, until Colette had been sent back to her hemming with a look that promised questions later, until Theo's absence had been counted once more among the ordinary costs of the day. Then she stood in the gray light of the window with her back to the room where she had spent eleven years making other men's coats decent, and she read the address.

*Minister Étienne Aubry.* Not a title anyone used anymore — the Ministry had been dissolved in the autumn, its ministers scattered like seed from a split sack — but the ink was confident, official, written by a hand that believed titles still meant something. Beneath the name: *Rue des Tanneurs, No. 9.*

Their address. Their father's address before it was theirs, the shop below and the rooms above, bought with thirty years of needle-work and kept through two changes of government because tailors were useful to everyone and dangerous to no one. Aubry had lived there for six years after their father sold it — or was made to sell it; the story changed depending on who told it and how much wine was open — and when Aubry died last week, coughing blood into a silk square in the back of a hired carriage, the house had passed to a speculator, the shop had stood empty, and the Jouberts had remained in their rented rooms across town, sewing, invisible, waiting for the century to finish falling down around them.

Except that someone, somewhere, still believed Étienne Aubry lived at Rue des Tanneurs, No. 9. And someone had written to him.

Mirabel turned the envelope over. It was sealed — proper wax, pressed with a signet she didn't recognize, some crest of interlaced lines that might have been a family arms or might have been nothing. The paper beneath the wax was good stock, cream-colored, the kind that cost more per sheet than Colette earned in a week of winding thread. And along the lower edge, where the envelope had been tucked inside a second wrapping for the courier's satchel, there was a strip of the inner enclosure showing: a line of handwriting, slanted and cramped, the letters leaning forward like runners at a start.

She knew that handwriting.

She had known it before she knew her own name. She had watched it move across tailor's chalk labels and ledger lines and the margins of her school copybooks, correcting her figures in that same forward-leaning script, impatient with waste, economical even in affection. Her father had been dead nine years, drowned — they said drowned; no one had ever produced a body — and his handwriting should have been as finished with the world as he was. But there it was, leaning into the light off the Rue des Tanneurs, alive on a strip of cream paper no wider than her thumb:

*aubry will know what to do with —*

And then the fold, and then nothing.

Mirabel set the letter on the cutting table, weighted it at the corners with four brass thimbles, and looked at it for a long time without touching it again. Outside, the lamplighter had moved on. The soldiers had not. Somewhere below, Madame Ferrand's dog began its evening argument with the stairs.

* * *

Theo came home after dark, letting himself in with the key he wore on a cord under his shirt, and he brought the cold in with him the way he always did, in his coat, in his hair, in the particular silence he made moving through a warm room. He was twenty-three and had been a diplomatic courier since he was seventeen, which meant he had spent six years carrying sealed things between men who would never learn his name, and it had taught him to enter a house the way water enters a crack: completely, quietly, leaving no trace except the sense that something had gotten in.

He stopped in the doorway of the mending room. He had smelled it before he saw it, he said later — or claimed to have; Theo liked to attribute to himself preternatural gifts that were really only attention. His eyes went from Mirabel's face to the table, to the four thimbles standing guard around the cream envelope, and Mirabel watched six years of training pass across her brother's features like weather: first blankness, then assessment, then the decision, already forming, about what this thing was and what it would cost.

"What is that?"

"A letter."

"I can see that it's a letter." He came closer. He did not touch it. "Whose?"

"Minister Aubry's."

Theo went very still. In their household stillness was a language; they had all learned it young, the way children of deaf parents learn hands. "Aubry is dead."

"Aubry is dead," Mirabel agreed. "But the post doesn't read the obituaries. A boy brought it at midday. Addressed to Rue des Tanneurs, Number Nine."

"And the registry forwarded it here why?"

"Because we're the nearest thing the name Joubert has to an address. Because the boy was lazy. Because God wanted us to have it." She shrugged, and hated how light the shrug sounded. "Does it matter?"

"It matters." Theo sat down across from her, slowly, the way a man sits when he wants his body to have time to think while his mouth catches up. "Mirabel. Look at me."

"I'm looking at you."

"Have you opened it?"

"No."

"Has Colette seen it?"

"She answered the door. She saw the outside. She's been asking about it since four o'clock, and I've told her it's a misdelivery and nothing else, and she's agreed to believe me the way she agrees to believe everything I say lately, which is completely and with her eyes open."

That almost got a smile out of him. Almost. Then his gaze dropped to the envelope again, to the strip of exposed paper, and Mirabel watched him see it — the slanted hand, the runner-lean of those letters — and watched him understand what she had understood at noon, alone, in the gray light, with her heart going like a trapped bird against her ribs.

"That's—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter, as if the words might be overheard by the coat rack. "That's Father's writing."

"Yes."

"He's dead, Mirabel."

"So is Aubry, and yet."

"It's not possible. It can't be his hand. You're tired, you've been bending over collars since—"

"I copied that hand for ten years doing his invoice books. I know it the way I know my own pulse. It's his, Theo. Or it's someone who learned it well enough to fool me, and I don't know which possibility frightens me more."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The stove ticked as it cooled. From Colette's room came the small rhythmic sound of her reading aloud to herself, which she thought they didn't hear and which they had both pretended not to hear for three years, because it was easier than telling her that at fifteen she was too old to need the practice and too proud to admit she still needed the company.

Theo reached out and turned the envelope, gently, with one finger, rotating it under the lamp so the seal faced up. He studied the crest. Whatever he found in it, he kept behind his face.

"We return it," he said. "Unopened. Tomorrow morning, first thing, I take it myself to the postal directorate, I say it was misdelivered, I say we never touched the wax. They log it. It goes wherever dead ministers' mail goes, which is a furnace, if we're lucky, and nowhere near us either way."

"You've thought about this already."

"I've thought about it since you said 'Rue des Tanneurs.'" He leaned back. His hands, Mirabel noticed, were flat on the table on either side of the letter, deliberately, the way a man puts his hands flat when he doesn't trust them not to reach. "Mirabel, listen to me. We are nobody. That has taken nine years to build and it is the only thing we own. Papa sold the shop, Papa disappeared, and the Joubert name went into the ground with him, and no one has dug for it since because no one has had a reason to. Do you understand what a gift that is, in this city, this winter? There are families being pulled out of their beds at night because somebody's cousin remembered something. And you are sitting there looking at a letter addressed to a dead man in our father's handwriting like it's a Christmas parcel."

"I'm looking at it," said Mirabel evenly, "like it's a question."

"There is no such thing as a question from the past. There are only summons." He tapped the table once, beside the envelope, not touching it. "You open that, and we stop being nobody. You open that, and somewhere — I don't know where, I carry enough seals in a week to furnish a cathedral and I promise you I don't know where — but *somewhere*, there is a man whose job it is to notice that a letter to Étienne Aubry went astray. And he notices, and he asks, and the asking arrives at our door the way the letter did: folded in a satchel, delivered by a boy with big boots, on an ordinary Thursday. That's how it always comes, Mirabel. It never comes wearing its own face."

She heard him. She heard all of it, and every word of it was correct, and she loved him for saying it, and none of it mattered, because her eyes kept going back to the strip of cream paper and the sentence that ended mid-flight.

*Aubry will know what to do with —*

With what? With the list, if there was a list — and her skin said *list* before her mind permitted the word, the way the body knows a fever before the thermometer does. With the names. With whatever her father had been carrying in the last months of his life, in the shop below the rooms where Aubry would later sleep, in the weeks before the river took him or a man did or the war simply closed over his head like water closing over a dropped coin. She had been eleven. She remembered the last winter mostly in textures: the smell of the pressing irons, the weight of snow on the awning, her mother's silence, which had begun that year and never properly ended. She did not remember her father afraid. But she remembered him *busy*, in a way that had nothing to do with coats.

"Theo." She kept her voice low, level, the voice she used with difficult clients. "When did you last eat?"

"This isn't about dinner."

"When did you last eat?"

"...Noon."

"Then eat something, and then we'll talk, because you argue better fed and I listen worse hungry, and this conversation is going to be long." She rose, gathered the thimbles one by one, and left the letter centered on the bare wood like a coin placed on a dead man's eye. "And Theo — it stays on the table tonight. Sealed. Where I can see it. If I put it in a drawer I'll hear it breathing in there till dawn, and I'd rather be tired than haunted twice."

He almost laughed at that. She felt the laugh in him the way you feel a fish take the line and refuse it.

* * *

They talked long. They talked past midnight, past the last tram groaning home along the boulevard, past Colette's reading voice fading into sleep. They talked the way siblings talk when they are also conspirators and also each other's jailers: in circles, in code, in the shorthand of a shared childhood. Theo laid out the case for the furnace with the patience of a man building a wall brick by brick — the registry logs, the courier's oath he had signed at seventeen, the neighbors, the soldiers at the corner who had been at that corner three days running now, had she noticed, three days, which was two days longer than corners usually warranted. And Mirabel heard the wall rise, and admired the workmanship, and stood on the far side of it.

Because here was the thing she could not say to him, or could not say plainly: that the letter was not a danger arriving from the past. It was a message *from* the past, and the past had been silent for nine years, and silence, in her experience, was never peace. Silence was just the sound a secret makes while it waits.

Her father had informed. She didn't have proof of it; she had something better, which was the shape of it, the way grief takes the shape of what it holds. A tailor on the Rue des Tanneurs, useful to everyone, dangerous to no one — and then a sale he couldn't have refused, and a disappearance no one investigated, and a widow who stopped speaking above a whisper, and two children raised afterward in the doctrine of invisibility that Theo had just recited to her like scripture. Nobody had ever told them *why*. Nobody had ever had to. The why had soaked into the walls of their lives the way smoke soaks into wool, and they had worn it all these years without knowing what the smell meant.

And now here was a letter. Cream paper, good wax, her father's forward-leaning hand — *aubry will know what to do with* — and a minister who had died a week ago at their father's old address, coughing blood into silk, taking whatever he knew down with him unless someone had written it down.

Unless her father had written it down. Before the river. Before the end.

"Give me one reason," Theo said finally, wearily, somewhere past two o'clock, his head in his hands, "one honest reason, that isn't just that you want to hear his voice."

And Mirabel, who prided herself on honesty the way Theo prided himself on discretion, found that she could not give him one. Wanting was the whole of it. Nine years of wanting, folded up small and carried under the ribs like a second heart, and now lying on a cutting table under four brass thimbles, breathing.

"Go to bed," she said instead. "We'll decide in the morning."

"There's nothing to decide."

"There's always something to decide. That's what the morning is for."

He went. She heard him pause at Colette's door — she heard the small wordless ritual of it, his shadow crossing the gap beneath the door, the pause that was his goodnight — and then his own door, and then the house settled into the particular silence of three people awake in separate rooms, pretending.

Mirabel stayed at the table. She banked the stove low and let the lamp burn, and she sat with the letter the way she sat with a garment that needed unpicking: studying the seams before committing the blade.

It would be so easy. Steam the flap over the kettle's spout, the way she relaxed a glued lining; lift it whole; read; reseal with a drop of the wax she kept for mending broken seals on officers' dispatch cases — she had done that twice this month already, for men who trusted her more than they trusted their own clerks. No one would ever know. The letter would go to the directorate tomorrow looking exactly as it looked tonight, and whatever it said would live thereafter only in her, and she was, she reminded herself, very good at keeping things. She had kept a household together on mending money through two winters of shortages. She had kept a dead man's reputation intact without knowing whether it deserved keeping. She had kept Colette fed and Theo honest and herself upright, and she could keep one secret.

The lamp guttered, righted itself.

On the table, the wax caught the light and threw it back dull red, like something cooling. *Aubry will know what to do with —*

Mirabel looked at her father's handwriting for a long time. Then she did not open the letter. She blew out the lamp instead, and sat a while longer in the dark, listening to the stove tick and the wind worry the shutter and Colette turn over in her sleep — and it was only when she finally rose, stiff and cold, to carry the letter to the drawer after all, against her own rule, because the dark had made it seem less like a living thing — that she understood what Theo would not learn until morning.

The letter was gone from where the thimbles had guarded it.

In its place, weighted neatly under all four brass cups, lay a single slip of paper torn from a school copybook, in a careful, childish, forward-leaning imitation of a hand she knew too well:

*I only wanted to look. Please don't be angry.*

Colette's room, when Mirabel pushed open the door, was empty. The window stood open to the freezing street, curtains stirring, and somewhere below, growing fainter, she could hear the quick, light, terribly familiar sound of running feet on wet cobblestones — heading, though Mirabel could not yet know it, toward the Rue des Tanneurs, toward Number Nine, toward the empty shop where a dead man's mail had once been delivered and where a fifteen-year-old girl was about to learn exactly how heavy other people's secrets could be.

Mirabel stood in the cold pouring through the window, in her nightdress, holding a forged apology in her father's handwriting, and heard herself say aloud, to no one, in a voice she did not recognize:

"Colette. Oh, child. What have you done."
