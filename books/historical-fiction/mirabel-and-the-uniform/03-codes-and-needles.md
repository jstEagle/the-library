## 3. Codes and Needles

Theo Joubert had been carrying secrets so long he had stopped thinking of them as weight. They sat in him the way a stone sits in a riverbed — settled, worn smooth, part of the architecture of the current that moved over it. He had carried dispatches through checkpoints where a wrong word meant a cell, codes sewn into his collarbone pocket by his own clumsy hand, memorized ciphers he recited to himself on night trains like prayers to a god he no longer believed in. None of it had ever felt like a choice.

The letter lying on the kitchen table felt like one.

It had been there for two days now, since the postman's knock, since Mirabel had turned it over in her flour-dusted hands and said, *This is Father's handwriting,* in a voice Theo had never heard her use before — not frightened, exactly. Recognizing. The way you recognize a face across a crowded station years after the funeral.

The letter was addressed to Minister Aubry, care of 14 Rue Verte — the address above the tailor shop, the flat where they had all once lived, before Aubry died there a week ago Tuesday with his boots off and his papers in order, and before the ministry's forwarding clerk, drunk or hurried or simply indifferent, sent the dead man's mail rattling down the chain of an address that no longer belonged to anyone who mattered.

*Return it unopened,* Theo had said. He had said it twice. He was saying it again now, standing at the stove while the soup did its thin best with a ham bone and three carrots, because saying things repeatedly sometimes made them true.

"You've said that," Mirabel said from the table. She wasn't looking at the letter. That was how he knew she was thinking about nothing else. She was mending a captain's greatcoat, needle moving in that rhythm she had — even, patient, unhurried, as though the coat would wait out the century if it had to. "Twice yesterday. Four times today."

"Then I'll say it a fifth."

"The wax isn't broken." She turned the envelope toward him without stopping her sewing. The seal was intact — the ministry's crest pressed into dark red wax, uncracked, whole. A sealed thing. An honest thing. "We could put it back in the box tomorrow and be finished with it. We could write *misdelivered, addressee deceased* on the front and be people who did the correct thing."

"We are people who do the correct thing."

"Are we?" She looked up then, and the lamplight caught her eyes, and Theo saw in them something that frightened him more than any checkpoint ever had: his own curiosity, reflected back, grown teeth.

He took the pot off the heat because his hands needed occupation. Outside, the city made its evening noises — the tram that only ran half its route now, a dog somewhere near the canal, the particular silence of streets that had learned to go dark early. The republic was dying the way their father had died: slowly, officially, with everyone pretending until the end that the paperwork still meant something.

"Mirabel." He set the pot down. "You don't know what's in these things. You can't know. I do."

"Do you?" Quietly. Not cruel. Just precise, the way she was precise about seams.

"I carry diplomatic correspondence for a government—" He stopped. Started again. "For a government that hasn't existed on any map for eleven days. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The ministry I answer to dissolved itself last Thursday afternoon between four and five o'clock. There was a memorandum. I was one of six people who read it. Then the men who wrote it got into cars and drove west, and the memorandum instructed me to continue operations as normal."

"As normal," Mirabel repeated.

"As normal," Theo confirmed. "I have been delivering coded instructions from ministers who have resigned to undersecretaries who have not yet been told, on behalf of a state whose borders exist now only in the ink of treaties nobody intends to honor. I carry the cipher keys for embassies that have already burned their files. Last week I delivered a packet marked urgent-priority-highest to a building that had been requisitioned by the other side, and the guard at the desk signed for it, and I saluted, and we both pretended." He laughed, briefly, and hated the sound of it. "I am a courier, Mirabel. But there is increasingly nothing left to deliver except the fact of delivery itself."

Colette was at the far end of the table with her schoolbooks, and Theo lowered his voice without deciding to — the habit of fifteen years of secrets compressing itself into the space of a sentence. Colette, fifteen years old, orphaned by the border war, given to them at nine with two suitcases and a cough that lasted a winter; Colette, who did her arithmetic and hummed when she thought no one listened and asked, at dinner, with the terrible directness of children, questions like *Why does Uncle Theo's coat have so many pockets?*

Colette was copying figures. Her pencil moved. Her eyes, Theo would have sworn later, did not move at all.

"The letter," Mirabel said. "What do you think is in it?"

And there it was. The question underneath the question, the one they had both been carrying around the flat for two days like a hot coal passed hand to hand. Because it was not Aubry's handwriting on the enclosure. It was their father's. Their father, who had been dead seven years, whose hands Theo remembered only as chalk-white with tailor's dust, whose last act of fatherhood had been measuring Theo for a coat the week before the cough took him — *you'll need good shoulders for whatever you end up carrying* — and whose name had surfaced, two days ago, in Mirabel's careful voice across this same table:

*I know his hand. I'd know it anywhere. He addressed envelopes like this, look — the little hook on the J.*

A letter written by a dead man, sealed, addressed to another dead man, arriving at the door of the dead man's children seven years too late to be innocent.

"I think," Theo said, and found he could not finish the sentence standing up. He sat. The chair complained. "I think it's a list."

"A list."

"There were lists. Toward the end of the border war, before you and Colette came here — there were men in the ministry who kept lists. Names. People who had... assisted. Who had passed information, or goods, or silence. Some of them were patriots doing dirty work for clean reasons. Some of them were just men who liked being owed favors. When the war ended, the lists were supposed to be destroyed. Most were. Not all. Things are never all destroyed; they're misplaced. Misdelivered." He gestured at the envelope, red wax gleaming. "Delivered to the wrong door by a tired clerk."

"And Father's name—"

"Father ran a shop on a street where officers lived. During the war. Before either of us understood what the war was. He mended uniforms for everyone, Mirabel. For every side that wore one." Theo heard his own voice go flat, the courier's voice, the voice that reported facts without reporting himself. "Do you remember the year the shop did badly? Nineteen-twelve. We ate potatoes for a season and he told us it was the tariffs."

Mirabel's needle had gone still.

"I remember the coat he made me," she said. "For my confirmation. I remember it was better wool than we could afford, and I never asked."

"Nobody asked anything, in those years. That was the arrangement. That was the whole country, really — a household where nobody asked anything." Theo looked at the letter. Up close, in the lamplight, he could see what he had been refusing to see for two days: the faint shadow of a second page folded inside, the thickness of it, the weight of paper that had been handled and rehandled before being sealed. Someone had held this letter a long time before sending it. Someone had hesitated. "If his name is in there, it's been in there for seven years. It changed nothing while it sat in a drawer. It changes everything the moment it's read."

"By whom?"

"That's the real question, isn't it." He rubbed his eyes. "Because whoever wrote it — whoever kept it — whoever finally dropped it into the post last week, seven years after Father died and one week after Aubry did — that person wanted it read. Dead men don't need lists. Living ones do. And right now, in this city, there are people assembling exactly such lists on the other side of the coming change, and a name on one of those lists is not a historical footnote. It's a sentence. Sometimes literally."

The clock in the hall ticked. Colette's pencil scratched. The soup cooled.

"So we return it," Mirabel said.

"Yes."

"To whom?"

And Theo opened his mouth and found the riverbed stone turning over, the current shifting, because the answer was: to the ministry. To what remained of the ministry. To undersecretaries and clerks and the six people who had read the dissolution memorandum, some of whom were loyal and some of whom were not, and he — Theo Joubert, courier, carrier of other people's certainties — could not tell which was which anymore. He knew faces and ranks and dead drops. He did not know, for the first time in his working life, who the letter would be safe with.

Which meant it was safe with no one.

Which meant, by the merciless arithmetic of such things, that the only place it was safe was inside this flat, behind this door, in the hands of a seamstress and a courier and a girl doing sums — until it wasn't safe anywhere at all.

"Mirabel," he said. "Come here. Both—" No. He corrected himself, and the correction cost him something. "Come here. Just you."

Colette's pencil did not pause. Theo gathered the letter, the lamp, his brotherly authority, and the last of his honesty, and went up the narrow stairs to the mending room, where the light was better and the walls were thinner, and where he shut the door anyway, because rituals matter even when they're theater.

* * *

The mending room smelled of steam and chalk and the particular clean dust of wool cut long ago. Uniforms hung from the rail along one wall like a row of headless men waiting their turn: a lieutenant's tunic missing two buttons, a colonel's coat with a torn lining, a conscript's jacket that belonged to a boy of maybe seventeen, its shoulders let out twice already to keep pace with a body growing through a war. Mirabel had mended uniforms for the army, the gendarmerie, the customs service, the railway police. Whoever governed next would bring her theirs, and she would mend those too, because cloth did not take sides and neither, officially, did thread.

She stood by the window with her arms folded, watching him, and Theo realized he was holding the letter against his chest like a shield, which was absurd, and put it down on the cutting table.

"If we open it," he said, "we can't un-open it. That sounds like nothing. It isn't nothing. Everything I have survived, I have survived by not knowing things. I've walked past cells full of people who knew things. The knowing is what they take you for. Knowledge has a smell, Mirabel. Dogs can't smell it, but men can."

"You're afraid."

"I'm terrified." He said it plainly, because lying to her had never once worked and he was saving what little skill he had. "But listen to me. Listen to the actual shape of it. If we return this letter, it goes into the machine. Into the files. Into whatever the files become when the government changes hands — and they will change hands, Mirabel, within weeks, maybe days. Every archive in this city will be read by new eyes. New eyes that are looking for exactly these names. If Father's name is on that list, returning this letter doesn't protect us. It delivers us. It puts our address on the envelope of his crime."

"And opening it?"

"Opening it means we know. And if we know, we might have time. Time to burn it, bury it, deny it. Time to be clever instead of correct." He laughed again, that awful small laugh. "Do you hear what I'm proposing? I'm proposing we commit the first dishonest act of our lives in order to protect a dead man's secret, and I can't even tell if it's for him or for us."

"For us," Mirabel said. "For Colette. Say it properly."

"For us. For Colette."

She was quiet a long moment. Down in the street a cart went by, iron-shod wheels on wet cobbles, the sound of ordinary life continuing its stubborn rounds. Somewhere beyond the blacked-out windows, the republic was finishing its accounts.

"He lied to us," Mirabel said at last. "All those years. The bad season, the tariffs. He sat across from us at that table and invented a reason, and we believed him because children believe, and I have spent seven years believing him, and I want—" Her voice caught, steadied. She was a woman who pressed seams flat; she pressed her voice flat too. "I want to know whether I loved a liar. Or whether I loved a man who did an ugly thing to keep two children fed, which is a different animal entirely, and I need to know which, Theo. I need to know it the way I need to know whether a tear goes with the weave or across it, because the repair is different. The repair is always different."

Theo looked at his sister in the lamplight — her steady hands, her ink-stained fingers, the thimble worn silver-bright on her middle finger — and understood that the decision had already been made, had perhaps been made two days ago at the postman's knock, and that everything since had been the two of them walking politely toward a door that was already open.

"Then we open it," he said.

They stood on either side of the cutting table. Neither moved for a moment. It reminded Theo of the moment before cutting cloth — his father's rule, spoken a hundred times in that shop below: *measure twice, because the second cut forgives nothing.* Seven years dead and the man was still instructing them.

Mirabel fetched the kettle. Steam, applied patiently, lifted seals the way patience lifted everything; she had steamed open and resealed enough intercepted parcels in her mending career — officers' wives' letters, once, for a lieutenant too ashamed to admit he couldn't read — to do it cleanly. The wax softened. Theo slid a thin blade beneath it, the courier's old trick, breath held, and the seal lifted whole as a lily pad, and the envelope exhaled the smell of paper kept long in a drawer: lavender, dust, and something faintly chemical, ink that had never quite dried.

Two sheets. The first was a list. Even upside down, even at a glance, Theo saw names — a column of them, dates beside some, amounts beside others, and one name circled in ink that had browned but not faded, the circle drawn hard enough to groove the page.

Their father's name. Étienne Joubert. Circled.

The second sheet was folded separately, smaller, and on its outside, in that unmistakable hooked hand, was written: *For my daughter, if it ever comes to her.*

Mirabel made a sound Theo had never heard from her — small, involuntary, the sound a seam makes just before it gives — and did not reach for the second sheet. She reached for the first. She read it top to bottom. Then she set it face-down on the table with exaggerated gentleness, as though it might bruise, and stood with both hands flat on the wood, head bowed, breathing.

Theo did not read the list. He found he could not. His eyes kept going instead to the folded sheet, to *my daughter*, to the fact that their father had written to Mirabel and not to him, and some old childhood arithmetic — the eldest son, the apprentice, the heir to the shop — turned over painfully in his chest.

"Later," Mirabel said, without lifting her head. "Both of them. Later. Tonight is for—" She straightened. Her eyes were dry and very bright. "Tonight is for being practical, because one of us has to be and it has always been me."

* * *

What she did then, Theo watched from the doorway with the list unread and burning a hole through the table beneath his elbow.

She went to the peg by the door where his courier coat hung — the grey coat, the one that had crossed more borders than most ambassadors — and she took it down and turned it inside out on the cutting table, and she began to unpick the left breast seam with a razor blade and a bone folder, movements quick and certain, forty stitches gone in a minute.

"What are you doing?"

"Making a pocket that doesn't exist." She didn't look up. Chalk appeared in her hand; she marked the lining in a place only she could find again — beneath the left breast, over the heart, where a passport or a folded page could lie flat against the body's warmth and pass a pat-down as nothing but chest. "Every search you've ever been through — they check the obvious pockets, the collar, the hem. Nobody checks a seam that was closed when the coat was made. Hold this."

He held. She fed a fold of the list — not the whole list; she had measured it against the space with a glance, folded it in thirds along lines she scored with the bone folder — into the cavity, basted it, and then began to close the seam with stitches so fine and so regular they vanished into the existing line like water into water. Thread matched. Tension matched. Theo, who had grown up in a tailor's shop and knew good work from great, watched his sister do work that was neither: it was invisible. It was the kind of mending whose entire purpose was to make history unverifiable.

"When they change the guards at the checkpoints," she said, needle flashing, voice level, "and they start searching couriers properly — and they will, Theo, they'll be hunting archives, and couriers are walking archives — you'll be carrying it. Not the original. I'm making a copy tonight, my hand, aged with tea. The original stays here, under a floorboard I choose tomorrow. You carry the copy, because if it's taken from you, it's taken from a stranger's body, in a stranger's hand, with no connection to this flat. And if you're searched and it's found—" She tied off, bit the thread, and looked up. "Then you were a courier carrying contraband for money. Men do it. Men have always done it. It's a survivable story. Ours is not."

Theo stared at her. "How long have you been planning this?"

"Since the postman knocked." She turned the coat right-side out and brushed it with her sleeve, and it hung on its hanger looking exactly as it had looked for eleven years — grey, shabby, loyal, empty. "You spend your life carrying secrets for a government. I spend mine making things disappear into seams. We're the same trade, Theo. You just get paid worse."

He should have laughed. Instead he felt something shift in him — the stone in the riverbed finally rolling, the current changing course — and he understood that they had crossed a line together, in this room, in the space of an evening, and that there was no returning letter now. The seal was lifted. The copy would be sewn. The family's careful invisibility, maintained for seven years with potatoes and silence and correct behavior, was spent.

"We're complicit now," he said. "Whatever's on that list. Whatever Father did. As of tonight we're protecting it."

"No." Mirabel took the second sheet — *for my daughter* — and slipped it inside her bodice, against her skin, where he pretended not to see. "As of tonight, we're *informed*. Complicity requires a choice, and we haven't made ours yet." She pinched out the lamp. In the sudden dark her voice came steady as a hem. "That choice comes when someone asks us what we know. And Theo—"

Downstairs, faint but unmistakable, Colette's chair scraped back from the table. Pencil down. Schoolwork done. Footsteps on the stair, light and unhurried, a girl climbing toward a room where her supper waited and her family sat above her with their secrets sewn shut against their hearts.

"—someone may ask sooner than we think," Mirabel finished, too quietly for anyone but him.

The footsteps paused on the landing.
