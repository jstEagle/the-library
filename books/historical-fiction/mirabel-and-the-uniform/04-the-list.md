## 4. The List

The letter had sat on the mantel for three days, and by the third night Mirabel could no longer pretend it was simply a thing that lived there, like the chipped figurine of a saint or the tin of buttons they never sorted. It was a presence. It had weight the way a sleeping animal has weight — you could feel the room arranging itself around it.

Theo had turned the envelope over perhaps forty times in those three days. Mirabel had watched him do it with the patience of someone watching weather: the thick cream paper going from lamplight to shadow, the wax seal catching orange, then dull, then orange again. He never touched the seal itself. His fingers hovered above it as though it were hot iron, and each time he set the letter back on the mantel he did so with the exaggerated care of a man returning something stolen.

"It goes back Monday," he said now, from his chair by the stove, where he sat with his frost-scarred hands spread toward the coals. "Courier post. I'll route it through the ministry dead-letter office and no one will ever know it passed through this house."

"You've said that every night."

"Because it remains true every night."

Mirabel said nothing. She was working on a colonel's greatcoat by the window, where the last grey light came down through the frosted glass in bars, and her needle moved in the small steady rhythm that was the only prayer she'd ever learned. But her eyes went, once, twice, to the mantel. To the second envelope inside the first — the enclosure their father's hand had addressed, the slanted, confident script she would have known anywhere, even after eleven years, even upside down, even burned into ash. *To A. Aubry, private.* Their father had written those words. Their dead father, whose shop still stood shuttered on Rue Verte, whose name no one in the household spoke above the volume of the stove's crackle.

"Mirabel." Theo's voice had gone quiet in the way it did when he was about to argue. "I know what you're thinking."

"You don't."

"I know exactly what you're thinking because I'm thinking it too, and one of us has to be the responsible one, and historically that's been me."

"Historically," Mirabel said, threading her needle, "you were the one who stole Father's good shears and buried them in the garden so I couldn't use them."

"I was seven. And I confessed within the hour, which is the relevant point." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the firelight moved across his face, hollowing his cheeks, making him look suddenly much more like their father than either of them liked to acknowledge. "Mirabel. That letter is addressed to a dead minister at an address that hasn't been ours since before Colette could walk. Someone made a mistake somewhere in a dying postal system, and mistakes are not invitations. If we open it — if anyone ever learns we opened it — we're not curious anymore. We're implicated. Do you understand the difference?"

She understood it perfectly. She also understood that her father's handwriting was sitting ten feet away from her, sealed behind wax, and that Theo had never once, in eleven years, been able to explain why their father had stopped writing letters in the final months of his life. Why the shop had closed. Why they had left Rue Verte the way one leaves a house where something has happened — quickly, at dusk, without looking back.

"Colette asked me today what's on the mantel," Mirabel said.

Theo went very still.

"She thinks it's a love letter," Mirabel went on. "She thinks it's mine." The needle paused in the wool. "Theo, she's fifteen. She was born in this city, she lost her parents to the border war, and she lives in a household where her guardians whisper around a sealed letter like it's an unexploded shell. She is going to find out something. I would rather she found out nothing, but if she must find out something, I would rather it be from us."

"So your argument is that we should open it for Colette's sake."

"My argument," Mirabel said, setting down the greatcoat at last, "is that I have been telling myself for three days that we shouldn't open it, and every reason I give myself is a reason about other people, and I am tired of lying to myself when there's apparently a whole profession for it across the room."

Theo looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a short, defeated sound, smoke-colored. "Father used to say honesty ran in the family like bad eyesight."

"He said worse things about it too."

"He did." Theo rose. His hands, even thawed, never fully straightened anymore; they opened like something half-folded. He crossed the room to the mantel and stood before the letter the way men stood before doors in stories, and Mirabel found she had stopped breathing without deciding to.

"If there's nothing in it," he said, "we burn it tonight and speak of it never."

"And if there's something in it?"

He didn't answer that. He picked up the letter, worked his thumbnail under the wax seal — which cracked with a small, obscene click, like a knuckle or a lock — and drew out two sheets of paper. The outer sheet was official stock, watermarked, typed. The inner sheet was the enclosure. Their father's hand.

Theo read the typed page first. His lips moved slightly; Mirabel saw them form the salutation, the formal courtesies of a government writing to its own dead. Then his eyes dropped to the second page, and whatever was there struck him so squarely that he sat down without appearing to decide to, the chair taking him the way ground takes a man who has been shot.

"Theo."

He held the page out to her. His hand was shaking, and it was not the cold.

* * *

It was a list. That was all, at first glance — a list of fourteen names in their father's upright, schoolmasterly script, each followed by a date and a sum, the kind of ledger any tradesman might keep. Mirabel's eye went down it the way an eye travels a familiar street, and then stopped, and would not move, because the third name from the top was *Joubert, Émile,* and beside it, in ink so dark it seemed still wet, their father had drawn a circle. Not a neat circle. A circle pressed hard enough to score the paper beneath, the kind of mark a man makes when his pen has become something else in his grip.

The dates beside the names ranged across nine years. The sums were modest, mostly — forty francs, sixty, one entry of two hundred that sat apart from the others like a wound. At the bottom of the page, in smaller script, almost apologetic: *Aubry will know what to do with this. No one else must.*

"Aubry died Tuesday," Theo said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used reading official telegrams aloud. "At the Rue Verte address. Which means this letter has been traveling for — how long? Weeks? It was written before he died. Father wrote it before he—" He stopped. Started again. "Father has been dead eleven years, Mirabel. Who posted this letter? Where has it been for eleven years?"

But Mirabel wasn't listening. She was looking at the names. She knew some of them — merchants from the old quarter, a customs man her father had played cards with, a name that might have belonged to a priest. Ordinary names. Names that had bought bread and quarreled about prices and walked past the shop window every morning of her childhood. And threaded among them, twice, in positions that meant nothing and everything — fourth on the list and eleventh — a name she did not know at all.

*Delorme.*

Twice. Every other name appeared once. Delorme appeared twice, with different dates four years apart, and the second time the sum was blank, as though whatever Delorme had given or taken had not been the sort of thing one priced.

"Do you know a Delorme?" she asked.

"No." Theo took the page back, studied it, and she watched him arrive at the same arithmetic she had. "Do you?"

"No."

"Then who—"

The door opened.

They came apart from each other too fast, the way people do when they are guilty of nothing yet — Mirabel turning to the window, Theo sliding the pages under the cushion of his chair with a motion so fluid it frightened her a little, that ease, that naturalness, as though concealment were a muscle he'd been training all his life without knowing it. And then Colette was in the doorway, stamping snow off her boots, cheeks scarlet with cold, a paper bag of chestnuts clutched to her chest like contraband.

"They had them at the corner again! The old ones, the good ones, not the woody ones." She pulled off her mittens with her teeth. "Madame Fournier says there's talk at the market that the ministry's evacuated the archives already. She says her nephew saw the lorries at midnight, dozens of them, going east with the blinds drawn." Colette said all this while shrugging out of her coat, and only when she'd hung it did she pause, tilt her head, and look at the two of them with the sudden unnerving directness that had been arriving lately in place of childhood. "What were you doing when I came in?"

"Tidying," Mirabel said.

"The cushions?"

"The general area."

Colette looked at Theo. Theo smiled the smile of a man with papers under his cushion, and Mirabel saw that Colette saw it, and filed it, and let it go — which was somehow worse than if she hadn't.

* * *

Dinner that night was chestnuts and the end of a loaf, and a soup Mirabel had stretched with water and onions until it was less a food than a memory of food, and Colette talked the entire time, which she rarely did, and Mirabel understood within minutes that this was interrogation wearing the costume of chatter.

"Why does Theo get to keep his coat on indoors?" — because the courier coat with the new hidden pocket hung on his chair, and he would not be parted from it. "Why did Madame Fournier cross the street when she saw me coming?" — because Madame Fournier had mended nothing herself in years and resented paying. "Was Papa Joubert rich, before?"

The question dropped into the middle of the table like a spoon into soup.

"Not rich," Mirabel said carefully. "Comfortable. Tailoring was better work, then."

"What happened to the shop?"

"We closed it."

"Why?"

"Business got slow."

Colette chewed her chestnut. Her eyes, dark and quick as a bird's, went from Mirabel to Theo and back. "That's not why," she said, not accusingly — worse, conversationally, the way one notes the weather. "Madame Fournier's nephew works at the registry office now. He told her there are old files being moved, and that some families' files are thicker than others." She peeled another chestnut with terrible precision. "He says the thin ones are the lucky ones."

"Colette." Theo's voice was gentle. "Eat your dinner."

"I am eating my dinner." She was. But she looked up once more, and what was in her face was not suspicion, exactly. It was the beginning of understanding that the adults in her life kept a locked room, and that she had just heard, through the wall of it, furniture moving.

* * *

Afterward, when Colette had gone up to bed — after the long creak of the stairs and the longer silence that followed it — Mirabel laid the two pages side by side on the mending table and put the lamp between them, and she and Theo read the list again the way one rereads a diagnosis, hoping the words will have rearranged themselves.

They hadn't.

"Informers," Theo said finally. The word fell out of him like a tooth. "These dates, these sums — this is a payment record, Mirabel. Or a blackmail record. Either way, our father was keeping accounts with—" He gestured at the names. "With these people. With whatever network this was. And he circled his own name."

"People circle their own names on lists all the time."

"Not like that. Not hard enough to cut the paper." Theo pressed his half-folded hands flat on the table. "That's the circle of a man who wants the name to hurt. Who comes back to it. Who touches it the way you touch a bruise." He looked up. "Eleven years, Mirabel. Eleven years I built him a story. The shop failed, the times turned, honest men go under. I carried that story like a courier carries dispatches — sealed, protected, delivered intact. And now there's a piece of paper on our table that weighs more than every dispatch I've ever moved."

Mirabel said nothing. She was looking at *Delorme*, written twice in her father's hand. There was something in the way the name sat on the page — the others were entries, but Delorme was a relationship; she couldn't have said how she knew, only that the second instance had a small dash after it, a hyphen trailing into nothing, as though her father had begun to write something more and stopped himself.

"The letter was addressed to Aubry," she said. "'No one else must.' So whoever posted it broke Father's rule. Or obeyed a newer one." She looked up. "Someone out there knows this list exists, Theo. Someone decided, eleven years late, that a dying minister needed to see it. And that someone knows it was misdelivered to this address — because the address on the envelope is this address, and the postal service doesn't correct its mistakes, it abandons them."

Theo was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lower, stripped of argument. "You're saying someone may come looking for it."

"I'm saying a letter that took eleven years to travel six streets didn't get lost. It got sent. And things that are sent," Mirabel said, "have senders. And senders," she added, as the lamp guttered and righted itself, throwing both their shadows huge against the wall, "sometimes want to know whether they arrived."

Neither of them slept well. Near dawn, Mirabel rose and stood at the window over the darkened street, the list folded against her breastbone like a second heartbeat, and watched the snow fall on the empty avenue — and told herself the shiver at her neck was only the cold, and did not entirely believe it, because far down the street, beneath the gas lamp at the corner, a man stood without an umbrella in the falling snow, quite still, looking up at their windows as though confirming an address.
