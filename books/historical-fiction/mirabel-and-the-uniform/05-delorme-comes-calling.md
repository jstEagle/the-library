## 5. Delorme Comes Calling

Theo Joubert had carried secrets across three borders and two changes of government without ever once being asked to lie for them. That was the peculiar mercy of his profession: the courier's virtue was not cleverness but silence. He could stand in a minister's anteroom with a satchel chained to his wrist and say nothing at all, and the saying-nothing would be counted as loyalty. Honesty, in Theo's experience, was mostly a matter of never opening your mouth.

He was about to learn that lying required considerably less effort than he had spent twenty-eight years believing.

It began on a Thursday, in the gray hour between the lamplighters' rounds and full dark, when the city was too tired to be either day or night. Mirabel was in the mending room with her needle going like a small patient clock — she worked by touch now more than sight, he'd noticed, her fingers reading seams in the dark — and Colette was meant to be doing arithmetic at the kitchen table but was instead drawing something she kept covering with her forearm whenever he passed. The stove ticked. Somewhere down the Rue Verte a dog was barking at nothing, or at the occupation everyone said was coming and no one said was coming.

The knock came at half past six exactly. Three knocks, evenly spaced, neither timid nor demanding — the knock of a man who had calculated precisely how hard to knock on a stranger's door.

Theo opened it with the chain still on, an instinct so new to their household that the chain itself had gone up only last week, after the letter. The man on the landing was perhaps fifty-five, dressed in a coat of good wool that had been let out once at the shoulders and let back in again, the kind of alteration Theo recognized because his sister made it. Gray at the temples, a face built for pleasantness the way some faces are built for authority — soft around the mouth, quick around the eyes. He held his hat in both hands like a man presenting credentials.

"Monsieur Joubert," he said. Not a question. "Theobald Joubert. I'm sorry to call unannounced."

"You have me at a disadvantage," Theo said, which was true, and which he would remember afterward as the first honest thing he'd said all evening.

"Delorme." The man inclined his head a fraction, a bow compressed into a nod. "Augustin Delorme. Your father and I served together — the Fourth Regiment, before you were born, or very nearly." A smile arrived on his face the way weather arrives, gradually and from several directions at once. "I was passing through the quarter. I thought — well. One hears things. One hears that the old shop is still standing."

"The shop is closed," Theo said. "Since my father died. Eight years now."

"Eight years." Delorme repeated it softly, tasting the number. "Yes. Yes, that would be right. May I come in? It's bitter out here, and I confess I've walked further than my knees approve of. If it's inconvenient—" He spread his hands slightly, already withdrawing, already making it easy to say yes. Theo had watched men do this in anterooms for a decade: the retreat offered in advance so that pursuit feels like hospitality.

He unlatched the chain.

Later, standing at the window of the mending room with his heart going like a trapped bird, Theo would try to reconstruct the moment of decision and find that there wasn't one. No weighing, no warning bell. Just the cold air coming off the man's coat and the word *regimental* hanging in the stairwell like a lamp someone had lit in a room where they kept the dark.

* * *

Mirabel came through from the mending room wiping her hands on her apron, and Theo watched her take the measure of the visitor in the space of two seconds — the good coat, the soft voice, the way his eyes went past her shoulder into the flat and catalogued it: the stove, the table, the girl at the table pretending not to look up, the door of the mending room standing half open behind which God knew what was visible. Their father's daughter did her own arithmetic faster than any customs officer Theo had ever lied to.

"Madame Joubert?" Delorme guessed.

"Sister. Mirabel." She did not offer her hand. "You knew our father?"

"In another life, mademoiselle. We were boys together in the Fourth — your father the finest tailor's son in the battalion and me the worst shot." He laughed at himself, warmly, inviting them into the warmth. "I've been away. Abroad. Business. When I came back I looked up old names, old addresses, and yours was one I remembered. The shop on the Rue Verte. Your father patched my greatcoat twice and refused payment both times, and I have felt indebted for thirty years."

"Then you're owed nothing," Mirabel said pleasantly. "He charged everyone else double."

Delorme laughed — genuinely, Theo thought, or convincingly enough that the difference stopped mattering — and the evening arranged itself around him the way water arranges itself around a dropped stone. He sat where he was put. He admired the mending laid out on the sideboard with the connoisseur's eye of a man who understood cloth. He accepted coffee he plainly didn't want and praised it sincerely. And all the while, beneath the courtesy, Theo could feel the second conversation running like a current under ice: the small pauses, the eyes returning again and again to the mending-room door, the questions that seemed casual and were not.

"And the business?" Delorme asked, somewhere in the second cup of coffee. "Your father's trade. Someone carries it on, I hope?"

"I mend," Mirabel said. "Uniforms, mostly. There's more call for mending than making, these days."

"There always was," Delorme said, "in the years that matter." And then, lightly, turning his cup on its saucer a quarter-turn, the way a man turns a key: "Tell me — did he leave papers? Accounts, patterns, that sort of thing? There may be debts outstanding, you understand. Regimental matters. Old subscriptions. It would be a kindness to settle them before — well." His hand made a small gesture that encompassed the flat, the street, the government dissolving beyond the blacked-out windows like sugar in bad tea. "Before things change hands."

Theo heard himself answer before he had decided anything at all.

"Papers burn fast," he said. "We burned what we couldn't use. Cloth patterns keep better than promises."

The words came out smooth, unhurried, even faintly bored — the voice of a man discussing firewood. Theo listened to his own mouth as though it belonged to a colleague. Twenty-eight years of telling the truth, and the first lie of his life emerged fully formed, wearing his voice like a borrowed coat that fit better than his own.

Mirabel's needle-hand, resting on the arm of her chair, went still. Only that. She picked up her cup and drank, and over its rim her eyes met his for a quarter of a second, and in that quarter-second Theo understood something that rearranged him entirely: his sister was not surprised. She had been lying since the letter came — lying with her silence, with her pleasantness, with the apron and the needle and the whole domestic theater of an ordinary household — and she had been waiting to see whether he would join her or break.

He had joined her. It had taken him one sentence and no effort whatsoever, and that was the part that frightened him. Not the fear of Delorme — fear of Delorme was simple, animal, manageable. What frightened him was how natural the falsehood had felt, how little it had cost, how the truth had simply stepped aside when the moment required, like a servant who knows when to leave the room.

* * *

Colette was sent to bed at nine and went without protest, which meant she was listening from the landing, which meant Theo would deal with that later. Delorme rose at ten, thanked them for their hospitality with a formality that belonged to another century, and stood in the doorway working the hat brim between his fingers.

"One thing more," he said. "Forgive an old soldier's directness. Your father — in the last years — did he ever speak of me?"

"No," Theo said honestly.

Delorme nodded slowly, and something moved behind his pleasantness, some sediment stirred up and settling again. "No. He wouldn't have. He was a careful man." He settled the hat on his head. "Careful men leave careful traces, Monsieur Joubert. I'd take it kindly if you'd let me know if any surface. Debts of honor are the only ones I collect personally." He smiled at them both, warm as a stove. "You have my address. Or you will have — I'll write it down before I go."

He wrote it in the hall, on the back of a card, in a hand that was small and precise and — Theo's courier's eye caught it before he could stop noticing — familiar. Not identical. Familiar. The same schooling, the same era, the way two officers' signatures resemble each other the way two uniforms do.

They closed the door. They waited until the footsteps reached the bottom of the stairs, crossed the courtyard, and faded toward the Rue Verte. Then Mirabel turned to him in the lamplight, and neither of them spoke for a long moment.

"He wrote the card with his left hand," Theo said finally. "But he knocked with his right. And he took the coffee with his right." He pressed his palm against his own sternum, where his heart was still misbehaving. "Mirabel. I lied to him."

"Yes."

"To his face. About Father. About burning things we haven't burned."

"Yes."

"And it was—" He searched for the word and hated every candidate. "Easy. It was easy, and I don't understand why. I've told the truth my whole life like it was a job. Carrying other people's honesty across borders in a locked case, and I never once—"

"Theo." Mirabel touched his sleeve. Her hands smelled of thread wax and coal smoke, the smell of their entire childhood. "You've been lying since the day Father died. You just called it keeping the peace. Tonight you only changed the audience."

He wanted to argue. He stood there wanting to argue, assembling the counterargument piece by piece — that there was a moral architecture to his silences, that omission and fabrication lived in different wings of the same house — and found, examining the structure closely for the first time in years, that the walls were thinner than he had always believed. That the whole edifice rested on the fact that no one had ever knocked.

Someone had knocked tonight. Three times, evenly spaced, by a man who had measured the force in advance.

"We should give him the letter," Theo said quietly. "Give it back, return it, whatever he wants. Whatever it costs. Because he'll come again, Mirabel, and next time he won't bring coffee."

"And if what's inside it can clear Father's name?" she said. "Or damn it worse? You want to hand that to a stranger who lies with his left hand and calls it friendship?"

"He isn't a friend."

"No," Mirabel agreed. "But he might be the only person alive who knows what Father did. Think about that before we decide what he deserves."

She went back to the mending room. Theo stayed at the window a while longer, watching the empty street where a courteous man had recently stood measuring their windows with his eyes. Down the block, a poster-paster was working late, brushing paste along a wall in long strokes, laying paper over brick in the dark — new notices, fresh ones, the ink probably not yet dry. Theo couldn't read them from this distance. He found he didn't need to. The city was writing its lists, and every family on every street was about to learn which column their name fell in, and the only question — the only one that would matter by morning — was who got to hold the pen.

Behind him, from the mending room, came the small patient sound of his sister's needle, sewing something he hadn't seen her cut yet.

He went to see what she was making.
