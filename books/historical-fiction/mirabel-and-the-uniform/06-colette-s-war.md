## 6. Colette's War

Colette had learned early that the world told its secrets to anyone patient enough to seem small. At seven, hiding in the root cellar while soldiers searched her village, she had heard the sergeant tell his corporal exactly which farms still had grain and which families were already dead, and no one had thought to check behind the barrels where a child the size of a sack of turnips was pressed so still she could feel her own heartbeat in her teeth. That was the first lesson. The second came later, in Mirabel's mending room, and it was this: adults talked over children the way they talked over furniture — freely, carelessly, with the comfortable assumption that nothing listening could understand.

She was fifteen now and taller than a sack of anything, but the principle held. When officers brought their uniforms to be mended, they sat in the two chairs by the stove and drank the coffee Mirabel offered because it was warm, and they spoke as though Colette were a curtain. She kept a book open on her lap — *The Lark*, a school prize from before, its spine cracked white from rereading — and she turned pages at intervals whether or not she read them, and behind those pages she gathered everything.

She knew which regiments were being pulled north. She knew that Colonel Vasseur's wife had left for her sister's in the provinces and was not coming back. She knew that the paymasters had stopped paying on time, which meant the end was nearer than the posters admitted. And she knew, because Theo had come home three nights ago with frostbitten hands and a satchel he carried into his room like a sleeping animal, that something had shifted inside the Joubert household itself — something with a weight to it that made Mirabel burn the lamp oil faster and made Theo eat dinner without tasting it.

And then there was Luc.

Luc Perrin worked at the print shop off Rue Verte, the one that had once produced the republic's proclamations and now produced whatever paid, and he was seventeen and serious in a way that Colette found unbearable and irresistible in equal measure. He had a smudge of ink permanently at the base of his left thumb. He walked her home from evening services sometimes, not touching her hand but walking close enough that the backs of their gloves nearly met, and when he spoke about the war he spoke quietly, the way people spoke about weather they couldn't do anything about.

He never asked her for information. That was important. She needed him never to have asked.

What happened instead was smaller and more deniable, the way the worst things always are. He would mention, idly, that the print shop had orders for a new batch of ration notices, or that a certain colonel's name had come through the press twice in one week, and Colette — wanting to be interesting to him, wanting to be the girl who knew things, who mattered in some ledger beyond her age — would let slip what she'd heard under the eaves of the mending room. A regiment moved. A minister resigned. The docks busier than they should be. Little pieces. She told herself each one was harmless, each one was nothing, each one was only conversation between two people who trusted each other completely, which was itself a kind of intelligence, wasn't it? Knowing whom you could trust was worth more than any troop movement.

She did not think of herself as passing information. She thought of herself as talking to Luc.

* * *

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, and Colette knew within the hour that it was wrong, because Mirabel held it the way you hold a bird with a broken wing — carefully, and with your heart going too fast.

Colette was pinning a hem when the knock came, and she watched from the doorway as the postman handed over an envelope heavier than paper should be, sealed with wax, addressed in a hand that slanted hard to the right. She saw Mirabel go still. She saw Theo come down the stairs two at a time and take it from his sister without asking, and she saw him read the address twice.

"Aubry," he said. "It's addressed to Minister Aubry."

"He's dead," Mirabel said. "Everyone knows he's dead."

"It's addressed to this house." Theo turned the envelope over. "To this address."

That was all Colette got, standing in the doorway with pins in her mouth like a saint in a bad painting. But that evening she got more, because the officers came, and the officers talked.

"They're saying Aubry didn't just die," said the thin one, Captain Roche, whose uniforms always came in with sweat-stains at the collar because he was more frightened than he let himself appear. "They're saying there are papers. Lists. Names of men who helped the other side before the war even started, back when helping looked like diplomacy."

"Roche." The older officer, Major Brennand, said it the way you'd say *enough* to a dog.

"I'm only saying what's said."

"Well, don't say it here."

But of course he had already said it here, and Colette sat behind her book with her pulse tapping at her throat, thinking of the envelope on the shelf above Mirabel's workbasket, wrapped now in brown paper, tied with string, waiting for Theo to decide what honest men decided about things that arrived by mistake. Thinking of her father's name — no. Not her father. Her father was ash outside Vireloup with four hundred others; her father had been dead five years and could not be on any list. But the Jouberts' father could be. Papa Joubert, who had taught her to thread a needle when her hands were too small, who had called her *la petite couture*, little seam, because she fit into the family the way a stitch fits into cloth. His name might be on a list of traitors, sealed in brown paper ten feet from where she sat pretending to read.

She thought: I will say none of this to Luc.

She thought it very firmly, the way you press a thumb over a tear in cloth before it runs.

* * *

She lasted six days.

It wasn't weakness, exactly. Or it was weakness, but the ordinary kind, the kind that lives in every human seam. It was Thursday evening, cold enough that the river fog had come up the streets and turned the lamps into halos, and Luc walked her home along the quay and stopped at the bridge — their bridge, the one where the swans used to be before someone ate them — and he looked tired in a way that went past his eyes into his shoulders. The print shop had laid off two men. His mother was coughing again. The city felt like a room where the air was running out.

"Sometimes I think we'll wake up one morning and the country will just be gone," he said. "Not conquered. Just — dissolved. Like sugar. You'll look for it and there won't be anything to stir."

"There'll be us," Colette said. "We'll still be here. Somebody has to be the sugar after it dissolves."

He laughed — a real laugh, brief and surprised out of him — and he looked at her then with such attention that she felt lit from inside, and that light is how they get you, she would think later, that exact warmth, because a girl who wants to keep a boy looking at her like that will trade almost anything to hold it.

"Theo's courier work," Luc said casually, watching the black water. "Is it true he still runs the northern route?"

"Why?"

"No reason. Someone at the shop mentioned it."

And here was the moment, and she knew even as it opened under her feet that it was a moment, the way you know ice is going to crack half a second before it does. She could have said *I don't ask Theo about his work*. She could have said nothing at all. Six days she had held the letter behind her teeth like a stone, and she could have held it forever, and instead she heard herself say:

"There's more than courier work now. There was a letter. For a dead man — Minister Aubry — delivered here by mistake, and it hasn't been returned, and Mirabel and Theo have gone strange about it. They whisper. Theo carries it when he goes out, I've seen the shape of it under his coat." She paused, and then, because the warmth of his attention was still on her and she wanted to give him something worthy of it: "I heard an officer say there are lists. Names. Collaborators, from before the war."

The word dropped into the dark water below them and sank without a sound.

Luc was quiet for a long moment. Too long. When he spoke again his voice had changed — not much, a half-tone, the way a lock changes when it turns. "A list of names," he repeated. "You're sure that's what he said."

"That's what he said."

"And the letter's still in your house."

"Luc—"

"I'm just asking." He smiled at her, and the smile was right, everything was right, except that he put his hands in his pockets while he did it, and he had never done that before. "Forget I asked. Forget all of it. You shouldn't repeat things like that anyway, Colette. It could be dangerous — for your family, I mean. People talk. People write things down."

"People like printers," she said, and meant it lightly, and watched it land somewhere else entirely.

He kissed her cheek at her door, cold lips, ink-smudged hand briefly at her jaw, and said he'd see her Sunday. She stood in the entryway long after he'd gone, listening to the fog, feeling the shape of the conversation rearrange itself behind her like furniture moving in an empty room. Something had passed between them tonight that she had not agreed to pass. Something had been taken from her mouth and become a thing with its own weight and destination, and she stood there holding her cold hands together and understood, with the terrible clarity that arrives always a moment after usefulness has ended, what she had done.

She hadn't given Luc a confidence. She had given him a map.

* * *

She tried to fix it for three days.

On Friday she composed, in her head, seventeen versions of a warning to Mirabel — *someone may ask about the letter, someone at the print shop knows, I said too much* — and discarded every version, because each one began with the confession that she listened, that she remembered, that for months she had been carrying the mending room's secrets out through the streets like water in her cupped hands. To warn them she would have to show them what she was. And what if they looked at her afterward — Mirabel, who had braided her hair during the fever winter; Theo, who had carried her on his shoulders through the evacuation crowds of Vireloup when she was ten — the way people look at a thing that has been in the house all along and only now shows its teeth?

So she said nothing, and told herself that saying nothing was protection, that the letter would leave the house soon anyway, that Luc loved her and would do nothing with a map drawn by her own hand.

On Saturday night she lay awake doing arithmetic of guilt: one tidbit, times one boy, times one print shop, equals what? She thought of the officers in the mending room, of Captain Roche's frightened collar. She thought of the word *lists* spoken aloud above her head as though she were upholstery. She had spent years making herself furniture, and now the furniture was burning the house down.

On Sunday, Luc did not come to evening service.

She waited by the church steps until the bells finished and the crowd thinned and the verger began stacking chairs, and he did not come, and his absence had a texture to it — deliberate, folded, put away. Walking home she passed the print shop. The shutters were up but lamplight showed through the seams, and through the gap at the side of the shutter she saw movement, several bodies bent over something flat on the counter, and she saw — she was almost sure, in one flicker of lamplight through the gap — the corner of a sheet covered in handwriting, and beside it another sheet, and a third, being compared.

Lists. Being copied. Being checked against each other.

Her stomach turned over slowly, like a boat coming about. Because she knew how printing worked, she had grown up around the smell of ink, and she knew that a list in a print shop was not a list anymore — it was a list multiplied by however many copies the press could make in a night. Whatever she had said on the bridge had gone into type.

She ran the last three streets home.

* * *

The house was warm. Mirabel was at her frame, sewing buttonholes into an officer's greatcoat, the needle flashing in the lamplight, steady as a clock. Theo sat reading across from her, the brown-papered letter nowhere visible, and the whole scene was so ordinary, so lamplit and safe, that Colette's confession rose in her throat all at once and jammed there.

Because what would she even say? *I told a boy about the letter.* It sounded small. It sounded like nothing. And underneath the smallness lay the larger crime she could not name without unraveling everything: *I have been listening to you for years. Nothing in this house is private from me. I am not the child you think is sleeping upstairs.*

"Colette?" Mirabel glanced up. "You look like you've seen the river flood."

"I'm fine." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Fear, it turned out, gave you excellent diction. "Just cold. Is there soup?"

Theo looked at her a moment longer than usual — Theo missed nothing, that was the family disease, they all missed nothing and said less — and then went back to his page. Mirabel held out the coat she was mending. "Here, learn something. Buttonholes. Even tension, always."

Colette took the coat and the needle and sat, and her hands did the work while her mind ran ahead into the dark. Tomorrow, or the day after, someone would come to this door. She didn't know who — Luc's people, whoever Luc's people answered to, the new regime's men, the old regime's men, men with courteous faces and questions about papers — but someone would come, following the thread she had spun out across the city with her own foolish, loving mouth. And when they knocked, they would not look at her. They would look at Mirabel, at Theo, at the tall girl by the window as though she were a curtain, and they would speak over her head about matters too large for her, and they would leave, satisfied that nothing in this household could possibly understand them.

She pulled the thread tight. Even tension, always. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows, and somewhere down toward the river a ship's horn sounded once, low and long, like something clearing its throat before it spoke.

Three days later, a stranger called at the house — a courteous man, well-dressed, who introduced himself to Theo as Delorme, an old friend of their father's regiment, and asked, smiling, whether the family had any papers their father might have left behind.
