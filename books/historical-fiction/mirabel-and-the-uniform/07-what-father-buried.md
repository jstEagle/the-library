## 7. What Father Buried

The shop on Rue Verte had been locked for nine years, but the key still fit, because locks in this city were like promises — they rusted before they broke.

Mirabel stood in the doorway with the key warm in her fist and let the cold air of the place move over her. Dust hung in the light from the high window in soft grey columns, undisturbed, as if the room had been holding its breath since the day her father died. The cutting table sat where he had left it, chalk stubs aligned along its edge like a row of small white teeth. His shears lay open on a folded length of grey wool, waiting for a hand that had stopped coming. On the wall behind the counter hung the mirror with the crack running through it corner to corner, and Mirabel looked at herself in it the way one looks at a face in water — present, distorted, already half gone.

She had told Theo she was going to check on the property. She had told Colette nothing at all. Both statements were true in the way that mattered and false in every way that counted.

The letter to Minister Aubry was hidden now beneath the floorboards under Colette's bed, wrapped in oilcloth, and every hour that passed with it there made the house feel heavier. Two nights ago she had read the list by candlelight until the names swam: fourteen men, their father's name circled in ink that had been wet when he died, and below them, twice, in a stranger's hand, *Delorme*. Theo wanted to burn it. Mirabel wanted — she did not yet know what she wanted. But she knew what her hands wanted, which was different. Her hands wanted thread. Her hands wanted work. And so she had come here, to her father's shop, to be near the tools that had made him legible to himself, and to search — though she would not have admitted to searching — for whatever else he might have left behind.

Because a man who kept a second copy of a list somewhere did not keep only one secret. Her father had been a tailor. Tailors did not leave loose threads. Tailors left *hems*.

She began at the beginning, because he had taught her to always begin at the beginning: the fabric bolts against the far wall. She pulled each one forward and ran her fingers into the hollows behind them. Nothing but dust and the ghost of camphor. Then the drawers of the counter — buttons sorted by size, bone and horn and one small tin of black glass ones he'd saved from a coat belonging to a general whose name Mirabel had never learned. She lifted out the tray liners one by one and pressed her palms flat against the wood beneath. Solid. She checked the legs of the fitting stools, unscrewing the brass ferrules; she tipped back the three mirrors and looked for paper pasted to their backs. She went through his chalk box, his pin cushions, the lining of his own coat, still hanging on its hook by the door, and found only a theatre ticket from the winter before he died and a twist of peppermints gone to sugar.

An hour passed. The light through the high window shifted from white to the color of weak tea. Mirabel's knees ached from kneeling on the cold boards, and she understood, with a patience that felt almost like grief, that she was doing what she had always done: circling the wound, tending everything around it, unwilling to press directly on the center.

So she pressed.

Her father's fitting room was a small alcove behind a curtain at the back of the shop, where customers had stood on a low dais while he moved around them with pins held in his lips like a saint's halo. The dais was built of planks. Mirabel knelt and put her ear to the wood, then rapped it, plank by plank, listening for the difference between solid and hollow. The third plank answered differently. Not hollow, exactly — but looser, with a small dry knock underneath, like a knuckle on a door.

There were no nails. The plank lifted on two brass finger-holes bored into its underside, cut so cleanly they were invisible unless you knew to look, and beneath it was a cavity lined with cedar, and in the cavity, wrapped in the same grey wool that still lay on his cutting table upstairs in the world of the living — no. Wrapped in the same grey wool, folded with the precision of a man who had spent fifty years making cloth lie flat — were two things.

A document, folded in quarters. And an envelope with her name on it.

*Mirabel.*

Just her name. Not Theo's. Not the family's. Hers alone, in the handwriting she knew better than any handwriting in the world, the handwriting that had written her measurements in the margins of account books, that had labeled her first sewing box when she was six years old: *M. JOUBERT — NEEDLES ONLY.*

She did not open it immediately. She sat down on the edge of the dais where a thousand men had stood to be measured, holding the envelope in both hands as if it were something hot, and the dust settled around her, and somewhere outside a tram bell rang and did not stop ringing, or perhaps she simply did not hear it stop.

When she finally broke the seal — wax, unmarked, the plain wax of a man posting nothing — the paper inside was thin, covered front and back, written quickly but without correction, as though he had composed it in his head for years and only needed the one sitting to get it down.

*My Mirabel,*

*If you are reading this, then someone has come asking after me, and I am past answering for myself. I am sorry for that. A man should answer for himself. But I find I cannot write this aloud to you while my heart still beats, and so I have done what tailors do, which is hide the seam where no one will think to look.*

*You have found the list. I know you have, because you are reading this, and I know you, my girl — you would not have come digging unless the list was already in your house. I will not insult you by pretending otherwise.*

*Then let me tell you the truth of it, plainly, the way I never could at dinner.*

*In the year of the border war — you were four years old; Theo was nine; your mother was alive — I informed. There. It is said. I gave names to the military police. Eleven names. Men I had fitted for coats, men who ate at my table, one of them your uncle Armand's closest friend. They were organizing against the government of that time, smuggling arms through the shop district, and a man came to me — a courteous man, very polite, the sort who apologizes before he ruins you — and he showed me what would happen to my family if I did not help. He did not threaten loudly. He showed me a photograph of our street. He asked after my children by name. That was all. It was enough.*

*I informed, and the eleven were taken, and some of them died, and I lived with it by telling myself what all informants tell themselves: that I saved three lives by spending eleven. Your life. Theo's. Your mother's, who was ill and would not have survived what came next. I do not ask you to forgive it. Forgiveness is not yours to give; the dead hold that debt. I ask only that you understand it, because understanding is the only inheritance I have that will not rot.*

*Here is what I need you to know, and I need you to know it the way you know a stitch — in your fingers, not your head: everyone on that list, including me, including the man Delorme whose name you are wondering about, was once shown a photograph of a street and asked after his children by name. That is how it works. That is how it has always worked. No man wakes up wanting to be a collaborator. He wakes up a father, and someone makes arithmetic of his love, and he does the sums.*

*I am not excusing myself. I am instructing you. If the day comes — and the way the city sounds lately, I fear it may — when someone puts a list in front of you with names on it, remember that the list is not the truth. The list is a weapon shaped like the truth. What is true is the street, and the children, and the photograph, and the fear. Everything else is bookkeeping.*

*Mend well, my girl. Mend what can be mended. Burn what cannot. And when they come asking — and they will come asking — remember that a good seam shows no stitches.*

*Your father,*
*A. Joubert*

Mirabel read it twice. The second time she read it aloud, quietly, just to hear his voice again, and the sound of her own voice speaking his words in the empty shop made her feel less alone than anything had since he died.

Then she unfolded the document.

It was the list. Or rather — it was the list's elder sibling. Same fourteen names, same careful columns, but dated seventeen years earlier, in the border war, with eleven other names struck through in red ink. Eleven struck through. Eleven dead, or taken, or vanished — the arithmetic of her father's confession laid out in his own neat tailor's hand, the numbers balanced like a ledger. At the bottom, in fresher ink: the newer fourteen. And among them, twice, *Delorme* — once in the old column, unstruck, marked with a single word in the margin: *son?* — and once in the new column.

So Delorme had been there too. Seventeen years ago. Shown the photograph, asked after his children. And he had not informed — or he had, or the question mark meant her father never learned which. The question mark was the most frightening thing on the page. It was her father admitting he did not know everything, and her father had been a man who measured everything twice.

Mirabel sat for a long time on the fitting dais with the papers in her lap, and the grief that came up in her was not the grief she expected. She had braced for shame — hers, inherited, secondhand. Instead what rose in her was something closer to fury at the world itself, at the machinery that had made her father choose, that was even now, in a hundred rooms across the city, putting photographs of streets in front of fathers and asking after children by name. The letter did not excuse him. It armed her. It told her exactly what the list in her floorboard was: not truth. A weapon shaped like truth. And whoever held it — Delorme, the ministry, the coming regime — intended to swing it.

She thought of Theo, honest Theo, who carried codes for a country that existed nowhere anymore, and who wanted to burn the letter because burning felt like protection. He was wrong and right at once, and she loved him for it. Names only had power on paper — yes. But her father had left her something stronger than paper: he had left her the pattern. Once you saw how the weapon was forged, you could judge the hand that swung it. And Delorme's hand, when it came — and it would come; the question mark practically guaranteed it — would be a son's hand, or a father's, and it would apologize before it ruined them.

She refolded everything along her father's creases. She took the letter and the old list and pressed them to her chest for one long moment, the way she pressed finished garments before delivering them, as if to fix their shape permanently into memory.

Then, deliberately, she did something her father would have understood better than any priest: she did not hide the papers again. She memorized instead. She read the old list until every struck name and every living one sat in her mind the way her customers' measurements sat there — permanent, unspoken, available. Eleven dead names. Fourteen living. One question mark. Then she folded the documents small, tucked them into her bodice against her heart, and closed the plank over the cedar-lined dark.

Outside, the tea-colored light had gone to grey. As she turned the key in the shop door, she noticed what she had failed to notice on arrival: a chalk mark on the doorframe, small, at shoulder height. An X, drawn fresh, the chalk still bright against nine years of weathering. Someone had marked the shop recently. Someone was counting doors on Rue Verte, and hers — her father's — had been counted.

Mirabel walked home fast, head down, hand flat over her chest where the papers rode. By the time she reached her own street the lamps were being lit early, though it was barely dusk, and there was a figure standing across from her building in the deepening blue, hatless, patient, looking up at her windows as if measuring them.

He turned as she approached. Courteous. Very polite. The sort of man who apologized before he ruined you.

"Madame Joubert," Delorme said gently. "I hoped we might speak about your father."
