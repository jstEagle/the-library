## 8. The Evacuation Lists

The announcement came the way all the republic's final decisions had come that winter: printed on cheap paper, pasted up at dawn, and read aloud by strangers in voices that made no attempt to hide what they meant.

Theo heard it first from a lamplighter on the Rue Verte who had stopped working entirely and now spent his mornings going door to door with news instead of flame. *The Ministry of Marine announces the scheduled departure of vessels for the southern territories. Priority berths will be assigned according to civil registry. Applications close in nine days.* The man recited it like scripture, and like scripture it admitted no questions.

By noon the same notice was on every wall in the quarter, and by evening it was in every mouth. Theo stood outside the bakery — bread now being flour-and-water and a rumor — and listened to the arithmetic begin. It began everywhere that day and did not stop for weeks. A family of five with one registered name among them. An old teacher whose service record predated three governments. The widow Aubry, someone said, gone already, or not gone, depending on who you asked. Everyone was doing sums. That was what the announcement had actually published: not ships, not schedules, but permission to count what could not be divided.

Theo walked home the long way, because the short way passed the harbor road and he was not yet ready to look at the water. He carried his courier's satchel against his hip out of habit, though its contents were ash and ceremony now — the government he served existed in two rooms above a shuttered ministry, answering letters from itself. But the satchel still opened doors. That was the terrible joke of his profession: credentials outlived the things they credentialed, the way a key outlives the house it locked.

At home, Mirabel had already heard. She sat at the mending table with an officer's coat across her knees and her needle stopped mid-stitch, which Theo had learned to read as a barometer reading of the soul. Colette was at the window with the notice she'd torn off the post office wall, smoothing it flat against the glass as if pressure might change its terms.

"One," Colette said, without turning around. "It says priority consideration for couriers of the diplomatic service. One berth."

"Where does it say one?"

"It doesn't have to." She turned. Fifteen years old and she had the republic's whole talent already: knowing exactly how bad a thing was before anyone official confirmed it. "One is implied."

* *

Dinner that night was barley soup and the word *one*.

Theo tried to keep it off the table. He talked about the price of lamp oil, about the neighbor's dog, about anything with no numbers in it. But the word sat there anyway, between the salt dish and the candle, patient as a guest who knew he'd been invited and would not be rushed. One berth. Three of them. The mathematics did not require a diplomat; they required only a family, and families were required to do this sum whether or not any of them could bear the answer.

Mirabel refilled his bowl without asking. "The officer whose coat this is," she said, apropos of nothing, "has a wife in the south already. He told me he means to send the coat ahead of himself, as if wool could swim."

"That's not an answer to anything."

"It wasn't a question."

Colette put down her spoon. She had been winding herself up to something since the soup went cold, Theo could see it, the way you can see weather building behind a hill. "You should take it," she said. "The berth. You've earned it. You carried their codes through two winters and they never even gave you a coat that fit."

"The coat fits."

"The coat is a tent with buttons." She pushed back from the table. "And I'm not saying take me. I'm saying take *someone*, and it should be you, because you're the one who—"

"I'm the one who what?" Theo asked, more sharply than he intended. "Carries things? Leaves things? I'm very good at leaving, apparently. It's my entire professional history."

The silence after that had edges. Mirabel looked at him over the candle flame, and he saw that she had understood the sentence he'd just said better than he had — that he was not talking about couriers at all, that some part of him had already begun rehearsing the departure, and hated the rehearsal, and could not stop performing it.

"You haven't decided anything," Mirabel said quietly. "None of us has. There are nine days."

"There are nine days," Theo agreed, and the word *one* sat down again between them and ate nothing and stayed until the candle burned out.

* *

He could not sleep, so he did what he always did with sleeplessness: he worked. He took out the courier's ledger — the old one, the real one, kept in cipher in a false bottom of the satchel — and reviewed his assets the way a merchant reviews stock.

Here is what Theo Joubert owned, at thirty-one years, in the ninth winter of the republic: a service commendation signed by a minister now dead. A standing pass to the harbor authority, still valid, still honored by clerks too frightened to check whether the issuing government existed. The gratitude, genuine but unspendable, of four separate officials whose dispatches he had carried through the border war without losing so much as an envelope corner. And a favor owed to him by Commandant Ferrand of the port police, incurred three years ago when Theo had carried, personally and unlogged, a letter that saved Ferrand's brother from a court-martial.

Ferrand controlled berth assignments at the north quay. Not all of them — the top berths went to names on lists Theo would never see — but the margin. The overflow. The seats between the seats where favor lived, because favor lived nowhere else anymore; the republic had sold everything else and this was what remained in the till.

One call to Ferrand. One favor spent. One seat secured — not merely applied for, but guaranteed, nailed down, certain in a season when nothing else was. Theo sat in the dark of his narrow room and held the certainty in both hands and found that it weighed exactly as much as the other thing, the unsaid thing, the arithmetic.

Because here was what the ledger could not record: whatever he bought with that favor, he could buy only once. And whatever he chose to buy it with — his own escape, Mirabel's, Colette's — the choosing was the spending, and the other two names would remain in the ledger of the left-behind forever, in his handwriting, in effect, however much he told himself afterward that the lists had done it, the regime had done it, the century had done it.

He thought about his father. This was new; he had spent seven years thinking about his father only in the shape of absences, the empty chair, the shop with its shutters down. But lately the letter had changed all that, the letter with the list, the circled name. His father had also faced a table with fewer seats than people. His father had solved it by informing — by trading other families' safety for his own, once, long ago, and carrying it silently for twenty years until it killed him in installments. Theo had sworn, in the raw weeks after the funeral, never to understand that choice. Now he caught himself understanding it with horrible fluency, the way you catch yourself understanding a language you despise.

He got up and stood at the window. Across the yard, Mirabel's lamp was burning too. Of course it was. Neither of them slept these days; they simply lay in parallel insomnia, separated by one wall, each pretending not to hear the other turn over.

* *

In the morning the queue outside the harbor authority stretched six streets, and Theo did not join it. He joined it the following morning instead, early, before light, wearing his courier's coat with the hidden pocket Mirabel had sewn into the lining — the pocket that had once been meant for codes and now held nothing but a folded application form and his own heartbeat.

Ferrand received him in a room that smelled of wet wool and pencil shavings. The commandant had aged badly in three years; the war had taken his brother anyway, later, at sea, which struck Theo as the kind of cruelty the universe practiced when it wanted to remind everyone who kept the real ledgers.

"Joubert." Ferrand didn't offer a chair. "I wondered when you'd come."

"I've come about a berth."

"I know what you've come about. Everyone who walks through that door has come about a berth. Half of them cry." He slid a form across the desk. "Courier status carries priority consideration. You'll be processed with the second group. North quay, twelve days."

"Ferrand." Theo kept his voice level. "I'm not asking for consideration."

"No," Ferrand said. "You're asking for the favor."

The pencil shavings smell seemed suddenly very loud. Theo laid his hand flat on the desk, the way one does when steadying oneself or preparing to push. "One berth. Guaranteed. Not considered. Guaranteed."

"And I give it to you, and you go home to your sister and your ward, and then what happens in your house?" Ferrand's voice was not cruel. That was the worst of it; it was tired, and precise, and kind in the way a surgeon is kind. "I've watched this arithmetic for three weeks, Joubert. Every family in this city is doing it. The ones who can't solve it stop eating. The ones who solve it stop sleeping. Which one are you planning to be?"

"I haven't chosen who goes."

"Then you're not ready to spend the favor. Come back when you are. The favor keeps." He almost smiled. "That's the one advantage favors have over people. They don't starve while you decide."

Theo walked home through streets hung with evacuation notices, each one a little more explicit than the last, the bureaucracy shedding its politeness layer by layer as the deadline approached. *Priority consideration for minors with legal claims.* *Dependents of serving officers.* *Persons with verified southern residence.* Each line was a door closing somewhere, and he could hear them, faintly, all the way down the boulevard.

* *

That evening Mirabel met him at the door before he was fully inside, and he knew from her face that she had spent the day doing exactly what he had done: inventorying, weighing, rehearsing speeches.

"You went to see Ferrand."

"How—"

"His clerk's wife brings me uniforms. She tells me everything. She told me you asked for a guarantee and left without one." Mirabel untied her apron, folded it, set it down — all her rituals of composure performed at double speed, which meant none of them were working. "So we're going to settle this tonight. All three of us. At the table."

"He said the favor keeps—"

"I don't care what Ferrand said." Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, a hairline fracture in good porcelain. "Nine days ago there was no ship. Six days ago there was no berth. Now there is a berth and a man who owes you, and you're telling me we should wait for a better moment to spend the only certain thing we own. Theo. When has a better moment ever come to this family?"

From the kitchen doorway, Colette stood holding a loaf of the flour-and-water bread like an offering, like a prop in a scene she had not auditioned for but understood completely. Her eyes went from Theo to Mirabel and back, and Theo saw her do the sum again — the one she did at every meal now, the one that ended, always, at *one*.

"We'll talk at dinner," Theo said.

They talked at dinner. Or rather: they performed three versions of generosity at each other across the barley soup, each insisting the others must go, each refusing with increasing heat, until Colette slammed her spoon down and said, "You're both lying. You both want to give it away and you both want to stay, and nobody will say the true thing, which is that one of us gets on that ship and two of us watch it leave, and whichever way you arrange it, it's the same grief wearing different coats."

The candle flame bent in the draft from the window. Nobody corrected her grammar of it. She was fifteen and she had stated the family's condition more accurately than either of them had managed in nine days.

"The true thing," Mirabel said finally, very quietly, "is that I am not getting on that ship. Whatever you two decide. I need you to hear that now, so you can stop building plans that include me."

"You don't get to decide alone—"

"I decide alone," she said, "the way Father decided alone. Except I intend to decide better."

The name landed on the table like dropped cutlery. Their father, the shop, the list, the circled name — it all came with it, uninvited, and sat down beside the word *one*, and now there were two guests and neither had brought anything except weight.

Theo pushed back his chair. He needed air, or distance, or simply a wall between himself and the sum. At the door he turned, and what he meant to say was something reasonable, something about nine days, about options, about keeping the favor unspent until the last responsible hour.

What he said was: "There's a man coming tomorrow. Delorme. He's been asking after Father's papers."

Both women went still. Even the draft seemed to hold its breath.

"He came to the office twice," Theo went on. "Polite as a priest. Regimental friend of Father's, he says. He wants 'the papers' — and Theo, I lied to him. I looked him in the face and lied, and it was easy, and I've been trying to work out since Tuesday whether that makes me Father's son or someone else's."

Mirabel rose slowly from the table. In the candlelight her face had gone to the place she went when she was sewing the hardest seams — total attention, total quiet.

"Delorme," she repeated. "The name from the list. The one that appeared twice."

"Yes."

"Then tomorrow," Mirabel said, "he isn't calling on you. He's calling on all of us. And we had better decide, tonight, what story this family tells when the knock comes — because Theo, I promise you, the knock is coming."
