## 9. Delorme's Offer

He came at the hour when the light in the mending room turned from gold to pewter, that thin hour when Mirabel always felt the day slipping through her fingers like thread too fine to knot. She heard his footsteps on the stairs before she saw him — unhurried, even, the steps of a man who had never once needed to hurry — and by the time he filled the doorway of the shop on Rue Verte, she had already set down her needle and folded her hands in her lap, because some part of her had known since the first visit that this moment would come.

"Madame Joubert." He removed his hat. The courtesy of it was worse than a threat would have been. "You'll forgive the lateness of my call."

"The shop is closed," she said.

"I know. I counted on it."

Behind him, through the window, the street was emptying itself the way streets did now — not with panic but with that terrible tidiness, shutters drawn one by one, lamplighters skipping their rounds because the city had stopped believing in tomorrow's darkness or had simply stopped paying for it. Somewhere two blocks over a poster truck idled, its engine coughing. Mirabel thought of paste and paper and names going up on walls while decent people slept, and kept her face as smooth as a pressed seam.

Theo was upstairs. Colette was upstairs. The letter was where it had lived for eleven days now: sewn inside the lining of the old biscuit tin beneath the loose floorboard under the stove, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, guarded less by locks than by the sheer accumulated dullness of a seamstress's household. She had come to think of it as a splinter lodged near the heart — too deep to dig out without blood, too present ever to forget.

"You said last time you were an old regimental friend of my father's," she said. "I wrote to the regimental archive. There is no Delorme listed for his company."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Something almost like relief, the way a man might be relieved when a mask is finally pulled off him by someone else's hand rather than his own.

"No," he agreed. "There wouldn't be. May I sit?"

She gestured to the stool by the counter, the one customers used when they waited for fittings. He sat the way her father used to sit, knees together, hat on his thigh, and that small echo made her throat tighten with a hatred that had nowhere proper to go.

"My father's name was Émile Delorme," he said. "Perhaps you've heard it. Perhaps you haven't. He was on the commission — the one your father testified before, twenty-three years ago. The winter commission."

The stove ticked. Outside, the poster truck coughed again and went silent, as if it too were listening.

"You know what I'm talking about," Delorme said quietly.

"I know my father is dead," Mirabel said. "I know he's been dead nine years, and that dead men make convenient vessels for other people's guilt. Whatever was done in that room was done by men who are mostly dead now themselves, and I mend uniforms, monsieur. I don't keep accounts."

"That's precisely what makes you useful," he said, "and precisely what makes you dangerous. But let me stop pretending, since we've agreed there's no more use in it." He leaned forward slightly, elbows on his knees, and for the first time she saw how tired he was — not the theatrical weariness of a man performing sincerity, but the gray, ground-down tiredness she saw every day in officers' collars, in mothers' wrists, in the mirror. "Your father informed on mine. One name among forty, given to save his own family. My father was arrested in March of that year. He died at the border prison in August. My mother followed him within two years — grief, they called it; hunger, I call it — and I grew up in an aunt's house learning to say *my parents are abroad* the way other children learn their prayers."

Mirabel said nothing. What was there to say? The story was a key turning inside her, opening rooms in her father's old tailor shop she had only begun, these eleven days, to explore.

"And now the republic is dying," Delorme went on, "and everything buried is coming up like frost-heaved stones. The new regime has its lists. Your father's name surfaced — you may have seen what goes up on walls at night. Mine will surface too, eventually, because my father's file survived and files survive better than men do. So here is what I have come to propose, Madame Joubert, plainly, with no more regimental fictions between us."

He reached into his coat and laid three documents on the counter between them, squaring them with two fingers the way Theo squared his dispatches, and the resemblance of gesture — strangers, enemies, both men of papers — struck her like a needle slipped under a fingernail.

"Two berths on the evacuation ship *Lysandre*, sailing Thursday noon. Not steerage. Cabin berths, which means food, which means you arrive alive. And this—" he tapped the second document, "—is an administrative clearance. A finding that the late Aurélien Joubert acted under duress, testimony coerced, record expunged. It's already drafted. It requires only my signature and one countersignature I can obtain. When it's filed, your father's name comes off every list that exists or will exist. No posters. No mobs outside your shop. No children reading your name off a wall and learning to spit it."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, the letter. The whole of it — the enclosure, the list, everything. Unredacted. I know what it contains; I don't need you to tell me. I need to hold it, so that no one else ever does. Names only have power on paper, Madame. Give me the paper and I will give your family a future, and take my father's ghost home to be buried properly, with a name and a date and a grave my mother can stop waiting beside."

*Two berths.* The arithmetic arrived in her chest before her mind could refuse it. Two. Herself and Theo. Or herself and Colette. Or Theo and Colette, and Mirabel stayed — and God forgive her, some cold clerk in the back office of her soul had already begun sorting the combinations, weighing them, laying them side by side like fabric samples. That was the obscenity of offers like this one, she understood suddenly: not the price asked but the arithmetic they forced upon you, the way they turned love into bookkeeping. She had spent eleven days refusing to count, and this man had walked into her shop and counted for her.

"And the third of us?" she said, and hated how carefully casual her voice came out.

Delorme looked at her, and did not pretend to misunderstand. "There are only two," he said. "I'm sorry. If I could buy four, I would buy four. I am not a monster, whatever you've decided I am. I'm a man with a limited number of favors left in a collapsing state, spending all of them at once."

"And after Thursday? After the ship sails and the clearance is filed — what guarantee do we have that either document means anything?"

"None," he said simply. "You have my word, which is worth exactly what you think it's worth, and the observation that a man who cheats you has cheated himself, because the clearance protects him too. We are bound together by this, whether either of us likes it. That is the only kind of guarantee that exists anymore."

Through the ceiling came the faint creak of floorboards — Theo pacing, his evening habit, three steps and turn, three steps and turn. Mirabel pictured him above them, walking out his anxiety over the letter, unaware that the man who wanted it sat below like a patient spider. She should call down to him. She should wake Colette from her homework and put all three of them between this offer and themselves, let the decision be shared the way all their decisions had been shared since their mother died — evenly, painfully, together.

Instead she heard herself say: "Why come to me? Why not to my brother? He's the courier. Papers are his trade."

"Because couriers are trained to see documents and not people," Delorme said. "And because I watched him lie to me last week, and a man who lies that badly about a letter will burn it out of fear, and then both our fathers stay ghosts forever. You—" he hesitated, and the hesitation was genuine, she could hear it, "—you mended my glove. First visit. I'd torn the seam along the thumb and you noticed before I did, and you fixed it while we talked, and handed it back without comment. I've thought about that for days. A woman like that doesn't destroy things. She keeps things. She keeps things *well*."

Mirabel looked down at her hands. They lay in her lap, needle-calloused, ink-stained at the right index finger from marking cloth, steady. Her father's hands, everyone had always said. Her father's hands, which had held a pen in a cold room twenty-three years ago and written a name — *Émile Delorme* — that ended a life.

*He informed to save the family.* She still didn't know that for certain; she knew only what the letter's existence implied, what Theo had pieced together, what her sleepless nights had assembled. But sitting across from this man, hearing his mother die twice — once of grief, once of hunger — she found she no longer needed the confirmation. Some truths you could read the way she read cloth: by grain and weight, by how it fell.

And the recognition rose in her then, unwanted as nausea, unwelcome as pity: *he is doing for his father what I would do for mine.* If the positions were reversed — if it had been her father in the border prison, her mother starving, and a single folder of papers standing between her family's honor and its ruin — she would have crossed any city, told any lie, worn any friendly face. She would have sat on this very stool with her hat on her knee and offered anything she owned.

That was the trap of it. Not the berths, not the clearance. The trap was that she could not hate him cleanly, and a hatred you can't hate cleanly is a wound that won't close.

"You look at me like something you recognize," Delorme said softly. "I know. I looked at your brother the same way, and he didn't notice. You notice everything. It must be exhausting."

"It is," Mirabel said.

She rose, went to the window, and stood with her back to him, watching the street. The poster truck had moved on. A woman hurried past the shopfront with a bundle of firewood, head down, and Mirabel recognized her — Madame Ferrand from the corner house, whose son's coat she had mended free last month, whose gratitude had been warm and real — and watched her pass the lit window of the Joubert shop without looking at it, quickening her step, and understood that word had already traveled. Neighbors knew someone was asking questions at the seamstress's door. In a dying city, that was enough to make you invisible to the people who owed you kindness.

"How long do I have to answer?" she asked the glass.

"Dawn Thursday. The ship sails at noon regardless; the berths must be named by dawn." He stood, settled his coat, put on his hat. In the window's reflection she watched him do it, and thought: *he has rehearsed leaving this room too.*

"Madame Joubert." He paused at the door. "Whatever you decide — burn it or trade it or bury it under your floorboards until the century turns — I want you to know that your father's crime bought nine years of your childhood. Mine cost me everything after the age of six. Neither of us chose our inheritance. We're only deciding, now, what to do with it. Most people never get even that."

Then he was gone, his footsteps descending the stairs with that same unhurried evenness, and the street door closed, and the bell above it — her mother's bell, brought from the old house — gave its small bright shiver and went quiet.

* * *

Theo listened without interrupting, which was unlike him, and then sat down on the workbench stool and put his face in his hands, which was very unlike him.

"Two berths," he said finally, through his fingers.

"Yes."

"He knows about the floorboard?"

"He didn't say so. He didn't need to. He knows the letter exists and he knows it's here, and a man like that doesn't leave a thing like that to chance."

"So we're not choosing whether to accept his offer. We're choosing whether he comes back with friends." Theo dropped his hands and looked at her, and she saw her brother — her careful, honest brother, who had carried codes for a government negotiating its own funeral and never once lied to her — already calculating, already frightened, already halfway to yes. "Mirabel, two cabin berths. Do you understand what that means? Real beds. Food. Colette out of this city before the walls grow teeth. We could have her on that ship Thursday morning and by spring she'd be—"

"*We* could have *her*," Mirabel said quietly. "Say the rest of it, Theo. Say which two."

The silence stretched. Down in the street, someone laughed — actually laughed, a young man's laugh, startling as birdsong — and was gone.

"I can't," Theo admitted. "That's the truth. I've been trying since you started talking and I can't make the arithmetic come out honest. Any two I choose, I've chosen against the third. And any two you choose—" He stopped.

"Any two I choose, I've chosen against myself, because we both know I'm the one without a claim. No courier status. No minority. Just a seamstress with steady hands." She picked up the shirt she'd been mending before Delorme came — a captain's, collar frayed at the fold line — and smoothed it across her lap without seeing it. "He said something at the door. He said neither of us chose our inheritance, we're only deciding what to do with it. And I stood there wanting to throw him through the window, and instead I thought: *he's right*. That's what I couldn't stand, Theo. Not the berths. Not the threats. He wasn't threatening. He was right."

Theo crossed the room and knelt by her chair, the way he hadn't done since he was a boy with skinned knees. "So what do we do?"

"We don't decide tonight. Tonight we eat dinner, and we don't tell Colette, and we let the offer sit in the room with us like a fourth guest until we can look at it without flinching." She threaded a needle without thinking, the motion older than memory. "And tomorrow I'm going back to Papa's shop. There's something there — I've felt it since the letter came. A second copy, or a note, or nothing at all. But before I sell our father's name to a stranger's grief, I want to know exactly what it weighed."

Above the stove, beneath the loose floorboard, the letter waited in its oilcloth shroud, patient as the dead. Outside, far off toward the river, a ship's horn sounded once across the black water — long, low, unmistakable — as though the *Lysandre* itself had spoken: *Thursday noon. Thursday noon.*
