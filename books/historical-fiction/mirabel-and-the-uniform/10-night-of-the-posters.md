## 10. Night of the Posters

Colette heard the city before she understood it.

It came through the walls of the mending room as a sound like rain that had learned to walk — hundreds of boots, hundreds of hands, a wet slapping rhythm of paste and paper against brick. She was in bed, or nearly; she had been reading Luc's latest note by candle-stub, holding it so close to the flame that the corner had gone amber and soft, and when the noise began she blew out the light without thinking, the way one does when the body learns fear before the mind consents to it.

The house held its breath. Downstairs, floorboards creaked — Theo, she thought, moving from window to window with his courier's habit of counting exits. Mirabel's door stayed shut. The paste-brushes kept their wet applause outside.

She should have stayed in bed. She knew that even then, some honest ledger in her chest recording the fact: *you know what is on those posters, or you suspect it, and either way you are safer not knowing.* But fifteen is an age built for windows, and Colette went to hers the way she always did — barefoot, silent, careful of the third board from the sill that spoke if you asked it anything at all.

The street below was full of men with lanterns.

Not soldiers. That was the first thing she noticed, the thing that made her stomach drop in a slow, cold way she would remember for years. Soldiers would have been almost bearable — soldiers were weather, they happened to you, you waited them out. These were neighbors. She recognized the baker's son from Rue Verte, the one who used to steal sugar cubes off the counter while his mother scolded him fondly. She recognized old Marchand from the tobacco shop, whose cough she could pick out of any crowd because he'd stood next to her father at the funeral, hat in hand, saying nothing useful but standing anyway. They moved along the wall of the shuttered pharmacy across the street with brushes and buckets, unrolling long sheets of paper that caught the lantern-light like fish bellies.

They were pasting up lists.

Colette pressed her forehead to the cold glass. From this height she couldn't read the names, only see the shape of them — columns, dense as a page of scripture, going up under the eaves where everyone who walked to market would pass beneath them. Someone laughed. It was a small laugh, almost embarrassed, the kind of laugh men make when they are doing something they will later say they were made to do.

*Collaborators,* said a voice below, carrying in the frozen air. *Read it yourself tomorrow. Read it and see who fed at your table.*

Colette stepped back from the window as though the glass had grown warm.

Her heart was doing something strange — not racing, exactly, but knocking, the way Luc knocked at the service door of the mending room: three quick, one slow, a signal rather than a panic. Because she knew about lists now. She knew there was a letter in this house with names in it, names she wasn't supposed to know existed, and she knew — had known for days, in the place where knowledge waits to become unbearable — that one of the things she had passed to Luc, scribbled in pencil on the back of a laundry list, was that *the Jouberts had papers, papers that mattered, papers people were asking after.*

She hadn't known what she was passing. That was the sentence she had constructed for herself, the little boat she meant to ride out the storm in: *I didn't know.* She had told him gossip, she had told him atmosphere, she had told him the texture of the household the way you'd tell anyone you loved — and Luc had carried it to whoever Luc carried things to, because Luc believed, with the whole burning sincerity of seventeen, that information belonged to everyone, that secrets were how old men started wars.

She had underestimated herself. Everyone did. A girl who fetched thread and kept quiet was furniture; furniture saw everything and repeated nothing. Except she had repeated. One sentence, one evening, whispered under the awning by the fountain with her hand inside Luc's coat pocket where the notes changed hands — and now men with lanterns were pasting the world onto walls, and somewhere between her mouth and this street her sentence had grown legs.

She sat down on the edge of her bed in the dark and put her face in her hands and did not cry, because crying made noise, and noise brought Mirabel, and Mirabel would look at her with those tired seamstress eyes that missed nothing, and Colette could not yet afford to be seen clearly.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, the kind of morning that feels complicit.

The poster across the street had dried overnight into permanence. Colette saw it first from the kitchen window while she set the kettle — saw it as a white scar on the pharmacy wall — and then made herself go out, because not looking was a kind of cowardice she hadn't yet decided to practice.

The paper was cheap, already curling at one corner. The heading was printed in heavy black type, the kind used for war news: *FOR PUBLIC HONOR — PERSONS GUILTY OF TREASON AGAINST THE PEOPLE.* Below it, three columns of names. Some were crossed out already, struck through in pencil by hands she supposed considered themselves righteous. Others had been circled. Beside certain names, someone had written small annotations in the margin — *grocer, took payment 1917*, *wife still in the Ministry*, things like that, the city annotating its own damnation the way students mark a text they've been assigned to hate.

She found the J's by running her finger down the column the way she ran her finger down inventory lists for Mirabel.

Joubert, Émile. Tailor, Rue des Tanneurs.

Circled.

The ink of the circle was fresh — fresher than the print, done last night or early this morning by someone standing exactly where she stood. And beside it, no annotation. No crime listed. Just the circle, which was somehow worse than any accusation, because a circle says *we all know* and refuses to say what.

Their father. Dead four years, dead in the ground under a modest stone in Père Lachaise's crowded new section, dead with his thimble still in his effects box — and here he was, resurrected overnight as a name on a wall, promoted from grief into infamy by strangers with lanterns and paste.

Colette read the name again. Then a third time. There is a superstition among children that reading makes things truer each time, and she was trying, desperately, to read it false.

"Girl."

She turned. Old Marchand stood at his doorway with his broom, sweeping ash toward the gutter that had blown in from nowhere, sweeping nothing really, keeping his hands busy so he wouldn't have to meet her eyes above the poster. He had swept that same stretch of pavement every morning for as long as she'd lived in the quarter.

"Shouldn't stand under it," he said gruffly. "Bad luck."

"Is my papa's name," Colette said. Her voice came out flat and foreign to her. "On your wall."

"Not my wall." Sweep, sweep. "Wall of the republic, such as it was."

"You knew him. You stood at his grave."

Marchand stopped sweeping. For one moment his face did something complicated — guilt moving behind it like a fish under ice — and then the face closed, the way faces in the quarter had been closing all winter, and he said, "A man can stand at a grave and still not know what a man was. Go home, girl. Take your kettle off before you crack it."

She went home. Behind her she heard the broom start again, faster than before, sweeping harder at nothing.

* * *

By ten o'clock there were nine of them outside the shop.

Colette counted from the upstairs window, where she had stationed herself with a pretext — sorting buttons, actually, a bowl of them on the sill, her fingers moving through mother-of-pearl and bone while her eyes did the real work below. Nine people, drifting in ones and twos, the way crowds assemble when nobody wants to be first but everybody wants to be present. Two women from the fish market. A boy of maybe twelve who kept touching the poster and pulling his hand back. A man in a good coat who stood apart and said nothing and looked, once, directly up at the Joubert window — looked at *her*, she was almost sure, though the distance made it deniable — before walking on toward the river.

Nobody shouted. That was the terrible thing, the thing she would carry: nobody shouted, nobody threw a stone, nobody did anything at all. They simply gathered, the way weather gathers, and stood in the street outside the tailor shop that had been closed four years and let their presence do the work their voices didn't dare. A crowd that says nothing is louder than a crowd that screams. Screaming spends itself; silence accrues interest.

Madame Fouchard came out of the cheese shop opposite and looked at the poster for a long moment. Madame Fouchard, whose winter coat Mirabel had re-lined for free two years running, whose son's shirts Mirabel mended without charge because the son was at the front and then because the son was buried and then simply because that was what Mirabel did — mended for people who couldn't pay, quietly, entering nothing in the book. Madame Fouchard read the name Joubert twice. Then she went back inside her shop and turned the sign to *FERMÉ* and stayed closed the rest of the day, which was its own kind of statement, though not the kind Colette needed.

By noon there were more. By noon someone had thrown ink at the poster — not at their door, at the paper, a black starburst over the neighboring name — and the ink had run in the damp and streaked downward, so that a thin black line now dripped from the top of the list all the way past *Joubert, Émile* and pooled slightly at the bottom of the sheet, like the whole document bleeding from a wound near its head.

Mirabel came to stand beside her at the window sometime in the afternoon. Colette didn't hear her come up — she never did, the woman moved like thread through cloth — and suddenly she was just there, sleeves rolled, flour on one wrist from bread she hadn't baked, looking down at the street with an expression Colette had no name for. Not fear. Not quite grief. Something older than both, something that had perhaps been waiting in her all these years for exactly this view.

"He'd have hated the spelling of his trade," Mirabel said at last, quietly. "*Tailor.* He always wrote himself *tailleur pour dames*. Men's coats offended him."

Colette laughed — one broken syllable of it, surprising them both — and then pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"They're not going to do anything," Mirabel said. "Look at them. Half of them are waiting to see if the other half goes first. That's not a mob, Col. That's a mirror." She touched Colette's hair, once, at the crown, the way she had when Colette was small enough to be carried. "But mirrors can be pushed. We'll keep the shutters closed tonight."

Below, the twelve-year-old threw a pebble at the shop's darkened window. It ticked off the glass and fell into the gutter. Nobody applauded. Nobody stopped him. He picked up another pebble and held it, considering, and the holding was worse than the throwing.

"The neighbors—" Colette began.

"I know," Mirabel said.

"But Madame Fouchard—"

"I know."

"You mended her coat. You mended it *twice* and she never paid and you never—" Colette's voice cracked sideways into something younger than fifteen. "Doesn't that count? Doesn't any of it count?"

Mirabel was silent for a moment. Down in the street, the man in the good coat had returned and was speaking low to the fishwives, and whatever he said made them nod, and the nodding had the smoothness of arrangement rather than anger.

"It counts," Mirabel said finally. "It counts the way stitches count. One at a time, and none of them holds the coat together alone, and the first hard pull undoes the lot unless the cloth itself was strong." She turned from the window. "Our cloth had a name written on it, Colette. That's all. That's the whole tragedy. Everything we sewed was fine until the day someone read the label."

She went downstairs. Colette stayed at the window until the light failed, watching the crowd thin and regather and thin, watching the poster take on the blue of dusk, watching her father's name dissolve by degrees into the general darkness — except that it didn't dissolve, she knew; it would be there at dawn, more legible than ever, refreshed by the night the way all printed things are.

* * *

Theo came home after curfew, which meant he came home through means he did not explain, and found both of them sitting in the kitchen by a single lamp with the shutters barred, the table laid for dinner no one had cooked.

He listened. That was the thing about Theo that Colette had only lately learned to value — he listened the way a lock listens to a key, completely, taking the shape of what was said. Mirabel told him about the poster, the crowd, Madame Fouchard's turned sign. Colette told him about Marchand's broom. When she got to the part about the man in the good coat who had looked up at the window, Theo's face went very still, stiller than Mirabel's had been, and he asked precise questions — which direction had the man gone, had he carried anything, had he spoken to the fishwives before or after — and she answered as well as she could, and hated how easily the answers came, hated that her eyes had apparently spent years training for espionage without her consent.

"So they know the address," Theo said. "They've known the address. The poster isn't information, it's permission. Somebody leaked a fragment of a list to make the rest feel inevitable." He rubbed his frost-scarred hands together, a habit when thinking. "Tomorrow there'll be a second poster. Bigger. With dates and amounts. Leaks always come in installments — keeps the audience buying."

"Then we leave," Colette said. "Take the letter, take whatever, get out of the quarter—"

"Where?" Theo asked, not unkindly. "The ships aren't sailing yet and every road out is watched by men who'd love a courier and two women with a famous name. No. We stay. We open the shop."

Mirabel looked up sharply. "Theo."

"We open the shop," he said again, and there was something in his voice Colette had never heard, something forged rather than born. "Shutters wide. Lamp lit. Mirabel at the window sewing where every soul on this street can see her. Let them watch a Joubert mend a uniform. Whose uniform? Anybody's. Everybody's. You don't fight a mirror, Col — you *become the reflection they can't argue with*."

"And if they throw stones?" Colette asked.

"Then I stand in front of the glass," Theo said, "and they can throw stones at a courier of the republic, which is at least a target with a rank."

Later — much later, when the lamp was trimmed and Mirabel had gone up and Theo sat alone at the table turning his cup without drinking — Colette stood in the stairwell shadow with her hand pressed flat against her own sternum, feeling the knock-knock-knock under the ribs.

Because she knew something neither of them knew. She knew that the leak had not come from Delorme, or the Ministry, or the ghost of the dead minister Aubry. She knew it had come out of her own mouth, in fragments, folded into a boy's coat pocket by the fountain, in the currency of love, which spends like any other.

The second poster would come tomorrow, Theo had said. Leaks come in installments.

And Colette stood in the dark on the stairs and understood, with the terrible clarity of the underestimated, that she was the installment plan — and that tomorrow, before the paste dried on whatever the city hung next, she had to find Luc, and either save him from what she'd made him carry, or confess to Mirabel what she'd made them all carry, and she did not know which of those frightened her more.
