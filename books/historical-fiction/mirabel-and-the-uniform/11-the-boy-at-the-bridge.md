## 11. The Boy at the Bridge

Theo Joubert had spent eleven years learning to read a city the way other men read a newspaper, and what he read that Tuesday morning was bad news set in large type.

It began with the trams. The line along the Quai des Orfèvres had stopped running — not delayed, not thinned, but stopped, its cars standing empty in a row like pulled teeth. Then there were the gendarmes at the Pont-au-Change, four of them where usually there was one, and they were not waving people through as they had all winter, with the bored indifference of men guarding nothing in particular. They were stopping boys. Every boy between fourteen and twenty who crossed alone got a hand on the shoulder and a question Theo could not hear over the river.

He should have gone home. That was the discipline of his trade: when the pattern breaks, walk away, come back tomorrow, let someone else be the news. Eleven years of carrying satchels whose contents could end governments had taught him that curiosity was a luxury purchased by people who had nothing to lose, and he had three someones at home who would not eat if he were careless.

But he counted the boys being turned back, and he counted the ones being taken aside, and among the second group — smaller, thinner, collarless, hands jammed in pockets against the cold — he recognized the stoop of Luc Ferrand.

* * *

Luc was not, strictly speaking, anyone's anything. He was the boy Colette loved, which in the arithmetic of their household made him a kind of relation already, though no paper said so. He was sixteen, apprenticed to a printer on Rue Verte who had lately found more profitable work than printing, and he had the particular beauty of adolescent boys who have decided to be serious: a jaw held slightly forward, eyes that watched doors. Mirabel fed him whenever he appeared, which was often, and pretended not to notice how he looked at Colette across the dinner table like a man reading a letter he'd been forbidden to open.

Theo liked him. That was the trouble. It is easy to spend a stranger; it costs something real to spend a boy you've watched grow taller at your own table.

The gendarmes had put Luc against the parapet. One of them went through his coat pockets with the thoroughness of a man looking for weapons and finding instead a folded packet of paper, small enough to fit in a palm, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string. Theo saw the moment the gendarme's face changed — the small settling of the features, the shift from routine to interest. Interest, in this city, in this season, was the most dangerous weather there was.

They took Luc by both arms and walked him off the bridge toward the prefecture annex on the far bank, and the crowd on the bridge did what crowds do, which is nothing, beautifully, comprehensively nothing, an entire theater of people studying the river as if the Seine had suddenly become interesting after nine hundred years.

Theo stood very still and thought about the favor.

* * *

Every courier carried one. It was never spoken of as such — officially it did not exist, which was precisely why it worked. Call it a debt, call it credit, call it the memory of a night in March four years ago when Theo had carried a sealed case through three checkpoints during the general strike because the man who needed it carried was Commandant Roche-Vasseur of the Sûreté, and the case contained something without which Roche-Vasseur's daughter would have died in a clinic in Basel, or so Roche-Vasseur believed then and perhaps still believed now. Theo had never asked what was in the case. Asking was how favors curdled into obligations. He had simply carried it, and afterward Roche-Vasseur had gripped his hand too hard on the prefecture steps and said, *Joubert, if you ever need a door opened, mine opens for you,* and Theo had filed those words away the way he filed everything: dry, dark, and folded exactly once.

A man in Theo's profession could spend that favor on many things. A warning before an arrest. A passport in a false name when the evacuation lists were drawn. Passage out — a berth, even, if the right officer owed the wrong allegiance. In six weeks, maybe less, when the last ship sailed and the codes in his satchel became worthless paper, that single unspent favor might be the difference between his family standing on the quay and watching, or standing on the deck and leaving.

And here was Luc Ferrand, sixteen years old, being walked into a building where questions were asked with rubber hoses and electric wire, carrying notes that Colette had written in her careful schoolgirl hand about conversations she'd overheard in a mending room — about a letter, about a minister's address, about names.

Theo stood on the bridge for the length of time it takes a barge to pass beneath it. The barge was loaded with scrap iron going nowhere; the war had ended the market for everything except itself. When the stern cleared the third arch, Theo turned and followed the gendarmes' route to the prefecture annex, and he noticed, with the detached clarity that comes in the moments when a life changes shape, that his feet had already decided, and that some part of him had decided weeks ago, months ago, perhaps since the night the misdirected letter first lay on their table like a live coal — that whatever currency he had was going to be spent keeping children alive, and that he had never once regretted the spending until this exact moment, and even now the regret was thin, a frost on a window rather than a wall.

* * *

Roche-Vasseur had aged badly and elegantly, the way certain furniture does. His office in the annex smelled of cold tobacco and wet wool. Behind him, through frosted glass, Theo could see the shapes of men moving, and hear — faintly, unmistakably — the rhythmic sound of water being poured, though no one in this building was thirsty at ten in the morning.

"Joubert." Roche-Vasseur did not stand. "You're a long way from your routes."

"My routes are mostly gone. I carry letters now."

"To whom? There's hardly a government left to receive them."

"That," Theo said, "is a matter of opinion, apparently, still under negotiation."

The old man almost smiled. Almost, and then didn't, because behind the frosted glass the pouring sound started again, and both of them heard it, and both of them knew what it was for, and the knowing sat down between them like a third guest.

"You want something," Roche-Vasseur said. "You came through my front desk without an appointment, which you've never done. You're wearing your honest face, which means you're about to ask for something dishonest. Ask."

"A boy was brought in off the Pont-au-Change this morning. Luc Ferrand. Sixteen. Printer's apprentice. He was carrying papers."

"Papers." Roche-Vasseur's voice flattened. "In this city, everyone is carrying papers. Half the intelligence flowing across my desk now is written by children, because children are invisible and adults are stupid. Do you know this boy?"

"He eats at my table."

"Ah." Roche-Vasseur leaned back. The chair creaked with the dignity of a dying institution. "Then you know what he was carrying."

"I know he's sixteen. I know he's in love with my sister — my ward, my sister, we don't stand on the distinction. I know that whatever he was carrying, he carried it because a girl asked him to, and girls ask boys to do foolish things, Commandant, and boys say yes, and that has been the history of the world since before either of us was born."

"And the papers themselves?"

"Theo Joubert," the old man said, before Theo could answer, "do you remember March, four years ago? The strike? The clinic in Basel?"

"I remember rain," Theo said carefully.

"I remember a courier who didn't ask questions. Who carried a case through three checkpoints with soldiers' rifles an inch from his neck, and never asked what was inside, and never asked me for anything after. Eleven years on my rolls and never once called in the debt. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know how much I have *wanted* you to ask?" Roche-Vasseur passed a hand over his face. "I have lain awake hoping you'd never need it, Joubert, because of what I'd have to tell you."

"Tell me now."

"The boy can be released. That part is easy; he's nobody, the notes he carried are trivia, a girl's transcription of shop talk, half of it garbled. I'll burn them myself. He walks out the front door within the hour, frightened, wiser, intact." Roche-Vasseur paused. "But the favor buys more than the boy. The favor, spent today, is spent entirely. And what it would otherwise buy — Joubert, listen to me, because I am going to say this once and then deny I ever said it — what it would otherwise buy is this: when the final manifests are drawn up for the evacuation, there is a clerk on the fourth floor who owes me his career, and I could have made sure the name Joubert appeared on the protected list. Not a berth. Berths are above even me now. But protected status. Priority consideration. A foot in the queue, when the queue is going to be ten thousand families long."

The room was very quiet. Through the frosted glass, the pouring had stopped.

"So that's the price list," Theo said. His voice sounded strange to himself, level and far away. "The boy, or the place in the queue."

"The boy, or the place in the queue. And I will honor whichever you choose and never speak of it again, and neither will you."

Theo thought about Mirabel's hands. She mended uniforms for officers of a government that was negotiating its own funeral, and she did it with a straight back and clean stitches, and every franc she earned went into Colette's shoes and Theo's boots and the coal scuttle, and she never once complained, because complaining was a form of arithmetic she refused to practice. He thought about the arithmetic he practiced anyway, the constant silent tallying of a courier's life: routes and risks, weights and distances, and now seats and souls. One berth. Two berths. A place in the queue. A boy of sixteen with ink stains on his fingers who looked at Colette like a man reading a letter.

If he kept the favor, he kept it for them. That was the argument he'd have made, and it would even have been true. But favors kept are futures imagined, and futures imagined are not futures owned, and somewhere in the last month Theo had learned — lying to Delorme had taught him — that a man discovers what he actually believes only when he hears his own voice saying it.

"Open the door," Theo said. "Let the boy go."

Roche-Vasseur studied him for a long moment. Whatever he saw in Theo's face seemed to settle something; he nodded once, heavily, like a judge closing a file.

"Ferrand. Luc." He wrote the name on a slip, initialed it. "Front entrance, within the hour, papers destroyed, no record beyond my desk. If he's picked up again carrying anything, Joubert, there is no favor in the republic that saves him, because there won't be a republic." He slid the slip across the desk and held it down with two fingers. "And Joubert — the queue. When you stand on that quay in six weeks and the names are called and yours isn't among them, I want you to remember that you chose this with your eyes open. I want you to remember it was arithmetic, and you did the sum yourself."

"I know," Theo said. "I'm good at sums."

"No," Roche-Vasseur said quietly. "You're good at carrying. There's a difference, and you've just paid for the lesson."

* * *

Luc came out of the annex at twenty past eleven, moving fast, coat buttoned wrong, a red mark on one cheek where a hand had landed but no worse, and he walked past Theo on the quay without seeing him — head down, breath ragged, a boy fleeing a room he'd spend the rest of his life describing badly. Theo let him go. Some exits should not be interrupted.

He waited until Luc reached the corner of Rue de la Monnaie, and then Theo turned and walked home along the river with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders bent into the wind, and he did the accounting he knew he'd have to do eventually, standing at his own kitchen table with Mirabel's eyes on him.

The favor was spent. The queue was closed to them. Whatever happened at the docks in six weeks would happen on merit and mercy alone, and the republic had little of either left in stock.

At home, Colette met him in the doorway with her face already asking the question, because Luc had come to her first, stammering, half a story, and Theo saw in her eyes the terrible beginning of understanding — that her notes, her boy, her war had cost something, though she didn't yet know what, and wouldn't, because Theo had already decided he would never tell her the price. Sacrifice, he understood then, holding the door for a child who smelled of fear and printer's ink, was only real if it went unwitnessed and unbilled.

He hung up his coat. He kissed the top of Colette's head. He went to find Mirabel, because there was one thing he had to say to her before the day grew any older:

"Mira. We need to talk about the ship."
