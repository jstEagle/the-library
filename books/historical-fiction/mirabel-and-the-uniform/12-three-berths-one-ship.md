## 12. Three Berths, One Ship

The note came at dusk, folded once and slipped beneath the door like a coin to a beggar. Mirabel found it when she went down to bank the stove, the paper pale against the dark floorboards, and for a long moment she stood with her hand still on the iron latch, not picking it up. She had known it would come. Knowing did nothing to soften the weight of it.

She carried it upstairs to the lamp before she opened it. The handwriting was small and precise, a clerk's hand, a careful man's hand: *Dawn. The letter, complete. Two berths on the morning sailing — I could only hold two; forgive me, the arithmetic is no kinder to me than to anyone. Noon departure. If the letter does not come, the offer dies with the ship.*

No signature. He didn't need one.

Mirabel set the note on the table between the lamp and the bread board, where Theo would see it when he came in, and then she took up the mending that had waited all day — a colonel's greatcoat with a torn lining, the wool gone thin at the shoulders from years of being worn proudly and badly. Her needle moved through the cloth in small even stitches while the light outside died and the city's bells rang six o'clock, seven o'clock, their bronze voices hoarse now, half of them silenced by shell damage or decree.

Colette was at the window seat with a book she wasn't reading. Mirabel had watched the girl turn the same page four times. Fifteen-year-olds thought they were invisible; Mirabel remembered being fifteen, remembered believing her own face a locked room. But grief and fear showed on Colette the way weather shows on water, in ripples she couldn't feel herself making. Something had been eating at her for days. Mirabel told herself there would be time to ask. There was always time, right up until there wasn't.

Theo came home at nine, smelling of coal smoke and the river, his courier's satchel empty for perhaps the last time. The government he served had shrunk to three rooms above a café and a man with a rubber stamp; even the fiction of it was dissolving now, the way ice goes out of a river, all at once after seeming permanent forever. He hung his coat, saw the note, read it twice, and sat down across from Mirabel without taking off his boots.

"Two," he said.

"He said two."

"It was three yesterday."

"People are fleeing, Theo. Even Delorme can't conjure berths out of water."

He turned the note over as though something might be written on the back. His hands were steady — they were always steady, the courier's hands, trained never to tremble over anything entrusted to them — but his jaw worked once, twice, the way it did when words were queuing up inside him faster than his honesty could sort them.

"Then we take them," he said quietly. "We take both."

Mirabel's needle stopped.

* * *

Colette was sent to bed without ceremony — or rather, she went, because Mirabel asked her to and the girl obeyed too readily lately, gratefully, as if sleep were a country with better news. They heard her door close. They heard the boards creak as she lay down. And then the house held its breath around the two of them and the lamp and the note.

"We take both," Theo said again, "and we let the past burn. That's the whole of it, Mira. That's the entire argument." He pushed his hands through his hair, and for a moment he looked fifteen himself, the boy who had once cried for an hour over a sparrow their father made him bury in the shop yard. "Delorme gets the letter. The list goes with it, every name, Father's name included. Whatever he does with it, whatever the new regime writes in its histories — none of it follows us across the water. We start clean. Colette starts clean."

"And the names?" Mirabel kept her voice low and even, the seamstress's voice, the one she used for difficult customers. "The other families on that list. The ones who informed like he did, and the ones who didn't, and the ones who were informed *on*. Do they start clean too?"

"They were never ours to carry."

"They're on our father's paper. In our father's hand." She set down the greatcoat, the needle resting in the seam like a small silver bone. "You want to hand Delorme the only proof of what happened, and then sail away and call ourselves innocent."

"I want us to *live*." It came out louder than he intended; they both glanced toward Colette's door, and Theo dropped his voice to a fierce whisper. "Mira. Look at me. Look at what's coming. You've seen the posters going up on the Rue Verte. You know what the new regime does with lists — it doesn't weigh them, it doesn't ask what a man's reasons were twenty years ago. It uses them. Names become arrests, arrests become disappearances, and nobody who stays gets to be a bystander. Not you. Not me. Not a fifteen-year-old girl who's already lost one family to this country's wars." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, she could see him hating it. "Father informed to save us. Whatever else he was, whatever he wrote to you in that letter you keep under your floorboards like a relic — he did it for *us*. Don't you dare let his sacrifice buy us nothing but a grave."

"The truth isn't a grave."

"No. It's just where graves get dug."

They stared at each other across the table, across the lamp, across thirty years of shared beds and shared bread and shared silences, and Mirabel felt something shift in the foundations of her love for him — not break, nothing so dramatic as breaking, but settle, the way a house settles, with a sound only its inhabitants hear.

"You'd burn it," she said. "If it came to burning. You'd burn the list and the letter and everything he confessed to me, everything he was ashamed of, everything he wanted me to understand about why we are who we are. You'd burn my inheritance."

"Our father left us a tailor's shop and a bad reputation waiting to happen. Some inheritance."

"Some inheritance is a wound, Theo. You don't heal it by pretending the scar isn't there. You heal it by learning what cut you." She rose and went to the window, because looking at him was suddenly more than she could do. Below, the street lay blacked out and empty, snow beginning again, softening the city's edges the way lies soften the edges of things. "I've spent my whole life mending uniforms. Officers' coats, soldiers' jackets, the clothes men wear into history. And every single garment tells you the same thing if you look closely enough: nobody's story is seamless. Everybody has a place where they tore and someone sewed them shut again. Father's letter is a tear in our cloth. I will not pretend it isn't there. I'll mend around it if I have to, I'll live beside it, but I won't hand the whole coat to a stranger and walk away whistling."

"And if walking away is the only way Colette sees nineteen?" He came and stood beside her, not touching, close enough that she could feel the heat of his anger cooling into something worse — reasonableness. "Two berths, Mira. Out of three people. You've done this arithmetic already; I've watched you doing it for a week. So let's say it plainly. Someone stays. If we refuse Delorme, maybe no one goes. If we accept, two go. Which of us do you propose to leave behind? Because I promise you I've run the sums too, and every version ends with you staying, and I will not sign my name to that."

"Then don't sign anything. Just listen to me for once in your careful, honest life."

"I am listening. That's what frightens me. I'm listening to my sister choose a piece of paper over her own skin."

"A piece of paper," Mirabel said, turning to face him at last, "is the only thing that ever survived any of this. Regimes fall. Ships sink. Cities change hands and change their street names and pretend they were always called what the conquerors call them. But paper keeps. Someday — ten years, fifty — someone opens a file, someone reads a list, and the truth of what our father did and why he did it is either there or it isn't. If it isn't, then he's only ever the monster on a poster, and I'm only ever the daughter who ran away and let him be one."

Theo was quiet a long time. Snow ticked against the glass. From somewhere streets away, muffled by walls and weather, a patrol's boots kept uneven time.

"You memorized it," he said finally. "The list. You told me you did."

"Every name."

"Then what does it matter if the paper burns? You're carrying it anyway. You're the archive, Mira. You're the worst kind of paper — the kind that argues back."

"Paper doesn't argue. Paper testifies. And someday, if I'm still alive to speak, I'll testify. That's different from handing the evidence to the first man who offers us a boat."

He looked at her then with an expression she had never seen on him before — or rather, an expression she had seen only on strangers, on customers, on men deciding whether to trust a woman with their uniform. Doubt. Her brother doubted her, and the seeing of it hurt more than any shout could have.

"When did you become so hard?" he asked, and it wasn't cruel; it was bewildered, which was crueller.

"I'm not hard. I'm mended." She picked up the greatcoat again, needing her hands occupied, needing the honest work of thread and cloth. "There's a difference. Hard breaks. Mended holds."

* * *

They did not resolve it. That was the fracture's true nature — not a clean break but a load-bearing wall cracked down the middle, still standing, still holding up the roof, groaning in the wind. By midnight they had agreed on nothing except not to wake Colette, and they sat on opposite sides of the dying lamp like two witnesses to an accident, each certain of what they'd seen.

It was Theo who spoke last, near one o'clock, his voice scraped down to the grain.

"If you stay, I stay."

"Don't be a fool."

"I'm the eldest. I'm the courier. I'm the one who taught you to read a tide table and forge a customs stamp. Everything you are, you learned watching me, so don't tell me what I am and aren't allowed to be foolish about." He rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. "One berth. Maybe that's the answer neither of us wants. One berth, and it goes to Colette, and we stand on the dock together like idiots and watch the ship go."

"That's not the offer. The offer is two, and it dies at dawn."

"Then let it die." He said it simply, terribly, and Mirabel understood that some part of him had already crossed over — had chosen the truth, had chosen her, had chosen the burned paper and the stayed-behind life — not because she'd persuaded him but because he loved her more than he feared the future. "Let the whole rotten offer die. We were Jouberts before Delorme ever learned the name. We'll be Jouberts after."

And that should have been the end of it — reconciliation, exhaustion, sleep. But Mirabel lay awake in her cold bed listening to the snow, running the arithmetic that never changed, and thinking about dawn, and about how many hours remained, and about a girl asleep down the hall who had been asking too many questions lately and answering none.

At some hour past three, she heard a floorboard in the hall. Small feet, trying to be quiet. The particular silence of a door easing open.

Mirabel was out of bed before the thought finished forming, and the letter's hiding place behind the loose brick was already open, and the oilcloth wrapping was already gone, and the letter —

— was gone.

Downstairs, the front door stood ajar on the snow, filling the kitchen with blue winter light, and small footprints led away toward the river, toward the docks, toward the occupied quarter where a courteous man named Delorme was waiting for dawn.

"Colette!" Mirabel's voice broke against the empty street, and no one answered but the snow.
