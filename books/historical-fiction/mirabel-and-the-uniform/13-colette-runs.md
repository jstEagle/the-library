## 13. Colette Runs

Colette had learned to count by listening. Before she could read, before she could hold a needle without stabbing her thumb, she had learned that a house told you everything if you paid attention: the number of stairs Theo's boots took on his way down (fourteen, always fourteen, unless he was late, and then nine with the last three skipped), the particular drag of Mirabel's chair when her back ached from bending over an officer's collar, the difference between the front door opening for a customer and opening for trouble. Trouble knocked twice, softly, like it didn't want to wake anyone.

So when Mirabel's voice came through the floorboards that evening — low, urgent, the voice she used only with Theo, never with customers, never with her — Colette did not need to hear the words to know what they were.

She sat at the little table in the attic room that had been hers since she was seven years old, since the border war had made her an orphan and made the Jouberts her family in the same terrible afternoon, and she pretended to copy sums into her exercise book. The sums were lies. She hadn't done real schoolwork in weeks; there was no one to check it, no school left to attend, and the exercise book had become instead the place where she wrote down what she heard below, in a cipher of her own invention — dates disguised as arithmetic, names hidden in margins, troop numbers folded into the columns of a pretend grocery ledger. She had been so proud of herself once. She remembered the pride the way you remember a coat you've outgrown: it still existed, but it wouldn't close around her anymore.

Because she knew now. She had known since Tuesday, since the boy Luc had pressed her hand too hard under the bridge arch and said, *Colette, the man who reads my notes asked me about a letter. A sealed letter, misdelivered. He said it came to a tailor's family on this street.* And she had laughed — actually laughed, because it was absurd, because what did letters matter to men like that — and then she had seen Luc's face, and stopped laughing, and understood that the war had been sitting in her kitchen all winter wearing an apron and mending collars, and she had been feeding it information through a boy who loved her.

She had given them the letter. Not the paper itself — she'd never touched the paper — but the shape of it. The fact of it. She had told Luc about the dead minister's name on the envelope, about how Mirabel had gone strange and quiet after it arrived, about how Theo had come home twice that week smelling of other people's cigarettes and fear. Small things. Tidbits. She had thought of them as tidbits, the way you think of crumbs as nothing until you find the whole loaf gone.

And now Mirabel's voice through the floorboards, and Theo's answering voice, and the word *dawn* said twice, and the word *berths* said once in a tone Colette had never heard either of them use — a tone like arithmetic being done on something that shouldn't be counted.

She closed the exercise book.

Below, the house went quiet in the way houses do after decisions are made: not silence but settlement, the creak of two people each lying down on their own conclusions. Colette lay in her narrow bed with her eyes open, watching the water stain on the ceiling that looked, depending on the light, like a country or a bird or a spilled cup, and she did the counting that mattered.

One berth. Maybe two, if Delorme's offer still stood, and offers like that don't stand long. Three of them. Mirabel would give hers away — Colette knew this the way she knew the fourteen stairs, the way she knew the double knock of trouble; Mirabel had been giving things away since before Colette arrived, had been built, apparently, out of the same material as saints and doormats, though no one dared say the second part aloud. Theo would refuse to leave without Mirabel, because Theo was honest the way stone is heavy — not a virtue but a property. Which meant the berths would rot unused at the dock while the three of them stood on the quay holding each other and drowning together, out of love, out of principle, out of the Joubert talent for making a virtue of staying.

And it would be her fault. All of it. The letter had been safe in its drawer, unknown to any man in a good coat, until Colette had opened her mouth in a stairwell in November and handed the war its address.

She got up before the church bells finished striking five.

* * *

The letter lived where it had lived for three weeks: inside the false bottom of Mirabel's sewing basket, beneath the pincushion shaped like a tomato that their mother — no, not theirs, theirs was ash and a border crossing — beneath the pincushion that had come with the basket. Colette had watched Mirabel hide it there on the first night, had watched with the same attention she gave everything in that house, filing it away, and she had told herself she filed it away for no reason. She had been lying to herself even then. That was the worst discovery of the winter: that she had always known, somewhere underneath, that she was building toward this. That the notes for Luc, the careful ears, the cipher in the exercise book — that some part of her had wanted to matter to the war, had been flattered by it, a fifteen-year-old girl carrying secrets like a torch and telling herself it was patriotism when it was mostly vanity. And the vanity had a price, and the price was written on a wall outside the shop in letters a meter tall: JOUBERT. TRAITOR'S NAME. Her father's name, which was also her guardians' name, which was now also hers.

The sewing basket sat on its shelf in the back room where Mirabel did the fine work. Colette lifted the tomato pincushion. Pins rained softly against the wicker. The false bottom came up on its leather hinge, and there it was — the envelope, gray with handling now, soft at the corners, addressed in her father's handwriting to a man already dead. Their father's handwriting. Colette had seen it only in the shop ledger, in the tall patient figures of accounts settled, but she knew it instantly, the way you know a voice through a wall. It looked like Mirabel's writing. It looked like the future looking backward.

She took it. She put the pincushion back. She left a note, because leaving without a note was unthinkable, because even traitors and thieves in this house left notes — *Gone to Madame Ferrand's, back before dark* had been the liturgy of her whole childhood — and because whatever else happened, they should know she had chosen this, that it wasn't the city that took her. She wrote it on the back of a pattern piece, in pencil, fast:

*I know it was me. I heard what I said to Luc got out. I'm going to fix it. Don't follow me. C.*

Then, because she was fifteen and honest even in betrayal, she added: *I'm sorry.*

She put on her coat — Theo's old courier coat, cut down for her two winters ago, the hidden pocket Mirabel had sewn into the lining still empty and waiting, irony being the one thing the war produced in abundance — and she went down the stairs, fourteen of them, taking the last three slowly for the first time in her life.

* * *

The occupied quarter began where the streetlamps changed.

That was how you knew, in a city that officially remained one city: the lamps in the free quarter were broken or unlit, victims of the blackouts and the general decay, while the occupied quarter burned bright and regular, every lamp fed, every street washed, order imposed like paint over rot. Colette crossed the line at the fountain where the fishmongers used to argue about eels, and the change came over her skin before it came over her eyes — a coldness, a prickling, the animal certainty of being watched. There were sentries at the corner of the Rue aux Chiens, two of them, young, stamping against the cold, their breath rising in white flags of surrender. She had walked past soldiers a hundred times. She had never walked past them carrying the reason three governments wanted her family destroyed folded against her ribs.

Don't run, she told herself. Running is a confession with legs.

So she walked, at the speed of a girl going somewhere dull — an errand, a sister's thread, a mother's medicine — past the shuttered café where officers drank coffee that had once been coffee, past the requisitioned hotel with its new flag hanging straight and wrong above the door, past the poster wall where the occupation announced its truths in three typefaces. Her father's name was there. She made herself look. JOUBERT, A., it said, in the list of collaborators published by the incoming regime, the leaked fragment that had set the neighborhood muttering, and beside it the regime's own verdict: TO BE INVESTIGATED. Two lists, two regimes, one name. Everyone wanted to own her father now that he was safely dead. She felt something rise in her that wasn't grief exactly, or wasn't only grief — something with teeth in it. Good, she thought at the poster. Look at him. Investigate him. You'll find a man who informed once, twenty years ago, to keep two children fed, and you'll find it cost him more than your whole commission will ever pay, and he died with a needle case for a fortune, and your ink doesn't reach him where he is.

But her hands were shaking, and she put them in her pockets, and walked.

The streets narrowed as she went deeper. Here were the shops that stayed open, selling things that required permission; here were women with the particular posture of those who have decided to survive rather than to live, spines straight, faces shut; here a queue outside a baker's, silent, orderly, the queue of people practicing for worse. An old man sold roasted chestnuts from a cart and called out his price in a voice worn smooth as a stair rail, and the ordinariness of it nearly broke her — that the world could contain chestnuts and annexations in the same hour, that hunger and history shared a street without embarrassment. She bought six chestnuts she didn't want because refusing to buy felt dangerous, and the old man wrapped them in newspaper, and the headline on the newspaper was about evacuation ships, and she read the number of berths remaining in the two seconds it took him to make change.

She had known the number. Knowing it in print was different. Numbers in print have authority, like scripture. *Forty-one berths remaining,* said the newspaper, and she stood on the corner of the occupied quarter eating chestnuts she couldn't taste, doing the arithmetic that had kept her awake: forty-one berths, three Jouberts, one letter. If the letter bought two berths, and Theo refused his, and Mirabel refused hers —

No. She stopped the thought the way you stop a clock before it strikes. She was not going to the docks. She was going to Delorme. One transaction, clean, final: the letter for the family. Papers cleared, names erased from lists, passage secured. She had heard enough in the mending room to know such trades existed; she had spent a year learning how men negotiated over the bodies of cities, and it had never occurred to her until this exact frozen moment that she had been studying.

Luc, she thought, and stopped walking.

Luc was still detained. Had been for four days now, held at the prefecture annexe for carrying notes no one could prove were anything but a schoolboy's scribbles — except they could prove it, of course, they could prove anything they wanted, proof being another thing the war manufactured in abundance. Every plan she had ever made had included Luc the way maps include rivers. And she was walking away from him, toward Delorme, carrying the single object that might buy his release — because surely it could, surely a man who traded in berths and cleared names could trade in one boy from the annexe, it would be nothing to him, a signature, a phone call —

She stood very still in the bright cold street, chestnut paper crushed in her fist, and understood that she had just tried to negotiate with herself, and that the negotiation had no end. The letter was small. Its power was enormous. Everyone wanted it, and everyone would promise anything for it, and promises from men like these were printed on the same presses as the posters. But Luc was in a cell, and her family was in a house pretending to sleep, and the ship sailed at noon, and she was fifteen years old and had run out of adults to believe in.

She ate the last chestnut. It tasted of nothing. Then she turned up the Rue Verte toward the address she had copied months ago from a calling card Theo kept meaning to burn — 9, Rue des Orfèvres, third floor — and walked faster now, because dawn was coming, because dawn was the deadline, because somewhere behind her Mirabel would be waking to a note and a missing letter and a silence in the attic room, and Mirabel would understand in one cold instant what had happened, and Mirabel would come.

Good, Colette thought. Come. But by the time you find me it will be done, and you'll be angry, and we'll all be on a ship, and anger keeps better than grief.

The Rue des Orfèvres was quieter than the rest of the quarter, residential, high-windowed, the kind of street where money had learned to keep its voice down. Number 9 had a brass plate polished by someone whose job it was to polish brass plates, a fact that seemed obscene to her, a person employed full-time to make metal shine while children queued for bread. The outer door stood open for the sweep-out. She climbed the stairs — marble, then wood, then carpet worn to the weave — counting out of habit. Thirty-one steps to the third floor.

There was a light under the door.

She raised her hand to knock and found it wouldn't move. Her whole arm, her whole body, had arrived at the edge of what it could do, and stood there trembling on the brink like a diver on a board. Through the door came sounds: drawers opening and closing. Paper being folded. A man humming — actually humming, three bars of something old and gentle, a lullaby almost, the tune half-remembered from a country that no longer existed.

He was packing. She knew the sound of packing the way she knew the sound of everything; she had grown up in a house of departures. Drawers opening and closing, paper folding, a lullaby hummed by a man alone at dawn in a city about to fall.

Colette Joubert — Colette of nowhere, Colette the ward, Colette who had given away a secret for the flattery of being trusted — stood in the carpeted corridor with a stolen letter against her ribs and her knuckles an inch from the door, and knocked.

Twice. Softly. Like trouble.

The humming stopped. Footsteps crossed the floor, unhurried, and the lock turned, and the door opened onto lamplight and the smell of cigar smoke and packed trunks, and Delorme stood there in his shirtsleeves with a pistol lying on the table behind him, plain as a teapot, and he looked at her for a long moment with an expression she would spend the rest of her life trying to name — recognition, maybe, or dread, or simply the face a man makes when the future knocks and turns out to be a child.

"You," he said. "You're the Joubert girl."

"I've brought you what you want," Colette said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "But we're going to talk about the price."
