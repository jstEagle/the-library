## 14. The Exchange That Wasn't

The occupied quarter had a smell of its own now — coal smoke and wet stone and something under both that Mirabel could not name, a smell like a house where someone has died and no one has opened the windows. She moved through it with her collar up and her heart going like a shuttle in a loom, fast and mechanical and useless against the larger cloth.

Colette's bed had been empty when the bells rang seven. The blanket folded back with care, which was worse than disorder — disorder would have meant panic, and panic could be chased. Care meant decision. A fifteen-year-old girl had made her bed before she ran away to sell herself for their sake, and she had left the mended coat hanging on the hook because, Mirabel realized with a sickness in her stomach, Colette had not wanted to wrinkle it.

She had taken the letter. It was not even hidden well anymore; they had stopped hiding it from each other days ago, the way a family stops locking doors when there is nothing left inside worth more than trust. And Colette knew where Delorme lodged — of course she did. She had known everything for weeks. That was the arithmetic of children: you thought you were keeping them small, and all the while they were counting your coins over your shoulder.

Mirabel crossed the Place aux Herbes at a walk because running drew eyes. The market stalls were shuttered, but a few figures stood in doorways with the particular stillness of people who have learned that stillness is safety. A poster on the customs house wall had been half torn down in the night; what remained showed a printed eagle and the corner of a name list, and Mirabel did not let herself look at the corner. She had seen her father's name on enough walls this week. One more would not teach her anything except how much a person can bleed without spilling.

*She is fifteen*, Mirabel told herself. *Fifteen and carrying a letter heavier than any parcel I ever sewed.* And Delorme — what would Delorme do with a child who walked into his rooms offering contraband? A courteous man, a patient man, a man who wore his menace the way officers wore braid: as decoration, as grammar. He would receive her. He would make her tea. He would explain gently that the trade required an adult's signature, and then he would keep the letter and send the girl home with nothing, or worse than nothing, or—

She climbed the stairs of the Hôtel Perrin two at a time and made herself stop outside his door and breathe.

Through the wood came a sound she had not expected. Not voices. Not the low courteous murmur of negotiation. Paper. The dry whisper-slide of paper being stacked, folded, packed. And under it, once, the flat knock of a drawer closing on empty space.

She knocked. Three times, the way one knocks at a neighbor's, not a stranger's.

"Enter," said Delorme, and his voice had changed. She noticed it before she saw anything else. The courtesy was still there, but it sat differently in his mouth — less like a garment worn for others, more like a garment worn out.

* * *

He stood in the middle of the room amid open luggage, and for a moment Mirabel's mind refused the scene the way an eye refuses a wrong lens. She had built him so carefully these past weeks: the regimental friend, the seeker after papers, the patient hand behind polite threats. She had built him large, because fear builds everything large. What stood before her now was a tired man of perhaps fifty, in shirtsleeves, folding documents along creases worn soft by handling. Two valises, one of them small — the kind of case a person takes when they expect never to come back and cannot bear to admit it. His coat hung on the wardrobe door, and she saw with a professional's eye that it had been brushed and pressed within the hour, brushed the way one brushes a coat before a funeral.

"You came alone," he said. Not surprised. Almost relieved. "I hoped she'd have the sense to wait until— no." He closed his eyes briefly. "No, I hoped she wouldn't come at all."

"Where is she?"

"Sitting." He nodded toward the window alcove, and Mirabel's chest unlocked so suddenly it hurt.

Colette sat in the window seat with her hands folded in her lap and her chin up, the way she sat through dental appointments and sermons, performing bravery with every muscle. When she saw Mirabel her face went through three expressions in a second — guilt, relief, defiance — and settled on defiance, because Colette had never yet let anyone see her land anywhere else.

"I did it for us," she said.

"I know," said Mirabel.

"You weren't going to do anything. You were all going to argue until noon and then it would be too late, and I—" Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, Mirabel could see her hating it, hating her own fourteen years of throat. "I'm not a child."

"No," Mirabel said quietly. "You're not. That's exactly what frightens me."

Delorme had gone back to his packing while they spoke, and this too was information. A man mid-negotiation does not fold shirts. She watched him tuck a bundle of papers into the lining of the small valise — into a pocket sewn along the seam, and she recognized the stitchwork of it, the tight little whipstitch of a man taught by someone who knew cloth, and something turned over in her.

"You're leaving," she said.

"I am being left," Delorme said. "There is a distinction." He held up a printed page between two fingers, and even across the room she could read the layout of it — columns of names, the dense official typography that had become the city's new literature. "My name appeared last night. Third column, near the bottom. Apparently my services were rendered somewhat too enthusiastically for the incoming administration's taste. They are tidying. We are all being tidied, Madame Joubert — the ones who helped too much and the ones who helped too little, and the ones like your father who helped just exactly enough to be useful and not enough to be forgiven."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a floor tilts on a ship. "You're on the list," she said. "Your own list. The kind of list you've been hunting us for."

"My father's kind of list," Delorme corrected, and there it was — the thing underneath, surfacing at last like a body rising in water. He sat down heavily on the edge of a chair that still had wrapping paper on the seat. "Do you know what my father was? A customs clerk. Thirty-one years. He counted other men's cargo and kept his head down and taught me that the way to survive a state is to be furniture. Then the border war came, and the furniture was asked to stand up and point at neighbors, and he pointed, because the alternative was that they pointed at him, and he had a wife and a son. He informed on a shipping family named Vasseur. All of them went east. I was eleven years old when he told me, and he told me because he couldn't carry it alone anymore, and I have carried it forty years, and do you know what I learned?" He laughed, short and airless. "That it doesn't get lighter. That it gets heavier. Grief gets lighter. This only gets heavier."

Mirabel stood very still. Behind her, Colette had stopped performing bravery and was simply watching, the way children watch weather.

"The letter," Mirabel said. "My father's letter."

"In the valise. Unopened — no." He corrected himself with terrible honesty. "Opened. Read. But not used. Nothing copied, nothing sent. You can verify the seal—"

"The seal is broken."

"Yes. I broke it the first night. Forgive me. Or don't; forgiveness has stopped being currency here." He rose, took the letter from between two folded shirts, and held it out. "It's yours. It was always yours. I wanted it for the same reason you wanted to burn it — because paper is power, and I am a coward about power, always have been. My whole career is the biography of a careful man." His hand did not waver. "But I find, packing to run for my life, that I no longer want to spend whatever years remain carrying two families' names instead of one."

Mirabel took the letter. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the valise.

* * *

They sat, the three of them, in the wreckage of a departure — Delorme in his chair, Colette in the window seat, Mirabel on the trunk by the door because her legs had opinions of their own. Outside, somewhere below, a patrol went by with the particular unhurried rhythm of men who now belonged to the city rather than the city to them.

"He offered me two berths," Delorme said, unprompted, staring at the wall. "Yesterday. Before the list. Two berths on the noon sailing and a cleared file, for the letter and the names. I want you to understand the offer was real. It is worthless now — I have nothing left to trade with, no access, no favor. By tomorrow I'll be a man in a queue like everyone else." He looked at her. "But yesterday it was real, and I want you to know that when I made it, I was doing for my father exactly what you would have done for yours. Trading other people's futures for our dead men's reputations. That is the whole war, Madame Joubert, boiled down. Everyone trading someone else's future to buy back their dead."

"Did you take berths for yourself?" Colette asked. Her voice was smaller than she wanted it to be. "When you had them. Did you take them?"

"For whom?" Delorme smiled, and it was the saddest thing Mirabel had seen since the posters went up. "There is no one. There was a wife, once; she chose a country that still existed. No. I was going to trade the berths onward, actually — sell the favor up the chain, buy protection for myself. That was the plan. That was always the plan." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked like a customs clerk's son, like furniture, like any tired man in any collapsing year. "And then the list came out with my name in it, and I sat on this floor all night realizing that I had spent four months threatening a seamstress over a piece of paper that would not have saved me either."

"It might have saved us," Mirabel said.

"Yes. And I'd have slept beautifully." He met her eyes. "You don't believe me. Good. Don't. Believing men like me is how families end up with lists of their own."

Mirabel looked down at the letter in her lap. Her father's hand on the enclosure, slanting rightward, hurried — the hand of a man writing against something, though she had not known, when she first saw it, whether it was time or conscience. She had read it twice already, in the nights since Theo burned his patience arguing and she burned hers refusing; she knew every line of it, the admission, the old winter, the name Vasseur—

The name landed in her like a dropped needle.

"Vasseur," she said aloud.

Delorme went very still.

"My father informed on a shipping family," she said slowly. "To save us. Once, long ago. He wrote the name himself — it's in his letter, it's the whole reason for the letter, he needed someone to know what he'd done and why." She looked up. "What was your family name, before you were Delorme?"

For a long moment the only sound was the valise strap creaking in Delorme's grip.

"Baptiste," he said finally. "My mother's name. Delorme was my father's; I shed it at twenty-two when I understood what it cost to wear it. But yes." He said it the way a man says a diagnosis. "Yes. The Vasseurs. That was us."

The room held its breath. Colette's hand found Mirabel's without looking, cold fingers closing around hers, and Mirabel gripped back hard enough to hurt, because if she did not hold on to something she was going to rise off the trunk and float through the ceiling.

Two families, she thought. Two fathers, two winters, one arithmetic. A man points at a shipping family to save his wife and son; decades later his son hunts a dead tailor's papers to save a name; and in between, a seamstress mends uniforms for whichever uniform is in fashion, and calls it survival, and it is, and it isn't, and there are no winners, there was never going to be a winner, there is only who is left holding the paper when the music stops.

"We should hate each other," Mirabel said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "By every law I was raised with, we should."

"We should," Delorme agreed. "And yet here we sit, and I find I haven't the energy, and I suspect neither have you. Hatred requires a future, Madame Joubert. I'm told mine is abbreviated." He stood, buttoned his cuffs with fingers that trembled only slightly, and took up the small valise. At the door he paused, and did not turn around.

"The boy who runs messages past the bridge checkpoint — Luc. He was detained Tuesday. The new provost's office holds a list of detainees; your brother will know the format, couriers always know the formats." He said it flatly, a man paying a debt he could not repay any other way. "There is a clerk there named Anselme who owes me. Tell Theo to invoke the Perrin channel before Friday. After Friday there is no Perrin channel, and there is no me."

"Why?" Colette asked. "Why help him? Why give back the letter?"

Delorme considered the question with the seriousness he gave everything, and Mirabel understood, watching his back, that this man had been courteous his entire life precisely because courtesy was the only honesty available to him.

"Because my father pointed at your grandfather's family," he said, "and I have spent forty years pointing at everyone else, hoping the habit would look like duty. It doesn't. It looks like pointing." He opened the door onto the dim corridor. "Someone in this story has to stop."

Then he was gone, his footsteps going down the stairs light and quick, a man practiced at leaving.

* * *

Colette burst into tears the moment the door clicked shut — great ugly silent sobs, the crying of someone who had held it together past all reasonable accounting. Mirabel pulled her off the window seat and held her, and the girl shook against her shoulder and got snot on the good wool and Mirabel did not care, would never again care about wool.

"I thought if I brought it he'd take it and leave us alone," Colette gasped. "I thought I could fix it, I thought — you always say I'm clever, you and Theo both say it, so I thought—"

"Clever isn't armor," Mirabel said into her hair. "Clever is just a sharper knife to cut yourself on. Listen to me. Listen." She took the girl's face in her hands. "You crossed an occupied quarter alone to save us. That was brave and foolish and I will be angry about it for approximately the rest of my life, and I need you to hear the rest of the sentence: it was also love. Whatever happens next, whatever we decide at that table tonight — you acted out of love, and I will never punish anyone in this family for that."

"What happens next?" Colette whispered. "Is Papa's name still on the walls? Is it still — are we still—"

Mirabel looked at the letter in her lap, its broken seal, its confession in her father's hurried hand. Somewhere across the city Theo was sitting up with it tonight, she knew, the way he had every night, arguing with the dead. Somewhere south the ships waited with their counted berths. Somewhere near the bridge a boy named Luc sat in a room with a list of names, and the hours on that were shorter than the hours on the ships.

"Go home," Mirabel said. "Walk in front of me, not behind. And tell your brother—" she hesitated, feeling the night assemble itself ahead of them, feeling which way the wind had turned in her chest, though she could not yet have said why — "tell Theo I'm bringing the letter home. Tell him it's time we decided what fire is for."

Colette wiped her face on her sleeve, straightened her spine, and went to the door — and paused there, hand on the latch, looking back with an expression far older than fifteen.

"And the berths?" she asked. "If there aren't any more offers. If there's only ever the one. Who goes?"

Mirabel picked up the letter and held it against her chest like a bandage pressed to a wound.

"Ask me at dawn," she said. "I think I've known the answer longer than I've admitted."
