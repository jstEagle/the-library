## 15. Ashes in the Mending Room

Theo had never thought of himself as a violent man. He was a courier; his whole life had been built on the principle that paper should travel and not be harmed. Paper carried orders that fed armies and starved cities, yes — but it traveled intact. It arrived as what it was. That had been his one article of faith in a profession built on sealed flaps and wax stamps: *what I carry is not mine to change.*

He broke it at half past nine on a Thursday night, in the mending room, with a candle stub, a tin plate, and his sister's letter in his hands.

Mirabel sat across the worktable from him, her needle still, her thimble pushed back to the second knuckle of her forefinger like a small silver helmet abandoned mid-battle. Colette had gone to bed an hour ago — or at least they had heard the floorboards above them creak their way toward the little room under the eaves, which was the same thing as far as either sibling could prove. The stove ticked. Snow came down past the window in the fat, indifferent flakes of a city too tired to notice weather.

"Read it again," Mirabel said.

"You've read it four times."

"I want to hear how it sounds out loud."

"It sounds like a man confessing," Theo said, "to a crime we can't prove he committed, for reasons we can't undo, twenty years too late." He smoothed the page flat against the table anyway, because his hands wanted something to do and because she had asked. "Father wrote this before the war. Before Colette. Before any of it mattered to anyone but him."

"And then he kept it," Mirabel said. "For twenty years. In a shop where anyone could have found it."

"He kept it because he couldn't burn it and couldn't send it. I know that arithmetic. I've spent my whole life carrying things I couldn't open."

She looked up at him then, lamplight pooling in the hollows under her eyes, and Theo understood — not for the first time, but more completely than ever — that his sister had been doing the harder version of his job for years. He carried secrets across borders. She carried them across dinner tables.

"The list," she said.

He took the second sheet from inside his coat — the one they'd found folded into Father's old ledger at the shop, the duplicate, the one with Delorme's name appearing twice like a stain that wouldn't wash. Eleven names in all. Their father's own name circled at the bottom in ink that had browned to rust. He set it beside the letter and did not touch it again.

"I'm going to burn them," he said. "Both."

Mirabel's needle hand went still entirely.

"Theo—"

"Listen to me first. You always let me talk first and then you win, so tonight let me try." He stood, and the chair legs scraped, and somewhere above them Colette's floorboard answered with a single sympathetic groan. He lowered his voice without deciding to. "Names are only ink. You know this better than I do — you've spent years taking names off uniforms, stitching new ones over old ones, making men into other men with a length of thread. A name has exactly the power we give it. On paper, Father is a collaborator. In this room, he is a dead tailor who mended coats for free during the hunger winter and taught his daughter to set a sleeve so well that officers cross town to pay her for it. If this paper survives, the paper decides who he was. If it burns—" he gestured at the tin plate, the candle, the whole small arsenal of his intention— "then we decide. And I say we were there, and it wasn't, and we should get the vote."

"That isn't truth, Theo. That's editing."

"Yes," he said. "It is. And I am a courier, Mirabel. Editing is what I've watched governments do with history my entire adult life. They burn the archives when they fall and print new ones when they rise. The only difference between me and them is scale." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, hated the boyish break of it, and pressed on. "I'm not asking you to lie. I'm asking you to stop carrying this. You've been walking around for two weeks with your spine bent like the list is sewn into your coat. Let it go. Let it be ash. Ash doesn't testify."

Mirabel looked at the letter a long time. Then she looked at the stove — the iron door with its worn latch, the fire banked low for the night — and Theo saw her arrive somewhere ahead of him, some conclusion he hadn't reached, and come back changed by it.

"All right," she said.

He hadn't expected that. He had prepared arguments for the next hour, and she dismantled all of them with two syllables, and he stood there feeling like a courier whose satchel had been opened for him.

"Just like that?"

"Just like that." She rose, took down the good scissors — the ones with Father's initials scratched into the handle — and cut the letter cleanly in half, lengthwise, before he could react. Then the halves again. Then the list, once, decisively, through the middle of the eleven names. "If we're doing it, we're doing it properly. No half-burned pages for someone to piece together. I've read enough intercepted mail in this house to know how that ends."

"You've been reading intercepted mail?"

"I mend the pockets those letters ride in, Theo. Where do you think I learned discretion?" She gathered the pieces into the tin plate like a woman collecting scraps of fabric — briskly, without ceremony, because ceremony would make it real and realness was a luxury the household could no longer afford. "Stove or candle?"

"Stove. Candle smoke smells. And if Colette comes down—"

"She won't. Not tonight. Tonight she's learning that the world is larger than she thought, and children sleep hard after lessons like that." Something crossed her face when she said it — quick, private, gone — and Theo filed it away without understanding it, the way he filed everything about his sister: carefully, faithfully, meaning to examine it later and never finding the time.

* * *

The stove door opened onto orange light and the smell of yesterday's coal. Mirabel held the plate while Theo fed the pieces in one at a time, watching each fragment catch, curl, blacken, and lift briefly on its own heat before settling into ash. He did it slowly. He told himself it was thoroughness. It was not thoroughness.

Their father's handwriting went last — the salutation, *To my daughter Mirabel*, the words that had started all of this, the words that had turned a misdelivered envelope into a wound. Theo watched the ink bubble and vanish and felt nothing like relief. He felt the specific emptiness of a man who has finished a long journey and found no one waiting.

"There," Mirabel said softly. "That's done."

"That's done," he agreed.

They closed the stove door. The room returned to itself: lamp, worktable, bolts of cloth, the dress form in the corner wearing half a cavalry coat like a headless patient. Outside, the snow kept falling on the occupied quarter, on the posters, on the wall outside the shop where their father's name had hung in stenciled capitals for six days until rain softened it and someone — Theo had never learned who — had pastched a bill for a lost dog over the worst of it.

He sat down heavily. "I keep thinking about Delorme."

"Don't."

"He's fleeing now, did you hear? Marta at the chandlery says lights burning all night in his rooms, crates going down to the quay. Whatever list he was working from, the new regime has one of their own, and he's on it." Theo laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow as a struck pot. "The collaborator-hunter, hunted. Do you understand what that means? It means none of it meant anything. All that careful paper, all those names, ours included, his included — and the men who inherit it will burn it in a fortnight and start fresh. We could have spared ourselves the entire winter."

"No," Mirabel said.

He looked up.

"No, Theo. Don't you dare make this cheap after the fact. It cost us something. It cost *me* something, learning what Father did — learning what he thought he had to do. That wasn't paper. That was true whether it's written down or not. Burning the list doesn't unmake what he was any more than writing it made him that way." She pulled her thimble off entirely and set it on the table between them, a small silver fact. "I let you burn it because I love you, and because you needed it, and because some mercies are worth the price of a lie. But don't tell yourself it solved anything. Names only have power on paper? No. Names have power wherever people remember them. Paper is just the slowest kind of memory."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls a pipe knocked twice, the building's old complaint, and settled.

"What are you saying, Mirabel? Say it plainly. You're the only person in this family who can."

"I'm saying I memorized it."

The words landed in the room like dropped glass.

"Every name," she went on, level as a hem being measured. "All eleven. Aubry, Vasseur, Kellner-Loos, the rest. Delorme, twice — once as informant, once as the man who signed the second page. I read that list three times before you ever saw it, brother, and I have been carrying it in my head since the night we opened the envelope, because I am my father's daughter and my father taught us both that the safest archive is the one no one can seize." Her eyes were bright and dry and terrible in the lamplight. "You burned the paper. Good. Burn the guilt too, if you can manage it — truly, I hope you can, one of us should be free. But don't stand there telling me we've buried the truth, because the truth is standing right here holding a thimble."

Theo stared at her. Anger came first — hot, reflexive, the fury of a man who has just performed a sacrifice and been told the altar was empty. *You let me.* The phrase assembled itself in his mouth, accusatory, ready.

And then it fell apart, because he saw her face properly, and what was in it wasn't triumph. It was exhaustion. It was the look of a woman who had volunteered for the heavy end of a load and was telling him so not to shame him but so that he would stop trying to take it from her, because the taking cost more than the carrying.

"You absolute mule," he said, and his voice broke again, worse than before, and this time he didn't hate it.

"Family trait."

"We could have shared it."

"With what? Your honest face? You'd have told Delorme the names by Tuesday out of sheer inability to lie." But she smiled when she said it — small, crooked, the smile from before everything, from childhood, from the days when the worst thing in the world was a torn seam and the second worst was Theo's honesty — and Theo felt the fracture in them, the one that had opened over the berths and the burning and the long arithmetic of who leaves and who stays, close by a hair's width. Not healed. Closed enough to hold.

He got up, went to the shelf, and poured the last two fingers of the plum brandy Father had distilled the year Colette was born — saved through two Christmases and one occupation for no occasion good enough. This, apparently, was the occasion: two siblings, a stove full of ashes, and the complete text of their inheritance stored in one stubborn head.

"To Father," he said, raising the cup. "Tailor. Informant, maybe. Liar, certainly. Man who loved us badly and thoroughly, in that order most days."

"To Father," Mirabel echoed, and drank.

They passed the cup back and forth without speaking until it was empty, which did not take long, and Theo sat looking at the stove and thinking about memory — the paper kind and the living kind — and about how his sister had out-carried him at every stage of this catastrophe while letting him believe otherwise, out of kindness, out of strategy, out of whatever fierce economy governed her. He decided, in the end, to be honored instead of wounded. It was easier, and he had learned this winter that easy things were not therefore false.

"One thing," he said finally. "Delorme's name. The twice-name. When this is all ash and everyone's gone — when the century turns and there's a new flag and nobody remembers which lies were official — what will you do with it? With him? If he ever stands in front of you again?"

Mirabel was quiet for a moment. In the corner, the dress form seemed to lean in.

"I'll decide then," she said. "That's the whole point of carrying it instead of burning it, Theo. Ash forgives everyone at once. Memory gets to choose." She picked up her thimble, worked it back onto her finger, and took up the cavalry coat and its unfinished seam. "Now go to bed. Tomorrow we go to the docks, and whatever happens there, I want you rested enough to argue with me properly. One of us will need to lose gracefully."

He was halfway to the stairs when she spoke again, softer, almost to herself — but the mending room was small and the night was quiet and every word reached him clearly:

"Because tomorrow, Theo, somebody sails, and somebody stays, and I already know which one I am."
