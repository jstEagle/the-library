## 16. The Last Berth

The harbor had never smelled of anything but tar and herring and the particular rot of old rope, but on that last morning it smelled of fear, which has a scent of its own — sour, close, like a room where too many people have slept badly for too many nights.

Mirabel stood at the foot of the gangplank with Theo on her left and Colette on her right, and she thought: this is how it will be decided. Not in Delorme's rooms, not over the burned letter, not in any of the conversations they had rehearsed in whispers after Colette went to sleep. Here. On wet stone, under a sky the color of unwashed linen, with a clerk holding a clipboard and a ship's horn waiting to speak.

The *Aurore* rode low in the water, black-hulled, her paint blistered where some earlier life had left her in the sun. She was smaller than Mirabel had imagined. All week the evacuation had been spoken of as if the ship were an ark, vast and holy, capable of carrying away a whole civilization — the ministries, the archives, the paintings wrapped in canvas, the professors, the printers, the children. But from the quay she looked like what she was: one boat, one noon, one hundred and forty seats for a city of four hundred thousand people who had all, in the past ten days, discovered that they were indispensable.

They had queued since five o'clock. Around them the line shifted and murmured like something alive. A woman two places back held a birdcage with no bird in it; she had told anyone who would listen that the bird had died of the cold in November and that she could not bring herself to leave the cage behind, and Mirabel understood this completely. A man in a good coat that had been mended — she recognized her own stitching at the shoulder seam, done free of charge three winters ago for his wife's funeral — kept checking his papers with hands that trembled so badly he dropped them twice. Nobody laughed. There was no laughter left to spare.

"Joubert." The clerk said the name without looking up. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the ink-stained fingers of a man who had spent his whole career making lists that other people's lives depended on. "Theo Joubert. Courier class."

"That's me." Theo stepped forward. His voice was steady. Mirabel heard the steadiness and knew what it cost him, because she knew his voice the way she knew her own hands — every tremor, every false note, every place where it had been broken and sewn back together.

The clerk ran his finger down the page. Ran it again. Looked up, and for the first time actually saw them: three people standing close together the way families stand when they have already counted and already know the number is wrong.

"You're listed singly," the clerk said. "Courier status. One berth."

"There are three of us," Theo said. "My sister. Our ward. Colette is fifteen. She's a minor, she has a legal claim—"

"The legal claim applies to orphans unaccompanied by family," the clerk said. His tone wasn't cruel. That was the worst part. It was the flat, tired courtesy of a man who had said these words four hundred times since dawn and expected to say them four hundred more before the gangplank came up. "If she boards alone, she boards under the children's protocol. If she stays with you, she stays under your claim, and your claim is one seat. I don't make the arithmetic, citizen. I only write it down."

"One seat," Theo repeated.

"Noon sailing," the clerk said. "No extensions." And then, because he was human after all, or because he had a face somewhere in his memory that looked the way theirs did now, he added quietly: "Decide before eleven. The queue behind you won't wait, and neither will I."

He moved them aside to a stretch of railing near a bollard furred with old mooring ropes, and there they stood, the three of them, while the morning got on with its business around them. Crates went up the plank. A chaplain walked the length of the queue with his collar turned backward against the wind. Somewhere a loudspeaker coughed static and read out names in a bored official voice, and each time it did, someone in the crowd made a small sound — not quite a cry, not quite relief — and pushed forward out of the line forever.

Colette had hold of Mirabel's sleeve. She had been holding it since the tram stop, not pulling, just holding, the way you hold a rail on a dark staircase. Fifteen years old, and the war had put lines around her mouth that belonged to a much older woman. Luc's arrest, the posters, the wall outside the shop with their father's name on it — she had carried all of it in silence for six days, and now she carried this too, and Mirabel could feel the weight of it through the wool of her sleeve.

"I'll board alone," Colette said. "Under the children's protocol. I've thought about it. They take minors first anyway, everyone knows that, and then you two can—"

"No," Theo said.

"You didn't let me finish—"

"There's nothing to finish. You're not boarding alone. You're fifteen, Collette, and the crossing is nine days in winter water, and the reception camps—" He stopped himself. He was very good now at stopping himself; he had learned it the hard way, lying to Delorme in this same parlor where he had once never lied about anything. "No."

"Then you take it," Colette said. "You're the courier. They owe you. You gave them eleven years and your hands—" she took his right hand, the frostbitten one, the one whose fingertips had never quite come back to life, and held it up like evidence in court. "Look at them. They should carry you up that plank on a chair."

"And leave my sisters in a city that puts our name on walls?" Theo said gently. "Collette. Sweetheart. Think about what you're proposing."

"I think about it every hour," Colette said, and her voice cracked on the word *hour*, and she pressed her face against Mirabel's shoulder for a moment, briefly, fiercely, and then straightened because she was fifteen and proud and had learned from watching them that you do not weep in queues.

Mirabel said nothing.

She had known her answer for days. Perhaps longer than days — perhaps since the night she had knelt in their father's ruined shop among the scattered buttons and found the second list, and the letter addressed to her in his cramped, careful hand: *I informed to save you. I would do it again. Forgive me by living well.* Since then she had been walking toward this railing the way you walk toward a door you must open eventually, and now the door was here, and behind it was the arithmetic, and she was not afraid of the arithmetic. She had been a seamstress all her life. She knew exactly how much cloth there was and exactly how many bodies needed covering, and she knew, better than either of them, what it meant to work with insufficient material — to cut the lining smaller, to turn the collar twice, to make something whole out of what remained.

But she said nothing yet. Because Theo had to say his piece, and Colette hers, and love that is decided too quickly looks like it was never weighed at all. So she waited, and watched the gulls fight over the harbor filth, and felt the truth sitting inside her chest like a stone warmed by long carrying.

* * *

They argued for two hours. Not loudly — the queue was too exhausted for loudness, and grief in that city had learned to whisper — but thoroughly, in the way of people who know each other's arguments better than their own.

Theo went first, because he was eldest and because he believed, with a conviction Mirabel both loved and mourned, that being eldest meant something. "I'm the courier," he said, pacing the few feet the crowd allowed him. "Eleven years. I carried codes through checkpoints when the men who wrote them were already arrested. I have a service record, I have contacts on the other side, I can get work the day I land — I can send for you. Papers, money, a sponsor, whatever they require. Six months. Eight. I'll bring you across if I have to row."

"You can't even swim," Mirabel said, to give him something to argue against.

"I'll learn. I'll learn to swim and I'll learn the shipping laws and I'll stand on that dock on the far side every single day until your boat comes in." He stopped pacing. He took her shoulders, and his hands — the dead-fingered hand and the live one — were gentle. "Mira. Listen to me. I'm the one with the claim. It's clean, it's documented, nobody can dispute it. If I give it away, it goes to a stranger, and strangers don't send for their sisters. Family sends for family. That's the whole point of having one."

"That's the whole point," Mirabel agreed. "So stay."

"I can't stay and be useful. You can't cross and be useful. One of us has to be the bridge, and I'm the one they'll let onto the bridge."

"You're the one who *thinks* he's the bridge," Mirabel said. "Theo. Be honest with yourself for one minute, the way you were honest with Delorme. Your courier protection ended when the government did. The man at that desk isn't honoring your service — he's clearing a ledger before he burns it. If you sail alone, you'll spend the crossing writing letters, and the letters will come back, because occupied ports don't take mail from the people who left, and you know it. You carried the codes. You know exactly what happens to communication lines when a regime falls." She softened it as best she could, which was not very. "You'd be writing letters without addresses for the rest of your life."

She saw the words land. Saw him flinch and refuse to flinch.

"Then Colette," he said, turning, desperate, reasonable. "She's a minor. The children's protocol is real, Mira, I checked it twice this week — unaccompanied minors get priority placement, foster sponsorship, schooling. She'd have the best chance of anyone in this queue. Better than mine. Better than yours."

"She's fifteen."

"She's fifteen and she smuggled intelligence across a checkpoint for love of a boy and never got caught until the world caught her. She's tougher than both of us."

"She is," Mirabel said, "and that is exactly why we don't put her on a boat alone. Because she'll survive it. She'll survive it and she'll spend the rest of her life believing it was her punishment — for Luc, for the letter, for everything. You weren't at Delorme's rooms, Theo. You didn't see her face when she thought trading herself away was the only thing left she could do for us. We are not teaching that child that she is the price we paid. We are not doing it."

Colette, who had been standing at the railing with her back to them, pretending to watch the ship, turned around. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious.

"Stop talking about me like I'm luggage," she said. "I'm standing right here. I can hear you deciding things." Her chin came up — the Joubert chin, their father's chin, the stubborn shelf of bone that Mirabel saw every morning in her own mirror. "Here's what I think. I think Theo sails. I think he's right about the bridge. And I think Mira—" and here her voice did break, finally, all at once, like ice going out in spring, "—I think Mira thinks she's the one who doesn't count. Because she's not a courier and she's not a minor. Because she's the one who's always the leftover, the one who does the mending, the one who stays. Well, I won't do it. I won't be saved at the cost of her. I'd rather drown in this harbor than—"

"Enough." Mirabel said it quietly, and quietness, in their family, had always carried further than shouting. Both of them stopped. Even the queue seemed to lean in.

She looked at her brother. She looked at her sister. Behind them the *Aurore* blew off steam in a long white sigh, impatient, indifferent, beautiful in the way of large machines that will keep their schedules whether or not the world deserves them. The clock on the customs house said twenty past ten.

"I've already decided," Mirabel said.

"Mira—"

"Let me say it once, all the way through, because I've earned that much and I intend to have it." She kept her voice level. Her hands, she noticed with distant interest, were perfectly still — the hands of a woman doing fine work, which was, after all, what she was doing. "One berth. Three of us. Someone crosses and two stay. You've each argued for the others, which is very noble and completely useless, because neither argument changes the numbers. So look at the numbers plainly, the way Papa taught us to look at a garment before we cut. Theo: if you stay, you're a former courier of a dead government in an occupied city, and your name is next to ours on enough paper to hang us twice. If you go, you might save yourself and nothing else. Collette: if you go alone, you arrive fatherless, motherless, sisterless, brotherless, into a camp that processes children like coal. If you stay, you grow up in a city that has already written your family's name on a wall. And me—" she smiled, and was surprised to find the smile real, "—me, they ask nothing. I'm not a courier. I'm not a minor. I have no claim and no category and no value to any government, dead or living. Which means, my darlings, that I am the only one of us who loses nothing by staying, except the thing we've all been pretending we wouldn't have to count."

"The thing being—" Theo began.

"Everything," Mirabel said. "Everything except you two. And I find I'm willing to trade everything else."

"It's not fair," Colette whispered.

"No," Mirabel said. "It's arithmetic. Fairness is a country none of us lives in anymore. Now listen to me, both of you, because we have fifty minutes and I need them." She took Colette's hand and Theo's and drew them close, the three of them forehead to forehead above the harbor smell, and spoke fast and low, the way their father had taught them to memorize patterns — not the words but the shape of the words, the shape of what had to be done.

Because she had not told them everything. She had not told them what was folded flat against her ribs, sewn into her own coat that morning in the dark of the mending room: Delorme's final papers, returned unfinished, bearing his signature and his seal and the clearance stamp of a ministry that had fallen but whose stamps still worked on clerks too tired to check. Two names' worth of safe passage, signed by a man who had fled in the night, addressed to no one — blank in the way that mattered.

She had not told them which name she intended to write on it.

She looked at Colette's frightened, furious, fifteen-year-old face, and she thought of the letter in her father's hand: *Forgive me by living well.* And she understood, with a calm that felt almost like happiness, that honesty had limits — that there was one dishonesty left in her, one single seam sewn crooked on purpose, and that she would sew it beautifully, invisibly, so well that no one, not even God looking closely at the lining, would ever see where it had been done.

"All right," Theo said hoarsely, reading her face and understanding less than half of it, which was the plan. "All right, Mira. Then we decide who sails. Together. Right now."

"Yes," Mirabel said. "Together. Right now."

And the clock on the customs house ticked toward eleven, and the gangplank waited, and in the lining of her coat, against her heart, the blank paper waited too.
