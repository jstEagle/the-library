## 17. What Mirabel Decided

The forgery took her forty minutes, and it was the finest work of her life.

Mirabel had told herself all night that she would not do it. She had said so aloud, to the lamp, to the cold stove, to the letter and the list and the two berths sitting on the table like a debt come due. *I will not lie. Not now. Not with the truth finally in our hands.* But honesty, she had learned over one long winter of dead ministers and wet ink, was a luxury stitched for people whose fathers had not informed, whose brothers were not couriers of a government negotiating its own funeral, whose sisters did not have until noon.

She sat at the mending table with Colette's travel papers spread flat before her and Delorme's cleared name written out twice on a scrap of the man's own stationery — he had left it behind in his haste, along with his seal impression pressed into a stub of wax, and Mirabel had thought at the time: *what a careless man.* Now she understood it was not carelessness. It was an inheritance. He had given her the tools without knowing it, the way her father had given her his hands, his patience, his terrible capacity for doing wrong in the shape of right.

The name was Delorme. Not the father — the son's own clearance, earned or inherited, stamped by an office that would be rubble by spring. She practiced first on brown paper, matching the slant, the pressure, the little hesitation in the crossbar of the capital D that she remembered from the visitor's book at the tailor shop, where Henri Delorme had signed himself in as a regimental friend eleven days before everything broke open. She had watched him write. That was the whole trick of forgery, her father used to say — you must love the hand you are stealing. You must know how it hesitates.

*You must know how it hesitates.* Her father had known. All those years, signing receipts and invoices in the shop on Rue Verte while he carried a list of names in his chest like swallowed glass — he had known exactly where his own hand trembled, and he had hidden the trembling inside the most ordinary strokes. Mirabel pressed the pen down and let her hand shake where Delorme's shook, and the false name came up perfect and breathing.

She worked by lamplight because the shutters were painted black now, every window in the city wearing its mourning band. Outside, the harbor bells rang the hours in a new sequence — three short, one long — which everyone had agreed to pretend meant nothing. The ink needed to dry. The papers needed to look handled, folded, worried at the edges by a clerk who had processed a thousand of them. She crumpled each sheet gently in her fist, smoothed it, crumpled it again. She rubbed graphite into the fold with her thumb. She even, after a moment's hesitation, touched the corner of the page to her tongue and blotted it faintly, because documents that have never met a human mouth are documents that have never been human.

Then she woke Colette.

It was still dark. The girl came down the narrow stair with her hair loose and her shoes in her hand, the way children learn to move through frightened houses, and when she saw the papers on the table she stopped as if the floor had ended.

"Those are mine," Colette said. "Theo said—"

"Theo says many things." Mirabel held them out. "Look at your name."

Colette looked. Her lips moved through it once, silently, and then again, and then her face began the slow collapse that Mirabel had been dreading and engineering all night — the understanding arriving not all at once but in stages, like water finding its level. "This isn't my name."

"It is now." Mirabel kept her voice level, workroom voice, the voice she used for telling customers their coats could not be saved. "Delorme's clearance is real. His papers are real. The office that stamped them won't exist long enough to check anything, and no one checks a girl anyway. They check men. They check couriers and soldiers and men with lists. A fifteen-year-old with clean papers walks onto a ship like weather."

"I'm not going." Colette set the papers down as if they were hot. "I'm not leaving you both. I did this — I told Luc about the letter, I—"

"You told a boy you loved something true," Mirabel said. "That isn't the crime this winter. Everything else is." She took Colette's hands. They were small hands, needle-scarred already from two years of helping in the shop, hands that should have been learning piano or nothing at all. "Listen to me. There is one berth. One. Theo has the courier claim, you have the minor's claim, and I have neither, and I am not going to stand at a gangplank at noon and perform arithmetic in front of strangers. I've decided. This is not a discussion we're going to have."

"You can't just decide—"

"I'm the eldest daughter of a man who decided things for us our whole life," Mirabel said, and heard her own voice go strange and quiet. "He decided to inform. He decided to hide it. He decided what we would carry without asking if we wanted to carry it. So yes — I can decide. And what I've decided is that you get on that ship, and you live somewhere the century hasn't happened yet, and you become whatever you were going to become before the border war took your parents and this city took everything else."

Colette was crying now, the silent kind, the kind she had taught herself in some other household before this one. "And you? What do you become?"

"Someone who mends uniforms," Mirabel said. "For whoever governs. It turns out that's a trade that survives anything."

They stood there holding hands across the table, across the forged name, across the burned letter's absence — because Theo had burned the letter two nights ago, and Mirabel had let him, and had memorized every name first, and none of them had spoken of it since. The stove ticked. Somewhere below the black windows the city went on being occupied, quietly, the way snow occupies a field.

"There's something else," Mirabel said, before her courage could cool. "There's something I have to tell you, because if I don't tell you now I'll keep it my whole life, and I promised myself this family would stop keeping things." She breathed. "Your papers. I didn't steal them fair. I wrote that name myself. Every honest bone in my body is screaming about it, and I did it anyway, and I want you to know that. Because someday you'll be tempted to lie for someone you love, and you'll remember that I did it too, and I need you to remember it wasn't free. Nothing I've done this winter was free."

Colette looked at her for a long moment. Then she picked the papers back up and held them against her chest the way you hold something that might still be taken away.

"It was free for me," she said. "That's what matters. Isn't it? Somebody paid, but it was free for me."

Mirabel laughed — a short, broken sound she hadn't made in weeks. "Yes," she said. "That's exactly what it means. Go dress. Wear the gray coat. And Colette—" the girl paused at the stair, "—when you're across the water, don't be careful with your life. Be generous with it. We spent enough on it."

* * *

The docks at noon were a country of their own.

They had sprung up overnight, it seemed — the barricades, the document tables, the soldiers of a republic checking papers with the diligence of men guarding the last exit of a burning theater. The evacuation ship sat low and gray in the harbor, funnels breathing, and around her the crowd moved in that particular shuffling density of farewell: carts abandoned at angles, a piano someone had tried to bring and given up on, standing alone on the quay like a drowned thing washed ashore. Children were lifted over heads. An old man argued with a clerk about a name spelled differently on two documents, and the clerk's face had gone past anger into stone.

Theo carried Colette's single bag. Mirabel walked between them with her hands in her pockets, and in her pocket her fingers found the spare needle she always carried there, and she held it the whole way like a talisman, like a profession, like the smallest possible weapon.

At the gangplank the officer barely glanced at the papers. He looked at Colette's face, and at Theo's, and at Mirabel's, and something in his expression suggested he had stopped believing in documents entirely and was simply admitting whoever looked like they could not bear to stay. "Delorme," he read aloud, flat. "One berth. Board."

And here was the moment Mirabel had rehearsed all night and still could not make gentle. She took Colette's face in her hands. The girl was trying not to cry and losing, beautifully, terribly.

"You'll write letters," Mirabel said. "Even if they can't reach us. Write them anyway. Put them somewhere safe. Because addresses come back. Cities come back. I'll find them."

"How will you—"

"I mend things," Mirabel said. "That's what I do. Go."

Colette hugged Theo first, hard, and then Mirabel, and then she was moving up the plank with the bag and the gray coat and the name that belonged to another family's ruin, turning once at the top to look back — and Mirabel raised her hand, and held it raised, and did not lower it until the deck had swallowed her sister whole.

Then she turned to Theo, ready for the second half of the arithmetic, and found him not looking at her at all.

He was looking at the gangplank, where a commotion had started — a young woman, hollow-eyed, clutching a bundle wrapped in a soldier's blanket, arguing with a clerk in a voice scraped down to wire. The bundle cried. The clerk shook his head and pointed at the manifest, at the word FULL, at the order of the universe itself. "No berth. No name on the list, no berth. Next."

"They gave me a promise—" the woman was saying. "My husband's regiment—"

"A promise," the clerk repeated, without cruelty and without mercy, which was worse than either.

Mirabel saw it happen. She saw it the way you see weather change — Theo's shoulders, his breath, the courier's reflexes waking in him one last time. He stepped forward, and he said, in the clear carrying voice of a man who had spent six years delivering other people's words and knew exactly how much weight a voice could bear:

"Take mine."

The clerk looked up. "Courier berth, section four. Yours?"

"Mine," Theo said. "Joubert. Take it off the list and put the child on. The mother can board when a place opens at the next port — she'll fit anywhere; the infant needs the crossing warm." He was already unpinning his credential disc, the brass circle that had hung at his throat through frost and checkpoints and the slow death of everyone who had issued it, and he set it in the clerk's palm like a man paying a debt.

"You understand what you're surrendering," the clerk said. "Protection status. Priority on any future manifest. This disc is worth more than your name right now."

"Yes," Theo said. "I know precisely what it's worth. That's why I'm spending it here."

The woman was staring at him. "Monsieur, I can't—"

"You can," Theo said. "You'll feed him well and forget my face. That's the whole price." And then, softer, almost to himself: "Someone else's child crossed on my father's sins. Let mine balance it."

The bundle was transferred. The woman was waved toward the side ramp. The clerk scratched out Joubert and wrote nothing in its place, because the list was closed, because the arithmetic of the republic had finally finished dividing itself down to nothing.

Mirabel took her brother's arm. "You absolute fool," she said, and her voice cracked straight through the middle of it. "We could have drawn lots. We could have—"

"We could have," Theo agreed. "And one of us would have gotten on the ship and spent thirty years wondering why the other one's face was the last thing they saw on this quay. I'd rather wonder nothing. I'd rather stand here with you and watch that ship go and know that the last thing this republic did for me was carry a baby I'll never meet across the water." He exhaled, long, and she felt the six years leave him all at once, the codes and satchels and sealed doors draining out of his spine. "Besides. You're staying. Someone has to teach me to run the shop."

"You don't know the first thing about tailoring."

"I know how to keep accounts," he said. "And you know how to do everything else. It's the same marriage every family makes eventually."

Out in the harbor, the ship's horn sounded — one long note that rolled across the blacked-out city and came back changed. The gangplank groaned up. Lines snapped loose, coiling into the water and rising dripping. Along the rail, small figures pressed to the gap, and Mirabel searched for the gray coat and found it — third porthole from the bow, a small hand raised, waving, waving, not stopping.

"She'll be all right," Theo said. "Look at her. She's already giving orders to that sailor next to her."

Mirabel laughed and cried at once, badly, in public, not caring. The ship gathered itself against the dock, kissed the pilings, and slid. The wake opened behind it like a page being turned. On the quay, hundreds of people stood watching the water take their families away, and nobody spoke, because there was nothing left to say that the horn hadn't already said.

"She has the letter's silence," Mirabel said at last. "She has the name we gave her. And she has—" her hand went to her breast pocket, where a folded scrap of paper rode over her heart: the first line of a letter she had begun at dawn and could not finish, addressed to nowhere. "She has the beginning of an answer, whenever she comes looking."

"And us?"

Mirabel watched the ship shrink. Gray water, gray sky, the smoke thinning to a pencil line and then to nothing. Behind them the occupied city waited with its posters and its curfews and its shopfronts to be reopened under neutral signs. In her memory, in the vault no fire could touch, the list of names sat complete and quiet — Delorme among them, and her father, and all the others, waiting to be forgiven or avenged or simply, someday, understood.

"Now," she said, taking her brother's arm, turning them both toward the Rue Verte, "we find out what a person builds instead."
