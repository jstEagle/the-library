## 18. The Century Turns

The bells began at midnight, and Theo counted them the way he had once counted telegraph clicks — one for each year of a century that had ended without asking anyone's permission.

He stood at the window of the flat above the shop with the lamp shuttered and the curtain drawn to a finger's width, watching the occupied city celebrate its own surrender. Snow came down in fat, indifferent flakes, catching the light of the few lamps still permitted to burn. Somewhere across the river, a firework went up — green, then gold — and he thought: they have money for fireworks. They have money for fireworks and we have flour rationed by the spoonful. The thought arrived without bitterness. Bitterness, he had learned over these past months, was a currency that devalued faster than the republic's own paper.

Behind him, Mirabel sat at the table with her hands wrapped around a cup of chicory she wasn't drinking. She wore her coat indoors. They both did. The stove ate coal like a starving thing, and the coal merchant had begun to look at them sideways since the posters, since the name on the wall outside the shop, since everything. Sharing one coat between two people was a phrase Theo had heard once in a song his father used to hum — *we'll share one coat when winter comes* — and he had understood it as romance then. He understood it now as arithmetic. One person's body heat, given freely to another, is nearly half again as warm as two separate shiverings. His sister knew this without ever having said it aloud. She simply arranged herself near him in the evenings, shoulder to shoulder inside the same grey wool, and neither of them called it anything.

"Twelve," Mirabel said, when the last bell faded. "It's done."

"It's not done," Theo said. "It's only turned."

"That's what I said."

"No." He turned from the window. The room was small enough that turning from the window meant facing her fully; there was nowhere else to face. "You said it's done. It isn't. The new regime prints its lists every week. Delorme is gone — where, we don't know. Your handwriting is on a set of papers that shouldn't exist. And tomorrow morning you intend to take down the old sign and put up a new one as though none of it happened."

Mirabel looked at him over the rim of the cup. Her face in the lamplight — the good lamp, the one they saved — had the particular patience of someone who has already won an argument and is waiting for the other party to arrive at the same conclusion.

"What would you do instead?" she asked. "Hide? We tried hiding. Hiding is how Father got where he got. A man who makes himself invisible long enough forgets which parts of himself he cut away to do it."

Theo opened his mouth and closed it. This was the trouble with arguing with Mirabel: she did not argue to win, she argued to be right, and those were different sports entirely.

"The sign," he said instead. "Tell me about the sign."

She reached beneath the table and brought out the board. He had seen it taking shape for weeks — her painting after hours, lettering in a hand so plain it looked printed — but tonight it lay finished under a cloth, and she drew the cloth off the way a magician draws off a table, with a little flourish he hadn't seen from her since before the war at the border, before Colette, before the letter.

**Joubert & Fils — Tailoring and Repairs of Every Kind.**

*Fils.* Son. Their father had always been Joubert & Fils, though there had never been a fils working the counter — only Mirabel, doing all the work and none of the naming. Theo felt something twist in his chest, part grief and part fury at the grief, because even now, even dead, their father was spending Mirabel's labor under his own name.

"You kept *Fils*," he said carefully.

"I kept what the street expects," Mirabel said. "A tailor named Joubert is furniture. A seamstress named Joubert, alone, unattached, is a question. Questions get answered by people with clipboards." She smoothed the painted letters with her thumb. "Besides. It's true enough. You'll be behind the counter."

"I'm a courier."

"You were a courier. There's no government left to courier for. There's no government left to arrest you for having couried for, either, if we're honest, but I'd rather your history stay folded in a drawer than walked past a window every day." She looked up at him. "Theo. It's neutral. That's the whole point. Uniforms for whoever governs. Buttons for whoever marches. We are not for anyone. We are for everyone's sleeves."

*We are not for anyone.* He heard it and heard, underneath it, everything she was not saying: *not for Father, not against Father, not for the republic, not for whatever comes next.* His sister had spent the autumn learning the one skill the century demanded — how to belong to nothing without being destroyed by it — and she had learned it faster than he had, faster than the whole city.

"Colette should see this," he said.

Something crossed Mirabel's face then, quick as a bird's shadow — there and gone, but he caught it, because he had spent thirty years learning to read his sister the way he'd once read dispatches. Colette was in Le Havre by now, or aboard a ship bound past it, wearing papers in a name Mirabel had forged with a steadier hand than she'd ever shown on an invoice. Colette was a hundred miles or a thousand miles away, depending on the sea. Colette was a fourth guest at a dinner table that now seated two.

"She'll see it," Mirabel said, "when she sees it," and that was all, and it was everything, and they let it lie between them like the folded coats they could no longer afford to keep separate.

* * *

They hung the new sign at first light on New Year's Day, and the cold was the kind that made metal sing.

Theo stood on the ladder with frost biting through his gloves while Mirabel held the base steady, and together they bolted the board over the place where the old sign had hung — the old sign, which had come down in October along with the door glass, which had been shattered by boys who had read their father's name on a wall and decided the shop owed the neighborhood a debt. Theo remembered standing in this same doorway that night with a broom handle in his grip, watching Madame Ferreuil from the corner house look directly at him and then directly away, and understanding that the silence of neighbors is louder than any mob. He had hated them all, briefly and completely. Then Madame Ferreuil's grandson had brought a pane of glass to the door three days later, saying nothing, setting it down, leaving, and Theo had hated less completely. The ordinary world, he had learned, betrays you and mends you in the same afternoon, often without acknowledging either.

The new sign went up without incident. A woman from the bakery across the way watched them work, arms folded against the cold, and when the board swung level she nodded once — the nod of someone approving a change of awning, nothing more. Furniture, Theo thought. We are furniture. God, let us be furniture until the century learns some manners.

Inside, the shop smelled of chalk and steam and the ghost of their father's pipe, which no amount of scrubbing had dislodged from the floorboards. Mirabel lit the big iron and began pressing a stack of officer's coats — new officers now, the occupation's men, their grey-green wool coarser than the republic's blue had ever been. She pressed them the way she pressed everything: with a care so complete it looked like love and was, Theo suspected, something more complicated. Care as craft. Care as armor. Each seam she laid flat was a seam no inspector would look twice at.

He took up the ledger and ruled fresh lines. *Joubert & Fils.* His hand hesitated on the page. Under the heading, in the space where their father had once recorded every commission, every debt, every name — including, somewhere in the back pages, in some season Theo had been too young to notice, the names of men who had later appeared on other lists — Theo wrote the day's date and beneath it: *2 officer coats, pressed. 1 civilian overcoat, reline. Paid.*

Small transactions. Honest arithmetic. A life built out of entries no one would ever need to burn.

At noon a soldier came in with a torn sleeve and a broken button, and Mirabel mended it while he waited, and thanked her in accented French, and left a coin on the counter that was worth perhaps half what the work deserved, and Theo watched his sister accept it with a bow that gave away nothing. Not for anyone. For everyone's sleeves. When the bell above the door had finished swinging he said:

"You're better at this than I am."

"At what?"

"At being nobody."

Mirabel set down the iron. Steam curled up between them. "I'm not nobody," she said. "I'm everybody's tailor. There's a difference, and the difference is that I remember everyone." She picked the iron back up. "That's the inheritance, if you want one. Not the list. Not the name. The remembering. Somebody in this family has to hold all of it, and Father held it wrong, and Grandfather held it wrong, and I have decided — " she pressed the iron down, hard, and lifted it, and the wool lay perfect — "I have decided to hold it right."

Theo looked at his sister across the steam and understood that she had already written the next fifty years in her head, the way she once chalked a garment's alterations before touching the cloth. And he understood, too — with the relief and the grief arriving braided together, indistinguishable, exactly as they had arrived all autumn — that the family was smaller now, two people and a stove, and larger than it had ever been, reaching across water to a girl with forged papers and forward into a century that had not yet learned their address.

The bell above the door rang again at dusk.

Theo looked up from the ledger expecting the bakery woman, or a soldier, or snow blown in off the step. Instead a girl stood in the doorway, fifteen or sixteen, hood up, cheeks red with cold, holding a parcel wrapped in brown paper against her chest the way you hold something you've carried a very long way.

"We're closed," Mirabel began — and then stopped, because the girl pushed back her hood, and the lamplight found her face, and Theo felt the ledger slide from his knee to the floor.

"Madame," the girl said, in an accent that was not quite the city's, "I was told — someone told me you mend things. No one else would take it."

She held out the parcel. Her hands were shaking, and not only from the cold.

Mirabel set down the iron and came around the counter, and Theo saw his sister's face do the thing it did — the closing and opening at once, the tailor assessing damage before deciding what could be saved.

"Show me," Mirabel said.
