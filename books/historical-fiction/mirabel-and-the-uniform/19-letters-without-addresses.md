## 19. Letters Without Addresses

The sea took three days to forgive them, and even then it never quite did.

Colette learned this in the second week of the crossing, when the gray water finally went flat and the other passengers came up from the hold blinking like moles, and the horizon showed them a coast that was not their coast. She had spent those first days below with her cheek against a bench that smelled of tar and sick, holding the name of home in her mouth the way you hold a pebble under your tongue — not to eat it, only to know it was still there. *Mirabel. Theo. Rue Verte.* If she said them silently enough times they would still be true on the far side of the water.

They were not true. That was the first lesson.

The city that received her was loud in the wrong language and bright in the wrong way, all awnings and brass and gulls screaming over fish stalls where women argued prices with hands as much as mouths. It had never been bombed, or if it had, it had been long ago and by someone forgiven. Children ran through fountains in October. Colette stood at the edge of the fountain square with her single cardboard suitcase and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down her spine, that she had been evacuated into a place where nobody was afraid.

She was fifteen years old and she had crossed a war to arrive at peace, and peace turned out to be the loneliest country she had ever visited.

* * *

The woman who took her in was called Madame Ferrand, though everyone said *Tante Rose*, and she ran a laundry behind the Church of Saint-Éloi that steamed day and night like a small industrial heart. Tante Rose was sixty, built like a dock crane, and had lost two sons to a war twenty years older than this one, which meant she recognized the look in Colette's face before Colette had spoken ten words of it.

"You'll sleep in the attic room," she said, not unkindly, handing her an apron already soft from another girl's use. "You'll wash, press, sort. You'll eat what we eat. You'll write your letters on Sundays and I won't ask who they're for." A pause, the kind that contained a whole biography. "Everyone here is from somewhere they can't go back to, little one. You'll learn the trade."

"The trade," Colette repeated.

"Survival." Tante Rose said it the way Mirabel said *seamstress* — as though it were a thing one could be good at, and take pride in being good at. "It's got apprenticeships same as anything else. First year you'll be useless. Second year you'll be useful. Third year you'll start teaching somebody else."

Colette wanted to say she was already useful, thank you; that she had run intelligence through an occupied quarter for months; that she had carried notes sewn into coat linings past checkpoints while the women of this steamy, safe, unbombed city were arguing about fish. But the words died in her throat, because looking around the laundry — at the Polish girl with the burned hands, at the old man who sorted collars and never spoke, at Tante Rose herself — she saw that every person in the room had a story shaped exactly like hers and none of them told it either.

So she took the apron. She washed. On the first morning she scalded her wrist; by the third week her hands moved in the rhythms of the mangle without her mind's attendance, which left her mind free, which was both a mercy and a danger, because a free mind goes home whether you tell it to or not.

* * *

She wrote the first letter on a Sunday in November, sitting on the attic floor with the paper balanced on a bread board, and discovered that she had no address to put at the top.

That was the cruelty of it that nobody warned you about: the letters weren't forbidden, they were *impossible*. The occupied quarter received no civilian post; the ports were closed; the censors of two governments sat between her ink and her sister's lamp. She could write *Dear Mirabel* and mean it with her whole chest, and the letter would still be a bottle thrown into a locked harbor.

She wrote it anyway.

*Dear Mirabel. Today I learned to press a uniform collar so it stands up on its own, which you would have done better and faster and without burning yourself once. There is a boy here named Émile who works the docks and has a laugh like Luc's but isn't Luc, and when he laughed yesterday I nearly cried in front of forty baskets of linen, so I told him I had soap in my eye. Don't tell Theo I'm crying. Don't tell Theo anything. Tell Theo I said his chess is sloppy and I've been practicing. Tell him —*

She stopped there, pen hovering, because *tell him* was a door and everything behind it was dark. Tell Theo what? That she had taken the letter? That she had crossed half a city alone believing she could fix with one desperate trade what she had broken with a dozen careless sentences? That she knew — she had known since the night she slipped out with the letter inside her coat — that everything after had bent around her mistake like iron around a mis-strike?

No. Some confessions were too heavy for paper. Paper could be read by anyone; that was the whole point of paper. So instead she wrote:

*I am learning the trade. You always said I'd be good at something stubborn. This is it.*

She folded the letter into quarters, then eighths, until it was a hard little seed of paper, and she didn't know what to do with it, and she sat with it in her cupped palms like a bird with a broken wing for the rest of the evening.

* * *

The smuggler found her before she found him, which she would later understand was how smugglers worked.

He came to the laundry on a wet Tuesday to collect a bundle that was not laundry — Colette saw it change hands in the back room, oilcloth-wrapped, heavy, and she saw Tante Rose's face while it changed hands, and Tante Rose's face was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she had done a thousand times and hated every time. Afterward the old man who sorted collars touched Colette's elbow, the first time he had touched anyone in living memory, and jerked his chin toward the alley door.

His name, she eventually learned, was Anselme, and he had been a ship's cook before the wars made shipping a crime with extra steps. He spoke perhaps forty words a week and used them like coins. In the alley, in the rain-smell and the fish-smell, he looked at her with eyes the color of dishwater and said:

"Tante Rose says you write letters."

"I write letters."

"Letters need addresses."

"They need more than addresses," Colette said. "They need a miracle with a boat."

Something moved at the corner of Anselme's mouth, which in his currency was laughter. He reached into his coat — slowly, the way you move around frightened animals and teenage girls — and produced a letter, worn soft at the folds, addressed in a looping hand to a town she'd never heard of in a country whose name had changed twice since the address was written.

"My daughter," he said. "In Marseille. Then Lyon. Then nowhere. I have written her every Sunday for six years." He turned the letter over. "I send them anyway."

"Where do they go?"

"Nowhere, mostly. Sometimes somewhere." He shrugged, a whole philosophy in one motion. "There are boats that go out for sardines and come back with other things. There are men on those boats who read what they carry, because men are men. But the letters go. And one time—" he held up one finger, and Colette saw that the finger was crooked, an old break badly set— "one time, an answer came back."

Colette's heart did something complicated and painful. "One time."

"One time is enough to keep a man writing for six years." He tucked the letter away again. "Sunday. Bring yours. Don't seal them — the men on the boats don't like breaking seals, it feels like theft to them. Leave them open like honest mail and they'll read and pass them along like honest mail." He turned to go, then paused. "And girl. Write nothing you couldn't survive a stranger knowing. That's the whole art of it."

* * *

She became good at the art faster than she expected, and it surprised her to discover that being good at it felt like power.

The trick, she worked out over the winter, was layering. The surface letter was for the smugglers: weather, laundry, the price of soap, a fictional aunt with a cough. Beneath it, in the spaces between lines, lived the real letter — coded not in cipher, which she didn't trust herself to invent well enough, but in *meaning*, in the private language of a household of three. When she wrote *Theo's chess is improving*, she meant *I think of my brother daily and fear he blames himself*. When she wrote *the shop on the corner reopened under a new sign*, she meant *I know what you two must be surviving and I am proud and ashamed in equal measure and I cannot say so directly because strangers' eyes are on these lines*. When she wrote *I dream of the fountain in the square at home*, she meant simply that, and let it stand naked on the page, because some things needed no armor.

Every Sunday she walked to the harbor wall with Anselme or alone, once she'd earned the route, and handed her letter to men with salt-cracked hands who read them openly, sometimes aloud, laughing at the surface letter's invented aunt and seeing, she was nearly certain, nothing of the letter underneath. And every Sunday the letter went into an oilcloth packet, and the packet went onto a boat that pretended to fish, and the boat went out into a gray sea toward a coast that might as well have been the moon.

Nothing came back. Nothing came back for months.

But here was the thing she hadn't anticipated, the thing that crept up on her one evening in February while she pressed a stranger's officer's coat — a coat of the occupying pattern, she noticed, because the world kept sending its uniforms across water too, and her hands knew them now, and hated them, and pressed them flat regardless — here was the thing: the letters were changing *her*.

Because writing weekly to people you cannot reach forces a kind of honesty that speaking never demands. Mirabel would have deflected with work; Theo would have deflected with logic; Colette had always deflected with noise. But a letter has no interruptions. A letter just sits there while you decide who you are. And week by week, in the margin-space beneath the smugglers' reading eyes, she caught herself writing truths she had never said aloud to anyone:

*I took the letter because I thought love meant fixing things fast. I know now it mostly means standing still while things hurt.*

*I was the reason the wall had our name on it. I have decided to become someone worth the cost of that, rather than someone who spends her life apologizing for it. Apology is cheap. Becoming is expensive. I choose expensive.*

*Tell Mirabel — no. I will tell her myself. One day I will walk into the shop and put my hand on the counter and say it out loud, and she will probably be mid-stitch, and she will not look up right away, because she never does, and then she will look up, and that will be enough.*

She read that last line back and her ears went hot, and then, in the empty attic with the gulls quarreling outside, she smiled for the first time in longer than she could measure. Because it wasn't homesickness anymore, exactly. Homesickness was passive; it lay on you like weather. What she felt now had a direction and a spine. It was loyalty with a plan.

* * *

Spring came to the port city in a riot of blossom that struck her as almost indecent — trees flowering over streets where, in her city, the walls still carried the names of the condemned. She had grown taller. Her hands were a laundress's hands now, red-knuckled and strong, and Tante Rose had begun, in the third year's tradition, letting her teach the newest arrivals — a thin girl from the border provinces who flinched at doors slamming, whom Colette taught the way she wished someone had taught her: *you're useless the first year, useful the second, teaching by the third; the trade is survival and you're going to be excellent at it.*

Then, on a Thursday in April, the packet came back.

Anselme brought it himself, which he never did; he appeared at the laundry's back door holding an oilcloth square the size of a hymnal, and his dishwater eyes were doing something she had never seen them do. Inside were six letters — hers, returned unread, the smugglers' route dead, the boats scattered — and beneath them, wrapped in a scrap of newspaper, a seventh letter in handwriting she knew better than her own.

Mirabel's handwriting. Steadier than she remembered it. Addressed in full, impossibly, miraculously: *Colette Joubert, care of Tante Rose, behind Saint-Éloi.*

Someone had paid in favors or gold or both. Someone had found the thread through the locked harbor and pulled it across the whole gray distance of the sea.

Colette sat down on the laundry steps with the letter unopened in her lap, because her hands had started shaking, and because some part of her — the part that had been writing into silence for eighteen months — wanted to hold the moment unbroken just a little longer, the way you hold a note of music.

Then she opened it.

*Dear Colette. The shop is open. Your room is exactly as you left it, except cleaner, because I couldn't help myself. Theo has gone grey at the temples and cheats at chess worse than ever. Come home when you're ready — not before, and not because anyone needs you, but because the counter misses a second pair of elbows. P.S. There is someone who comes to the shop on Thursdays. She wears a coat I mended. We should talk about it when —*

The page ended mid-sentence, the final lines torn away — cut, deliberately, cleanly, by someone who understood that even a miracle travels through strangers' hands.

Colette read the letter four times. Then she folded it along its old familiar quarters, tucked it inside her shirt, over her heart, where important papers had always lived, and stood up.

"Tante Rose," she called through the steam. "How much would a berth cost, these days?"

From the wash-room, unhurried, unsurprised, as if she'd been waiting three years for precisely this question: "More than you've saved, little one. Less than you'd think. Ask me again Sunday — and this time, write and tell them you're coming."
