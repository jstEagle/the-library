## 21. The Berth That Came Back

The harbor had changed, or perhaps Colette had changed and the harbor had merely stayed itself. She stood at the rail as the ferry nosed toward the pier, watching gulls argue over something in the wake, and tried to remember whether the cranes on the far shore had always been that color — a rusted red like dried blood, or was that her memory borrowing from other things she'd seen since? Four years was long enough for a city to grow a new skin. Long enough for a girl to grow out of three names.

She counted them the way she'd learned to count everything aboard ship — quietly, without moving her lips.

*Colette Joubert*, on the papers sewn into the lining of her coat, the ones Mirabel's needle had made true when they were nothing but ink and forgery. *Colette Vasseur*, the name the smugglers' network had given her in the second year, when the first became dangerous in certain ports. And *Lise Marchand*, the name on the document currently folded against her heart, bought from a clerk in Varennes who had never asked why a nineteen-year-old needed papers in a dead woman's name, only how much she could pay.

Three names. Three lives she could put on and take off like coats. And underneath all of them, unchanged, unchangeable: the girl who had passed notes across a bridge because a boy smiled at her, and who had very nearly burned down everyone she loved with the matches of her own cleverness.

"You're doing it again," Luc said behind her.

"Doing what."

"Counting." He came to stand at the rail beside her, his hands jammed in the pockets of a coat that had been good four years ago in another country. "Your mouth does this thing. Like you're chewing each number before you swallow it."

"I don't do that."

"You've done it since I've known you. Since the bridge." He said it lightly, but the bridge sat between them anyway, as it always did — not an accusation, never an accusation, Luc had been too careful for that even at seventeen, but a fact they carried the way porters carry furniture, shifting its weight every few streets so neither shoulder gave out.

The gangplank came down. Neither of them moved.

"They won't know me," Colette said. It wasn't what she'd meant to say first. What she'd meant to say was something grand, something rehearsed on three trains and two ferries — she had composed speeches in her head the whole way across, speeches about forgiveness and homecoming and the years between, and now that the plank lay flat and ordinary on the wet boards of the pier, every one of those speeches died in her throat.

"Of course they'll know you," Luc said.

"I left at fifteen. I'm nineteen. Mirabel will look for a child."

Luc considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, which had once made him seem slow and which she now understood was a form of devotion. "Then we'll stand there until she sees us," he said. "I'm good at standing in doorways. You said so yourself, once."

*Once.* Four years ago, in the dark under the bridge arch, when the word doorway had been a joke between them and not yet the place where he'd waited for her notes, and later the place where the police had taken him, and later still — after Theo spent whatever currency couriers carry — the place where he'd come back to her, thinner, quieter, holding her hand like it was something that might be confiscated.

She picked up her case. It was a small case; she had learned to travel small. Inside it were two dresses, a sewing kit Mirabel would recognize instantly by the mended handle, and nothing else worth naming.

They walked down the gangplank together, and the harbor air came up to meet her — coal smoke and fish and the particular green smell of the river at low tide, and beneath it all something else, fainter, that made her stop halfway down the plank with her hand tight on the rope rail.

Soap. Lye soap and hot iron and wool.

It was absurd. The wind couldn't possibly carry it from wherever the shop was now — Rue Verte, the last letter had said, though the letters had stopped arriving regularly more than a year ago, and the last one had been six months old when it reached her, water-stained, most of a page gone. But the smell was there anyway, or she put it there, and either way it walked up the gangplank with her like a fourth passenger.

* * *

The city wore occupation the way an old man wears a borrowed coat — wrong in the shoulders, but worn long enough now that no one remarked on it anymore. Posters on the walls, newer ones over older ones over older ones still, layers of paper like the layers of paint in Mirabel's mending room, each one announcing some new arrangement of who governed whom. Soldiers at the corners with the bored faces of men who had been standing in the same corners for years. A tram rattled past with a cracked window mended badly, and Colette found herself looking at the mend — a strip of leather riveted over the break, functional, ugly, permanent — and thinking, *Mirabel would have done it better,* and then thinking, *Mirabel taught me to do it better,* and then having to stop walking for a moment on the corner of the Rue des Écoles because her eyes had gone hot without asking her permission first.

"Colette?" Luc touched her elbow.

"It's nothing. The light." The light was grey and flat and could not have hurt anyone's eyes. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and kept walking.

She had planned the route a hundred times in her head, and in her head it had always been dramatic — the shop appearing around a corner like something out of a story, the bell above the door, the cry, the embrace. In reality the city refused to cooperate. Streets looked like other streets. A bakery stood where a cobbler had been; the cobbler, presumably, stood where the bakery had been, or was ashore somewhere across the water like everyone else the republic had forgotten to take with it. Twice she had to stop and ask directions, and both times she used the name Lise Marchand out of pure reflex, and both times the strangers directed her kindly toward Rue Verte without knowing they were guiding her home under a false name, and something about that — the ordinariness of it, the way the whole occupied city conspired unknowingly in her return — made her want to laugh until she cried or cry until she laughed.

Rue Verte, when she finally found it, was narrower than she remembered. That was the thing no one told you about memory: it widened things. The street was barely wide enough for two carts to pass, lined with plane trees that had grown scraggly in four untended years, their trunks scarred where someone had stripped bark in the hungry winter — whose winter? Hers? The one after? The winters ran together like wet ink.

And there, halfway down, between a shuttered milliner's and a house with geraniums still stubbornly alive in its window boxes despite everything, despite all of it:

The shop.

*Joubert — Mending & Tailoring*, painted on the glass in gold letters gone slightly cloudy at the edges. No first initial. No political sign. Just the name, neutral as water, exactly the kind of sign Mirabel would have chosen, exactly the kind of sign that said *we mend for whoever comes through the door* and meant nothing else by it and everything else by it.

The window display held a single uniform — an officer's coat, army indistinct, the sort of coat that belonged to whichever government was current — and it was mended so beautifully that Colette stopped dead on the pavement. She could see the repair if she looked for it, because she had been taught to look: a sleeve rebuilt along the forearm, the weave matched thread for thread, invisible unless you knew invisibility when you saw it. In the corner of the window, half-hidden by the coat's shadow, someone had pinned a scrap of paper with a price written in a hand Colette would have known anywhere in the world, upside-down, in the dark, underwater.

Mirabel's hand. Still slanted the same way. Still pressing too hard on the downstrokes, as if the pen owed her money.

"She's in there," Luc said unnecessarily. "There's a light."

There was a light. Lamplight, warm through the frosted upper pane of the shop door, the same amber glow that had spilled under the door of the mending room on winter nights when Colette was small — smaller — when the three of them had lived above the shop and the whole world had been wool and steam and Mirabel's voice counting stitches like a rosary.

Her feet would not move.

This was the moment she had crossed the sea for. This was the moment she had rehearsed. And her body, that traitor, that veteran of borders and checkpoints and smugglers' cellars, stood frozen on a quiet street in front of a lit doorway like a child outside a schoolroom on the first day.

"Go on," Luc said gently. "I'll wait here."

"No. You come too."

"He may not want—"

"He waited four years in a doorway," Colette said. "He can wait five minutes in this one. Come *on*."

She pushed the door before she could think better of it, and the bell rang — the same bell, she was almost sure, the same slightly cracked note, unless she had invented the crack — and the warmth of the shop closed around her like a hand: coal heat, iron-steam, soap, wool. A workroom beyond the little entry hall, a counter, bolts of cloth, and at the counter with her back turned, bent over a sleeve clamped in a mending clamp, a woman with dark hair pinned up carelessly, humming.

Humming. Some tuneless thing, half a hymn, half nothing. Mirabel hummed when she worked. Mirabel had always hummed when she worked. Colette had forgotten that — or had she? Had she buried it deliberately, the way you bury the things that hurt most, the small domestic details, the humming and the burnt Sunday potatoes and the sound of needles through cloth at midnight — had she buried all of it so deep that now, hearing it, it rose up out of the ground of her like something planted and patiently bloomed?

"Closed at six," Mirabel said without turning around. "Unless you're the colonel's man, in which case you're early and the coat isn't ready."

Her voice was lower than Colette remembered. Rougher. A voice that had spent four years talking to customers and soldiers and strangers.

Colette opened her mouth and found that all three of her names had deserted her at once. She stood in the doorway of the workroom holding her small case, and the woman at the counter went on sewing, and the lamp hissed softly, and somewhere outside a cart went by, and none of it — none of it — was the scene she had composed on three trains and two ferries.

So she did the only thing her body offered her. She set the case down on the floor, with the click it had always made, that specific click of its broken-cornered brass catch against wood, the click that had punctuated her entire childhood—

Mirabel's hands stopped.

She didn't turn quickly. That was the strange part, the part Colette would remember all her life: she didn't whirl around crying out. She set down the needle. She smoothed the sleeve. She took off her thimble and laid it beside the needle with great care, the way she did when she intended to leave a piece of work for a while, and then she stood very still, facing the counter, her back a straight line, her shoulders rising once with a breath drawn so deep it seemed to move the whole room.

"You'd better," Mirabel said finally, to the wall, to God, to nobody, "be who I think you are. Because if you're not, I'm going to make a fool of myself in front of a customer, and I haven't done that since the winter of the posters."

"It's me," Colette said.

Mirabel turned around.

Four years. Her hair had grey coming in at the temples she hadn't bothered to dye, and there were lines around her eyes that hadn't been there, and she was thinner, and she was wearing an apron with a scorched hem, and she was the most beautiful thing Colette had ever seen in her life, and neither of them moved for a full three seconds, which felt like a year, which felt like the whole distance of the sea.

Then Mirabel crossed the workroom in four strides and hit her — actually hit her, a half-embrace half-collision that knocked the breath out of her — and grabbed her by the shoulders and pushed her back to arm's length to look at her, and pulled her in again, and pushed her back again, as if she couldn't decide between inspecting the damage and repairing it.

"You've got tall," Mirabel said, and her voice broke clean in half on the word *tall*, and she didn't bother to fix it.

"You've got old," Colette said, because that was how they loved each other, that had always been how they loved each other, insults like coins passed back and forth, and Mirabel laughed — a wet, cracked laugh — and hit her again, softer, on the arm.

"Let me look at you. Turn around. *Turn around.*" She turned her like a garment on a form, and Colette let herself be turned, laughing now too, helplessly, while Mirabel catalogued her — the coat, the boots, the calluses on her fingers ("you've been working with your hands, good"), the shadows under her eyes ("you haven't been sleeping, bad") — and then Mirabel stopped turning her, abruptly, because she had seen Luc.

He was standing just inside the shop door, hat in his hands, wearing the expression of a man at a funeral who isn't sure he's been invited.

For a long moment Mirabel looked at him. Colette watched her brother-in-everything-but-blood take inventory of the boy at the bridge, grown into a man with a scar she couldn't see and a coat four years out of fashion, and she saw Mirabel's face go through recognition, and memory, and something complicated and painful and tender, all in the space of a breath.

"Theo's boy," Mirabel said at last — for that was what Luc had always been in the family's private grammar, *Theo's boy*, ever since the gangplank — and Luc's shoulders came down half an inch, and he said, "Ma'am," which was absurd, and Mirabel said, "Don't you *ma'am* me, you stood in my kitchen eating my bread for three years," and then she had him by the collar and was looking him over too, and Luc endured it with the stoicism of a man being fitted for a uniform, and Colette stood there with tears running freely down her face and did nothing whatsoever to stop them.

* * *

The kettle was on. The kettle was always on; some things survived occupations and republics and seas. They sat in the back room among the bolts of cloth, on chairs that had been re-caned since Colette left, drinking tea out of cups that had not been re-caned, that were the same cups, chipped in the same places, and the conversation was — there was no other word for it — awkward.

Not painfully awkward. Awkward the way a shoe is awkward the first hour back on a foot: familiar, misfitting, certain to soften.

"So you're—" Mirabel began.

"Nineteen. In the spring."

"Nineteen." Mirabel said it as though Colette had announced a profession. "And these papers. Three names, Theo wrote me—"

"You got Theo's letter?"

"Months ago. He hears things, being—" Mirabel waved a hand, the family wave that meant *being Theo, being who Theo is, being the courier who stayed.* "He heard you were in Varennes. Then he heard you'd gone north. Then nothing for a year, and I—" She stopped. She wrapped both hands around her cup. "We wrote. Care of everyone we could think of."

"I got two of them. One came open — the smuggler reads everything — I'm sorry, I know you wouldn't have—"

"Did it say anything that matters?"

Colette thought about it honestly. "It said you were alive. That was most of it."

Mirabel nodded slowly, and Colette understood that the reverse had also been true, all these years, in both directions — that the letters' entire cargo had been a single sentence wearing different clothes.

"And Luc?" Mirabel asked, not lowering her voice enough, because Luc was ten feet away pretending to study a bolt of grey flannel with scholarly intensity. "How did he—"

"He followed me. To Varennes. He'd been—after the detention, after Theo got him out, he couldn't stay here. Everyone knew his face." She glanced over. "Everyone knew *our* faces, by then. So he went first, and I followed a year later, and we—" She faltered on the edge of the sentence, the one that explained what they were to each other now, because the truth was that she didn't have a word for it. They weren't married. They weren't not. They had simply continued, the way a stitch continues, each loop holding the last.

"We shared rooms," Luc said from the flannel, calmly, rescuing her. "Separate ones, ma'am, mostly."

"*Mostly,*" Mirabel repeated, with an eyebrow that required no translation, and Colette went scarlet to the ears, and Luc had the grace to examine the flannel even more closely, and Mirabel laughed her real laugh — the short, surprised one, the one she'd always tried to suppress and never managed — and just like that, the shoe began to fit.

They talked until the tea went cold and Mirabel made more without asking. Talked in pieces, in fragments, the way people talk when four years is too much for narrative and must be taken in installments: the shop had nearly closed the second winter; Theo had a limp now, weather in the knee, from the old courier routes; the colonel whose coat hung in the window was a fool but paid promptly; Delorme's daughter had come in once with a coat and an envelope, and Mirabel had mended the coat and kept the envelope, and Colette did not ask what was in it because she had read enough letters in her life to know when one was still sealed on purpose.

"And Theo," Colette said. "Where—"

"At the market. Arguing with the fishmonger, with any luck. He argues better than he buys." Mirabel stood, brushing off her apron, suddenly businesslike in the way that meant she was feeling too much. "Stay here. Both of you. Don't — just stay."

She went out the shop door and left it swinging, and Colette heard her footsteps quicken up the street, and Luc sat down beside her on the bench among the cloth bolts, close, his knee against hers.

"Well," he said.

"Well," she agreed.

"You're shaking."

"I'm not shaking. It's the tea. It was hot."

"It's been cold for an hour."

She looked at him. He looked at her. Outside, the lamplighter was making his rounds down Rue Verte in the grey dusk, and the geraniums in the neighbor's window glowed redder as the light failed, and Colette thought: *this is it, this is the whole of it, this ordinary bench in an ordinary room, and I crossed a sea for it.*

Footsteps in the street. Two sets now — one quick and light, one heavier, favoring the left leg. The shop door banged open hard enough to ring the bell twice.

Theo stood in the doorway with his cap crushed in one fist and his chest heaving from whatever pace Mirabel had set him, and he looked at Colette sitting on the bench, and his face did something she had never seen it do in all her life — it simply came apart, all the careful courier composure, all the practiced blankness of a man who had carried secrets across a dying country, gone at once, undone by a girl on a bench.

"Papers," he said hoarsely, holding out his hand, because he was still Theo, he would always be Theo, the man who checked documents first and embraced afterward. "Let me see them. All three."

Colette dug them out — Joubert, Vasseur, Marchand — and put them in her brother's hand, and Theo read them by lamplight with trembling thoroughness, checking seals, checking dates, and then he handed them back and sat down heavily on the bench on her other side, so that she was between them, between the brother who had burned the list and the sister who had memorized it, and none of them said anything at all for a while, because there are moments when speech is just noise and presence is the whole message.

It was Theo who broke the silence, in the end, with the practicality that was his own dialect of love.

"There's a bed upstairs," he said. "It's been yours the whole time. Nobody slept in it. Your mother's quilt's still on it — Mirabel washed it every spring, don't ask me why, she'd have denied it under oath." He cleared his throat. "And tomorrow, first thing, we start new papers. Real ones. One name. Yours."

"One name," Colette repeated.

"The right one. The only one that was ever true." He looked at her over the rim of his cup, and his eyes were wet and steady. "You came back by your own road. The least we can do is give you back your own name."

Outside, the lamplighter finished Rue Verte and turned the corner, and the shop windows glowed gold onto the wet cobblestones, and upstairs, in a small room that had waited four years, a quilt lay aired and clean on a bed that had never been given to anyone else.

Colette set down her cup, and reached for her case, and started up the stairs toward it.
