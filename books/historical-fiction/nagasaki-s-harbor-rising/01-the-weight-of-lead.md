## 1. The Weight of Lead

The cold came up through the floorboards first, as it always did, rising from the frozen harbor beneath the pilings like a slow animal breathing against the underside of the world. Cyrus Whitlock had learned to read that cold the way other people read weather. It told her when the ice was thick enough to bear the smugglers' sledges, when the fish market would open late because the harbor master's men could not crack their barrels free of the frost, and when it was safe to work by lamplight with the shutters half-open—because on nights when the ice groaned loudly enough, no patrol cared to stand at the quay and listen for anything else.

Tonight the ice sang. Long, low notes of it, like a bow drawn across a wet string, and so she left the shutters alone.

She sat at the sorting table in the dark, or nearly dark—one candle stub burning behind the lead case so its light fell only on her hands—and she sorted type by feel. This was the discipline her father had taught her before she could write her own name properly: *eyes are for reading, girl; fingers are for knowing.* A compositor who looked at her type was a compositor who looked away from the door. A compositor who knew her type blind could keep her head bowed, her gaze down, her face arranged into the pleasant blankness that the inspectors' clerks expected from a printer's daughter. Invisibility was not something that happened to a person. It was a craft, practiced daily, and Cyrus Whitlock was very good at her craft.

The letters came to her fingertips in their families. The round-bellied sorts—the o's and e's and c's—rolled gently under her thumb like river stones. The n's and u's and h's were all shoulders and legs, and she distinguished them by the depth of their counters: the *n* hollow to the second joint of her little finger, the *u* shallower, the *h* tall as a spire. The k's were treacherous, always had been, their arms catching at the partitions of the case like burrs on wool. She moved them one by one into their compartments, and each small weight settling into its slot made a sound like a held breath released.

*What can be carried,* she thought, in the rhythm of the work. *What must be left.*

It was a game she played with herself on long nights, though it was not entirely a game. If the soldiers came—if the knock ever finally came to the door of Whitlock & Son, Printers, and the word *Son* proved to be the lie everyone already knew it was—what would she take? Her father would say the punches. He had cut those steel letter-punches himself, thirty years ago, in another city, before Nagasaki's harbor district had a name anyone printed willingly. They fit in a box the size of a hymnal. But Cyrus had done this arithmetic too many times, and she knew the truth of it: you could not carry thirty years. You could carry one satchel of finished matrices and perhaps the good burnisher, and everything else—the press with its iron screw worn smooth by her father's palm, the paper stock stacked in the back room like pale loaves, the type itself, tons of it, an entire alphabet in metal—would be left to whoever came for it. Left to be melted, probably. Lead went back into the pot. Lead did not care what words it had said.

She weighed an em-quad in her palm. Cold, dense, silent. Everything she owned was somewhere between that weight and nothing at all.

From below, through the floor, she heard the harbor: the creak of moored hulls locked in the ice, the distant bark of a dog near the fish market gate, and once—fainter, farther out on the frozen expanse—the sharp crack of pressure splitting that every winter made her heart stop for a beat before her mind reminded her that the river would not break for weeks yet. The seals would come first, the harbor men said. The ice would seal the port until the thaw, and then the last ships would go, and after that only fools and smugglers crossed the water.

Her father had gone down to the quay at dusk to settle accounts with the paper merchant and had not yet returned. She did not worry. That was part of the discipline too—not worrying visibly, not even worrying privately if she could help it. Worry was a luxury for people whose names appeared on things.

Because that was the whole of it, wasn't it. She thought of it while her fingers walked the case: *b*, *d*, *p*, *q*—the mirror twins, the ones young compositors mixed up until their hands learned better. Her name appeared on nothing. Not the shop license, which read *Whitlock & Son* and had read so since before she was born, her father having understood, with his usual terrible foresight, that a shop belonging to a son would be left alone where a shop belonging to a daughter might be taxed, inspected, noticed. Not the guild rolls. Not the tax ledgers. Not even the colophons of the books they printed—those few respectable volumes, almanacs and ledgers of account and the harbor pilot's tide tables, which paid for the coal and the lamp oil and the quiet life. Those said *Whitlock & Son* too. Cyrus existed in the space between the letters, the way the counters existed inside the type: essential to the form, visible to no one.

She had stopped minding years ago. Or she had told herself she had, which amounted to the same thing, most days.

* * *

The candle guttered, and she trimmed the wick with a pair of tweezers kept for exactly that purpose, and in the fresh steadiness of the flame she allowed herself to look—actually look—at the room she knew by touch better than by sight. The composing stone with its shallow groove worn by forty years of setting sticks. The galley trays stacked like drawers in a morgue, each holding a page of metal waiting to be printed or distributed. The press itself, squat and black in the corner, an old hand press her father called the Widow because it had outlasted two owners before him and would likely outlast the regime besides. Iron does not take sides. Iron takes orders.

And above it all, along the back wall, the type cases—hundreds of them, labeled in her father's cramped hand, arranged in the order he had learned in another country and refused to change. Garamond and Caslon and a dozen anonymous jobbing faces. And in the third row from the top, second case from the left, the face her father called simply *the Vane cut*—no relation to any family, just a name he'd given it after the magistrate who'd commissioned it, years ago, back when commissions still came down from the magistracy to the harbor shops without anyone thinking twice.

Cyrus's fingers found that case without her meaning them to. She stood, took down the stick that lay on the stone, and set a line of it in the dark, purely from habit: *THE HARBOR IS SEALED UNTIL THE THAW.* She held the stick up to the candlelight and studied the letterforms. Slender serifs, a high waist, an italic-leaning *S* that her father had drawn himself and cut himself and then taught her to repair, file by patient file, when the soft lead wore round.

It was a beautiful face. She had always thought so. There was something in its proportions—a kind of restrained elegance, authority without cruelty—that made ordinary sentences look like proclamations. Which was, she supposed, why the magistracy had wanted it. A warrant set in the Vane cut carried weight that the same words in a jobbing face would never have.

A warrant. She frowned at the thought, chasing it the way one chases a draft through a shuttered room, finding the gap but not the source. Two winters ago—or was it three? The winters here blurred together, each one a repetition of white and gray and the groan of ice—there had been a commission. An execution warrant, of all things, which was unusual work for a harbor print shop and had been unusual enough that she remembered it at all. Her father had taken it without discussion, as he took all official work, and she had set it herself because her father's eyes had been bad that season and because there was no reason not to. Names, crimes, signatures. She had set them the way she set tide tables, without reading them more than twice, without letting them become hers.

That was the rule, and it had kept them alive through two changes of government: *set the words, do not hold them.* Words in the hand became opinions, and opinions became questions, and questions, in Nagasaki's harbor district, became disappearances. She had seen the broadsides pasted up on the customs house about the men who asked questions. She had read them, because reading a notice was safe, and had noted how the authorities used the same high-waisted serifs she loved, borrowed dignity for their lies.

She slid the line of type back into the stick's bed and left it there a moment, unexplained, like a sentence waiting for its verb.

Outside, the ice groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere down the quay a watchman called the hour in a voice roughened by cold. Third bell after dark. Her father should have been home by second bell.

*What can be carried,* she thought, and answered herself, as she always did: *very little. What must be left: nearly everything.*

* * *

She worked until the candle was a coin of wax drowning in its own grease. There was always work. The tide tables needed resetting for the new month—though what tide meant under a foot of solid ice was a question she'd stopped asking aloud—and there was a ledger of accounts for the rope-walk that had to be ready by morning, and the distribution of the type cases needed seeing to because her father, for all his precision with letterforms, put sorts back wherever his hand happened to be standing. She corrected the *k* drawer, which he had fouled again, and straightened the furniture, and swept the metal dust from the stone with the soft brush, and all of it was prayer, all of it was liturgy, the daily office of a woman whose religion was small, exact, and invisible.

It was during the sweeping that she saw the pamphlet.

It had come in under the door sometime that evening—slid through the gap the way such things traveled through the quarter now, hand to hand, door to door, a paper migration that no one admitted to and everyone participated in. Cyrus stood with the broom still and looked at it lying on the boards, white as a fish belly in the last of the candlelight.

She should leave it. That was the discipline. Leave it for her father to see or not see, let it pass through the house the way water passes through sand, touching nothing. She had burned three of them already that winter, feeding them to the stove without reading past the first line, because the first line was always enough to tell her what the rest would demand of her: *attention. Anger. Choice.* All the things a person in her position could not afford.

But the ice was singing tonight, and her father was late, and she was tired in the bone-deep way that makes discipline feel less like armor and more like a cage.

She picked it up.

*TO THE PRINTERS OF THE HARBOR,* it began, and she felt the words land in her chest like a dropped sort. Not to the workers, not to the faithful, not to the citizens in the regime's bloodless vocabulary. To the printers. Someone had written a letter addressed specifically to the eleven licensed shops of the harbor district—to her father's shop, to her—and someone was walking the frozen streets at night delivering it by hand.

She read it twice, standing in the cold room, the broom forgotten. It was well written. That frightened her more than the content. Fanatics wrote badly; fanatics trusted volume. Whoever composed this had understood economy, had understood the quiet power of a short declarative sentence, had even—she checked, disbelieving—understood the necessity of white space, of margins generous enough to make a reader feel respected rather than assaulted. It spoke of the grain levies that vanished between the granary and the tables. It spoke of the men taken from the fish market last autumn whose names had never been posted, whose trials had never been announced. And it ended with a single line, set apart:

*A press that prints only lies is not neutral. It is the lie.*

Cyrus folded it along its crease, slowly. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady and was grateful, the way one is grateful for small mercies.

She did not burn it. She told herself she was keeping it to show her father, which was true, and also that she was keeping it because a printer ought to study the competition, which was also true, and both truths were smaller than the real one, which was that the sentence had gone into her like a punch into soft lead and she could not yet know what it would print.

She hid it in the case of broken type—the battered sorts too worn for pages, the case her father called the graveyard—and slid the graveyard back onto its shelf just as she heard boots on the stair outside.

Two sets of boots. One heavy and familiar, dragging slightly on the left, her father's gait these past years. The other lighter, uneven, stumbling.

Cyrus blew out the candle and stood in the dark, listening.
