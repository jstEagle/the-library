## 3. The Man from the Ice

The bell above the shop door had a crack in it—a hairline fracture that turned its ring into something closer to a wheeze—and Cyrus Whitlock had spent eleven years listening to it and wishing her father would replace it. He never would. *A shop should sound tired,* he liked to say. *Tired means honest.* So when the bell gave its cracked complaint at quarter past six on the last evening of the fish market's winter session, Cyrus did not look up from the case she was distributing. She simply said, "We're closing," in the flat voice she kept for strangers, and kept sorting type by feel.

No answer came.

That was the first wrong thing. In Nagasaki's harbor district, even the deaf answered. A man who didn't respond to a closed door was either drunk, dying, or dangerous, and Cyrus had learned long ago that all three categories demanded the same response: nothing. Stillness. The discipline of invisibility that had kept the Whitlock print shop standing through two purges, one bread riot, and the entire slow strangulation of the free presses. She set down the type stick with exaggerated care, the way her father had taught her—*never clatter, never hurry; hurried hands are noticed hands*—and turned toward the door.

The stranger was slumped against it from the outside.

She could see him through the frost-ferned glass only in fragments: a shoulder, dark wool stiff with ice; a hand pressed flat against the pane as if he'd been trying to push the door open and had run out of the will halfway through; and below that, catching the lamplight, the dull gleam of something wrapped in oilskin, clutched against his chest with both arms like a child holds a stolen thing.

Cyrus stood very still.

Outside, the harbor wind came off the frozen bay in long white gusts, driving snow sideways down Rope Walk until the lamp posts disappeared. It was the kind of cold that killed quietly—the kind that found a man asleep in an alley or drunk at the quay edge and simply stopped his heart while he dreamed of warmth. She had found bodies before. Everyone in the harbor district had. There was a particular ritual to it: fetch the watch, stand back, say nothing, be seen saying nothing. That was how you survived here. You learned to see everything and touch nothing.

But the man moved. His hand slid down the glass, leaving a smear in the frost, and his forehead tipped forward until it rested where his palm had been. Through two inches of frosted pane and one of old cedar, Cyrus heard him make a sound—not words, just a single exhaled note, low and ragged, the sound of a body spending its last coin.

She looked at the door. She looked at the packet under his arm. She thought about the counter-edict drafts still smoldering in the stove upstairs, and her father's careful face as he'd refused Colonel Ashe's commission that morning—*we are a neutral house, Captain, we print what is paid for and nothing else*—and the way the captain's aide had smiled without warmth and written something in a little book.

*Neutral houses don't drag strange men inside.*

"Damn it," Cyrus said, very quietly, because there was no one to hear her swear but herself, and then she crossed the shop in four strides and threw the bolt.

The cold hit her like a wall of wet stone. Snow poured past the doorway in sheets, and the man half-fell into her as the door swung inward—heavier than he'd looked, or perhaps simply more dead weight than living, his boots scraping grooves in the threshold stone. He smelled of brine and woodsmoke and something underneath that was sharper: blood, maybe, or the chemical reek of river ice that had been dynamited too near a swimmer. His lips were gray. His eyelashes were crusted white, and beneath the crust his eyes were open—not seeing, but not entirely gone, tracking her face with the desperate animal focus of a drowning man watching a rope.

"The door," he rasped. His voice was ruined, sandpaper dragged over rust. "Bolt the—"

"I know." She hauled him across the threshold and shouldered the door shut behind him, and the bell above them gave its cracked wheeze, and the wind screamed once against the glass and gave up.

For a moment they simply stood there in the sudden stillness, breathing. Snowmelt spread around his boots in a dark pool. His weight leaned into her, and she felt how far gone he was: the shivering that should have been racking him had gone shallow and intermittent, which every harbor child knew was worse than violent shaking. Violent shaking meant the body was still fighting.

She got him onto the floor by the stove—dragged him, really, taking handfuls of his coat and walking backward—and fed kindling into the banked coals with hands that were steadier than she'd expected them to be. While the fire caught, she knelt and went through his pockets with the impersonal efficiency of someone who had helped dress corpses for burial and saw no reason this should be different unless he made it different. No papers. No money beyond three coppers and a foreign coin she didn't recognize. A knife, good steel, which she set aside within her own reach. And everywhere, soaked through, the smell of the sea.

He wasn't local. That much was certain before he even spoke properly. His coat was cut in the southern style, close-fitting, meant for salt spray rather than dry cold, and his skin held the brown of a man who worked outdoors in warmer latitudes. A sailor, then, or something adjacent to a sailor. Smuggler. Runner. One of the names the harbor used for men who moved things that needed moving.

His eyes had closed. She put two fingers to his throat and found a pulse—slow, thready, but stubborn—and made a decision she would examine later, when there was time for examination: she fetched the brandy her father kept hidden behind the ledger shelf, pried the man's jaw open, and poured.

He choked. Coughed. Came back to himself in stages, each one uglier than the last—first the coughing, then the full-body shudder as warmth finally reached whatever core of him still functioned, then a groan that seemed to come up from his boot soles. When his eyes opened again, they were clear, and they were fast. They went from her face to the stove to the bolted door to the knife lying beside her knee, and Cyrus watched him catalog the room the way she would have: exits, weapons, threats.

"You're not dead," she said.

"Not yet." His voice was wrecked but working. He tried to sit up, failed, tried again, and this time she took his arm—muscle under the sodden wool, more than she'd expected—and hauled him upright against the wall. He sagged there, breathing hard, steam beginning to rise faintly off his clothes in the stove's heat. "How far to the customs bridge?"

"You swam the harbor channel." She said it flatly, and watched him flinch. "In January. With the ice floes coming down off the weir."

"I swam part of it," he said. "The rest I went under."

Something in the way he said it—matter-of-fact, almost bored, the tone of a man reciting inventory rather than confessing a miracle—made the hair rise on her arms. People who had nearly drowned spoke about it afterward like priests or poets or sobbing children. This man spoke about it like a courier reporting a delivery.

"Who are you?"

"Tomas Reyes." He said the name easily enough, but his eyes flicked to the oilskin packet—which had stayed clutched against his chest through all of it, through the collapse and the dragging and the brandy, his arms locked around it like a man holding a plank in open water. "I need a printer."

"We're closed."

"I can see that." A ghost of humor moved across his face and died there. "I'll pay."

"With three coppers and a coin nobody in this harbor will take?" She stood, keeping the stove between them, an instinct she didn't examine. "Whatever you're carrying, you carried it into the wrong district. Try the licensed shops on Merchant Row. They have seals and stamps and friends in the magistrate's office."

"That's exactly why I'm here." He pushed himself straighter against the wall, and though his body was failing him, something in his face had hardened into a kind of terrible clarity. "Merchant Row prints what the magistrate pays them to print. Every press in this city with a government seal has already refused me or sold me. I've been walking this quarter since noon asking after a shop that isn't watched." He looked at her, and his voice dropped, roughened. "Your sign says WHITLOCK & SON. Your window hasn't been repainted since before the Proclamation. You don't fly the registry pennant. Either you're a dead business or a careful one, and I'm betting on careful."

Cyrus said nothing. Her heart was doing something she disapproved of.

"Reyes," she said instead. "That's a southern name."

"It is."

"And you came across the channel at night, in January, carrying—" she nodded at the oilskin "—that. Which you haven't let go of since you fell through my door. Which means it's worth more to you than your life, because you nearly spent yours on it." She folded her arms. "I'm the daughter of a printer, Master Reyes. I know what paper weighs. Nobody risks the channel for blank paper. So either you tell me what's in that packet, or you get back out into the snow and freeze somewhere it won't be my problem."

For a long moment he only looked at her. The fire ticked and settled. Outside, the wind had dropped into that deep nighttime moan it made along the quay pilings, and somewhere distant a dog was barking at nothing—or at patrols; in the harbor district those were often the same thing. Steam rose off his shoulders. He was still shivering, but less now, and she hated herself for noticing that he was going to live, because some cold practical corner of her mind had already understood that a living man in her shop was infinitely more dangerous than a dead one.

"If I show you," he said slowly, "you can't unknow it. Do you understand? Whatever you think you know about how this city runs—whatever you've told yourself to sleep at night—it's built on top of this. Once you see it, you're in it. Same as me."

"My father always says ignorance is a load-bearing wall."

"He sounds like a wise man." Reyes's hand moved at last—slowly, deliberately, letting her watch every inch of the motion—and loosened the knot at the oilskin's throat. "I'm sorry to load-bearing walls."

He unwrapped it right there on the floor: three folds of waxed cloth, and inside them, protected from the sea by layers of tallow and canvas, a bundle of letters tied with cord, and beneath the letters a single sheet of heavy vellum that even upside down and spattered with brine Cyrus recognized in one lurching heartbeat, because she had set its twin with her own hands.

She didn't reach for it. Some instinct older than curiosity nailed her feet to the floorboards.

"Letters," Reyes said. "Written by Aldous Vane."

The name landed in the room like a dropped typecase. Cyrus heard herself breathe. *Aldous Vane*, the magistrate, the reformer, the man whose trial and execution two winters ago had marked the moment the city's last freedoms died—executed on a warrant for treason, his presses seized, his associates scattered or worse. She remembered the proclamation nailed to the customs house. She remembered her father reading it twice and putting it down and going back to work without a word, which had frightened her more than any speech could have.

"Aldous Vane is dead," she said.

"No." Reyes's voice was quiet and absolutely steady. "Aldous Vane is alive, and he wrote these letters eight months ago, in hiding, and I have carried them from the southern provinces through three checkpoints and a war's worth of bad weather to bring them to a printer who can copy them." He tapped the vellum with one finger, gently, the way a man touches a relic. "And this is the warrant for his execution. Look at the date. Look at the signature. Look at it closely, Miss Whitlock—I'm told you people see what other people miss."

She looked.

She shouldn't have. She knew, even as she knelt and drew the lamplight close and tilted the vellum to catch the flame, that she was crossing a line her father had spent eleven years drawing around their lives—but her hands were already moving, already smoothing the damp parchment flat, already reading. The seal was intact. The chancery hand was flawless. The date was two days before the execution. The warrant ordered the death of Aldous Vane for crimes against the Provision—

Except.

Her eye, trained on ten thousand documents' worth of official typesetting, snagged on it the way a finger snags on a splinter. The spacing between the fourth and fifth lines. Too wide by a hair. The sort of error that happened when a compositor justified a line in a hurry, or when a document was assembled from pieces that had never shared a galley. Official warrants were set with religious care. This one had been *assembled*.

"This isn't—" she began, and stopped, because the rest of the sentence was a door she suddenly did not want to open in front of a stranger.

"It isn't genuine," Reyes finished for her. "It's a forgery. Vane was never lawfully executed, because there was no lawful execution. There was a staged death, a bought coroner, and a forged warrant, and the men who signed this have been running this harbor on the strength of it ever since." He leaned forward, firelight carving his face out of the dark. "Now. You asked what's in the packet. You've seen it. And I need to ask you the question I've been carrying across half the country along with these letters."

The stove ticked. The snow moaned against the glass. Somewhere above them, a floorboard settled in the old building's bones, and Cyrus thought of her father asleep upstairs, of the burned drafts still warm in the ash pan, of the little book the captain's aide had written in that morning.

"Don't," she said.

"Will you print it?"

Above them, the floorboard creaked again—slower this time, deliberate. Footsteps, moving toward the stair. Her father, woken by the stove's heat rising through the ceiling, or the bell, or the sixth sense that fathers kept in reserve for exactly this kind of night. Reyes heard it too; his hand drifted, casually, back toward the knife she had set aside.

"Bolt the door," Cyrus whispered, "and pray to whatever god smugglers keep that my father decides we're closed."

* * *

Edmund Whitlock descended the stairs the way he did everything: without hurry, in his nightshirt and dressing gown, candle in hand, his bare feet making almost no sound on the treads. Cyrus had inherited her quiet from him. She watched him pause at the bottom step, candle raised, taking in the scene with a single sweep of his pale eyes—the puddle, the stranger, the oilskin bundle open on the floor, the warrant gleaming under the lamp—and she watched her father's face do something she had seen it do only twice before in her life: go completely, perfectly blank, like a page wiped clean for setting.

"Well," Edmund Whitlock said mildly. "It appears we are not, after all, closed."
