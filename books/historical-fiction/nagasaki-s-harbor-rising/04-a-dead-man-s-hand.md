## 4. A Dead Man's Hand

Tomas woke to the smell of ink and lamp oil and the sound of a woman moving through a room as though she wished it were empty.

He lay still and took inventory of himself the way he'd been taught—feet first, then hands, then ribs, then head. His boots were gone. His coat was gone, which meant his knife was gone, which meant he had been stripped like a corpse by someone practical enough not to trust him. His feet were wrapped in something wool and scratchy that smelled faintly of camphor. The rest of him ached in the deep, bruised way of a body that had been in water it should never have entered.

The harbor ice. He remembered the harbor ice giving way beneath him with a sound like a struck bell, and the black water closing over his head, and the packet under his shirt going cold against his chest while his lungs burned and his hands scrabbled at the underside of the world.

His hands went to his chest now, before anything else.

Still there. Still dry. The oilskin was stiff with salt where the river had gotten past the knot, but the knot itself—the triple hitch, waxed cord, Maren's own knot, though she didn't know he carried this—held fast.

"Your coat is drying by the stove," said the woman's voice from somewhere above him. "I went through the pockets. If you object, you can object when you can stand up without falling down."

Cyrus Whitlock. He had learned her name from the sign on the door before he'd learned it from her: *Whitlock & Whitlock, Printers*, the smaller Whitlock painted over at some point, the letters ghosting through like an old scar. She sat at a workbench across the room with a composing stick in her hand and her back half-turned to him, sorting type by feel in the lamplight, and she did not look at him, and the not-looking was so deliberate it was almost louder than speech.

"I object," Tomas rasped. His throat felt full of gravel and river. "To all of it."

"You're welcome," she said.

He tried to sit up. The room tilted, righted itself, tilted again. He was on a cot behind a partition of shelved paper reams, in the warm heart of the shop, and the warmth itself told him something—that she had stoked the stove for him, that she had done it grudgingly, that she had done it anyway. Above the partition he could see the tops of type cases ranged along the wall like a cabinet of small drawers, hundreds of them, each one holding its alphabet in lead.

"How long?"

"Since midnight. It's past noon." She set a piece of type into the stick with a small precise click. "You were blue when I dragged you in. I thought about dragging you back out."

"But you didn't."

"No," she said, and the word had a weight to it, an admission she clearly resented. "I didn't."

* * *

She fed him because leaving a man to die on your floor was one thing and watching him die slowly of soup deprivation was another—he suspected her reasoning ran along exactly those lines, and he did not disabuse her of it. The soup was fish and barley and too much salt, harbor food, dock food, and he ate it hunched over the bowl like a man who had forgotten manners existed, because he mostly had.

She watched him eat the way a person watches weather they don't trust.

"Tomas Reyes," he said, when the bowl was empty.

"I know. Your papers."

"Then you know I'm worth money dead or alive, and you let me sleep eight hours under your roof."

Her jaw tightened. In the lamplight her face was all planes and discipline, a face built for keeping things off of it, and yet he could see the war happening behind her eyes—the same war he'd watched happen in a hundred faces since the regime came, the one between what a person knew was right and what a person knew would keep them breathing. Most people settled it quietly and called the settlement wisdom. He was beginning to suspect she hadn't settled it at all. He suspected she simply refused to look at it directly, the way you refuse to look at a hole in the ice you must cross anyway.

"Eat your bread," she said.

* * *

He waited until the lamps burned low, until the shop settled into the particular silence of a building full of small heavy things, until her father's footsteps creaked overhead and then went still. Then he unwound the oilskin packet from his chest, and his fingers shook—not from cold now, not anymore, but from what he was about to do, because there was no taking it back after this, no returning to the country of people who didn't know.

"You should know," he said, "that everything gets worse for you starting tonight. And I'm sorry. And I'm going to do it anyway."

Cyrus looked up from her type case then, really looked at him, for the first time since he'd woken. "That's a strange way to ask for help."

"It's not a request."

He untied the triple hitch. His fingers remembered it even when the rest of him was failing; some things lived deeper than exhaustion. The oilskin opened with a soft crackle, and inside, wrapped in a second layer of waxed linen, were the letters.

Six of them. He laid them out on the workbench one by one, squaring their edges, handling them by the corners the way he'd been taught to handle things that could not be replaced. They were ordinary-looking at first glance—foolscap, folded twice, water-stained at one edge where two years of hiding had let damp reach them once. Ordinary stationery. Ordinary ink gone brown at the folds.

Except for the hand that had written them.

"What are these?" Cyrus asked, and her voice had changed—flattened, careful, the voice of a woman who has decided not to be interested and is failing at it.

"Letters," Tomas said. "Written by Aldous Vane."

The name dropped into the room like a coin into a well.

He watched her refuse to react. He watched the refusal cost her. Her hand, still resting on the type case, went very still, and the lamp flame leaned in a draft from the stove, and for three full seconds the only sound in the shop was the harbor outside—the ice groaning against the pilings, that long slow complaint the frozen river made at night, like a giant turning in its sleep.

"That's not funny," she said finally.

"No," Tomas agreed. "It isn't."

"Aldous Vane has been dead for two years. Everyone saw the announcement. There was a warrant. There was a—" She stopped herself. He watched her stop herself, watched her swallow whatever she'd been about to say about the execution, and he thought: *she knows something*. Everyone in Nagasaki's harbor knew something; that was how terror worked, it made every citizen a small locked room.

"There was a warrant," Tomas finished for her. "Signed and sealed, ordering his execution for sedition. Posted on the customs house. Read aloud in every square. And two days later the regime announced that the sentence had been carried out, and Aldous Vane became a warning instead of a man." He turned one of the letters toward the lamp. "These are dated *after* the execution. All six of them. Written over fourteen months, from a place he calls only 'the house by the salt marsh,' in a hand I would know anywhere, because I spent four years copying documents in that hand."

He slid the top letter across the bench toward her.

She didn't touch it. That was the first thing—the refusal to touch, as if contact might make it real, might make it hers, might make her responsible. Her eyes went over it anyway. He saw her reading despite herself, the way a person reads a name on a coffin lid.

"He writes to a woman named Ilse," Tomas said quietly. "His daughter. She was sent north after the arrest; the letters were meant to follow her but never could, because officially her father was ash. So he wrote them anyway. Fourteen months of letters to a dead-addressed girl, describing his captivity, naming his keepers, and asking—" Tomas's voice caught, and he hated it, and he pushed through. "Asking her forgiveness. For signing a confession he was told would save his family. A confession that was used to justify everything that came after."

"The regime was founded on that confession," Cyrus said. Her voice was barely above the sound of the ice. "On the treason trials. On Vane's guilt."

"On Vane's guilt," Tomas said, "which was purchased. Which was staged. He didn't die on the date they gave the world, Miss Whitlock. He died fourteen months later, alone, in a marsh house, writing letters no one would ever read. And the warrant that started it all—the warrant ordering his execution, the founding document of the new order—" He reached into the oilskin and drew out the last item, a folded legal sheet, heavier stock, bearing a seal and a signature. "Is a forgery. Dated eleven days before the arrest it claims to answer. Signed by a magistrate who, we can prove, was in the capital on the date of signature. The entire revolution's cornerstone is paper, Miss Whitlock. Bad paper."

* * *

For a long time she said nothing.

Tomas understood the silence. He'd seen it before, in the moment he'd shown these letters to others—a schoolteacher in the fishing villages, an old priest, Maren herself, briefly, by firelight. The silence was the sound of a life being rearranged. Every person who looked at that handwriting had to choose, in the space between one breath and the next, between the world they had learned to survive in and the world that was actually true. Some of them had chosen survival. The priest had prayed over the letters and given them back and asked never to see them again. The schoolteacher had wept and done nothing. Maren had looked longest of anyone, and said, "What do you want me to do with it?"—which was the bravest question he'd ever heard.

Cyrus Whitlock did none of those things.

She picked up the warrant.

She held it to the lamp, tilted it, and Tomas watched something move through her face that he had not seen in any of the others—something colder and more precise than horror, something that looked almost like recognition.

"This seal," she said. Her thumb hovered over the embossed crest without touching it. "This presswork. This isn't a clerk's copy—it was printed. Set in metal, struck on a jobbing press, someone with access to good stock and a compositor's training." Her eyes came up to his, and in them was a vertigo she couldn't quite hide, a ground-giving-way. "Do you understand what you're saying? You're saying the regime's founding lie came off a press. Someone *set* this. Someone chose the type, spaced the lines, inked the form, and pulled this sheet—and knew, the whole time, what they were making."

"Yes."

"And you brought it here." Her voice stayed level, but underneath it he heard the tremor, the fault line. "To a print shop. To a printer's daughter."

"I brought it here because it's the last shop in the harbor district that hasn't been searched, and because you pulled a drowning man out of the ice, and because—" He stopped. Honesty was the only currency he had left; he'd spent everything else getting here. "Because I have nowhere else. Because the men who killed Aldous Vane know the letters exist, and two of them are in this city, and by tomorrow night they'll know I'm here. I didn't come to make you complicit, Miss Whitlock. I came because you already were. We all were. Everyone who read the announcement and believed it. Everyone who repeated it. The lie needed ten thousand voices and we gave it ours for free."

The lamp flame steadied. Outside, the ice groaned again, longer this time, a sound like something enormous beginning to move after years of stillness.

Cyrus looked down at the forged warrant in her hands—at the false seal, the false date, the false signature of a man two years dead and writing letters in a marsh house—and her lips parted, and whatever she was about to say, Tomas could see it frightened her more than the soldiers, more than the gallows, more than the black water under the ice.

Then she went very pale, and very still, and reached for the type case at her elbow with a hand that was not entirely steady.

"Show me the warrant again," she said. "Slowly. Line by line."
