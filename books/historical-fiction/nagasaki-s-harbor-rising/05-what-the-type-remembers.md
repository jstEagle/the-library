## 5. What the Type Remembers

The oilskin packet lay on the composing table between them like something that had been dredged from the harbor floor—and in a sense it had been, Tomas half-drowned as he was, fished out of the night by Cyrus's own unwilling hands.

She had not wanted to open it. She wanted that recorded somewhere, even if only in the ledger of her own conscience: she had stood with her thumb on the waxed flap for the better part of a quarter hour, listening to Tomas Reyes breathe in the corner where she'd propped him against flour sacks, listening to the wind worrying the shutters, listening to the ice groan out on the black water like some vast animal turning over in its sleep. She listened to all of it because it was better than listening to herself decide.

In the end she decided the way she did everything—quickly, quietly, and with the certain knowledge that her father would have told her not to.

"Sit still," she said, though he was already sitting, more or less, folded into the shadows like a man made of coat and guilt. "If you die in my shop I'll be the one who has to explain the mess."

He managed something that might have been a laugh if laughter could freeze. His lips were still the color of slate. She had forced sweetened gin down him earlier, and a basin of snow melted over the stove, and now he watched her with the particular patience of a man who has carried something too far to put down.

She broke the seal. The oilskin crackled, stiff with cold, and inside were letters—a bundle of them, perhaps thirty, tied with tarred cord—and beneath them, wrapped separately as if it knew itself to be the heart of the matter, a single sheet of heavy rag paper bearing a great red stamp.

Cyrus unfolded it under the lamp.

*By order of the Provisional Authority of the Harbor District,* it began, in language she knew the way a sailor knows weather, *the magistrate Aldous Vane, having been found guilty of treason against the lawful government, is hereby sentenced to death by hanging, the sentence to be carried out on the morning of the ninth of Frostmoon, in the year—*

She stopped reading the words. That was the strange thing she would remember afterward, how the words simply stopped being words, because below the signature block, below the flourish of some clerk's practiced hand, the document said something else entirely. It spoke in a smaller voice. It spoke in its type.

She read type the way other people read faces. Her father had trained her ear first—*listen to the press, Cyrus, listen to what it complains of*—but her eye had taught itself, in ten years of nights like this one, sorting sorts by lamplight until the letterforms lived behind her eyelids. Every face in the drawer had a history. The worn *e* with the chipped crossbar. The *g* whose descender curled a hair too far, because her father had cut it in his youth with a graver borrowed from a man who later sold him out. The *k* with the crooked arm that everyone who'd ever bought stock type from Whitlock & Daughter complained about and paid for anyway, because it was honest work at an honest price.

This warrant was not set in stock type.

It was set in their type. Their *e*, chip and all. Their crooked-armed *k*, standing at the start of *killing* no less, looking up at her from two years ago like a child who didn't know what it had been made to say.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere across the room, Tomas Reyes shifted against his sacks.

"No," she said aloud, to the paper, as if it could be argued with.

She held the sheet closer. The impression was deep, well-inked, pulled on a cylinder press—the Authority's own works, downtown, the big Heidelberg that ate reams by the hour—but the type itself, the metal, the metal was theirs. And there was worse. There was always worse; the night seemed built of trapdoors opening one onto another. In the lower margin, where careful printers sometimes left a maker's mark the way tailors left their stitches hidden in a seam, she found it: three small pinpricks in the shape of a triangle. A quirk of their forme-locking, a quirk of her father's quoins, which he tightened in a particular sequence because a pressman in Vlissingen had taught him so twenty years before Cyrus was born.

Three dots in a triangle. She had seen them on every job they'd ever run longer than four pages. She had seen them last week, on the hymnals for the Chapel of the Redeemed Tide. She was seeing them now on a death warrant that had sent Aldous Vane to a gallows in front of four hundred witnesses, and the gallows had been empty, and this paper proved it, and her father's quoins had locked the lie tight as a coffin lid.

Her hands were steady. That frightened her most of all, afterward—that her hands went on holding the page with a compositor's care, thumbs at the margins, never touching the ink, while the rest of her came apart somewhere underneath like a keel scraping rock.

"Cyrus." Tomas's voice, roughened by river water and disuse. "You see it."

"I see type," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's, someone sensible, someone standing slightly outside her body taking notes. "I don't know yet what I see."

"You know."

She knew.

Two years ago. The winter of the execution. She could place herself exactly: at this same table, under this same lamp, working late because her father had come home with a commission he wouldn't discuss, only a wrapped forme already locked and ready, telling her to run off two hundred copies of an official document and ask nothing, *ask nothing, Cyrus, some clients pay extra for silence and we're behind on the paper debt.* She had been nineteen and tired and glad of the coin. She had pulled the proofs, checked the register, trimmed the sheets, and gone to sleep dreaming of nothing at all, because that had always been her talent. Dreaming of nothing. Wanting nothing loudly enough to be noticed by no one.

Two hundred copies of a forged death warrant, printed by Whitlock & Daughter, distributed to the clerks, the criers, the archives. And somewhere in the city, a man named Aldous Vane—who should have died on the ninth of Frostmoon—had instead walked out of history through whatever door men with money and friends could buy, and the world had rolled on believing the gallows, because the gallows had paperwork, and the paperwork had been *good*. Clean register. Deep impression. Three little dots in a triangle in the margin, quiet as a heartbeat.

She had set it. Not designed it, not composed it—her father had brought it locked—but she had fed it through the press with her own two hands, and the press was an extension of her hands, and so the lie bore her fingerprints in the only way that mattered to a printer. You could wash ink off skin. You couldn't wash it off iron type. Iron remembered.

"I printed this," she said.

Tomas pushed himself upright, wincing, dragging his coat around his shoulders. Up close, in the lamplight, he looked younger than his voice—mid-thirties maybe, with the sunburn lines of a man who worked decks or docks, salt cracked into the creases of his knuckles. But his eyes were old in a way she distrusted on principle, eyes that had seen things happen to people and kept walking.

"You printed copies," he said carefully. "Someone else wrote the sentence."

"Type doesn't care who writes the sentence." She heard her father in her own mouth and hated it. "Type says what it's told. That's the whole trade. That's the whole—" She stopped. Set the warrant down on the table with exaggerated gentleness, the way you set down something that might go off. "Who gave my father this forme?"

"I don't know your father."

"Someone did. This wasn't stolen. Stolen type gets scattered, sold, melted. This was *commissioned*. Somebody walked into my father's shop two winters ago with copy in hand and money up front, and my father set it, and neither of them has ever mentioned it since." She was pacing now, three steps to the window, three steps back, the shop suddenly too small for what it contained. "Do you understand what that means? Everyone believes Vane died because there are two hundred pieces of paper saying so, and every one of those pieces came off our press. If this comes out—if anyone ever holds this warrant next to our work—"

"Then the forgers hang instead of the hanged," Tomas said. "Yes. That's rather the point of it. Whoever arranged this built themselves a beautiful insurance policy. The printer is the alibi."

The word landed in her chest like a dropped ballast stone. *The printer.* Not her father. Not even both of them. *The printer*, singular, a function, a thing to be used and discarded when the paper yellowed. All those years she had spent perfecting invisibility—keeping her head down, keeping her name out of pamphlets and petitions, declining the guild dinners, declining everything—she had thought she was safe because she was small. She had mistaken herself for a mouse in a wall. She was not a mouse. She was a tool, and tools do not get to be invisible; they get to be *used*, and used again, and finally thrown out or turned against their owner, whichever serves.

"How many others?" she asked. The question came out of her before she could weigh it. "How many warrants, how many sentences, how many *executions* that never happened—or worse, ones that happened without paper at all? How much of what this city believes about itself came off our press?"

"I don't know," Tomas said. "That's why I'm here. That's why the letters exist." He nodded at the oilskin bundle. "Vane wrote them himself. Over two years, smuggled out a page at a time. Names, dates, payments—the whole architecture of it. The men who staged his death sit on the Authority now. Ashe. Corvain. Half the council. They needed a dead magistrate to frighten the district and a live one they could deny, and they got both, and they paid a printer to make it true."

"And killed him twice over," Cyrus said. "Once with rope that never touched him, once with this." She touched the warrant's edge. "Because whoever reads these letters will want to know how the lie was manufactured. And the manufacture leads here. To us." She looked up. "Why bring it to us, then? Of all the doors in Nagasaki's harbor, why carry your dead man's letters to the shop that helped forge them?"

Tomas held her gaze. He didn't flinch, which she gave him credit for, and didn't look away, which she liked less.

"Because I've been carrying these for eleven days," he said, "through three towns and a frozen estuary, and every safe house I knew of is burned or bought. Because a dying courier told me the only man in this district who could verify the type on that warrant was the man whose shop it came from. And because—" he paused, choosing, she thought, the truth over the comfortable lie, which was either honesty or strategy and might be both— "because I'm not asking you to hide me, Cyrus Whitlock. I'm asking you to read. You're a printer. Reading is what you're *for*. Read Vane's letters, and then tell me you wish you hadn't opened the packet."

She should throw him out. The thought arrived fully dressed, reasonable, wearing her father's voice: *some clients pay extra for silence.* Throw him out, melt back into the discipline of smallness, let the tide take him and his dead magistrate and their terrible paper elsewhere. The harbor was full of ice; the city was full of informers; the stove was warm and the door was right there.

Instead she untied the tarred cord.

The letters were written in a fine, furious hand, the script of a man educated past patience, and they began, the earliest of them, with a line she would carry for the rest of her life:

*I am told the district believes me dead, and I find I cannot improve upon their belief except in one particular: they believe it honestly.*

She read. She read while the wind rose and the lamp burned low and Tomas Reyes sat watching her with the wariness of a man who has handed a stranger a lit fuse and is waiting to learn whether she is the kind who runs or the kind who stands. She read accounts of bribes with sums attached, of a gallows built for a crowd that would never see it used, of a coffin filled with stones and a grave dug by men who were paid twice, once to dig and once to forget. She read the names of the men who had arranged it, and the name of the print shop was not in the letters—not yet—but she knew it was coming. Documents have gravity. Everything rolls downhill toward the workshop.

Outside, the ice groaned again, long and low, the sound of a season straining at its seams.

She reached the fourth letter and stopped, because there it was: *the printing was handled locally; I know the house but will not write the name, for the printer is either a coward or a hostage and I will not make him a target twice.*

*A coward or a hostage.* Her father. Her silent, careful, unbribeable-seeming father, who refused commissions and burned drafts and kept his head down so beautifully, so professionally, for thirty years. Either he had been threatened—and she could imagine it, could see the plainclothes men in the shop, the polite voices, the arithmetic of a daughter's future laid out on the composing stone—or he had taken the money. She genuinely did not know which. That was the vertigo of it: two years of shared meals and shared work, and she could not tell whether the man upstairs was a victim or a partner.

The lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick without thinking, her hands doing the small faithful work while her mind stood in the wreckage.

"Tomas," she said quietly. "The man who set that warrant—whether he knew it was false or not—is the only living soul besides you who can testify to how the lie was made. Do you understand? Your letters name the men. My father's type proves the mechanism. Without the mechanism, the letters are the ravings of a dead traitor. With it—"

"With it, it's a confession signed in lead," Tomas finished.

"In our lead." She looked down at the warrant, at the three small dots in their small triangle, and felt the full weight of the irony settle over her like snowfall: she had spent her whole life learning how not to be seen, and it turned out she had been seen all along—seen, priced, purchased, and pressed into service, her anonymity just another material in someone else's composition, like ink, like paper, like the bodies of hanged-and-unhanged men.

Above her head, through the ceiling, came the sound of her father's footsteps crossing his room. Slow. Even. A man sleeping soundly, or a man lying awake rehearsing innocence.

Cyrus folded the warrant along its original creases, returned it to the oilskin, and retied the cord with fingers that had finally, finally begun to shake.

"Tomas," she said. "Stay here tonight. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow—" She glanced at the stairs, at the darkness above, where her father's silence had just acquired a completely new texture. "Tomorrow I'm going to ask my father what he knew. And God help us both, I'm going to watch his hands when he answers. A liar can school his voice. No one alive can school his hands."

From the top of the stair, faint through the floorboards, came the creak of a board bearing weight—her father, she told herself, going to the window, checking the street, the way he always did.

Always. The way he always did.

Or the way a man does when he already knows what has come through his door.
