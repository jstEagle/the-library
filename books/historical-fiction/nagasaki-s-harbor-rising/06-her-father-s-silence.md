## 6. Her Father's Silence

The lamplight in the workroom had gone the color of old honey by the time Cyrus came down the stairs, and her father was still at his bench, though the shop had been shut for two hours. He was sorting type, she saw—sorting it badly, his thick fingers moving through the cases with none of their usual certainty, lifting pieces and setting them down again as though the letters themselves had become a language he'd forgotten.

She stood in the doorway a long moment, watching him. Above them, through the floorboards, she could hear Tomas moving in the guest room—slowly, the careful tread of a man whose body was still remembering how to be warm. The oilskin packet lay upstairs in the font case of broken type, wedged among the ruined sorts she'd never had the heart to melt down, and every hour it sat there she could feel it the way one feels a splinter too deep to draw: not the thing itself, but the body's knowledge of it.

"Father," she said.

He didn't look up. "The twelve-point Caslon is mis-sorted. Third case from the wall. Someone has put the long s in with the f."

"I know. I did it. Six months ago." She came into the room and stood across the bench from him, and when he finally raised his eyes she saw that he had known, before she said a word, what she had come down to say. His face did that thing it always did—went smooth and mild, a shopkeeper's face, a face for showing to inspectors—and something in her, something that had been cracking all evening, broke clean through.

"Don't," she said. "Don't do that. Not with me. Not tonight."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the wind had come around to the north and was worrying at the shutters, and beneath it all, faint but constant, came the sound she had heard every winter of her life: the harbor ice, groaning as it shifted against the pilings, like some enormous animal turning over in its sleep.

"You've seen it," her father said quietly. It was not a question.

"You know I've seen it."

"The letters." He set down the piece of type he was holding, set it down with exaggerated care, as a man sets down something that might go off. "The warrant."

"You knew." Her voice came out lower than she intended, almost a whisper, and she hated it—hated that even now, even with the truth sitting in the room between them like a third person, she was keeping her voice down. Two decades of practice. Two decades of being the quiet daughter of a quiet shop in a quiet street, and she could not unlearn it even to accuse her own father. "You knew Aldous Vane's execution was staged. You knew the warrant was false. You set it—you let *me* set it—and you never said a word."

Her father closed his eyes. For a moment he looked very old. He was not an old man—sixty-one, still strong through the shoulders from thirty years at the press—but the lamplight found every line in his face and deepened it, and Cyrus thought: *this is what he has looked like for two years, and I never saw it, because I never let myself look.*

"Sit down, Cyrus."

"I'll stand."

"Sit down." There was no command in it. There never was. It was the weariness of a man who had said the same thing to the same stubborn child for thirty years. She pulled the apprentice stool from the corner—the one Maren used to sit on, the one that still bore the ghost of Maren's initials cut into the leg with a graver, back when they were all younger and the world was differently shaped—and sat, and waited.

Her father took off his apron. She had never seen him do that indoors, never, not since her mother died; the apron was his armor, the way her silence was hers. He folded it over the back of his chair and sat, and put his hands flat on the bench among the scattered type, and looked at them as if the answer were written there in mirror-image, waiting to be inked and pulled.

"Do you remember the winter your mother took the fever," he said.

"Father—"

"Answer me. Do you remember it."

She did. She remembered it with the whole of her body, the way one remembers the winters that make or unmake a person. She was nine. The harbor had frozen early and hard that year, the river choked with ice by the first week of the dark month, and no ships had come, and no ships had gone, and the fish market had been a place of empty hooks and men standing in doorways with their hands in their sleeves, watching the sky. Her mother had coughed through all of it, and then stopped coughing, and then stopped everything, in the third week, with the snow so deep on the roof that it bent the rafters inward like a hand pressing down on the shop.

"I remember," Cyrus said.

"Do you remember what came after."

She did. She remembered the debt. It had come in the spring, when the ice broke and the first ships came up the gray water, and with them the harbor guild's assessor, a soft-voiced man named Corvel who had smelled of cloves and had counted out their obligations on his fingers: the loan against the press, the loan against the building, the loan against the loan. Her father had stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands, and Cyrus had stood behind his legs, and she had learned, in that doorway, at nine years old, the first and most important lesson of the harbor district: that everything could be taken, and that the taking was always polite.

"I remember Corvel," she said.

"Corvel." Her father smiled, and it was the worst thing she had seen all night, worse than the warrant, worse than the letters in a dead man's hand. "Corvel was nothing. Corvel was a clerk with clove oil on his cuffs. But behind Corvel, Cyrus—behind every assessor, every inspector, every guild man who ever stood in that doorway with his hat in his hands—there was the Provisional Authority. And the Authority had decided, that same winter, that the harbor district was too fond of its own opinions. There had been pamphlets. You wouldn't remember. You were nine, and I kept you from remembering, which was the whole of my work those years, though you never knew it. Pamphlets, and a printer on Tanner's Row who had printed them, and what was done to that printer and his press and his two apprentices, and how the pieces of the press were left in the street for a week so that everyone who walked past could read the lesson in them."

The ice groaned beneath the floor, long and low, and Cyrus found that her hands had closed into fists in her lap without her permission.

"The Authority wanted a warrant," her father said. "A clean one. A legal one, with the right seals, the right faces, the right type—the type of a respectable shop, a shop with no history, a shop that had never printed so much as a rude word against the Provisional Authority. They came to me because I was respectable. Because I was afraid. Because a man who is afraid does good work." He turned a piece of type over under one finger, absently, the way he had done all her life, an unconscious caress of the craft. "They brought the text already written. All I had to do was set it."

"And you didn't read it." It was not a question either, and she was ashamed of it even as she said it, because she knew—she knew with the certainty of the daughter who had learned composition at his elbow before she could write a full sentence of her own—that he had read it. Every word. Her father read everything twice. He read the guild circulars twice. He read the customs manifests twice. He had read the text of that warrant twice, and he had set it in twelve-point Caslon with the long s that she had mis-sorted six months ago, and he had pulled the proof, and he had signed the guild register, and he had gone home and eaten his supper across the table from her and said nothing.

"I read it," he said.

The words fell into the room and lay there.

"Say it," Cyrus said. Her voice was steady now, which frightened her more than trembling would have. "Say it plainly. I want to hear it in your voice, not in mine. I've spent two days hearing it in my own voice, Father, and I can't— I need to hear what you did. From you."

Her father looked at her for a long time. And then, because he was her father, because he was the man who had taught her that a compositor's first duty was to the text—to the truth of the text, whatever the text was—he did not flinch from it.

"I knew the execution was staged," he said. "I knew because they told me, not in words but in the shape of the commission. A warrant for a magistrate already in custody, dated backward, sealed by an office that had no authority to seal it, signed by a colonel whose name I did not know and whose signature I was instructed to *imitate* rather than set in type, because a signature in type can be compared and a signature in ink cannot. I knew the trial had been a pantomime. I knew that Aldous Vane had stood up in that courtroom and said the one true thing anyone had said in that building in ten years, and I knew that the Authority could not let that thing be written down anywhere, and so they had me write it down wrong. I set a lie in twelve-point Caslon, Cyrus. My type. My hands. Your hands, because I put the case in front of you and told you it was a customs form, and you were sixteen, and you believed me, because I had spent sixteen years making sure you believed me about everything that mattered."

"Why." The word came out of her like something wrenched. "You could have refused. Tanner's Row—"

"Tanner's Row is a street with no printer on it now." He said it without heat, without drama, and that was the horror of it. "They gave me a choice, and I made it in the time it took them to say it. The shop, or the warrant. You, or the truth. And I chose you, and I have chosen you every day since, every day that the shop has stood and the bread has been on the table and you have grown up in a house that was not burned, and I would choose it again, Cyrus, I would choose it tomorrow, and I know—" his voice did break then, just slightly, a hairline fracture in a wall that had held for thirty years—"I know what that makes me. I know what it made me the day I did it. I have known for two years. I have set type for two years knowing that the finest piece of work this shop ever produced, the cleanest setting, the truest impression, was the instrument of a murder. And every morning I have opened this shop and sorted this type and taught you the craft, knowing that the craft itself—the thing I love most after you, the thing I love *in* you—had been used to kill a good man and bury the truth of it under ten thousand honest pages."

The lamp guttered. He rose, without seeming to decide to rise, and went to the window, and stood looking out at the dark where the harbor was, though nothing could be seen of it—only the far lights of the customs bridge, and the low white mass of the ice field, and the black seam of open water where the ice-breaker would work come thaw.

"You want to know the worst of it," he said, still facing the window. "The worst of it is not that I was afraid. Fear is honest. Fear I could carry. The worst of it is that after the first year, I stopped feeling it as a weight. I set it down somewhere, Cyrus, in some room in myself, and I shut the door, and I went on. That is what survival is. Not one great choice in one terrible moment. It is the choice, and then the forgetting, and then the forgetting of the forgetting. And I forgot so well that when you came down those stairs tonight and said the word *warrant*, some part of me—the part that shut the door—actually reached for the handle and found it rusted shut."

Cyrus sat very still. She was aware, distantly, of the sounds of the house: the wind, the ice, Tomas's slow tread overhead, the tick of the press cooling in the corner like a sleeping animal. She was aware that she was angry—she could feel it in her body, a hot wire running from her jaw down through her chest—and that the anger was real and earned and *not enough*, that it was already, even now, even in this first hour of knowing, curdling into something else. Something worse. Understanding.

Because she knew about the door. She had one too. She had kept her head down for twenty years; she had learned to sort type by feel in the dark so that she would never need a lamp that might be seen from the street; she had measured her whole life in what could be carried and what must be left, and she had called it discipline, and it had been, all along, the same rusted door in a different wall. Her father had set the lie. She had spent two years not asking what lay beneath the honest pages. Between them they had built a silence with the load-bearing strength of a cathedral, and she had helped build it, and she had called it love.

"Corvel came back," her father said. He had not turned from the window. "After the warrant. He stood where you're sitting—no. Where I'm sitting. And he said, *You've bought yourself a long life, printer. Spend it quietly.*" He laughed, short and terrible. "And I have. Twenty years of spending it quietly. And now a half-drowned man from the ice comes through my door with a dead man's letters, and my daughter looks at me across this bench, and I understand that the bill has come due. The Authority always sends its bills, Cyrus. Corvel taught me that. They just let the interest run."

"Then we pay it," Cyrus said.

He turned. The lamplight was behind her and she could not see his face well, but she could see the set of his shoulders, and she knew that set; it was the set he had when a job was hard and the deadline was close and the type was fighting him, the set that meant he had stopped being afraid and started being a printer.

"You don't know what you're saying."

"I know exactly what I'm saying. I've been saying it to myself for two days and pretending I wasn't." She stood up from Maren's stool. "You chose me over the truth twenty years ago, and I'm not going to shame you for it. I'm not. I sat where you sat, Father—I kept my own door shut my own way. But the truth is in this house now. It's in the font case with the broken type, and it's upstairs sleeping in the guest room, and it's not going back under the ice. The only question—the *only* question—is whether we carry it or whether it carries us."

For a long moment her father said nothing. Then he came back to the bench, and he did a thing he had not done since she was a child: he put his hand on the back of her head, among the loose hair at her neck, the way he used to when she got a line of type right on the first try, and he did not say *I'm sorry* and he did not say *forgive me*, because they were printers, and printers know that some things cannot be reset, only carried forward into the next page.

"The Authority will come," he said quietly. "When they trace the letters—when they trace *him*—they will come here, and they will not be Corvel with his clove oil. And they will not ask me twice."

"Then we'd best not be here when they do."

He nodded, slow, and turned back to the bench, and began, with hands that had gone steady again, to sort the mis-sorted case—the long s back among the s, the f among the f, the language restored to order, letter by letter, the way it always had been and, if she had anything to say about it, always would be.

It was while he worked, his head bent, that Cyrus saw it through the window over his shoulder: a light in the street. Not a lamp. A lantern, carried low, swinging with the rhythm of men walking—not one man, not two. A patrol, moving up the far side of the lane, unhurried, stopping at each doorway.

Stopping at the door of the shop two houses down.

Her father's hands went still in the type case.

"Cyrus," he said, without turning, without raising his voice at all, in the flat tone of a man who has spent twenty years being afraid and knows exactly what fear looks like when it comes up the street, "get the packet. And wake the boy. Quietly."

* * *

Upstairs, the guest room was dark. Cyrus stood in the doorway with her hand on the cold latch, listening to Tomas breathe, and below her, through the floorboards, she heard the knock at their own door—polite, measured, three raps, the knock of men who did not need to hurry.

"Tomas," she whispered. "They're here. Get up. We have minutes, not hours."

The bed creaked. In the dark, his eyes opened, already awake, already afraid, and Cyrus felt the night tilt beneath her like the ice shifting on the harbor—the sense, sudden and total, that the long quiet was over, and that everything she had ever carried was about to be weighed.
