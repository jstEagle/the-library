## 7. Boots on the Stair

Tomas Reyes had learned to measure danger in sounds.

In the capital, before the fall, he had measured it in silences—the particular quality of a room that had been listened to, the way a servant's footsteps changed when a letter had been opened and resealed. But Nagasaki's harbor taught a different grammar. Here danger came as sound: the groan of ice shifting in the roads, the creak of a hull frozen into its berth, the scrape of a shutter in wind. And now, on the fourth morning since he had crawled half-dead through the print shop door, it came as boots.

He was in the loft above the workshop, where Cyrus had made him a bed of sailcloth and wool blankets that smelled of ink and lamp oil. The fever had broken two nights ago; his hands had stopped shaking enough yesterday to hold a pen. He had been copying the letters—not the originals, never the originals, Cyrus had been emphatic about that, her voice dropping to a whisper as though the walls had ears, which in a print shop, Tomas supposed, they might—when the boots began.

Not one pair. Many. And not the loose, ambling tread of dockworkers or the scuff of fishermen coming up from the quay. These were boots that struck the frozen cobbles in unison, a rhythm like a drum, like a heart that had forgotten how to do anything but beat in time to orders.

Tomas set down the pen. His mouth had gone dry.

Below him, he heard Cyrus stop sorting type. The little sounds of her work—the click of lead against lead, the soft rasp of the composing stick—ceased all at once, the way a bird stops singing when a hawk's shadow crosses the grass. Then her footsteps, quick and light, up the stairs.

She came through the door without knocking, which she had never done before, and he understood from her face that she had heard them too.

"Inspectors," she said. "Six of them, maybe eight. They're working the fish market first. We have minutes. Perhaps less."

"The back—"

"Leads to the alley, and the alley leads to the customs road, and the customs road has a checkpoint at either end, and every soul coming out of this quarter this morning will be stopped and questioned." She was already moving as she spoke, pulling a blanket off the bed, folding it with the swift, violent neatness of someone who needed her hands to be doing something. "You can't run, Tomas. You'd be a limping man in a quarter where no one limps. They'd remember you by the time you'd gone fifty paces."

"So I hide."

"So you hide." She looked at him then, and he saw something in her face that frightened him more than the boots did: not fear, exactly, but the cold arithmetic of a woman calculating odds she did not like. "And the packet hides with you. No—wait." She held up a hand. "Not with you. If they find you, they find it. If they find it, they find you, and then everything—Aldous, the warrant, all of it—dies here in this room, and my father with it, and probably me."

"Then where?"

She didn't answer at once. She was looking past him, at the wall of the loft, where the old font cases were stacked—wooden trays of pigeonholes, each compartment once holding a thousand pieces of a single letter. Most of them were half-empty now, the type worn down or melted in lean years, the compartments holding nothing but the memory of letters. Broken type, her father called it. The ghosts of the alphabet.

"The cases," she said slowly. "The ones with the worn sorts. Nobody counts broken type. Nobody counts anything they think is worthless." She crossed to the stack and pulled out a case near the bottom, its compartments choked with battered pieces of lead, letters worn past legibility, some of them cracked, some fused into little clots by a fire years ago. "Help me empty the middle row. Quickly. And then you'll want to see where you're going, because once they start on this shop, you will not move a muscle until I come for you. Not a muscle. Not a breath if you can help it. Men have suffocated themselves hiding from searches—panicked, made a sound. You will not be one of them."

"How do you know all this?" he asked, working his fingers into the pigeonholes, prying out clumps of dead lead. "You speak like a woman who's been searched before."

The look she gave him was brief and did not invite further questions. "My father's shop has printed things the government did not like. Once, when I was twelve, they came. I hid in the paper cellar under a bale of rag stock for four hours. I could hear them upstairs, breaking things, asking my father questions in that pleasant voice they use." She emptied a compartment into her apron with a clatter like hail. "They left. My father smiled and bowed and they left, and we swept up, and he never spoke of it. That is how it is done, Tomas. You bow. You smile. You let them break what they want to break, and you keep what matters somewhere they will never think to look, because they are men who believe that everything worth having is worth displaying."

She lined the emptied compartments with a strip of oilcloth—the same oilcloth, he realized, that had wrapped the letters when they came to her—laid the packet flat, and covered it with a layer of broken type, and then another, and then slid the case back into the bottom of the stack and dragged a crate of binding presses in front of it.

"There," she said. Her hands were steady. His were not. "Now you."

* * *

The hiding place she had chosen for him was the paper cellar—the same cellar, he understood, where a twelve-year-old girl had once listened to her father's world being broken upstairs. It was a low brick vault beneath the workshop, reached through a trapdoor hidden under the flat-topped table where the great sheets were folded. It smelled of size and damp and the ghost of a thousand pages. She gave him a shuttered lamp, a flask of water, and instructions delivered in a voice so low he had to lean close to catch them.

"If we are taken, you stay here. Do you hear me? However long it takes. There are biscuits in the tin, there's water, there's a candle for when the lamp oil runs out. Maren may come—Maren is—was—my father's apprentice. If she comes, she'll knock twice, pause, knock once. Anyone else, you stay silent until they go away, and then you go out through the coal chute to the alley, and you take the letters, and you get to the ship, and you do not look back."

"And leave you?"

"Leave us." She said it without flinching, which somehow made it worse. "Tomas. Listen to me. The letters are not a favor to a dead man. They are the only thing standing between this city and another ten years of the lie. If the choice is ever between us and them, you choose them. Promise me."

He promised her, because there was no time to argue, and because he suspected that she was the sort of person who would know a false promise when she heard one, and he could not afford for her to know this one was false.

She closed the trapdoor above him, and the world became the size of a coffin.

* * *

He heard them come.

First the shop bell above the street door, its cheerful jingle absurd against what followed. Then a voice—smooth, unhurried, the voice of a man who had done this many times and enjoyed it in a way he had stopped examining—announcing an inspection under the authority of the Harbor Commission, pursuant to the search for a fugitive of the capital, one Tomas Reyes, late secretary to the magistrate's office, wanted for theft of official documents and conspiracy against the state.

His own name, in that pleasant voice, in that frozen cellar, made his skin crawl as though something had walked over his grave.

Then the boots. They came into the workshop above him, and the ceiling between Tomas and the world became a drumhead. Every footfall pressed down through the joists. He heard drawers opened—the wooden shriek of runners, the crash of contents dumped on the floor. He heard the font cases pulled from the stack, one after another, each one set down with a knock that went through his chest like a fist. He heard Cyrus's voice, level and polite, answering questions he could not make out, and her father's voice, older, slower, with the careful blankness of a man who had practiced being uninteresting for twenty years.

A case hit the floor. Not dropped—thrown. The clatter of type scattering across boards was deafening, and Tomas pressed his back against the brick and put his hands over his ears and hated himself for it.

"Your journeyman's papers," the pleasant voice said.

"I have no journeyman. It is myself and my daughter. My apprentice left us—three years ago now. Girls do not stay in the trade."

"And your daughter. Where is she?"

"Upstairs. Cyrus! Come down and show the inspectors your hands."

A pause. Footsteps on the stairs. Then Cyrus's voice, closer now, directly overhead, so near that Tomas could have touched her through the floorboards if the floorboards were not the whole width of the world between them. "My hands, sir?"

"Ink," the pleasant voice said, with satisfaction. "A printer's daughter with a compositor's calluses. And yet your father says you set no type. Curious."

"I set type, sir. What my father means is that I don't set it for customers. Father says my spacing is too tight. He says I crowd the letters like a woman packing a trunk." A small, self-deprecating laugh, and Tomas marveled at it—marveled at the craftsmanship of it, the way she had built a small, foolish, harmless person out of her own voice and offered it up like a coin to a tollkeeper.

"Mm. And this press. When did you last run it?"

"Sunday. Broadside notices for the fishmongers' guild. You'll find the forme still on the stone, if you'd like to read it. It's fish prices, sir. I'd offer you a copy but I don't imagine the Commission needs to know what herring costs."

Someone laughed—a real laugh, brief, quickly smothered. Even inspectors, it seemed, were men who had bought herring.

The search went on. It went on for what Tomas would later calculate as two hours and what felt, in the dark, as the length of a season. They went through the loft. They went through the living quarters. They pulled the paper cellar's legitimate stores—Tomas heard the bales of rag stock dragged, cut open with knives, the stuffing pulled out and left like the entrails of the room. Twice, boots stopped directly above the trapdoor, and he held his breath until his vision sparked, and once he felt the trapdoor shift—a hand testing it, finding it latched, moving on.

They questioned the father at length. Tomas caught fragments: *the stranger, the stranger, a man was seen, the night of the ninth, a man was carried into your shop—* and the old man's voice, puzzled, mild: *a man? Inspector, the only man who comes to my shop at night is the night-soil collector, and I'd thank you not to confuse my establishment with his.* A lie so smooth and so perfectly timed that Tomas understood, suddenly, where Cyrus had learned it. It was a family trade, this. Invisibility was not a temperament. It was an apprenticeship.

And then—near the end, when the pleasant voice had begun to fray at the edges, when the crashing had grown listless and the men were ready to be done—a new sound. Boots on the stair, heavier, and a second voice, younger and harder, and words that stopped Tomas's heart in the dark:

"Sir. The harbor watch reports a man answering the description was seen two nights past, near the fish market. A lamplighter swears to it. Tall, dark-haired, walking with a limp, favoring his left side."

A long silence. Tomas could hear the whole shop thinking.

"A limp," the pleasant voice said at last, thoughtfully. "And your household, printer, has no men in it at all. How convenient for you. And how inconvenient—for you—if it should turn out otherwise." Footsteps, slow, crossing the floor above. "I will leave two men outside your door. For your protection, you understand. The quarter is dangerous. There are fugitives about." A pause, and then, softer, almost kind: "We will come again, printer. We always come again. And when we do, I should very much like to meet this daughter of yours properly. She has clever hands, I think. Clever hands run in certain families. The Vane family, for instance. You printed for the magistrate's office once, didn't you? Before the traitor's execution. I've seen the warrants. Beautiful work."

Another silence, longer than the first.

"We printed what was commissioned," Cyrus's father said. "As every shop in this city does."

"Of course. Of course." The pleasant voice had gone quiet in a way that was worse than any shouting. "Keep your type warm, printer. Lead remembers. So do we."

The bell above the door jangled. The boots receded into the street, in step, in rhythm, fading toward the fish market. But not all of them. Tomas, listening with his whole body, counted the departing footsteps and counted again, and knew that two pairs had not gone with them. They settled outside the shop door with the patient weight of furniture, of winter itself, and one of them began, softly, to whistle—a marching tune, slow and tuneless, a sound designed to be heard through walls.

* * *

It was dark when the trapdoor opened.

Tomas's hand was on the coal-chute lever before he saw the shape above him and heard the knock—two, pause, one—repeated in a whisper, and then Cyrus's face, pale in the shuttered lamp light, looking down at him like a moon over a well.

"They're gone. Most of them." She lowered a hand and helped him up out of the cellar, and his legs, cramped from hours of stillness, nearly failed him. The workshop was a ruin—drawers gutted, type scattered across the floor in drifts, a press stone cracked where someone had set a boot on it to test the weight. Her father sat amid it in his chair, untouched, hands folded, wearing the expression of a man who had been through a storm and found the storm boring.

Tomas went straight to the font case. His fingers found the latched bottom of the stack, dragged the case out from behind the binding crate, and pried up the oilcloth, and there they were—the letters, the dead man's hand, the whole impossible truth of Aldous Vane, wrapped in lead and lying among broken letters like a body among its mourners.

"They never counted it," he said. "They pulled every case off that stack and never once counted the broken type."

"Because it's worthless," Cyrus said. "Because I made it worthless." She was looking at the scattered type on the floor, at the wreck of her father's shop, and her face in the lamplight was not relieved. It was something colder. "Tomas. What he said. About the lamplighter. About a man with a limp, two nights past, near the fish market."

"Yes."

"That was the night I carried you in." Her voice was very quiet. "They have a sighting. They have a description. And they've left two men at our door, and an inspector who thinks my father printed something beautiful once, and who intends to come back and meet me properly." She turned to him, and he saw that the fear in her face had finished its long fermentation and become something else entirely—something harder, and more dangerous, and more like resolve. "We are not hiding from a search anymore, Tomas. We are inside a hunt. And if we stay in this shop, the hunt ends here, with all of us in it."

Above the street door, through the shuttered window, the whistling went on—patient, tuneless, endless, like ice waiting for the spring.

"We need Maren," Cyrus said.
