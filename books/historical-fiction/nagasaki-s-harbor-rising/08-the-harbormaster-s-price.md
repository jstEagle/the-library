## 8. The Harbormaster's Price

The harbormaster's office sat at the end of a crooked pier like a tooth left standing in a rotted jaw, and Tomas Reyes approached it the way he had learned to approach all doors in the past two years: slowly, from the side, with his collar up and his face angled away.

Snow came off the harbor in flat gray sheets. Below the boards, the ice groaned—a long, tectonic complaint, the sound of a frozen river dreaming of movement it could not yet permit itself. He had crossed half the country on that sound's promise. *Hold until spring*, he told himself. *Hold until I am on the water.*

He climbed the salt-eaten stairs and knocked twice on the door marked with a faded anchor and the word *HARBOR* in letters that had once been gilded and were now the color of old teeth.

"Enter, then, if you're entering."

The office smelled of tallow, tar, and tea gone bitter from sitting too long. Odile Brannigan—no, that was not her name; the man behind the desk was called Quill, though no one living remembered whether that was a surname or an occupation—sat behind a counter scarred by twenty years of coins dragged across it. He was a small man built like a bollard: squat, iron-hooped, immovable. A stove ticked in the corner. On the wall behind him hung a board of keys, forty of them at least, each tagged with a ship's name, and beneath the board a shelf of ledgers bound in green canvas, their spines cracked like knuckles.

Quill looked up. His eyes did the thing Tomas had seen a hundred eyes do since the execution two years ago: they flicked to the wanted bill nailed beside the door, then to Tomas's face, then back to the bill, performing the arithmetic of reward versus trouble.

"You're not from here," Quill said.

"No one is from here," Tomas said. "It's Nagasaki's harbor."

A grunt that might have been laughter. "Sit, then, not-from-here. What do you want? Passage, I'm guessing, or you'd be selling me rope instead of looking at my ledgers like they owe you money."

"I want passage south. Before the ice breaks."

Quill poured himself tea without offering any. "Everyone wants passage before the ice breaks. After it breaks, nobody wants anything but to be warm." He drank. "When do you think the ice breaks, boy?"

"A fortnight. Maybe less."

"Maybe more. The ice doesn't keep your schedule." He set down the cup. "There's the *Kestrel's Mercy* going out when the channel opens. Grain run. She'll take deck passengers for eight silver if you can stand the smell of rats who've gotten fat enough to have opinions."

Tomas sat. His hands ached; they had ached since the river crossing, since the night he had come out of the shallows with the oilskin packet strapped against his chest like a second heart. He kept his hands below the edge of the desk. "Eight silver is fine."

"For deck. For a berth, twelve. For papers saying you're someone else—" Quill tapped the counter once, "—that's extra, and it depends on who you are. Or rather, who you aren't."

The stove ticked. Outside, through the frost-blinded window, a dockworker led a mule along the quay, its hooves throwing up little fans of snow. Somewhere beyond the customs bridge, a bell rang the hour—four bells, late afternoon, the light already dying into that blue-gray nothing that passed for evening here.

"My name is Reyes," Tomas said carefully. "Tomas Reyes."

"Mm." Quill turned a page in his ledger as though consulting something. He was not consulting anything; the page was blank except for a coffee ring shaped like a crescent moon. "Funny thing about names. I've been keeping this book eleven years, and every name in it has a price next to it, and every price gets paid eventually. Names are just prices wearing coats." He looked up. "So what's your coat worth, Reyes?"

"I don't follow."

"You follow fine. You're just hoping I'll lose interest before you have to answer." Quill folded his hands atop the ledger. "Here's how it goes, so we're both honest, which is a novelty for this office. You want out of the harbor district. You want it badly—I can tell because you've got the look of a man who's slept outdoors recently and eaten worse. There are three checkpoints between this pier and open water, and every guard on them answers to Colonel Ashe, and Colonel Ashe has put out bills with faces on them. Yours may or may not be one of the faces. I haven't decided yet whether to check."

Tomas's pulse went slow and cold, the way it did when fear stopped being a feeling and became a calculation. "And if you check?"

"Then I sell you to the colonel for whatever he pays for men, which is less than he pays for secrets but more than you're carrying in that coat, I'd wager." Quill said it pleasantly, almost gently, the way a man describes weather. "Or—and I offer this alternative purely out of professional curiosity—you tell me what you're carrying that makes you walk like a man with a knife between his shoulder blades, and we negotiate."

"I'm carrying nothing of value."

"Then you've got nothing to lose by showing me."

*Nothing to lose.* It was the oldest trap in the world and Tomas walked toward it anyway, because there was no way around it. Every road through this city ran through this office. He had known it since the fish market, since the whistling above the shuttered window, since the girl with the ink-stained fingers had dragged him half-dead across her threshold and asked him no questions—which should have warned him. Kindness always cost later. That was the lesson of the last two years, written in the margins of everything: *every ally is also a seller.* Some sold cheaply. Some sold dearly. But everyone's loyalty had a ledger line somewhere.

"The packet stays closed," Tomas said. "But I'll tell you what it is."

Quill spread his hands. *Go on.*

"Letters. Correspondence. In the hand of Aldous Vane."

The stove ticked. Somewhere under the floor, the ice groaned again, longer this time, like a door being forced in a house where no one lived.

Quill did not move for the space of three breaths. Then he rose, crossed to the door, opened it, looked both ways down the empty stair, shut it, dropped the bar, and came back to the desk moving faster than his shape suggested he could.

"Say that again," he said quietly, "and say it slower."

"Aldous Vane," Tomas repeated. "The magistrate they hanged—or said they hanged—two years ago. These are letters in his hand. Recent ones. Dated within the year."

"And you're telling me this why?"

"Because I need to reach the capital with them, and you control the only ship that sails before the seals close the harbor."

Quill lowered himself back onto his stool. He rubbed his jaw. Under the lamplight his face had changed—not softer, exactly, but hungrier, the hunger of a man who has spent his whole life trading in small coin and has suddenly heard the ring of gold.

"Do you know," he said, "what I was, before the regime gave me this office? I was a tally-clerk. Twelve hours a day counting herring heads for a merchant who cheated me of my winter wage and called it patriotism. When the new government came, they asked me one question: *can you count?* And I said yes, sir, I can count, and they gave me a chair and a stamp and a cut of every cargo that moves through this harbor, legal or otherwise, and I have counted ever since." He tapped the green ledgers. "Everything that passes this quay, I know its weight. Everything that leaves, I know its destination. And in eleven years I have learned exactly one law that never fails."

"Which is?"

"Everything is for sale. Everything. Loyalty, silence, love, God—the only variable is the number of zeroes." He leaned forward. "So let us speak numbers. You want passage on the *Kestrel's Mercy*. Berth, papers, and my personal guarantee that none of my people mention your face to the colonel's people. I can do that. I have done it for smugglers, deserters, and one bishop whose tastes I will take to my grave."

"Name it."

"The letters."

The word landed in the room and stayed there, enormous, absurd.

Tomas laughed before he could stop himself—a short, ugly bark of disbelief. "The letters. You want the letters."

"I want the letters."

"They're worth nothing to you. They're paper. They're dangerous paper—they're a death sentence to anyone caught holding them. You'd be buying your own gallows."

"I'd be buying leverage," Quill corrected. "Do you understand what a dead magistrate's handwriting is worth in this city? Half the quarter whispers that Vane was murdered and the other half calls them liars, and the government hangs anyone loud enough to prove it. If those letters are what you say—if they're genuine—then whoever holds them holds the regime's founding lie in his fist. That's not paper, boy. That's a crown. Or a noose. Depends entirely on the hands." He spread his own hands, palms up, calloused and steady. "Mine are careful hands. I've held dangerous cargo for eleven years without spilling a drop."

"And what would you *do* with it? Sell it back to them? Blackmail the colonel?"

"What I do with my purchases is my business. What concerns you is simpler: no letters, no ship." Quill's voice stayed pleasant, but the pleasantness had hardened around something now, the way ice hardens around a stone. "You misunderstand me if you think this is greed. Well—no. It is greed. But it's also arithmetic. If I put you on that ship and Ashe learns afterward what sailed out of my harbor under my nose, I hang. My ledgers burn. My wife and daughter inherit my debts. So either the letters stay with me as insurance, or you find another harbor, and I wish you joy of the swimming, because the channel is sealed."

Tomas stood up. The stool scraped. His hands had begun to shake and he pressed them flat against his thighs.

"There's another copy," he said. "You should know that before you bargain. If anything happens to me, the letters surface anyway. From directions you can't predict."

"That's a lie," Quill said comfortably. "If there were copies you'd have burned yours by now and sent the copies ahead. No one carries the original unless it's the only one." He poured more tea. "Sit down, Reyes. We're not enemies. Enemies don't negotiate; they just hurt each other until one of them stops. We're traders. Sit."

Tomas did not sit. He stood at the barred door and looked out the frost-furred window at the harbor, at the black shapes of hulls locked in ice, at the far shore where the customs bridge lanterns were coming alight one by one like a string of feverish eyes. Two years ago he had been a clerk too—a records clerk in the Hall of Writs, a man who believed in the difference between ink and blood. Then Aldous Vane had handed him a satchel in a corridor and said, *if anything happens to me, these go east,* and two weeks later the government announced the magistrate's treason and his hanging, and the body they showed was face-down and long-dead, and every record bearing Vane's seal vanished overnight from the archives Tomas himself maintained. He had understood then that ink was blood—that the whole regime rested on a single forged warrant, a lie set in type and stamped and filed—and that the only honest thing left in the world was a dead man's handwriting.

He had carried it through rivers and forests and the backs of wagons. He had watched the courier before him die in a ditch outside Marren Ford with the oilskin still under his coat. He had promised—God help him, he had promised a dying man's memory, a live man's ghost—that these letters would reach daylight.

And now a bollard-shaped man with bitter tea wanted to buy them for a berth on a grain ship.

"Every ally is also a seller," Tomas said aloud, not meaning to say it aloud.

"Yes," Quill agreed, with real warmth. "Isn't it wonderful? At least sellers are honest about the transaction. It's the ones who claim to be giving freely you want to watch." He slid a sheet of paper across the counter and a stub of pencil after it. "Now. Here's my final position, and I'll not move from it. Passage, papers, protection: yes. Free: no. The price is the letters—all of them—or you can leave my office and try the fish market gate with your pretty face and see how far your principles carry you. Though—" he raised one finger, "—I'll tell you something free, since I'm feeling generous. The girl. The printer's daughter. She and her father have moved cargo through this harbor for twenty years without paying me a copper, because old Whitlock once held a debt over my head that I won't discuss. If you're staying under their roof—and you are, don't look surprised, I know everyone in my harbor—then you already have better allies than me. Ask them about the third checkpoint. Ask them who keeps it. Then come back and we'll talk numbers again."

The cold that went through Tomas had nothing to do with the stove's failing heat.

Ashe. The commandant. He had not told Cyrus and her father about the third checkpoint's keeper; he had barely let himself think it. If Quill knew—if the whole harbor knew—then the net was smaller than he'd dared fear, and the letters had been heavy in his coat for nothing, because weight meant nothing to a regime that owned every gate.

He picked up the pencil. Put it down.

"I need time," he said.

"Time is also for sale," Quill said. "Yours is running."

* * *

Tomas walked back along the quay with the wind at his back and the packet against his chest, and the ice spoke underneath him in voices.

He tried to reason with himself the way he used to reason over ledger columns. Give the letters to Quill: the man was right that careful hands existed, and a harbormaster's blackmail might buy years, might even buy reform—but blackmail was a candle; it burned down. Sooner or later Quill would spend the secret, or sell it, or die holding it, and the truth would die with him in a locked drawer. Keep the letters and flee without papers: the fish market gate, the customs bridge, the quay—three doors, all locked, all watched, all answering to the signature he had found scrawled beneath Vane's forged death warrant when Cyrus held it to the lamp two nights ago. *Ashe.* The enemy was the architecture itself.

And beneath the reasoning, quieter, a thought he did not want:

*The printer's daughter set that warrant. Her own hands.*

She had gone white as paper when she saw it, and she had not spoken of it since, but Tomas had watched her sort type by feel in the dark that first night, quick and sure, measuring her life in what could be carried—and he had wondered what else her hands had carried without her knowing. She owed the truth a debt. Perhaps she knew it. Perhaps that was why she'd said *we need Maren* instead of *you should go alone*—why she was already spending herself on his mission as though paying something back.

The lamp in the print shop window was lit when he reached the street. Through the shutter slats, a thin blade of light lay across the snow. Inside, faintly, he could hear the small iron music of type being sorted—her rhythm, patient, endless, like ice waiting for the spring.

He stopped in the shadow of the doorway, hand on the packet, and made himself face the arithmetic honestly, because Quill was right about one thing: sellers were the only honest men left.

Quill wanted the letters. Maren would want something—he had met smugglers; they always wanted something. The sympathetic world, the kind world, the world of dragged-in-from-the-snow mercy: it all had prices, and he had arrived with no coin but the truth itself, which meant that sooner or later he would have to decide what the truth was *for*. To be traded. To be hidden. Or to be spent—all at once, wildly, in a way no one could buy back.

He pushed open the shop door, and the warmth and the smell of ink rolled over him, and Cyrus looked up from the case, and her face told him she had already guessed how the negotiation had gone.

"He wants the letters," Tomas said. "Doesn't he. Everyone does."

Cyrus set down the composing stick very carefully, the way people set down things they are afraid of breaking.

"Then it's time you met Maren," she said. "Before someone sells you to her first."
