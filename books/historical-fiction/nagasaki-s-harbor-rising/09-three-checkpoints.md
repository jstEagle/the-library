## 9. Three Checkpoints

The map was drawn in charcoal on the back of a ruined proof sheet—a misprint of a grain tariff notice that had been destined for the pulping barrel anyway. Cyrus chose it for exactly that reason: if the inspectors came again, no one would look twice at a spoiled broadside pinned beneath the press. The harbor district spread across it in her quick, sure strokes, the streets she had walked every day of her life rendered suddenly as territory, as terrain, as a thing with teeth.

She worked by the light of a single lamp turned low, its shade angled toward the wall so that nothing spilled into the street below. Tomas sat across from her, his hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea gone cold an hour ago. He hadn't drunk it. He hadn't done much of anything since the inspectors left except watch her draw, and she could feel his attention on her hands the way one feels weather changing—pressure without sound.

"The fish market gate first," she said, and touched the charcoal to the paper at the harbor's eastern mouth. "It opens before dawn and closes when the last cart leaves, which in winter is mid-afternoon. Two guards on the gate itself, one on the weighbridge, and a sergeant who walks the line of stalls pretending he isn't counting heads." She drew the gate as a small square, then crossed it out and drew it again, larger. "In herring season there are forty carts through that arch between first light and eight o'clock. Nobody searches forty carts. They search the ones that look nervous."

"And we'll be the ones that look nervous."

"We'll be barrels," Cyrus said. "Barrels don't look nervous."

Tomas almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. He was younger than she'd first thought—or perhaps only thinner than she'd first thought, hunger and fear having pared him down to something wiry and essential. The frostbite on his left ear had scabbed over; the bruise along his jaw had gone the yellow-green of old ice. Two days in the shop's back room, sleeping in the type store among the cases of discarded matrices, had brought him back from whatever gray country the river had nearly kept him in. But he still moved like a man listening for boots, and when the wind rattled the shutters his whole body flinched before his mind caught up.

"Second checkpoint." She dragged the charcoal down the curve of the harbor road to where the customs bridge crossed the tidal channel. "The bridge is the real wall. Everything coming off the water passes over it—the fish, the timber, the mail, the passengers. There's a customs house at the near end with four clerks and a chit system. You can't walk onto that bridge without a stamped chit naming your cargo, your origin, your destination, and the fee paid. The stamps are brass, kept under lock, and each clerk has his own countermark besides." She paused, charcoal hovering. "I've set chits before. Legitimate ones. I know the format down to the kerning."

"You're saying you can forge them."

"I'm saying I know what they look like." She would not say more than that yet, not because the lie required effort but because the truth did. Her father's shop had printed half the paperwork that moved through this harbor for twenty years—bills of lading, manifests, permits, the dull administrative sediment of a regime that loved stamps the way other governments loved flags. A printer who could not reproduce a seal was a printer who did not eat. That thought arrived and sat down inside her chest like a guest who intended to stay, and she pushed past it. "The third checkpoint is the problem."

She drew the ice-breaker quay at the harbor's western tip, where the channel met the open water, and above it she wrote nothing at all, because there was nothing to write that would make it smaller.

"The *Meridian* sails on the spring tide or not at all. Once the ice breaks and the harbor seals for the season, nothing larger than a fishing skiff moves until thaw—and a skiff won't outrun a patrol cutter. So everyone leaving legally funnels through the quay, and the quay has a commandant's checkpoint, not a customs one. Soldiers, not clerks. Papers checked against the passenger manifest, names read aloud, faces looked at properly." She looked up at him. "Your face is on a wanted bill in the fish market. Mine isn't on any bill, but my name will be in the manifest records the moment anyone thinks to look for a printer's daughter traveling with a stranger. And the letters—" she tapped the oilskin packet, wrapped now in oilcloth and hidden inside the case of broken type where the inspectors' fingers had passed within inches of it two nights ago, "—the letters are worth enough that they'll turn that quay inside out looking for them."

"So we need papers that survive being looked at properly," Tomas said slowly, "at a military checkpoint, run by whoever commands this harbor."

"Colonel Ashe," Cyrus said, and watched the name land on him like a dropped weight. "You know him?"

"I know the name. Everyone from the capital knows the name." His voice had gone flat, careful. "He signed the order for the executions. All of them. Vane's included."

*Vane's included.* The words passed through her and left a coldness behind, though she could not have said why—she had never known Aldous Vane except as a name on pamphlets and proclamations, a magistrate made into a martyr, a story everyone in the quarter told in lowered voices. But the warrant in her type drawer bore signatures too, and she had been turning that fact over in the dark hours like a stone with something underneath it. Later. There would be time for later, if they lived to have it.

She bent back over the map. "Then here is what we have, and here is what we don't."

* * *

What they had: a press, a stock of type, ink enough for a season, a cellar of paper rags waiting to be pulped, and two days of relative quiet while the inspectors chased their fugitive through the tanners' quarter on a false trail Maren's people had laid for them. What they had, though Cyrus did not say it aloud, was also herself—her hands, which had been setting type since she was six years old and cutting it since she was twelve; her memory, which held the exact dimensions of every official form this harbor used; and her father's silence, which she had not yet found a way to forgive and so carried instead, the way one carries a stone in the boot.

What they didn't have: passage money beyond a few coins; a single legitimate document between them; and any guarantee that the *Meridian*'s master would take passengers whose papers were even slightly wrong, given that the penalty for carrying fugitives was the same as the penalty for being one.

"It's not enough," Tomas said, when she finished laying it out. He said it without drama, the way a man reports the depth of a hole he's fallen into. "Even perfect forgeries. Even luck. Three checkpoints, each one worse than the last, and the third one commanded by the man who signed the death warrants. We're carrying the one object in this city that he cannot allow to reach a ship."

"Yes."

"You say that very calmly."

Cyrus set down the charcoal and looked at her hands. They were stained black to the second knuckle, as they always were, as they had been since childhood; her mother used to joke that she'd been born mid-print. Calm was not quite the word for what she felt. It was closer to the stillness that came over her at the composing stick, when the work demanded everything else fall away—fear, hope, the noise of the street, the whole trembling apparatus of being a person. There was a job in front of her. The job had steps. Steps could be taken.

"My father spent two years teaching me that the safest place in the world is the one nobody looks at," she said. "Be dull. Be punctual. Be forgettable. Print wedding announcements and grain tariffs and let the revolution happen to other people." She flexed her stained fingers. "That worked right up until the truth landed in our doorway half-frozen. Now nobody's looking away, Tomas. The inspectors came to our shop. They put their hands in our type cases. The invisibility is already gone—I'm just the last one in this room to admit it." She met his eyes. "So if I can't be invisible anymore, I'd rather be useful."

For a long moment he said nothing. Outside, the wind came off the frozen harbor with that low moan it made at dusk, the sound the old fishermen called the ice talking, and somewhere down the lane a dog answered it, and then the street was quiet again in the particular way streets were quiet now—listening.

"All right," Tomas said finally. "Teach me."

"Teach you what?"

"Whatever you know. The forms, the seals, the format of the chits. If we're going to do this, I can't be cargo the whole way. Cargo gets thrown overboard when the ship takes water." He leaned forward, and in the lamplight she saw that the desperation in him had changed shape overnight—it was still desperate, but it had organized itself, the way water organizes into ice. "You said the guards search the carts that look nervous. I've been nervous my whole life. Show me how to stop."

* * *

They began with the customs chit, because it was the simplest and because beginning with the hardest thing would have broken them both.

Cyrus pulled a genuine chit from the drawer of saved samples her father kept against the day a client disputed a bill—one from the previous autumn, stamped and countersigned, issued for a shipment of cured cod. She smoothed it flat on the table between them and watched Tomas study it the way he probably studied wanted bills: memorizing, cataloguing, committing every detail to the part of himself that survived.

"Four sections," she said. "Cargo description, origin, destination, fee. Then the stamp, then the clerk's countermark. The stamp is the hard part—we can't cut brass, but we don't need to. The stamp goes on in ink, and ink can be transferred. I'll show you tomorrow. Tonight you learn the hand."

"The hand?"

"Clerks write these out themselves before stamping them. Each clerk has his own script, and the sergeants at the bridge know their clerks' hands the way you know a friend's knock. Get the hand wrong and the stamp doesn't matter." She took up a pen and, on scrap paper, reproduced the looping, cramped script of the sample chit—once, twice, a third time, faster each pass. "This is Clerk Osei. Left-handed—you can tell because his letters lean the wrong way and his ink smears trailing, not leading. He abbreviates 'cured' as 'cur.' He writes his sevens with a crossbar. Small things. The kind of small things that hang people."

Tomas picked up the pen. His first attempt was terrible. His second was worse, because he tried too hard. But his third had the lean of it, almost, and by the tenth sheet—he filled them methodically, without complaint, the way a man bailing a boat works—something in his wrist had learned what her wrist had known for years.

"You've forged before," she said. Not a question.

"I've survived before." He blew on the wet ink. "There's a difference, but it's narrower than you'd think."

They worked until the lamp oil ran low, and then she trimmed the wick and they worked in the smaller light, and the shop around them settled into its nighttime sounds—the tick of cooling metal, the sigh of the building against the wind, the groan far off of the harbor ice shifting in its sleep. Twice, footsteps passed in the lane below, and both times they stopped working and listened until the steps faded, and both times resumed without discussion, and Cyrus understood that they had passed some threshold neither of them had named: they were no longer two strangers sharing a crisis. They were conspirators. The word should have frightened her. Instead it felt like setting type—each letter in its place, the line beginning to hold.

Near midnight her father came down the stair, moving carefully in the dark, and stood at the edge of the lamplight holding a candle he did not light. He looked at the map on the table, at the practice sheets scattered thick as snowfall, at his daughter's ink-blackened hands and the stranger bending over them, and whatever he meant to say, he looked at it a long time and did not say it. He only set a loaf of black bread and a wedge of hard cheese on the table's corner, the way he had set food before his apprentices in the years when he'd had them, and went back up the stairs without a word.

Cyrus watched him go and felt the stone in her boot shift.

"He knew," Tomas said quietly, when the door above closed. "About the warrant. Didn't he."

"Yes."

"And you haven't—"

"There's no time to fight about yesterday," she said. "Yesterday is a locked room. Tomorrow is a quay with soldiers on it." She gathered the practice sheets into a stack, to burn them in the morning stove, and paused with the topmost page in her hand—Tomas's best attempt, the Osei hand nearly true, leaning its wrong-way lean. "But when this is over, that conversation happens. I want you to remember that I said so."

"I'll remember."

She believed him, and that surprised her—how quickly belief had become possible again, after two years of practicing disbelief. She banked the lamp, folded the map into the oilskin packet alongside the dead man's letters, and slid the packet home into the font case of broken type, where the evidence of a murdered magistrate lay hidden among the orphaned letters of words no one would ever print.

Tomorrow they would cut a seal. Tomorrow she would teach him transfer and countermarks and the thousand small treacheries of paper. Tomorrow they would walk the route once, casually, separately, the way people walk everywhere in this city—heads down, hands in pockets, forgettable.

And in five days, if the ice held and the forgeries held and their nerve held, the *Meridian* would take the truth out of Nagasaki's harbor or take them down trying.

Cyrus lay awake in the loft above the press long after Tomas's breathing slowed, listening to the ice talk out on the black water, and for the first time in two years the sound did not feel like a warning.

It felt like a countdown.
