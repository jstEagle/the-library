## 10. Lessons in Forgery

Cyrus woke before dawn to the smell of hot metal and the sound of Tomas already awake.

He was at her workbench when she came down the narrow stair, sitting where she sat, his sleeves pushed up, turning one of her gravers over in his fingers with the careful ignorance of a man holding a surgeon's blade. The lamp between them threw their shadows huge against the type wall, two giants bent over a scrap of brass no bigger than a thumbnail.

"You're up early," she said.

"I slept four hours and dreamed of checkpoints." He set the graver down as if it might go off. "I thought I'd practice looking innocent."

"Sit still, then. Innocence begins with your hands." She took the graver back from him. "A man who fidgets at a customs bridge has already confessed to something. Half of forgery isn't the paper. It's the body behind the paper."

"And the other half?"

She lit the second lamp and pulled her loupe from its drawer, settling it against her eye socket the way she had done ten thousand times, until the world shrank to a bright circle and everything outside it became rumor. "The other half is knowing what the examiner expects to see," she said, "and giving him exactly that, and nothing more."

---

The seal was the harder lesson, so she started there.

Colonel Ashe's commandant's seal—the one that would open the third checkpoint, if it ever came to that—was a fortress: crossed anchors beneath a rising sun, ringed in lettering worn nearly smooth by twenty years of stamping. But the customs chit for the bridge only needed the deputy harbormaster's mark, a plain ship's wheel inside a double border, and the deputy harbormaster, if he had not changed his habits since Cyrus last saw his signature on freight manifests, pressed his wax carelessly and always slightly off-center, tilted to the left like a man leaning out of wind.

"We don't copy the seal," she explained, heating the sealing spoon over the lamp flame while Tomas watched the wax soften with the fascination of a child at a hearth. "We copy his *mistake*. A perfect impression on a chit would be more suspicious than an imperfect one. Perfection says *made with intent*. His tilt says *a tired man at the end of a long queue*."

"That's monstrous," Tomas said, and there was something in his voice—not quite admiration, not quite horror—that made her glance up.

"It's observation," she said. "Everything a forger needs, someone honest already made. We just keep it."

She poured the wax onto a slate tile, pressed her own carved matrix into it—she had cut the wheel overnight, three hours of work compressed into strokes she could have done sleeping—and peeled it back. Tomas leaned close.

"It's good," he said. "It's very good."

"It's adequate." She held it beside the reference impression she had copied from a genuine chit Maren's people had once carried through, back when Maren was still speaking to this family. "Look at the border. Here—" she touched the upper rim with a needle "—his wax pooled thin and the letters broke. Mine are whole. If a clerk studies it, whole letters will itch at him without his knowing why. So we break them." She took the finest graver and opened two hairline gaps in the wax matrix itself, so every future impression would carry the flaw faithfully, a small inherited wound.

Tomas watched her do it and was quiet a long moment.

"You could have been anything," he said finally. "With hands like that."

"I am something," Cyrus said. "I'm a printer's daughter who can make a lie look tired." She did not know why the words came out bitter; she only knew that they were true, and that Tomas heard the truth in them because he looked away quickly, and said nothing, and began learning to pour wax.

* * *

He was a bad student and then, abruptly, a fair one.

His first attempts at the transfer—lifting the deputy harbormaster's hand from a model chit onto blank stock—came out ghosted and smeared, ink bleeding into the paper's tooth like a bruise. He swore under his breath in a language she didn't recognize, some coastal dialect full of soft vowels, and Cyrus found herself almost smiling.

"You're pressing too hard. You're treating paper like an enemy."

"In Quintero we treated everything like an enemy."

"Well, stop it. Paper remembers pressure. You want to persuade it, not defeat it." She stood behind him—close, closer than she had meant to—and put her hand over his on the burnisher. "Here. Long strokes. Let the tool do the work. Feel it?"

Under her palm his hand went still. Not stiff—still, the way water goes still, waiting. "I feel it," he said, and his voice had dropped half a register, and Cyrus stepped back faster than she needed to and busied herself with the ink stone.

They worked through the morning like that. He learned the countermarks, the little private marks clerks added in margins—a dot above the date, a hook through the fee numeral—to prove the chit hadn't merely been copied but *processed*. He learned which inks faded and which endured, which papers carried watermark threads and which lied about it. He learned that a forged document should never be too clean; it should smell of the office it came from, tobacco and damp and other people's hands, so Cyrus brewed tea and let the finished chits dry above the cup until they drank the steam and the staleness both.

By midday he could produce a customs chit that she judged—turning it in the lamplight, hunting for the flaw that would hang them—would pass a bored clerk nine times in ten.

"The tenth time?" he asked.

"The tenth time you talk," she said. "That's why we're practicing your talking next."

* * *

In the afternoon, as promised, he taught her the streets.

Not by map—he drew none, saying maps got people killed when they fell into the wrong pockets—but by walking, separately, ten paces apart, the way they'd agreed. He led her out through the fish market's back lanes while the gutters still ran with meltwater and the ice-sellers were shouting down the price of yesterday's blocks, and he pointed with nothing, told her things with his eyes and a tilt of his chin, and she memorized them the way she memorized type cases: by position, by weight, by the logic of the whole.

The smokehouse alley where the guards took their bribes at noon and were therefore blindest. The courtyard behind the salt warehouse with a drain wide enough for a slim man—if the drain grate hadn't been replaced since autumn; check it. The roofline of the cooperage, low on the harbor side, that a desperate person could cross in eight seconds if the person on watch knew to count. He taught her which doorways belonged to which loyalties—which shutter would open two inches at a whistle, which cellar held men who would sell you to the magistracy by supper and which held men who would simply charge you more.

"Why are you telling me all this?" she asked quietly, when they paused under the eaves of a shuttered chandlery, watching sleet needle the harbor. "You could keep this knowledge. Make yourself indispensable."

Tomas looked out at the gray water, at the ice plates grinding their slow argument against the quay pilings.

"Because knowledge kept is knowledge that dies with you," he said. "I've carried these letters alone through two provinces. Do you know what that's like? Every stranger is either a knife or a wall, and you can never be sure which until it's too late. For two years I have known things no one else knows and it has made me—" he searched for the word, found it, didn't soften it "—lonely in a way I wouldn't wish on anyone. You're the first person since Aldous died who knows what I know. I'm not going to hoard that. Hoarding is how they win."

Cyrus said nothing. Under the eaves, out of the sleet, she found that her throat had gone tight, and she resented it, and filed the feeling away with the other dangerous inventory she was keeping lately.

They walked on.

* * *

The smugglers' quarter proper began where the city stopped pretending. Past the customs house the streets narrowed and leaned together overhead until the sky was a gray seam, and the shops sold things with the prices unwritten, and everyone looked at everyone else exactly long enough to establish that neither of them had seen anything. Tomas moved differently here—shoulders loose, pace unhurried, nodding to certain doors without stopping—and Cyrus studied the transformation the way she studied a new typeface, cataloguing what changed.

"How do you know this quarter?" she murmured. "You washed up on our step half-drowned."

"Aldous sent me this way twice before, carrying messages. There's a network here. Small. Careful." His eyes flicked to a woman mending nets under an awning, then away. "Mostly it's people like you. Printers, dockclerks, a harbormaster's widow, a guard or two whose consciences cost them sleep. People who were invisible right up until they chose not to be."

"And they're still alive?"

"Some." He let the word stand. "Enough."

At the mouth of Tanner's Row he stopped her with a hand—not touching her, hovering an inch from her sleeve, a boundary politely observed—and nodded across the street. A boy of perhaps twelve was chalk-marking doors, a quick symbol at each lintel, moving with the bored efficiency of a child doing a chore.

"What's he marking?"

"Prices," Tomas said. "Or warnings. Or nothing. In this quarter the chalk means whatever the reader needs it to mean. That's the whole art of surviving here, Cyrus. Say true things in a language only your friends read." He glanced sideways at her, and there was something wry and warm underneath the wariness. "It's not so different from what you do at the press. You just use lead instead of chalk."

"It's very different," she said. "Chalk washes off."

"So does blood," Tomas said, "eventually. That's the problem with this city. Everything washes off eventually. That's why the letters matter—they're the one thing nobody's managed to wash away yet."

* * *

They came back to the shop as the light failed, taking separate streets and arriving within minutes of each other, casual as strangers. Her father was asleep in his chair with an almanac open on his chest, and Cyrus covered him with a blanket without waking him, and tried—failed—not to think of what he had confessed, the warrant, the lie he had set in type to keep bread on this table. Understanding, she was discovering, did not end betrayal; it just moved in upstairs from it.

In the workshop, Tomas laid out his day's work: six chits, drying on the line above the teacup, smelling convincingly of bureaucracy. She inspected each one. On the fourth she stopped.

"This one's better than mine."

"No, it isn't."

"Tomas." She held it to the lamp. "The tilt. You got the lean better than my master impression. Look—it pools here, at the bottom left, exactly like a tired man rushing. I engineered the flaw and you *inhabited* it."

He laughed—a short, surprised sound, as if it had escaped custody—and rubbed the back of his neck. "Maybe I've spent enough years being tired and rushed that it comes naturally."

"Then you're wasted as a courier. You should have been a forger."

"My mother would be horrified. She wanted me to be a priest." He said it lightly, but something moved behind it, some country and family very far away and probably gone, and Cyrus recognized the tone because she used it herself whenever anyone asked about her mother.

They worked on in silence after that, but it was a different silence than the shop had ever held. Two years of her father's heavy, guilty quiet, broken now by the small sounds of a shared labor: the scrape of the burnisher, the hiss of wax, Tomas humming three notes of some song under his breath when he thought she wasn't listening, catching himself, then—not catching himself. Once, reaching for the same ink stone, their hands touched and neither of them withdrew immediately, and Cyrus felt the moment the way she felt a hairline crack in a forme—as something that must be noted, measured, decided upon later, when there was time.

There would be no time. In four days the ice would break or it wouldn't. She withdrew her hand and passed him the stone.

* * *

Near midnight they walked the route one final time, together this time, because tomorrow there would be no more walking it. Fish market gate. Customs bridge. Ice-breaker quay. They stood at the rail above the black harbor and looked at the third checkpoint's lanterns burning far off on the quay, small and orderly and lethal, and Cyrus felt the old vertigo rise—Ashe's name in her mind, Ashe's signature beneath the forged warrant, the enemy waiting at the gate wearing the law like a coat.

"If it goes wrong at the bridge," Tomas said, "you run. Not me—you. You take the copies we made and go to ground in the quarter, and I'll draw them."

"They'll shoot you."

"Probably." He said it the way he said most true things, plainly, watching the water. "But the letters survive either way, and the letters are the point. I'm a courier, Cyrus. Couriers are replaceable. Truths aren't."

"You're not replaceable," she said, before she could stop herself, and then the words were out in the cold air between them, irrevocable as wax on paper, and neither of them took them back.

Below them the ice groaned—a long, deep complaint, the sound of a season ending—and somewhere out on the black water a fissure ran with a crack like a mast going. Closer than yesterday. A day sooner than the harbormaster had promised.

Tomas straightened. "That's early."

"It's early," Cyrus agreed, and the countdown in her chest, which had been a comfort, turned suddenly into something with teeth. Four days had become fewer. And fewer days meant one thing she had been avoiding since the plan was drawn: the fish market gate was too well watched to pass on charm alone, and charm was the one thing neither of them could forge.

There was exactly one person in Nagasaki's harbor who could get herring barrels past that gate without questions—and Cyrus owed her family nothing, and the favor Maren would ask had no ceiling on it.

"She'll drive a hard bargain," Cyrus said quietly, to the harbor, to Tomas, to herself.

"Then tomorrow," Tomas said, "we find out what it is."

Out on the water, the ice groaned again, longer this time, and did not stop.
