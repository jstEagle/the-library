## 11. The Fish Market Gate

The herring had been dead for three days, and Tomas Reyes was beginning to envy them.

He lay curled in the bottom of a barrel that smelled of brine and old ice, his knees drawn to his chest, his spine bent against the curved staves in a way no living body was meant to bend. Above him, stacked barrel upon barrel, the morning's catch pressed down like a lid of silver scales. Every jolt of the cart sent a fresh seepage of fish liquor through the gaps between barrels, dripping cold against the back of his neck, soaking into the collar of a coat Maren's man had given him — a coat that had belonged, by the smell of it, to a fisherman who had died in it.

*This is what freedom smells like,* he thought, and almost laughed, and did not dare.

The cart moved slowly. That was the worst of it. He had imagined speed, imagined the gate flashing past in a blur of torchlight while he held his breath for ten seconds, twenty, thirty. But the fish market road was packed with carts this morning — the last great hauling before the harbor sealed, every merchant in the district desperate to move their goods before the river closed like a fist — and so they crept, stop and start, stop and start, toward a checkpoint he could hear long before he could see it: the ring of iron-shod boots on frozen cobbles, the flat voice of a sergeant calling *papers, papers*, the wet cough of a man who had been standing in the cold since midnight.

Cyrus was somewhere behind him. Another barrel, another cart — Maren had insisted on splitting them, two carts, two chances, because one cart stopped was a catastrophe and two carts stopped was only half one. Tomas had argued. She had looked at him with those grey eyes that never seemed to blink enough, and said, "You're the one on the wanted bills. I'm just a printer's daughter carrying herring." As if that settled everything. As if her face wasn't known too now, after the search of the shop, after the inspector's clerk had copied their names into his ledger with a pen scratch that Tomas still heard sometimes when he tried to sleep.

The letters were against his chest. They were always against his chest. The oilskin packet rode under his shirt, bound tight with cord, warm from his own body heat, and he had caught himself more than once resting his hand over it the way a man touches a wound — not to heal it, but to confirm it's still there. Two years of Aldous Vane's handwriting, carried through rivers and checkpoints and the smugglers' quarter where even Maren's people walked in pairs and spoke in half-sentences. Two years of proof that the regime's founding atrocity was ink and nothing more. And now all of it came down to a gate, a cart, and whether a barrel of rotten herring could hold its silence.

The cart lurched forward again. Through a knothole in the stave beside his ear — a knothole he had found in the first minute and memorized like a prayer — Tomas could see a slice of the world: grey sky, the corner of the customs house with its new-painted eagle above the door, the paint still sharp because the building was only four years old, because everything official in this city was only four years old or younger, built atop the bones of whatever had stood before.

And then, sliding across the slice of sky, a figure in a long grey coat.

He knew the silhouette before he knew anything else about it. Broad shoulders. A saber at the hip worn low, cavalry-fashion, though the man had never ridden anywhere in his life except behind other men's horses. Sergeant Duvall. Tomas had seen him twice from a distance in the old days — back when Tomas had been a courier for the magistrate's office instead of a courier for the magistrate's ghost — and once up close, the night they arrested Vane's secretary, when Duvall had held the man's arms and chatted amiably about the weather while the woman wept.

*He's not assigned here,* Tomas thought, and the thought arrived already dressed in panic. *He works the customs bridge.* He made himself breathe. Men transferred. Regimes shuffled their dogs the way any kennel-master did. It meant nothing.

It meant everything. Because Duvall had seen him up close. Not across a room — across a table, in the anteroom of the magistrate's offices, when Tomas had waited an hour for a signature and Duvall had waited beside him and complained about his boots.

The knothole showed him sky again. Boots passed. The sergeant's voice drifted through the staves, bored and carrying: "Next. Papers."

A merchant ahead of them, arguing about a tariff. The wet cough. Somewhere a gull screamed over the market and was answered by fifty others, a shrieking congregation wheeling above the stalls where fishmongers were laying out the morning catch on beds of ice, and the smell of it all was so thick and salt-sweet that Tomas's stomach turned over and he clamped his jaw and thought, absurdly, of Cyrus sorting type by feel in the dark, her fingers walking the cases like a blind woman reading.

They had rehearsed this. In Maren's back room, over a map drawn in charcoal on the top of a herring crate, Cyrus had walked him through it twice: the gate opened at dawn, the queue backed up past the shrine by mid-morning, guards worked in pairs, one checked papers, one checked cargo, and the cargo check was a formality for fish — nobody hid in fish, the smell alone announced you, and besides, who would choose it? That was the whole architecture of the plan. Not cleverness. Disgust. They were betting their lives on a guard's reluctance to stick his hand into three days of herring.

"You're betting your life on a lazy man," Maren had said, watching them from the doorway with her arms crossed and something unreadable in her face. "Best odds there are. Lazy men are predictable. Clever ones improvise."

The cart stopped.

Not the slow rolling stop of traffic. A full stop, the wheels locked, the horse blowing steam. And then boots coming around the side of the cart, unhurried, and a voice Tomas didn't know saying, "Whose load?"

"Mine." Maren's driver — a broad silent man called Piet who had not spoken six words in Tomas's hearing — answered in a voice like gravel settling.

"Manifest?"

Papers rustled. Tomas pressed his eye to the knothole. The slice of world gave him a grey coat sleeve, a gloved hand taking the manifest, a second figure beyond it — younger, in the black-and-red of the harbor watch, holding a lantern even in daylight because the regime liked its lanterns; visibility as theater. Behind the young one, further off, the queue of carts stretched back into the fog, and among the waiting drivers Tomas could see women with market baskets, children running between wheels, ordinary morning life queuing up to pass through iron.

"Barrels," the first guard said. "Open one."

Tomas's heart did not stop. He wished it would have the decency to stop. Instead it sped up, hammering against the oilskin packet, against the letters, against two years of a dead man's handwriting, loud enough that he was certain — *certain* — the guard would hear it through the wood.

"Which one?" Piet asked.

The pause that followed lasted a year. Then: "Top of the stack."

The top of the stack was not Tomas's barrel. The top of the stack was herring, actual herring, packed in ice that morning by men who were being paid, per Maren's arrangement, to be incurious about what else went into the cart. Tomas heard the hook bite into the top barrel, heard it dragged and dropped to the cobbles with a sloshing thud, heard the lid pried. Brine ran. Gulls descended screaming like something out of an old story, a plague of small white demons, and the guard swore and stepped back and the fishmongers in the queue laughed — laughed, openly, at a regime guard besieged by gulls — and something in the air loosened by a single degree.

"Ice looks good," the guard said, wiping his glove on his coat. "Close it up."

But the young one in black-and-red hadn't moved. Through the knothole Tomas watched him turn his head, slowly, scanning the queue, the carts, the crowd — scanning with the particular patience of a man who has been told to find something and intends to find it, because finding things was how a young man got noticed in the harbor command's service. His gaze crawled along the line of carts. It reached theirs.

It stopped.

No — it stopped one cart short. It stopped at the cart behind theirs, where a driver sat slumped and sleepy, and the young guard stared at him for three full seconds, and Tomas understood with sickening clarity that the boy wasn't looking at faces. He was looking at loads. Weighing which cart would be least trouble to dig through. And their cart, piled high, smelling like a week-old grave —

"Move along," the older guard said. "Next!"

The whip cracked. The cart rolled. The knothole slid away from Tomas like a page turning, and he lay in the dark of the barrel with brine in his hair and his heart going like a press-run drum, and he let himself think, for exactly three seconds, *we're through, we're through, we're through* —

"Hold."

The word came from behind them. From the gate. From a voice Tomas knew, and the knowing landed in his stomach like swallowed ice.

Duvall.

The cart slowed. Stopped. Tomas heard boots approach at a walk — not hurrying, never hurrying, Duvall had never hurried in his life, hurry was for men who feared being wrong. "That driver," Duvall said. "The big one. Where's he signed on?"

"Maren Kessler's run," Piet said. "Out of the salt lane."

"Kessler." A pause Tomas could feel the shape of. "She runs wide these days. Lot of odd freight for a woman who used to deal only in what she could count." Boots circled. Came closer. "Lot of odd freight this season altogether. You hear about the printer? Shop off Tanner's Row? Inspector turned it inside out Tuesday night."

Tomas stopped breathing entirely.

"Man and daughter gone missing," Duvall went on, conversational, almost pleasant, the way he'd talked about weather over a secretary's sobbing. "Wanted bills went up yesterday. There's one right there on the gatepost, if anyone's inclined to read." A rustle of paper. "Young Reyes. Courier. Went missing the same night the magistrate's letters went missing, two years back, and wouldn't you know he surfaces now, same week the harbor seals. Coincidence is a luxury this quarter can't afford, eh, Piet?"

"No, sir," Piet said.

"So I'll have a look at your barrels after all. Starting from the bottom. I like to be thorough on my first morning at a new post." The boots stopped, and Tomas, eye pressed to the knothole, saw the grey coat bend, saw the gloved hand reach for the lowest barrel in the stack — his barrel, the barrel whose staves were already weeping brine, whose lid was nailed down with three nails that a hurried man had driven crooked —

A bell rang.

Not a church bell. The fish market's auction bell, high and sharp and insistent, and beneath it a rising roar — shouting, many voices, the crash of something heavy going over. Through the knothole Tomas caught a slice of chaos: the crowd at the market stalls surging backward, baskets scattering, a fishmonger bellowing about thieves, two boys sprinting through the gap they'd made with a string of stolen smoked eels streaming behind them like a banner.

And threading through the crowd, unremarkable, a woman in a fish-stained apron pulling a handcart — pausing just long enough, at just the right angle, for Tomas to catch the set of her jaw beneath her scarf.

*Cyrus.*

She'd been supposed to stay clear. Fifty yards back, behind the shrine, eyes down. Instead she was here, in the middle of it, doing — what? Making noise. Making *distraction*, crude and dangerous and exactly the sort of improvisation Maren warned clever people were prone to. The gulls wheeled. The boys escaped. The guards at the gate turned as one toward the commotion, and Duvall straightened, his hand leaving the barrel, his head turning with the professional reluctance of a man torn between duty and the knowledge that a sergeant's job was crowds, crowds were always the real threat.

"Theft at the stalls!" someone shouted. "Stop them!"

For three seconds — Tomas counted them, he counted every one — the gate belonged to no one. The queue surged. Carts advanced without orders. Drivers shouted. And in that gap, in that seam of pure human noise, Piet's whip cracked and their cart rolled forward through the gate arch, under the fresh-painted eagle, past the wanted bill nailed to the post — Tomas couldn't see it but he felt it pass overhead like a curse — and out onto the far road, where the fog of the harbor mouth opened gray and wide and cold.

Behind them, the auction bell still rang. Behind them, Duvall's voice rose over the din, calling for order, for names, for the theft-boys' descriptions — and then, cutting through everything, flat and carrying and aimed like a thrown knife:

"*Hold that cart!*"

Piet didn't hold the cart. Piet whipped the horse into a trot, then a canter, illegal within the market roads, drawing a shout behind them, drawing pursuit that was mostly confusion — because who flees a routine inspection over herring? Only everyone, this season; the guards hesitated, cursed, and chose the nearer chaos of the crowd. One whistle blew behind them, shrill, and another answered from somewhere ahead, and Piet muttered something in the dialect of the salt lanes and turned the cart hard off the main road, down a lane between net-drying racks, into the tangle of streets where the regime's maps went thin.

They rolled for ten minutes. Fifteen. The shouts faded. The whistles faded. At last, in the shadow of a broken crane at the edge of the smugglers' quarter, Piet stopped the cart and said his seventh word in Tomas's hearing:

"Out."

Tomas climbed from the barrel and stood on solid ground and discovered his legs barely qualified. Brine streamed off his coat. His hands, when he looked at them, were shaking — fine, rapid, involuntary, the tremor of a man who has been seconds from the bottom of a barrel and a sergeant's knife. He gripped the cart's rail until the shaking slowed.

Cyrus reached him first. She came out of the alley fog at a run, apron still stained, scarf loose, and for one unguarded moment her composure cracked clean through — she grabbed his arm, hard, checking him the way you'd check a type case after a fall, inventorying, counting, making sure nothing was broken. Her eyes went to his chest, to the oilskin packet's outline beneath the ruined coat.

"It's here," he said. His voice came out scraped. "It's here. Thanks to you. Which you weren't supposed to —"

"Somebody had to ring the bell." She said it flatly, but her hand was still on his arm, gripping, and he saw now that she was shaking too, finer than his shaking, deeper, the tremor of a woman who had walked into a checkpoint crowd with her father's name on a wanted bill and rung a bell and trusted arithmetic she couldn't verify. "The boys were Maren's idea. The bell was mine. If Duvall had started at the bottom—"

"He was starting at the bottom."

She went still. "You saw him?"

"Sergeant Duvall. Transferred to the gate this week." Tomas made himself say the rest, because she needed all of it, because softening it would be a kind of lie and they had both sworn off lies that weren't printed. "He knows my face, Cyrus. Not from a bill. From before. From the magistrate's own anteroom. Bills can be survived — bad likenesses, tired guards. But he stood next to me for an hour once and complained about his boots."

Cyrus looked back the way they'd come, toward the fog-bound direction of the gate, and Tomas watched her do the arithmetic he was dreading: if Duvall had held their cart thirty seconds longer, if the boys had been slower, if the bell had rung one minute later, then a barrel would have been pried open on the cobblestones and everything — the letters, the warrant, her father's sacrifice, all of it — would have ended in a puddle of brine.

"He'll report it," she said quietly. "A cart from Kessler's run, searched nearly, then gone. He'll write it down tonight, and Ashe will read it tomorrow, and the quay will know our shape before we ever reach it."

"Then we move faster than ink." Tomas pulled the ruined coat tighter around the packet. "One checkpoint down. Two left."

"And a harbormaster, and a ship, and a colonel who signed the warrant with his own hand." Cyrus turned back to him, and beneath the fear in her face there was something harder taking shape, something that looked, Tomas thought, like type being set — letter by letter, decision by decision, into a line that could not easily be undone. "Maren needs to hear what happened at that gate. All of it. Including Duvall."

She started toward the alleys. Tomas followed, wringing brine from his sleeves, and neither of them saw the boy on the crane's broken arm — a runner, quick and quiet, paid in copper — slip away through the fog toward the customs house, where a sergeant in a grey coat was already writing, in a careful bureaucratic hand, the first sentence of a report that would reach Colonel Ashe's desk by nightfall.
