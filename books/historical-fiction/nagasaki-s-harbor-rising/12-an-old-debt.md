## 12. An Old Debt

The smugglers' quarter did not appear on any map of Nagasaki's harbor district that the regime had ever sanctioned, which was precisely why it survived. It lived in the crook of land between the tanneries and the old salt warehouses, a place where the streets had never been given names because naming them would have meant acknowledging them, and acknowledging them would have meant taxing them, and taxing them would have meant admitting the harbor's second economy existed at all. Cyrus had been there exactly twice in her life—once as a child, carried on her father's shoulders to deliver a repaired press to a man who paid in coin wrapped in fish skin, and once four years ago, to beg Maren to come home.

She had not begged well, that day. She remembered the shape of her own pride now with something like nausea: standing in Maren's doorway with her chin up, saying *he won't ask you himself, but he misses you*, as though missing were a debt a person could be made to pay. Maren had looked at her for a long moment with those gray eyes that seemed always to be weighing cargo, and then she had said, not unkindly, "Tell him the door swings both ways," and closed it.

Cyrus walked into the quarter now the way she imagined a penitent walked into a church—knowing the architecture of her own guilt better than the layout of the streets.

* * *

The cold here had a different texture than the cold of the print shop. In the shop, cold was an absence; it pooled in corners and sat on the type cases and made the metal bite her fingers when she forgot her gloves. Here the cold was alive. It came off the harbor in long gray breaths, threading through the alleys between the salt sheds, carrying the smells of brine and tar and smoke from a hundred illegal stoves. Nets hung frozen on racks like the skeletons of enormous fish. A dog watched her from atop a barrel of what was probably, officially, nothing at all.

She had dressed for invisibility: her father's oldest coat, a shawl the color of dirty snow, her hair bound under a cap that made her look like every other woman trudging to market. But invisibility worked differently in the smugglers' quarter than it did on the respectable streets. There, one disappeared by being unremarkable. Here, one disappeared by being known—or rather, by being vouched for. A stranger who moved like she belonged attracted more attention than a stranger who moved like she didn't. So Cyrus let herself hesitate at the corner of the tannery wall, let herself check the sky for weather she could already feel, let herself be exactly what she was: a printer's daughter out of her depth, looking for someone.

"Looking for someone." The voice came from behind her, low and amused, and Cyrus turned too fast, her boot skidding on black ice.

A boy of perhaps fourteen leaned against a stack of crates, cracking sunflower seeds between his teeth. He wore three coats and none of them fit.

"Maren Okafor," Cyrus said.

The boy spat a shell into the slush. "Nobody's called her that since the winter she left the shop."

"I'm calling her that."

"Are you." He studied her, and Cyrus understood suddenly that this was not idle insolence but a kind of customs house—the quarter's own checkpoint, its first gate, manned by a child with sunflower seeds. People were weighed here before they were allowed further in. "You've got ink under your thumbnail," he said. "Left hand. And your coat's cut for a man twice your shoulders but mended by someone careful. You're shop-born."

"My name is Cyrus Whitlock."

Something shifted in the boy's face—not recognition exactly, but the recognition of a story. Everyone in the quarter knew the story of the old master printer and the apprentice who'd left. It had probably grown details over four winters, the way stories did, until it bore no more resemblance to the truth than a wanted bill bore to a face.

"He'll want to know why," the boy said.

"She'll," Cyrus said. "And I'll tell her myself."

The boy considered this, cracked another seed, and jerked his chin toward a lane that sloped down toward the water. "The Salt House. Blue door. Don't knock loud; the woman upstairs sleeps days." He paused, then added, with the gravity of a child repeating wisdom learned from adults: "Don't tell her I sent you. She hates owing anybody anything."

"So do I," Cyrus said, and walked on.

* * *

The Salt House was a warehouse that had died and been resurrected a dozen times. Its blue door was blue only in patches; the rest had gone gray or been replaced with wood that hadn't gotten the message. Inside, the smell was kelp and lamp oil and, beneath everything, faintly and impossibly, the ghost of printing ink—as though the building itself remembered what Maren had been before she became what she was.

Maren was working at a long scarred table near the window, and for a moment Cyrus simply stood in the doorway and watched her, because there was no helping it.

Four years had remade her. The girl who had left the print shop had been all sharp angles and quicker words, an apprentice whose hands were too impatient for the slow disciplines of type—who could cut a beautiful letterform and then ruin a whole page by rushing the lockup. The woman at the table had kept the angles but lost the hurry. Her dark hair was braided tight and practical, threaded with gray Cyrus refused to call premature. Her hands—those hands Cyrus knew almost as well as her own, hands that had guided hers over a composing stick when she was nine years old—were sorting coins into stacks with the same unhurried precision she'd once lacked entirely. Life had taught her the discipline the shop couldn't.

She looked up. Their eyes met across twenty feet and four years and one closed door.

"Cyrus Whitlock," Maren said. Not a greeting. An identification, like a clerk logging goods.

"Maren Okafor."

A muscle twitched at the corner of Maren's mouth. "So you can say it after all."

"You said the door swings both ways."

"And you stood outside it for two years without testing the hinges." Maren set down the coins and rose, wiping her hands on her apron—a printer's habit worn smooth into a smuggler's. "Sit. There's tea, if you don't mind drinking whatever the harbor gives me instead of asking questions about it."

Cyrus sat. The room around her resolved as her eyes adjusted: crates stenciled with shipping marks from five different ports, none of them matching the manifest chalked on the slate by the door; a rack of oilskins; a shelf holding, among the lamps and rope, a single brass composing stick that Cyrus would have recognized anywhere, because she'd helped her father engrave his initials on it the year Maren arrived at the shop, fifteen years old and furious at the world.

Maren followed her gaze. Said nothing. Poured tea from a dented pot into two tin cups and pushed one across.

"The boy outside will want paying for pointing you," she said. "He'll come collect. That's how it works here—every kindness is an invoice."

"I know. I grew up above a shop. Every favor is a ledger entry."

"Then you'll understand what I'm going to ask next." Maren drank. Set down the cup. "Why are you here?"

Not *what do you want*. Why are you here. As though the answer might be different from the request, and Maren intended to have both.

Cyrus wrapped her hands around the cup. The tin was hot enough to hurt, and she held on anyway, because the pain gave her somewhere to put the shaking. "There's a man in my father's shop," she said. "Half-dead when we found him. He's carrying letters."

"Letters."

"In Aldous Vane's hand."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Somewhere in the building, water dripped. Out on the harbor, faintly, the ice groaned—that deep, tectonic complaint the river made in the weeks before the break, the sound the whole city had learned to read the way farmers read clouds.

"That's a dangerous sentence," Maren said finally, quietly. "Even in this room. Even with me."

"It gets more dangerous." Cyrus set down the cup so she wouldn't crush it. "The execution warrant. Two years ago. The one that made Vane a traitor and a dead man. My father printed it. I set the type."

Maren went very still. It was the stillness of a person doing arithmetic they don't want to finish.

"I cut some of those letterforms myself," Cyrus went on, and heard her own voice go flat and strange, the way it did when she described things too large to feel in real time. "I was sixteen. Father said it was a routine commission, a magistrate's paperwork, and I believed him because believing him was easier. Two years later a stranger walks in out of the ice with letters proving Vane is alive, proving the warrant was forged, proving—" she stopped. Started again. "Proving that my hands built the lie everyone in this city has been living inside. And now the people who forged it are hunting the letters, and they've started searching the quarter, and we need to get past three checkpoints and onto a ship before the ice breaks, and I have run out of people to trust."

She ran out of words. The drip of water counted the silence for her.

"You trust me?" Maren asked. There was no softness in it. It was a real question, asked by a woman who had spent four years learning exactly how much trust cost.

"I trust that you left the shop rather than compromise it," Cyrus said. "Whatever else happened—I know that much. Father never told me why you went. He only ever said you wouldn't bend. I was angry about that for years. I'm not angry anymore."

Maren looked at her for a long time. Outside, the ice groaned again, longer this time, a sound like the harbor clearing its throat.

"You want passage," Maren said. "Papers. Men at the checkpoints. Maybe eyes on the quay."

"I want all of it. And I have almost nothing to pay with."

"No," Maren agreed. "You have almost nothing. Which means we're negotiating in the only currency that matters." She leaned back, and Cyrus saw the merchant surface in her fully now—the weighing, the tallying, the cold bright arithmetic underneath. "Here is what I can give. Passage on a ship that sails the morning the ice breaks, whether or not the harbor office knows it's sailing. Papers good enough to pass two of your three checkpoints—the fish gate and the bridge; the quay belongs to Ashe and no paper passes Ashe that Ashe hasn't priced himself. And six pairs of legs that can move anything through these lanes faster than any inspector can follow."

Cyrus's heart lifted—and Maren watched it lift, and waited, and Cyrus understood, with a sinking that felt almost physical, that the waiting itself was part of the price.

"And in return?"

"A favor."

"What favor?"

"I haven't decided yet."

The words landed wrong, and Cyrus flinched visibly, and Maren saw that too.

"That's not a bargain," Cyrus said. "That's a blank check."

"Yes," Maren said. "It is."

"Why would I sign it? Why would anyone?"

Maren rose and crossed to the shelf. She took down the brass composing stick, turned it over in her fingers, and set it on the table between them like a piece of evidence.

"Do you know why I left?" she asked.

"You wouldn't bend."

"Your father told you that?" Something moved behind her eyes—amusement, or grief wearing amusement's clothes. "He would. He's a good man, which means he lies best about himself." She sat back down slowly. "I left because he asked me to forge a date on a customs chit. A little thing. A shipment of his paper stock arriving late; a fine he couldn't afford. He asked me—the apprentice who worshipped him—to alter a document, and I did it, because I worshipped him. And afterward I sat in that shop for three months knowing that every beautiful thing he taught me could be bent to a lie whenever survival required it, and I couldn't bear it anymore. So I left, and I came here, where lying is honest work—everyone knows the game, everyone pays the toll, nobody pretends." She tapped the composing stick once, softly. "I took this because I hated him. I kept it because I couldn't stop being what he made me."

Cyrus stared at her. The story rearranged everything—her father's silences, the years of careful neutrality, the quiet refusal of the counter-edict commission that had nearly gotten them killed. He hadn't been innocent and unbending. He'd been guilty and afraid, for fifteen years, and had raised his daughter inside that fear like a plant grown in a cellar.

"So when I ask for a favor unnamed," Maren said, "understand what I'm asking. Your father taught me that the worst debts are the ones you can see coming. If you name the favor now, I'll weigh it, I'll price it, maybe I'll refuse it. But if I help you blind—if I spend my men and my papers and my standing in this quarter on your truth—then when I come to collect, you will pay. Because you'll owe me the way I owed him. Do you understand?"

The stove ticked. The ice groaned out on the water, and this time the groan ended in a crack—a short, sharp report, like a distant gunshot—and both women turned their heads toward the sound and then back to each other.

"How long do I have to decide?" Cyrus asked.

"You decided the moment you walked through the blue door," Maren said. "You just hadn't told yourself yet." She pushed the tin cup back toward Cyrus. "Drink your tea. Then tell me about these checkpoints, and this man of yours, and exactly how much trouble is coming down that hill toward my quarter. Because if I'm buying into this, I buy the whole ledger—not the parts you'd rather I didn't read."

So Cyrus told her. All of it: Tomas and the oilskin packet, the font case of broken type, the ransacked shop, the harbormaster's impossible price, the route mapped in candlelight, the clerk at the bridge who had waved them through with frightened eyes. She told it plainly, hiding nothing, and watched Maren listen the way she had once watched her father read a proof—finding errors, marking margins, saying nothing until the end.

When Cyrus finished, Maren was quiet for a moment. Then she began stacking the coins again, fast, her hands moving while her mind worked elsewhere.

"There's a thing you should know before we go further," she said, not looking up. "The boy outside—the one who pointed you here—he runs messages for half the quarter. This morning he carried one to the customs bridge. He didn't read it; reading isn't his trade. But he memorized the seal on it, because seals are worth money to boys who remember things." She looked up at last, and her gray eyes were flat and cold as the harbor. "It was the harbor commandant's seal. Colonel Ashe. Addressed to the bridge clerk—the one who waved you through."

The bottom dropped out of Cyrus's stomach.

"Ashe knows about the sympathetic clerk," Maren said. "Which means Ashe knows someone crossed the bridge yesterday who shouldn't have. Which means, Cyrus Whitlock, that your third checkpoint isn't waiting for you anymore." She snapped the last coin down on the stack. "It's hunting you."
