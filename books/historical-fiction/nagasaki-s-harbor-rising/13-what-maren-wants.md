## 13. What Maren Wants

Maren Kessler had learned to count in smuggler's arithmetic, which was not the arithmetic of honest folk. Honest folk counted what they had. Smugglers counted what they could move, and by how much light remained before the harbor watch changed shifts, and how many men at the fish market gate owed them favors against how many owed them grudges. It was a colder ledger than the one she'd kept as an apprentice, back when her sums had been quires of paper and pounds of ink and the small satisfaction of a well-set line.

She kept that old ledger anyway. She kept it in a oilskin packet beneath the floorboard of her room above the Anchor's Rest, and once a month—no more, because sentiment was a luxury with a price tag like everything else—she took it out and read the entries in her own younger hand. *Received of E. Whitlock: one case long primer, worn. Received instruction in the setting of display lines.* There had been an entry for warmth, too, though she hadn't written it that way. She'd written *supper, Thursdays*, and behind those two words lived three years of sitting across from Cyrus Whitlock while the old man read aloud from whatever he was printing, correcting their proofs between spoonfuls, and the shop smelling of lead and lamp oil and the particular cold sweetness of sizing solution, and Cyrus laughing with her mouth closed because she was self-conscious about a chipped tooth.

Maren had left all of it six years ago over a matter she still refused to call a betrayal. The Whitlocks would have called it one. The regime certainly had; her name was in a file somewhere, marked *apprentice, discharged under suspicion*. What she'd actually done was refuse to set a single line of an edict conscripting dock boys of fourteen, and Elias Whitlock—good, careful, frightened Elias—had chosen the edict over his apprentice, because he had a daughter to feed and a shop to keep alive and Maren had only herself. She understood it now better than she had then. Understanding had not made it hurt less. It had only taught her where to put the hurt so it didn't rattle when she walked.

Now the hurt came walking up her stairs wearing its old face.

* * *

Cyrus arrived at the Anchor's Rest after dark, hooded, alone, and asking at the bar for "the woman who handles cargo"—which was Maren's public trade and her thinnest cover. The barman, a one-eyed ex-soldier named Doss who owed Maren two favors and knew it, jerked his chin toward the stair without looking up from his cloth.

Maren heard the knock on her door and stood there a moment with her hand flat against the wood, feeling through it the particular quality of the silence on the other side. Cyrus had always knocked like that—apologetically, as if hoping no one would answer. Some people you knew by their footsteps. Cyrus Whitlock you knew by the way she tried to make none.

She opened the door.

The lamplight in the hall was bad, but not so bad that Maren couldn't see what six years and one hard winter had done. Cyrus had been thin as an apprentice; now she was thin the way people got when they forgot meals rather than skipped them, hollowed under the eyes, with a burn-gloss on two fingers of her right hand that Maren recognized instantly—a compositor's mark, from snatching type too fast out of hot metal or handling a form carelessly near the stove. Her hands were ink-stained past the wrist. That part hadn't changed. That part would never change; it was the family signature, the Whitlock stain, ground so deep into the skin's grain that soap surrendered decades ago.

"You shouldn't have come here," Maren said.

"I know." Cyrus's voice was rougher than Maren remembered, scraped down to something practical. "I need your boats."

Maren stepped back and let her in, because there was no version of this night in which she didn't, and because the sooner Cyrus said what she'd come to say, the sooner Maren could start counting the cost of it. That was the discipline. Name the transaction early. Feelings came later, if there was time, and there was almost never time.

* * *

The story took twenty minutes and Maren let her tell it without interruption, which was its own kind of mercy and Cyrus seemed to know it, pausing once at the middle with her jaw tight as though gathering herself around something too large to say quickly. The stranger. The letters. Aldous Vane's hand, alive on the page two years after the state had hanged him in effigy and printed ten thousand broadsides about justice served. And underneath it—the thing that made Cyrus's voice go flat and strange—the warrant. The forged warrant. Set in the Whitlock shop, in type Cyrus herself had cut, by Cyrus's own hands, at seventeen.

Maren listened and did not let her face move. This was another discipline, learned in six years of buying and selling things whose value depended on nobody knowing what you felt. But inside, where the old ledger lived, she felt the entries rearrange themselves. *Aldous Vane.* She remembered the magistrate vaguely from her apprenticeship—a lean man who had come twice to the shop to commission printing and had waited on his own carriage steps in the rain so his driver could finish his supper. A man who noticed apprentices. Who had asked her once, apropos of nothing, whether she found the work honest. She'd said yes. He'd looked at her a moment too long and said, *Keep finding it so.*

And now he was dead, or not dead, and the lie of his death sat in a font case in the Whitlock shop like a splinter working toward the heart of the whole frozen city.

"They're hunting him," Cyrus finished. "Hunting us. We have to get the letters out on the last ship, and we can't pass the checkpoints alone. I can forge papers. I've already started. But papers don't move barrels of herring, and papers don't buy silence at the quay. Boats do. You do."

"Say it plainly," Maren said. "What do you want?"

"The fish market gate, tomorrow night. Herring barrels, two of them with breathing room cut into the slats. Your people moving them, your word that no one at the gate looks too hard. And if the bridge goes wrong—" Cyrus stopped. Started again. "If anything goes wrong, a way off the ice. For me. For Tomas Reyes. For my father, if it comes to that."

Maren turned to the window. Below, the harbor lay black and locked, the ice stretching out to the breakwater in a vast groaning sheet that sang sometimes at night, a high thin note like a wire tightening. Two more weeks, maybe three, and the river would begin to lift. Then the seal ships would go, and the season with them, and every route inland would close like a fist.

"And your price?" Cyrus asked, when the silence had gone on too long. "You didn't come down the mountain to do charity, Maren. I'm not so foolish as to ask it free."

* * *

Here is what Maren had planned to say. She had rehearsed it on the walk over from the customs house, where she'd spent the afternoon bribing a clerk with brandy and learning which inspectors rotated onto the quay at dawn. She had rehearsed it because she believed in rehearsal the way other smugglers believed in saints. *The shop,* she would say. *When the ice breaks and this business of yours is finished—however it finishes—I take the lease. The press, the type, the goodwill, the name. Everything. That's my price.*

It was a fair price. It was, in the cold arithmetic, almost a generous one, given what she was being asked to risk: her boats, two of her best runners, and her standing with the harbormaster, who was a snake who kept accounts the way creditors keep souls. If the operation went wrong, the regime would not distinguish between the printer's daughter and the smuggler who moved her. Maren Kessler was pricing her own neck into the bargain, and the shop was worth it. More than worth it. The shop was the one piece of ground in Nagasaki's harbor district where Maren had ever been entirely herself—where her hands had known what they were for before her hands had learned to pick locks and palse documents and smile at men she intended to ruin.

She had rehearsed saying it plainly, and she had imagined Cyrus's face when she said it, and she had told herself that whatever crossed that face was not Maren's problem. Grief was not legal tender. Everyone in the harbor district paid in something.

But standing there with Cyrus watching her—Cyrus, who had come to her knowing exactly what kind of woman she'd become, and had come anyway, because there was no one else—that plainness curdled in Maren's throat. Not because she wanted to soften the demand. Because she suddenly saw how it would land, and understood that she wanted it to land clean, and that wanting that was vanity, and vanity was expensive.

So she said it plainly anyway.

"When the ice breaks," Maren said, "I take the shop."

Cyrus didn't move. Maren gave her the whole of it, unsoftened, because softening now would be worse than cruelty—it would be condescension. "The lease, the press, the stock, the name over the door. Elias signs it over the day the first ship clears the breakwater. I've wanted a press of my own for six years, and I'm done renting muscle to men who own presses. I'll be the one who owns the press. That's my price for the gate, the bridge, and the quay."

"You know what you're asking." Cyrus's voice was level. Her hands were not; they had curled into fists against her thighs, knuckles white, the burn-marks shining. "That shop is—"

"It's your inheritance. Yes. I know precisely what I'm asking." Maren let a breath out slow. "I also know what your father chose six years ago, when the regime put an edict on his stone and my name on his doorstep. He chose the shop. I don't blame him for it anymore. But understand me, Cyrus: I am choosing the same thing now. The shop. Over the risk, over the friendship, over whatever we were to each other. I'm telling you the truth of the transaction before you sign it, which is more courtesy than either of our trades usually gets."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you walk out that door and try the fish market gate with forged papers and a stranger's face, and we both pray your forger's hands are as good as your father's, and I attend whichever funeral comes first." Maren held her gaze. "I'm not threatening you. I'm describing Tuesday."

* * *

What Maren had not anticipated—and she cursed herself afterward, in the privacy of her own skull, for not anticipating it—was that Cyrus would not argue. That she would stand there in the bad lamplight doing the same cold arithmetic Maren did, and arrive at the same sum, and simply pay it.

"My father's safety," Cyrus said quietly. "The letters reach the capital. And the shop is yours. Agreed."

Just like that. No haggling. Maren had prepared for haggling the way she prepared for weather, and its absence left her oddly exposed, like a sailor whose storm fails to arrive.

"You should have fought me," Maren heard herself say. "Asked for terms. Staged it over two nights. Made me sweat."

"Would it have changed your price?"

"No."

"Then why waste the night?" Cyrus looked away, toward the window, toward the black singing ice. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to nearly nothing. "Do you know what I spent my whole life learning? How to carry little enough that no one notices you're carrying anything. Sort the type in the dark. Keep the shutters half-closed. Print what pays, burn what doesn't, survive." A short breath that might have been a laugh in a different life. "And now the one thing worth carrying is the one thing I can't carry alone, and the price of help is the future I was saving all that caution for. It seems—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. "It seems fitting. I don't know. It seems like something the harbor would do."

Maren watched her and felt the old ledger open itself unbidden, and hated it. *Supper, Thursdays.* The chipped tooth. The way Cyrus used to stand at the composing stone with her sleeves rolled, utterly absorbed, the rest of the world temporarily abolished—as if a galley of type were a country she ruled absolutely and justly. Maren had built six years of scar tissue over that girl and the girl's family, and now here was the family at her door, and the scar tissue turned out to be exactly as deep as it looked, which was to say not deep at all.

"There's a condition," Maren said, and heard her own voice roughen, and pressed on before it could betray more. "One condition, non-negotiable, and then I'll draw the papers tonight."

Cyrus turned back to her. Waiting. Braced for the second knife, Maren could see it—the assumption that a smuggler's generosity was a trap within a trap.

"You learn the press," Maren said. "Before the ice breaks. Whatever hours the forging leaves you, you spend them at my stone in the Anchor's cellar, and I teach you what your father never got to—display work, color passes, the two-color job I never mastered under him. Because when this is over, however it's over, I'll need a foreman who can run a shop, and I'm not hiring a stranger." She paused. "And because someone should carry what the Whitlocks know forward, even if the name over the door changes. That's the condition. Take it or leave it."

For a moment Cyrus said nothing at all, and Maren watched something move across her face that was not grief exactly and not gratitude exactly but some mineral compound of the two, precipitating slowly, like frost forming on glass.

"You're a terrible sentimentalist," Cyrus said finally, "for a smuggler."

"I'm the worst kind," Maren agreed. "The kind who charges for it."

They shook on it. Cyrus's grip was strong and ink-rough and brief, and Maren noted—with the part of her mind that never stopped taking inventory, the part that would count barrels and favors and debts until the day she died—that Cyrus's palm was cold as the harbor ice, and that she held the shake a half-second longer than the transaction required, and that Maren let her.

* * *

Maren drew the papers by candlelight while Cyrus sat across the table with her hood back, watching the pen move. Bill of sale, contingent on the breaking of the ice. Two copies. Maren wrote the clauses in her tight, upright hand—the hand that had once set wedding invitations and funeral notices for the whole quarter, back when her letters had been honest work—and initialed each page, and slid both copies across for signature.

Cyrus signed. Her hand barely shook.

"One more thing," Maren said, folding the copies away. "The man. Reyes. You trust him?"

"I trust what he carries."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know." Cyrus pulled her hood up, and the lamplight slid off her face and left only the shadow of her, the shape she'd been trained to make—unremarkable, forgettable, a figure in a doorway. But her voice, when it came, had an edge Maren hadn't heard from her before, something newly forged and still cooling. "He carried those letters through the ice water and half the city with soldiers on the streets. Whatever else he is, he didn't do it for money—he had chances to sell them and didn't take them. Tomorrow we find out what he did do it for." She moved toward the door, then stopped, hand on the latch. "Maren. The gate tomorrow night. If it goes wrong—if they take us—my father doesn't know about any of this. He knows nothing. See that it stays that way."

"He knows nothing," Maren repeated. "Even though the letters are hidden in his shop."

"*Especially* because the letters are hidden in his shop." Cyrus's knuckles whitened on the latch. "It's the only protection he has. Ignorance. I've watched what knowledge costs in this city my whole life. Let him keep his innocence a few days longer. It's the last thing I can give him."

She was gone down the stairs before Maren could answer, quick and silent, making no sound at all even on the boards that creaked for everyone.

Maren stood a long while at the window after, watching the street empty and refill with snowfall, listening to the ice sing its tightening wire-song out in the dark. In the oilskin packet under her floorboard there was an old ledger with an entry she'd never had the courage to write: *Owed to E. Whitlock: one apprenticeship, ended badly.* She thought about going down and adding to it. *Received of C. Whitlock: one future, signed over.* She thought about how the entry would balance, and whether anything in that arithmetic balanced at all, or whether the whole ledger was just a way of pretending that what people took from each other could be totaled.

Below, the harbor lay locked and waiting. Three days, maybe four, and the ice would begin to talk in earnest—groaning, splitting, letting go. Maren had staked her future on that breaking, and on the printers' daughter keeping her nerve, and on a dead magistrate's letters surviving three checkpoints and a colonel's greed.

Then boots sounded on the stair—not Cyrus's careful silence but heavy, doubled, official—and Maren's hand went to the candle and her mind went to the window and the drop to the yard beyond it.

Doss's knock landed on her door, three sharp blows, and his voice through the wood was low and fast: *"Maren. Watch patrol's swept the last two houses on the lane. They're asking after a woman and a stranger. They're coming up."*
