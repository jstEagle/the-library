## 14. The Customs Bridge

The fog came off the harbor before dawn, thick as wool, and Tomas Reyes walked into it carrying a dead man's letters against his chest like a second heartbeat.

Cyrus walked beside him, hood up, hands buried in the pockets of a coat that had belonged to her father and was too broad in the shoulders for her. She had not spoken since they left the smugglers' quarter. He had learned to read her silences the way he'd once learned to read weather off the water—there was the silence of calculation, the silence of fear, and now this one, which was neither. It was the silence of a woman rehearsing something over and over until the words wore smooth as river stones.

*The chit is good,* she'd said last night, holding it to the lamp one final time. *The seal will pass at arm's length. It will not pass in a man's hands.*

That was the whole of their hope, then: that no one at the customs bridge would want to hold their lie too closely.

The bridge itself announced its presence before they could see it—a lantern swinging somewhere ahead, a chain rattling in the wind off the ice, the wet slap of the river against pilings. Then the fog thinned for a moment, the way it sometimes did, grudgingly, like a curtain drawn back by an indifferent hand, and there it was: a stone span no wider than a cart track, arched over a channel of black water where the ice had been broken away to keep the current moving. A customs house squatted at the far end, low-roofed, its windows glowing amber. Two soldiers stood beneath the lantern with muskets slung and collars turned up against the cold. A third sat inside, visible through the glass, bent over a desk.

Tomas felt Cyrus go still beside him—not stopping, nothing so obvious, just a fractional tightening of her stride, the way a deer changes gait when it scents smoke.

"Remember," she murmured, barely louder than breath. "We are merchants' clerks carrying invoices to the fish factors on the quay road. We are bored. We are cold. We want our breakfast."

"And if they ask what's under my coat?"

"Nothing is under your coat. You are a thin man with poor circulation." Her mouth twitched, almost a smile, gone again. "Walk like your feet hurt. That part should be easy. Your feet have been hurting since the fish market gate."

They crossed onto the bridge. The stones were glazed with frost, and Tomas kept his eyes down as instructed, watching his boots, watching the black water slide beneath the gaps between them. The channel smelled of salt and rotting kelp and the iron tang of the ice-breaker's wake. Somewhere upstream, the river ice groaned—a long, deep complaint, like a giant turning in its sleep—and he felt the sound pass up through the soles of his feet and settle somewhere behind his ribs.

Every day now, the groaning came earlier. Every day, the ice grew more restless. The season was ending whether they were ready or not.

"Halt," said the nearer soldier.

He was young—younger than Tomas, certainly, with the raw pink face of a boy doing a man's work and the resentful set of shoulders that said he'd rather be anywhere warm. His companion was older, gray-stubbled, with the flat incurious stare of a man who had stopped seeing travelers as people years ago. The boy did the talking; the old one did the watching. Tomas understood the arrangement immediately and hated it, because it was the watcher who would notice things.

"Papers," the boy said.

Cyrus produced the chit from her sleeve with the unhurried competence of someone who did this every week. Tomas watched her face while she did it—the perfect blankness of it, the small yawn she permitted herself, the way she blew on her fingers as though the cold were the only inconvenience in the world. It was a performance, and it was flawless, and he marveled that he had ever taken her for a woman merely hiding. She was not hiding. She was *inhabiting* invisibility the way an actress inhabits a role, and she had been doing it her whole life.

The boy held the chit at arm's length, squinting. The lantern light caught the wax seal, the careful copperplate of the customs house lettering. Cyrus's work. Three nights of it, cutting and filing and pressing, teaching him between strokes how a forger thinks—which lines matter, which flaws the eye forgives, why a perfect forgery is more suspicious than a slightly imperfect one.

"Customs chit for the quay road," the boy recited, as though reading from a list. "Purpose of travel?"

"Invoices for the fish factors," Cyrus said. "Herring weights and barrel counts. My colleague carries the ledgers." A tilt of her head toward Tomas, apologetic, faintly bored. "Heavy as sin. You're welcome to carry them the rest of the way if you like."

The boy almost smiled. The old soldier did not.

"Names," the old one said.

And there it was—the question they had prepared for and dreaded, because names written in a ledger outlive the moment and can be compared, cross-referenced, summoned. Cyrus gave hers without a flicker: "Mistress Corla Henneman, clerk to Master Duvall, fish factor." The name of a woman who existed, a real clerk in a real office two streets from the shop, chosen weeks ago for the crime of being forgettable.

The old soldier's eyes moved to Tomas.

"Tomas Verhoeven," he said. The false surname sat strangely in his mouth—he had worn his own name like armor for two years, and shedding even part of it felt like unbuckling a plate. "Also Duvall's."

The old soldier grunted. He took the chit from the boy and stepped closer to the lantern, and Tomas felt the morning contract around him, the fog drawing tight as a fist. This was the moment Cyrus had warned about. *Arm's length, yes. In a man's hands, no.* The old soldier held the chit close to the flame, tilting it, and the wax seal gleamed, and gleamed, and—

"The seal's a shade dark," the old soldier said.

"Pressed in cold weather," Cyrus said. "Wax behaves badly below freezing. Everyone's seals run dark this month. Ask any factor on the quay."

It was true, which was the beauty of it. Cyrus had built the forgery around three truths so that only one thing about it was false.

The old soldier looked at her. Looked at Tomas. Looked at the chit again, turning it slowly, and Tomas let his gaze drift past the man's shoulder to the customs house window, where the clerk inside had lifted his head from his desk. He was a narrow man, forty perhaps, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles fogged at the edges, and he was looking at them with an expression Tomas could not immediately name. Not suspicion. Something quieter. Something like recognition—not of faces, but of a situation.

Then the clerk rose, opened the door, and the warmth of the stove inside spilled out into the cold along with the smell of lamp oil and paper.

"Ledger entries won't make themselves," the clerk said mildly. "Bring the chit in and I'll stamp it properly. You two can wait by the stove while I do it—it's bitter standing out here, and Corporal Voss has been out here since the middle watch and is in no mood for conversation."

It was not an order, exactly. It was delivered with the weary authority of a man stating how things were done, and the old soldier—Voss—grunted and handed the chit over without protest, because apparently this was also how things were done.

Inside, the customs house was small and stiflingly warm. A pot-bellied stove radiated heat from the corner; a map of the harbor district hung on one wall, annotated in a clerk's precise hand; shelves of ledgers lined the other, each spine labeled in the same hand. The clerk closed the door behind them with the unhurried air of a man shutting out the weather, and then, without looking at either of them, he slid the bolt.

Tomas's heart stopped. Beside him, he heard Cyrus inhale—a tiny sound, quickly mastered—but her hand had already drifted into her coat pocket, where she carried the little knife she used for trimming type.

The clerk hung the chit on a hook by his desk, poured three cups of something dark and steaming from a pot on the stove, and handed them around. Only then did he look up, and push his spectacles up his nose, and speak in a voice pitched precisely for three pairs of ears and no others.

"I stamped a chit for a woman matching your description four days ago," he said. "Heading the same direction. Also traveling with a companion who didn't look like a fish clerk." He sipped his drink. "I also stamped a requisition order last autumn that moved six crates of 'archival paper' from the magistrate's residence to the garrison incinerator. Paper that burned very quickly, for archival stock. Almost as though someone wanted it ash before anyone counted it."

The silence stretched. Outside, Voss's boots crunched on frost, pacing. The stove ticked and settled.

"You knew Aldous Vane," Cyrus said quietly. Not a question.

The clerk's jaw tightened—the first crack in his mildness, quickly smoothed. "Everyone in this building knew the magistrate. He audited this office twice a year. He found three errors in my ledgers over nine years and made me correct every one, and he never once reported me for them, though he could have ruined me." He adjusted his spectacles. "A man who finds your errors and keeps them in his pocket instead of spending them—that is a man worth knowing. When they told us he'd been executed for treason, I signed the register of public witnesses like everyone else. And when I read the warrant published afterward—" he paused, choosing words the way a man chooses footing on rotten ice— "the signature was right but the hand was wrong. A signature can be copied. The pressure of a hand cannot. I've spent thirty years reading hands across a desk. That warrant was written by a man imitating another man, and tired, and hurried."

Tomas's throat was dry. "You never said anything."

"To whom?" The clerk's laugh was short and entirely without humor. "To Colonel Ashe? To the tribunal?" He set down his cup. "I have a wife and a daughter, and this office is the only wall between them and the street. So I did what quiet men do. I remembered. And I waited to see if anyone braver than me would remember out loud."

He turned to his desk, produced a stamp and an ink pad, and pressed the customs mark onto the chit with a firm, deliberate motion—the official thunk of it absurdly loud in the little room. Then he wrote their false names into the ledger in his beautiful, careful hand, and Tomas understood that he was doing them a second kindness: making them official. Giving their lies a home in the record.

"Now," the clerk said, sliding the chit back across the desk, and his voice changed—lower, faster, the mildness stripped away like paint off rusted iron. "Listen to me, because I will only say this once and then you are strangers to me. The quay is watched. Not the ordinary watching—Voss and his kind, who can be bored and bribed and fooled. Colonel Ashe has doubled the watch since the fish market incident. There are men on the ice-breaker itself, in the wheelhouse, pretending to crew her. There is a man who sits in the net-mender's hut with a clear view of the gangplank, and he does not mend nets. And—" he hesitated, and for a moment the clerk's composure wavered, showing the frightened citizen underneath— "there is a list. Names of persons of interest, posted to the checkpoint captains yesterday. I saw it on Voss's clipboard. I could not read it upside down, but it was long, and the first page bore the seal of the garrison command."

Cyrus went very still. "Our names?"

"I don't know. But the fish market gate reported a woman and a man two days ago, and the report went up the chain, and reports that go up the chain come back down as lists." He glanced at the door. "You cannot go to the quay as yourselves. You cannot go to the quay at all, if you are wise. But you are clearly not wise, so I will tell you the one thing that might save you: Ashe trusts procedure above all things. He believes the ledger is the state. If your papers are perfect—if every stamp, every entry, every signature chains back to a real document in a real book—he will wave you through himself, because to stop you would be to doubt his own system. Build him a paper trail so clean he would have to accuse his own archives to reject it."

"He's the commandant," Tomas said. "Why would he check papers personally?"

"Because two days ago someone failed him, and Ashe does not forgive failure. He'll be at the quay at dawn tomorrow, when the ship takes on cargo. Standing at the gangplank. Reading every chit himself." The clerk picked up their cups and rinsed them in a basin, erasing the evidence of their visit. "Which means you have one day, and one chance, and it must be flawless."

He unbolted the door and the cold rushed back in, and just before they stepped through it he spoke once more, so softly that the words barely survived the wind off the channel:

"There are more of us than you think. Quiet ones. Ledger-keepers and stamp-lickers and men who sign registers with their eyes down. We have been waiting two years for someone to remember out loud." His eyes went to Tomas's coat, to the shape of the oilskin packet beneath it, and something passed over his face that was very close to reverence. "Don't waste it on escape. Escape is what we all assumed you'd try."

* * *

They walked away from the bridge without speaking, following the quay road as their papers demanded, because a watched road demands consistency. Fog swallowed the customs house behind them. The river ice groaned again, longer this time, a sound like the whole world clearing its throat.

It was Cyrus who finally broke the silence, and her voice was strange—thin and bright, like wire pulled taut.

"He knew. Thirty years reading hands, and he knew the warrant was forged, and he said nothing." She shook her head slowly. "I spent two years thinking I was the only one carrying that guilt. That the whole city believed the lie except me." A breath of fog escaped her. "There's an army of us, Tomas. An army of people who knew, and kept their heads down, and called it survival."

"An army that marches one day late," Tomas said, "is still an army."

She looked at him then, and in the gray half-light he saw something new in her face—not the wariness of the print shop, not the terror of the checkpoints, but the first pale dawn of something that might, in better weather, have been called hope.

"Ashe reads every chit himself at dawn tomorrow," she said. "One day. One chance. Flawless." Her hand went to her pocket, to the stub of pencil and scrap of paper she always carried, the tools of her trade. "Then we'd best get to work. Because I know exactly whose hand forged that warrant—and I'm going to forge it back."

Far down the quay, half-lost in fog, the net-mender's hut sat with its door open, and a man inside was watching them pass, and mending nothing at all.
