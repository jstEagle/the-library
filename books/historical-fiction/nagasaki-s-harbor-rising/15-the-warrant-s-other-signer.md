## 15. The Warrant's Other Signer

The lamp burned low in Maren's back room, and Cyrus had been bent over the warrant so long that the letters had begun to swim like fish beneath river ice.

She had copied it twice already—once in pencil, once in her own careful hand, the way her father had taught her to copy any document she meant to set in type. Measure first. Understand the bones of a thing before you dress it. A forgery was not a picture of a document; it was a document, rebuilt from its skeleton outward. And skeletons, her father liked to say, never lie even when the flesh does.

But this skeleton was lying to her, and she could not find where.

Tomas sat across from her, his bandaged hands flat on the table as though he feared they might be confiscated too. He had not spoken in an hour. He had learned, in their days together, that Cyrus working was Cyrus silent, and that silence in this room was not emptiness but pressure—the pressure of a woman holding a dead man's warrant up to lamplight and asking it questions.

"Say it again," she said finally, without looking up. "The day Vane was executed. Everything. From the beginning."

"I've told you four times."

"Tell me a fifth time. People leave things out when they think they've told the whole story. They keep the parts they're ashamed of."

Something moved through Tomas's face—not quite anger, something wearier and more honest than anger. "There was a crowd," he said. "Two thousand people, maybe more, packed into the magistrate's square behind iron barricades. The regime wanted witnesses. That's what I never understood until now—they *wanted* us to see it. A man in a grey coat with his head bowed, escorted up the scaffold steps by six soldiers. There were drums. You couldn't hear anything over the drums. The axe came down, they held up—" he swallowed— "they held up the head. Wrapped it in cloth after. And then the criers went through the streets for three days saying treason dies with the traitor, and the harbor believed it because we all saw it with our own eyes."

"And how near were you?"

"Close. Third row. Close enough that I'd swear on my mother's grave I saw his face." He paused. "It was his face. I'd have sworn it was."

Cyrus set down her pencil. "And yet he wrote these letters eight months ago, in a hand your brother verified against twenty years of correspondence."

"The hand is his. I told you—"

"I believe you. Which means one of two things." She turned the warrant toward him, angling it so the lamplight raked across the paper's surface, low and oblique the way she'd seen engravers examine plates. "Either Aldous Vane is alive and let another man die in his place—which is possible, but it isn't what these letters read like, because these letters are the work of a man who believes himself abandoned by everyone still living. Or—"

"Or the execution was real, and the warrant that authorized it was written afterward."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Worse than that. The warrant was written *before*. Look."

* * *

She laid out the copies side by side: the forged execution warrant of Aldous Vane, dated two years past, and beside it the oilskin packet's oldest letter, genuine, dated a year before that. Two documents, one dead man's hand.

"Here." Her finger hovered above the warrant without touching it. "The body text. The charges, the sentencing clause, the authorization line—all of it set in type. Standard practice; warrants are printed, not handwritten, so no clerk's pen can be questioned later. This is the same stock every tribunal uses. But a printed warrant needs signatures. Two of them. One is Vane's own deputy, Ferren—you can see where the ink has browned here, along this stroke, the way old iron-gall ink does. Genuine aging. Whoever made this used a real signature, probably lifted from a legitimate document Ferren signed before he died."

Tomas leaned in. His breath fogged faintly in the cold room; Maren's stove had gone to embers hours ago. "And the other?"

"The other is the problem." She pulled the original closer, tilted it again. "The second signature authorizes the sentence. It's the one that matters—the commanding officer's assent, the name that makes killing legal. And it reads *A. Corvane, Colonel, Harbor Command.*"

"So? Corvane signed it. Some officer I've never heard of."

"That's because there is no Colonel Corvane." She said it quietly, and the quietness made it worse. "I've lived in this harbor district my whole life. My father has printed harbor garrison rosters for fifteen years—every officer commissioned or transferred, because the garrison pays for the printing and my father never refuses paying work. I have set those rosters in type myself. There has never been a Corvane at Harbor Command. Not in fifteen years."

Tomas stared at her. "Then who—"

"Look at the signature properly. Not at the name—at the hand underneath the name. Names are costumes. Hands are bodies." She traced the air above the ink. "A. Corvane. Now watch what happens if you're writing quickly, carelessly, the way a soldier signs a thing he wants finished. You start with the *A*, and instead of lifting your pen you run straight down into a *C*—but a rushed *C* doesn't close. It opens. And an open *C* followed by *orvane*..." 

She wrote it herself on the scrap paper, fast, letting her hand do what hands do when they hurry. The letters tumbled out. Then she crossed through two strokes, added one.

**Ashe.**

The word sat on the page between them like something dredged up from the harbor bottom.

"Aldous Vane's execution warrant was signed by the man who now commands this harbor's gates," Cyrus said. "Colonel Ashe didn't just permit the forgery. His hand is *on* it. He signed the death of a man who—according to those letters—was preparing to expose him. And then he took the dead man's authority for himself, and he has held this harbor in his fist ever since."

* * *

For a long moment Tomas did nothing but breathe. Then he stood, abruptly, knocking his chair back, and went to the window, and stood there with his shoulders rigid, looking out at nothing—the fog, the dark shapes of moored hulls, the distant smudge of the customs bridge lamps.

"You understand what this means," he said to the glass.

"Tell me anyway. I want to hear it in someone else's voice."

"It means the third checkpoint isn't just an obstacle." He turned. "It means the man standing at the ice-breaker quay, reading every chit at dawn tomorrow, is the one man alive who knows exactly what the letters say—because half of them are about *him*. It means if we walk onto that quay with forged papers, we aren't hiding from a guard. We're hiding from the murderer holding the only list of everyone he's ever murdered. It means—"

"It means he'll recognize the game instantly," Cyrus finished. "Because he's played it himself. A forger always recognizes another forger's work. He looked at that warrant two years ago—maybe he even commissioned it—and he knows precisely how a thing like this gets made. Every trick we know, he knows, because he needed them done for him." She laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and sharp. "We're not carrying evidence past a checkpoint, Tomas. We're carrying it into the one place in this city where the evidence is a mirror."

Tomas came back to the table slowly. "Then we don't go to the quay."

"There is no route that avoids the quay. You said it yourself—the last ship leaves before the ice seals the harbor. Three checkpoints, and the third one is his front door."

"Then we burn them." His voice cracked on it. "Burn the letters, forget Vane, forget all of it, and live. Isn't that what you're good at? Living small? You told me—you told me the whole discipline of your life is being invisible. So be invisible. Let the dead bury their dead."

Cyrus looked at him for a long time. Outside, somewhere down the street, a patrol's boots rang off the cobbles in perfect unison, passed, faded. Neither of them moved until the sound was gone.

"Do you know what I did tonight, before I found the signature?" she asked. "I spent four hours trying to prove the warrant false so that we wouldn't need it. If the warrant were clumsy—if I could show it was a bad fake—I could tell myself the truth was cheap. That anyone could have found it. That we were brave for carrying something anyone would carry." She smoothed the scrap of paper flat with both palms. "Instead I found out the truth is expensive. It cost a man his head two years ago. It will cost us everything tomorrow. And knowing the price doesn't make me want to put it down. It makes me want to make sure it gets paid to the right people."

"That isn't courage, Cyrus. That's stubbornness wearing courage's coat."

"My father says the same thing about printers." She almost smiled. "He says stubbornness is the only honest part of the trade. Everything else—every page—is a lie arranged neatly enough to pass for truth. Except once in a generation, when the trade is needed to print the one true thing, and then all those years of lying neatly turn out to have been training."

She gathered the papers, squared their edges, and slid them into the hollow space beneath Maren's loose floorboard where they'd kept them since the fish market gate. But her hands slowed as she worked, and stopped.

"Tomas. There's something else. Something I didn't want to say in front of the light."

He went still. "What?"

"The signature. Ashe's hand—it wasn't unfamiliar to me. That's what took me four hours. I kept feeling I'd seen that particular rush, that way he crushes the final stroke of the *e*, somewhere before." She looked up. "I set that warrant. Two years ago. My father brought it home as a commission, urgent, well paid, and I set the type and pulled the proofs and never read past the charge clauses because you learn not to read what you print. But I handled the signatures. I positioned them on the page. I remember thinking the second one was written in haste—a man signing in a hurry, a man who wanted his name off the page as fast as his pen could manage." Her voice dropped. "I didn't forge Ashe's crime. But my hands dressed it. My type gave it a body to wear. And tomorrow morning that same man is going to hold our forged chit under a lamp, and he is going to look at the work of a printer trained in the same shop that dressed his warrant—and some part of him, some instinct older than thought, is going to recognize the family resemblance."

* * *

They worked until the lamp guttered and Maren banged on the door and told them, through the wood, that people who planned to be alive at dawn should sleep like people who expected to be.

Cyrus did not sleep. She lay on the narrow cot in the dark and listened to the harbor.

The ice was talking to itself out there. It did that in the deep cold—long groaning sighs that traveled the length of the bay, and then sudden cracks like rifle shots, and then a strange creaking, patient sound, like a door being opened very slowly by something with all the time in the world. The old fishermen said the ice spoke in the voice of everyone it had drowned. Cyrus had always thought that superstition. Tonight it sounded less like drowning and more like counting.

Ten thousand copies, she thought. If it came to that. If the quay closed against them, if Ashe stood at the gate with her father's life or her own in his fist—there was a press in this city, there was type, there were smugglers who owed Maren favors, and there were ten thousand pairs of eyes in this quarter that had watched a man die and been proud of their own good sight. Ink traveled further than ships. Ink didn't need a checkpoint.

She turned the thought over and was surprised to find it didn't frighten her. It steadied her, the way setting type steadied her: one letter at a time, in order, no letter asked to be more than itself.

Near dawn, footsteps on the stairs.

Not Maren's—Maren walked like a man settling a debt, heavy and deliberate. These steps were lighter, quicker, two at a time, and they stopped at the door and there was a soft knock, and then Maren's voice, pitched low and wrong:

"Cyrus. Get up. There's a boy downstairs with a message from the print shop." A pause, and when Maren spoke again the roughness had gone out of her voice entirely, leaving something careful and cold. "Your father's shop is surrounded by garrison men. They took him an hour ago. The boy says they left a note nailed to the door."

Cyrus was already reaching for her boots. "Read it to me."

"It's addressed to you. By name." Maren's voice dropped further, until it was barely breath through the door. "It says: *The letters for the printer. The quay, dawn tomorrow. Come alone, and come honestly, Miss Whitlock—or we will teach your father the difference between ink and blood.*"

Out on the water, the ice cracked once, long and loud, like a word being spoken.
