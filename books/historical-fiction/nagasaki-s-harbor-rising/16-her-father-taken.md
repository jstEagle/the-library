## 16. Her Father Taken

The knock came at the hour when the harbor fog thickened into something almost solid, when the lamps along the fish quay burned as smeared halos and even the gulls had given up their complaining. Cyrus heard it through the floorboards—three knocks, unhurried, the rhythm of a man who expected doors to open for him.

She was at the case, sorting type by feel in the dark the way she had done since she was nine years old, her fingers reading the notched shoulders of the letters like a blind woman reading faces. Her father was below, banking the stove for the night. She heard him cross the shop, heard the bolt slide back.

"Closed," he said. "Whatever you're selling, come back when the ice is gone."

"Master Whitlock." The voice was smooth as tallow, pleasant in a way that made the skin of her arms tighten. "I'm not selling anything. I'm collecting."

Cyrus rose from the case without making a sound. She had spent twenty-two years learning to move quietly through rooms where she did not wish to be noticed, and now every one of those hours paid itself out at once. She crossed to the window that overlooked the street, angling herself behind the shutter's gap, and looked down into the fog.

There were six of them. Not inspectors—the inspectors wore gray wool and carried clipboards and the bored expressions of clerks. These men wore black oilcloth over uniform blue, and they stood in the posture of soldiers who were off duty only because no one had told them yet. Two held lanterns. One held a coil of rope, coiled neatly, the way a butcher holds his steel between tasks.

Her father stood in the doorway with the lamp behind him, a thin old man in an apron dusted with ink, and he did not step aside.

"The shop is licensed," he said. "The license is current. Whatever warrant you carry, it will say *Whitlock & Daughter, Printers*, and nothing else."

"It says nothing at all," the smooth voice said. "That's rather the point. Colonel Ashe sends his regards. He remembers your work. He said—and I quote him exactly—that you have the most honest hands in Nagasaki."

A silence followed that Cyrus felt in her teeth. Below, her father's hand came up slowly and rubbed at his jaw, a gesture she had known all her life, the gesture that meant he was calculating odds he did not like.

"I don't know any Colonel Ashe."

"No," the man agreed pleasantly. "You don't. But you knew Aldous Vane."

Cyrus's breath stopped against the cold glass. Behind the shutter gap she watched her father's shoulders—not much, a settling, like a house taking on water—and she understood, in the way one understands lightning before the thunder arrives, what was about to happen.

"Two years ago," the voice continued, "a certain document passed through this shop. A warrant of execution. You set it yourself, Master Whitlock. Every letter of it. The regime has recently developed an interest in that document. Where the original went. Who kept copies. Whether the signature at the bottom is worth the paper it bleeds on." He paused. "You're going to help us answer those questions."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we'll ask more loudly."

Below, her father did something Cyrus had never seen him do: he laughed. It was a small sound, dry as a snapped type shoulder, but it carried up through the floorboards and into her chest.

"You've made a mistake," he said. "You think I'm a coward who can be leaned on. I was, once. I know better than anyone what leaning produces. I set that warrant. I set it knowing—" He stopped himself, and Cyrus closed her eyes, because she knew what came next would cost him. "Knowing it was false. And I have regretted it every day since with a thoroughness you cannot imagine. So no. I will not be leaned on twice. Take your rope and go home."

The silence that followed lasted four heartbeats. Cyrus counted them.

"Master Whitlock," the smooth voice said, almost gently now, "the colonel anticipated your courage. Courage is so common in this city lately. It's practically a local crop." She heard cloth shift, boots scrape on frost-slick stone. "He asked me to tell you that we won't need to touch you at all. We'll simply take you in for questioning—a printer, suspected of seditious sympathies; no one will blink. And while we're asking our questions, we'll be asking them near your daughter's lodging. Which we have already found. Above the chandlery on Rope Lane, isn't it? She keeps late hours. She walks home alone."

* * *

They took him without violence, which was somehow worse than violence would have been. Violence would have given her something to fight. Instead there was only the quiet theater of it: her father removing his apron and hanging it on its peg, folding his spectacles into his pocket, asking leave to bank the stove properly because a cold grate warped the type cases and he would thank them not to ruin thirty years of careful seasoning. The man in black actually permitted it. There was a courtesy among professionals, Cyrus supposed, even when the profession was abduction.

She stayed behind the shutter because going down meant dying uselessly, and uselessness was the one inheritance she had sworn she would never accept. She watched them walk him out into the fog—two men ahead, two behind, her father in the middle with his hands loose at his sides and his head up, the way he walked to market, the way he walked everywhere, a man who had decided long ago that dignity was cheaper than fear and better coin besides. At the corner he stopped. He did not turn around. But he raised one hand, briefly, the way a man signals a boatman across a channel, and then the fog took all of them, and the street was empty, and the lamp in the shop still burned with the light he would never come back to put out.

Cyrus came down the stairs on legs that belonged to someone else.

The shop smelled of ink and cold iron and her whole childhood. His apron hung on its peg, folded wrong—he had been hurried after all, or perhaps he had folded it deliberately, crookedly, the way he sometimes left a forme unlocked when he wanted her to notice something. She stood in front of the apron for a long time. Then she turned to the stove, which he had banked perfectly despite everything, and fed it, because the room was cold and because her hands needed work or they would begin shaking and might never stop.

It was while she knelt at the grate that she saw it.

A galley proof lay on the stone beside the stove, face-down, half tucked under the coal scuttle. He had printed something that evening—after she had gone upstairs, after Tomas had climbed to the loft to sleep with the packet strapped beneath his shirt. She pulled it out and turned it over and read her father's last composition in his own sure setting:

*A message was left for you. Under the third drawer, where the broken Bodoni sleeps. I could not burn it and I could not obey it. Forgive me both.*

The third drawer. The font case of ruined type, the same case where two nights ago she had hidden Aldous Vane's letters, wedged among dead sorts like a living thing buried in a pauper's grave. Her stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world. She crossed the shop in five strides, wrenched the drawer open, and lifted the tray of fractured letters out with fingers gone suddenly clumsy.

Beneath it, weighted under a slug of lead, lay a folded paper bearing the seal of the Harbor Command.

She broke the seal and read it by the light of the stove she had just fed, and the words were very short, and very clean, and written in a clerk's beautiful hand, because cruelty always hired good penmen:

*To the printer's daughter. Your father dines with us until further notice. He is comfortable. He will remain comfortable precisely as long as the letters of Aldous Vane remain out of circulation. Bring them to the ice-breaker quay at dawn on the third morning. Come alone, come unarmed, and tell your smuggler friends that Colonel Ashe counts noses. If the letters surface—if one copy appears on one wall in one quarter of this city—we will know the exchange has failed, and printers are cheap this time of year.*

*C. Ashe, Commandant*

Cyrus read it three times. On the third reading her hands had stopped shaking, and that frightened her more than the shaking had, because the stillness that replaced it was not calm. It was the stillness of a press at the top of its pull, the instant the platen hangs suspended above the sheet, when everything that happens next happens fast and cannot be taken back.

So. This was what loyalty demanded. Not the vague, warm loyalty of family dinners and inherited trade, but the kind with edges: her father's life weighed against ten thousand sheets of truth, a scale built by a man who understood exactly what each side of it was worth to her. Ashe had done his arithmetic well. He had taken the one person whose loss would make her obedient, and left her three days to convince herself that obedience was love.

She thought of her father walking into the fog with his head up. He had refused them to their faces. He had confessed his guilt—*knowing it was false*—to armed men in his own doorway, spending his last free words on honesty, buying nothing with it, protecting no one with it except perhaps her, because a confession that named no names and surrendered nothing was just a man telling the truth for the pleasure of doing it once, finally, correctly.

And underneath the devastation, rising through it like heat through floorboards, came the terrible clarity.

Ashe believed the letters were a weapon she would hoard. He believed the threat of a father's death would close her mouth and still her hands. He had built his trap out of her love, and he had made one error in the construction, one flaw in the forme, and Cyrus saw it now as plainly as she saw a bad impression on a pulled sheet: he had assumed she would treat the truth as cargo. Something to be carried, guarded, traded, delivered safely to some other shore, some other authority, some future that might or might not come.

But she was not a carrier of truth. She was a printer's daughter. She did not carry words. She multiplied them.

Ten thousand copies. The number rose in her mind unbidden, absurd, magnificent. Ten thousand sheets pasted to walls and slipped under doors and folded into fish barrels and blown down the frozen streets of the quarter overnight, faster than any soldier could tear them down, faster than any commandant could count noses. Let Ashe hold her father. Let him hold him in a glass house built of his own certainty—because the moment the truth was everywhere, her father's value as leverage would evaporate, and a hostage no one needs is a hostage who lives. That was the mathematics of it. Cruel arithmetic answered with crueler arithmetic.

Or it killed him. Both answers lived in the same equation, and no forgery skill of hers could strike out one line and keep the other.

From the loft above came the soft sound of Tomas shifting in his sleep, the creak of a board, the rustle of the oilskin packet's hiding place disturbed and resettled. Cyrus folded Ashe's letter along its crease, precise as a fold in a signature, and slid it inside her bodice against her heartbeat, where it sat like a second heart, colder and faster.

She looked around the shop—at the cases her grandfather had cut, at the press her father had rebuilt twice, at the apron hanging crooked on its peg, at the whole small kingdom of ink and iron that was the only country she had ever claimed—and she understood that whatever she chose in the next three days, she would be choosing it here, in this room, with these hands. Her father had taught them to set a lie once. They would spend the rest of their lives unlearning the lesson, or they would not spend any life at all.

The stove ticked as the iron warmed. Outside, the harbor ice groaned in the dark, that long subterranean complaint it made when the pressure of the river grew greater than the ice's willingness to hold, and Cyrus listened to it the way one listens to a clock counting down.

Then she went to the foot of the stairs and called up softly into the dark:

"Tomas. Wake up. We have three days—and I need to tell you what I'm going to do."
