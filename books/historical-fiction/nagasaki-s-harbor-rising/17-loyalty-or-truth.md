## 17. Loyalty or Truth

The message had come at dusk, folded into the hands of a child who vanished before Tomas could offer her a coin. *The letters for the printer,* it read, in a clerk's careful hand, *or the printer hangs at the fish market gate when the ice breaks.* Beneath it, someone had pressed a seal in red wax—the harbor commandant's own crest, stamped with the casual confidence of a man who did not believe he could be refused.

Tomas had watched Cyrus read it twice, watched her face do something he had never seen it do: go completely still, like water before it freezes. Then she had folded the paper along its crease, set it on the workbench, and picked up her composing stick as though the world were a thing that could be ordered back into columns.

"We have until the ice breaks," she said. "That's nine days. Maybe seven."

"Cyrus." He kept his voice low, though the shop was empty above and below, her father gone these six hours into whatever cell they'd built for him. "Sit down."

"I can't sit down."

"Then stand there and listen to me."

She didn't sit, but she stopped moving, and that was its own kind of surrender. In the lamplight her hands were gray with ink to the second knuckle, and Tomas thought—not for the first time—that she looked like a woman carved out of the very trade she'd tried to disappear into. Lead and lampblack. A person made of other people's words.

"The ship leaves when the river opens," he said. "Maren's man confirmed it this morning. If we're aboard with the letters—"

"I know what happens if we're aboard with the letters."

"—then your father is a hostage we no longer have. They hang him, yes. I won't lie to you about that. But they were always going to hang him, Cyrus. The moment they took him, he was already dead. This—" he tapped the oilskin packet where it lay hidden inside the case of broken type, beneath a false bottom of her own devising, "—this is the only thing that makes his death mean anything. Ten thousand copies of the truth, delivered to the capital, published under Vane's own hand. The regime doesn't survive that. His death becomes the first blow of the revolution instead of just another cruelty in a long ledger of them."

He heard himself, and hated the shape of the argument even as he built it, brick by brick, because it was a good argument, it was an *honest* argument, and it was also the argument of a man asking a woman to let her father die so that Tomas Reyes could finish what Aldous Vane had started.

"He's not dead yet," Cyrus said.

"No."

"You keep saying *hangs* like it's weather. Like it's something that happens to other people."

"It happened to me," Tomas said quietly. "Or nearly. You know my story. You know what I ran from, what I carried through the ice for. I have spent two years learning exactly how much a single life weighs against a truth, and I am telling you what I learned: not enough. Never enough. That is the arithmetic they want us to do, Cyrus—one life against ten thousand—and every time we do it, we lose, because they are willing to spend lives like water and we are not. So don't do their arithmetic. Don't trade the letters for him. They'll take the letters and hang him anyway. You must know that. A man like Ashe does not release hostages. He *spends* them."

"I know," she said.

"Do you? Because you're standing there looking at me like I've suggested we drown him ourselves."

She turned away then, toward the window, where the harbor lay black and groaning under its shell of ice. Far out, near the channel, the floes were shifting; he could hear them even here, a sound like a giant turning over in sleep, like the bones of the world resetting. Seven days, maybe less. The sea would open like a mouth and then close again for another season, and everything they had bled for would be sealed inside a frozen harbor with a colonel who signed death warrants in other men's names.

"My father taught me to set type when I was six years old," Cyrus said, without turning around. "Did I ever tell you that?"

"You told me you could sort a font by feel in the dark."

"He held my hands inside his hands and showed me how the letters wanted to be held. Not gripped—*held*. He said lead remembers pressure. He said every piece of type ever cast carries the memory of the hand that touched it, and that a printer's whole life is a conversation with everyone who will ever run a finger across her pages, most of whom haven't been born yet." She laughed, a small broken sound. "I used to think that was sentiment. Printer's poetry. And then I found my own cutting in that forged warrant, my own *hand*, and I understood he wasn't being poetic. He was confessing. He knew. All those years he knew what the warrant was, and he let me grow up touching it."

Tomas said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either cruelty or comfort, and he had learned in the smugglers' quarter that both were often the same thing wearing different coats.

"So understand me," she went on, still facing the window. "I have spent my entire life learning how to be invisible. How to keep my head down and my hands clean and my family alive inside a machine that eats families. And I told myself that was survival. I told myself it was wisdom. And then you fell through my door half-dead with a dead man's letters, and I learned that invisibility isn't survival—it's complicity with better posture. Every edict I never refused. Every commission my father took because refusing meant the shop burns and we burn with it. I have been *feeding* this regime my whole life one quiet job at a time, and now, at the end of it, you are asking me to make the largest payment yet. My father's life, spent on the altar of the truth, so that the truth can march on without us."

"Yes," Tomas said. Because lying to her now would be worse than any cruelty.

"And if it were your father?"

"I don't have a father. I have a grave in a city I can't return to, dug by the same men who dug Vane's fake one." He came closer, close enough to see his own reflection ghosted in the dark glass beside hers. "That's precisely why I'm telling you this. I chose once, Cyrus. I stood in a room very much like this one and I chose the people I loved over the evidence in my hands, and I waited—a week, only a week—to find a way to save both. And in that week the men I should have ruined burned the evidence and disappeared four of my friends into the night, and I have been carrying letters through ice ever since, trying to buy back a week I cannot get back. The regime counts on our hesitation. It *feeds* on it. Every hour we spend loving someone is an hour they spend destroying the proof."

"Then go," Cyrus said.

The word landed between them like a dropped weight.

"Maren has boats. Maren has men who owe her. Take the letters, take the packet, take the whole cursed case of broken type if you need to hide it in, and sail on the morning tide. You don't need me. I taught you the seal; you cut a passable chit now yourself. You've walked the checkpoints twice. You can do the rest alone."

"I'm not leaving without you."

"Why not?" She turned at last, and her eyes were wet but her voice was iron, and Tomas understood suddenly that this was the hardest thing she had ever said, harder than any confession. "Because I'm useful? Because I'm the forger's daughter and I know the quay? Or because somewhere between the fish market gate and the customs bridge you decided you couldn't finish this without someone to carry half the terror?" She stepped toward him. "I will not choose, Tomas. That's what you're really asking—not for the letters, not for the revolution. You're asking me to become the kind of person who can do arithmetic with her father's neck, and I am telling you I would rather hang beside him than learn it."

"And I'm telling you he would rather you sailed!" The words tore out of him louder than he intended, echoing off the cases of type, off the press itself, silent and enormous in the corner like a sleeping animal. "You think he confessed to you about the warrant so you'd stay and die with him? He told you the truth so you could *use* it. That's what truth is for, Cyrus. It isn't a keepsake. It isn't a candle you light in a window while the wolves circle. It's a weapon, and weapons are meant to be *carried out* of burning buildings, not held while they burn!"

For a moment neither of them moved. Somewhere below, the ice groaned again, longer this time, a low moaning note that seemed to come up through the floorboards and the soles of their feet, as if the harbor itself were taking sides.

Then Cyrus reached past him—took the oilskin packet from its hiding place—and held it against her chest with both arms, the way a woman holds a child, or a shield.

"If you want it," she said, "take it from me."

"Don't."

"Take it from me, Tomas. Cut the chit, walk the quay, publish your ten thousand copies in the capital, and let them write in the histories that the truth survived because one more loyal daughter was too small a price. Take it from me."

He raised his hands, and let them fall.

They stood there a long time, three feet apart, the packet between them like a border neither would cross, and Tomas felt the partnership they had built—forged in lamplight over stolen seals and smugglers' maps, tempered at two checkpoints, threaded through with something neither of them had dared name—crack straight down the middle. Not shatter. Crack, the way ice cracks: with a sound you feel in your teeth, along a line that runs invisible through the whole surface, promising that when it finally gives, it will give all at once.

"This is how they win," he said at last, and his voice came out hoarse. "Not with soldiers. With this. With making us choose, knowing whichever way we turn, we tear something we can't mend."

"Then maybe," Cyrus said softly, "we stop choosing what they put in front of us."

He frowned. "What does that mean?"

She looked down at the packet in her arms, and then up at him, and something in her face had changed—some decision arriving whole, the way type arrives in a compositor's hand, letter by letter until suddenly there is a word.

"Ashe wants the letters," she said. "Ashe took my father because he believes the letters are worth more than anything else in this harbor—including my father's life, including mine, including yours. Everyone in this city is trading in the same coin, and as long as the letters are the coin, we are beggars at their table." Her fingers tightened on the oilskin. "So we stop begging. We stop carrying the truth like contraband, parcel by parcel, checkpoint by checkpoint, praying some clerk waves us through. We make more of it. We make so much of it that it stops being treasure and starts being *weather*."

"The press," Tomas breathed. "Ten thousand copies. Here. Before the ice breaks."

"Not copies of the letters. Copies of the *confession*. Warrant, signatures, Ashe's name, the whole rotten architecture of it—set in my father's type, printed on my father's press, and in the streets before dawn. Let Ashe hold his hostage. What does a hostage matter when every dockworker, every soldier, every clerk at his own checkpoints has read his signature on a dead man's execution warrant?" She was moving now, pulling the lamp closer, dragging the great press cover off with a sound like a sail unfurling. "He wants to trade in secrets. Fine. We flood the market until secrets are worthless."

"They'll burn the shop. They'll burn you with it."

"Probably." She said it the way she might have said *probably rain*, and Tomas felt something in his chest turn over—not agreement, not yet, but the terrible gravitational pull of her certainty, the same pull that had dragged him half-drowned through a frozen harbor to this door in the first place. "But you asked me to choose between loyalty and truth, and I'm telling you the choice is a lie. Loyalty *is* the truth, Tomas. My loyalty is to the man who taught me that lead remembers pressure. And the truth is that a regime built on a forged page can be unmade by an honest one."

He stared at her across the press, across the waiting rows of empty type, and understood that the crack between them had not split them apart after all. It had split something else—the last of his two years of running, the arithmetic of survival he had carried since the night his friends disappeared. She was going to stay in the burning building and print.

And God help him, he was going to hand her the type.

"The distribution," he said slowly, "would take men. Men who know the quarter, every alley, every cellar door—"

"Maren," they said together.

Tomas almost laughed. Almost. "She'll want something."

"She always wants something." Cyrus was already sorting type by feel in the lamplight, her hands moving with the blind fluency of twenty years, letters clicking into the composing stick like rain beginning. "But tonight, I think, she'll work cheap. Because Maren hates Ashe more than she loves money—I've seen it in her face since the gate. And because tomorrow, when the pamphlets hit the streets, there won't be a harbormaster, a magistrate, or a colonel in this city whose word is worth the paper it's printed on."

Outside, the ice groaned again—and this time, faint but unmistakable, something answered it: a long, splitting crack that ran out across the black harbor toward the channel, toward the open sea, toward the ship that was supposed to carry the truth away.

Cyrus looked up from the case, ink to her elbows, eyes bright as fresh-cut lead.

"Fetch me the warrant," she said. "We set the headline tonight."
