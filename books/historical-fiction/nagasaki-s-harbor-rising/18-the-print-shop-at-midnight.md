## 18. The Print Shop at Midnight

The warrant lay on the composing stone like something dissected.

Cyrus had spread it flat beneath the lamp, weighted the corners with type slugs, and read it through four times before she touched it. Two years of avoiding that document—two years of keeping her eyes on her hands and her hands on her work—and now she bent over it the way a surgeon bends over a wound she made herself. The vellum had yellowed along its folds. The seal was Ashe's, pressed deep and confident. And there, in the lower third, in the neat clerk's hand that had once belonged to Aldous Vane: *I confess to conspiracy against the Provisional Authority, and submit myself to summary judgment.*

A confession written by a dead man. A dead man who, if Tomas Reyes spoke true, was not dead at all.

"It's good work," she said quietly. It was the only honest thing she could think to say about it. "Whoever set this knew their trade."

"You set it," Tomas said from the doorway.

"I know." She did not look up. Her fingers hovered over the forged lines without touching them. "That's what makes it worse."

Tomas came closer, boots damp on the floorboards, and stood watching her the way men watch water rise. Behind him, Maren leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed, snow melting off her shoulders onto Cyrus's father's clean stone step, and said nothing, because Maren understood—as Cyrus's father never quite had—that some silences were worth more than speeches.

"They'll come for the letters," Maren said at last. "Ashe doesn't send soldiers to arrest a printer as leverage and then wait politely for spring. You have until dawn. Maybe less."

"Then we'd best be quick."

"Cyrus." Maren's voice dropped into the register she used on drunk sailors and frightened apprentices. "Ten thousand copies. In one night. On one press. With three pairs of hands. That's not printing, girl, that's madness wearing an apron."

Cyrus finally looked up, and whatever Maren saw in her face made her stop talking.

For twenty-six years Cyrus Whitlock had measured her life in what could be carried. A drawer of type small enough to lift. A name small enough to hide. A conscience small enough, she had told herself, to fold up and put away like winter clothes when the season turned. She had watched pamphlets circulate through the market and kept her head down. She had burned counter-edicts rather than print them and called that neutrality. She had learned every trick of being invisible, and she had been so proud of the learning.

And all the while her own hands had set the lie. Her own cutting—the little serifed face she'd carved at nineteen, the one with the slightly heavy foot on the lowercase *a*—had carried Aldous Vane's false confession into every magistrate's office in the province. The regime hadn't needed a forger of documents. It had needed a printer who didn't ask questions, and it had found one, and she had gone home each night and slept soundly on her straw tick.

Invisibility, it turned out, was just complicity with better posture.

"No," Cyrus said. "Not madness. Arithmetic." She pulled the warrant toward her and began, with a penknife, to trim the confession free of the seal and signature. "One copy of these letters is evidence. Evidence can be seized. Evidence can burn. But ten thousand copies—" the knife whispered through the old vellum, and she felt something in her chest tear along with it, some last thread holding her to the woman she had been yesterday morning, "—ten thousand copies are already in people's hands before the first soldier reaches the fish market gate. You cannot confiscate a thing that has become everyone's."

Maren stared at her. Then, slowly, she grinned—not the smuggler's grin, but something older and hungrier underneath it.

"Where do you want the barrels?"

* * *

They worked the way drowning people swim—without elegance, without pause.

Cyrus's father's press was a flatbed built by his grandfather, iron-boned and temperamental, and it had never in its life been asked to run all night. She oiled the ways herself, wiped down the platen, and mixed ink by feel in the dark the way she'd done since she was nine years old: linseed, lampblack, a thread of turpentine against the cold. The smell filled the shop, that particular black sweetness that meant *something is about to exist*, and Cyrus breathed it in like a woman coming up from underwater.

Tomas set the body text. He was clumsy at first—a courier's hands, quick but untrained, dropping sorts and confusing his b's and d's in mirror—but he learned fast, the way he seemed to learn everything, and by the second hour he was setting lines that only wobbled a little. He worked from the letters themselves, copying out the passages that mattered: Vane's account of the staged execution, the payments routed through the customs house, the names. Ashe's name above all. Ashe's name, over and over, until it stopped looking like a name and started looking like a nail.

"The headline," Cyrus said. She held up a stick of type. "Read it back."

Tomas read: *"THE EXECUTION WAS A LIE. THE MAGISTRATE LIVES. THE MEN WHO SIGNED HIS DEATH SET IT DOWN IN THEIR OWN HANDS. READ AND PASS IT ON."*

"Not 'pass it on.'" She shook her head, pulling the sort back out. "'Read it aloud.' Paper can be confiscated. A voice in a fish market is harder."

She reset the line herself, letter by letter, and felt each piece click into place like a bone going back into a joint. *Read it aloud.* Her whole life she had communicated through intermediaries—ink speaking for her, paper standing where she would not. Tonight there would be no intermediary. Ten thousand strangers were going to open their mouths and say words she had set, and there was no taking that back, no anonymity left to retreat into. Somewhere between the first form locked and the tenth thousandth impression, Cyrus Whitlock was going to stop being invisible, and she found—to her own astonishment—that what she felt was not fear.

It was appetite.

* * *

The press began to groan around midnight, and the ice answered it through the floorboards, two great voices arguing under the city.

Her father sat by the stove, still bruised from the cell, wrapped in two blankets and refusing to go to bed. For the first hour he only watched, and Cyrus felt his gaze on the back of her neck like lamplight. Then, somewhere past the three-hundredth sheet, he rose stiffly and came to stand beside the press, and put his hand—his ink-stained, ruined, beloved hand—flat on the frame.

"Pull even," he said. "You're favoring your right side. You've done that since you were twelve."

She adjusted her grip. The impression came down square and full, black as a verdict.

"Papa." She didn't stop working. Sheets in, sheets out; the rhythm was the only thing keeping her upright. "The warrant. When they brought it to you."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. The press clattered. Outside, a patrol went by with lanterns swinging, and everyone in the room froze until the light slid past the frost-furred windows and faded down the lane.

"They paid triple rates," he said finally. "In advance. And the man who brought it watched me set the first line, and I understood—I understood immediately, Cyrus, I'm not the fool you've thought me these two days—that if I refused, I'd be a witness. And witnesses to that kind of work don't retire. They're retired." His hand moved on the press frame, absently, the way it did when he was thinking in type. "So I printed it. And then I told myself a story about survival, and I told it so many times I believed it. That's the trick of it, girl. They don't make us liars. They let us make ourselves."

"And me?" Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "You let me set it. You knew my hand would be in it."

"You were nineteen. If you'd known, you'd have carried it like a stone your whole life." He looked at her then, really looked, and Cyrus saw that his eyes were wet and that he wasn't ashamed of it. "Instead you're carrying it like a hammer. That's better. That's what lead is for."

She pulled the next sheet, and the next, and neither of them said anything else for a long time, because there are conversations that can only happen through work, and this was theirs.

By two o'clock the stacks reached the windowsills. By three, the shop smelled so thoroughly of ink that no inspection would ever believe nothing was printed here. Maren's people began arriving in ones and twos through the back alley door—a herring-girl with a market barrow, a customs runner with a false-bottomed crate, two brothers who stank of tar and said nothing at all. Each took a stack, folded the broadsides into oilcloth, and vanished into the white dark.

"Ten thousand," Maren said, counting the last bundle twice. "Give or take the misprints."

"Misprints go out too." Cyrus was washing the press, her arms aching down to the shoulder blades, her fingers cracked from cold lye. "An imperfect truth is still truer than a perfect lie."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. "You know what happens tomorrow. Ashe reads that at breakfast and the whole harbor becomes a hunt with you at the center of it. There won't be any invisibility to crawl back into."

"I know."

"And the ship sails when the channel opens. Reyes goes. You could go."

Cyrus looked across the shop at Tomas, who had fallen asleep sitting up amid a drift of wasted paper, his cheek smudged black, one hand still curled loosely around a composing stick as though even in sleep he wouldn't surrender it. He had crossed half a frozen country carrying those letters. He had earned the ship.

But the letters were going with him, yes—and so was everything else. Because the original packet would sail for the capital, where magistrates might someday weigh it. But the copies were staying here, in the harbor, where the weighing happened differently: dockworker by dockworker, soldier by soldier, kitchen by kitchen, in the low urgent murmur of *read this, pass it on, read it aloud.*

The capital could have justice, whenever the capital got around to it. The harbor needed a voice tonight.

"I'm staying," Cyrus said. "Someone has to print tomorrow."

* * *

By the gray hour before dawn the last bundle went out the alley door on the herring-girl's barrow, buried under silver fish that glittered in the lamplight like coins. Maren paused at the threshold, snow whirling in behind her, and gripped Cyrus's forearm hard enough to leave marks.

"Quay gate opens at first light," she said. "Ashe will be there. So will half the garrison. And by then—" she nodded at the empty stacks, the spent press, the whole night's mad arithmetic, "—so will everyone else."

Then she was gone, and the shop was quiet except for the ice, which had stopped groaning and begun, very faintly, to sing—that high crystalline keening the old fishermen said meant the river was deciding whether to hold or break.

Cyrus washed her hands at the basin. The ink came away slowly, staining the water black, and she watched the last of it swirl down the drain with something that was not regret. Her nails stayed dark. Her fingertips stayed dark. She suspected they always would now.

On the wall above the press, her father had pinned a single proof sheet—one misprinted copy, the headline blurred where the form had shifted—because he said every shop needed a flaw to aim past. *THE EXECUTION WAS A LIE,* it said, slightly crooked, entirely true.

Tomas stirred, woke, and looked at the empty racks where the bundles had been. "It's done?"

"It's done."

"Then we should sleep an hour before—"

A bell began ringing out over the harbor. Not the tide bell, and not the fire bell. The quay bell—rung only when the commandant himself came down to the water. And beneath it, spreading through the frozen streets in a rising tide, came another sound entirely: voices. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Men and women standing in their doorways in the blue dark, reading aloud.

Cyrus picked up the oilskin packet of originals and held it against her chest, feeling her own heartbeat answer it.

"That's our cue," she said. "The quay opens at first light—and so does the ice."
