## 22. The First Honest Page

The ice went out of the harbor on a Tuesday, with a sound like a door opening in the floor of the world.

Cyrus had been standing at the window of the print shop when it happened—had been standing there most mornings since the quay, since Ashe fell, since her father came home to her thin and gray and blinking at daylight as though he had forgotten what it was for. The river had held all winter in one gray plate, scarred with sled tracks and the black holes where fishermen cut their lines, and then some warm current from the sea reached up beneath it and said *enough*, and the whole harbor groaned, cracked, and began to move. Chunks of ice the size of carts ground against the pilings. Gulls screamed over them. Somewhere down the waterfront a crowd gathered and cheered at nothing, at everything, at water moving where water had not been permitted to move.

Her father had come to stand beside her, his hands smelling of ink and lamp oil as they always did, and watched without comment. Then he had gone back downstairs to his case of type, because there was work, because there would always be work now, and that was the strangest mercy of all: that after everything, life had resolved itself back into lead and paper and the small patient labor of putting words in order.

That had been six weeks ago.

Now it was late spring, and the harbor smelled of tar and herring and thawed rope instead of iron cold, and the shop above it belonged—to the extent that anything belonged to anyone anymore—to three people who had no business sharing a roof and had made a family of it anyway.

* * *

Maren took the stairs two at a time, which Cyrus could hear through the floorboards before she heard the voice: "The paper stock came. All of it. Nobody stopped it at the gate."

"Nobody stopped it," Cyrus repeated, not looking up from the stone where she was rolling ink. It still surprised her, the absence of stopping. For two years her whole craft had been organized around the fact that someone might come through that door at any moment wanting to know what she was printing and why. Now the customs men waved Maren's carts through with something between indifference and salute. One of them—the young one with the chapped hands, the one who had read the confession four times by his own admission—had started leaving his boots outside the shop door on delivery days so as not to track mud across her clean stones.

"Four hundredweight," Maren said, dropping onto the bench by the window and propping her feet on a crate of blank newsprint. She looked, as always, like a woman who had slept in her coat and won an argument about it. Her smuggler's ring glinted on her thumb—the one she'd taken as payment for the favor Cyrus still hadn't named, though they both knew by now that the name of it was *stay*. "You know what that means."

"It means we can print more than eight pages."

"It means," Maren said, with the gravity she reserved for matters of cargo and profit, "that you can print the shipping schedules. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Actual shipping schedules. Tides. Prices per barrel. Men will buy this thing for the schedules alone and then they'll accidentally read the rest of it."

"That was always the plan."

"I know it was your plan. I'm telling you it's a good plan. I don't say that often." She tipped her head back against the wall. "My people are asking when the next one comes out. Not the pamphlets. The *paper*. There's a woman on Rope Walk who reads it aloud in the doorway of the chandlery every morning for anyone who can't read themselves. She does voices. Your editorials get a voice."

Cyrus felt heat climb her neck. "They're not editorials."

"They're signed 'The Printer's Daughter' and they tell Colonel Ashe's successor exactly how much of a fool he is. They're editorials." Maren grinned. "He wrote back, you know. A letter. To a newspaper. He wants a correction about the tonnage figures."

"Did you bring it?"

"I brought it. I also brought bread, because unlike you I remember that people eat." She produced a loaf from her satchel and set it on the case of twelve-point bodoni like a trophy. "Eat while it's warm. Your father already had his."

Through the open door to the back room, Cyrus could see him: Elias Whitlock, sixty-one years old, three months free of a regime cell, bent over his composing stick with the concentration of a man performing surgery. He had lost weight in custody that never fully came back. His left hand shook now in the early mornings, and he hid it, and everyone let him hide it, and on bad days he set type slower than Cyrus did and hated himself for it and she pretended not to notice that too. But he was here. He was here at a frame by the south window with the good light, setting the market column the way he had set ten thousand market columns across thirty years, and twice this week he had laughed—actually laughed—at something Maren said, the sound rusty and startled out of him, like a hinge remembering its purpose.

She had traded everything for that. She would trade it again.

* * *

The newspaper had no name for its first three weeks. That had been a fight—or rather, a series of proposals, each more grandiose than the last, voted on informally at the kitchen table and rejected by majority. Maren wanted *The Harbor Voice*, which Cyrus vetoed for sounding like a smuggler's code name, which Maren took as a compliment. Tomas, in his last letter before the ship, had suggested *The Vane Gazette* in honor of the dead magistrate whose letters had started everything, and Cyrus had written back that Aldous Vane would have hated it, which was precisely why Tomas proposed it, and the matter died there. Her father offered nothing. When pressed, he said only: "Name it for what it is. Not what you want it to be."

So it was called *The Nagasaki Harbor Record*, which was dull, and honest, and printed at the top of every page in a typeface Cyrus had cut herself—her first new cutting in two years, her first that no one had ordered or paid for or would ever use to sign a lie.

Eight hundred copies the first week. Sixteen hundred the second. This week Maren had promised her three thousand, and Cyrus believed her, because Maren's network of boatmen and carters had turned out to be worth more than any army the revolution hadn't needed. The regime had fallen not to soldiers but to dockworkers who had read, overnight, in ten thousand pamphlets, exactly what their colonel had done—and who decided, collectively and without ceremony, that they were done taking orders from him. Cyrus had stood on the quay that dawn and watched it happen and understood, finally, what her father had tried to teach her all those years she thought he was teaching her cowardice: that ink is a slow weapon, but if you put enough of it in enough hands, it cuts faster than steel.

The confession itself—the full text, with the forged warrant reproduced in facsimile and both signatures named—had run as the Record's first front page. They had sold out by noon. By evening someone had pasted a copy to the door of the old magistrate's court with a note beneath it in charcoal: *WE READ THIS ONE. ALL OF IT.*

Cyrus kept that copy. It hung framed by the shop door, warped with weather, and she dusted it every morning along with the type cases.

* * *

At midday she climbed down to the flat to make tea and found her father standing at the window with a letter in his hand, and knew before he spoke that something had shifted in the air of the room.

"Not bad news," he said quickly, which meant it was complicated news. He held the letter out. "From the capital."

She dried her hands before taking it. The seal was official—not the old regime's broken wheel, but the new provisional mark, a plain anchor, chosen by committee and looking it. The letter inside was short. It offered Elias Whitlock a commission: master printer to the government press in the capital, a salary, a house, a title. Recognition, at last, of the man who had survived to tell the truth with his hands. And beneath it, in a different hand—a postscript, personal, almost shy:

*We are told your daughter is the better compositor. We would take her instead, if she would come. —T.R.*

Tomas Reyes. Alive, then, and writing letters on government stationery, which was a sentence she had never expected to compose even in her own head. His ship had reached the capital in April. His reports had done whatever reports do in rooms where power actually turns on hinges; the provisional council had used the Vane letters to unseat half the old judiciary in a season. He wrote once a month now, long letters full of capital gossip and terrible jokes and, always, near the end, one careful line that was not a proposal and not quite not one: *The city is loud but the streets are straight. You would like the light here. It comes early.*

"He should go," her father said quietly. "You know that. A salary. A house. No more watching the door."

"You should go," Cyrus corrected. "It's addressed to you."

"And I am seventy percent dead weight in a composing room these days, and we both know it." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "But I'm not going, Cy. I set my first page in this shop when I was nine years old. My father set his first page here before me. Whatever the harbor needs printers for—" he looked out the window, at the masts, at the water running free where the ice had been—"it doesn't need them in the capital. It needs them here. Where people learned what a lie costs."

Cyrus folded the letter along its original creases. "I'm not going either."

"I know." He almost smiled. "I told Tomas you'd say that in the first paragraph. I told him you'd stand at that window and look stubborn about it."

"What did he say?"

"He said, 'Good. Someone has to keep the record.'" Her father took the letter back and set it on the shelf where they kept things that mattered—under the framed warrant, next to the oilskin packet, empty now, its contents delivered into history. "Now come upstairs. Maren says the tide piece has to be set before three, and apparently the whole harbor's commerce collapses if we're late, and I'd like to see you argue with her about hyphenation again. It's the most entertainment I get."

* * *

The afternoon light came in low and gold across the composing frames, and Cyrus worked in it with the ease of long habit, her fingers reading the typecases by touch the way her mother had once read scripture. Lead type has a feel to it that no machine has ever matched—the slight burr of a fresh-cut sort, the worn smoothness of a letter that has been set ten thousand times, each piece carrying in its metal the memory of every page it ever made. She had spent years measuring her life in what could be carried: which typefaces to grab if the inspectors came, which memories to bury, which truths to leave in drawers. Her hands had learned to be invisible. Even her best work—the warrant, the beautiful, damning warrant—had been made by hands pretending to be nobody's.

But lead remembers. That was the lesson of the whole terrible winter, the lesson she would carry now instead of a packed bag: you cannot unmake what your hands have made. Better, then, to make things deliberately. To sign your work. To be somebody's hands.

"The tide table's wrong," Maren announced from the proof press, squinting at the galley. "Second column. High water's at 4:40, not 4:14. If we print 4:14 some fisherman's going to ground his boat on the breakwater and sue us, and I want it on record now that I told you."

"You didn't tell me. You just told me."

"I'm telling you retroactively. That's what editors are for." Maren pulled the slip and slapped it down on the stone. "Fix it, Printer's Daughter."

Cyrus fixed it. Four sorts, lifted, replaced, locked. Her father watched her do it and nodded once, the smallest nod, the nod he had given her whole childhood whenever she did the work correctly and needed no praise beyond confirmation that correct existed.

Outside, the harbor ran glittering toward the sea. A herring fleet was coming in with the evening tide, right on schedule, right on the time she herself had set in lead for three thousand people to read. On Rope Walk, in a doorway, a woman was probably doing the voices. In the capital, a man who had once been fished half-dead out of the harbor ice was reading her letters aloud to people with power, and telling them, she hoped, that the light here came early.

Three thousand copies. Tomorrow, maybe more. Not pamphlets thrown into the dark, but a paper—ordinary, weekly, dull in places, wrong in others until corrected, bought for the shipping schedules and read for everything else. Justice, it turned out, did not arrive like an army or end like one. It arrived like this: as a habit. As a page, set honestly, set again.

Cyrus locked the chase, lifted the forme, and carried it to the press—carried it easily, lightly, the weight of it nothing at all compared to what it held.
