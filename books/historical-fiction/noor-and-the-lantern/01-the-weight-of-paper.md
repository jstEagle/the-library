## 1. The Weight of Paper

The rain had been falling on Vessany's capital for eleven days, and by the twelfth even the pigeons had given up pretending to enjoy it. August Ashgrove stood beneath the broken awning of a shuttered tobacconist on Candlemaker's Row, watching water pour off the awning's torn corner in a thin silver rope, and counted the bells.

Six. He was early. Couriers were always early; it was one of the few luxuries the profession permitted. A courier could not be rich, could not be famous, could not be remembered — but he could be early, and August had learned to take his pleasures where he found them.

The city did not look like a capital anymore. It looked like a man who had dressed carefully for his own funeral and then sat down in the mud. Sandbags sagged against the fronts of respectable shops. The great clock over the Exchange had stopped at twenty past four some weeks ago, and no one had climbed up to fix it, which told you more about the state of the Republic of Vessany than any broadsheet could. Posters layered the walls three deep: STAND FIRM, older ones reading GOD PRESERVE THE CHANCELLOR, older still, half scraped away, advertising a brand of soap that no longer existed. The newest posters were recruitment notices, printed on paper so thin the ink bled through, and beneath them someone had scrawled in charcoal: *firm for whom?*

August read everything. It was habit, not politics. A courier who read nothing was a parcel; a courier who read everything was a liability; the trick was to read everything and remember only what kept you alive. He had gotten quite good at it, in nine years. There were whole ministries' worth of secrets folded into the soft places behind his eyes, and he carried them the way other men carried a stiff shoulder or an old debt — constantly, without thinking, and with no hope of setting them down.

At six and a quarter, the door of the candle shop opened. Not a candle shop anymore, of course. It had become, in succession, a records annex, a billet for engineers, and now nothing at all except a place where a certain kind of man might stand at a certain hour and pretend to examine candles that hadn't been stocked since spring.

"Raining," said the man inside, who was called Fenwick to anyone who asked and several things behind his back to those who didn't.

"Observation worthy of the Intelligence Bureau," said August, and went in out of the wet.

* * *

Fenwick was sixty if he was fifty-five, with the face of a disappointed schoolmaster and hands so soft they seemed embarrassed to exist. He poured tea from a dented pot into two cups that did not match, because matching cups, like matching stories, invited scrutiny. The room smelled of wax and mildew and, faintly, of lamp oil — the eternal smell of every safehouse August had ever known, as though the Republic's entire intelligence apparatus were one long preparation for a fire that never came.

"You look tired," Fenwick said.

"I look like a man standing in a candle shop during a siege."

"That too." Fenwick pushed a cup across the table. "Drink it. It's terrible, but it's hot, and I've gone to real trouble stealing it."

August drank. It was terrible. It was also, he noted with distant gratitude, laced with something that would keep his hands steady through the night ahead, and he made a mental note to eat something before it ate him.

"Now," Fenwick said, and set a leather case on the table between them. It was the size of a hymnal and about as welcoming. "Seven documents."

August looked at the case and felt the familiar cold settle behind his breastbone, the one he had learned to mistake for calm. Seven was a number. Numbers meant routes. Routes meant checkpoints, and checkpoints meant soldiers, and soldiers these days meant boys with rifles and no sleep, which was the most dangerous variety of soldier there was.

"Seven," August repeated.

"Seven sealed documents. Diplomatic weight. You will carry them from this city to—"

"Don't tell me where."

"I wasn't going to." Fenwick almost smiled. "Look at you. Nine years and still correcting me. It warms my heart." He opened the case. Inside, seven packets lay in oiled cloth, each one flat, each one sealed with wax stamped by an office whose seal August recognized and whose name he would forget by morning, professionally speaking. "You'll move north through Ashfield, west along the river road, and across the mountains at Dren Pass. Three jurisdictions. Technically three countries, depending on which map you buy and how recently."

"And depending on who's winning," August said.

"The maps haven't caught up yet. Yours will be current as of Thursday." Fenwick tapped the case. "Ashfield's burned. The river road has a republican checkpoint at Marlow Bridge and a loyalist checkpoint two miles past it, and both sides believe the other side is the traitors, which is convenient for us because neither side is wrong. Dren Pass closes with the first snow. You have perhaps three weeks of weather, less of luck."

August turned one of the packets over with one finger. The wax was good quality. Good wax meant important hands. Important hands meant that whatever was written inside mattered enough that men had already died for the privilege of not knowing its contents.

"What am I carrying?" he asked, though he knew the answer, and asked it anyway, because asking was part of the ritual, and rituals were how a man stayed human in a trade designed to make him otherwise.

"Paper," said Fenwick.

"Fenwick."

"Heavy paper," said Fenwick, with the ghost of warmth. "That's all you get. That's all there is. You're a hinge, Ashgrove, not a door. Hinges don't ask what's on the other side."

"I've been a hinge for nine years. I'd like to know what I'm swinging open."

"No." Fenwick closed the case and slid it across. "You wouldn't. That's the mercy of this work, though I know it doesn't feel like one. The day you know what you carry is the day you start having opinions about it, and a courier with opinions is a corpse with excellent posture."

August took the case. It weighed almost nothing. It always weighed almost nothing. That was the joke of it, the black joke at the center of his profession: seven documents that could unmake governments, lighter than a loaf of bread. He had once carried a single sheet that ended a truce and began the worst winter of the war, and he'd used it, briefly, to swat a fly.

"A courier's virtue," Fenwick said, rising, gathering the teacups with those soft apologetic hands, "is weightlessness."

"You say that every time."

"Because you keep forgetting it. Weightless, Ashgrove. Nothing in your pockets but what belongs there. No letters of your own, no photographs, no names in your boot. You are a man passing through. Nobody remembers a man passing through. That's not a tragedy, it's a career."

"I know."

"Do you? Because last month you gave your name to the ferryman at Corvel."

"I gave him *a* name."

"You smiled when you gave it. That's worse. A name delivered without a smile is noise. A name delivered with a smile is a memory, and memories get people hanged." Fenwick set the cups down and looked at him, and for a moment the schoolmaster fell away and something older and wearier stood there instead. "You're the best I have. Do you understand that? Out of forty couriers on the active rolls, you're the one I trust with seven seals. So don't make me regret it by being charming."

August almost laughed. Charming. If Fenwick only knew — if any of them knew — that the charm was armor, worn so long it had grown into the skin. A courier who was liked was forgiven small errors. A courier who was dull was scrutinized. August had chosen, long ago, to be the sort of man strangers confided in: pleasant, forgettable, a face like a well-worn coin. It had worked through two wars, one purge, and an affair involving a colonel's wife that he thought of, in his kinder moments, as a tactical error.

"I'll be weightless," he said.

"Good." Fenwick hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a flicker, gone almost before it arrived, but August had spent nine years learning to read flickers the way farmers read clouds. "There's one more thing."

"There always is."

"This is likely the last run. Either way it goes — the offensive, the negotiations, whichever way the wind sits — after this, the network stands down. Reorganization. New names, new routes. Everyone reassigned."

Everyone reassigned. August had heard those words before, in another country, another lifetime, and the men who heard them had mostly been reassigned into shallow graves outside the walls. But he said none of this. He said, "Where will they send you?"

"Me? I'm too old to reassign. Old hinges just get oiled and left in place." Fenwick busied himself with the teapot, rinsing it, though there was no reason to rinse it. "Go home. Sleep. Eat something that isn't ration bread, you look like a heron. Move at first light tomorrow. And Ashgrove—" He stopped, cup in hand, and for one strange moment seemed about to say something else entirely, something with a human shape to it.

"Yes?"

"Nothing. Mind the weather at Dren Pass."

* * *

August walked home the long way, because the long way had fewer patrols and better shadows, and because walking was the closest thing he had to thinking.

The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist that turned every lantern into a small blurred moon. The city moved around him in fragments: a woman hauling water in silence; two old men playing cards by lamplight under an archway, betting buttons, arguing happily about nothing; somewhere a violin, badly played, persisting anyway. Besieged cities were like that, August had found. They grew tender in their extremity. People hoarded kindness along with flour, because they'd learned what scarcity was and refused to let it claim everything.

He passed the bakery on Tanner's Lane where the queue formed at five each morning regardless of whether there would be anything to queue for, and he thought, not for the first time, that he knew exactly how the queue felt.

Home was a room above a cobbler's shop, rented under a name he had held for two years, furnished with the deliberate blandness of a hotel. He lit no lamp. He sat on the narrow bed with the leather case beside him and ate the heel of a loaf he'd bought that morning from a boy who'd charged him double and lied beautifully about why, and he let the dread come.

It always came the night before a run. Nine years, and it had never once failed to arrive, punctual as the bells. It didn't come as fear, exactly. Fear was for the checkpoints, the searches, the moments when a sergeant's finger traced your papers twice. This was quieter. This was the sound of his own life measured out in sealed packets — seven documents, three war zones, one man — and the growing suspicion, which he kept locked in the same soft place behind his eyes as everything else, that the Republic he served had stopped deserving service some time ago, and that he had noticed, and that noticing was itself a kind of treason he committed daily.

A courier's virtue is weightlessness.

And yet. He thought of the fly swatted with a treaty. He thought of Fenwick's flicker, that unfinished sentence hanging in the candle-smell air like smoke looking for a chimney. *This is likely the last run.* Last runs had a way of becoming last things.

He lay back on the bed without undressing and put one hand on the leather case, the way a man rests a hand on a sleeping animal, and told himself the old professional lie: *you carry paper, and paper is nothing, and you are nothing, and nothing can't be harmed.*

Somewhere below, the cobbler's dog stirred and settled. Somewhere across the city, the stopped clock held its breath at twenty past four forever.

Seven documents. He would count them again at first light. He always counted them again at first light, and they were always seven, and it was always somehow a surprise.

He slept, eventually, the way couriers sleep: lightly, fully dressed, one ear open for boots on stairs.

He dreamed, though he would not remember it, of a garden in peacetime, green and sunlit and impossibly far away, and of a friend laughing in it — and of a promise made there, drunk and solemn, that would come back for him sooner than he knew, wearing rain and carrying a note in a familiar hand.
