## 2. Instructions to Forget

The rain began an hour before Vessany's border did, as if the sky had been briefed on the occasion and wished to set a suitably miserable tone.

August Ashgrove walked into it without hurrying. Hurrying was for men with destinations they could admit to. He had seven documents sewn into the lining of his coat, a route through three war zones, and a face that three separate governments had paid good money to forget existed. The rain could do what it liked. It had never once managed to make him memorable.

The safehouse was supposed to be a millhouse outside the village of Corren Hollow, marked by a blue shutter and a horse trough that hadn't held water since the last war, which in this country meant roughly eleven months ago. He found the blue shutter easily enough. The trough was full of rain now, brimming, as though the house itself were apologizing on the republic's behalf. Everything else about the place matched the description in his briefing: the sagging porch, the door that opened on the third push rather than the first — a small courtesy to any pursuer too impatient to be worth deceiving — and the smell inside of wet stone and older smoke.

No lamp was lit. No handler waited. That was correct procedure; you never lit lamps at a safehouse you expected to be watched, and you never waited for a courier because a courier who knew his contacts by sight was a courier who could be tortured into naming them. August had spent nine years being deliberately unacquainted with everyone he worked for. It had made him, by the republic's own accounting, their most reliable man. Reliability, he had learned, was just loneliness that had been given a pension plan.

He closed the door behind him and stood a moment in the dark, letting his eyes adjust, listening to the rain hammer the roof like a creditor at the door of a dying business.

That was when he heard the breathing.

It was not the breathing of a man hiding in the rafters — August had been hidden in enough rafters himself to know the difference. It was small. Irregular. A little wet at the edges, the way breath sounds when it has recently been crying and has decided, on balance, to continue.

August went very still, the way he'd been trained to go still, which is to say completely and with his hand already moving toward the knife in his boot.

Then something in the corner of the main room shifted, and made a sound he had not heard in nine years of professional stillness.

It gurgled.

* * *

There was a basket on the floor beside the cold hearth. Wicker, water-stained, of the sort farmers used to carry apples to market or hens to a neighbor less fortunate in the matter of hens. Inside it, wrapped in a blanket that had once been white and was now the color of surrender, lay a baby.

A girl, by the look of her. Very small. Weeks old, perhaps two months — August's expertise in these matters extended precisely as far as the fact that babies came in one size and cried in one key, both of which struck him as design failures. She had a shock of black hair plastered to her head, fists like walnuts, and an expression of profound personal grievance, which under the circumstances August considered entirely reasonable.

On top of the blanket lay a folded piece of paper, weighted down by a smooth gray river stone so it would not be carried off by the draft that whistled through every window in the building.

He should have left it there. He knew that the way he knew his own heartbeat: a courier finds an object in a safehouse that is not on his manifest, the courier leaves the object, reports nothing, forgets everything. Weightlessness. Handler Sef had drilled it into him until the words lived in his spine. *You are a sleeve, Ashgrove, not an arm. Things pass through you. Nothing stays.*

But the baby sneezed.

It was a tremendous, whole-body sneeze, the kind that seemed to surprise even her, and afterward she looked up at him with an expression of such startled dignity that August felt something shift in his chest — some small structural failure in a wall he had spent nine years building, brick by brick, out of not-asking and not-noticing and not-knowing.

He picked up the note.

*Forget this face.*

Four words. He knew the handwriting before he finished the first one. He would have known it upside down, through smoke, across twenty years. The capital letter F leaned hard to the right, the way Ilan Voss had leaned in every argument they'd ever had, confident the world would eventually tip to meet him. The double s at the end crossed with a single impatient stroke, as if even finishing a word were a formality Voss resented.

Nine years since August had seen that hand. Three since the tribunals had declared its owner an enemy of the republic, stripped him of rank, burned him from the rolls, and begun calling him *the Butcher of Kelmar Pass* in newspapers whose editors had never seen a pass of any kind, let alone Kelmar's. And here was that same traitor's pen, writing four words on wet paper, in a safehouse that existed on no official map, addressed to no one — except that it was addressed to him, of course it was, because who else in all of Vessany would understand what those four words cost to write?

Beneath the note, half-tucked under the stone, was a second paper. Smaller. Folded twice.

August turned it over and saw the seal.

Wax, dark green, pressed with a signet ring he had last seen on a hand dealing him cards in a garden that no longer existed, in a country that technically no longer existed either, if you believed the maps the enemy was drawing. A lantern, crossed by a sword. The crest of House Voss, extinct by decree, sitting warm and whole in his palm like a coal that had refused the general conflagration.

His orders said seven documents. Seven sealed documents, sewn into his coat lining, each one a little engine of other people's fates. His orders did not say anything about an eighth item. His orders especially did not say anything about an item that breathed, sneezed, and looked up at him now with eyes the color of storm-water, tracking his face with the unnerving seriousness of someone taking attendance.

"You," August told her quietly, "are not on my manifest."

She blew a small bubble of spit, which he chose to interpret as contempt.

Outside, the rain kept on. Somewhere beyond it were three war zones, a besieged capital, a republic eating itself alive from the collarbones down, and seven documents that needed delivering more urgently than ever, because wars accelerated everything, including the speed at which failure became treason. Every hour he stood in this room was an hour the route didn't forgive. Sef had a saying for that too: *The road doesn't wait, Ashgrove, and neither do firing squads.*

And yet.

He crouched by the basket, joints protesting — he was thirty-four going on sixty, the courier's occupational hazard — and examined her the way he'd been trained to examine unfamiliar documents: provenance, condition, signs of tampering. Blanket, fine wool, expensive once. Basket, rural, chosen for anonymity. Baby: clean, fed recently, someone's careful work. Whoever had left her here had not abandoned her. They had *delivered* her, with the same discipline August brought to his own cargo, and then they had written four words in a dead man's hand and put a stone on top so the wind couldn't take the instruction away.

*Forget this face.*

The trouble was that August had spent nine years being excellent at forgetting faces, and it had required every trick he had. This face was doing something unfair. It had a dimple on only one side, which seemed like cheating. And when she yawned — an enormous, collapsing yawn that ended in a squeak she appeared embarrassed about — the wall in his chest developed a second crack to keep the first one company.

"Illegal," he informed her. "What you're doing is illegal under at least three statutes. I could name them."

She grabbed his finger with one walnut fist. Her grip was astonishing, frankly unreasonable, the grip of someone who had already decided that letting go was for other people. August knelt there on the cold floor of a safehouse in the rain, holding a traitor's daughter — assuming she was a traitor's daughter, assuming the seal meant blood and not merely message — and understood with cold clarity that he was in more danger at this moment than he had ever been crossing any checkpoint in nine years of professional weightlessness.

Because a courier who stops moving is a courier who can be found. And a courier who cares is a courier who can be broken.

He thought about Sef's voice. *Nothing stays.* He thought about the purge lists he wasn't supposed to know existed, the quiet way reliable men had been vanishing from the payrolls lately, their names scrubbed so thoroughly that asking after them had become its own offense. He thought about a garden, ten years ago, and a drunk man pointing a wine bottle at him across a table and making him promise something August had laughed at then, because peacetime had made them both idiots.

*Ilan,* he said silently, to a man who might already be dead, in a country that was certainly dying. *You always did have rotten timing.*

The baby fell asleep on his finger. Just like that. Mid-gaze, mid-grip, as if trust were a thing she could simply decide to do, the way other people decided where to eat lunch. August stayed crouched, afraid to move, watching the rise and fall of a chest smaller than his spread hand.

He looked at the note again. Then at the seal. Then, finally, at the seven lumps in his coat lining, each one a fate not his own, each one ticking like a clock wound by other people's ambitions.

Somewhere out in the rain, on the road he was supposed to take, the republic was waiting for its sleeve to pass things through.

August sat down properly on the floor, back against the hearthstone, and did the one thing his training forbade above all others.

He stayed.

* * *

He did not sleep, exactly. Couriers sleep the way cats do, in shallow armed slices, and August passed the night in a series of these, waking each time to check three things: the documents, the door, and the basket. The documents were fine. The door was shut. The baby, each time, had rotated into some new configuration of blanket and limb that defied both anatomy and the laws of fabric, and each time she was, impossibly, asleep.

Near dawn the rain thinned to a gray drizzle, and August made tea from the safehouse stores — a tin of leaves gone stale but serviceable — and conducted, over the steaming cup, the most dangerous conversation of his career.

"Here is the situation," he told her. She was awake now, propped against his rolled coat, watching him with the focused gravity of a magistrate. "I am expected in Tarrow Ford by tomorrow evening with seven documents. You are expected nowhere by anyone, which makes you, statistically speaking, the freest person in Vessany." He sipped the tea and grimaced. "I am expected to forget your face. That was the instruction. In writing. Sealed."

She kicked one leg free of the blanket and regarded it with surprise, as though it had acted without authorization.

"The difficulty," August continued, because talking to her was easier than thinking, and thinking had become a room he didn't want to enter, "is that I have an excellent memory. It's the entire basis of my employment. Faces, routes, names — I carry them all, and I've never once dropped one." He set down the cup. "So whoever wrote that note either doesn't know me anymore, or knows me perfectly. And I genuinely cannot decide which frightens me more."

Her answer was to hiccup. Twice. With the solemnity of a state funeral.

August laughed — actually laughed, a short rusty sound that startled him more than the gurgling had. He couldn't remember the last time. Somewhere in the capital, presumably, Sef was updating ledgers, and somewhere in this room, a courier's virtue was leaking out through a crack in the floorboards like the rain through the roof.

He gathered the note, the seal, and the stone, and weighed them in his palm. Evidence. Instructions. A message from a ghost with excellent penmanship. Procedure said burn them unread and report nothing. Procedure also said leave the child, and the child was currently attempting to eat her own fist with the determination of a starving scholar, and procedure, August was beginning to suspect, had never met anyone like her.

Outside, the drizzle thickened again. The road to Tarrow Ford ran east through country that was burning farm by farm, and every hour it burned, the seven documents in his coat grew heavier — not in grams, but in the way that matters. He had carried many heavy things. He had never once carried anything that carried him back.

The baby sneezed again, tremendously, and looked betrayed by the experience.

"All right," August said, to her, to the note, to the ghost with the leaning letters, to whatever was left of the man he'd been before this room. "All right. But I want it noted that this is your fault."

He took off his coat, unfolded the seam along the lining where seven documents slept in their oiled paper, and counted them by touch, one through seven, the way he had every night for a week.

Seven documents. One child. One promise he'd made in a garden ten years ago, drunk on cheap wine and peace, laughing at how seriously Ilan had taken it — *whoever survives, guards what matters* — and one long, rain-soaked night in which to decide which weight he could afford to carry.

August looked at the baby, and the baby looked back, and somewhere east of Corren Hollow the road didn't wait.

Neither, it turned out, did the dawn.
