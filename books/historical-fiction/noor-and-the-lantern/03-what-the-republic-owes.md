## 3. What the Republic Owes

The note in his hand was still damp at the corners where the rain had reached it through the gap under the door. August had read it four times now, standing in the safehouse with the infant's breath fogging faintly against his collar, and each time his eyes went to the same place: not the words, which were few and terrible enough, but the wax seal pressed into the bottom corner, cracked down the middle by a careless thumb.

He knew that seal. He had watched it being made once, years ago, by a man who thought sealing wax was a small vanity worth indulging because a republic ought to look like it believed in itself.

Forget this face, the note said.

August sat down slowly on a crate that groaned under him, and the past came up out of the floorboards like water into a cellar, the way it always did when he was too tired to hold the door shut against it.

* * *

Ten years ago, in the last spring anyone would have called peaceful, Ilan Voss kept a garden the way other men kept mistresses — secretly, guiltily, and with an expenditure of feeling that embarrassed them both.

It was behind the ministry annex, a strip of ground maybe twelve paces long, wedged between a brick wall and the loading dock where clerks smoked cigarettes they weren't supposed to smoke. Voss had bullied the groundskeeper into letting him have it, then bullied the groundskeeper again into pretending he hadn't. There were tomatoes involved. This was essential to the story, August would think later; there were always tomatoes.

"You're stepping on the basil," Voss said, without looking up.

August looked down. His heel was nowhere near the basil. "I am standing on gravel."

"You're standing near the basil with intent." Voss straightened, wiped his hands on his trousers — leaving a green smear he'd complain about for a week — and held out a tomato like a man presenting evidence in court. It was lopsided and split along one shoulder, ugly as a fist, and heavy in a way that seemed almost insolent.

"That's the ugliest vegetable I've ever been threatened with," August said, taking it anyway.

"It's the best thing you'll eat all year, and you know it. The market ones are water and lies." Voss sat back on his heels, squinting up at him against the late sun. He was thirty-one then and already going silver at the temples, which he claimed was hereditary and everyone else claimed was the ministry. "Sit down before you knock something over. You hover when you're happy. It's unsettling."

"I don't hover."

"You're doing it right now." He tugged August's sleeve until he folded down onto the low wall beside the bean row. The bricks were still holding the day's warmth. Somewhere beyond the wall a tram bell rang its patient evening bell, and a clerk on the loading dock laughed too loudly at something, and the whole city made that sound it made at the end of a working day — a long exhale of a hundred thousand people deciding, for one more evening, that everything was probably fine.

They drank wine from a bottle Voss had hidden inside a hollowed-out dictionary, which was the sort of thing he found hilarious and which August had told him, repeatedly, was how spies got caught. *Nobody suspects the dictionary,* Voss said. *Everyone suspects the dictionary,* August said. They had this argument the way other men played cards: often, affectionately, and with no intention of ever resolving it.

The bottle was half gone when Voss went quiet in the particular way he went quiet — not thoughtful quiet but braced quiet, a man hearing weather far off over hills.

"Do you ever think about what's coming?" he said.

August turned the tomato over in his hands. "You've been reading the dispatches again."

"I write the dispatches."

"I know." That was the trouble with Ilan Voss. Most men could be lied to by their own government. Voss composed the lies, and so carried around in his head a private, unspeakable map of the country's real condition — the grain shortfalls papered over, the border incidents invented or ignored depending on the week, the factions in the Assembly sharpening themselves against each other with the slow diligence of men whittling knives. He saw all of it and said almost none of it aloud, except in gardens, after wine, to people whose silence he trusted more than most men's oaths.

"So," August said. "What's coming?"

Voss pulled up a weed with great concentration, examined its roots as though they might contain the answer, and tossed it aside. "A storm," he said finally. "Not next year, maybe not the year after. But I can feel it the way you feel rain in your knee. Something is coming through this country, and it won't care who was clever or who was decent or who grew good tomatoes. It'll just come through." He drank. The wine had gone warm and tasted of dust and cherries. "And I'll be on the wrong side of it. Whatever side I stand on, I'll end up on the wrong side. That's what my job is now. Being early to the wrongness."

"You could leave. There's time. We both could."

"Leave to where?" Voss laughed, but there was no humor in it, only exhaustion wearing humor's coat. "Every road out of here leads somewhere that will also burn, just slightly later, with better wine." He looked at August then, really looked, with the full unguarded weight of himself, which he spent on perhaps two people alive. "You remember my mother's house? Up in the Vale country. The orchard behind it."

"The one with the crooked apple trees. You made me climb one and I fell and you laughed for a year."

"You fell with tremendous dignity. Like a felled oak." He smiled, briefly, and then the smile settled into something else. "If the storm comes, and if the house survives, someone should hang a lantern in that orchard. Old signal. My grandfather used it during the last war. Lantern in the tree means *safe, come in.* No lantern means keep walking, don't stop, don't trust anything." He turned the bottle slowly between his palms. "I'm saying — if it comes to it, if the world goes sideways — whoever's left standing should guard what matters. Not the ministries. Not the flags. What actually matters. People. Names. Faces." He paused. "The things a man would hang a lantern for."

August felt the wine and the warm bricks and the evening bell all at once, and heard himself say, with the loose gravity of a man three glasses past caution:

"All right. Whoever survives guards what matters. That's the promise."

"Don't make promises when you're drunk. That's how men end up owning boats."

"I mean it." And he discovered, saying it, that he did. "Whoever survives guards what matters. Even if surviving means nothing else afterward. Even if it costs—" he gestured vaguely with the ugly tomato, "—everything else. The route, the name, the pardon, all of it. What matters first."

Voss was silent a moment. Then he took off the plain signet ring he wore — the Voss seal, a lantern worked into the crest by some ancestor with a sense of humor about the family trade — and pressed it into the dirt of the bean row, twisted it, lifted it, and stamped the wax-seal shape into the soil like a child playing at documents.

"There," he said solemnly. "Witnessed and sealed. By the authority of the Republic of Vessany, population two drunk idiots and one garden." He laughed then, really laughed, and clapped August on the shoulder hard enough to spill wine, and for a moment the storm he'd spoken of was unimaginable — absurd, a story old men told. The sun came down gold through the tomato vines. The tram bell rang again. Everything was probably fine.

They shook on it, laughing, hands sticky with tomato juice and earth.

That was the last spring anyone would call peaceful.

* * *

The war, when it came, was courteous to neither of them. It took Voss's career first — chewed him through three commands and spat him out the far side wearing the word *traitor*, though August had never learned what exactly he'd betrayed, or to whom, or whether the charge was even true. The republic declared him enemy of the state in a proclamation written in the same bureaucratic hand that had once paid for his tomatoes' fertilizer, and August, who by then was a courier, carried the proclamation himself. He remembered the weight of the paper. Four sheets. Almost nothing. It had taken a decade to reduce a friendship to a weight a courier could carry without noticing.

After that, silence. Ten years of professional forgetting, which was the trade he'd chosen and the trade he was good at. A courier's virtue was weightlessness. You carried, you delivered, you forgot. You did not keep faces. You did not keep promises made in gardens by men who sealed oaths in dirt with a stolen ring.

Except that August had never quite managed the last part. He had tried. For ten years, in safehouses and border towns and the backs of mail wagons, he had caught himself doing it: checking, whenever a village burned, whether any orchards survived. Whenever he passed through the Vale country, riding routes that didn't require it, he looked at the hills above Voss's mother's house and noted, professionally, uselessly, whether a light showed among the crooked apple trees. It never did. He told himself this meant nothing. A courier notices things; noticing is not the same as remembering.

But the promise had stayed with him the way a splinter stays — not painful, exactly, just present, a small foreign thing lodged where the body couldn't reach it. Whoever survives guards what matters.

In the safehouse, the rain ticked against the shutters. On the doorstep behind him, wrapped in a blanket that had been fine wool once, the infant slept with the total unselfconsciousness of someone who has never yet carried anything.

August turned the note over. Beneath the seal, beneath the instruction to forget, there was a second line he hadn't seen at first — small letters, hurried, pressed deep into the paper by a pen driven too hard, the writing of a man who knew his ink might be the last thing he got to say plainly:

*You remember the orchard.*

August closed his eyes. Outside, somewhere down the street, boots were coming — several pairs, unhurried, the walk of men who owned the hour.

He looked down at the child who was supposed to be a face he'd never known.

"Well," he said quietly, to no one, to a garden ten years gone. "You absolute fool, Ilan."

The boots stopped outside the door.
