## 5. Seven Documents, One Child

The candle had burned down to a stub the width of August's thumb, which meant he had perhaps an hour of light and no honest reason to want more. He laid the seven documents out on the safehouse table in a neat row, the way he'd been taught to lay them out sixteen years ago in a basement classroom that smelled of lamp oil and fear.

*Weightlessness*, Handler Brech had said then, rapping his knuckles on a courier's empty satchel. *A man carrying paper is a man who can be caught. A man carrying nothing is smoke.*

August looked at the row of seals and wax and ribbon, at seven documents that together weighed less than the infant sleeping in the drawer he had lined with his spare shirt, and understood that Brech's lesson had a flaw in it, namely everything.

Noor — and he was still calling her *the child* in his head, mostly, though the word *Noor* kept slipping in like water under a door — Noor slept with one fist curled against her cheek and the other flung above her head as though surrendering to a dream she intended to win anyway. She had eaten most of a tin of condensed milk cut with river water. She had not cried since midnight. She had, at some point during the small hours, gripped his finger with a strength entirely out of proportion to her size and refused, through three attempts, to let go.

He should have left already. The route said leave before dawn, take the north road through Ashfield, cross at the mill bridge, deliver. Seven stops. Three war zones. One republic, dying, that wanted these papers delivered more than it wanted him alive, though it would never say so aloud.

He picked up the first document.

It was sealed with the chancellery's black wax, and it was addressed to the garrison commander at Vellin Ford, and August did not need to open it to know what it was. He had carried enough of them. Orders like this one traveled only in one direction, and they always ended the same way: a town on a list, a commander with a pen, a line of names read out at dawn. The purge lists had started in the capital as rumors, then as whispers, then as the particular silence that fell over certain streets where certain houses stood empty with their doors standing open.

He held it over the candle.

Here was the thing nobody told you about treason: it took forever. The wax didn't catch. The paper curled and went brown and smoked sullenly like a sulking child — like Noor, he thought, when the milk was too cold — and he had to feed it to the flame corner-first, patient as a man lighting a pipe in wet weather. The fire took the seal last, the black wax going soft and shining, and for a moment General Ilan Voss's imagined face seemed to swim up out of the melting crest before it collapsed into nothing.

"Sorry," August said quietly. Not to the chancellor. To whoever's name was inside.

The second document burned faster, because by then he had learned to tear the papers into strips first, and strips catch like they're grateful for it. The third he almost couldn't do, because the seal was the old republican blue, the seal from ten years ago, from peacetime, from gardens, and burning it felt less like treason than like attending a funeral alone. He burned it anyway. Blue wax dripped onto the table and hardened into a little blob shaped, if you squinted, like a bird. He decided not to squint.

* * *

Somewhere around the fourth document, Noor woke up.

She didn't cry. She made a sound like a small hinge considering whether to complain, and then she watched him with those grave dark eyes that seemed, absurdly, to be taking notes. August froze with the fourth document halfway to the flame, feeling exactly like a boy caught stealing jam.

"This isn't what it looks like," he told her.

She blinked.

"It's exactly what it looks like," he amended. "But I have reasons. Good ones. Well." He tore a strip off the corner of the document and fed it to the candle. "One good one. She's about your size."

Noor considered this, appeared to find it acceptable, and began working on her own fist with the focused determination of someone who had discovered that fists were edible.

The fifth document was the hard one. Not because of its contents — he didn't open it, he'd promised himself he wouldn't open any of them, because a courier who reads his cargo starts thinking, and a courier who thinks starts hesitating, and hesitation gets people shot against walls. It was hard because it was the thickest, the most official, wrapped in oilcloth against the rain, and some stubborn professional part of him — the part Brech had built brick by brick over sixteen years — kept whispering that this one mattered, this one was worth something, this one could buy passage, buy silence, buy a pardon when all this came crashing down.

And tucked into its folds — he found it while retying the cord — was a slip of paper he had missed before. A list. Nine names in a clerk's careful hand. Courier personnel, assets pending reassessment.

His name was sixth from the top. *Ashgrove, A. — see attached disposition.*

There was no attached disposition. There never was, for men like him. *Disposition* meant whatever the word needed to mean when the war got hungry enough.

August sat very still for a moment, listening to the rain on the roof and to Noor gumming her fist, and felt something in his chest go very quiet and very clear, the way a room goes clear after a window breaks. They were going to erase him. Not kill him — killing implied you'd been somebody. Erase. Strike the name, burn the file, forget the face.

Well. Two could play at that.

"You know what the funny thing is?" he asked Noor, holding up the list so she could admire it. "I'm actually quite good at my job."

She sneezed, which he chose to interpret as agreement.

He fed the list to the candle first, name-side down, watching his own name curl and blacken and lift away on the heat as ash, and there was a satisfaction in it so pure and so childish that he laughed — actually laughed, a short startled sound in the empty room, his first laugh in weeks. Then the fifth document followed it, and the sixth, and the fire got greedy and he had to open the shutters a crack to let the smoke out, cursing under his breath, a traitor worried about the carpet.

* * *

The seventh document he kept.

It was the smallest. A single folded sheet under the plain grey seal he had recognized on the doorstep two nights ago, in the rain, with the child screaming and the note in a hand he'd known across a garden and across a decade: *Forget this face.* Ilan Voss's seal. Ilan Voss's handwriting. And underneath the instruction, one line more, in cipher so old that only two people alive could still read it — the two boys who'd invented it to pass notes during a diplomatic banquet, because even at twenty-two they had both known that every official room was a trap.

August had decoded it by candlelight on the second night, and he had read it eleven times since, and each time it landed in the same place in his chest, like a stone dropped down a well:

*Orchard of the burned farm past Kettering Mill. Lantern on the eastern post means the road is true. If the lantern hangs, bring what you carry and don't stop for anything smaller than God. — I.V.*

Bring what you carry.

Ilan had known. Had known about the child, had known who might find her, had known that of all the couriers in a republic full of disappearing men, the one who'd come down that muddy lane might be the one who remembered a drunk promise made in peacetime under a plum tree: *whoever survives this storm guards what matters.* August had laughed at him then. Ten years later, here he sat in a stolen hour before dawn, holding the proof that Ilan hadn't been drunk at all. Ilan had been the only sober man in the garden.

He refolded the sheet along its worn creases and slid it inside his shirt, against his skin, where it lay flat and warm as a second heartbeat.

Then he turned to the packing.

* * *

The courier's satchel had been designed by someone who hated children, or possibly by someone who had never imagined one, which amounted to the same thing. It was waterproofed canvas, buckled twice, divided into compartments sized for dispatch tubes and ration tins and the small flat tools of a trade that officially did not exist. August emptied it completely — every compartment, every hidden seam, shaking out lint and a fossilized biscuit and one brass button he had been meaning to sew on for four years — and then he unpacked his spare shirt, the good wool one, and lined the largest compartment with it, folding and folding until the wool stood up in soft walls.

Noor watched this engineering project from the tabletop with the expression of a magistrate reviewing plans for a gallows.

"It's better than it looks," August said.

She continued to doubt him.

He tested it first with the bread loaf, then with his boots, and finally — heart hammering, hands unsteady in a way they had not been at any checkpoint or border or burning building in sixteen years — he lowered her in. She fit. Barely. Her head rested against the rolled cuff of the sleeve he'd placed as a pillow; her knees bent naturally against the wool; the flap closed above her with two inches of air between canvas and chin, and when he unbuckled it again she looked up at him, blinked once, and produced a fart of tremendous authority.

"That," August said, "is the most reassuring review I've ever received."

He packed the rest quickly now, because the sky beyond the shutters had gone from black to the bruised grey that meant dawn was coming to take attendance. Rations. The milk tin. A spoon. His knife. The flint. Six documents' worth of ash he swept into the stove and buried under cinders. He put on his coat, then stopped, took it off, and cut the collar badge free with his knife — the small silver thread-work of the courier service, the mark of a man whose virtue was weightlessness — and dropped it into the stove with the ash. His fingers wanted to hesitate. He didn't let them.

When he lifted Noor's satchel onto his chest, buckling it snug, her weight settled against his sternum like a hand pressed flat over his heart, and her breath fogged the little gap at the flap in slow, even pulses.

He opened the door onto rain-washed streets and a sky the color of pewter, and stood a moment in the threshold of the safehouse, feeling for the terror that was supposed to be there.

It was there. Don't mistake him. It was there the way cold is there in winter — ambient, total, informing everything. Every step from this doorway forward was a step outside the architecture of his entire life. There was no route now. There was no handler expecting confirmation, no dead-drop schedule, no cover story rehearsed until it wore smoother than the truth. There was only a burned farm past Kettering Mill and a lantern that might or might not be hanging and a promise made by two young idiots under a plum tree while the world was still pretending to be kind.

And yet.

And yet beneath the terror, down where he kept the things he never said aloud, there was a lightness so strange and so complete that it frightened him worse than the soldiers did. Because he had spent sixteen years being a man made of errands, a vessel, smoke — and smoke, he understood now, standing in the doorway with a baby strapped to his chest and a dead man's name in his pocket, smoke is just something that used to be on fire, drifting until it disperses.

Whereas this — the wool-lined satchel, the milk tin, the treason — this had weight. Glorious, damning, human weight. He was carrying something that mattered, and the something was breathing.

"Right," he told Noor, and stepped out, and pulled the door shut behind him without locking it, because he was done keeping things safe that weren't worth keeping. "North road. Ashfield. Try not to scream at the checkpoints."

From within the satchel came a small, thoughtful sound, neither agreement nor refusal — the sound, August would think much later, of a person reserving judgment on a companion she had not chosen but intended, all the same, to keep.

He turned north, into the wind, and the dawn came up grey behind him on the life he had abandoned, and ahead the road ran straight through the smoking fields toward everything he had ever promised anyone.

He walked.
