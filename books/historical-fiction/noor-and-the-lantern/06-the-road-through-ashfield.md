## 6. The Road Through Ashfield

The coat was the first thing to go, though he didn't know it yet.

August walked out of Ashfield proper at first light, when the smoke from last week's burning still hung in the valleys like weather that had forgotten how to leave. The farmland stretched north in black rows—wheat fields burned down to their skeletons, orchards reduced to charcoal fingers pointing accusingly at a sky that had done nothing to deserve them. Here and there a chimney stood alone in a field, marking where a house used to be the way a headstone marks where a person used to be.

Noor rode against his chest in the courier's satchel, her small weight warm through the wool lining, and she had discovered somewhere in the night a sound that was not quite crying and not quite contentment—a kind of rhythmic huffing, like a very small person doing very important work.

"I hear you," August said quietly. "I hear you. We're negotiating."

The truth of his situation, which he had been carefully not examining since dawn, was this: a man walking alone through burned country could be anyone. A deserter, a smuggler, a refugee, a fool. But a man carrying an infant through occupied territory was a story, and every story invited questions, and questions were how couriers died. His training said: be no one. His training had not accounted for seven pounds of baby who hiccupped like a tiny bellows and needed feeding on a schedule known only to herself.

His papers identified him as Tomas Rehn, a grain factor traveling to his mother's convalescence in Vellmark. The grain factor had been invented by the Republic's document office with great care and zero imagination; August had always felt mildly insulted by him. Grain factors did not walk. Grain factors did not smell of woodsmoke and wet wool and, increasingly, of sour milk.

He needed milk. This thought arrived with the force of military intelligence. He needed milk within hours or Noor would make his invisibility a matter of public record, loudly, at whatever moment least suited them both.

* * *

He smelled the farm before he saw it—a thread of woodsmoke too domestic to be soldiers', and beneath it, impossibly, the green sweetness of cows. The farm sat in a shallow fold of land the fires seemed to have leapt over, as if the flames had paused, considered the place, and moved on for reasons of their own. A low stone house with a sagging porch. A barn missing half its roof. And in the dooryard, a woman in a man's coat splitting firewood with the unhurried violence of someone who had decided long ago exactly how much effort the world deserved from her.

She saw him coming from two hundred paces. She did not stop splitting wood.

August stopped at the fence line, hands visible, which was the universal grammar of the road these days. "Morning," he called.

"Is it," the woman said. She set another log on the block.

"I'm looking for milk. For—" and here his mouth, trained for years to lie fluently about documents and routes and the whereabouts of men who mattered, produced the sentence he had been rehearsing without knowing it since the safehouse. "For my daughter."

The axe paused. The woman looked up. She had a broad, wind-scoured face and eyes the color of a winter stream—gray going on iron—and they went straight past him to the satchel, where Noor chose that precise moment to produce a sound like a small offended goose.

"Well," the woman said. "That's a daughter."

"She was born in autumn," August said, because the lie required furniture, and furniture was easier once you started building it. "Her mother—" He let the sentence die in the way that dying sentences do, and the woman's face did something complicated and then settled into pity, which was worse than suspicion, because pity came with obligations.

"Bring her in," the woman said. "You look like a coat that's been worn by three different men, and none of them ate breakfast."

The farmhouse kitchen was warm in the particular way of kitchens where people are being careful with fuel—a small fire, everything arranged around it like courtiers around a nervous king. A cow lowed somewhere behind the barn with the patience of a professional. The woman, whose name was Bresch and who volunteered nothing else, warmed milk in a dented pan while August stood in the middle of her floor holding his daughter—who was not his daughter—with the rigid posture of a man defusing something.

"You can sit," Bresch said. "Babies don't break from chairs."

He sat. Noor, presented with the bottle Bresch assembled from parts that had clearly served three generations of farm children, seized it with the determination of a general taking a bridge, and the kitchen filled with the small urgent sounds of her drinking.

Bresch watched this with her arms folded. "How old?"

"Six weeks." A guess. It seemed like a six-weeks sort of face.

"Five weeks, maybe less. You're new at this."

"Is it that obvious?"

"You're holding her like a diplomatic pouch." Bresch turned back to the stove. "My husband held our first like that. Like it might go off." She said *our first* and *my husband* in the flat voice of a woman reading a casualty list, and August understood that the barn's missing roof and the man's coat were part of the same story, and that asking would be a cruelty dressed as conversation.

"He's not—"

"He's dead or conscripted, and those are the same thing now." She said it without heat, the way you'd report the price of flour. Then, nodding at Noor: "She yours? Truly?"

And here was the strange thing—the moment August had not prepared for, because no handler had ever warned him about it. Lying about grain shipments was nothing. Lying about a child, to a woman with iron eyes and a stove-warmed kitchen and a dead-or-conscripted husband, felt like forging scripture. His training offered him the line, smooth and ready: *Yes.* And some other part of him, some part he'd assumed had been amputated years ago along with everything else a courier couldn't afford to keep, hesitated.

"Yes," he said, and hated how it caught. "Truly."

Bresch looked at him for a long time. Whatever she saw—the hesitation, the catch, the whole architecture of the lie standing there sweating in her kitchen—she apparently filed under *none of my business*, which was the most valuable currency left in Vessany.

"Milk I have," she said. "Coat you're wearing is army issue, officer's cut, and any checkpoint between here and Vellmark will want to know why a grain factor wears a major's greatcoat. Trade me. There's my father's old driving coat on the peg, ugly as sin but honest-looking. And take the goat's milk too—it keeps better than cow's."

"Bresch—" He didn't know what he was going to say. Thank you. Why. What do you know.

"Don't thank me, it makes it a transaction and then we're both criminals." She took the greatcoat off its hook herself and held it out. "The sergeant at Harrow Cross has been stopping everyone since the bridge went. He likes paperwork. Give him paperwork."

"How do you—"

"Because I sold him eggs yesterday," Bresch said, "and watched him read a fishmonger's travel permit four times like it was a love letter. Go out the north lane and you'll hit the cross by noon. Walk slow. Men who hurry look guilty, and men who look guilty get remembered."

* * *

Harrow Cross was a farm intersection with pretensions: a chapel, a well, a barricade of plow blades welded to a wagon bed, and a checkpoint hut with a tin stovepipe breathing gray smoke into the cold. Two soldiers manned it—one young enough that his uniform seemed to be wearing him rather than the reverse, and one sergeant built like a door, with a mustache that preceded him into conversations and a face that suggested all surprise had been removed from it surgically, years ago.

August walked slow. He wore his dead father-in-the-lie's driving coat, brown and shapeless and smelling faintly of horse, and he carried Noor against his chest where she slept with the boneless totality of infant sleep, and he recited his story the way other men recited prayers: *Tomas Rehn, grain factor, mother convalescing in Vellmark, daughter born in autumn, wife dead, walk slow.*

"Papers," said the young soldier, who looked like he'd rather be anywhere, including the war proper.

August handed over the folder. Inside: the grain factor's identity book, the travel permit stamped in three colors, the letter of introduction from a merchant house that had probably burned down by now. Counterfeit work of genuine artistry. He had made worse himself.

The young soldier glanced at it, and might have waved him through, might have been already looking past him at the empty road—but Sergeant Mustache emerged from the hut wiping his hands, and said, in the tone of a man settling in for entertainment:

"Give it here."

And then the sergeant read.

August had been studied before. Handlers studied him to see if he'd crack. Border officers studied him to see if he'd run. He knew the taxonomy of scrutiny. But this sergeant read the way Bresch had described—slowly, twice, turning pages with thick careful fingers—and August understood that the danger wasn't discovery. The danger was duration. Every second the sergeant spent on page two was a second in which the young soldier had time to notice the baby, the milk smell, the way a grain factor's hands were far too clean for a grain factor.

Noor stirred. Shifted. Made the pre-cry sound, the small gathering thunder.

August did the only thing his training and his terror jointly proposed: he began, quietly, to bounce her. And as he bounced her, he spoke—not to the sergeant, not to the soldier, but to Noor, in a low running murmur that was half-soothing and entirely calculated.

"There now, there now, none of that, we'll be home by dark and Granny will fuss and you'll spit on her good shawl like you spat on mine—" It was nonsense. It was a performance of a life so mundane it ached: a grandmother, a shawl, a home by dark. He watched it land. The young soldier's shoulders came down. Even the sergeant's mustache relaxed a millimeter.

Because here was what August knew, in the marrow the Republic had trained into him: no one suspects a man mid-babble. Babble is the opposite of guilt. Guilt is silent; fathers are noisy.

"Harrow Cross," the sergeant said suddenly, still not looking up. "What's the cross for?"

August didn't miss a beat. Didn't even look. "There's a crossroads and a chapel, so they named it after the chapel. My grandfather said the original marker was a gallows, but the chapel folk won out." A shrug traveled through his voice. "Towns do that. Rename the hanging."

A silence. The sergeant looked up for the first time, and for one suspended moment his eyes were not bureaucratic but sharp as awls, and August felt the whole fiction stand on one leg above a very long drop—

—and then the sergeant grunted. Almost smiled. "Grandfather talk too much, did he?"

"All grandfathers talk too much. It's the job."

"Sergeant's own grandfather claimed he fought at Marrow Field." The sergeant handed the papers back at last, folding them closed with something dangerously close to care. "Fought at Marrow Field, drank with the chancellor, wrestled a bear. Dead forty years and still lying."

"They keep us honest," August said, "from underground."

"Move along, then." The sergeant was already turning away. "Mind the north fork—bridge at Callow's Mill is out. You'll wade the ford, and mind you keep the little one dry."

*Little one.* The sergeant had seen her after all. Had seen her and classified her, filed her under *grain factor's granddaughter, none of my business*, the same filing system Bresch used, the same filing system that kept the burned country limping along beneath the official version of itself.

"Thank you, Sergeant."

"Don't thank me," the sergeant said, without turning around. "Makes it a transaction."

August walked through the checkpoint, past the young soldier who was already yawning, out onto the north road with the wind at his back and his heart conducting a full percussion section against his ribs. Only when the cross was a smudge behind him did he permit himself to breathe properly, and the breath came out shaking, and Noor woke at the sound of it and regarded him with wide dark eyes full of ancient judgment.

"We're fine," he told her. "We're completely fine. Your father"—the word slipped out before he could catch it, and he stood there on the empty road, appalled at himself—"your *father* is a very good liar."

Behind him, faintly, he heard the sergeant call something to the young soldier—too far to catch the words. Something about papers. Something about remembering.

August walked faster anyway, and told himself it was just the cold.

By late afternoon the north fork split, as promised, toward the drowned ford at Callow's Mill—and there, drawn up beside the road with the ease of a person who has never once hurried, stood a cart, a mule, and a lean woman in a patched riding cloak who watched him approach with the frank appraisal of someone counting doors.

"You're the third man this week," she said, "walking north with a story that doesn't fit his coat. Get in. We'll talk about what you're carrying."
