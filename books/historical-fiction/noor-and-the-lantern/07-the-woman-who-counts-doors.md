## 7. The Woman Who Counts Doors

Marta Vale had a habit that the soldiers of two armies had never managed to break her of: she counted doors.

It was not superstition, whatever her brother used to say, and it was not nerves. It was arithmetic. A town was a sum, and every door was a number in it. The green door on Tanner's Row with the loose hinge — three seconds to force, occupied by a widow who slept with the window open. The blue door behind the chandlery that opened onto an alley instead of a street — useful for exits, useless for entrances, because the chandler's dog hated everyone equally and without discretion. The red door of the customs house, which had been kicked in by republican provosts in October and never repaired, so that it now hung at an angle like a drunk leaning on a friend, and anyone entering had to duck, which meant anyone entering could be heard ducking.

Forty-one doors in Sablemere worth knowing. Marta knew all forty-one, and she knew which ones lied about what was behind them.

She was counting them again this morning out of pure boredom, walking the market square with a basket of turnips she had no intention of eating, when the stranger came down from the north road with a baby strapped to his chest and guilt written across his face in letters large enough to read from across the square.

Oh, thought Marta. *There* you are.

She had been expecting him for eleven days, which is to say she had been expecting *someone*, because the roads north of Ashfield had gone quiet in the way roads go quiet just before something comes down them. And here he was: tall, mud to the knee, a courier's walk worn down to a trudge, carrying his satchel against his body the way a man carries a thing he has decided to die for. And the baby. The baby was new information. Babies were not standard cargo. In eleven years of moving other people's secrets through occupied territory, Marta had smuggled documents, diamonds, one very indignant archbishop, and twice a live falcon, but never a baby.

The stranger stopped at the well, worked the pump handle with the patience of a man who had learned that everything takes longer than it should, and filled a tin cup. Then — and this was the detail that made Marta's eyebrows rise above the rim of her basket — he held the cup up to the baby first. Let the child drink before he did.

Couriers didn't do that. Couriers drank first, ate last, slept standing up, and trusted nothing wetter than ink. She knew, because she had spent two years moving Ilan Voss's messages during the war's first winter, back when Voss was still a name the republic printed in gold instead of ash, and she had known his couriers the way a fisherman knows gulls: by their appetites and their silences.

This one had broken pattern. Which meant either he was not a courier anymore, or he was about to stop being one.

Both options interested her.

* * *

She let him come to her, because they always came to her eventually, but she made it easy. She took the corner table at the Salt & Anchor where the light was bad and the landlord was deaf in one ear and merciful in the other, and she ordered tea for two without being asked, because the second cup arriving unbidden does more to loosen a tongue than any amount of direct questioning.

He came in twenty minutes later, damp, hollow-eyed, the baby awake now and regarding the room with the grave, appraising stare of a small tax collector.

"Sit," Marta said. "Before you fall down. Falling down draws attention."

He sat. Up close he was worse than he'd looked in the square — cheekbones like a debt notice, hands cracked from cold, a healing rope-burn across one palm that told its own story. But his eyes moved the way a courier's eyes move: corners first, windows second, faces last.

"You're Marta Vale," he said.

"I'm a woman drinking tea," Marta said. "You're a man who needs me to be Marta Vale, so let's agree I am, and move on to the interesting part." She nodded at the baby. "Whose is it?"

"That's not your business."

"Everything in this town is my business. That's not vanity, it's bookkeeping." She poured his tea. "But fine. Keep your little mysteries. Here's mine: you came down the north road, which means you crossed the Ashfield flats, which means you passed through Corven's checkpoint, which means a sergeant with a head cold studied your papers for a long time and let you go anyway, and you've been asking yourself ever since whether he let you go or marked you. Am I warm?"

The silence went on long enough that the baby reached over and patted the table, as if applauding.

"Warm," he admitted.

"Mm. And somewhere between there and here you decided you couldn't take the river ferry because ferries keep lists, and you couldn't take the coach road because coaches have drivers, and drivers have mouths, and so you walked into Sablemere hoping to find a boat that doesn't officially exist." She sipped her tea. "I own the boat that doesn't exist."

His eyes came off the window and settled on her properly for the first time. "How much?"

"There it is," Marta said warmly. "The courier question. Not *who are you*, not *why the child*, not even *please* — how much. You people are all the same. Ilan Voss himself once opened a conversation with me by sliding a purse across a table before I'd said a word."

Something flickered across the man's face at the name — quick, involuntary, like a door blowing open on a lit room before slamming shut. Marta noted it and filed it where she filed everything.

"So you do know him," she said.

"I know *of* him."

"You flinched at him."

"The wind," he said.

"Indoors," she agreed. "Very drafty, the Salt & Anchor." She leaned back. "Here's how this works. Passage to the upper river, three days, food included, conversation optional. I'll get you past the toll chain at Weir's Bend, which the republic guards, and the mill rapids, which the loyalists guard, and the stretch of marsh where nobody guards anything because the fever got the village and the village got the water. Price to be named."

"Not now?"

"I don't know yet what it costs me," Marta said. "That's the honest answer, and honesty is expensive, so appreciate it. When I know what it costs me, I'll tell you what it costs you. If you don't like the price then, you can swim."

"That's not—"

"It's entirely reasonable," Marta said. "Every smuggler who names a price up front is a smuggler who's already decided to cheat you. I prefer to be surprised by my own greed. It keeps life interesting."

The baby chose that moment to sneeze, magnificently, spraying a fine mist of milk across the table, and then to laugh — a sudden, startling sound like coins dropped in an empty church — as though the sneeze had been a deliberate performance and she was pleased with the reception.

Marta looked at the child. The child looked back at her with eyes too dark and too old for her face, and something turned over in Marta's chest that she recognized with irritation as *feeling*.

"She laughs loud," Marta said.

"She laughs loud," the man agreed, and there it was again — that crack in the courier mask, warmth leaking through like lamplight under a door. He put his hand over the child's ear, absently, protectively, the way a man shields a flame.

And on the inside of his wrist, where his sleeve had ridden up, Marta saw it: a smudge of black wax, the ghost of a seal pressed and broken. She caught only the impression — a circle, a tower, a line beneath — but she had spent a winter handling exactly that seal, pressing it herself into cooling wax on Voss's behalf, and some shapes a hand never forgets.

She kept her face pleasant and her voice bored. "What's in the satchel?"

"Clothes."

"Marta," she said, gesturing at herself with both hands, "Vale. Remember? Everything is my business. What's in the satchel?"

"Wool. For the baby. She cries without it."

"And under the wool?"

The man looked at her for a long moment. Outside, a republican patrol clattered past the window — six horses, one officer, the usual theater — and neither of them glanced toward it, which was its own kind of professional courtesy.

"A paper," he said finally. "One paper."

"Sealed?"

"Was."

"With a tower seal."

He went very still. Stillness, Marta thought, was its own confession; guilty men fidgeted, but men guarding something precious went stone.

"Where did you get it?" she asked, quieter now.

"That's my business."

"Yes," she said. "I imagine it is. And it's the business of half the officers in this province, whether they know it or not. That seal hasn't been seen since the autumn purges. There are men who would burn a town to find paper with that mark on it, and I include the town we're sitting in."

"Then I'll leave tonight."

"You'll leave when the tide leaves, which is dawn, and not before, because the channel mouth is mined and I'm the only person alive who knows the safe line, and I'm not telling it to a dead man walking." She stood, gathered her basket, and dropped a handful of coppers on the table — enough for the tea, the second cup, and the landlord's mercy. "Room at the back of the chandlery. Blue door. The dog will bark; tell him Bess sent you, he's sentimental about that name. Don't open the door after dark for anyone, including me. Especially me."

"Why especially you?"

"Because if someone pays me enough between now and dawn," Marta said, "I'd like you to have the satisfaction of resisting me."

* * *

The chandlery's back room smelled of tar and tallow, and the bed was short, and none of it mattered, because by midnight Marta was awake anyway, sitting in her own kitchen with a candle stub and the ledger she kept in her head rather than on paper, doing sums.

She counted doors when she was thinking. There were no doors in her kitchen worth counting — hers was the ninth door on Cutpurse Lane and the least interesting on the street — so she counted other things. Eleven days of quiet roads. One courier off-pattern. One child with a laugh like money falling. One broken seal she'd have sworn on her mother's grave belonged to Ilan Voss, and her mother's grave was the only grave Marta had ever sworn on, which made it binding.

And underneath it all, the sum she'd been avoiding: Tamir. Her brother. Four hundred miles east in the prison camp at Kesselmark, alive as of the last letter in September, which was the last letter, full stop. The republic wanted eight hundred crowns in "processing fees" to transfer him to a labor battalion where the mortality was merely terrible instead of total. Marta had four hundred and sixty, hidden in three places, and a smuggling route whose value dropped every week the war chewed closer to the coast.

She did not yet know what the paper was worth. But she knew what paper like that could be worth, and the arithmetic sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She hated herself a little for doing the sums. She did them anyway. Survival, her father used to say, was just love with worse manners.

* * *

Dawn came gray and smelling of salt. Marta met him at the weir steps with the boat — a flat-bottomed barge called the *Patient Wife*, because, as Marta explained, "she waits, she endures, and she has never once been appreciated" — and the courier came down the steps with the baby bundled against his chest and the wariness of a man boarding a vessel owned by a woman who had admitted, cheerfully, to considering betrayal.

"Bess says hello," he offered.

"Bess is a liar and a coward and I love him," Marta said. "In. Sit amidships. Touch nothing. If the child cries going past the toll chain, feed her, don't hush her — hushing sounds like hiding, crying sounds like a fishing family, which is what we are today."

"We are?"

"You're my cousin Aldric, a widower, dull as ditchwater, God rest your wife. I'm your cousin's unfortunate relation who helps out. Practice looking dull."

"I've been told I manage it naturally."

"Good. First honest thing you've said." She pushed off from the steps, and the *Patient Wife* slid out onto the river with the low, contented groan of a hull that had done this a thousand times and intended to complain about every single one.

The fog lay on the water like a secret. Marta steered by feel and by the counting she did without meaning to — bridge pilings, moored skiffs, the doors of boathouses along the bank, each one filed away. Behind her, the man hummed to the baby, tunelessly, and the baby gripped his finger with her whole fist as though signing a contract.

At the bend below Weir's Mill, where the toll chain hung dripping and two bored soldiers warmed their hands at a brazier, Marta hailed them by name, complained about the price of coal, collected the family rate with a discount she extracted as a matter of principle, and punted through. Only when the mill was a mile astern did she allow herself to breathe fully.

"You've done this before," the courier said.

"I've done everything before. That's the tragedy of middle age." She looked back at him. The fog was lifting, and the child had fallen asleep against his chest, one cheek flattened, utterly boneless with trust. "Last chance to ask cheap questions, cousin Aldric. Once we're past the marsh, my price gets named, and after that, everything costs double."

He was quiet for a while. The river talked to itself among the reeds.

"What's the price likely to be?" he asked.

Marta watched the water, and the water gave her nothing, as usual.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But I know it's going to hurt one of us."

Ahead, the marsh opened its gray arms, and the *Patient Wife* went in.
