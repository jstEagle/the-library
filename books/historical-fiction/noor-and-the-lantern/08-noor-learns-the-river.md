## 8. Noor Learns the River

The barge was called *Patient Margit*, which August decided was either a joke or a prayer, and after three days aboard he suspected it was both.

She was a flat-bottomed grain hauler gone soft with age, her hold half-full of beet sugar and her deck piled with crates of pickled herring that perfumed everything within ten meters. Her captain was a broad, silent woman named Duska who chewed the same wad of tobacco from dawn to dusk and communicated chiefly through gestures and one raised eyebrow that could express disapproval, curiosity, contempt, or — rarely, and only when the light hit it right — something almost like kindness.

Duska asked no questions. This was why Marta had chosen her, Marta said, and also because Duska owed her, though when August pressed on what exactly Duska owed her for, Marta only smiled and said, "A door I closed behind her once," and refused to elaborate.

So they went upriver on the Patient Margit, slow as syrup, past drowned willows and the skeletons of ferry landings, past villages where the church towers had been knocked down like teeth and the fields stood in stubble nobody had come to cut. The war was somewhere behind them now, and somewhere ahead of them, but on the river it existed only as smoke on the far horizons, thin gray smudges like the fingerprints of a careless god.

August learned quickly that a barge is the closest thing to a monastery a fugitive can buy.

There was nothing to do. That was the whole of it. There was wood to split for the stove, and there were herring barrels to shift when Duska jerked her chin at them, and beyond that there was the river, the banks unspooling at the pace of a man walking very slowly while thinking hard about whether to bother.

He had not been idle in eleven years. He had been a courier; idleness was not in him. A courier's mind was supposed to be like his papers: sealed, weightless, moving without friction from hand to hand. In the capital he had slept four hours a night and considered five decadent. Now he sat on a coil of rope in the autumn sun with an infant asleep against his chest, and the terrible luxury of it frightened him more than the checkpoints had.

Because tenderness, he knew, was a liability. Every courier learned it in the first year. You did not learn faces. You did not learn names. You learned routes, seals, countersigns, the smell of wax that had been tampered with, the particular way a man's shoulders sat when he was about to reach for a weapon instead of a purse. The Republic of Vessany had built its courier corps on a simple arithmetic: a man carrying nothing in his heart could carry anything in his satchel and still walk away from it. Love was ballast. Love sank you.

And here he was, eleven days out of the safehouse, with a child's breath warming his collarbone, discovering that the ballast had been loaded while he wasn't looking.

* * *

Noor — and he caught himself using the name even in his own head now, which was its own small treason — Noor was teething.

This was not in any of the documents. He had carried treaties, cipher keys, death warrants, a vial of vaccine once strapped against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and not one page of his training had prepared him for the fact that a seven-month-old with sore gums becomes, between the hours of midnight and four, a creature of pure grievance. She cried with a fury entirely disproportionate to her size, arching her back, drumming her heels against his forearm, glaring at him with wet indignant eyes as though he personally had invented gums purely to betray her.

The first two nights Duska endured it with her eyebrow. On the third night she emerged from her cabin, took the baby out of August's arms without a word, dipped a cloth in cold river water, tied it around her finger, and let Noor gnaw on it while humming something low and tuneless. Noor quieted in under a minute. Duska handed her back, gave August a look that translated roughly as *incompetent*, and returned to her cabin.

"She's a captain," Marta said later, amused, "not a nursemaid."

"She has children?"

"Had. Grown. One runs ferries south of the delta. The other," Marta paused, choosing, "the other died in the first mobilization. She doesn't say so. But she keeps his boot-polish kit on the shelf above her bunk and polishes boots that don't need polishing, some nights, when she thinks no one sees."

August filed this away the way he filed everything, automatically, in the sealed rooms of his memory — then caught himself doing it and stopped. These were not secrets to be kept. They were just the sad furniture of other people's lives, offered freely, meant to be held lightly. He was learning, slowly, the difference.

Marta herself spent the days at the stern with a fishing line she never baited. When August asked about it, she shrugged. "It makes people leave you alone. A man fishing is invisible. A man sitting doing nothing gets talked to." She squinted at the water. "Also I like the motion of it. It's honest work that catches nothing. Very Vessanyan, if you think about it."

"You're from Vessany?"

"I'm from everywhere the river goes." She rebaited the hook — she did know how, then — and cast it into the brown water with a flick. "That's the trouble with rivers. They go everywhere. Including places you swore you'd left."

* * *

On the fifth day the river widened and slowed, and the banks turned soft and green-gold, orchards running down to the water, and the war seemed very far away indeed, and that was the day Noor laughed for the first time.

August would remember afterward exactly how stupid the circumstances were. He was attempting, on Duska's grudging instruction, to repair a tear in the canvas cover over the herring crates, and he had managed to get the needle stuck through his own sleeve to the skin beneath, and while wrestling free of it — pinned, essentially, by his own coat, flapping like a netted bird — he lost his balance, sat down hard on the deck, and knocked over a coil of rope, which unrolled across the deck in a long lazy spiral and came to rest against the rail.

Noor, propped in a nest of blankets beside the stove housing, watched the entire catastrophe with round, grave attention.

Then she laughed.

It was not a polite laugh. It was not a baby's chuckle. It was a full-bodied, astonished, delighted bark of a laugh, the laugh of someone who has waited her whole life — all seven months of it — for an adult to be this ridiculous in front of her, and it went on and on, her whole body shaking with it, her fists thumping the blanket, until August forgot the needle in his sleeve and started laughing too, helplessly, the two of them cackling on the deck of the Patient Margit while Duska watched from the wheelhouse with an expression August could not read and Marta reeled in her unbaited line and pretended, badly, to be interested in the river.

"Congratulations," Marta said finally. "You've been promoted. From courier to clown."

"It was the rope," August said, winded. "She liked the rope."

"She liked you falling down. She'll like it again tomorrow. Children are tyrants that way." Marta coiled the line. "You know what you did wrong, don't you?"

"I sat on the deck."

"Before that." She looked at him steadily. "You made her laugh. You made her *like* you. That was the mistake."

"I thought liking me was the point."

"The point is delivering her." Marta said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "You're not her father. You're not her uncle. You're transport. The moment you start being a person to her, you become a person she can lose. And the moment she becomes a person to you—" She stopped, and for once the smuggler's smoothness failed her, and August saw something old and tired move behind her eyes. "The moment she becomes a person to you, every soldier on this river stops being an obstacle and starts being a threat to something you'd die for. And men who'd die for something make mistakes. Men carrying nothing make none."

"A courier's virtue is weightlessness," August quoted. His handler's words, drilled into him on the first day, nine years ago, in a room smelling of ink and floor wax.

"Marta Vale's virtue is the same," she said. "I move what pays. I don't love what moves." She picked up her rod, then set it down again, and her voice gentled by a degree. "But I'll tell you a secret, since we have nothing but river. Everyone breaks that rule eventually. The good ones just break it slowly enough to finish the job first."

* * *

He broke it thoroughly that night.

Noor woke at midnight, as was her custom, but instead of crying she lay in her blanket nest making small experimental sounds, and August, who should have been sleeping because dawn brought the lock at Kesselmark and the lock at Kesselmark meant soldiers, got up instead and lifted her out and carried her to the rail because she was restless and the moon was out and the river was silver and he wanted — God help him — to show her.

"That's the moon," he told her gravely. "Do not trust it. It lies about the size of things."

She grabbed his nose.

"Yes. That's my face. Also yours to do with as you please, apparently."

She grabbed it again, delighted by her discovery, and he let her, standing in the cold with his collar open and his breath smoking, while the Patient Margit whispered upstream through country that belonged to no one anymore.

And then it happened, quietly, the way the worst things happen: without ceremony, without alarm. He was talking to her — narrating, actually, the way Duska hummed and Marta fished, just filling the silence because silence with a baby in it felt like negligence — and he heard himself say:

"And tomorrow we'll be through the lock, little Noor, and then—"

He stopped.

*Little Noor.* Not *the child*. Not *the package*, which was the official term and which he had used faithfully for eleven days precisely because it kept the thing weightless. He had been saying it less and less. Somewhere in the last week *the package* had become *she*, and *she* had become *Noor*, and tonight she had become *little Noor*, spoken aloud, in the dark, on a river full of checkpoints, with the seal of Ilan Voss folded inside his shirt and the name of a dead republic stitched into everything he was.

Couriers did not name what they carried. Names were hooks. A thing with a name stayed in you; a thing without a name passed through. He had carried a vial of vaccine once and could not now tell you what disease it was for. He had carried a death warrant and could not tell you the name on it, only the weight of the paper, which had been heavier than paper ought to be. He had carried seven sealed documents out of a burning capital and could describe the wax, the ribbon, the count, the route — and had deliberately, professionally, refused to imagine their contents.

But the child he could describe down to the exact color of her eyes in lamplight, the chipped tooth coming in crooked on the bottom left, the way her hair smelled of wool and milk and woodsmoke, the precise pitch of her fury at four in the morning, and the sound — God, the sound — of that laugh on the deck with the rope.

She had a weight now. She had a gravity. And gravity, he knew, pulled things down. Pulled barges aground. Pulled couriers off their routes. Pulled men back into burning houses.

"Little Noor," he said again, deliberately this time, testing it, the way you test ice before committing your weight.

She sneezed, which he chose to take as ratification.

He stood at the rail a long time after she fell asleep against his shoulder, watching the banks go by black and silver, doing the arithmetic he had been avoiding. Because the truth was that he had already broken the rule completely, days ago, and the naming was only the paperwork catching up. He had broken it in Ashfield when he lied to a checkpoint sergeant that the baby was his daughter and found the lie came easily, warmly, almost gladly. He had broken it the night she fell asleep holding his thumb and he had sat unmoving for two hours rather than wake her, while his legs went numb and the stove burned low.

Marta was right. Weightless men made no mistakes. But standing there in the dark with the child breathing against his neck, August found he no longer much admired weightless men. They delivered everything and kept nothing. They crossed the world and arrived nowhere. He had been one for eleven years and the sum total of what he owned was a satchel, a coat, and a talent for being forgotten.

The girl in his arms was the first thing he had ever carried that he did not want to put down.

Which meant, he understood with a cold settling in his stomach that had nothing to do with the river air, that he was now the most dangerous kind of courier there was: one with something to lose, and no intention of losing it.

* * *

They came to the Kesselmark lock in a drizzle just after dawn.

The lock was a stone throat in the river, old imperial masonry stained black with a century of floodwater, and above it, flying from a pole someone had painted over with white paint in a hurry, the new flag of the Provisional Directorate — the republic's own colors rearranged, as if the revolutionaries couldn't be bothered to design anything and had simply hoped no one would notice.

Two soldiers worked the lock. One was seventeen and bored and kept his rifle slung like a schoolbag. The other was older, maybe forty, with a sergeant's chevrons sewn on crooked, and he walked the length of the barge with his hands clasped behind his back, looking at the herring, the sugar, the stove, the rope coil — and at August, and at the blanket-wrapped bundle in August's arms, and back at August, with the unhurried thoroughness of a man counting doors.

"Papers for the cargo?"

"Grain sugar, going up-country to my wife's family. Personal effects otherwise." August produced the papers Marta had forged — a merchant's transit pass, dull and correct, the kind of document designed to be waved through. The sergeant read it twice. Rain dripped off the brim of his helmet.

"The child yours?"

"My daughter." Easy. Warm. Almost true. Noor, mercifully, slept. "Feverish. We're taking her to her grandmother in Terevan. Cleaner air up there. The doctor said."

The sergeant studied him for a long moment. Rain ticked on the canvas covers. Down in the lock basin the water level began, slowly, to fall, the stone walls rising around them inch by inch like the sides of a well.

"Fever," the sergeant said at last. "Bad season for it." He handed back the papers. Then, as he turned away, almost casually: "Terevan road's closed since Tuesday. Shelling. You'll want the mountain track past Sarno. Longer, but it breathes."

August blinked. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me." The sergeant was already walking toward the next barge in the queue, a coal lighter stacked with sacks. "Just hate seeing feverish children routed into artillery. Bad for morale all around."

The gates groaned open ahead. The Patient Margit slid through into the upper pool, where the river ran green and quiet and the hills rose up steeply on either side, forested, empty, promising nothing.

Marta appeared at August's elbow, chewing on the end of her unlit pipe. "That sergeant went to school with my brother," she said. "Or his ghost did. Either way, he owes the Vale family one favor, and now he's spent it on you, which means when I need it back there won't be one." She said it lightly, but her eyes stayed on the receding lock, on the flag, on the bored seventeen-year-old shouldering his rifle like a schoolbag. "We're lucky today. I don't trust luck. Luck on a river is just a current that hasn't turned yet."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Marta said, "that word travels faster than barges. Somebody on the lower river saw us board. Somebody always sees. And if the Directorate is looking for a man with a child — and they will be, soon, because men with children are rare and memorable — then the question isn't whether they find us." She tapped her pipe stem against the rail, once, twice, like a clock deciding something. "It's whether they find us before we find the mountains."

Ahead, past the bend, the hills closed in, and the river narrowed, and somewhere in the trees along the bank — August would swear this later, though he saw nothing at the time, only felt it, the way animals feel weather — something watched them pass.
