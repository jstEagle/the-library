## 9. The Handler's Ghost

The town was called Bellamy's Rest, which struck August as a joke someone had made and then forgotten to laugh at. Nothing about it rested. The market square had been half-emptied by conscription and half by rumor, and the rumor moved faster. He counted doors the way Marta had taught him—not for exits but for eyes. A town this size had maybe three people who reported to somebody, and one of them would be watching the well, because everyone watched the well.

He came in on foot, two days behind the barge, with Noor riding against his chest in a sling that had once carried diplomatic correspondence. She had grown opinionated about the arrangement. She liked to face forward now, so she could supervise the world, and she supervised it with a gravity that made strangers smile despite themselves.

"Keep your chin down," August murmured into her wool-wrapped warmth. "We're nobody today."

She blew a raspberry against his collarbone, which he chose to interpret as agreement.

The plan was simple, and therefore almost certainly doomed: buy bread, buy milk, buy nothing memorable, sleep one night at the inn whose sign showed a stag with an absurd number of antlers, and be gone before the dew dried. Marta had gone ahead to arrange the next leg of passage and had left him with a single instruction, delivered while picking wax out of her ear: *"If anyone from your old life finds you, don't explain yourself. Explaining is how they get the thread."*

August had spent ten years being the thread. He knew exactly how it worked.

So when he pushed open the door of the Stag and Antlers and saw a man sitting alone at the corner table with two cups already poured, he did not explain himself. He turned around.

"Don't be tedious, Ashgrove," said the man behind him. "The tea's getting cold, and I paid for it out of my own purse, which should tell you how far the Republic's credit has fallen."

* * *

Edran Kole had been his handler for nine years, and in all that time August had never once seen him sit with his back to a window. Now here he was, angled toward the glass like a man sunning himself, gray at the temples where there had been no gray before, wearing a civilian coat that fit him the way a disguise fits a man who has stopped caring whether it works.

"You look terrible," Kole said pleasantly.

"You look retired."

"I look promoted. It's worse." Kole nudged the second cup across the table. "Chamomile. You always hated chamomile. I ordered it because I wanted to see if you'd still make that face."

August made the face. Then he sat down, because sitting down was the only move that didn't look like running, and because Noor was beginning to squirm with the particular intensity that preceded either hunger or commentary, and neither was best performed standing up.

Kole looked at the sling. His expression did something very small and very controlled, the way a pond does something very small when a stone goes in.

"Well," he said. "That's not diplomatic correspondence."

"It's a baby."

"I have eyes, Ashgrove. I trained those eyes." He leaned back. "Whose is it?"

"A widow's. From Ashfield. Her husband died at the ford and she couldn't feed her, and I—" He heard himself constructing it, brick by brick, the little house of lies couriers lived in, and hated how easily the mortar still spread. "I'm taking her to her aunt. South."

"Mm," said Kole, in the tone of a man filing something under a heading you wouldn't like.

Noor chose that moment to produce from somewhere within her small person a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, followed by a laugh—a real one, bright and unguarded, aimed at the antlered stag on the sign outside as though the stag had told her a joke. Both men flinched as if shot.

"She laughs," Kole said, after a moment.

"Babies do. It's their whole economy."

"Yes. Well." Kole turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a habit August knew meant he was choosing between versions of the truth. "I didn't come here for the tea, August."

"No one comes to Bellamy's Rest for the tea."

"I came because you're carrying seven documents through three war zones and you burned six of them in a grate in Ashfield while a farmer's wife watched from a window she thought you hadn't noticed."

The room got very quiet. Or perhaps it had been quiet all along and August had only now arrived in it.

"The milk purchase," he said. "The coat trade."

"The coat trade confirmed what we suspected, which is that our most reliable courier had suddenly become unreliable in every direction at once. You went north instead of south. North is Voss country. North is where documents go to die or to matter, depending on who burns them." Kole's voice stayed level, conversational, almost kind. "Do you know what happens to me if I report what I know?"

August said nothing.

"Nothing good," Kole supplied. "But also nothing interesting. And I have discovered, in recent months, that I mind. Being uninteresting. It's a new development. Late-onset conscience—the physicians say there's no cure."

"Is there a point, Edran?"

"There's an offer." Kole set down the cup. "Reinstatement. Full pardon, back pay, and a posting anywhere you like that isn't here. The war is ending—one way or another, within the year, everyone sensible knows it—and when it ends, there will be a Republic-shaped hole where the paperwork used to be, and the men who helped close the ledger will be remembered kindly. All you have to do is hand over the child."

The word *child* sat between them on the table like a third cup nobody had ordered.

"As evidence," August said.

"As evidence. Against Ilan Voss. Whose seal was on that note you found, whose child that almost certainly is, and whose name is currently worth more to certain ambitious people than the entire eastern fleet. You wouldn't even be hurting her, August. Think it through. She'd go to a state nursery. Clean linen, proper food, tutors. In fifteen years she won't remember your face any more than she remembers mine. Whereas you—" Kole spread his hands, salesman's gesture, worn soft with use. "Whereas you are currently a dead man walking with a price on him from both sides, carrying cargo that will get her killed along with you. Handing her over isn't betrayal. It's arithmetic."

August looked down at Noor. She had fallen asleep mid-supervision, her cheek mashed against his chest, one fist curled around the sling's strap with the grip of someone who had decided, provisionally but firmly, that this particular thing was hers.

Arithmetic. Yes. He had been built for arithmetic. Nine years of training: *the courier carries; the courier forgets; the courier's virtue is weightlessness.* They had sanded everything off him that might catch—opinions, attachments, the habit of staying anywhere longer than a night—and then they had sent him out into the world to be a hand that passed things along without ever asking what they were.

And then some drunk spring evening ten years ago, in a garden, a friend had grabbed his wrist and made him promise otherwise, and the promise had caught in him like a splinter too deep to draw out, and everything since had been the flesh growing around it.

"No," August said.

"August—"

"No. Listen to me, Edran, because I'll only manage the speech once." He kept his voice low, level, the professional calm laid over the dread like frost over a field. "You're right about the arithmetic. Every figure is correct. I've run them myself, at night, when she's asleep, and I want you to understand that I ran them honestly, because I was taught to run them honestly. And the sum keeps coming out wrong. Not because I'm sentimental. Because whoever wrote the numbers forgot to include her. She isn't evidence. She isn't leverage. She's a person who laughed at a wooden stag five minutes ago, and the Republic—which I served faithfully, which I bled for, which put my own name on a purge list inside the very pouch I was carrying for it—does not get to file her under *assets*."

Kole was quiet for a long moment. Outside, someone led a limping horse past the window, and the hoofbeats sounded like a slow argument.

"The purge list," Kole said finally. "You found it."

"I read everything I carry. That was the first rule you ever taught me."

"It was. And it was the rule I hoped you'd break." Something moved behind Kole's eyes—not warmth exactly, but the memory of where warmth used to be kept. "Do you know why your name was on it? Not treason. Not incompetence. *Weight.* Someone upstairs noticed that August Ashgrove had never once lost a package, never once broken, never once become predictable. Do you understand? Your perfection frightened them. A courier who can't be bought can't be trusted to stay bought when it matters, and they intend for it to matter." He shook his head slowly. "I argued for you. For what it's worth, which is less every day."

"What's it worth now?"

"One day's head start. Maybe two." Kole reached into his coat, and August's hand moved of its own accord to the knife at his belt—but Kole only produced a folded slip of paper, plain, unsigned, and slid it across the table. "A route south that doesn't appear on any patrol schedule. I'd tell you not to ask how I know it's clean, but you'd only make that face again."

August didn't take it. "Why let me go?"

"Because I'm marking you instead." Kole said it plainly, without apology, and somehow that was worse than apology would have been. "In the report, you died at the ford at Ashfield. Heroic. Documents destroyed rather than surrendered. It's even mostly true, and the best lies keep their shoes on. As far as the Republic is concerned, August Ashgrove is a name in a column now, closed out, balanced." He stood, buttoning his civilian coat with the crisp motions of a man dressing a ghost. "Which means the man at this table is no one at all. And no one, August, is the only rank left in this country that can't be stripped from you."

"And the price?"

"There isn't one. That's the part you'll lie awake not believing." Kole paused at the edge of the table. For a moment the handler's mask slipped entirely, and underneath was just a tired man in a bad coat in a dying town. "Nine years I moved you around the board like you were made of glass and gunpowder both. You were the best thing I ever handled. I'd like at least one thing I handled to survive me." He glanced down at the sleeping child, and his voice dropped to nearly nothing. "Get her north. Get her to whoever that seal belongs to. And then disappear so thoroughly that even I can't find you—because the next man they send won't order chamomile."

He left coins on the table, too many, and walked out into the gray morning without looking back.

* * *

August sat for a long while after the door closed, holding a cooling cup of chamomile he had no intention of drinking.

Grief, he discovered, was not what he'd expected. He had grieved before—for friends at the ford, for the capital burning, for the version of Voss that existed in a garden ten years ago. This was quieter and stranger: a grief shaped exactly like himself. Somewhere between the table and the door, the last of the courier August Ashgrove had packed his things and gone, and what remained was a man with no handler, no route, no republic, no name worth killing for—only a sleeping child, a stolen document, and a promise.

He tested the feeling the way you test a missing tooth. *They're not coming for me anymore,* he thought. *Not as their own.*

It felt like falling and floating at once, and he understood suddenly that he would never be able to explain it to anyone who hadn't been hollowed out the same way.

Noor stirred, frowned at the ceiling with tremendous editorial disapproval, and then located his face and forgave it everything.

"All right," August told her softly, gathering the route paper off the table and folding it into his boot. "You heard the man. We're no one. Best job security in the country."

She seized his finger with her whole fist and held on, and he stood, and shouldered the satchel, and walked out of the Stag and Antlers a free man and a marked one, north, toward the mountains, toward a door he was only half sure existed—

—and did not see, three streets back, the second watcher Kole had brought with him, peeling away from a doorway to find a telegraph office.
