## 10. Occupied Winter

Marta Vale had survived eleven years of smuggling by following one rule: never watch anyone longer than you have to. Watching bred attachment, and attachment was a debt collected in blood. She had broken that rule exactly twice in her life—once for a man named Ilan Voss, who had paid his debts, and once now, for a courier named August Ashgrove, who was teaching a baby to walk against a cellar wall as though the world outside were not actively trying to kill them.

"You see?" August said, holding Noor's hands above her head like a puppeteer with a very small, very determined marionette. "Left foot. Then right foot. It's not complicated."

Noor sat down.

"It's not complicated," August agreed gravely, "but it does require cooperation."

Marta leaned on the cellar ladder and told herself she was only observing because observation was habit. The cellar beneath the tanner's house in Veymar smelled of salt and old brine, which was foul but honest, and honest smells were hard to come by in an occupied town. Above them, the town went about its winter under republican occupation: bread lines, curfew bells, soldiers stamping boots on frozen cobblestones like a clock that hated everyone equally.

The child took two more steps along the wall, gripping the stones with both fists, her whole face folded into an expression of furious concentration that Marta recognized from every gambler she'd ever known at the moment of a dangerous bet.

"She'll be running by spring," August said, without looking up.

"Spring's optimistic."

"I'm an optimist." He caught Noor as her knees buckled and swung her up onto his shoulder, where she immediately began investigating his ear with the thoroughness of a customs inspector. "It's a professional flaw. Couriers are supposed to assume the road is passable or they'd never leave the door."

"And what happens when the road isn't passable?"

He was quiet a moment, bouncing the child gently. "Then I've been carrying the wrong thing," he said, "and it's time to put it down."

Marta looked away first. That annoyed her. She was forty-one years old; she had stared down customs officers, border patrols, and one memorable bandit chief with a fondness for knives, and here she was losing staring contests with a man whose greatest weapon was sincerity.

She climbed back up to the kitchen and put water on to heat, because hands needed work or they started thinking.

* * *

Veymar under occupation ran on three currencies: coin, favors, and information. Marta traded in all three, but information was the only one that appreciated. She kept her ear to the ground the way other people kept a hand to a rosary, and by mid-December she knew things.

She knew the garrison commander drank and was sentimental about his mother. She knew the checkpoint sergeant on the north road had been passed over for promotion twice and would trade a blind eye for flattery. She knew there was a prison camp at Kolvary where the republic sent men it didn't want to shoot outright because shooting them might make martyrs, whereas frost and dysentery made only statistics.

And she knew—because a clerk in the quartermaster's office owed her money and talked too much—that among the statistics was a man named Denny Vale, arrested in the second week of the war for moving letters that belonged to no one's approved correspondence. Her brother. Who had inherited their mother's stubbornness and none of their mother's sense, which meant he had written her exactly one letter before being taken, and the letter had said, *Don't do anything stupid.*

Which was, coming from Denny, practically a love poem.

Marta sat in the kitchen with her tea going cold and did arithmetic. A bribe for a camp transfer required connections in the garrison command, and those connections were expensive. Coin she could eventually scrape. Favors she could eventually call in. But the thing that opened Kolvary's gates—the thing that made clerks move names up lists without asking why—was leverage. Something someone important wanted hidden, or something someone important feared revealed.

Something, say, bearing the seal of General Ilan Voss, carried across occupied territory by a courier who should by rights have been dead three times over.

She looked at her tea. The tea offered no counsel. Tea was useless that way; brandy at least had the decency to lie to you comfortably.

* * *

The trouble—and she admitted this to herself with the reluctance of a woman admitting a debt—was that the document wasn't hers to price.

She had found August half-frozen on the river road in November, a man with a satchel and a baby and a story so thin she could have read a broadsheet through it. She had taken them in for reasons she still hadn't fully itemized: the seal on the paper he carried, yes, but also the way he'd stood between the baby and the door when the patrol passed, without seeming to decide to do it. She'd moved Voss's messages once, years ago, in peacetime, when the seal had meant something better than it meant now. Sentiment. Another debt.

But sentiment didn't buy Denny out of Kolvary. And if the republic caught them all in this cellar—which was not an if so much as a when, given how long they'd stayed, and she'd stayed too long already, and she knew it—then the document wouldn't buy anything at all except a faster execution for everyone involved.

Use it or lose it. That was the other rule. The first rule was don't watch anyone longer than you have to, and look how well that had gone.

* * *

On the last night of December, the snow came heavy enough to muffle the curfew bells, and the three of them ate stewed turnips and black bread by lamplight while the wind tried the shutters like a debt collector.

Noor, seated on a flour sack between them, had developed opinions about turnips. They were expressed at volume.

"She hates turnips," August said.

"Everyone hates turnips."

"You hate turnips?"

"I'm everyone," Marta said. "Eat your turnips."

Noor picked up a piece of turnip, considered it with the gravity of a magistrate, and threw it at August's face. It missed by a wide margin, striking the wall, and Noor laughed—the big, gasping, scandalized laugh of a person discovering comedy—and then laughed harder at her own laughing, and August laughed too, helplessly, the sound cracking out of him like ice on a thawing river, and Marta sat there holding her cup and understood, with cold clarity, that she was watching a man fall in love with a child in real time, and that neither of them knew yet what that cost.

Couriers weren't supposed to name what they carried. Smugglers weren't supposed to love whom they moved. The professions had that much in common.

"Tell me about her," Marta said, before she could stop herself.

August's laugh settled. He wiped turnip from his collar. "You know I can't."

"I know you won't. Different thing." She turned her cup slowly. "I moved Voss's letters for two years, back when he was just a colonel with good handwriting and bad taste in wine. I never asked what was in them either. But I'm asking now because the arithmetic has changed, courier. Whatever you're carrying, it's worth more than my life right now, and I'd like to know if that was true in October too."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the wind dropped, and in the sudden quiet they could hear the boots of the night patrol crossing the square, unhurried, counting doors.

August looked at Noor, asleep now against the flour sack, her fist curled around a crust she'd refused to surrender even in sleep.

"She's somebody's granddaughter," he said finally. "Somebody who matters. And there are people who'd rather she never grew up."

"That's it? That's the whole story?"

"That's the part I know is true. The rest is paper, and paper lies depending on who's reading it."

Marta nodded slowly. It was more than she'd expected and less than she needed, which was, in her experience, the exact recipe for most bargains.

* * *

She made her decision the way she made all her best decisions: alone, at night, pacing.

Not because she wanted to. Because the alternative was to keep watching August teach that child to walk along a wall, day after day, until the republic knocked on the tanner's door, and then everyone's accounts came due at once—hers, his, the baby's, and Denny's, rotting in Kolvary under a number instead of a name.

Compassion, her mother used to say, was a fine quality in a person and a fatal one in a business. But her mother had also crossed a frozen river at fifty-two to smuggle her son out of a conscription roundup, so Marta had always taken her pronouncements with a grain of salt the size of a fist.

Here was the truth she paced around: she liked him. She liked his stupid optimism and his careful hands and the fact that he'd learned to fold cloth diapers into origami cranes out of pure boredom. She liked that Noor reached for Marta now with both arms whenever Marta came down the ladder, and that Marta pretended not to notice, and that pretending was getting harder, which was itself a kind of confession.

And the truth underneath that truth: the document could save one life she loved or another. Not both. Nobody's ledger balanced that neatly, whatever the priests said.

If she asked August for it, he'd refuse—she'd seen his face when he touched the inside pocket of his coat, the way some men touch a wound. He'd refuse, and she'd have to decide whether to take it, and taking it meant becoming the kind of person who robbed sleeping men with babies, and she'd spent forty-one years not being quite that person, though the margin had narrowed in wartime.

If she didn't ask, Denny froze in Kolvary, and she became the kind of person who let that happen while drinking stolen tea and watching a child learn to walk.

There was no clean option. There was only the least filthy one, and the least filthy one, she decided somewhere around the third hour of pacing, was to ask. Out loud. Like a person. Let him refuse like a person too. Whatever happened after, at least it would happen in daylight, between two people who'd shared turnips.

Her mother would have been proud. Or horrified. With Mother it was usually both.

* * *

In the morning the snow lay knee-deep and blue-white, and the town below the tannery looked almost innocent, smoke rising straight from chimneys, children shrieking happily in the square until a soldier shouted at them. Marta stood at the frosted window with her breath fogging the glass and rehearsed the sentence until it stopped sounding like a knife.

Down in the cellar, she could hear the morning lesson underway. August's voice, low and patient. *"That's it—one more. Left foot. You're a tyrant, you know that? Left foot—"*

And then a small, triumphant shriek, the unmistakable sound of a human being standing upright on her own authority for the first time, followed by applause—August applauding, actually applauding, clapping like a fool at a coronation—and Marta closed her eyes and let herself feel the full weight of what she was about to do, because she'd earned the right to feel it, at least for a minute.

Then she went down the ladder to name her price.
