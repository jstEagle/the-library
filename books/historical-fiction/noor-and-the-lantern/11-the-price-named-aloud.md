## 11. The Price Named Aloud

The snow came down on Kellin's Row like the republic was trying to bury its own mistakes, and Marta Vale counted doors the way other people counted money.

August had learned her habit by now — three weeks in the occupied town of Vellmara, hidden in the cellar beneath a cooper's shop that hadn't coopered anything since the artillery came through. She would stand at the narrow window above the stairs each morning with a cup of barley tea going cold in her hands, and she would count. One, two, three, down the row of gutted houses. Then back again. He had asked her once what she was counting and she'd said, "Doors," as if that were a complete answer, and in a way it was. Marta was a woman who liked to know how many ways out of a room existed before she entered it. August had been a courier long enough to respect that. He had also been a courier long enough to know that people who counted exits usually expected to need them.

He was mending Noor's sock by candlelight when Marta came down the cellar steps without her tea, which was the first sign. The second sign was that she sat across from him instead of beside the stove. The third sign was that she didn't joke.

Marta always joked. It was her armor, worn over the same kind of scars he carried under his own. When the checkpoint sergeant at Ashfield had nearly found Noor in the satchel, Marta had defused it afterward by observing that August lied about fatherhood with the conviction of a man who had clearly never held a baby before, because no real father holds one like it's a live grenade. When the barge captain overcharged them, she'd haggled him down and then tipped him extra "for the pleasure of my company, which he got for free." When Noor had screamed through the whole of the second night in Vellmara, Marta had sung republican marching songs to her with deliberately wrong words until even August, exhausted and terrified, had snorted into his collar.

She was not joking now.

"August." She folded her hands on the table between them, a negotiator's gesture. "I'm going to say something, and I'd take it kindly if you let me finish before you do whatever it is your face is about to do."

His face, apparently, was already doing something. He set down the sock. "Go ahead."

"You remember I told you I'd name my price when I knew it."

"You said that on the river. You've said it eleven times since."

"Twelve," Marta said. "You weren't listening the time I said it while you were asleep, but it counts. A debt doesn't stop existing because the debtor snores." She took a breath, and he watched her arrange the words like cards she'd been holding a long while. "My brother is in the camp at Draven Hollow. You've heard me mention Bren. The one who taught me to read river water and lie to customs men. They took him in the first purge — not because he did anything, but because he moved messages once for a man they'd decided to hate, and guilt by association is the only arithmetic this war does correctly."

August felt it coming the way you feel weather in an old wound. "Marta—"

"Let me finish." Her voice stayed level. That was the frightening part. Anger he could have handled. This was a woman reading from a ledger she'd balanced three times. "I found a broker. There's always a broker; there are men in this country who could sell sand to a desert and sleep well after. He can get Bren out. It will cost more money than either of us has seen in two years." She paused. "But there's paper in your coat that isn't money and is worth more. A document bearing a certain seal, kept dry through a burned province and a frozen river and one very undignified night in a pig shed that you swore me to secrecy about and which I have honored, mostly, except to Noor, who can't talk."

"The document is not for sale."

"There it is," Marta said softly. "The face. I told you not to do the face yet."

"Marta—"

"August, please." And there — just for a moment — the ledger slipped, and underneath it was a woman who had spent twenty years moving other people's secrets and had finally, for the first time, asked for something of her own. "I have carried you and that child across half an occupied country. I have fed you from my own ration when yours ran out. I have not asked you one question about what's in that paper, not one, though it's been sleeping against your ribs the entire journey and I could feel it every time we embraced like a second heartbeat. I have earned the right to ask. So I am asking. Give me the document. Let me trade it for my brother's life."

The candle guttered. Above them, faint through the floorboards, the cooper's widow coughed in her sleep.

"No," August said.

It came out quieter than he intended. He tried again, louder, because some part of him believed that volume was honesty. "No. I can't. I won't."

Marta sat back. She looked at him the way she looked at doors: counting the exits from this conversation, finding them all bricked up. "May I ask why? And before you answer — 'because I said so' is a reason for generals and fathers, and you are neither, whatever you told the sergeant at Ashfield."

He had known this moment would come. He had perhaps pretended, in the small hours, that it wouldn't. Now it was here, sitting across the table with its hands folded, waiting.

"It's proof," he said. "Of Voss's innocence. Of what actually happened — the massacre, the war, all of it. Everyone believes Ilan Voss ordered the killing at Serel Ford. Everyone including, I think sometimes, Ilan Voss himself. This document says otherwise. It names the man who gave the order. If Voss is ever brought before anything resembling a court, if there is ever a history written that isn't written by the victors, this paper is the difference between a monster and a scapegoat. It's—" He stopped, because the next word was the true one and the true ones were the expensive ones. "It's a promise. Made flesh. Ten years ago, in a garden, I promised him that whoever survived would guard what mattered. He sent me his daughter instead of a message. The document is part of the message. I don't get to spend it on anything, Marta. Not even on something good."

"A promise," Marta repeated.

"Yes."

"To a man who declared himself an enemy of the state. Who may be dead. Who abandoned a child on a doorstep in the rain rather than raise her himself."

"He had reasons."

"There are always reasons," Marta said. "Bren has reasons too. His is that he's still breathing, currently, in a camp where the winter rations were cut in November, and I have had a letter — one letter, smuggled out in a laundry cart — in which he wrote, and I quote because I have it memorized down to the ink blot, *'If the chance comes, take it. Don't wait for perfect.'*" She leaned forward. "You're waiting for perfect, August. You're waiting for some grand tribunal that doesn't exist, some honest court that this country hasn't seen since before either of us could walk. Meanwhile there is a living, specific, warm-handed man who taught me everything I know, and he will be dead by spring, and you want me to weigh him against an abstraction. Justice. History. A drunk man's garden promise."

"It's not an abstraction to me."

"I know," she said, and gentled, which was worse. "I know it isn't. That's what makes it cruel. If you were hoarding it out of greed I could hate you and be done. But you're hoarding it out of love, and love makes terrible bookkeeping."

He laughed despite himself — a short, unhappy sound. "That's a horrible sentence."

"I'm a horrible woman. Ask anyone I've smuggled." She rose and walked to the far wall, arms crossed, then turned back. "Then hear my counteroffer. Not the document itself. A copy. You have hands; you've forged transit papers twice in my presence, badly both times, but the seal — the seal you could press into wax and I take the copy, and the original stays against your ribs where it can keep being holy. The broker takes the copy. Bren lives. Your promise stays intact."

For one dangerous moment, he wanted it. That was the thing about Marta — she built doors where you'd sworn there was only wall. A copy. A compromise. Everyone walks away carrying something.

And then he thought it through, courier-fashion, cold and fast. A copy meant the seal existed twice. It meant the truth could be questioned — *how do we know this facsimile is genuine?* It meant a broker's hands on it, a broker who sold to the highest bidder, and the highest bidder in an occupied town was the occupation. It meant the chancellor learning that proof of his crime was loose, and sending men, competent men, the kind who had staked out orchards. It meant Noor — chancellor's blood or not, whatever she was — becoming a thing worth hunting harder than ever.

"No copies," he said. "No halves. I'm sorry, Marta. The answer is no."

The silence that followed had weight. He felt it settle on his shoulders like the old satchel, the one that had carried seven documents and then one child, and he understood suddenly that he had been light only briefly, that the weight always came back wearing new clothes.

Marta nodded slowly. "All right," she said. "All right." She picked up her tea-less cup, looked at it, set it down again. "I want you to understand something, and I'm not saying it as a threat, though you'd be forgiven for hearing it that way. I named my price. You refused. That's fair — a price refused is a deal unmade, and I keep clean books. But I'm not going to pretend nothing changed." She met his eyes. "I'll still get you to the orchard. I gave my word on passage, and passage is sacred, it's the one law of the rivers. But after the orchard, August — after you've delivered your paper and your child and your precious promise — you and I are finished. I don't travel with men whose loyalties are made of stone. Stone doesn't bend, and roads bend, and if you can't bend, eventually the road goes on without you."

"Marta—"

"Don't." She raised a hand, and he saw her fingers tremble, just slightly, and understood the cost of her own speech better than she wanted him to. "Don't apologize. Apologies are for things you regret, and you don't regret it. I watched your face. You'd refuse again tomorrow and the day after, and part of me — the part that used to be young and stupid in a garden of my own — respects that enormously." She climbed two steps, stopped, spoke without turning around. "For what it's worth, I hope your paper saves him. Voss. I hope it burns the right people down. I hope your garden promise turns out to be worth more than my brother's life, because if it doesn't, then the two of us spent a winter teaching a baby to walk in a cellar for nothing, and I find I mind that."

Her footsteps went up the stairs. The door closed. Not slammed — closed, precisely, like a ledger shut.

August sat alone with the candle and the unfinished sock and the flat rectangle of sealed oilcloth against his ribs, and he pressed his hand over it, and it did not comfort him the way it usually did. For the first time since the rain-soaked doorstep in another life, the document felt less like a promise than a stone — heavy, silent, and demanding to be thrown at someone.

Noor woke in her basket and made her small complaint, and he lifted her out, and she grabbed his nose with the uncomplicated tyranny of infants, and he loved her so much it frightened him, and he thought: *this is what the paper protects. This is why.* And then, treacherously, from nowhere, from the place where couriers keep the thoughts they're forbidden to think, another thought arrived:

*What does it say, exactly?*

He had carried it for months. He had defended it, nearly died for it, refused a friend's brother's life for it. And he had never — not once, not since Voss's note had instructed him to forget faces and carry weight — actually read what the seal was sealing. He knew what it proved. He had been told what it proved. Couriers were not told things; couriers were given containers and directions and the virtue of weightlessness.

But the republic had put his name on a purge list, and the republic was dead or dying, and Marta's footsteps overhead had gone quiet, and Noor was watching him with eyes the color of the river in autumn, eyes that belonged to somebody, that had a history, that were *her*, and the thought arrived again and this time it stayed:

*A man cannot guard a promise he has never opened.*

The candle flickered. Against his ribs, the oilcloth waited, patient as paper always is, certain as paper always is that sooner or later, everyone reads.

* * *

He almost didn't do it that night. He got as far as holding the oilcloth packet over the candle table, turning it, feeling the wax seal's ridge under his thumb — Voss's crest, the lantern and the ford — and then he wrapped it away again and paced the cellar like a man arguing with himself in two languages.

The third watch of the night, with Noor breathing her small furnace-breath against his shoulder, he stopped pacing.

Because he remembered something. Something small and stupid and courier-shaped: the seal on the packet was pressed in *green* wax. And diplomatic seals — he had carried hundreds — were pressed in black for orders, red for writs, gold for the chancellor's own hand. Green was for personal correspondence. Private letters. Which meant that somewhere inside the packet that he had treated as scripture, there was a letter, an actual letter, in Voss's actual hand, addressed — possibly — to someone. Perhaps to the family. Perhaps to the child. Perhaps—

Perhaps to him.

August sat down slowly on the crate. Above him, Marta slept or didn't. Outside, the snow went on burying the republic's mistakes, door by door by door, and Marta was somewhere in the dark counting them.

He set Noor gently in her basket. He took out the packet. And this time, when he reached the point of turning back, he didn't.

He broke the green seal.
