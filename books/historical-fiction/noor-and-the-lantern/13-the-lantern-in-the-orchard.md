## 13. The Lantern in the Orchard

The coded instructions had been folded inside the lining of Noor's satchel, sewn there by a hand August now knew belonged to Ilan Voss himself, and they read like a letter written by a man who expected his reader to be tired, cold, and slightly stupid.

*If you are reading this, you kept her alive longer than I gave either of us credit for,* the first line said. *Go north past Ashfield until the road forgets itself. There is an orchard on a hill above a stone farmhouse. The trees are dead but they are not useless. Count seven rows from the gate. In the seventh row, look up.*

August had read it eleven times since leaving the occupied town, and each time the same joke occurred to him: Voss had always been terrible at goodbyes and worse at directions, and here he was doing both at once, from whatever grave or disgrace he'd been poured into.

Marta had wanted to come.

"I know these hills," she'd said, pulling her boots on with the violence of a woman who considered footwear a moral question. "I moved messages through this valley when your republic was still pretending to be decent."

"No," August said. "Someone stays with Noor."

"And that someone is you, walking into whatever's waiting up there, while I sit in a cellar knitting?"

"You don't knit."

"I'd learn. Out of spite."

But he'd won, mostly because she couldn't argue with the logic and hated it. If the orchard was watched—and after what he'd learned about the document, about whose granddaughter he'd been carrying through a war like a loaf of contraband bread—then the safest place for the child was far from it. He'd left before dawn, taking the long way around the frozen creek so his tracks would read as a farmer checking fences, and he'd carried nothing but the document against his chest, where it sat like a second heartbeat.

Now the hill rose ahead of him through the gray morning, and the orchard stood on its crown like a congregation of burned matches.

He stopped at the fence line and let the wind tell him what it could. Snow, the smell of cold iron from somewhere—the creek, probably, or a trough—woodsmoke from the valley below, thin and domestic. A rooster somewhere, complaining about the century. All of it ordinary. All of it exactly what a hillside should sound like.

That was the first thing his training said, in the flat voice of every handler he'd ever had: *ordinary is a costume.* The second thing it said, softer, in his own voice: *you're allowed to hope. You're just not allowed to hope carelessly.*

He went over the fence.

* * *

The trees were apple, once. He could tell by the shape of them even blackened, the way they'd been pruned low and wide by someone who loved the work. Fire had taken the farmhouse roofline years ago—you could see the old scorch climbing the stone like ivy—but someone had patched part of it since, badly, with tin and tarpaper. People lived there, or hid there. The lantern signal made sense: light the lamp in the seventh tree, and whoever came looking would know the family still breathed.

August counted rows from the rusted gate. One. Two. His breath hung around his head like a thought he couldn't finish. Three. Four. Somewhere behind him a crow said something rude to another crow. Five.

Six.

In the seventh row he looked up, and there it was.

A lantern hung from a branch as bare as an arm offering something. Not lit—not now, in daylight—but real, glass panes fogged with age, hanging from a nail driven with care. Someone had climbed this dead tree with cold hands and hammered a nail and hung a promise on it, and the promise was still there, swinging faintly, patient as only objects can be.

August stood under it for a long moment and felt something crack open in his chest that had been sealed since a garden, ten years ago, since a drunk man gripping his shoulder saying *whoever survives, we guard what matters.*

"You sentimental idiot," he told himself quietly. "She's going to be fine. You found it. That's the hard part done."

Which was, he would reflect later, precisely the moment he noticed the smoke.

Not the valley smoke—that was hearth-smoke, lazy, rising straight. This was different. Down-slope, east of the house, tucked into a fold of land where a collapsed barn formed a windbreak: a thread of smoke too disciplined to be a chimney. Smoke that had been fed carefully. Smoke from a fire someone was keeping small on purpose.

And beneath it, if you didn't blink—if you let your eyes go soft the way handlers taught you to—a shape that wasn't a barn wall. A coat, moving. Then another.

August did not run. Running was a message. He bent instead, as if inspecting the base of a dead tree, the way any half-wit farmer might check whether blight had reached the root crown, and while bent he counted.

Six men. Maybe seven; one shape refused to resolve. Two posted near the ruined barn, one walking a slow loop along the eastern fence line with the unhurried gait of a man killing time on watch, and three—three were the ones that turned his blood to well water—positioned where they could cover the track that led up from the valley road. The approach. *His* approach. The only sensible approach.

They weren't hiding from the farmhouse. They weren't besieging anyone.

They were waiting for a courier.

* * *

He gave the blight inspection another full minute, because patience was free and panic cost everything, and he used the minute to understand how wrong he'd been. He'd assumed the trap, if any, would be at the farmhouse—soldiers on the family, the lantern a lure. But the house showed no soldiers, no disturbance, only that honest patchwork roof and a thread of cooking smoke. The agents weren't interested in the family yet.

They were interested in the paper. In whoever came to deliver it.

Which meant someone had talked. Not Marta—he'd stake his last coin on that, and he found he was willing to; that surprised him more than the soldiers did. But someone. The handler, maybe. The ghost who'd offered reinstatement and, refused, had marked him instead. A marked courier was a lantern himself, wasn't he? Light him up and follow where he glowed.

*I'm not carrying the child to the signal,* August thought, crouched in the snow among dead roots. *I'm carrying the signal to the child.*

The bitter little laugh almost escaped him. Almost. Instead he began, slowly, on hands and knees as though drunk or exhausted or simply a man who'd dropped a glove, to move backward out of the seventh row.

It was going beautifully until the crow returned.

The same crow, or its cousin, or some feathered collaborator of the Republic of Vessany, chose that moment to object loudly to August's existence from the fence post directly beside him, and the walking watcher on the eastern fence stopped. Turned. Squinted up the hill.

August became a farmer. It was, he reflected, the role he'd played most often that winter: the milk-buying liar, the fence-checker, the man with a baby who was absolutely his, sir, look at the nose, identical. He straightened, brushed snow off his knees with theatrical annoyance, tipped an imaginary cap toward the distant figure, and began descending the hill at the pace of a man with nowhere better to be.

The watcher watched him go. Did not call out. Did not raise the whistle August could now see hanging from the man's fist, curled there casual as a cigarette.

They were letting him leave. Of course they were. He was the delivery mechanism; shooting the mailman solved nothing. They'd wait for him to fetch the document—or the girl, God, maybe both—and lead them straight to everything.

Behind him, patient in its dead tree, the lantern swung in the wind, still keeping its promise to nobody.

* * *

He did not take the direct path back. He took a path so indirect it qualified as philosophy: down through a drainage ditch, along the frozen creek with its skin of gray ice, doubling back twice through a stand of pines where he stood so long among the trunks that a hare regarded him with open pity. By the time the occupied town's rooftops came into view, dusk had arrived early the way it did in winter, falling like a dropped curtain rather than fading, and the windows below had begun to glow.

His heart was doing something complicated. Part of it was simple arithmetic: six or seven men, one whistle, one hill between them and the cellar where Marta was teaching herself, out of spite, to entertain an infant. Another part was colder and quieter and wore his old handler's face: *they followed you here. Which means they can follow you anywhere. Which means the town was never safe either.*

He slipped in through the tannery yard, past the tanner's dogs—who had accepted him as furniture weeks ago and merely opened one eye each—and down the cellar steps, and knocked the pattern on the door, and heard Marta's boots cross the floor at her usual pace, which was the pace of a woman charging rent.

She opened it, took one look at him, and said, "You found it."

"How could you possibly—"

"Your face. You've got the face of a man who found what he was looking for and wishes he hadn't." She stepped aside to let him in. Behind her, on a blanket by the stove, Noor was sitting upright with tremendous dignity, gnawing on a wooden spoon, wearing a wool hat two sizes too large that made her look like a very serious mushroom.

"We have to leave tonight," August said.

"Of course we do." Marta was already moving, gathering, wrapping bread in cloth with brisk economy. "Tell me while I pack. How many?"

"Six. Maybe seven. Republican agents, not regulars. Good ones. They're watching the orchard, not the house—they're waiting for me to come back with the document."

Marta's hands paused over the bread, just for a beat. "Waiting," she repeated.

"They let me walk away. I'm the errand. They want me to run it for them."

Noor chose that moment to drop the spoon, consider the injustice of gravity, and emit a single outraged syllable that sounded remarkably like *well.* August crossed the room and picked her up before he'd decided to, and she settled against his shoulder with the sigh of a small empress accepting tribute, and he held her and looked at the stove and understood, with total clarity, what he'd known since the seventh row:

There was no route clever enough. There was no ditch or pine stand or false trail that would lose professionals who had all winter and all the roads. Every direction was a direction they'd predicted, because every direction ended where he ended: at Ilan Voss's door, with the girl, with the paper. He could not outwalk the trap. He could only choose which door it sprang at—and who was standing in the room when it did.

"Marta," he said. "How fast can you get a message to the farmhouse without crossing that hill?"

Her eyes narrowed. "Depends who it's for."

"The people in the house. Voss's family. They need to know the orchard is watched—that the signal's been seen. And they need to know tonight."

"And why," Marta said slowly, folding the bread cloth closed with unnecessary precision, "do I think you're telling me only half of what's in your head?"

Because, August thought, the other half was this: someone would have to carry that message across ground staked out by men with whistles, and he was not going to ask her to do it, and he was beginning to suspect she was not going to give him the chance to do it himself.

"The other half," he said, "is that in the morning, one of us is going to be very busy, and I'd rather it was me."

Outside, far off toward the hills, so faint it might have been the wind worrying a loose shutter, something whistled—two notes, low then high, and then silence, the particular satisfied silence of an order being passed along.

Noor stirred against his shoulder and yawned, utterly unafraid, trusting the whole rotten world to the arms that held her.

"Hold that thought," August murmured to her, and reached for his coat.
