## 14. Marta's Choice

Marta Vale had counted doors for thirty years, and she had never once miscounted.

It was the first thing her father taught her, back when the smuggling was only fish and brandy across a lazy border: *A door is a mouth, moya dochka. It opens or it shuts, but it always means something.* She had built a life on that arithmetic — every safehouse a door worth knowing, every checkpoint a door worth avoiding, every man's face a door into what he would do next. She could read a garrison commander's door from fifty paces. She could read August Ashgrove's door from across a room, and it said: *this man will die before he hands over that child, so don't bother asking twice.*

She liked him better for it. That was the trouble with liking people.

Now she sat in the back room of the Kettle & Crow, a tavern in occupied Vellin that smelled of wet wool and boiled turnips, watching a republican captain read her letter and thinking about doors.

The captain's name was Dresch. He had soft hands and a soft voice and eyes like coins left too long in water. He wore his uniform the way some men wear borrowed coats — correctly, but without conviction — which told Marta he was ambitious rather than loyal, which made him predictable, which made him cheap. Not free. Nothing was free anymore. But cheap.

"You understand," Dresch said, not looking up, "that this constitutes collaboration with the Provisional Authority."

"I understand you'll pay me."

"I'm noting it for your file." He turned the page. Her handwriting looked small on his big table. "You're certain of the location."

"The ruined orchard above Stelka Hollow. The lantern's already hung." She kept her voice level by giving it nothing to do — no warmth, no apology, no room for regret to stand in. "There's a farmhouse below it. Two men watching it, yours, dressed as bandits. They're bad at it. One of them whistles."

Dresch allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "And the courier?"

"August Ashgrove. Carrying a child under two. Carrying paper I can't read but can describe." She described it: the seal pressed in green wax, the fold pattern, the weight. She had held it exactly once, on the barge, when August slept and she'd told herself it was prudence. It had been curiosity, and curiosity was just greed wearing its Sunday clothes.

"And the child itself," Dresch said. "Describe the child."

Marta thought about Noor. Noor was learning to walk along the cellar wall in their hiding place, one hand flat against the stone, her other fist out for balance like a tiny drunk aristocrat. When she fell she didn't cry; she laughed, astonished, as though gravity were a game someone had invented for her benefit. Last week she had discovered her own reflection in the bottom of a copper pot and conducted a lengthy negotiation with it, ending in a treaty of slobbery kisses.

"A baby," Marta said. "They all look alike at that age."

Dresch's coin-colored eyes came up. For a moment she felt the old cold slide down her spine — the feeling of a door she hadn't counted — but then he nodded, satisfied, and wrote something brief, and she understood that to him the child was not a person yet, only cargo, and cargo did not need describing.

That should have comforted her.

"The price," she said. "My brother. Tomas Vale. Camp at Krevna, work detail, number—" she recited it, having recited it nightly into her pillow for a month until the numbers lived behind her teeth. "He walks out within the week. Alive. Unmarked."

"He walks out," Dresch agreed. He signed. The pen scratched like a mouse in a wall. "Provided your information proves accurate, and provided the operation concludes cleanly. If there are complications—"

"There won't be."

"—the arrangement dissolves." He blotted the page with a small sand shaker, delicately, as if seasoning a dish. "You understand these are wartime terms. Everyone understands things, in wartime. It's practically our national industry now."

"When?" Marta asked.

"Tomorrow night. There's a moon problem, you see. We want the dark." He stood, buttoned his coat with those soft hands. At the door he paused, and offered her the smile of a man doing her an enormous favor. "For what it's worth, madam, you've done the republic a service. History will be kinder to you than you expect."

"History," Marta said, "has never once bought me a drink."

He laughed, genuinely, and went out into the snow.

* * *

She walked back through Vellin the long way, past the shrine with its shattered saint, past the baker who charged her double and winked while he did it because they both knew the price of bread was theater. Snow came down in fat, indifferent flakes. Somewhere a dog was singing to the artillery, which had grown distant enough lately to become almost musical, like thunder that had taken up an instrument.

Tomas. She let herself think the name properly now, the way you let yourself look directly at a fire after a day of not looking. Tomas, who at fourteen had carried her across the flooded Sereva on his back, laughing, telling her the river couldn't drown anything as stubborn as a Vale. Tomas, who had stayed behind in the retreat to pull a wounded boy off a road, and been taken, and written her one letter in eight months in handwriting gone thin as thread: *Sister. Still stubborn. Don't do anything stupid.*

Well. She was about to do the stupidest thing of her career, and she was doing it for him, and she found — walking through the falling snow with the captain's receipt folded against her heart — that she could not make herself feel guilty, and that frightened her more than guilt would have.

One courier. One child. One piece of paper.

Against one brother.

The arithmetic was clean. She had spent thirty years keeping her arithmetic clean. A smuggler who wept over freight died poor, and a smuggler who died poor died anyway.

So why did her feet keep stopping?

* * *

She got back to the cellar before dusk. August had the fire down to embers, the way he always did — a man trained to be invisible even in a room no one was searching — and Noor was asleep in the wool-lined satchel that had been her first cradle, her mouth open, one hand curled around the strap as though holding on even in dreams.

"She ate everything," August whispered. "Including half a candle stub. I got most of it back."

"Most of it?"

"There was a negotiation."

Marta huffed something that was almost a laugh and hung up her coat. And that was when she saw it, laid on the crate beside the bedroll: a folded paper, official cream, the kind of stationery that gets people shot, that August must have lifted off a messenger outside the bakery that morning — she'd watched him do it without understanding why — and opened with a knife.

She should not have read it.

She read it.

*Operation LANTERN DOWN. Subject: courier A. Ashgrove, deserter. Secondary subject: infant female, approx. 14 months, presumed Voss line. Orders follow. On confirmation of subjects at target, take courier alive for interrogation. Infant: no prisoner status applies. Eliminate on site. Silence preferable; report will reflect recovery attempt, tragic outcome, etc.*

*Eliminate on site.*

Marta stood very still in the doorway with the snow melting off her shoulders and dripping onto the floor, and the words rearranged themselves in her head with terrible patience, the way words do when they want to be understood completely.

Not cargo. Not freight. Not a nameless baby who all looked alike at that age.

*No prisoner status applies.*

She thought of Dresch's soft hands shaking sand onto wet ink. She thought of the moon problem. She thought of Tomas at fourteen, laughing in the river, saying the water couldn't drown anything as stubborn as a Vale — and here was the trap of it, the beautiful ugly symmetry: she had sold a child to buy a man who once refused to leave a child on a road.

The arithmetic was still clean. That was the horror. Every column balanced. Brother gained, stranger lost, ledger closed. Thirty years of her father's teaching, and it all worked, and none of it meant anything anymore, because somewhere between the bakery and the cellar a fourteen-month-old girl had learned to walk along a wall with her fist out for balance, and laughed when gravity caught her, and negotiated over a candle stub, and fallen asleep holding on to a strap as if she already knew the world required it.

Doors, her father said. Every face is a door.

Noor's face was a door, and Marta had walked past it every day for weeks pretending it was a wall.

"Marta?" August was looking at her now. He was good at reading people, the way couriers are — it's the whole trade — and whatever he saw made him move slightly, casually, so that his body stood between her and the satchel. "You're dripping."

"Yes," she said. "I am."

She crossed the room, took the paper off the crate, folded it along its creases, and put it in the fire. The embers took it slowly, reluctantly. August watched her do it and said nothing, which was its own conversation.

"Tomas," she said. "My brother. I traded you for my brother. Tonight, tomorrow night — there are men coming to the orchard, and I drew them the map myself, and I'm telling you because—" Her voice did something she hadn't authorized. She stopped, started again, flatter. "Because the orders say the child doesn't get taken. The child gets ended. And I find I have a problem with that."

August was quiet for a long moment. The fire ticked. In the satchel, Noor sighed in her sleep, a small sound of complete trust, and it went through Marta like a bayonet going through bread.

"You could not tell me," August said finally. "Take your payment and walk."

"Yes."

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because I'm a smuggler, not a murderer, and apparently at my age I'm still learning the difference." She pulled her coat back off its peg. "And because I know how Dresch thinks. He wants you taken alive for interrogation, which means the outer cordon closes slow and the inner net closes fast. But I sold him the orchard, not the hollow. There's a drainage cut under the north fence, old smuggler's run, my father's, actually, and if you go tonight instead of tomorrow—"

"Noor wakes at dawn. She'll cry. They'll hear."

"So you go now, in the dark, and I stay, and when they come asking after their deserter I give them a route south, loud, detailed, wrong. I'm good at wrong routes. I once sent a customs inspector chasing a phantom barge for eleven days. He wrote poetry about it afterward." She was lacing her boots as she talked, fast, because if she slowed down she would stop. "Then I come north after you, and we cross the mountains together, and you never mention any of this again."

"Marta." August's voice was careful. "If you stay and they catch you with lies in your mouth—"

"They won't catch me. I count doors, courier. It's the one thing I've never gotten wrong."

It was, she would think later, the first lie she ever told herself out loud.

* * *

They left within the hour. August packed in silence, quick hands, courier's economy — wool, bread, the green-sealed document wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. He woke Noor just enough to feed her, and she blinked at the candle flame with the solemnity of a magistrate, and reached out one hand toward Marta, and made a sound like a question.

Something in Marta's chest broke quietly, like ice on a river, in a way she suspected would not freeze over again.

"She likes you," August said.

"Everyone likes me. It's a professional hazard." She took the small warm fist, shook it gravely, once. "Goodbye, little magistrate. Walk far. Fall down laughing."

At the cellar door August paused. "Marta. What you're doing—"

"Is stupid. Yes. My brother warned me about it in writing." She pushed him gently out into the snow. "Go. North fence. Count five fence posts from the dead oak, then dig where the ground sounds hollow. Tell the ground I sent you."

He went. She listened to his footsteps fade, two sets really, his and the small one carried against his chest, until the snow swallowed even the memory of them.

Then she climbed the stairs, put another log on the tavern's public fire, poured herself the Kettle & Crow's worst brandy, and waited to be found.

* * *

They came at midnight. Of course they did. Dresch was punctual the way small men are punctual, needing the world to keep its appointments. Four soldiers in the common room, snow steaming off their shoulders, and Dresch with his soft hands folded, smiling his favor-doing smile.

"Madam Vale. You'll accompany us."

"Of course." She set down the brandy. "Though I warn you, I'm no use to anyone before sunrise. Ask anyone."

Outside, the snow had stopped. The moon — the moon problem, the moon they'd wanted dark — slid out from a torn cloud and lit the street blue-white and empty, and Marta looked north, toward Stelka Hollow, toward the drainage cut and the dead oak and the man counting fence posts with a sleeping child against his chest, and she gave Dresch exactly what she had promised herself she would give: a map drawn aloud, confident, precise, entirely false. South road. A ferryman named Brac. A barn with a red roof, third past the mill, you can't miss it, everyone misses it, that's rather the point of barns.

Dresch wrote it all down. Then, at the edge of town, where the road forked, one of the soldiers handed him a second paper — fresh, sealed, brought out from the orchard position — and he read it by lantern light while Marta stood in the cold doing sums she couldn't finish.

"Your deserter wasn't at the orchard," Dresch said mildly. "Yet." He folded the paper away. "The standing order stands regardless. If the infant is located in company with the courier, no prisoner status applies. You did mention, madam, that you might know where he'd go next."

"I mentioned a red barn."

"You mentioned many things." His coin-colored eyes caught the lantern light. "We'll want them all. Starting tonight, continuing for as long as it takes. And your brother's release, I'm afraid, remains contingent on outcomes."

There it was. The whole architecture of it, visible at last: she had never been buying Tomas. She had been renting him, week to week, on her good behavior, and the rent had just gone up, and the currency was now a child's life, and the landlord was smiling at her with his soft hands folded like a man at prayer.

The soldier took her arm. Not roughly. That was somehow worse.

They marched her back through the blue-white empty street, past the shuttered bakery, past the shrine with its shattered saint, and the moon slid free of the cloud entirely and lay down on the northern hills like a lamp set in a window — and Marta, thirty years a counter of doors, saw with perfect clarity the one door she had left uncounted: the door marked *what you'll do when the arithmetic stops working.*

It was opening now. It had been opening since a baby laughed at the bottom of the world.

"Captain," she said pleasantly, as they passed the shrine, "you'll want to search the well there. Couriers love wells."

Dresch waved a man toward it — and in the half-second his attention split, in the exact seam between his orders and his obedience, Marta Vale tore her arm free and ran.

North. Into the snow. Toward the hills, toward the lantern light lying pale on the ridgeline, toward a fence and a dead oak and a hollow-sounding patch of ground, arms pumping, breath tearing, fifty-two years old and running like the river girl she had been, while behind her the shouting started and a whistle split the night and boots hammered the frozen road.

She had sold a child to save a brother.

She intended to be extremely inconvenient about it.
