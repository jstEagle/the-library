## 15. Running the Stakes

The lantern hung in the orchard like a small yellow accusation.

August had seen it from the ridge at dusk — a single flame swinging in the bare branches of what had once been somebody's pride, a cider orchard by the look of the stumps and the one gnarled survivor still standing near the farmhouse. The agreed signal. *When the lantern burns, come down through the north rows.* Voss's handwriting had said as much, in that careful code of his that disguised itself as a recipe for pickled plums.

What the note had not said was that the farm below would be crawling with republican agents like ants on a dropped sweet.

"Three by the barn," Marta whispered beside him. She lay flat in the frozen furrow, her breath making little ghosts above her head. "One on the porch. Two more walking the fence line. And there—" she nodded toward a dark shape at the tree line, "—that's a transport wagon. They're not waiting for you, August. They're setting a table."

"They knew the signal."

"They knew the signal." She said it without drama, the way she said everything, as though the world were a ledger she'd long since balanced and found disappointing. "Which means someone talked. Which means either Voss is careless or Voss is bait."

"He's not."

"You've known him how long?"

"Too long," August admitted. Below them, one of the soldiers stamped his boots and laughed at something. The sound carried up the hill with terrible clarity, ordinary and obscene. Somewhere down there, behind that lit kitchen window, was the family he'd crossed a country to reach. And somewhere between here and there were seven men with rifles who very much wanted him not to make it.

Noor stirred against his chest, inside the wool-lined satchel, and made the small grumbling noise she made when a dream displeased her. August's whole body clenched around the sound. He'd spent four months learning every noise she made — the hungry cry, the wet-happy gurgle, the furious squeak when her own fist surprised her — and now every one of those noises sounded like a death sentence being read aloud in a quiet field.

"We go around," he whispered. "West fence. There's a gap in the hedge — I saw it from the ridge."

"There's a gap because they want there to be a gap."

"Then we find another one."

"And if we don't?"

He didn't answer. Marta turned her head and looked at him in the starlight, and he watched her do the arithmetic she was always doing — doors, exits, prices, odds — and arrive at a number she didn't like.

"I'm going to tell you something," she said, "and you're going to ruin it by arguing, so let me finish first." She shifted onto her elbows. "They're watching the house and they're watching the approaches. That's two things. A man can only watch so much before his eyes get tired and his feet get cold and he starts thinking about his supper. What they are *not* expecting, because nobody has ever been fool enough to do it, is for the courier they're hunting to walk out into the open field in front of all of them and make himself interesting."

"Marta—"

"I told you not to argue."

"—you are seventy paces from seven rifles—"

"Eighty-five. I counted." Her voice stayed level, but her hand found his sleeve and squeezed it once, hard, which from Marta Vale was roughly equivalent to another man weeping. "You go west, low, through the hedge, along the irrigation ditch to the cellar door your plum-recipe friend described. I go east, loud, and give them a story worth chasing. By the time they work out I'm not carrying anything but my bad disposition, you'll be through the wall."

"No."

"Yes."

"No. Not this way. Not—" He stopped, because the sentence he was about to say was *not after everything*, and everything was four months of shared bread and shared danger and a woman who had taught a foundling child to clap her hands, and he could not say it aloud without admitting what it would cost to lose her, and saying that felt like handing the war a weapon.

Marta smiled. It was a real smile, which frightened him more than the rifles.

"When my brother was small," she said, "he used to hide behind me during thunderstorms and insist he was protecting *me*. Big lummox, terrified of thunder, standing behind a girl half his size going 'I've got you, Marta, don't worry.' And I let him believe it, because it helped him, and because it cost me nothing to be brave in front of a six-year-old." She sat up. "This is the same, only the thunder shoots back."

"The price," August said desperately. "Your brother. The camp. If you're captured—"

"Then I'll be interrogated by men whose questions I've been rehearsing answers to since I was fourteen, and my brother will still be alive because you'll be delivering that document, and one of us will have gotten something done tonight." She was already moving, checking her boot knife, pulling her scarf up over her chin. "Besides. You forget who you're talking to. I have lied to generals, customs men, three husbands, and a bishop. Seven farm boys with rifles are a Tuesday."

"Marta."

She paused. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the cold came up through the ground like water, and far away a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"When you get through that wall," she said quietly, "and you put that child in front of Ilan Voss, and he asks you what it cost—" she shrugged. "Tell him it cost nothing. Tell him it was a Tuesday. Let him carry that instead of you."

Then she was gone, sliding down through the frozen beet rows toward the eastern field like water finding its level, and August had perhaps forty seconds to hate her, admire her, and obey her, in that order.

* * *

He went west.

He went the way she'd told him, low along the hedge line, satchel pressed to his chest, moving in the stuttering rhythm couriers were taught — ten steps, listen, ten steps, listen — except that his listening was ruined entirely by the pounding of his own heart, which had opinions.

Behind him, to the east, a shot cracked the night.

Then another. Then shouting — Marta's voice, high and furious and utterly convincing: *"Don't you touch me! Don't you know who I am? My husband will have your commission, you illiterate ditch-diggers—"*

God, she was good. She sounded like exactly what she wanted them to see: a smuggler's wife caught out after curfew, indignant, dangerous, *valuable*. Boots crunched through frost. Lanterns swung east. The whole careful trap leaned after her like a drunk after a coin.

And then, because the universe had never once given August Ashgrove a clean exit, Noor woke up.

Not crying. Worse. She made the curious sound, the *oh* sound, the one she saved for new things — new dogs, new snow, new faces. He felt her shift against his ribs, felt one small hand emerge from the satchel flap and grab, with astonishing precision, a fistful of his collar, and she said, clearly, delightedly, at full baby volume:

*"Da!"*

Every soldier within half a mile heard it. August was certain of it. He flattened into the ditch as a voice to the south called out — *"There! In the field! Movement!"* — and a rifle shot kicked frozen dirt six inches from his ear, and the night came apart into running shapes and swinging lights and orders shouted in three overlapping registers of panic.

He ran.

He ran the way he hadn't run since basic training, hunched, satchel clamped, breath tearing white ribbons out of his mouth. Behind him the eastern commotion redoubled — Marta, bless her terrifying heart, had begun screaming about her husband's commission again, louder, drawing eyes back to herself even now — and August understood with a clarity that hurt worse than any bullet what she was doing, and what it would cost, and that she knew both things perfectly well when she started.

*It was a Tuesday,* he told himself, and ran faster.

The irrigation ditch took him along the western wall, black water under thin ice cracking like knuckles under his boots. Twice he went down. Twice he got up, shielding Noor with his forearms, whispering nonsense — *almost there, little one, hold on, hold on to Da* — the word escaping him again, unforbidden now, unashamed. There were no rules left to break. He'd burned them all in a stove in Chapter One of this disaster, back when he'd still believed he might survive it with his soul intact.

The cellar door was where Voss's coded recipe said it would be: third stone past the dead pear tree, iron ring, rusted hinge. It opened with a groan that seemed loud enough to wake the province, and August fell down the steps into darkness and straw and the smell of apples rotting sweet, and pulled the door shut behind him, and lay gasping on the earth floor while the war went on overhead.

Above, muffled by beams and frozen ground: boots. Voices. A lantern beam sweeping the gaps between boards, striping the ceiling gold and black, gold and black.

A voice directly overhead said, "Check the orchard. The old man's family's got to be close — the courier wouldn't come all this way for nothing."

Another voice, younger, uncertain: "And the woman?"

"What about her?"

"She keeps asking for a priest. Says she won't talk till she gets one."

A laugh. "She can ask for a parade. Colonel wants her moved at first light. Command says she's worth more than the courier anyway — smuggler network, contacts across the whole river country." The beam swept past. Gold, black, gold. "Courier's just paper. She's infrastructure."

Their footsteps faded toward the farmhouse. August lay in the dark with Noor against his chest, her heart tapping his like a second, smaller clock, and understood that Marta had known this too. Had known her capture would buy more than minutes. Had walked into eight rifles carrying nothing but her bad disposition and a lifetime of rehearsed lies, because she had already decided, somewhere in that ledger-keeping head, exactly what she was worth and exactly what she'd spend it on.

He did not cry. Couriers don't. But he lay in the apple-dark for a long time, breathing, until Noor patted his cheek with one hand — pat, pat, pat, her solemn rhythm, the way she comforted him when he couldn't sleep on the barge, the way no one had ever comforted him in his life before her — and then he got up, because momentum was all that was left, and momentum was a kind of promise.

The passage behind the cellar wall was narrow and dry and smelled of a decade of disuse. At its end, according to Voss's instructions disguised as a recipe (*proceed to the final jar; do not open before the last page*), it climbed toward the orchard's far corner, where the last surviving tree stood over a gate that led out of the farm and up into the hills beyond the mountains' foothills.

He was halfway up the passage when he heard, distantly, carried on the wind through some crack in the earth itself, the sound of the transport wagon leaving — wheels in frozen ruts, and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, a woman's voice reciting something rhythmic and profane, giving the soldiers what-for the entire way.

Still fighting. Still lying. Already building the false routes she'd feed them like poisoned honey.

August pressed his forehead against the cold stone and made a promise to no one in particular: *I will come back for you. When the girl is safe and the document is delivered, I will come back, and we will settle accounts, and you will yell at me for an hour about how close that rifle shot came, and I will let you.*

It was a foolish promise. He knew it was a foolish promise. Everything he'd done since the doorstep had been foolish by the republic's arithmetic, and the republic was dead and its arithmetic with it.

He climbed.

The gate at the top opened onto a hillside silvered with frost, and beyond it, faint in the starlight, the line of mountains Voss had written about — *beyond them, the last jar; tell them the plums came through* — and August stood for one moment in the open with the child awake against his chest and the document dry against his skin, the last honest paper in Vessany, and looked back once at the burning lantern in the orchard.

Soldiers were taking it down.

He turned uphill, into the dark, and began to climb, one step and then another, carrying everything.

* * *

They walked most of the night, because stopping meant freezing and freezing meant failing her.

Toward dawn, in the shelter of a rock overhang with frost ferns climbing the stone, August fed Noor the last of the milk-bread from his coat pocket and told her, in a whisper, about the barge. About the river. About the day she'd laughed for the first time, at nothing, at a heron, at the sheer idiocy of existence, and how he'd broken a rule older than the republic by naming her right there in the sunlight.

"You remember none of this," he told her gravely. She stared up at him with Voss's gray eyes — though he'd never tell Voss that, never give the man the satisfaction — and chewed her bread with the focused intensity of a scholar. "That's the trouble with babies. All the best material, wasted on you." He touched the satchel strap where the document rode flat against his chest. "But someday someone's going to ask what happened, and I'm going to tell them about a woman named Marta Vale who charged eight soldiers armed only with a fictional husband, and you are going to sit still for the whole story. That's the price of the bread."

Noor offered him a piece of soggy bread. He ate it. It was terrible. He would have refused anything better.

Far behind them, down in the valley, the farmhouse smoke rose straight into a windless morning sky, and the road below wound east toward whatever cell they were taking her to, and August stood at the mouth of the overhang watching it until the light hurt, memorizing the direction, the distance, the lie he'd keep.

Then he picked up his daughter and turned to face the mountains, and began, step by step, to run.
