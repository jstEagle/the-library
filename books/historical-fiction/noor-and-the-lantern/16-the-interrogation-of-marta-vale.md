## 16. The Interrogation of Marta Vale

They took her boots first.

Marta noticed that, in the way she noticed everything—professionally, cataloguing, filing. Not the coat, which was worth more and would have kept them warm. Not the knife hidden in the seam, which they hadn't found yet and which she intended to keep not-found. Just the boots. Someone in this building had cold feet, or wanted her to.

The room was a former schoolhouse office, by the look of it. Chalk dust still ghosted the walls where a slate had hung. Someone had pinned a map of the eastern provinces over what was probably a child's drawing of a sun with a smiling face, and the corner of that sun peeked out from behind the map like a witness afraid to speak.

The man across the desk had a kind face. That was the first thing she noted about him too, because it was the thing he'd been issued along with his uniform. Kind faces were standard equipment for interrogators now; the war had learned that fear made people stupid, and stupid people lied badly. A kind face got you better lies, and better lies were easier to catch.

"You're cold," he said.

"I'm fine."

"We can get your boots."

"I've gone barefoot through worse than this floor." True. She let him have it, small truths being the currency that bought credit for large lies later. "Though I'll admit the hospitality leaves something to be desired. No tea. In Ashfield they at least offered tea before they shot at you."

Something flickered behind his eyes—the kind face recalculating. Good. Let him think she was frightened enough to chatter. Frightened people chattered. So did clever people pretending to be frightened, but a good interrogator knew the difference, and Marta's whole life now depended on him being merely adequate.

"Name," he said.

"Marta Vale."

"Occupation."

"Seamstress. Mender of coats, hems, hearts. The last one's free of charge, though I can't say my success rate is high."

He wrote it down. She watched him write *seamstress* with the particular patience of a man who did not believe a single word and was prepared to spend all week demonstrating it.

"Where were you on the night of the ninth?"

"The ninth," Marta repeated, as if consulting an internal calendar. She had one. It was excellent. "Let me think. The ninth was market day in Kolvary, no—the tenth was market day. The ninth I was repairing a tarpaulin for Widow Hessa, whose roof leaks worse than the republic's secrets, if you'll forgive me saying so in present company."

"I'll forgive most things," he said mildly, "if you tell me about the courier."

*There it is.* Sooner than she'd hoped. She'd budgeted two days of stalling and they'd spent only four hours. That meant someone above this man was impatient, which meant someone above this man was afraid, which meant August and the girl had made better time than she'd dared expect.

"What courier?"

"The one you were seen with. Tall man. Tired eyes. Carrying a baby."

Marta laughed. It surprised even her, how easily it came—not performed, exactly, but assembled quickly from genuine parts, because there *was* something funny in it. "A baby? You think I was running contraband infants? Sir, do you know what babies cost to feed? If I could turn a profit on babies I'd be living in Kolvary's finest hotel, not mending tarps for widows."

"The man, then."

She let the laugh die slowly, reluctantly, like a candle going out. "I help travelers," she said. "It's what I do. I move people through places they shouldn't be. Sometimes their messages. Once, memorably, a crate of pigeons, though that ended badly for everyone including the pigeons. If a tall man with tired eyes paid me, then he was a traveler and I was paid, and whatever he carried is his affair, not mine."

"And where did you take him?"

"Nowhere. He asked directions."

"To where?"

"Away," said Marta, and smiled her best smile, the one she used on customs men and border guards and men who thought a smile was a door left open.

* * *

They gave her tea after that, which she counted as progress, and a stool instead of the chair with the straight back, which she counted as strategy—comfort loosened tongues, they believed. They were right, generally. Marta had watched it work on lesser smugglers, men who talked themselves into nooses one friendly cup at a time.

But Marta Vale had spent thirty years learning the geography of other people's questions, and she knew a truth: you cannot hold out forever by giving nothing. Give nothing long enough and they stop asking and start doing. The trick—the art, really, the craft she loved almost as much as sewing—was to give them so much that they drowned in it.

So when the kind-faced man came back in the evening, older somehow, wearier, rubbing his eyes like a schoolmaster grading terrible essays, she gave him a river.

"Sit down," she said, patting the opposite chair. "You look terrible. Ask me again about the courier, and this time I'll tell you everything, because frankly, sir, I've done the arithmetic, and I don't like my odds either way. All I want is to be treated fairly when this is over. I was a messenger once, years ago, for people whose names would interest you very much. I'm tired of carrying other people's wars."

It was beautiful, she thought, listening to herself. Every word true except its direction.

He sat. He poured the tea himself. And Marta told him about the courier—about the man with tired eyes—and she told him about routes.

She gave him the Salt Road through the Vhern Pass, detailed to the last toll station, complete with a bribe she'd allegedly paid a sergeant named Doss (poor Doss, who would indeed take bribes from anyone, and would spend a miserable month explaining himself). She gave him the ferry crossing at Old Brannick, the miller who forged papers, the widow who hid fugitives in her hay loft—all real, all functional, all currently useless, because she'd burned those contacts out months ago or moved them or warned them. She gave him a safehouse in Tessel that had been raided in autumn and now housed nothing but mice and one extremely indignant goat.

She recited routes the way a seamstress recites stitches, and here was the secret stitch inside all the others: every route she gave them was *true*. Verifiable. They could check each one and find the stones turned just as she described, the payments made, the beds slept in—and none of it leading anywhere, because the routes were old ones, dead ones, the shed skins of her trade. By the time their officers had confirmed three of them, they'd believe the rest without checking, and the rest pointed north, away from the orchard, away from the mountains, away from a lantern hanging in a dead tree.

"North," the interrogator said at last, making a mark on his map. "Through the high pass at Sorrel Ford."

"That's the road I put him on," Marta agreed, sipping her tea. "God help him. It's a brutal climb this time of year."

"And the child?"

"What child?" she said pleasantly, and when his eyes lifted to hers, she added, with the weary honesty of the converted: "He didn't travel with any child. You said yourself—a courier travels light. That's the whole virtue of them, isn't it? Weightlessness. I heard some officer say that once at a checkpoint, lecturing his men. A courier's virtue is weightlessness." She shrugged. "Whatever your tall man was carrying, it wasn't a baby. Babies cry. I'd know. I heard him plenty on the barge, and there wasn't a baby aboard, I promise you."

*There.* She felt the lie land and settle, felt him file it. Because it was half-true—there *had* been crying on the barge, hers, privately, into the river water, the night she'd decided the price she'd ask—and half-truths were the strongest timbers in any false house.

* * *

They questioned her for three days.

On the second day a harder man came, with a colder face, and asked different questions in a flatter voice, the kind of voice that had stopped believing in tea. He asked about General Ilan Voss. He asked whether she had ever carried Voss's messages. He laid photographs on the table like cards: Voss in dress uniform, Voss at a tribunal, Voss's wife, and—last, placed gently, deliberately—a photograph of a farmhouse in the hill country, burned years ago, its orchard black.

"Do you know this place?"

"No," said Marta, and it was almost true. She knew a place like it. Everyone from the hills knew a hundred places like it. Grief had standardized its architecture across the whole country; that was the republic's great achievement, uniformity.

"The courier was headed somewhere specific," the cold man said. "Someone is waiting for what he carries. Where?"

"If I knew," Marta said, "I'd sell it to you. I'm not sentimental. Ask anyone."

And that, God forgive her, was also nearly true, because three days ago she *had* sold something—had traded a man's location for her brother's name on a prisoner exchange list, had walked out into the snow knowing exactly what she'd bought and what she'd spent, and had gotten perhaps forty steps before she'd seen the orders in the lieutenant's hand, the word written plainly in the margin beside the description of a child: *no witnesses.*

Forty steps. She'd counted them. Then she'd run back through the snow shouting that she'd made a mistake, that the courier was armed, that he'd gone east toward the river, anything, anything, and when that hadn't worked she'd done the only thing left, which was to stand in the soldiers' path screaming until they arrested her too—which was, she reflected, the most expensive purchase of her entire career, and the only one she'd never once regretted.

So now she sat in a cold schoolhouse and sold them rivers of lies, and the strange thing, the thing she'd remember afterward, was how much it felt like love. Not the tender kind. The kind that hauls freight. The kind that stays up all night mending a stranger's coat because morning is coming and morning will be cold. Defiance, it turned out, was just love wearing work clothes.

* * *

On the third night the kind-faced man came alone.

The lamp was low. Outside, snow ticked against the shuttered window, patient as a clock. He set two cups down and poured, and for a while neither of them spoke, and Marta understood that something in him had shifted—he'd believed her. Or near enough. Three days of verified routes will do that; truth is seductive even when it's counterfeit, because checking it feels like work accomplished.

"They found tracks going north," he said quietly. "At Sorrel Ford. Old ones, but—" He stopped himself, as if finishing the sentence might count as a confession. "You understand I have to report what I find."

"Of course," said Marta. "Report the ford. Report the pass. Report Sergeant Doss and his flexible morals. Report the goat in Tessel, if it makes your report fuller." She wrapped her hands around the cup, letting its warmth lie to her fingers. "You seem like a decent man in a rotten machine. So let me give you one more piece of trade, free of charge, and you can decide what to do with it."

He waited.

"When this war ends—and it's ending, we both smell it—there will be proclamations. Amnesty papers fluttering off every fence post like autumn leaves. And the men who gave the orders, the ones who write *no witnesses* in margins—they'll be the ones signing those proclamations. Not answering for them. Signing them." She looked up at him, and let him see her, all of her, unmasked for the first time in three days. "You'll sleep better for the rest of your life if tonight, in your report, you are a little bit slow. A little bit thorough. Men who are thorough get promoted, yes. But men who are slow sometimes get to live with themselves. I hear that's worth something."

The kind face did something complicated. He gathered his papers. At the door he paused, back to her.

"The woman in the photograph," he said. "Voss's wife. You're sure you know nothing?"

"I mend coats," Marta said. "I don't mend countries. Ask me about hemming."

He left. The lock turned. Snow ticked at the window.

Marta sat in the dark and allowed herself, for exactly one minute, to feel the full weight of what she'd done—the brother saved, the child hunted, the routes scattered north like false stars, and two people somewhere ahead of her, a tired man and a laughing baby, walking toward a door she would never see. She'd given them everything she had left: time. Every hour these fools spent confirming Sergeant Doss's bribes and chasing the ghost of the Sorrel Ford pass was an hour bought with her own skin, and she meant to make them spend slowly.

Then she got to work. There was a loose board under the stool, and under the board, still undiscovered after three days, a knife. And beyond the knife, beyond the wall, beyond the snow—a guard rotation she'd been memorizing since the first night, four men, changing at the half-hour, one of them young and homesick and prone to humming when he thought no one listened.

Marta Vale had been moving people through places they shouldn't be for thirty years.

Tonight, for the first time, the person she needed to move was herself—south, through the snow, toward a ruined orchard and a signal fire she'd promised herself she'd light if she got free, so that anyone watching from the hills would know: the way is clear. Go. Be quick. Raise the child, and raise her kindly, and tell her someday, if telling is ever safe, about the woman who counts doors—who counted them all her life for money, and gave away the last count for love.

She worked the knife free of the seam.

Outside, the sentry began, softly, to hum.
