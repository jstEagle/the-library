## 19. What Cannot Be Carried

The knock came at dusk, and it was wrong.

August had spent eleven days in the farmhouse learning its sounds the way a blind man learns a room: the settling groan of the roof beams when frost gripped them, the tick of the stove iron cooling, Voss pacing upstairs with Noor on his hip in that slow metronome of a man memorizing something he expected to lose. August knew which floorboards spoke and which kept silent. He knew the difference between a branch scraping shingles and a boot on the porch.

This was a boot. Three boots. And then a fist on the door with the flat, patient rhythm of men who did not need to hurry because they already owned everything around them.

"Voss." He said it quietly, from the kitchen doorway, one hand already moving Noor's wool blanket off the bench where she'd been sleeping.

Voss was at the table with the last of Marta's maps spread out before him, and he didn't look up right away. That was the thing about Ilan Voss that ten years of disgrace hadn't touched: he took an extra second before every decision, as if consulting some inner clerk who filed everything under *consequences*. Then he folded the map along its creases, precise as a man folding a letter he intended to answer later, and stood.

"How many?" he said.

"At least three. More waiting past the lane, I'd guess. They knocked like they have all night."

"They do have all night. They have the whole republic." Voss crossed to the window, parted the curtain a finger's width. The lantern light behind them must have shown them as shadows; outside, the snow held a blue darkness that made even the orchard's dead trees look like standing water. "Lieutenant Osten's men, by the formation. He was a corporal when I knew him. Chewed tobacco through staff briefings. Promoted, apparently."

"There's a back way—"

"There is a cow track through the birches that ends at a stream, yes. I walked it in summer when this farm belonged to my wife's aunt and the worst thing in these hills was a poacher." Voss turned. In the lantern light August could see how much older he'd gotten since the door had opened to him three nights ago — or perhaps only how much older he let himself look now that the pretending was over. "In winter it's a slide into black water carrying a child. You'd make half a mile before your hands stopped working."

"Then we go another way."

"August." Voss said his name the way he'd said it once in a garden ten years ago, over spilled wine and a promise neither of them had taken seriously enough — except that both of them, it turned out, had carried it like a stone in the boot ever since. "Sit down. Please. We have perhaps four minutes of being two men talking instead of a general and a fugitive, and I intend to spend them properly."

"I don't want to sit down."

"No. You wouldn't." Something moved across Voss's face that was almost a smile, worn thin as paper left out in rain. "You were always the most stubborn man in any room, and you hid it better than anyone. That's why they made you a courier. That's why you're still alive. Sit down anyway."

August sat. It felt like surrender, so he sat on the very edge of the chair, ready to stand.

Upstairs, faintly, Noor laughed at something in her sleep. Both men looked at the ceiling. Neither said anything for a moment, and the moment stretched long enough to hold everything: the garden, the burned documents, the road through Ashfield, Marta's face going away down the white lane with soldiers' hands on her arms, the whole ridiculous improbable chain of accidents that had put a chancellor's granddaughter asleep above their heads while the republic she'd been hidden from climbed the hill below.

"She has her mother's laugh," Voss said finally. "Elena laughed exactly like that. Once, in the palace garden, when the old chancellor — her father-in-law, God rot him — fell into the ornamental pond reaching for a grape. She laughed until she cried, and everyone pretended not to hear, because you weren't permitted to hear the heir's wife laughing too loudly." His jaw worked. "They shot her for laughing quieter than that, in the end. For less."

"The document said—"

"I know what the document said. I've read what the document said every night for three years, in memory, before there was a document." Voss pulled out the other chair and sat across from him, close, knees almost touching. Outside, the knocking came again, more insistent, and a voice called out — muffled by door and snow, but legible enough: *General Voss. We know you are within. Surrender the premises.* Premises. As if the house were the point. As if men marched through a civil war for real estate.

"They'll burn it regardless," Voss said. "The house. It's what Osten does; I've read his after-action reports. He burns and then writes paragraphs about regrettable necessity. So the question isn't whether this farm survives. The question is what walks out of it."

"You walk out of it. Back door, the stream, the birches—"

"With the child? On ice, in the dark, pursued by men with dogs?"

"With me carrying the child."

Voss looked at him for a long time. And August watched the idea arrive in his friend's face — watched it come the way weather comes over a plain, changing everything without raising its voice.

"No," Voss said.

"Yes."

"You can't outrun them either. You know that. One man alone might slip the cordon. A man with a baby in a satchel cannot."

"Then we both go, and we split the risk—"

"*No*." And now the general's voice was in it, the parade-ground voice that had once moved ten thousand men, and immediately Voss seemed ashamed of it, dragging it back like a dog on a chain. He lowered his head. When he spoke again it was quiet. "Forgive me. Old habit. Shouting at men I'm about to spend."

"That's exactly what you're doing," August said. "Spending me. Don't dress it up as arithmetic."

"It is arithmetic. It's the only honest kind left." Voss leaned forward. "Listen to me, and listen without arguing for once in your life, though I know the request is hopeless. They want me. Me specifically — alive, ideally, for the tribunal; the republic needs my treason displayed in a cage, it's worth ten victories to them. They do not particularly want you. You are officially dead already — I've seen the lists, your name has been struck through twice, which in this country is nearly a pension. If they find you here, they will detain you, interrogate you, and eventually release you into the amnesty that's coming, because the amnesty is coming, August, everyone in that army knows the capital will fall by spring, and the men who surrendered couriers will be forgotten by summer."

"And if they find her—"

"They will not find her." Voss's hand closed briefly on August's wrist, hard, a grip meant to transfer weight. "Because you will be beyond the mountains before they finish counting the silverware in my study. There is a family in Tarnow — the Ferencs, a midwife and her husband, they owe Elena their firstborn's life twice over. I wrote to them in autumn; the letter went by Marta's people, God rest her luck wherever it's landed. They are expecting a courier and a child. Not my name. Never my name. A name given freely, August — remember that phrase. Give her a new one. Let her grow up owing nothing to generals."

"And you'll be—" August heard his own voice go flat and ugly. "In a cage. Or against a wall."

"In a cage, probably. I'm worth more breathing; the tribunal wants to perform my repentance publicly. Perhaps they'll get it. Perhaps I'll give them such a performance." Voss smiled, and it was terrible, because under it August could see the young officer he'd known in peacetime, the one who'd drunk too much plum brandy and sworn a drunken oath about guarding what mattered. "Or perhaps I'll stand up in front of their judges and tell them precisely who ordered the massacre at Kellin Bridge, with dates and signatures, from memory, since someone was considerate enough to burn my copy. Let them decide what to do with a general who testifies. It should make excellent theater either way."

"They'll kill you."

"Eventually. Everything kills everyone; I merely get to choose the manner of my appointment." Voss stood, and began — absurdly, terribly — to tidy. He stacked Marta's maps. He straightened the chairs. He picked up Noor's dropped wooden horse from under the table and set it on the bench where she'd find it, and August understood that he was saying goodbye to objects because he couldn't yet say it to people. "I've done the sums, my friend. There is no version of tonight in which we all walk out. There is only the question of what gets carried forward and what gets left. That's the whole of war, really. That's the entire curriculum. I learned it late and badly."

"So stay and fight. Two rifles, the stairway—"

"—and a child sleeping twelve feet from the firing line. Yes. Splendid tactics." Voss shook his head. "I spent twenty years learning how to spend soldiers, August. I refuse to spend her. And I refuse to spend you uselessly. Which brings us to the part you're going to hate."

Outside, boots crunched in the snow, spreading — moving to flank the barn. A voice barked an order. Somewhere a dog complained, high and eager.

Voss took the folded map from the table and pushed it into August's coat pocket himself, firmly, like a father dressing a child against cold. "The stream path. Follow it east until the birches end, then north along the ridge — the ridge, not the valley, whatever the valley tempts you with. Two days to Tarnow if the weather holds, four if it doesn't. The Ferencs will feed you and ask no questions, which in Tarnow is the highest form of hospitality. From there, south, over the pass at Zeleny before the passes close. Beyond the mountains none of this exists. No republic, no Voss, no chancellor, no purge lists. Just a girl growing up."

"Ilan—"

"Let me finish, because if you interrupt I'll lose my nerve, and I am a vain man who would rather die composed." Voss's voice cracked slightly on *composed*, and he covered it by clearing his throat. "Ten years ago I made you a promise in a garden, and I have thought about that promise every day of a decade, mostly with contempt, because promises made by drunk men in peacetime are the cheapest currency there is. But you kept it. Do you understand? I declared myself a traitor, and the world agreed with me enthusiastically, and you — you, who owed me nothing but a bad bottle of wine and a sentimental evening — you burned your own pardon to keep a word." He stopped. His eyes were wet, and he let them be. "So the debt runs the other direction now, and I am paying it tonight, and you will let me pay it, because refusing a gift is just pride wearing generosity's coat."

"What's the gift?" August asked, though he already knew, and hated knowing.

"Myself. Loudly. At the front door." Voss was putting on his old uniform coat now — the one with the faded braid, the one he'd arrived in, disgraced and unmistakable. He buttoned it with steady hands. "Osten wants the famous traitor delivered to the capital in irons. He will pursue me to the ends of the earth if I run, because my capture is his career. But if I walk out that door with my hands visible and my name in my mouth — if I give him a clean, simple, glorious arrest — he'll take it. Men like Osten always take the bird in hand. And while they're binding my wrists and congratulating themselves and marching me back down the valley at ceremonial speed—"

"We go the other way."

"*You* go the other way." Voss corrected him gently. "And here is the price of the gift, August, the only condition, and I will haunt you if you break it: you do not stop. Whatever you hear behind you — and you may hear shots, though I doubt it, they need me breathing — you do not turn around. You climb. You cross. You deliver her. That is your whole remaining duty in this world, and it fits in a satchel, thank God, because you were never built for anything heavier."

From upstairs: small bare feet on boards. Then Noor appeared at the top of the stairs in her nightclothes, hair wild, the wooden horse — no, she'd found the spare button from August's coat, her treasure, her current obsession — clutched in one fist. She looked down at the two men and said, with the imperious clarity of the very young:

"Up. Pick up."

And both of them moved at once, and both stopped at once, and Voss laughed — actually laughed, a short helpless sound — and went up the stairs two at a time and gathered his daughter into his arms, and August stood in the kitchen and looked at the ceiling and tried to be anywhere else and failed completely.

* * *

She did not want to go into the satchel. This was new; she'd ridden in it for weeks without complaint, a warm loaf of bread and wool, but tonight she arched her back and pushed at his chest and said "Down, down" with gathering outrage, and August knelt on the frozen ground by the back step and reasoned with a person who owned eleven words, and lost.

It was Voss who solved it. He knelt beside them in the snow, heedless of his braid, and held out his arms, and said, "Noor. Look at Papa."

She looked. Children always look, when a voice takes that tone.

"Papa has to go on a journey," Voss said. "A long one. And you're going on a different journey, with August, who is the best carrier in the whole republic — the very best, I promise you, and Papa never breaks promises to carriers." His voice held level almost all the way through. "And one day, when you're big, you'll understand all of it, and I will hear about it from wherever I am. But tonight you ride in the bag like a good soldier, because soldiers do the hard thing, and you, my darling, are the bravest soldier in this entire stupid war."

She considered this. She was fifteen months old and she weighed the offer with total seriousness, thumb in mouth, button in fist. Then she reached for August, and went into the satchel without another protest, and settled against his spine with a sigh that broke him somewhere beneath the ribs.

Voss stood there in the falling snow with his empty arms, and August saw him memorize it — the weight gone, the shape of her fading from his chest — and he saw the exact moment the general locked it away behind whatever door men like him kept for things that could not be carried further.

"Go," Voss said. "Now, before I invent a reason to keep you."

August got one sentence out. He'd meant to say something worthy; what came out was: "The lantern. In the orchard. Who hangs it next year?"

Voss smiled, and the snow caught in his gray hair, and he looked for a moment like the young man in the garden, the one the world hadn't yet taught to file everything under consequences.

"You do," he said. "That's an order."

Then he turned, squared his shoulders, and walked around the corner of the house toward the front door, and August heard him call out in the full parade voice, rich and carrying, *Osten! You tobacco-chewing corporal, stop skulking in my orchard and come take your prisoner like a gentleman!* — and there was a shout, and scrambling boots, and the sudden chorus of many voices all talking at once, triumphant, astonished, falling over each other with the great prize.

August went east through the birches with a child on his back and tears freezing in his beard, and he did not stop.

* * *

He stopped.

Not far — forty paces, maybe fifty, where the stream path bent north toward the ridge and the farmhouse vanished behind black trees. But the shots he'd braced for didn't come, and in their place came voices, faint and festive, and the rattle of wagon wheels, and he stood in the snow with Noor's breath warm against his neck and told himself to walk, and his legs declined the instruction.

"Idiot," he whispered to himself. "He told you. He *ordered* you."

"Pa," said Noor, into his ear. Her first attempt at it. Wrong, and perfect.

"Not yet," August said, which answered nothing, and promised everything, and was the most honest sentence he'd produced in years.

He made himself count to sixty. Then he made himself walk. The ridge rose ahead of him, white and endless, and beyond it the passes, and beyond the passes a world with no names in it. Behind him, receding, the republic arrested its greatest traitor with tremendous ceremony while the true treasure went north on the back of a dead man, humming tunelessly, holding a button.

At the tree line he paused once more — just once, he allowed himself, just three seconds — and looked back at the orange glow beginning to bloom through the orchard where they were burning the farm, exactly as Osten's reports always said he did, regrettable necessity, paragraph upon paragraph.

August watched the light for his three seconds. Then he turned his back on the country that had made him, and climbed.
