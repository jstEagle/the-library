## 20. After the Republic

The capital fell on a Tuesday, which August found fitting. Empires, in his experience, preferred to die on days when nobody was watching—market days, harvest days, the gray midweek hours when even history seemed to be looking elsewhere.

He heard it before he understood it: a sound like the city exhaling after holding its breath for three years. Then the bells—not the alarm bells he'd known so well he could sleep through them, but the old church bells, the ones that had been silent since the first winter, rung now by hands that didn't seem to care whose side they announced anything to.

August stood at the window of a room above a cooper's shop in the river district and watched the republic end.

It did not end with fire, as he had always privately assumed it would. It ended with flags coming down. All across the rooftops of Vessany's capital, men on ladders were unhooking the blue-and-silver from their poles, folding them with the strange tenderness people reserve for things they are embarrassed to have loved. Somewhere a woman was sweeping ash off her doorstep as though it were autumn leaves and not the debris of a government. A boy ran down the middle of the boulevard dragging a stick along the iron railings, making that rattling clatter children make when the world has briefly stopped telling them what to fear.

In his arms, Noor slept against his shoulder, her breath warm and damp through his collar. She had learned, in the months since the orchard, to sleep through anything. He hated that she'd learned it. He hated that he'd taught it to her without ever meaning to—the way you teach a child everything, mostly by accident, mostly by being what you are.

"Almost done," he murmured into her hair. "Almost, almost."

She sighed in her sleep, deeply, the sigh of someone with no documents to carry and no names to remember, and August felt the particular ache he had come to know as hope: painful, insistent, refusing entirely to leave him alone.

* * *

The amnesty proclamations began appearing by afternoon.

They were pasted to walls, nailed to doors, tucked under the wipers of abandoned delivery carts. August collected one from where it had fluttered against the cooper's fence, its corners already softening in the river damp, and read it standing in the cold with Noor perched on his hip like a sack of flour he happened to love.

BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL ASSEMBLY, it began, in type that had been set in a hurry—he could tell because the *R*s leaned drunkenly leftward—ALL PERSONS WHO TOOK UP ARMS OR RENDERED SERVICE IN THE LATE UNFORTUNABLE CONFLICT ARE HEREBY PARDONED, SAVE THOSE GUILTY OF CRIMES AGAINST THE PEOPLE, WHICH SHALL BE DETERMINED BY COMMISSION.

*Unfortunable.* Someone had actually printed the word *unfortunable*. August read it twice and then, absurdly, laughed—a short, cracked sound that woke Noor, who looked up at him with Voss's gray eyes and frowned her small frown until he showed her the paper, which she grabbed and attempted to eat, which struck him as the most sensible review any proclamation had yet received.

"Careful," he said, prying it from her fists. "It's not nourishing. Believe me. I carried enough of them to know."

But he folded it carefully anyway and put it inside his coat, over his heart, out of an old courier's habit he suspected would follow him to his grave. A document was a document. Even one that pardoned you for crimes nobody had bothered to name.

The trouble—and August had spent three years learning that there is always a trouble, usually wearing the uniform of a solution—was this: the amnesty forgave soldiers, smugglers, deserters, even, presumably, the occasional repentant chancellor. But it could not forgive a dead man. And August Ashgrove had been officially dead since the orchard, since Marta had sold his name to men who wrote it down in ledgers and then crossed it out again when he slipped their fingers. Somewhere in some clerk's careful hand, August Ashgrove had been marked *deceased, presumed*, those two words doing more work than any pardon ever could. Dead men were outside the law's arithmetic entirely. Dead men could not be forgiven because there was nothing left to forgive.

Which meant that when the war ended, everyone in Vessany walked free except the one man who had never wanted anything but to walk away.

"Noor," he said gravely, bouncing her once on his hip, "we appear to be the last criminals in the republic."

She blew a raspberry at him. He decided to take it as legal counsel.

* * *

The city in the days after the fall was a strange, gentle chaos, like a house after a party where everyone had cried a little and danced a little and nobody remembered exactly how the evening went. Soldiers of the late republic sat on curbs in their uniforms, boots off, rubbing their feet, waiting to find out if they were heroes or criminals or merely unemployed. Women traded bread for buttons and buttons for news. In the square by the customs house, somebody had erected a gallows and nobody had used it; instead a fruit seller had hung baskets of winter apples from the beam, and children climbed the scaffold to steal them while their mothers pretended not to see, and August thought: *there it is. There's the whole century, right there. We build a thing to kill each other with and within a week it's selling apples.*

He moved through it all carefully, hood up, beard grown in, walking with the slight stoop of an older, tireder man. Old habits. But here was the thing he discovered, the thing that kept catching him off guard like a stair that isn't where your foot expects it:

Nobody looked at him.

Not the patrols of the new provisional militia, green armbands over mismatched coats, too busy arguing about rations to study faces. Not the clerks hauling filing cabinets into the street to burn papers that had, only last month, been worth more than the cabinets. Not the crowds surging toward whatever bell was ringing or banner was rising or speech was being shouted from whatever steps were available. For ten years, August Ashgrove had been invisible by discipline—head down, eyes moving, weightless. Now he was invisible by neglect, and it turned out neglect was far easier to carry.

A courier's virtue is weightlessness, Sera had told him once, in another life, in an office that no longer existed because the office itself had been arrested. He had spent a decade becoming weightless. And now, walking through a capital that had forgotten itself overnight, carrying nothing but a child and a memory of a burned document, August realized he had finally achieved his profession's highest ideal at the precise moment his profession had stopped existing.

There was a joke in there somewhere. He was too tired to find it, but he was fairly sure Noor would have appreciated it. She appreciated everything indiscriminately, including door hinges and the concept of pigeons.

* * *

On the third morning, he went back to look at the safehouse.

He told himself it was practical—that he needed to know whether it had survived, whether anything of the network remained, whether there was money cached somewhere, contacts, a route south. This was perhaps thirty percent true. The other seventy percent was that a man does not simply walk away from the place where his life changed shape, even if the place is a rain-soaked doorway behind a chandler's shop that smells perpetually of tallow and wet stone.

The shop was boarded up. The doorway was exactly as he'd left it, which is to say ordinary, forgettable, the kind of threshold ten thousand people passed without seeing—which was, he supposed, the entire point of it, and the reason he had seen it forever.

He stood there longer than a practical man would have. Rain had not fallen in two days but the stones still held their damp, and the light came down thin and silver between the buildings, and August tried to remember himself as he'd been that night: a man with seven sealed documents and a route through three war zones and no face of his own anywhere in the world. A man who had opened a door and found a basket.

"You were very small," he told Noor, who was riding his shoulders now, gripping his hair with the confidence of an empress. "You were extremely loud. You had opinions about being left on doorsteps, and you expressed them at considerable length."

"Pigeon," said Noor, pointing at a pigeon, correctly.

"Yes. That's a pigeon." He paused. "That's the whole of your contribution to this moment, is it."

"Pigeon," she agreed, satisfied, and tugged his hair forward like a tiller, steering him back toward the river, and he went, because there was nowhere else with better pigeons.

* * *

He found Sera on the fifth day, or rather she found him, because some relationships do not respect the laws of mortality.

He was buying milk—or standing near milk, in the vicinity of milk, engaged in the delicate negotiation that post-war commerce required, which consisted of offering a button, having it refused, offering two buttons, and watching the milk seller's face perform a small tragedy—when a voice beside him said, "You're holding your elbow wrong."

He didn't turn. His heart performed a maneuver it had learned in the orchard, a fast drop-and-recover, and his hand did not move toward the knife because it wasn't there anymore, because he'd thrown it in a canal in the winter, because he was trying, badly, to become a different kind of animal.

"A courier holds his elbows in," the voice continued. "Close to the body. You're standing like a farmer. It's a good disguise. I almost believed it."

"Sera." He turned.

She looked terrible, which is to say she looked exactly like herself: thin as a blade, gray at the temples now, wrapped in a man's coat three sizes too large, her eyes doing that thing where they catalogued everything in the scene including, he was certain, the exact number of exits and the approximate value of the milk seller's cart.

"I attended your funeral," she said.

"I wasn't informed."

"There wasn't a body. There was a file with a black line through it and a note saying *lost with materials*. Which, in the old service, meant you'd either died gloriously or betrayed us comprehensively, and honestly, Crane, we gave odds." She glanced at Noor on his shoulders. "I put money on the betrayal."

"You always were a gambler."

"I was always a realist. It looks similar from the outside." Something moved through her face then, quick and quickly buried—the thing that lived under Sera's professionalism, the thing that had once, years ago, poured him tea when his hands shook after his first dead-drop gone wrong and said nothing about it, ever. "You're alive," she said, in the tone of a person confirming a figure in a ledger.

"So are you."

"Barely. Provisionally. Like the Assembly." She looked out at the square, at the gallows full of apples, at the flags coming down. "It's over, you know. Truly over. They're going to spend ten years arguing about what to call it, and meanwhile everyone will quietly plant things and sell things and marry the wrong people, and it'll be a country again. It's astonishing how little it takes. Just everyone getting tired at the same time."

"That's not what they'll write in the histories."

"The histories will be written by whoever can still hold a pen in June." She turned back to him, and now she looked at Noor properly, and August felt the old protective flinch, the tightening, the calculation of distances. But Sera only studied the child for a long moment, and then something in her jaw softened by perhaps a single degree, which for Sera was roughly equivalent to weeping. "Voss's granddaughter," she said quietly. "We figured it out too late to matter. The chancellor's men, I mean. By the time they understood what she was, she was a rumor in a satchel." She exhaled through her nose. "You know what the funny thing is? If you'd delivered her to us instead—to the service, I mean—we'd have made her a symbol. Put her on posters. *The Innocent Future of Vessany.* Little flag in her fist." She shook her head slowly. "You did the only unforgivable thing. You kept her human."

"Somebody had to."

"Mm." She reached into the enormous coat and produced, of all things, an envelope, worn soft at the corners. "Before I go. Since you're dead anyway, this can't hurt you. Your final performance review."

"Sera—"

"It's the file on August Ashgrove," she said. "All of it. Every route, every drop, every commendation and every reprimand, including the one from the year I don't talk about and neither do you. The commission is burning archives. By spring there won't be a single piece of paper proving you ever existed—which, frankly, is how you always behaved anyway, so consider it consistency." She pressed it into his hand and closed his fingers over it with hers, brief and dry and firm. "Burn it or keep it. A free man gets to choose, I'm told. First time for everything."

"Why?" he asked. "Why give me anything?"

Sera considered the question with the seriousness she brought to all questions, which was total. "Because you were the best courier I ever trained," she said at last, "and the service wasted you on paper. You should have been carrying people." She stepped back, pulling the huge coat around herself, already becoming a stranger in the crowd. "Elbows in, Crane."

And then she was gone, into the current of the square, a gray-haired woman buying nothing, going nowhere, and August stood holding the whole of his former life in an envelope while the milk seller, sensing weakness, raised the price by one button.

* * *

That night, in the room above the cooper's shop, he fed Noor the last of the barley porridge and then sat by the candle with the envelope on his knee for a long time.

Noor played on the floor with her treasures: a wooden spoon, three corks, and a scrap of blue ribbon she had appointed, by decree, to be both a bird and a blanket depending on need. She narrated her arrangements to herself in her serious half-language, and August listened the way he had once listened for footsteps on stairs—with his whole body, though now for joy instead of danger, which was the same posture with opposite weather.

Finally he opened the envelope.

His own handwriting looked back at him from the top page—young, confident, insufferable. *Received route 9, memorized, destroyed original.* His first report. Below it, years of him: the routes, the codes, the small neat record of a man who had made himself into a tool and called it virtue. He read until Noor climbed into his lap, heavy and warm and smelling of porridge, and fell asleep against his chest with the wooden spoon still in her fist.

Then he took the pages to the stove, a few at a time, and burned them, and watched the ink curl and the names he had carried lift off the paper as smoke and go up the flue into the air over a city that was, tonight, for the first time in years, not shooting at itself.

He saved the last page for last. It was the commendation—*for exceptional discretion in the matter of the Vessany–Corvane correspondence*, signed by a director long since fled abroad. August held it over the flame and paused.

"Not for me," he explained to Noor, who slept on. "I just want to watch it properly."

He let it burn all the way to his fingertips, feeling the heat approach, and dropped the last corner into the grate, and sat back, and discovered that his eyes were wet, and could not have said, if anyone had asked—and no one asked, no one ever asked, that was the whole point of him—whether they were tears of grief or relief, and suspected, with the mild wonder of a man reading his own amnesty proclamation, that the answer was: yes.

* * *

They left the capital on a bright cold morning, among the columns of people heading everywhere at once, the great unhurried migration of the finished war. Farmers going home to farms. Soldiers going home to arguments. A widow pushing a handcart of copper pots that sang against each other at every cobblestone, so that she moved through the streets accompanied by bells.

August walked with Noor on his shoulders, her small hands in his hair, her voice announcing the world item by item—"Dog! Smoke! Bird! *Big* bird!"—and nobody gave them a second glance. He was a tall man with a beard and a tired coat and a child, and that was all, and it was everything, and it was, he realized somewhere around the eastern gate, with the road open before them and the mountains faint blue in the distance like a promise that intended to keep itself—

It was the first time in his adult life that no one, anywhere, was looking for him.

The thought landed strangely. Not triumphant. Lighter than triumph, emptier than grief. He had spent ten years being valuable, and value, he now understood, had only ever been another word for hunted. What remained was a man with no route, no handler, no seal, no name worth a bullet—nothing to carry but the girl asleep against his neck, and the girl, thank God, was not cargo. She was the destination. She had been the destination since a rainy night at a safehouse door, and every road after this one would be chosen, freely, by a free man, which was a condition so unfamiliar that August kept testing it like a sore tooth, expecting it to hurt.

At the crossroads beyond the gate, where the road forked—one branch running down to the river towns, the other climbing toward the mountain passes and the border villages beyond—August stopped.

Noor stirred, blinked at the two roads with imperial gravity, and raised one arm.

Not toward the river. Toward the mountains.

"Well," August said, adjusting her on his shoulders, and set off uphill, "you're the boss."

He did not know yet what waited in the border villages—what quiet schoolroom years lay ahead, what truths waited patiently to be handed down like an inheritance, what lanterns would someday hang in ruined orchards each autumn, lit by small sure hands for reasons half-remembered. He knew only that the road rose, and the air was clean, and the war was over, and the girl on his shoulders was pointing at the mountains as though she'd lived there once, long ago, in a story she couldn't quite recall.

And August Ashgrove—who was no one at all, and therefore everyone, and therefore finally free—climbed.
