## 22. The Lantern Rehung

The lantern had a crack in its glass, and Noor had decided years ago that the crack was not a flaw but a signature.

"Things that survive have scars," Papa—her papa, the one who was actually her papa in every way that mattered except paper, which was to say Tam—had told her once, when she'd asked why he didn't just fix it with wire and wax. "You don't sand those away. You hang them up where people can see them."

So she hung it up where people could see it, which was to say: no one at all, because no one came to the orchard ruins anymore except her, and the crows, and one extremely opinionated goat named Biscuit who belonged to nobody and therefore belonged to everyone in the village of Sennet's Ford, population forty-one on a good day and thirty-nine when the miller's sons were off arguing about whose turn it was to muck the byre.

The orchard had been somebody's pride once. You could still see it in the way the rows ran straight as ruled lines under the grass, and in the stumps of trees too old and too burned to ever come back. Half of them had been cut for firewood during the hard winters after the war; the other half stood like old soldiers who refused to sit down, black-limbed, patient, blooming anyway in some stubborn half-measure every spring—a few white flowers from wood that should have given up decades ago. Noor loved those flowers more than any flower that grew easily. Easy things didn't count. She had learned that early, though she couldn't have said where.

She climbed the ladder she'd dragged out from behind the schoolhouse—dragged, because carrying it would have been admitting it was heavy, and there was a family rule about that, an unwritten one, the kind of rule you absorbed rather than were taught—and hooked the lantern onto the iron bracket that someone, long ago, had nailed to the biggest surviving tree. The bracket rusted a little more each year. The lantern went back up each year anyway.

Autumn did this. Autumn made her hands remember things her head didn't.

* * *

Here is what five-year-olds know: everything, and nothing, and usually not which is which.

Noor had been five once. She remembered it the way you remember a warm coat—you remember the warmth, not the coat. There had been a room with a low ceiling and a stove that ticked, and a man who sat across from her at a small table and told her all of it, all of him, even the parts that hurt. He had a voice like a door opening slowly. He had shown her a piece of burnt paper kept in a tin box, and he had cried, which confused her terribly at the time because she'd thought big people were waterproof.

"You'll want to be angry," he'd told her. "At me, or at him, or at all of them. That's allowed. But don't let anyone tell you the story for you."

Then he'd walked her down to the village well and introduced her to Tam and Lise Crane, who had wanted a child the way dry ground wants rain, and he'd shaken Tam's hand so hard Tam winced, and then he'd walked back up the hill alone, whistling something off-key, and she had watched him go and felt—this was the strange part, the part she only understood much later—that he wasn't leaving her. He was delivering her. The whole journey had been the delivery. Everything before the well had been the carrying, and carrying ends when the weight is set down gently, which is the entire art of it.

His name had been August. She'd been told that plainly, on her fifth birthday, along with everything else: the republic that fell, the general who surrendered himself to buy her time, the smuggler woman who gave them false roads until the soldiers believed her, the document that burned because some things are worth more as ash than as leverage. She'd listened to all of it sitting on a kitchen stool with jam on her chin, and she'd asked exactly one question:

"Did I cry a lot? When I was a baby?"

August had laughed—the first real laugh anyone had heard out of him in years, Tam said afterward—and said, "Constantly. You had opinions about rivers. You hated barges. You screamed the entire length of the Vessa like a tiny outraged official."

"And now I love the river."

"Now you love the river. People change. It's the best thing they do."

He'd stayed three days. On the fourth morning he was gone, and there was a wooden horse on her windowsill carved badly enough to be obviously handmade, and a note in careful schoolmaster's letters that said: *For reasons she half-remembers.* Which hadn't meant anything to her then, and meant everything to her now, because that was how August worked. He left sentences lying around like seeds and then had the decency to die—no, not die, he wasn't dead, she'd checked, everyone checked, he wrote once a year on a postcard with no return address saying only *Still teaching. Still terrible at chess.*—he had the decency to leave, so that the seeds could grow without being hovered over.

* * *

The lantern caught the late light and threw it through its crack in a long gold sliver across the grass, and Noor sat down against the old tree's trunk with the satisfaction of a job done properly.

Biscuit arrived, as Biscuit always did, at the exact moment sitting became comfortable, and shoved his nose into her satchel with the shamelessness of a creature who has never once been punished effectively.

"There's nothing," she told him.

There was half a roll. There was always half a roll. She broke it and fed it to him in pieces while the light went long and amber, and she thought—not for the first time—about the strangeness of her own life, which was that everyone in Sennet's Ford knew exactly two true things about her (that she was the Cranes' girl, and that she was going to be a teacher someday if her handwriting ever improved) and nothing else, and yet the nothing else was enormous. It had mountains in it. It had a river and a barge and a woman named Marta Vale who had gone into a republican interrogation room and come out with nothing broken that mattered. It had a farm below this very orchard where men had waited in the snow to do a terrible thing, and a man who had walked into their guns so a courier could walk out of them.

The republic was a word now. Kids at school used it to mean *a long time ago*, the way they used *the flood* or *the bad winter*. There were amnesty proclamations in the archive in Corvess that tourists sometimes looked at, fluttery old documents full of forgiveness granted by people who needed forgiving themselves. Her history master called it "the Collapse" with great relish, as if history were a dessert. Nobody in Sennet's Ford called it anything. They called it *before*, and then they talked about the price of wool.

But the orchard remembered. That was the thing nobody else seemed to notice. The orchard remembered, and once a year, when the light turned to honey and the air got that cold-clean edge on it, Noor's body remembered along with it—an ache in her arms like she'd been carried a long way, a smell of wet wool and lamp oil, the sensation of being rocked by water. Not memories, exactly. Memories need a mind that was paying attention. These were older than that. These lived lower down, in the blood and the bones, in whatever part of a person holds on before the person knows there's anything worth holding.

Tam said that was nonsense. Tam was a carpenter and believed everything could be explained by joinery.

Lise said it wasn't nonsense at all. Lise had held her on her first night and said Noor had gripped her finger with the grip of someone who had already lost a great deal and did not intend to lose the rest.

They were both right, probably. That was marriage.

* * *

She'd started hanging the lantern at nine, the autumn after her birthday, and she'd never been able to explain to herself—or to Tam, who had found her up the ladder the first time and aged ten years on the spot—precisely why.

"It's a signal," she'd tried.

"A signal for whom? We're the only ones who come up here."

"For whoever needs one."

Tam had looked at her for a long moment with that expression he got, the one where love and bewilderment shared a face, and finally said, "Well. Mind the third rung, it's rotten." Because that was the kind of father he was: he didn't need the reason to be legible. He just needed her to mind the third rung.

But she knew—or half-knew, which was her favorite state of knowing, the state where you got to keep discovering—that somewhere in the story she'd been told on her fifth birthday there was a lantern in an orchard, hung as a signal, a promise that the door below was open and the road was real and the carrying was almost over. And she knew—or half-knew—that the man who had read that signal had done the hardest walking of his life because of it, and that he had survived to carve badly-made horses and lose at chess by post.

So the lantern went up every autumn. Not as mourning. Marta Vale had come through Sennet's Ford twice—once when Noor was eight, a broad weathered woman who looked at her a beat too long and then bought her honey cakes and taught her three ways to cheat at cards, and once when Noor was twelve, grayer, slower, with a limp she claimed was from a cart accident and everyone politely agreed was from a cart accident—and Marta had said, over those honey cakes, the sentence Noor kept folded up inside her like the crack in the lantern glass:

"Don't you go making it sad, girl. Sadness is what people do instead of remembering properly. Hang your light because it's lit, not because it hurts."

Hang your light because it's lit.

Yes. That was it. That was the whole theology of it, if a girl raised on carpentry and honey cakes could be said to have a theology: the lantern went up because lights that can go up should, because signals outlive the reasons for them, because somewhere in the world a man who carried her when carrying was everything might look toward the hills on an autumn evening—he wouldn't, he was four hundred miles away teaching arithmetic to children who mispronounced everything—but *might*, and if he did, let there be a light. Let the light simply exist. Let it be proof that the child reached the door.

* * *

The sun went down behind the western ridge and the lantern took over the work, throwing its cracked gold sliver across the darkening rows, and Noor pulled her coat tighter and did not go home yet, because home could wait and this couldn't. This was a yearly appointment. She'd missed it once, at fourteen, with a fever, and had made Tam carry the lantern up in the rain on her behalf, and had lain in bed listening to the wind and feeling like a letter that never got delivered—which she supposed was funny, given everything, and she had laughed about it later with Marta, who laughed like a door slamming, cheerfully.

Footsteps on the path. Not Tam's—Tam walked like furniture being moved. These steps were light, uneven, leaning on a stick.

Marta came around the burned row with a basket on her arm and Biscuit immediately abandoned Noor as a lesser source of bread.

"You're late," Noor said.

"I'm seventy-one. I'm never late. Everyone else is early." Marta lowered herself onto the grass beside her with the elaborate care of the old and the complete disregard for dignity of the tough, and looked up at the lantern, its crooked gold line swinging faintly. "Every year. Same tree."

"Same bracket."

"Same crack." Marta nodded at it, approving. "Good. If you fixed it, I'd have to stop visiting."

They sat in the quiet a while, the kind of quiet two people can share when one of them was interrogated by a republic and the other one is the reason why. Down the hill the village lamps were coming on one by one, little yellow answers to the dark, and the river beyond was doing its endless patient work of going somewhere, and somewhere far north a man who was officially dead was probably grading sums.

"I brought something," Marta said, and dug in the basket.

It was a postcard. No return address. The handwriting was still careful, still schoolmaster-neat, though shakier than last year's. It said: *This year I lost at chess in eleven moves. Tell the orchard hello. Tell it the carrying was the best work I ever did.*

Noor read it twice. Then a third time, because the third time was when her eyes did the thing eyes do, and Marta, who had faced down interrogators without blinking, looked tactfully at the lantern and pretended to be fascinated by Biscuit.

"He's slowing down," Marta said.

"Yes."

"You're going to go see him."

It wasn't a question, and Noor didn't treat it as one. She folded the postcard along its old creases and put it in her satchel next to the remaining quarter of the roll, next to the tin box she carried now herself, with its scrap of burnt paper, its impossible inheritance.

"After the harvest," she said. "I'll teach through the term, and then—" She stopped. Looked up at the light, the long gold sliver swinging its slow pendulum across the ruined rows where the stubborn flowers slept waiting for spring. "And then I'm going to take the long road north. Through Ashfield. By the river, if there's still a barge."

Marta snorted. "There's always a barge. There's always a smuggler, too, and I hear she gives good rates to girls who hang lanterns." She levered herself up, stick and all, and stood looking at the light a moment longer, her face doing something complicated and then settling into something simple. "Leave it burning," she said. "Come on. Your mother's making stew and she counts heads."

Noor took one last look before following—the lantern steady on its rusted bracket, the crack throwing its faithful gold line out into the dark like a hand extended—and thought, not for the first time and certainly not for the last, that empires were paper and paper burned, but a light hung up year after year by someone who half-remembered why?

A light like that outlived everything.

She was halfway down the hill when she realized she'd forgotten to blow it out, and she smiled, and kept walking, and let it burn.
