## 1. The Unfinished Coastline

The ink had gone the colour of old bruises.

Cyrus Blackwood held the pen above the vellum and watched the last of it pool at the nib, black as a drowned thing, waiting to be spent. Outside the workroom window, November rain came down in grey ropes, lashing the cobbles of Chantry Lane, and the light — what there was of it — fell thin and watery across the great chart spread before him: the coastline of a country no one had finished mapping, drawn by a hand that would never draw again.

Master Ambrose Fenn. Dead three months now, or so the whole of Wexmouth believed. Buried beneath the yew in St. Aldric's churchyard with a brass plate and a sermon about a life given to the honest measure of the world. Cyrus had stood at the graveside in his one good coat, rain running off his chin, and felt nothing he could name — only a vast hollowed-out silence, like the space on a map where a cartographer has died mid-stroke.

He set the nib down. Not yet. The coast could wait another hour; it had waited eleven weeks already.

The workroom smelled the way it always had — tallow smoke, size and glue, the vinegar bite of iron-gall ink, and beneath it all the ghost of Master Fenn's pipe, though no one had smoked it since July. Cyrus knew every inch of the room the way he knew the back of his own hand, which was to say intimately and with a certain grief: the banks of shallow drawers labelled in the master's slanted script, *Headlands — minor*, *Soundings, doubtful*, the whale-oil lamp with its green glass shade, the long pine table worn into a shallow valley by forty years of elbows. On the wall hung the framed commission from the Admiralty, its wax seal cracked like dried blood.

And everywhere, everywhere, the maps.

Cyrus was nineteen years old and had been an apprentice for six of them, taken off the streets of Wexmouth at thirteen because he could copy a chart upside down without error and because Master Fenn had caught him, of all things, drawing the harbour from memory on the back of a fishmonger's wrapping paper. *You have the sickness*, the old man had said, not unkindly. *The mapmaker's sickness. You'll never see a place plainly again. Only its edges.* It had sounded like a curse delivered as a blessing, which perhaps it was.

Six years. And in six years Cyrus had learned that a coastline was the most dishonest thing in nature. It changed with every tide, every storm, every winter's fury. The charts men trusted were lies agreed upon, and the great work of a cartographer's life was not to fix the world but to keep apologising to it.

*The Atlas of the Western Approaches*. That was the master's life's work, seven years in the making — forty-one sheets, each one a miracle of hachured cliffs and soundings plumbed from a borrowed fishing smack, each one signed *A. Fenn* in that unmistakable hand, the capital A with its little crossbar like a gallows, the double f falling like a bird taking flight. Thirty-nine sheets finished. Two remained: the treacherous stretch between Hartmoor Point and the Gannet Rocks, where the tide ran like a millrace and three ships had gone down in living memory.

And then, in July, the master had walked out to the cliff path at Creggan Head in a fog so thick the gulls flew blind, and had not come back. They found his hat. They found his stick, wedged in the rocks below the path. They found, two days later, what the sea chose to give back, and what it gave back was enough for the coroner, if not quite enough for a man who looked at dead faces for a living. *Drowned*, said the verdict. *Misadventure.* The Guild struck his name from the roll of fellows with the ceremony due a fallen soldier, and the atlas passed to his apprentice, unfinished, unfinishable, a wound in vellum.

Cyrus rose and went to the window. Rain on glass, rain on stone, the town dissolving below in smears of lamplight. Down in the harbour the masts swayed like a forest deciding something. He thought, as he did every night, of the last conversation. Not the last words — those had been ordinary, *mind the drying ink, boy* — but the last real conversation, a fortnight before the end.

They had been working late over sheet thirty-eight, the master's finger resting on the blank space where the Gannet Rocks should have been.

"You know why I've left them till last," Fenn had said.

"Because they're dangerous to survey."

"Because they're dangerous to *draw*." The old man had smiled, and there had been something in the smile Cyrus had turned over a hundred times since, unable to classify it. "A map is a promise, Cyrus. Every line you draw, you're telling some captain, some crew, some wife waiting on a shore: this is true, trust your life to it. I've been making promises for forty years. I'd like the last ones to be kept."

He had said nothing else. But his hand, Cyrus remembered now — his hand had trembled, just slightly, and Ambrose Fenn's hands had never trembled, not in six years, not once.

The bell over the shop door rang at half past nine, when the rain had thickened to something vertical and the lamps along the quay were haloes drowning in their own light.

"We're shut," Cyrus called, not looking up. His fingers were black to the second knuckle. He had been fighting the Hartmoor Point headland for two hours and losing; hachures were supposed to follow the truth of the rock, and the truth tonight would not sit still under his hand.

The bell rang again. Then footsteps, unhurried, crossing the shop floor past the racks of county maps and pilot books, past the globes gathering dust in their mahogany cradles, and stopping at the threshold of the workroom doorway.

"I'm looking for the apprentice," a voice said. "The one they left the atlas to."

Cyrus looked up.

The stranger stood half in shadow, dripping. A travelling cloak the worse for weather, sea-boots, a hood pushed back from a face that was hard to read in the lamplight — young, perhaps, or simply fine-boned, with dark eyes that took in the room the way Cyrus's own eyes took in a coastline: edges first, always edges first. There was salt dried white on the shoulders of the cloak. Whoever this was had come by water, and recently, and in foul conditions.

"The atlas isn't for sale," Cyrus said. "And it isn't finished. If you want the completed sheets, the Guild holds copies—"

"I don't want sheets." The stranger stepped into the light, and Cyrus saw that under the cloak was a woman, young, with wind-scoured cheeks and hands that were not a clerk's hands, scarred across the knuckles like a sailor's or a smuggler's. She was holding something against her chest, wrapped in oilcloth, the way a person holds a thing they have carried too far to put down. "I want the man who made it."

"He's dead."

"Yes," she said. "That's what everyone believes. It's rather the point of it."

The room went very quiet. The rain filled the silence, drumming on the leaded panes, running in beads down the glass. Cyrus became aware of his own heartbeat, slow and enormous, and of the smell of the ink, and of the fact that he had not moved, had not breathed properly, since the word *point*.

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," she agreed. "But you will." She crossed the room — Cyrus noticed how she moved, sure-footed on a heaving deck of a floor that was flat — and laid the oilcloth bundle on the pine table, in the valley worn by forty years of elbows, directly beside sheet forty, the unfinished coast. "I was told to bring this to the apprentice. To you. By name. Cyrus Blackwood." She unwrapped it with the efficiency of someone who had done it many times, in worse places than this. "Before the Guild comes. They'll come soon. That's part of what you need to understand."

Inside the oilcloth lay a letter. That was all. A single letter, folded twice, sealed with plain black wax, unstamped — no crest, no mark, nothing but the wax itself. It might have been anything. It might have been nothing.

Except that Cyrus, looking at it across three feet of lamplit air, knew the handwriting on the address before his mind had finished forming the thought.

*Cyrus Blackwood, care of the Fenn Map Works, Chantry Lane, Wexmouth.*

The capital C with its little crossbar, like a gallows. The letters leaning forward as if eager to arrive. He knew it the way a son knows a father's step on the stair — instantly, completely, against all reason.

"That's impossible," Cyrus heard himself say. His voice sounded far away, a sounding dropped into deep water. "That's his hand. That's—" He stopped. Started again. "Ambrose Fenn is dead. I stood at his grave. I helped carry—"

"I know whose hand it is," the stranger said quietly. "I've spent four months learning exactly whose hand it is, and exactly what it costs to carry a letter written in it." She glanced toward the shuttered window, and for the first time something moved through her composure — not fear, precisely, but its close cousin, vigilance. "My name is Noor. And I'm going to tell you a story, Mr. Blackwood, that begins with your master walking off a cliff that he never walked off. But first—" she nudged the letter an inch toward him, "—first you should read it. Because I suspect there are only two people alive who could identify that handwriting, and after tonight, I'm hoping very much that both of us are on the same side."

Cyrus reached out. His own hand, steady enough to draw a coastline to a hair's breadth, shook now like the master's had trembled over the Gannet Rocks.

He broke the seal.

The black wax parted. Inside, on a single page, in the unmistakable hand of a dead man, were words addressed to him — and the first line alone was enough to make the whole room tilt, the rain go silent, the unfinished coastline on the table wait forever:

*Cyrus — if you are reading this, then I am sorry for the grave, which is empty, and for the lie, which is not. Burn nothing until you have read it all.*
