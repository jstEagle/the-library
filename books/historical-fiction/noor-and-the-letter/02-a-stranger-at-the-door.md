## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The rain came in off the harbour like something with a grievance, flinging itself against the shutters of the mapmaker's house in long, exhausted sheets. Cyrus Blackwood had learned to work through it. For three months he had worked through everything — through the guild's polite letters asking after the estate of the late Master Aldous Fenwick, through the landlady's sighs about unpaid rent, through the silence that lived now in the chair by the window where his master had sat each evening with his pipe and his terrible opinions about Dutch cartographers.

Three months since they had pulled what was left of the boat from Blackwater Sound. Three months since the coroner's man, a soft-bellied fellow who smelled of gin and lamp oil, had pronounced it a drowning and looked relieved to have said so.

Cyrus sat at the long drafting table beneath two lamps, his sleeves pinned back, his fingers stained to the second knuckle with Prussian blue. Before him lay the coastline of the northern reaches — the unfinished coastline — rendered in ink so fine it seemed less drawn than whispered. He was working from memory now, and from the sheaf of soundings Master Fenwick had made in the last survey season, and every line he committed to paper felt like a small act of resurrection. The atlas would be finished. It *had* to be finished. A dead man's great work should not die with him; that was the whole argument Cyrus had conducted nightly with himself since the funeral that wasn't one, since the closed coffin the guild had insisted upon, since the strange, saltless smell of the thing when he'd leaned close to say goodbye.

He told himself he hadn't noticed. He told himself grief made men imagine things.

The knock came just past eleven.

It was not a neighbour's knock. Neighbours knocked twice, apologetically, as though asking pardon for existing. This was three blows, evenly spaced, deliberate as a surveyor's chain laid across a field. And it did not come from the street door. It came from the *back* — the door that opened onto the ginnel behind the house, the door Master Fenwick had always used when he didn't wish to be seen coming or going, which, in the last year of his life, had been more often than Cyrus cared to think about now.

Cyrus set down his pen. The nib left a small blue bloom on the blotter, like a bruise.

He crossed the workshop, past the shelves of rolled vellum, past the brass instruments hanging in their felt-lined case like surgical tools, past the master's chair with its worn arms and its faint ghost of tobacco. The rain filled the pause between himself and the door with noise. He lifted the lantern from its hook and unbarred the door.

She stood in the gennel with the rain streaming off her hood, and the first thing he saw was not her face but her boots — sea boots, tarred at the seams, the kind that cost more than a clerk earned in a month. Then her hands, bare despite the cold, holding a satchel against her chest the way one holds a child or a wound. Then her face: young, perhaps his own age, brown-skinned, rain running off her chin, eyes that took his measure in a single sweep — the lantern, the doorway, the width of his shoulders, the distance back to the drafting table — with an efficiency he would later recognise as the habit of someone who has needed to know exits quickly.

"You're the apprentice," she said. Not a question.

"I'm nobody's apprentice," Cyrus heard himself say. "My master is dead."

Something moved behind her eyes at that — not surprise. Confirmation. As if she were ticking an item off a list she carried somewhere behind her ribs.

"May I come in out of the weather? Ten minutes. That's all I'll ask of you."

Every sensible instinct he possessed said no. Sensible instincts had served him poorly these three months, so he stepped aside.

* * *

She stood well back from the fire, drying nothing, warming nothing, as though heat were a courtesy extended to other people. She set the satchel on the table between them but did not open it. Her name, she said, was Noor. She said it plainly, without surname, the way people do when the surname would answer too many questions.

"How did you find this house?" Cyrus asked.

"The same way your master found the northern shoals." She nodded at the charts racked along the wall. "I read."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest kind I have tonight." She turned, slowly, taking in the room — the lamps, the half-finished sheet under its weights, the empty chair. Her gaze rested on the chair a moment longer than anywhere else, and Cyrus felt that look like a finger laid gently on a bruise he'd been pretending wasn't there.

"My master drowned in March," he said. "If you've come about debts—"

"I haven't come about debts." She unslung the satchel at last and drew from it a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed twine. She unwrapped it on the table with the care of a surgeon opening a dressing, and what lay inside was a letter.

Cyrus had spent six years handling paper. He knew rag stock from wood pulp, iron gall from walnut ink, the way humidity swelled a page and how a forged hand always hurried where a true one lingered. He reached for the letter because reaching was reflex, and stopped with his fingers a hair's breadth from the surface.

The address was in Master Fenwick's hand.

Not similar. Not a clever imitation. It was the hand itself — the slightly hooked ascenders, the way he crossed his *t*s low and leftward like a man impatient even with his own alphabet, the little flourish at the end of *Fenwick* that he'd affected after some ambassador praised it in Lisbon and never let Cyrus forget. Cyrus had traced that hand ten thousand times, had copied it as exercises, had grumbled over it, had known it the way one knows a father's tread on the stairs.

Dead men did not write addresses in their own hand.

"He drowned in March," Cyrus said again, and his voice came out smaller than he intended.

"Yes," said Noor.

"The body — they recovered—" But they hadn't, had they. They had recovered a boot, a coat button, a boat stove in upon the rocks. The coffin had been closed by order of the guild, sealed before the inquest could grow curious. He had signed papers at the guildhall with a hand that shook, and the registrar had not met his eyes, and he had told himself it was kindness, that men dislike looking at grief.

Noor watched him arrive at it, watch the ground shift under him, and said nothing, which was its own mercy.

* * *

The fire ticked. Rain ran its fingers down the glass. Cyrus sat very still with the letter unopened in front of him, because opening it would make the world a different shape and he was allowing himself one last breath of the old shape.

"Why bring it to me?" he asked finally. "Why not take it to the guild?"

"Because the guild believes him dead," Noor said, "and it is very much in certain interests that they go on believing it. If this letter passes through guild hands, it will pass into other hands within the day. Hands with seals on their signet rings." She paused. "You loved him. I'm betting my life that you'd rather he stayed alive than stayed convenient."

"Betting your life."

"That's what this is." For the first time something warmed in her voice, dry and dark as good chocolate. "I sailed nine days to reach this coast. I've walked since dawn. Whoever wrote that letter trusted me to put it in the right pair of hands, and the only instruction given was yours: *the apprentice at the house behind the chandler's*. So either I am right, or we are both ruined together, which at least has a certain symmetry."

Cyrus broke the seal.

The paper smelled of ship's biscuit and something resinous — pitch, maybe, or the tar of whatever hold it had been written in. The handwriting strode across the page exactly as it had once strode across survey notes and marginalia and the birthday card he still kept folded in his strongbox:

*Cyrus —*

*By the time this finds you, you'll have called me a fool twice and worse things once. Deserved, all of it. What I charted in the north was never meant for guild eyes, and when they understood what I had, dying became the safest profession available to me. I am alive. I am also hunted, and the hunting has grown closer than I can abide while the atlas sits unfinished in your keeping.*

*Burn nothing yet. Trust the bearer — her name is Noor and she saved my life once, which puts her among the shortest lists I keep.*

*Come to me. Bring the finished sheets. There is a place, and Noor knows the road, and there is no time to explain in ink what ink can be made to confess.*

*— A.F.*

*P.S. You were right about the Dutch.*

Cyrus read it twice. On the second reading his vision blurred, and he discovered his own breath was doing something unsteady, and that Noor had turned politely toward the window to give him the fiction of privacy.

Alive. *I am alive.* The words rearranged three months of grief the way a flood rearranges a valley — nothing where it had been, everything scoured and new and terrible. Grief had at least been a settled country. This was open water.

"He faked his death," Cyrus said.

"He faked his death," Noor agreed.

"To escape the guild."

"To escape what the guild sold to others. There's a difference, and it matters." She finally came nearer the fire, held her palms out as if conceding to it. "Your master found something in those northern waters that certain men will pay any price to possess and any price again to bury. The atlas is not merely a book of maps, apprentice. It is evidence. And evidence burns."

Cyrus looked at the drafting table, at the coastline whispering under its weights, at six years of his own life inked into another man's great design. Then at the letter, at the hooked ascenders, at *burn nothing yet*, at the postscript that had undone him more thoroughly than all the rest.

"When do we leave?" he said.

Noor smiled for the first time — quick, crooked, gone almost before it arrived. "Tomorrow's tide," she said. "And Mr. Blackwood — before you pack your pens, you should know we're already followed. The man in the street has been counting your windows since dusk."

Cyrus went cold. Through the rain-blurred glass, across the lane, a figure stood beneath the dripping eaves opposite, hat brim low, perfectly still, patient as a watermark.

Waiting for morning. Waiting for them.

The guild's letters asking after the estate had begun, Cyrus realised, exactly one week after the funeral.
