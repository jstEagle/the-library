## 3. Handwriting of the Dead

The candle had burned to a stub before Cyrus dared touch the letter again.

He had carried it from the doorway like something scalding — Noor still standing in the rain behind him, dripping on the threshold stones, patient as a heron — and laid it on the workbench beneath the good lamp, the one with the brass reflector that threw light in a hard, honest circle. Around them the workshop kept its night sounds: the tick of the longcase clock in the hall, the settling sigh of paper in the plan-chest drawers, the sea working at the harbour wall two streets away with the persistence of a man trying to remember a name.

Cyrus did not open it at once. He looked at it first.

A single sheet, folded twice, travel-stained at the edges, spotted with salt or sweat. It bore no direction but *To my apprentice*, which was answer enough. And across its face, in the upper corner, a small device he knew better than his own heartbeat: a compass rose no bigger than a thumbnail, drawn freehand, its north point finished with a flourish like a gull's wing.

Master Ambrose Quill had signed nothing without that flourish. He had said once, laughing over his pipe, that a mapmaker who trusted his own hand too much would die believing in flat horizons. The flourish was his doubt made visible. Every chart he ever issued wore it, tucked into the cartouche like a private joke between him and God.

And here it was. On paper dated — if the smudged ink at the foot could be believed — eleven days after they had nailed shut the coffin containing his boots, his coat, and four hundredweight of wet sand from the estuary.

"Open it," Noor said softly. She had not moved from the doorway. Water pooled around her boots; she seemed unaware of it. "Or I will begin to believe I dreamed you."

"I'm deciding whether I want to know."

"You already know." Her voice was low, unhurried, an accent he could not place — somewhere east of everywhere, he thought, sand and spice and long roads. "You have known since you saw the seal. A man does not stand this way over a letter unless the letter has already spoken."

Cyrus wiped his palms on his apron, a habit Master Quill had scolded him for ten years — *clean hands for clean lines, boy* — and broke the fold.

The handwriting hit him first, harder than any sentence could have. That strong rightward lean, the letters tall and narrow like masts, the peculiar doubled *t* crossed with a single impatient stroke. He had spent six years learning to imitate it for title-pieces, and had never once succeeded. There was a pressure to the master's hand, a certainty in the downstrokes, as though the pen were driving stakes into the page.

*Boy,* the letter began, and Cyrus's throat closed, because only one person alive called him that, and that person was ashless and unburied and apparently writing to him from the far side of his own funeral.

*If you are reading this, then Noor found you, which means Noor kept faith, which is more than I deserve and more than I expected. Listen carefully and do not weep over the ink; it smears.*

*I am not dead. You knew that within a week of the burial — don't trouble to deny it, your grief had a question in it — but you did not know why, and the why is the dangerous part.*

*The atlas is not what the Guild believes it to be. It never was. The coastlines I have spent nine years charting carry more than soundings and headlands. They carry names. Places the Guild has agreed do not exist, harbours where certain cargoes come ashore without ceremony, channels by which men vanish who ought to be hanged. The Guild paid me to make the sea legible so that they might own every inch of its reading. I have instead written a book of doors.*

*They know. Wexford knows. Which is why I am dead, officially, and why I must remain so a little longer. The inquest was bought and brief; let it stay buried with the sand.*

*Burn nothing. Trust no member of the Guild above the rank of clerk. If you finish the atlas — and you will, you were always better with the eastern approaches than I admitted aloud, which cost me something to write just now — do not deliver it to Cartwright Hall. Deliver it to me.*

*Noor has the coordinates. She will argue with you about the route; let her win the arguments about the sea, lose none about the land. I am at the end of a line of harbours, each one less reputable than the last, waiting for the tide and the courage to meet in the same place.*

*You were the son I did not plan for and could not now do without. Come careful. Come soon.*

*— A.Q.* *(and beneath it, small and wry:)* *the flourish means I still doubt. Especially this.*

Cyrus read it three times. The third time his eyes blurred so badly the masts of the handwriting swam like reeds, and he set the page down flat on the bench and pressed both palms against the wood until the trembling stopped.

Nine years of charts. Nine years of dawn soundings and midnight inking, of the master's cough growing worse each winter, of the slow, meticulous assembly of *The Mariner's Complete Atlas of the Northern Coasts* — and all of it a cipher. All of it doors.

"He told you none of this?" Noor asked. She had come closer without seeming to cross the room; there was something of the tide in her movements.

"Nothing. He was — he was the most honest man I ever met. Brutally so. He'd tell me my graticule was crooked before I'd finished ruling it." Cyrus laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "He lied to me for nine years."

"He lied to protect you. It is a different thing. Not a kind thing," she added, seeing his face, "but a different one." She nodded at the letter. "May I?"

He slid it across. He watched her read — quickly, lips moving slightly at the difficult English phrases, pausing longest at *a book of doors* — and noticed for the first time the details the lamplight gave him: a scar through one eyebrow, old and white; rope-burn grooves on her palms, healed shiny; a canvas satchel slung across her body even now, even indoors, worn at the strap from long carrying. She had the look of someone who had slept in many places and trusted few of them.

"You were his courier," Cyrus said. "Before the death."

"For four years. Letters, mostly. Once a parcel I was not permitted to ask about." She smiled faintly. "I never asked. Asking is how couriers become corpses." She tapped the page. "'Noor has the coordinates.' Do you want them now, or after you have slept? You look like a man who has been awake since the funeral."

"I haven't slept since the funeral."

"Then you will take them now, and misread half of them, and we will correct them tomorrow." From the satchel she drew a second paper — smaller, folded into eighths, soft as cloth from handling — and unfolded it on the bench beside the letter.

It was a list. Seven entries, each a string of figures in that same unmistakable hand: latitude, longitude, and after each pair a word. *The Anchorite. Saltmere. The Gull's Oath.* Names of taverns, perhaps, or coves, or ships — Cyrus couldn't tell, and the uncertainty made his skin prickle. The final entry sat apart from the others, underlined twice:

*Last of all: Blackreef Roads, berth of the *Meridian Star*. Ask for the bosun. Say the flour came in wet.*

"The *Meridian Star*," Cyrus repeated. "That's a coaster out of — I've seen her in the harbour books. Grain run. Innocent as bread."

"No ship is innocent that keeps a berth on a list like this." Noor refolded the coordinates with the tenderness of someone folding a wound dressing. "Seven stops. The Guild watches the first three, I think — we will pass through them quickly and quietly. By the fifth, if Wexford's people are competent, they will know a stranger is asking questions in port towns. By the seventh—" she shrugged, and the shrug contained shipwrecks, "—we are either aboard or we are evidence."

"*We*." Cyrus heard himself say it, and heard, beneath the word, the shape of everything it committed him to: the workshop cold behind them, the atlas half-finished in its chest, the inquest's tidy verdict sitting in the parish records like a stone over a well. "I can't just leave. There's the atlas. There's the — the Guild expects delivery of the fair copies by quarter-day. Master Cartwright himself wrote to ask after my health."

"And what did you write back?"

"That I was grieving. Which was true."

"Grief is excellent cover," Noor said. "Use it. Grieve loudly and slowly, miss the quarter-day, and let them think the apprentice has come apart. Meanwhile—" she touched the letter, "—he asks you to bring him the atlas itself. Not the copies. The original. The book of doors."

Cyrus turned to the great plan-chest against the far wall — the one Master Quill had built himself, oak and iron fittings, heavier than sin. Inside lay the atlas: forty-one completed sheets and nine in draft, the work of nearly a decade, the thing Cyrus had sworn over the coffin to finish. To carry it out of this room meant carrying it past the Guild's clerks, past Wexford's watchers, past the whole polite machinery of an institution that had purchased a funeral and expected a book.

"They'll search the house when I miss the quarter-day," he said slowly. Thinking it through as he said it, the way the master had taught him to plot a course — one fix at a time. "Which gives us until then to be gone. Three weeks, maybe four."

"So we leave in three days. Enough time to look bereaved. Not enough time to be caught preparing."

"You've done this before."

She looked at him then, straight on, and the lamplight showed him her eyes were dark and quite calm, the calm of deep water. "I have done many things," she said. "Most of them I would not recommend. Sleep now, apprentice. Tomorrow we begin with the first coordinate, and tomorrow night, if the wind serves, I will teach you how to walk through a port town without being remembered."

* * *

But sleep did not come, or came only in fragments, and near dawn Cyrus rose and went back down to the workshop with the candle cupped in his hand like a small stolen sun.

He unlocked the plan-chest. He lifted out the atlas — the true atlas, sheets heavy as sails — and spread upon the bench the easternmost chart, the one they had been working when the master died: the approaches to Blackreef Roads, unfinished, its coastline dissolving into pencilled conjecture at the northern margin.

And there, in the lower corner, in the space reserved for the title-cartouche that was never yet drawn, Cyrus found what he had missed a hundred times before.

A compass rose, thumbnail-small, north point finished with a flourish like a gull's wing. Beneath it, in letters so fine he needed the loupe to read them, a single line of instruction:

*If the boy is reading this, the flour came in wet. Count the sheets. One is not mine.*

Cyrus stood very still while the candle guttered and the sea worked at the harbour wall and somewhere overhead the first grey light began, indifferent and enormous.

Forty-one completed sheets. He counted them twice, then a third time, laying each one out across the benches until the workshop became a sea of coastlines, every harbour in the north spread out beneath his hands like a sleeping country.

Forty-two.

One of these charts was a lie. One of them had been slipped among his master's life's work by a hand he did not know — and somewhere out in the rain-washed dark of the town, in the halls of the Guild, in the offices of a man named Wexford, someone already knew which one.
