## 4. What the Guild Knows

The Guildhall smelled of tallow and old paper, a smell Cyrus had loved since boyhood — the smell of other people's maps, other people's certainties. This morning it made him ill.

He had dressed carefully for the visit, in his second-best coat and the collar Noor had mended without being asked. The stranger had been in the workshop three days now, sleeping on the settle beneath the south window, eating little, saying less, and every hour of that silence had pressed against Cyrus like water against a dam. The letter sat folded inside his waistcoat, over his heart, where he fancied he could feel its weight even through wool and linen. *Master Aldous is alive.* He had not dared say the words aloud since the night Noor arrived. Saying them would make them real, and real things could be taken away.

So instead he had done what he always did when frightened: he had gone looking for information.

The Guild of Cartographers and Hydrographers kept its offices above the fishmongers on Cordwainer Lane, and its reading room was open to any member in good standing — which Cyrus was, technically, by virtue of his apprenticeship papers, though Master Aldous's death had left those papers in a curious limbo. An apprentice whose master was dead was an apprentice belonging to no one. The Guild had sent no word about reassignment. Cyrus suspected they were waiting to see what else surfaced from the wreck of the *Meridian*'s last voyage before deciding what the Blackwood name was worth.

"Blackwood." The clerk at the desk looked up from his ledger with the expression of a man who has already decided how much trouble you are. "You've not been in since the funeral."

"There was work to finish," Cyrus said.

"There is always work to finish." The clerk — Mr. Pellings, his brass name-pin said — sniffed and turned the ledger toward himself. "And yet here you are. What do you want?"

"The voyage records for the *Meridian*. Final expedition. I'd like to see what was filed."

Something moved behind Pellings' eyes — quick, furtive, gone before it could be named. "Filed?"

"Charts. Logs. Correspondence. Whatever came back with the ship."

"The *Meridian* came back with very little," Pellings said, and there it was again, that flicker, like a fish turning under grey water. "Master Aldous drowned off the Skerries. The ship returned under Lieutenant Hargrave. What survived of the survey materials went into evidence at the inquest. You know this. You were at the inquest."

"I was at the inquest," Cyrus agreed. "I read nothing that explained why a ship charting the northern approaches would carry a second set of sealed orders."

Pellings' pen stopped.

For a moment the only sound in the hall was the tick of the great regulator clock and, somewhere below, the wet slap of fish on marble. Then the clerk set down his pen with exaggerated care, as though it might go off.

"Young man," he said, lowering his voice, "I am going to tell you something once, because your master was respected here and because you look like a boy who will get himself hurt asking questions in worse places than this. Come away from the desk."

* * *

They stood in the narrow passage between the reading room and the map archive, among the smell of camphor and dust, and Pellings spoke quickly and quietly, glancing twice at the door.

"The *Meridian*'s materials did not all go to the inquest. There was a chest — a locked survey chest, brass-bound — that came ashore at Whitby before the ship ever docked at London. It was carried inland by private carrier, not by the Admiralty courier, which was irregular, and it was received here by—" he paused, choosing— "by the Second Warden himself. Not through channels. By hand."

Cyrus felt the floor tilt slightly, the way a deck tilts. "Which Warden?"

"Pellings does not gossip," the clerk said, which was answer enough. "What matters is what happened after. Three weeks ago — before your master's body washed up, mind you, *before* — men came asking after the contents of that chest. Not Guild men. They carried warrants signed by someone at the Admiralty, but the signatures were wrong. I have copied ten thousand signatures in thirty years at that desk, Blackwood, and I know when ink lies. They took the chest anyway. And they took the Warden's personal correspondence while they were at it. Everything touching the northern survey."

"And the Guild permitted this?"

"The Guild was not asked." Pellings' mouth thinned. "The Guild was *told*, after, that no such visit occurred. Which is a very strange thing to be told unless someone wants it known that it must never have happened." He straightened, and his voice resumed its dry official register, pitched now to carry to anyone who might be listening. "There are no voyage records available for the *Meridian*, apprentice. The matter is closed. Good morning."

But as Cyrus turned to go, the clerk's hand shot out and gripped his sleeve — one hard squeeze, deliberate — and something small passed between them, pressed into his palm. Cyrus walked out onto Cordwainer Lane with the gulls screaming overhead and did not open his hand until he reached the mouth of the alley behind the cooperage.

A slip of paper. On it, in Pellings' cramped copperplate: a date, three days hence; an hour; and a single word — *Archive*.

* * *

Noor was waiting when he got back, sitting cross-legged on the workshop floor amid the scattered sheets of the unfinished atlas, holding one up to the light with an expression Cyrus had learned to dread and love in equal measure: the expression of someone finding a flaw in something sacred.

"Your master's coastlines drift," she said. "Here — the headland at Thorns Ness. In his earlier charts it sits a full half-mile east."

"It sits where he drew it," Cyrus said stiffly. "He was precise to a fault."

"He was precise to a fault in everything except this." She laid the sheet down gently, almost tenderly. "Unless the fault was the point. Unless he wanted whoever followed these charts to end up half a mile from where they thought they were." She looked up at him, her dark eyes steady. "You went somewhere this morning. You came back with your hands closed like fists."

Cyrus hesitated. Three days ago he had pulled a stranger out of the rain, armed with nothing but a letter he couldn't show anyone and a story he half believed. Every sensible voice in him still whispered *trap*. But Noor had recited, unprompted, the exact bearing notation Master Aldous used — a private quirk, degrees measured from true north rather than magnetic, a habit no forger could have guessed — and she had wept when she described the old man teaching it to her, years ago, in a language Cyrus hadn't recognized and she had translated haltingly: *the sea does not lie, so neither shall my maps.*

He opened his fist and showed her the paper.

She read it in a breath. "Three days. The Archive." Her fingers tightened on the sheet until the paper creased. "Then we have three days to learn everything the letter can tell us, because whatever the Guild knows, the men who took that chest know it too — and if they found what I think they found, they will not stop at taking things." She rose, restless, moving to the window where the harbour lay flat and silver under a high pale sun. "My father trusted your master with more than maps, Cyrus. He trusted him with names. If the chest held those names, then somewhere in this city there are men who would burn down the world to keep them buried — and your master faked his death to stay ahead of the fire."

Outside, a bell buoy clanked in the offing, slow and patient, counting out the tide. Cyrus looked down at the atlas spread across the floor — four years of his life, the coastline running off the edge of the last sheet and stopping, unfinished, like a sentence cut short — and understood suddenly, completely, that the atlas was never only an atlas. It was a message. His master had drawn the truth into the margins of the world itself and left it for whoever loved him enough to read closely.

"We'll need the original field notes," Cyrus heard himself say. "The ones he kept apart from the fair copies. He hid them somewhere — he was always hiding things — and if anyone knows where—"

He stopped. Because he did know. He knew exactly where, had known since he was twelve years old and caught the old man returning at dawn with salt spray on his boots and a lantern that smelled of whale oil, coming not from the harbour road but from the cliff path north of town — the path that led nowhere, according to every chart the Guild had ever approved, except a sea cave marked on no map at all.

Noor watched his face change. "You know where."

"I know where," Cyrus said, and reached for his coat. "And if the men who forged those warrants know it too, then we are already late."
