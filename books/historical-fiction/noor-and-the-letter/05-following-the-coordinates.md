## 5. Following the Coordinates

The letter gave them three places and no explanations, which was, Cyrus had learned from his master, exactly how a careful man spoke when he feared being overheard even across years and an ocean of silence.

*Gravesend, where the river forgets its name. The Anchor & Chain — ask for the room that smells of tar. Then north to Whitby, if the tide allows; a chart lies where the light falls at noon. Last: Harwich. The *Kestrel* sails when she sails.*

Noor read it aloud twice on the coach out of London while the fields went by grey and dripping, her voice low so the other passengers heard only the murmur of a woman reciting scripture. She had folded the original away inside her glove, against the skin of her palm, as though it might sweat through paper and be lost.

"You knew him," Cyrus said. It was not the first time he had asked. "Before all this. You knew my master."

"I knew a man called Aldous Wren," she said, watching the rain stitch patterns on the glass. "Whether that is your master's name or another of his costumes, I cannot say."

"He never told me about costumes." The words came out harder than he intended. "He told me about coastlines. About how a map is a promise you make to strangers — that the water will be where you drew it. That was all he ever—"

"That was the costume," Noor said gently, and turned back to the window.

They rode in silence after that, but it was not an unfriendly silence. It was the silence of two people rowing the same boat, each too occupied with the pull of the oars for talk.

* * *

Gravesend received them in the smell of tar and herring and cold smoke off the river. The Thames there was wide as a sea in its own right, brown and muscular, crowded with colliers and hulks rotting at their moorings, and the gulls screamed overhead like creditors at a funeral. The Anchor & Chain stood where the street gave up trying to be anything but a dockside lane, its sign swinging on one rusted hinge, creaking a note that Cyrus would hear in his dreams for weeks afterward.

The publican was a slab of a man with forearms like capstans and eyes that performed their arithmetic before his mouth opened. When Cyrus asked — feeling foolish even as he said it — whether there was a room that smelled of tar, the man did not laugh. That frightened Cyrus more than laughter would have.

"Upstairs. End of the passage. Sixpence the night, and I don't clean under the beds." He slid a candle stub and a key across the bar, and his voice dropped into something almost like kindness. "There's been others asking after that room. Two days back. A gentleman, well-dressed, gloves. Said he was a surveyor."

Cyrus felt Noor go very still beside him, the stillness of a deer that has caught the scent on the wind and knows running is already too late.

"What did he look like?" she asked.

"Like a man who'd never been wet in his life," said the publican. "Guild-grey coat. Little silver pin on the lapel. Compass rose, it was."

The Guild of Chartered Cartographers. Cyrus's own guild, whose letters he had spent three years answering in his dead master's hand, whose dues he had paid from money scraped together by selling small charts of harbours he'd never seen. They had come looking. Which meant they were not merely suspicious anymore. Which meant—

"The atlas," Noor murmured as they climbed the narrow stair. "They know it isn't finished. Or they know it *is*."

The room at the end of the passage smelled overwhelmingly of tar — great black ropes of it sealed along the floorboards where the sea's damp crept through — and beneath the tar, faintly, of tobacco and of a soap Cyrus knew intimately: the sandalwood blocks his master had kept on the washstand at Rookery Lane. He stood in the doorway breathing it and could not move for a moment. Noor touched his elbow, once, and he came back to himself.

The room held a bed, a chair, a table scarred by rings of spilled ale — and, scratched into the table's underside where only someone kneeling or crawling would find it, a set of figures. Cyrus knelt. His candle guttered in the draught from under the door, and he shielded it with his hand and read:

*51°26′ N 0°22′ E — but charts lie, boy. Trust the tide-tables. W.*

His throat closed. *Boy.* As if no time had passed at all. As if the old man had merely stepped out for tobacco and left instructions the way he always had, terse and fond and infuriating.

"It's a place on the marshes east of here," Cyrus managed. "Higham. There's an old tide-mill — I surveyed it myself two summers ago, for a harbour chart that never sold. The coordinates are near enough. But he says trust the tide-tables." He frowned, working it aloud. "At this state of the moon... the causeway to the mill floods at half past nine tonight. Anyone following us who doesn't check will walk into the creek in the dark."

"And anyone who does check," Noor said, "will arrive dry, at the same hour we do." She smiled, thin and quick. "So we go now, at low water, and we are gone before they can follow. Your master thinks like a smuggler, Mr. Blackwood."

"He taught me to think like a mapmaker. I begin to suspect those are the same trade."

* * *

The marshes at dusk were a country of whispers. Reeds hissed in the wind off the estuary; somewhere a curlew cried its two descending notes over and over, grief rehearsed until it became mere habit. Mud gleamed in the failing light, ribbed like the surface of a brain, stitched with creeks that branched and rebranched in silver. Cyrus navigated without thinking, the way his master had drilled him: read the ground as you'd read a chart, let the land tell you what the water has been doing. Noor walked behind him, sure-footed, silent, and when he glanced back once he saw her face lifted toward the sky, reading clouds the way he read contours.

"You've done this before," he said. "Walked strange country."

"I have walked a great many strange countries." Her voice carried strangely in the flat air. "My father was a pilot out of Alexandria. Before the plague took him, he taught me that every coastline has two shapes — the one the maps show, and the one the smugglers use. He was both kinds of man."

Ahead, the tide-mill resolved itself out of the gloom: a hunched black shape, its wheel long since rotted to a skeletal quarter-circle, leaning over a stagnant millpond. The causeway ran out toward it across the mud, planks missing here and there like teeth knocked from a jaw. And in the mill's upper window — impossible, unless — a light moved. Once. Twice. Three times.

A signal. Answering theirs, though they had shown none.

"Noor." Cyrus stopped. "We haven't a lantern showing. Whoever that is, they lit it first."

"They expected us." She was already moving forward, faster now. "Or they expected *him*, and any arrival will do."

Inside, the mill smelled of rot and salt and old grain gone to dust. The staircase survived just well enough to bear them up to the floor where the light had been, and there, seated on an upturned crate with a shuttered lantern at his feet and a pistol lying openly beside him on his knee, waited a small man with a face like a dried apple and eyes that missed nothing, least of all the mud on their boots.

"Cyrus Blackwood," the man said. Not a question.

"How do you—" Cyrus began.

"Because you stand the way he stands when he reads a room. Feet planted, head tilted, measuring." The old man snorted. "I'm Josiah Pell. I steered for Wren eleven years, before he decided to die. I've been waiting in this drafty ruin four nights because a boy came to my sister's tavern in Gravesend asking after tar-rooms, and I thought — that'll be either Wren's ghost or Wren's apprentice, and either way I'd best see." He leaned forward, lantern-light carving deep the lines of his face. "Where is the atlas?"

"Half-finished," Cyrus said. "Hidden."

"Good. Keep it so." Pell spat, expertly, through a gap in the floorboards. "The Guild's put men on every road from London to Edinburgh. They're saying Wren forged crown charts — sold altered soundings to foreign buyers, got ships wrecked on coasts he'd drawn safe. There's a price on proof of where he's hid. Dead or alive, and dead pays better, because dead men can't testify."

"That's a lie," Cyrus said hotly. "He wouldn't. He used to make me redraw a chart three times over one fathom's error. He said a wrong sounding was a murder committed in advance."

"Aye." For the first time, Pell's voice softened. "That's why I know the charge is a lie, lad. But knowing won't stop a hangman." He dug into his coat and produced a packet wrapped in oilcloth, holding it out. "Whitby. That's where the chart waits, per his letter — I've had a copy. Take the packet, take the morning coach, and whatever you do, don't take the direct road. The Guild's man rides it, grey coat, silver pin, name of Verrall. Clever as a knife and twice as cold."

Noor took the packet, weighing it in her palm. "And the *Kestrel*? At Harwich?"

"Sails Friday week, if the wind serves, and not one day later. After that, the port closes. Revenue cutters, new orders straight from the Admiralty." Pell looked between them, and something in his gaze grew troubled, the look of a man reading weather he doesn't like. "Two berths were promised. Only two. I'd get yourselves aboard and pray Wren finds his own way out, because between you and me—" he lowered his voice though the marshes outside held nothing but curlews and wind, "—between you and me, I don't think he means to leave at all. I think he means to draw them all onto himself and burn the evidence with him standing in the middle of it."

Cyrus heard the words and felt the floor tilt beneath him, though the mill stood perfectly still.

Outside, far off across the black marshes, a lantern flickered to life. One flash. Two. Then a third — but wrong, somehow. Too regular. Too patient. The kind of light a man makes when he wants to be found.

Pell blew out his own lamp instantly. In the sudden dark, his whisper was barely breath.

"That's not my signal code. Someone's coming up the causeway — and they checked the tide-tables."
