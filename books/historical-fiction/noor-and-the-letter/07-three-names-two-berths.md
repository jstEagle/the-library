## 7. Three Names, Two Berths

The tide was going out when we reached the harbor at Porthollow, and it took the fog with it, peeling the mist back from the water in long gray ribbons until the ships emerged one by one like sketches inked onto wet paper. I had drawn a hundred harbors from charts and hearsay, but never had I seen one so changed by what it held. Three weeks ago this had been an abstraction to me — a coastline, a sound, a scatter of depth-soundings. Now it was a door, and behind that door, perhaps, my master.

Noor stood beside me at the rail of the customs house steps, her hood thrown back despite the cold, her eyes moving across the moored vessels with the quick arithmetic of someone counting exits.

"Which one?" she asked.

I unfolded the letter — Elias Wren's letter, his handwriting crawling across the page like something alive — and read the coordinates again, though I no longer needed to. *The Kestrel. Berth eleven. She sails on the morning tide or not at all.* The smugglers' berth he'd written of was aboard a brigantine riding low in the outer roads, her paint gone the color of old bone, her name barely legible on the stern: *Kestrel*.

"There," I said, and pointed.

We came down through the fish market, where gulls screamed over barrels of herring and the cobbles ran silver with scale and brine. A boy selling winkles watched us pass with the flat, assessing stare of a child who has already learned which strangers are worth remembering and which are worth reporting. Noor dropped a coin into his basket without breaking stride, and the boy's expression did not change, but I understood the transaction all the same. It bought nothing except, perhaps, a small delay in betrayal.

Berth eleven lay at the far end of the quay, past the customs shed where two men in Guild gray stood talking with the harbor officer. My heart went cold at the sight of them. The Guild's colors — slate and silver — were unmistakable, and the taller of the two carried the rolled leather case of a surveyor, the kind used for taking official measurements of vessels bound for inspection.

"Don't look," Noor murmured.

"I wasn't."

"You walk differently when you're afraid. Slower, as if the ground might be listening." She took my arm, and her grip was warm through my coat sleeve. "Walk as if you're bored."

I tried. We passed within twenty feet of them. One of the men laughed at something; the sound carried across the water, ordinary and human, and I hated how much it steadied me. They were only men. But men of the Guild had been asking questions in every port since Falmouth, and each time the questions grew more specific. A mapmaker's apprentice. Traveling with a foreign woman. Carrying documents.

The *Kestrel* rose above us at last, barnacled and patient. A gangplank ran down to the quay, and at its foot sat a barrel-chested man mending a net with fingers like knotted rope. He looked up once, took us both in — my ink-stained cuffs, Noor's foreign cut of coat, the way we stood too close together — and returned to his net.

"We're looking for passage," I said. "Two berths."

"Passage costs money."

"We have money."

"Money buys you a seat on a mail packet." He pulled a knot tight with his teeth. "The *Kestrel* isn't a packet."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

Something moved behind his eyes then — not warmth, but recognition. The look of a man who has been expecting someone and is relieved, and annoyed about being relieved. He set down the net.

"Names," he said.

"Cyrus Blackwood."

He waited. Noor said, "Noor Haddad."

"And the third name?" the man asked quietly. "The one that bought these berths three weeks ago? Because there were two berths purchased, friend, and paid for twice over, and neither payment mentioned a woman."

My mouth went dry. "What name did he give?"

"He gave no name. He gave a seal." The man reached into his shirt and drew out a wax impression on a scrap of parchment, holding it up between two fingers. Even half-worn, I knew it instantly: the compass rose with the broken northern point, the mark Elias Wren had pressed into every chart he ever finished, the mark I had been pressing into his unfinished ones for three weeks, praying no one would notice the difference in my hand.

"That's my master's seal," I said.

"That's the seal of a dead man," the fisherman said, "according to the notices posted in four ports. Dead men don't buy berths. So either you're lying to me, or somebody very clever is lying to everybody." He stood, joints cracking like ship's timbers. "Either way, you'll come aboard and say your piece to the captain. And you'll do it now, because the tide won't wait and neither will the revenue cutter that's been circling the headland since dawn."

I had not seen the cutter. I turned, and there it was — a dark shape beyond the breakwater, sails trimmed neat and official, moving in that unhurried way that meant it had nowhere else to be and all the time in the world to be there.

*

The cabin below decks smelled of tar, tallow, and years. A lantern swung on its hook, throwing shadows that climbed the curved walls like slow animals. The captain sat behind a table bolted to the floor — a narrow woman with iron-gray hair cropped short and hands folded flat before her, the posture of someone who had spent a life waiting for bad news and refusing to flinch from it.

She listened to everything. To my account of Master Wren's death — the fever, the funeral, the sealed workshop. To the letter that arrived six months later in his hand. To the forged charts, the port towns, the pursuit that had sharpened behind us mile by mile. She did not interrupt once. When I finished, she let the silence sit until I felt it pressing on my chest.

"The atlas," she said at last. "Where is it?"

"In my bag. Wrapped in oilcloth."

"Show me."

I unwrapped it on her table. She turned pages without touching them, using the edge of a ruler, the way an examiner handles evidence. Her face revealed nothing, but when she reached the final chart — the one I had completed myself, the northern coast Elias had died before finishing — she stopped.

"This isn't his line work."

"No, Captain. That's mine."

"It's good." She said it grudgingly, as if paying a debt. Then she looked up at me, and her eyes were the color of deep water. "Here's what your master bought with his gold, boy. Two berths. Passage out on tomorrow's tide, before the Guild's writ reaches this harbor — and it reaches it tomorrow night, because I have friends who tell me things. Two berths, and instructions that they go to his apprentice and his apprentice alone. Not to a stranger. Not to a woman carrying letters." Her gaze flicked to Noor, who had not blinked. "His words, written in his hand, which I confess is the finest forgery I've ever been asked to honor."

"It isn't a forgery," I said.

"Then your master is alive, which is worse, because it means he's spent six months letting a boy believe he buried him." She leaned back. "Either way, the arrangement stands as contracted. One berth for Cyrus Blackwood. The second berth was purchased as provision against exactly this situation — a spare, in case the first passenger arrived pursued. It is not for sale, and it is not for giving away. If the Guild takes this ship, I lose everything. So I will carry two names out of this harbor tomorrow, and those names will be written in my log tonight, and after that nothing on God's green water changes them."

"Which two names?" Noor asked.

"That," the captain said, "is not my problem."

*

We walked back along the quay as the light failed, lamps blooming one by one along the harbor wall, their flames doubled and warped in the black water. Neither of us spoke for a long time. Somewhere behind us, the *Kestrel*'s rigging ticked and groaned against the dusk.

"He bought one berth for you," Noor finally said. "And one for whoever you chose."

"Yes."

"And you've known since the fisherman spoke."

"Yes."

She stopped walking. Below the sea wall, the tide sucked at the stones, patient as time itself. "Then say it, Cyrus. Say whatever it is you've been composing since we left the ship, because I can see it building behind your eyes like weather."

I looked at her — at the sharp, tired, unbreakable outline of her against the lamplight — and found that every speech I'd drafted dissolved. What remained was simpler and harder.

"If I take the berth," I said, "the atlas goes with me. Every chart. Everything he risked his life to keep out of the Guild's hands, and everything they'll use to find him if it stays here with me."

"And if you stay—"

"They'll take me to the inquest. I'm the one who completed the charts. I'm the one whose hand is on the last page. Whatever story the Guild has built, I'm the center of it, and Noor — they don't know your face. Not yet. By tomorrow night they might."

The wind came off the water and flattened the lamp flames. In that guttering light her expression didn't change, and I understood that she had done this arithmetic before I had, and had already begun grieving it, and would fight me anyway.

"You're a fool," she said quietly.

"Probably."

"My mother put me on a boat once to save my life. I spent eleven years wishing she hadn't." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and she mastered it the way she mastered everything — quickly, completely, leaving no evidence. "Don't make me spend another eleven years wondering."

Before I could answer, boots rang on the cobblestones behind us — two sets, heavy, unhurried, the cadence of men who did not need to run because they knew their quarry could not. We turned together.

The tall surveyor from the customs shed stood ten paces off with his companion flanking him, and in his hand, unrolled and catching the lamplight, was a chart. Not any chart. *One of ours* — one of the forged coastal charts we had left behind in Falmouth, weeks ago, certain it had burned.

He smiled without warmth. "Cyrus Blackwood," he said. "You're a difficult man to find. Your master sends his regards."

The world tilted. "You've seen him?"

"Oh, yes." The man stepped closer, and behind him the revenue cutter's lights slid around the headland, coming into the harbor. "In fact, he asked us to bring you in. There's a great deal he wants to explain — about the atlas, about the death, and about why, Mr. Blackwood, the Guild believes your master drowned in Falmouth Bay while standing on our payroll."
