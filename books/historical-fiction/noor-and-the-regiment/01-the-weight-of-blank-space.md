## 1. The Weight of Blank Space

The morning the child arrived, the frost on the workshop windows had grown teeth.

I know this because I was tracing them when I heard the knock—or rather, the absence of one. Twelve years in a mapmaker's workshop teaches you to distinguish sounds by their silences. A courier's boots announce themselves. The milk cart complains at the corner. But whoever left that bundle at our door understood silence as well as any cartographer, and they left nothing behind it but cold air and a shape too small to be anything but deliberate.

I had been up since four, working by lamplight on the atlas that would not finish itself. Master Hollis—dead three months now, and still the most present man in the room—had left it half-done: forty-one plates engraved, nine blank, and a set of instructions so vague they read like a riddle composed by a dying man who knew exactly how much time he had and chose not to spend any of it explaining himself. *Complete it as I would,* he'd written, which is the kind of instruction that means nothing and everything, depending on what you know about how he would.

I knew more than I wished to.

People assume a mapmaker's apprentice learns maps. What an apprentice actually learns is clients. You learn which men pay in coin fresh from the mint and which pay in notes that smell faintly of other people's fortunes. You learn that the gentleman with the shipping interests orders coastal surveys twice as often as his fleet requires, and you learn never, ever to ask why the regiment's quartermaster needs detailed elevations of valleys the regiment has no official business in. You learn that ink dries over everything, if you let it.

I had let it for twelve years. Silence was the first thing Master Hollis taught me, before lettering, before projection, before the difference between a meridian and a promise. *"A mapmaker sees everything,"* he told me my first week, tapping my chest with the brass edge of a ruler, *"and says nothing. That is the entire trade."* I had been fourteen and grateful for the work; I took it as craft advice. It took me years to understand it was also a survival strategy, and by then it fit me like my own skin.

So when I opened the door that December morning and found a basket instead of a visitor, my first thought—not my finest moment, but an honest one—was: *whatever this is, I want no part of it.*

* *

She was maybe three weeks old, wrapped in wool that had been good once and was now only determined. Her face was scrunched against the cold with the fury of someone who had been promised better. Beside her, weighted down with a stone, lay a note written in a hand that trembled at every ascender:

**Forget this face.**

Four words. No name, no date, no explanation. Just the imperative, laid out flat like something already surrendered.

I stood in the doorway long enough for the cold to make its arguments, looking up and down the lane for whoever had done this. Nothing. The frost held its impressions the way snow does—there were boot prints, one set, large, coming from the direction of the river road and returning to it. Someone had walked here carrying her, set her down, and walked away without running. That detail unsettled me more than the rest. People who abandon children run. This person had strolled, as though dropping off a parcel. As though certain the parcel would be received.

Inside, the workshop smelled of linseed oil and old paper and the ghost of pipe smoke that three months had failed to evict. I set the basket on the drafting table—the good table, the oak one Master Hollis had imported at ruinous expense because a warped surface makes liars of straight lines—and the child stopped crying immediately, which struck me as suspicious. Even at three weeks old, she seemed to understand the transactional nature of attention.

I am not a sentimental man. I will say that plainly, because what follows may not sound like it. I fed her warmed milk from a cloth because there was no one else and no alternative except knocking on doors, and knocking on doors meant questions, and questions meant the wrong ears, and the wrong ears belonged to men whose names appeared nowhere on any ledger but whose money kept the lamps lit. I changed her on a spread of backing paper meant for a survey of the Norfolk coast. She slept through the afternoon in Master Hollis's reading chair with her fists curled like fern heads, and I told myself, with each passing hour, that this was temporary. That I would think clearly tomorrow. That tomorrow I would carry her to the parish, or the constable, or some institution built precisely for abandoned children, and the whole affair would become someone else's problem, filed away like a misprinted plate.

Tomorrow came, and she was still there.

* *

Here is what I did instead, over the following days, while telling myself I was doing nothing at all: I worked.

There was comfort in it, I'll admit. An atlas does not care whether you have slept. Engraving forgives nothing but rewards everything. And the nine blank plates could not stay blank—not if I wanted to eat past winter, and not if I wanted Colonel Ashgrave's office to believe the workshop was operating normally, which I very much did, though in those early days I couldn't have told you why beyond instinct. Instinct said: *when the master dies and the atlas is unfinished and the clients are what they are, the safest thing you can be is boring.*

So I bored myself diligently. I drew coastlines. I lettered towns whose names I checked three times against the master's drafts, because a misspelled village is a small thing unless someone decides it isn't. And through all of it, the child watched me from whatever soft surface I'd rigged up nearby, tracking the movement of my hands with an intensity that flattered me until I realized she was probably just watching the lamp.

Her note stayed pinned above the engraving bench, where I'd put it intending to study the handwriting. I told myself I was examining it forensically—for clues to her origin, you understand, purely practical. But I caught myself reading it the way you read a line of poetry, again and again, listening for what's underneath. *Forget this face.* Not "her" face. Not even "this child's" face. *This face.* Whoever wrote it had stood where I stood and looked down at exactly what I looked down at, and had known, even then, that forgetting was impossible, and had asked for it anyway.

That is not the handwriting of someone disposing of a burden. That is the handwriting of someone breaking.

And broken people, in my experience, are usually broken by the same class of men who bought our maps.

* *

On the ninth day—it was the twenty-third of December, and the cold had settled in like a tenant with a lease—I made my mistake.

I had her propped in the master's chair while I corrected a river's course on Plate Thirty-Six, and she was doing her new trick, which was making a sound halfway between a coo and a demand, aimed generally at the room. I glanced over to tell her—as one does, alone, unwatched, having lost all dignity—that she had no standing to complain, and I saw her face properly for the first time in the good light.

Not beautiful in any way the carol-sellers would celebrate. Red-faced, snub-nosed, furious-eyed. Ordinary. Except—

Except that I had seen that face before. Or its architecture. Somewhere in the forty-one finished plates, in the hundreds of faces I'd passed through this room without cataloguing, there was a jaw like hers, a set of the eyes, and the knowledge of where sat in my head like a splinter I couldn't reach. I spent that evening going through the master's reference sketches, portraits done for commission, survey crew records, and found nothing, which should have relieved me and didn't. Because the splinter wasn't in the drawings. It was somewhere deeper—in the ledgers, maybe. In the client lists. In some afternoon years ago when a face like hers had stood in this doorway and I had been trained, thoroughly, to see everything and say nothing.

"Forget this face," I said aloud to her. She blew a bubble at me. "Yes, well. We'll see who manages it."

That night I dreamed of blank space. Nine plates' worth of it, stretching white to every horizon, and somewhere in the middle of the whiteness, a valley that I could almost remember drawing and could not, waking, recall at all—which is the strangest part, because I remembered every plate. I remembered them the way you remember your own signature. If there was a valley missing from my memory, then either I was going mad, or the memory had been taken from me so smoothly I'd never felt the knife.

I woke with the child's cry and the conviction, cold as the floorboards, that Master Hollis had not died of the fever the doctor wrote down.

I don't know why the two thoughts connected themselves that morning rather than any other. Grief does its accounting late, perhaps. Or perhaps it was simply that holding her—all seven pounds of her, warm and unreasonable and *placed*, deliberately placed, on our doorstep by someone who knew this workshop received things it must never acknowledge—made the silence feel different. Heavier. For twelve years silence had been my craft and my armor. Now it had weight, and the weight had a heartbeat, and the heartbeat was in my arms demanding breakfast.

* *

Mrs. Okafor came by on Christmas Eve, ostensibly returning a borrowed trivet, actually conducting one of her inspections disguised as neighborliness. She is a broad, unhurried woman who has lived across the lane longer than I've been alive and who misses nothing—a dangerous combination in a street where everyone has something to keep quiet.

"Milk," she said, setting a covered jug on my bench without being asked. "You look like a man who's forgotten food exists."

"I eat."

"You eat like an engraver. Standing, fast, and badly." Her eyes moved over the workshop with the efficiency of a customs officer, and I watched them pass over the basket—draped now, I thought cleverly, with a spare coat—and pause there for exactly half a second too long. Then they moved on. "Cold winter to be alone in."

"It suits me."

"Hm." She collected her trivet, paused at the door. From under the coat came a small, indisputable sound—the particular complaint of an infant who has opinions about nap schedules. Mrs. Okafor looked at me. I looked at her. Neither of us said anything, and in that shared silence I understood we had concluded a treaty older than both of us: *I saw nothing, and neither did you, and God help us both.*

"Merry Christmas, Mr. Yarrow," she said, and left the milk.

* *

I named her Noor on New Year's Eve, mostly because the alternative was leaving her unnamed another week, and an unnamed child starts to feel like contraband. It wasn't a family name—I haven't got a family, or hadn't until recently, depending on how you count. I found it in the master's dictionary of place-names, in the margin, in his hand: *Noor—light.* He'd written it beside a coordinate for a valley I have searched every atlas in this workshop to find and cannot find anywhere at all, a valley that appears on no survey I own, as though it had been drawn once and then unwritten.

The child slept in the basket. The fire ticked. Outside, the last day of the decade froze itself shut, and somewhere south of us men were planning the century's final year, and I sat with a pen hovering over the parish record question—*name of guardian*—understanding, finally, that I had already answered it.

Twelve years of careful silence, cracked down the middle by seven pounds of fury in a basket.

I wrote her name. And then, because the dread had settled into me like frost into glass and would not be warmed out, I went back to the drafting table, pulled the master's unfinished instructions toward me, and began, for the first time, to read them the way a guilty man reads them—looking for what he was trying to tell me before someone made sure he couldn't.

The first line, in his shaking late hand, said: *If you are reading this, Wendell, count the rivers.*

I counted the rivers.
