## 2. What the Master Drew Last

The master had been dead three months, and his ink was still wet.

That is not a figure of speech, though I grant it sounds like one. On the second morning after the child arrived—after the note that said *forget this face* in a hand I did not recognize—I found a bottle of iron-gall on the workbench with its skin unbroken, fresh as the day it came from the chandler. The master had been dead since September. Either the bottle had sat there untouched through an entire autumn of my grief, which I would have sworn on any Bible you liked was impossible, or someone had set it there recently.

I chose not to think about it. This was, at the time, my primary method of problem-solving: choose not to think about things until they went away. It had served me admirably for twelve years in a workshop that served both the Crown and men whose cargo manifests read *wool, salt, wool*. But the child had cracked something in me—a hairline fracture running straight through my careful silence—and now the things I chose not to think about kept leaking out sideways.

The baby slept in a drawer lined with flannel. Not a crib; we had no crib, and ordering one from the carpenter would have been an announcement. A drawer, though, a drawer is furniture that asks no questions. She slept with the absolute authority of the very young, fists curled like ferns before they unfurl, and every hour or so she made a small sound like a hinge deciding whether to open.

I had papers to catalogue. That was the arrangement, such as it was: the master dead, his final atlas unfinished, and I—the apprentice, the orphan he'd taken in when I was eleven and taught to hold a pen so still that water would pool on the back of my hand without trembling—left to complete what he'd begun. The Atlas of the Northern Provinces. Commissioned by the Office of Survey and Boundaries, funded, as far as I could tell, by nobody who had ever stood in a northern wind in their life.

So I began with the papers.

His study smelled the way it always had: beeswax, pipe ash, and the particular dusty sweetness of sizing. I worked by the window because December gives you perhaps six honest hours of light, and because the light let me pretend I was doing ordinary work—sorting, collating, numbering—in an ordinary workshop, for an ordinary dead man.

The first discrepancy announced itself politely, the way discrepancies do.

It was a survey of the Kelder Valley, dated fourteen years back, in the master's early hand—confident, economical, every contour placed like a stone in a wall. I knew this valley. Every apprentice knew it. It appeared in three provincial atlases, two military surveys, and the great railway prospectus that had eaten half the region's capital and returned nothing but debt and a tunnel that flooded.

Except.

Except that the valley in front of me was wider than the one I knew. Its river ran differently. And where the published surveys showed a modest fold of hills on the eastern flank, this map showed a basin—a broad, sheltered basin—with settlements marked on it. Eleven settlements, each named in the master's tidy lettering, each rendered with the loving detail he reserved for places he'd actually walked.

I checked the published atlas. The eastern flank was blank space. Not empty in the way mountains are empty—not the white nothing of terrain too high to bother with—but blank in the way a page is blank when someone has decided it should be. I had spent twelve years learning the difference between a map that doesn't know and a map that won't say. The master had taught me that difference himself, though I don't think he'd meant to.

"Someone folded you away," I told the valley, and felt foolish, and did it anyway.

The child woke and made her hinge-sound, so I fetched her, wrapped her in the grey blanket she'd arrived in, and settled her against my shoulder while I worked one-handed. This was becoming habit with alarming speed. Twelve years of disciplined solitude, undone by four pounds of infant who gripped my thumb like it was a rope over a cliff.

She liked the maps. This I noticed on the third day, and dismissed on the fourth, and could no longer dismiss on the fifth. When I spread a sheet across the drafting table and held her where she could see it, she went still—that particular infant stillness that is not sleep but attention—and she would make sounds at it. Small, urgent, conversational sounds. *Da. Ah-da. Oo.* Directed, always, at specific places on the page. Not the coast, not the borders, not the decorative cartouche with its flourished title. Inland places. River bends. The spaces between things.

"She's a baby," I informed the workshop at large. "Babies look at everything."

But babies do not look at *everything*. Babies look at faces, at lamps, at movement. This child looked at coordinates.

* * *

By the end of the week I had stopped cataloguing and started comparing, which is a different activity entirely, and a more dangerous one.

I laid the master's drafts against the older surveys—the ones locked in the flat cabinet, the ones commissioned by the regiment, stamped with the crown-and-crossed-lances that meant *this map is true because we say so*. I worked evenings, after the child slept, by lamplight, because daylight work can be seen through windows and I had begun, without quite admitting it, to want not to be seen.

Here is what I found, and I will set it down plainly because setting it down plainly is the only way I know to keep it from curdling into nightmare:

There were corrections. Dozens of them. Places where the master's finished drafts disagreed with the regiment's surveys, and where the disagreement was not error. Rivers moved—not randomly, but to where older, civilian surveys said they'd run. Roads vanished. Villages that existed in the census rolls of '91 were absent from every map drawn after '93, replaced by terrain features that fit the space like a lid fits a pot.

And in the margins, in pencil, in a hand that deteriorated across the months—from the master's neat italic to something cramped and shaking, letters leaning like drunks propping each other up—there were notes.

*Check the '89 parish roll for Ashcombe.*

*They burned it twice? Ask T.*

*T. gone. Do not ask again.*

I sat with those three lines for a long while. The lamp hissed. Upstairs, or rather three feet away, in her drawer, the child breathed.

T. I turned the initial over in my mind. Tam? Tobias? There had been a junior surveyor named Tolliver who left suddenly in the spring—left, the master had said, for a post in the colonies, though he'd said it in the flat voice he used for things he did not believe. Tolliver had been the one who walked the northern valleys. Tolliver had been the one who took the field measurements the master trusted more than his own eyes.

*Do not ask again.* Because asking got people posts in the colonies. Or graves. The distinction, I suspected, was thinner than ink.

The shaking hand frightened me most, I think. Not what it said—what it said was terrible, but terrible in the abstract way of things happening elsewhere to strangers. What frightened me was the tremor itself. The master had been the steadiest man I ever knew. His plumb lines hung true; his temper never rose; once, when a regimental captain put a boot through our door frame, he'd simply measured the damage and sent the bill. For that hand to shake, the fear must have been enormous. Sustained. The fear of a man who knows exactly how much he knows and has done the arithmetic on what knowing costs.

He died in September. Fever, the doctor said. Quick fever, the kind that takes a grown man in four days. He'd been buried before I could get word to his sister in the south.

Fever. Yes. And the fresh bottle of ink on the bench of a dead man. And a child left at the door with orders to forget her face. And a valley that eleven atlases agreed did not exist, sitting in the master's drawer with all its villages named.

I am not a clever man, whatever Mrs. Okafor says about me. But I am a mapmaker's apprentice, and mapmaking is the art of noticing what does not fit, and nothing about the master's death fit.

"He didn't die of fever," I said to Noor, because saying things aloud to her had become the way I thought. "Did he."

She was awake past her hour, propped against my arm, watching the lamplight move across the great draft of the atlas. She looked at the place where the Kelder Valley should have been—where I had, following the master's instructions and the regiment's surveys, been drawing pleasant, innocent hills—and she made her sound. *Ah-da.* Insistent. Almost reproachful, if a five-month-old can be reproachful.

"I'm drawing what they paid for," I told her. "It's not lying. It's—" And I stopped, because I heard, arriving in my own voice, exactly the tone the master must have used on himself for years. The reasonable voice. The voice that calls surrender by a softer name.

Noor stared at the blank eastern flank and babbled at it, cheerful and certain, as if she could see the eleven villages underneath. As if she'd been there. Which was absurd. Which was impossible.

Which raised the question I had been circling for days the way a moth circles a flame, wanting the light, dreading the heat: why her? Why leave a child—this child—at the door of the one workshop in the province where maps got made honestly and then dishonestly? Whose face was I supposed to forget?

I looked down at her, and she looked up at me, and she reached up and closed her fist around my lapel with the fierce grip she had, the rope-over-a-cliff grip, and I understood that I already cared about the answer more than I cared about my own safety. That was the trouble with cracks. Things got in through them.

The next morning I went back to the drawer with the shaking-hand notes, determined to be systematic, to list every correction, every erased name. And at the bottom of the folder, beneath a false lining of blotting paper that I had never found in twelve years of dusting that desk, I found a sealed envelope.

On it, in the master's steadiest hand—the good hand, the pre-fear hand—were two words:

*For Wendell.*

Underneath, smaller, pressed hard enough to score the paper:

*Burn this, or finish it. There is no third thing.*
