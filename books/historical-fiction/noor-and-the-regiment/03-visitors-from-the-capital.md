## 3. Visitors from the Capital

They came on a Tuesday, because calamity keeps office hours.

I knew before I saw them. The dog next door—Mrs. Okafor's great ugly hound, Brutus, who had never once barked at anything worth the breath—went quiet in the particular way animals go quiet when soldiers pass. Not frightened. Calculating. Then came the boots: three sets, unhurried, spaced like men who had never needed to hurry for anyone.

I had perhaps forty seconds. In forty seconds a man can do very little. I could hide Noor, which was impossible; I could hide myself, which was pointless; or I could put the kettle on, which was the only genuinely useful option available to me. Nothing disarms a soldier like tea he feels obliged to accept.

I swept the master's unfinished atlas under a linen cloth—too late, too obvious, but a man must perform his own guilt with conviction—and I carried the cradle from the front room to the back, behind the drying racks where the maps hung like flayed things, and then I opened the door wearing my best expression of a tradesman mildly inconvenienced.

There were three of them. Two soldiers in the grey-blue of the Crown's survey escort, faces like shut doors, carbines slung with the ease of furniture. And between them, a colonel.

I will say this for Colonel Ashgrave: he did not look like the villains in the penny dreadfuls Mrs. Okafor's nephew reads aloud through our shared wall. He looked like a schoolmaster who had grown tired of children. Grey at the temples, a face built for patience, gloves so clean they seemed to generate their own light. He smiled at me the way winter smiles at a late harvest.

"Master Wendell Yarrow," he said. Not a question. My name arrived in his mouth already filed, cross-referenced, and slightly disappointing.

"The workshop is closed to commissions," I said. "Since Master Hollis's death."

"I'm not here to commission." He stepped past me without asking, the way a man steps into his own house. "I'm here to collect."

* * *

The soldiers took up positions by the door with the instinct of men who had done it ten thousand times, and Ashgrave walked the workshop as a bishop walks a nave. His gloved fingers hovered near the hanging maps—not touching, never touching, touch would be vulgar—but reading them. I watched him read the coastline of the northern firth and felt something cold settle behind my sternum.

"You've kept the place well," he said. "Hollis always said you were the careful one. Twelve years an apprentice—is that not unusual? Most lads are made journeyman by seven."

"The master believed in thoroughness."

"The master believed in many things." He turned. "Tell me about his last weeks."

And there it was—the first question that mattered, dressed as small talk. I gave him the answer I had rehearsed against my own insomnia. "Slow. His hands shook. He worked less each day and corrected more. By October he could barely hold a pen."

"Corrected," Ashgrave repeated, tasting the word. "He was correcting old work, then. Not new surveys?"

"An atlas is mostly correction, sir. The world is badly drawn and insists on moving."

Something flickered across his face—amusement, possibly, or its official equivalent. "You have his wit. Did he leave notes? A journal? Draftsmen of his generation are terrible archivists; they keep everything and organize nothing."

"He left papers," I said. "I'm cataloguing them. For the atlas."

"Naturally. The atlas." He let the phrase sit between us like a coin he suspected was clipped. "When will it be finished?"

"Spring. Perhaps early summer. There are plates still to engrave—"

"February," Ashgrave said pleasantly. "It will be finished in February. The Office has waited long enough, and dead men are notoriously poor correspondents. You'll send word when the first complete proof is ready, and I'll return for it personally. I do prefer to collect things myself." His eyes moved over the shelves, the racks, the locked drawer where the master's true working drafts slept under false labels. "You strike me as a man who understands the value of finishing what others start."

I understood him perfectly. That was the trouble with courteous men; they never had to raise their voices, because they'd already decided the ending and were merely letting you catch up.

* * *

He asked about the last weeks four more ways before he asked the question underneath all of them.

"Hollis travelled, in September. Before he took ill. Where did he go?"

I had spent three months not knowing the answer, and I had learned to make ignorance sound like diligence. "His itinerary is among the papers. I haven't reached September yet. I'm working chronologically—it's the only defensible method with a dead man's handwriting."

"Chronology." He nodded slowly, approving, the way one approves of a well-made coffin. "And his health. This illness. Who attended him?"

"Dr. Penhale, from the lane."

"Penhale." He produced a small book—I want to say notebook, but it had the weight of something worse, a ledger of names already weighed—and wrote nothing in it. That was its own message: *I know this already; I am merely watching whether you lie about what I know.* "A country doctor. How convenient that no London physician examined him."

"The roads were bad."

"They usually are," Ashgrave agreed, "when someone prefers them that way."

Then his gaze went past my shoulder, and everything in me went still.

Noor's cradle stood behind the drying racks, half-hidden, but the blanket had slipped—a scrap of white in a room of parchment browns, visible to any eye trained to notice what doesn't belong. Ashgrave noticed. Of course he noticed; noticing was clearly the whole of his profession. He looked at it for perhaps two full seconds. Long enough. Long enough to count blankets, to estimate the age of the child by the size of the pillow, to file the fact in whatever ledger lived behind those patient grey eyes.

He said nothing.

I want to be clear about what that silence did to me, because anger would have been mercy. Anger has edges; you can push back against edges. But Ashgrave simply let his gaze rest on that small white corner of blanket, then lifted it away as though from a shelf item he'd decided not to purchase today, and resumed his survey of the map cases as if cradles were a category of object beneath his jurisdiction—for now.

My hands, holding the teapot I'd fetched during some earlier, saner moment of the interview, did not shake. I am rather proud of that. Everything else in me shook; the hands held the line.

"Tea?" I said.

"No, thank you." He smiled again, and it reached his eyes this time, which was infinitely worse. "I don't take anything I might later be asked to account for."

* * *

At the door he paused, one glove resting on the frame, and delivered the parting words he had plainly been saving since the doorstep.

"Hollis was a great man," he said, "and great men are frequently misunderstood by the smaller ones around them. If, in your chronological catalogue, you come across anything that seems—strange. Anomalous. A valley out of place, a river that argues with itself." His voice stayed warm, almost fatherly. "Bring it to me. Don't puzzle over it alone. Puzzling is how clever apprentices become cautionary examples."

"I'll bear it in mind, sir."

"I'm sure you'll bear everything in mind. It's what you're for." He tipped his hat a precise inch. "February, Master Yarrow. And mind the damp—paper spoils, and so do the people who keep it."

The boots receded down the lane. Brutus, sensing the balance of terror restored, resumed barking at a fence post.

I stood in the doorway until they turned the corner, then I closed the door, slid down it to the floorboards, and permitted myself exactly one minute of being a terrified young man. I kept time by my own pulse, which was generous of it.

* * *

Noor woke as I picked her up—she had slept through the entire visit with the magnificent indifference of infants, who understand instinctively that the important things are milk, warmth, and being held, and that colonels fall into none of these categories. She blinked at me, considered crying on principle, then instead seized my lapel with one fist and inspected it.

"He knows you're here," I told her. She gurgled, unimpressed. "He knows, and he said nothing, and I need you to appreciate that *nothing* is the most expensive thing a man like that ever gives away. He's not ignoring you, Noor. He's reserving you. Like a table."

She blew a bubble. Conversation concluded.

I carried her back through the workshop, past the racks, past the linen-draped atlas, and set her on my knee at the worktable while I did what I should have done months ago and had been too busy surviving to do: I went through the master's papers properly, looking not for the atlas but for September.

Where had he gone? What had he seen with those shaking hands that a colonel in spotless gloves wanted named?

The itinerary wasn't filed anywhere sensible—of course it wasn't; the man organized his life the way rivers organize valleys, entirely by accident. But tucked inside a folio of coastal soundings, folded twice, was a railway ticket stub. Third class. Dated the eleventh of September.

Destination: a station whose name had been torn away.

Not lost. Torn. Deliberately, cleanly, by someone who understood paper the way I did—who understood that a name, once removed, leaves only blank space, and blank space asks no questions.

I sat there with a sleeping child on my knee and a hole in a ticket, and I thought about the way Ashgrave's eyes had rested on that white blanket corner without a flicker of surprise. As though he'd known precisely what he would find behind my drying racks. As though he'd come, in part, to confirm a report.

Someone had told him. Someone close enough to see the cradle.

Outside, the December dark came down early, the way it does, indifferent and total, and I lit the lamp and pulled the master's September correspondence toward me, and began, chronologically, to search for a river that argued with itself.
