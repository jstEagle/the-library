## 6. The Second Set of Eyes

I have always believed that a map is the most honest liar ever devised. It tells you exactly what it wants you to believe, in a voice so calm you never think to check whether it's breathing.

For eleven days after I understood what the master had done—after the river that wasn't, after the valley folded into blank space like a body tucked into a too-small coffin—I told myself that understanding was enough. That knowledge, once held, was safe by virtue of being held quietly. This is the sort of arithmetic a man performs when he has spent twelve years learning to keep his head down: *I know a thing; no one knows I know it; therefore the thing and I are both invisible.*

The arithmetic failed on a Tuesday.

It was the seals that undid me. Wax is an honest material—it cracks where it must, it remembers its own breaking. The folio of the master's final surveys sat on the shelf where I'd left it, tied with the same cord, but the seal at its throat had been re-poured. Not well. Whoever had done it knew seals the way a forger knows faces: from the outside only. The wax sat atop the old scar instead of sinking into it, the way new snow sits on old footprints. A soldier wouldn't notice. Mrs. Okafor wouldn't notice. But I had spent my apprenticeship learning to read paper the way other men read weather, and the workshop's silence had never lied to me before.

Someone had opened the folio. Someone had looked at what the master had hidden in his margins—and then put it back, carefully, the way a burglar straightens the silverware.

* * *

I did not panic. I want credit for that. I stood in the cold center of the workroom with Noor asleep against my shoulder, her breath fogging the collar of my coat, and I performed the only ritual my training had equipped me for: I made an inventory.

Who entered this workshop? I asked myself. And the answer came back with terrible brevity: almost no one, which meant almost anyone.

There was Mrs. Okafor next door, who left milk and coal and questions she was too proud to ask aloud. There was the boy from the chandlery who delivered tallow every second Thursday and had the attention span of wet paper. There were the crown's couriers, who arrived perhaps six times a year, took their parcels, and treated conversation as a form of tax evasion. There was Colonel Ashgrave, twice now, courteous as frost. And there was Mr. Quill—the name was almost certainly false, but he paid in good coin for "antique surveys," a collector of the obsolete, a soft-spoken man with ink-stained cuffs who had visited four times since autumn and once stayed long past closing while I fetched him a specific folio.

Five doors into my life. One of them had been opened without my knowing.

I began that night to set traps, because apparently I had become the kind of man who set traps, and if you'd told the apprentice of three months ago that he would one day thread horsehair across a folio seam at eye level, checking it each morning with a jeweler's loupe, he'd have assumed you were describing some elaborate suicide.

The horsehair moved twice in the following week.

* * *

Here is what I could determine, working nights while Noor slept in the drawer-cot I'd built her beside the stove: whoever it was had not merely looked. They had *read*. Pages within the folio had been reordered—subtly, a survey dated the fourth placed ahead of one dated the second, the kind of shuffle a careless hand makes and a careful hand corrects except they hadn't corrected it, or couldn't remember the original order, which told me something else. They didn't know the master's habits the way I did. They were reading his corrections as corrections, not yet as testimony.

They knew there was something in the margins. They didn't yet know what the margins said.

That was worth perhaps a fortnight of sleep, total, spread thin.

I reorganized everything. The true sequence of the master's surveys I memorized and burned into memory the way you press flowers: the valley called Haddanmere, erased from the atlas and renamed as a tributary of the Sorrel; the coordinates corrected in that shaking hand; the little marginal notation in the master's cipher that I had finally cracked—a date, and the word *witnessed*, repeated three times, as though he'd needed to tell himself it was real.

And it was on the third repetition of that word, copying it out by candlelight while the wind worried the shutters, that Noor woke and looked at me across the room, and I saw it.

I don't know how to describe the recognition except to say it was geographic. Her face assembled itself in my mind the way coastlines assemble on a chart—suddenly, all at once, features snapping into relation. The dark eyes. The particular set of the brow, already forming the architecture of a face that would be striking rather than pretty, decisive rather than sweet. And underneath it all a resemblance to someone I had seen only as a notation, a name in an erased valley, written in the master's shaking hand beside the word *witnessed*: *the Aldana child, spared.*

Spared by whom? Left at whose door? I had spent three months assuming the note—*forget this face*—was an order from the regiment, a loose end delivered to the man hired to tidy it. But what if it was the opposite? What if the master had been the one doing the leaving, smuggling the last witness of Haddanmere out of the valley under the very nose of the men who'd emptied it, delivering her to the one place in the province where erasure was a trade?

Forget this face. He hadn't been instructing me to destroy her. He'd been begging me to protect her—with the only language our profession shared: the imperative mood.

Noor watched me across the room with those dark eyes, chewing on the corner of a practice sampler, entirely unaware that she was evidence. "Bah," she informed me, with great authority.

"Yes," I agreed. My voice came out strange. "Precisely."

* * *

Paranoia, I will say in its defense, is simply attention with nowhere left to go. Once I had seen the re-poured seal, I saw the workshop differently. The dust on the shelf ledges had been disturbed in patterns too regular for drafts. My spare key—I kept it behind the loose brick by the water barrel—sat a half-turn off from where my fingers expected it. Small things. The kind of small things that are either nothing at all or everything, and no instrument in the workshop could measure the difference.

I caught myself watching Mrs. Okafor over the fence. She brought milk as usual, set it down as usual, complained about the price of coal as usual, and I stood there cataloguing her hands for wax residue like a customs officer. She was seventy years old if she was a day. She had known the master for thirty years. She had also, I realized with a lurch, known him better than I had—informed on him, perhaps; conspired with him; possibly loved him, in whatever complicated way people love across fences. Any of those histories could make her a spy. Any of them could make her the only ally I had. The two possibilities wore identical coats.

"You look ill, Yarrow," she said, squinting at me. "Sit down before you fall down."

"I'm perfectly well."

"Mm." She gathered her basket. "When a man says *perfectly*, he means the opposite. Your master used to do the same. Perfectly well for a decade, and then dead in October." She left the milk. She did not ask about the smoke I'd seen her noticing, or the light burning at four in the morning. Trust, in that yard, was a currency neither of us would spend aloud.

Quill came on the Friday.

He arrived with snow in his collar and his usual gentle apology for existing, and he wanted, he said, anything from the master's later period—provincial surveys, working drafts, "the imperfect things, Mr. Yarrow, the things a great mapmaker would be embarrassed by." He smiled when he said it. His cuffs were ink-stained, as always. And I heard myself say, easily, warmly, lying through my teeth: "Most of the working drafts went into the fire last month. Chimney trouble."

A lie. My first deliberate lie to a client in twelve years, and it came out smooth as varnish, which frightened me more than the seal had. Because here was the thing I understood, standing there in the lamplight while Noor napped twenty feet away: the spy and I were now doing the same job. We were both reconstructing the master's secrets from residue. The only question was which of us would finish first, and what we intended to do with the answer.

Quill bought two obsolete coastal charts and left forty minutes of coin on the table—generous, I noted, for maps any fool could buy elsewhere. At the door he paused. "Your little one," he said, nodding toward the drawer-cot. "She has her father's coloring, I'd wager?"

"She's not mine," I said. "Nor anyone's, that I know of."

"Ah," said Quill. "Foundlings. The century seems full of them." And he tipped his hat and went out into the snow, and I stood listening to his footsteps until they faded, telling myself that the question had been idle courtesy, that men ask such things of bachelors with cradles all the time, that the sick lurch beneath my ribs was only indigestion.

But that night, after the lamp was out, I lay awake doing the arithmetic again, the honest kind this time. Someone was reading the master's margins. Someone knew—or suspected—that the atlas was testimony wearing the costume of geography. Ashgrave wanted the child. Quill wanted the drafts. The seals were being re-poured by patient hands. And somewhere in an erased valley called Haddanmere, a name existed that matched the face of the sleeping girl whose existence I had been ordered to forget and had instead, without ever deciding to, made the center of my life.

Trust, I thought, staring at the ceiling beams, had become a luxury item. Like vermilion. Like saffron. Something beautiful you purchased knowing it might be cut with poison.

In the morning I would move the true folios—out of the workshop, into hiding, split between places no single search could reach. In the morning I would decide whether Mrs. Okafor's fence-side silences were loyalty or surveillance, and stake everything on the answer.

And in the morning, too, there came a knock at the door—early, unhurried, and wrong somehow in its rhythm, the knock of a man who had knocked here before but never at this hour.

I picked up Noor, who was warm and heavy and utterly oblivious to being the most dangerous document in the province, and I went to see who had learned to open doors without opening them.

Through the frosted glass: a silhouette. An officer's cap. And standing slightly behind it, half-hidden by the frame, a second figure whose posture I recognized even before the latch turned—

Mr. Quill, holding his hat, waiting to be let in.
