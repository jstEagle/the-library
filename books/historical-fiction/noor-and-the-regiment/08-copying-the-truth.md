## 8. Copying the Truth

The trouble with forging a lie is that you only need to be clever. Forging a truth requires you to be a saint, a criminal, and a very good draughtsman, all at once, and preferably before the ink freezes.

I learned this over the first week of December, working by candlelight in a workshop so cold that my water pot grew a skin of ice each night, which I broke each morning like a man cracking an egg he intended to eat for courage. Ashgrave had given me until the New Year. What he meant was: give me the atlas by the thirty-first, faithful as scripture, and hand me the girl. What I had decided—somewhere between his offer of a post in the capital and the memory of the master's shaking corrections—was something else entirely. I would finish his atlas. Faithful as scripture.

Scripture, after all, has two testaments.

By day I worked on the false one. This was easier than it should have been; I had twelve years of practice drawing what men wanted to see. The valley became a dry ravine. The village of Kessel's Hollow—I had finally found its name, scratched into the margin of a survey from '93, half-erased but legible if you held the page to the window light—became a bend in a river that did not exist. I gave the river a gentle course, a merciful geography. No burned crofts. No churchyard with more graves than parishioners. Just water, going about its honest business.

Ashgrave would love it. It was exactly the kind of map a man draws when he has been promised something.

* * *

By night, I made the other one.

The problem was not drafting it. The master had done the hard work years ago; all I had to do was read him correctly, and reading him correctly turned out to be a matter of humility. His true map—the one encoded across four separate plates, each fragment innocent alone—was not drawn in ink at all. It was drawn in *choices*. Where a contour bent too early. Where a stream was named twice. Where blank space sat where blank space had no business sitting. Assemble the choices and you got the valley entire: every homestead, the mill, the road the regiment took in February of '96, and the coordinates of the place where the road stopped taking anyone anywhere.

The problem was duplication. A copy of the true map, hidden where? Under a floorboard? Ashgrave's soldiers had lifted three floorboards already, casually, the way a man checks a watch, while telling me about the weather in London. In the stove? The first thing anyone does in a freezing workshop is light the stove. Behind the plaster? I had watched them tap the walls with a knuckle like physicians percussioning a chest.

No. Whatever hid the truth had to hide it somewhere no soldier would ever think to look, because looking there would require imagining that a mapmaker loved anything.

It was Noor who solved it, in the way children solve things, by being entirely unaware there was a problem. She sat on the floor by the stove with her sampler—Mrs. Okafor had insisted a girl must learn her stitches, and had provided the linen and the floss and the moral certainty—and she was embroidering the alphabet. Lopsided A. Drunken B. A C that had clearly lost an argument with the needle. Below the letters she was working a border of little flowers, and I watched her needle dip through the linen, and I thought: *stitches*. Rows of them. Thousands. A grid.

A grid is a coordinate system. Any schoolgirl's sampler is a map of thread.

I could draw the true map in silk. Stitch it into the flowers of a child's sampler, line by line, in colors no one would question—a green here for a stem, a blue there, a brown for soil. The valley's contours running through the floral border like a river through a garden. The coordinates tucked into the cross-stitch of the alphabet itself, letter by letter, number by number, legible only if you knew to count the stitches between them. And Noor would carry it. Who searches a four-year-old's embroidery? Who looks at a child's clumsy flowers and sees a massacre's testimony?

Who, having seen it, could prove it was anything but a little girl's stitching?

I sat down that night and began designing flowers that were also hillsides. It was the strangest commission of my career, and I have drawn coastlines for smugglers and estate boundaries for men whose lawyers wept. I worked until the candle drowned in its own wax, and then I worked a while longer by the stove-light, and when I finally looked up, Noor had fallen asleep against the master's old chair with the needle still in her fist, and I carried her to bed thinking: *this is what it costs, and this is what it buys*, and for once those sums balanced.

* * *

There remained the matter of delivery. A map in a sampler is evidence, not action. Evidence locked in a workshop burns with the workshop. It needed to travel—to London, to a printer with a press and a spine, someone who could strike copies faster than Ashgrave could burn them. And I knew precisely no one. Twelve years of careful silence means twelve years of carefully knowing nobody. My circle of acquaintance consisted of a dead man, a colonel who wanted me compliant or dead, a toddler, and Mrs. Okafor next door, who left milk on our step and coal in our scuttle and questions nowhere in evidence.

So naturally it was Mrs. Okafor I went to, because when your list is short enough, prudence becomes a luxury good.

She received me in her kitchen, which smelled of bread and camphor and the particular authority of a woman who has outlived three landlords. She poured tea without asking whether I wanted any. She was, I noticed, not surprised to see me, which should have warned me.

"Mr. Yarrow," she said. "You've come about the child."

"I've come about—" I stopped. Started again. "How did you know it was about the child?"

"Because everything in that house is about the child now, dear, and you've just taken four minutes to work up to asking me something, and you only do that when it's dangerous." She folded her hands. "Ask."

And here is where I must confess to cowardice of a very specific vintage. I had prepared a speech. A careful speech, hedged and deniable, offering her a fiction about delivering a gift to a cousin in the capital. I opened my mouth to deliver it, and what came out instead—because some part of me had evidently grown tired of my own handwriting on the world—was the truth.

"The atlas the master made isn't a map," I said. "It's a confession. He drew the regiment's crimes into it and disguised them as geography. Ashgrave wants it delivered clean, and the girl with it, and I intend to give him the atlas and keep the truth and send it to a printer, and I need someone to carry it to London who doesn't look like a man carrying something. That's the whole of it. You may throw me out now."

Mrs. Okafor looked at me for a long moment. Then she got up, took down a tin from the high shelf, and opened it. Inside lay a packet of letters, tied in ribbon gone soft with age, and I recognized the hand on the topmost envelope before I recognized anything else about the situation.

The master's hand.

"He wrote to me every month for six years," she said. "Names. Dates. What the maps said and what the ground said. I passed them to a man at a printing house off Paternoster Row, a Mr. Adeyemi, who has been waiting a long time for enough paper to make the empire blush." She set the tin on the table between us like a card laid face-up. "He begged me never to tell you. Said your silence was the safest thing in that building, and that silence, kept long enough, becomes a kind of innocence worth protecting."

"And now?"

"Now he's dead, the colonel comes and goes like bad weather, and you're stitching secrets into nursery linens—which, incidentally, I know about, because I supplied the floss and I can count, Mr. Yarrow, whatever else you believe." Her eyes were not unkind. They were simply finished with pretending. "Now the silence has a price on it either way, so it may as well buy something."

I sat there feeling like a man who has spent twelve years guarding a door, only to learn the house had another entrance the entire time, and that his landlady held the key. "Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because I didn't know yet whose side you'd landed on. Silence keeps both ways open." She untied the ribbon, selected one letter, and pushed it toward me. "Read the last line."

I read it. The master's writing, steadier than I'd ever seen it in his final year, dated the spring before he died:

*If anything happens to me, the boy will know what to do. He has been learning longer than either of us admitted.*

"He knew," I said. My voice came out strange. "He knew I'd find it. He left it for me to find."

"He left it for whoever had the spine," Mrs. Okafor corrected gently. "It happened to be you. Don't make it mystical; make it useful." She refilled my cup. "The sampler goes to London with me Thursday, when I take the market cart. Adeyemi will cut the stitches from the linen himself—he's a printer, they're used to disassembling things—and have plates cut within the fortnight. You'll want the atlas finished and convincing by the thirty-first?"

"You'll carry it knowing what it is? Knowing what happens to couriers when this goes wrong?"

"Mr. Yarrow." She looked at me the way one looks at a slow apprentice. "I have been carrying things for that house since before you could hold a pen. The only thing that's changed is that now you're asking me to."

* * *

That night I stitched until my fingers ached, Noor asleep upstairs, the stove ticking, frost ferning up the inside of the glass. The border flowers grew hillside by hillside. The alphabet accumulated its secret arithmetic. Somewhere around the third hour I realized I was humming, which I never did, and stopped, and then started again, because no one was listening and the tune belonged to nobody.

Silence, I thought, pulling a knot tight. Everyone treats it as the absence of a thing. But it isn't. It's a room, and you decide what's kept in it. For twelve years I'd kept nothing in mine—kept it swept and bare and safe. Now it held a valley, a village, a name, a child, a plan, and a landlady with a tin box and a Thursday cart.

On Wednesday evening, as I trimmed the last thread and held the sampler up to the lamp—flowers, alphabets, a sleeping child's small masterpiece, innocent as morning—there came a knock at the workshop door below.

Three knocks. Even. Patient. The knock of a man who already knows the door will open.

Through the frosted pane, a shadow in a greatcoat, and the smell of woodsmoke that was not coming from my stove.

Mrs. Okafor was not due to leave until morning. But the cart, I remembered suddenly, with a cold that had nothing to do with December, was loaded the night before.

And someone had just arrived to see what it carried.
