## 9. The Century Turns

The last day of the century arrived the way debt collectors do—on schedule, and with soldiers.

I had spent the morning pretending it was an ordinary one. I fed Noor her porridge. I sharpened two pencils to needles and let them go blunt again, because my hands wanted occupation more than they wanted sharp pencils. I wound the workshop clock, which had run four minutes slow since October, as if accuracy in small things might compensate for the enormous fraud sitting wrapped in brown paper on my workbench.

The atlas weighed less than a truth should have. That was the first thing I noticed when I lifted it: how light a lie can be, when you've made it well.

Mrs. Okafor had come at dusk the night before, ostensibly returning a borrowed colander, actually confirming the route. "The printer's man waits until the second of January," she'd said, not looking at me while she said it, which was her way of granting deniability to us both. "After that, your needlework is nobody's child but its own." She'd set down the colander, and beside it, without comment, a jar of goose fat for Noor's chapped cheeks. Then she'd looked at me once—just once—with the expression of a woman who has buried enough husbands' debts and sons' wars to know exactly what a last night looks like, and gone home.

Noor was four now. Four years old and old enough to know something was wrong, because children are barometers and I am apparently transparent as glass. She kept tugging my sleeve and asking, in her solemn way, whether we would hear the church bells from the bridge this year.

"We'll hear bells," I told her.

I did not tell her where we would be standing when we heard them. Even then I hadn't decided. A mapmaker's failing, perhaps—I couldn't chart a route until I knew the terrain, and the terrain was Ashgrave's face, and no survey of mine had ever captured what lived behind it.

* * *

They came at half past three, while the light was going thin and blue. I heard the wheels first—the capital sends carriages where the county sends carts—and then boots on frozen mud, six boots minimum, which meant soldiers, because the Colonel traveled like a man who expected to be ambushed by arithmetic.

I put Noor in her corner with the sampler and the slate. She had been drilled on this the way sailors drill for fire: if men come, you sit, you sew, you look at nothing. She was very good at looking at nothing. She had learned it from me, which is the kind of inheritance I never intended to leave anyone.

Colonel Ashgrave entered the workshop the way he always did, removing his gloves finger by finger as though the room were a document he intended to read closely. Two soldiers took up positions by the door. A third remained visible through the window, breath steaming, doing an extremely poor job of pretending to admire our woodpile.

"Yarrow." He said my name the way he said everything, courteously, as if we were colleagues. "A significant evening."

"The turn of the year," I agreed. "And the delivery of the master's final atlas." I lifted the parcel from the bench. My hands, I am pleased to report, did not shake. Twelve years of apprenticeship had taught them steadiness under every pressure except tenderness; fraud, apparently, they could manage.

"You've checked the plates?"

"Every sheet. Every coordinate." This was true. I had checked them obsessively, the way a forger checks his forgery, and found them flawless—which is to say, faithful to the falsified surveys, innocent of valleys, silent as the grave the master was presumably resting in, though I had long since stopped believing he was resting anywhere peacefully.

Ashgrave untied the string himself. He was not a man who let subordinates touch his documents, and I understood why now; trust, in his profession, was just a slower form of suicide.

He turned pages. The room was quiet enough that I could hear the clock losing its four minutes, patient as ever.

He reached the valley.

I had done my work well. Where the master's true map held a valley with eleven settlements and a river and the coordinates of a massacre dressed as a flood, my copy held the official version: blank space, the polite nothing the Crown had ordered, a river drawn slightly east of where any river actually ran. I had copied the erasure itself, faithfully. There is a horrible craft in copying absence.

Ashgrave's finger stopped on that page.

"Here." He tapped twice. "You'll confirm this region was surveyed in November."

"It was surveyed," I said. "The results were... inconclusive. Weather, mostly." The master's own phrase, recycled. It had worked on him in September. It did not work now.

Ashgrave looked up at me, and for a moment—and I want to be honest here, because honesty is the whole point of what I burned for—he wasn't angry. He looked almost disappointed, the way a teacher looks at a student who has failed an exam he secretly hoped the boy would pass.

"Yarrow." He set the atlas down. "In March, I stood in this room and asked you about your master's final weeks, and you gave me answers that were technically accurate and entirely useless. I noted it. I said nothing, because an apprentice who lies badly is an apprentice who can be taught. In October I offered you a future, and you declined it with such exquisite politeness that I nearly admired you." He straightened his gloves over his arm. "But I have read this man's maps for thirty years. I know his hand better than his own mother did. And this—" he touched the blank space gently, obscenely, like a man stroking a headstone he'd commissioned, "—is not his hand. This is yours. You've copied his silence and gotten it wrong."

Outside, the soldier by the window shifted his weight. Snow creaked somewhere.

"There is another map," Ashgrave said. It was not a question, so I did not insult him with an answer. "There is always another map with this man. Where is it?"

"In the fire," I said, "where all drafts go."

It was, I reflected later, the correct answer delivered at precisely the wrong altitude of calm. Ashgrave studied me for a long moment, and I watched him arrive at the arithmetic: an apprentice, a dead master, an erased valley, and a cradle that had stood in this workshop since the spring of '95. Four facts. He had known three of them for months. The fourth he had merely needed confirmed, and I had just confirmed it with my entire face.

"Noor," he said.

My blood did something dramatic. I kept my voice level through what I can only assume was sheer spite. "She's asleep."

"She isn't. Children never sleep when it matters." He turned toward her corner, and I moved—one step, involuntary, a body betraying its training—and he stopped. Smiled, thinly. "There it is. The whole confession, in a single step."

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, the way catastrophes do, with none of the dignity that novelists will someday lend them.

He did not search for the map. That was the thing I had prepared for all winter, the ransacking, the torn bindings, the lantern held to every seam—and instead he simply looked around the workshop, at the flat files and the copper plates and the press, at twelve years of my life arranged neatly against the walls, and made his decision the way empires make decisions: cheaply, and from a distance.

"Bramley," he said to the doorway. "Send for the oil."

I heard the words before I understood them, the way you hear thunder before you accept the storm.

"Colonel—"

"The regiment cannot afford examples it doesn't make, Yarrow. A cartographer who buries evidence inside an atlas is a traitor. An apprentice who abets him is a loose end. But a *fire*—" he picked up his hat from the bench, unhurried, "—a fire is a tragedy. A fire explains a missing apprentice, a missing child, an incomplete atlas. Grief is very thorough. I recommend it to everyone."

"And the girl?" My voice cracked on it. I have never forgiven my voice that crack, though Noor has long since told me she finds it heroic, which is the sort of lie daughters learn specifically to give their fathers.

Ashgrave paused at the door. For a moment something moved behind his eyes—not mercy exactly, but the memory of mercy, the shape it leaves. "The master begged too," he said quietly. "At the end. Same offer, same terms. He chose his maps over the post I gave him the chance to earn." He settled the hat on his head. "Choose faster than he did."

Then he was gone into the blue evening, and the soldiers were pouring oil along my floorboards with the brisk competence of men who had done this to other workshops in other counties, and the century had perhaps ninety minutes left to live, and so, I understood, did I.

* * *

I will not describe the next hour in full. Some chapters of a life deserve the same treatment the master gave the valley: compression, omission, a river drawn slightly east of the truth.

Suffice to say that Noor did not cry. Suffice to say that Mrs. Okafor's back gate had been left unlatched for three days on the chance it would be needed, and that her coal shed smelled of her bread and her lavender and safety, and that we waited in the dark among the coal sacks while the light through the cracks went from blue to orange, and Noor held her sampler against her chest the way other children hold dolls.

The sampler. God help me, the sampler. Fourteen inches of homespun with a wobbly alphabet across the top—NOOR AGE 4, in thread she'd chosen herself, the *R* backwards—and beneath the letters, invisible unless you knew to count stitches, the valley. Eleven settlements. The river where it truly ran. Coordinates corrected in a shaking hand, translated by a forger's apprentice into cross-stitch, hidden in plain cloth. I had spent November nights teaching myself to think in thread. I had pricked my fingers bloody. It was the best map I ever made and it looked, to any soldier who might ever inspect it, like a child's gift to her guardian.

Through the cracks in the shed door I watched the workshop burn.

I had expected grief. What I felt instead was stranger—a terrible lightness, like setting down a load I'd forgotten I carried. Twelve years of silence, going up. The master's press, the flat files, the false atlases and the true ones and all the careful neutral paper between them, the smell of ink and linseed that had been the only home my nose knew—all of it feeding the flames with a sound like applause. The empire was destroying the evidence, and the evidence was mostly me, and I stood there in a coal shed holding a four-year-old and thought: *let it*. Let them burn the archive. We kept the original.

Noor watched the fire too, her face lit orange, utterly solemn. Then, as the roof beams fell in a fountain of sparks—

"Bells," she said.

She was right. Midnight had come. Somewhere beyond the smoke, the parish church was ringing in the new century, and the sound came over the rooftops indifferent to arson, indifferent to empire, indifferent to the fact that the old world had just tried to kill us and missed. Bells, ringing out over a burning workshop and a sleeping town and a valley whose name no living map admitted to, and my daughter—because she was mine now, whatever the papers said, whatever the colonel believed; four years of porridge and letters and stitched coordinates make a claim no registry can contest—my daughter counted the strokes with great seriousness, got lost after nine, and declared the century officially begun.

We went out the back while the town still watched the front. Over the wall, down the frozen lane, toward the coast road and the second of January and a printer who owed Mrs. Okafor a favor older than either of us. Behind us the sky glowed like a wound. Ahead of us the road was black and unmapped, which is to say, honest.

Noor walked with the sampler bundled inside her coat, and once, when the sparks blew far enough to reach us, she asked whether we could go back for anything.

I thought of the copper plates. The press. The master's chair with its worn arms.

"One thing," I said. "We're carrying it."

She patted the bundle at her chest, satisfied, and lengthened her stride to match mine, and together we walked into the year 1900 with the truth in needlework and nothing else to our names—which, if you must know, is one more thing than the empire could say it owned honestly.

The bells followed us for a mile. After that, only our footsteps, and the sea, waiting somewhere ahead in the dark to be drawn correctly.
