## 10. Margins and Open Country

The sea took some getting used to. I had spent my life drawing water as lines on paper—hatched, stippled, obedient—and here it was, all of it, moving without permission. For the first week in Greyharbor I stood at the window of our rented room like a man who suspected the tide of forgery.

Noor had no such suspicions. She called it "the big grey" and demanded it be taken to bed with her.

It is March now, or nearly. Four months since the workshop burned, since I walked out of Ashgrave's firelight with a child on my hip and a sampler under my coat, and every day since has been a small act of cartography: finding where we were, marking it, deciding whether to stay.

We stayed.

* * *

Greyharbor is the kind of town that exists on official maps as a smudge—a fishing port too poor to flatter, too far east to matter. The kind of place, I have learned, where erased people wash up. The landlady, Mrs. Tregowan, asked no questions beyond whether we'd be wanting the room heated, which was fortunate, because my answers would all have been lies of varying quality.

I set myself up as what I am: a draughtsman. Charts for fishermen, plans for the chandler, a fair copy of the parish bounds for the vicar, who paid me in shillings and disapproval in equal measure. Honest work. The irony is not lost on me that I spent twelve years learning to lie with ink and now make my bread telling the truth about where the rocks are, so no one drowns.

The sampler hangs on our wall. To any visitor it is a child's needlework gone strangely wrong: wobbly alphabets, a lopsided house, and then a stretch of what looks like decorative squiggling—the master's valley, its rivers, its villages, its coordinates stitched in cross-stitch by a two-year-old's hands guided over mine. Ashgrave burned the workshop looking for a map. It hung in plain sight the whole while, next to the dried lavender.

Mrs. Okafor's letter reached us in January, carried by a printer named Hobb who smelled of lamp oil and revolution and pretended to smell only of lamp oil. She wrote four lines:

*The boy is safe. The printer asks for the pattern. Send it by the usual means. You did well, Wendell, though you will not believe me until you see it done.*

I copied the coordinates into a proper chart—clean, professional, unimpeachable work—and sent it folded inside a delivery of fishing charts to Hobb's cousin in the capital. If you know where to look, and the survivors of the valley are learning to look, the numbers resolve into a place the empire insists does not exist.

* * *

They began coming in February.

First a woman with a limp and her sister's child, who stood in my doorway holding a scrap of paper like it might bite. "You're the one who draws," she said. Not a question. "My brother said: find the man who draws, and he'll tell you true whether your village is still on the earth."

I spread out the old surveys on my table and showed her. There. And there. Renamed, re-rivered, folded into blank space—but there. Her hands went flat on the table and she cried without sound, which is a skill I recognized, having practiced it myself for twelve years.

Then came others. A stonemason who'd been press-ganged into building a road through the valley and wanted the road marked so his dead could be found under it. An old schoolteacher who remembered every family name and dictated them to me for three days straight while Noor slept against my back, and I wrote them into the margin of the true map, because a coordinate without a name is just arithmetic.

Hobb's second printing sold out in a fortnight. He writes me now twice a month, always in the same dry code: *Demand continues. Certain parties in the capital remain displeased but appear uncertain where to direct their displeasure.* Uncertain, I suspect, because the empire cannot burn down every fishing town in the country, though I do not doubt Ashgrave would try if ordered.

I think about him more than I should. Not with fear anymore—or not mostly fear. With the wary attention of a draughtsman who knows an unfinished survey when he sees one. Men like Ashgrave do not accept being made ridiculous. Somewhere he is redrawing his own map of the world with me in it, and I would be a fool to assume the margin around my name stays blank forever.

But that is a worry for daylight hours. At night there is only Noor.

* * *

She is three now, or near enough—we settled on a birthday in autumn, arbitrarily, because every child deserves a day when people say her name kindly. She talks constantly, mostly to the maps. This remains unnerving.

Today I sat her on my knee at the drafting table with a stub of pencil and taught her coastlines.

"No, little one—not like that. A coast isn't a fence. It isn't straight because the sea wouldn't allow it. Watch." I drew the line slow, letting it wander, dip, jut. "The truth of a coastline is that it's rough. Anyone who gives you a smooth line is lying to you, either about the coast or about something else."

"Rough," she repeated gravely, and dragged her pencil along, and produced a shape like a startled hedgehog.

"It's honest," I told her. "That's what matters."

"Is it *true*?" she asked, because I have made the mistake of teaching her that word, and now everything must be interrogated. Is it true, the cat is orange? Is it true, supper? Is it true, we can't go back?

"It's true enough for now," I said. "When you're bigger, you'll go and measure it yourself, and then it'll be true because *you* say so. That's how it works. That's how it's supposed to work."

She considered this with the full weight of her small serious face—her face, which once I was ordered to forget, and which I have instead committed to memory more thoroughly than any atlas. Then she bent over her hedgehog-coast and added, unprompted, a small stick figure standing on it.

"Who's that?"

"You," she said. "And you." Another figure, taller, holding what was unmistakably meant to be a pencil. "We're looking at true things."

I had to leave the table for a moment. Some feelings arrive like high tide; you cannot argue with them, you can only wait them out in another room.

* * *

This evening Mrs. Tregowan brought up a parcel that had come on the mail coach. Hobb's wrapping. Inside: a printed sheet, fresh off his press, and beneath the false imprint—"J. Merriweather, Marine Surveyor"—a title that made my chest hurt to read:

*A True Description of the Vale of Ellowmere, with its Rivers, Roads, and Inhabitants, as Erased from the Public Record. Together with the Names of the Dead.*

Not my name anywhere. Not the master's. Just the work, loose in the world like a seed on the wind.

And tucked behind it, a note in Hobb's hand: *A woman came asking after the draughtsman who supplied this. Military bearing, civilian coat. I told him nothing, because there was nothing to tell—but he bought a copy, and he read it standing in the street, and he smiled. Thought you'd want to know. Burn this.*

I held the note over the lamp and watched it curl.

Ashgrave had read it. He knew what it was. He had smiled.

I sat for a long while listening to the sea work at the shore below, patient as a proofreader, and I thought about what the smile might mean. Condemnation, probably. Contempt. Or perhaps—he was a careful man, a thorough man—perhaps he had read the names of the dead, all forty-one of them in the margin, and understood for the first time exactly what his regiment had erased, and what manner of man that made him. A smile can be many things. So can a map.

Behind me, Noor slept in the big bed, one arm flung over her hedgehog-coast drawing, guarding it the way children guard treasure: completely by accident.

I took a fresh sheet and began to draw. Not orders this time. Not evidence, even. Just the harbour at Greyharbor as it actually is—the crooked mole, the honest rocks, the church spire slightly leaning because everything true leans somewhere. And in the margin, where the master once hid his dead, I wrote the only legend that seemed fitting, the one I intend to teach her first, before all the others:

*Drawn from observation. Corrected by love. Subject to revision.*

Somewhere out in the dark the tide came in, keeping its own accounts. We would keep ours.
