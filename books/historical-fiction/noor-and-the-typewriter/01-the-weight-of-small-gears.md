## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The summons arrived on a Tuesday, which Odile would later consider the last ordinary day of her life.

It came in the form of a boy—fourteen, maybe fifteen, with the pinched, important look of someone who had been told he was delivering urgent news and had practiced looking grave the whole way across town. He stood in the doorway of Voss & Nephew Clockmakers with his cap clutched to his chest and announced, to the shop at large, that Director Kelweg's office required the presence of a clockmaker at the observatory before nightfall.

"Required," Uncle Marek repeated from behind the counter, in the tone of a man tasting a word to see if it had gone off.

"Required," the boy confirmed. "The master clock has stopped."

At this, every clock in the shop did what clocks do, which is nothing at all except continue ticking, but Odile felt the silence between their ticks stretch suddenly thin, like wire drawn to breaking.

"The *master* clock," Marek said. "Stopped."

"That's what they told me to say." The boy shifted his weight. "There was more but I've forgotten it. Something about authority and seals. There's a seal." He produced an envelope, damp where his hand had sweated against it, bearing a stamp that looked official enough to bite.

Odile took it before her uncle could. This was her habit—not bravery, exactly, but a kind of practiced interception, the way she'd learned to step into doorways, conversations, moments of danger, so smoothly that no one afterward could quite remember whether she had always been there or had simply appeared. Invisibility, she had found, was not the art of hiding. It was the art of being already present when people looked, so unremarkable that their eyes slid past without registering the effort it took to be so perfectly unremarkable.

She broke the seal. The letter inside was brief, typed, and terrible in the way official letters are terrible: not through threat but through precision. *The Observatory requires the services of a qualified repairer. The Voss establishment is named. Report by six o'clock. Credentials will be verified.*

"Credentials will be verified," Marek read over her shoulder, and then, lower, only for her: "Well."

"Well," Odile agreed.

They stood together in the shop while the boy waited by the door, pretending not to listen, which meant listening with everything he had. Around them the clocks ticked on—wall clocks and mantel clocks, carriage clocks and one enormous regulator that had come out of a customs house and kept time with the smugness of a retired bureaucrat. Her uncle's shop smelled of oil and brass polish and the faint sweetness of cedar in the old cases. It was, Odile thought, the safest room she knew. Every gear in it obeyed honest laws.

"I'll go," she said.

"You don't have to. I can close up. My legs still work, whatever the doctor says about my knees."

"And you'll spend the whole tram ride composing speeches for the Director's underlings, and by the time you arrive you'll have offended someone important." She handed back the letter. "I'm quieter than you."

"You're quieter than furniture," Marek said. "That's precisely what worries me."

* * *

Here is what Odile Voss knew about herself, arranged in the order she thought of these things each morning like a woman checking the locks on a door:

Her name was Odile Voss. This was both true and not true, depending on how far back one looked, and the trick of her life was never letting anyone look further back than Tuesday.

Her father had died four years ago in Rastenberg, two hundred kilometers south, and was buried there under a name that was almost his real one—one letter different, chosen because a stonecutter charging by the character could be trusted to be discreet about discounts.

Her mother, Anouk Voss, lived above the shop with Odile and her brother Tomas, and had not left the apartment in three weeks, and was ill in a way the doctor described with gentle vagueness—"tiredness," "the heart's own schedule"—while his eyes said something else entirely.

Their papers were forged. Not crudely—her father had been many things, careless not among them—but forged all the same, by a man in Rastenberg whose fee had been paid in watch movements and whose last words to her father, according to family legend, were "Never let them count your shadows twice."

And she was good with small gears. Better than good. Her hands, when they worked, belonged to a person who had no secrets at all, and this was the closest thing to rest she ever found.

She dressed for the observatory the way she dressed for everything: plainly, correctly, forgettably. Grey skirt, white blouse with the collar buttoned to the second button, her father's old work apron folded into a canvas bag along with her tools. In the bag's inner pocket, sewn there by her own hand, lay the slim leather folder holding the family papers—the ones that would be verified, if verification came, and which would hold, because they always held, because they had been made by a man who understood that a forgery's genius lies not in its perfection but in its believable imperfections. One ink blot on Tomas's birth record. A slightly crooked stamp on her mother's residency paper. Flaws you could find, if you went looking for fraud, and would reassure you that you had found all there was.

She kissed her mother's forehead—Anouk was sleeping in the chair by the window, a shawl around her shoulders even though it was June—and told her she'd be back by nine. Her mother opened her eyes halfway.

"The observatory?" she murmured. "Why you? Why not Marek?"

"Because Marek charges too much and talks too much, and the city wants cheap and silent."

"Mm." Her mother's hand found hers briefly. Her fingers were cool. "Be careful, little owl. Big buildings eat quiet girls."

"They'll choke on me, Mama."

This got the ghost of a smile, and Odile carried it down the stairs like a coin in her pocket, spending its warmth slowly all the way to the tram stop.

* * *

Vellengard in June was a city pretending to be well. The harbor worked; the fish market shouted; the trams rang their bells with the confidence of machines that expected tomorrow to arrive on schedule. But Odile had grown up learning to read cities the way she read movements—by the parts that hesitated. And everywhere, lately, there were hesitations. Posters reprinted with new rules pasted crookedly over old ones. Men at the harbor office who used to wave people through now wrote things down. The word *registry*, which two years ago had meant nothing, now arrived in sentences the way weather arrives in sailors' talk: often, and with lowered voices.

The census takers had started coming in spring. Polite young men and women with portable typewriters in leather cases, going door to door, asking names and birthplaces and occupations, typing answers onto carbon-backed forms with a brisk clatter that Odile had heard through the shop window three times now and hated each time a little more. They were not unkind, most of them. That was the worst part. Kindness, her father had taught her, makes people answer questions.

The tram climbed away from the harbor and the city opened below it: red roofs, grey water, the cranes standing in the mist like patient herons. And above everything, on the hill at the city's northern edge, the observatory—a pale dome and a square tower, built sixty years ago by men who believed that a city ought to know exactly what time it was, and had built the machine to tell it.

Every clock in Vellengard answered to that tower. The harbor bells, the train schedules, the post office date-stamps, the courtrooms, the registry itself—all of it set by the master clock, which received a telegraph pulse from the national observatory to the south and distributed correct time to the whole city like a benevolent monarch distributing bread. Odile had serviced the secondary clocks that hung in the post office and the harbor office for years. She knew the system the way she knew her own heartbeat.

Which is why, stepping off the tram at the base of the hill, she already knew the summons made no sense.

The master clock did not simply *stop*. It was designed not to. If its mainspring failed, a gravity escapement kept it swinging. If power failed, weights drove it. If the telegraph line failed, it ran free on its own excellent heart until the line returned. A stopped master clock meant either catastrophic damage—or a hand inside it.

*Or,* said the cautious voice in her head, the voice that kept her alive, *it means they want a clockmaker up that hill for reasons that have nothing to do with clocks.*

She climbed anyway. Curiosity, in Odile, was not a virtue; it was a symptom. Her father had called it the disease of the trade. *A clockmaker who isn't curious is just a repairman,* he'd said, winding the regulator one evening, *and a curious clockmaker is either rich or dead. Aim for rich.*

* * *

The observatory keeper who met her at the tower door was named Lindqvist, a round, apologetic man with ink-stained fingers and the manner of someone who had spent the afternoon being shouted at by telephone.

"Fräulein—Voss, is it." He squinted at a card in his hand, then at her, doing some private arithmetic. "You're younger than I expected."

"Everyone says that."

"And your uncle?"

"Indisposed." She smiled the smile that ended conversations. "You have a stopped clock."

"We have a catastrophe." He led her up the spiral stair, talking the way frightened people talk, to fill space. "Stopped at eleven minutes past four yesterday morning. We didn't notice for—well, we noticed, but the telegraph correction should have restarted the distribution, and it didn't, and the harbor office has been ringing us since dawn because their stamps are running slow, and the Ministry wants a report tonight, and Director Kelweg himself asked who maintained our secondary clocks, and your name came up, and—" he glanced back at her, embarrassed, "—frankly, fräulein, I argued for the firm from Hohenfeld, they're older, they're established—"

"But I was closer," Odile said pleasantly, "and cheaper."

"...Yes," Lindqvist admitted. "Also the Director specified you. By name."

The stairwell went cold around her, one degree, maybe two. She kept climbing.

*By name.* She turned the phrase over as her boots rung on the iron steps. A director of ministries did not know the names of apprentice clockmakers. Directors knew lists. And somewhere, apparently, her name sat on one.

"Tell me about the stopping," she said, to make him stop talking.

"Eleven minutes past four. The duty clerk swears the pendulum simply—stopped. No wind-down, no stutter. As if it decided."

Clocks don't decide. Someone had helped it decide. She filed the thought where she filed dangerous thoughts—in a small locked drawer behind her sternum—and stepped through the doorway into the clock room.

It was a tall, plain chamber smelling of dust and machine oil, dominated by the master clock itself: a case two meters high, glass-fronted, brass and steel within, its great pendulum hanging dead-still for the first time in sixty years. The stillness of it was obscene. Odile had the sudden, irrational feeling of standing beside a giant holding its breath.

She set down her bag, opened it, and put on her apron, and here was the miracle she could never explain to anyone: her hands went steady. Whatever the dread was doing beneath her ribs—quiet, patient, coiled like the mainspring of something—it stopped at her wrists. Her hands entered the room ahead of her fears, the way they always did.

"May I?"

Lindqvist nodded. She unlocked the case—Lindqvist fumbling for the key, then remembering it hung on its nail—and looked inside at the movement with a craftsman's first cold assessment, the way a doctor looks at a stranger's body: professionally, tenderly, without hope or blame.

The mainspring was intact. The escapement was intact. The weights were wound full. The pendulum suspension—she touched it lightly, and it swung obediently, a soft tick, tick, tick, mocking the room.

"It runs," she said.

"It doesn't," Lindqvist said miserably. "It stopped."

"It runs *now*. Which means it was stopped by something that has since removed itself." She crouched, peering into the works, and after a moment saw it: a sliver of metal, no bigger than a thumbnail, wedged with surgical delicacy between the escape wheel's teeth—a piece cut from clock steel, polished, shaped to jam the pallets without damaging them. Whoever placed it had known exactly how much pressure a tooth could bear. Whoever removed it had taken the time to leave no scratch.

She drew it out with tweezers and held it up to the lamplight. On one edge, half worn away, was a punch mark—a maker's mark, the kind stamped on stock steel. She had seen that mark before. She saw it every day, on the coils of wire and blanks of steel delivered to her uncle's shop.

It was local steel. Vellengard steel. Bought within this city, probably within the year.

"Fräulein?" Lindqvist hovered. "What—what was it?"

"A piece of steel." She wrapped it in cloth and put it in her apron pocket, because leaving evidence lying around in rooms where directors sent for you by name seemed unwise. "Someone stopped your clock deliberately. Then someone—possibly the same someone—released it. Before I was summoned."

"Released it? Then why call me at all—"

"Because," Odile said slowly, working it forward the way she'd work a seized screw, "they wanted it on record that a professional examined the mechanism and found it sound. Because whatever happens next with this clock, the record will say: inspected, certified, repaired." She stood, brushing dust from her knee, and gave Lindqvist the blandest face she owned. "Who signs your maintenance records?"

"I—well. You will. Today. That's the arrangement."

"Of course it is." She took out her notebook and began, in her small, precise hand, to write: *Movement examined. Foreign object removed. Cause unknown. Mechanism sound.* Each word a small brick laid into a wall she didn't yet understand, on the foundation of a signature that wasn't entirely her name.

Outside the tall window, the sun was dropping toward the harbor, and the city below was lighting its lamps, every one of them believing the tower above them. Somewhere down there, a ministry was preparing a report, a director was keeping a list, and a piece of Vellengard steel was warm in her pocket like a swallowed secret.

She finished the entry, signed it—*O. Voss*—and blew gently on the ink.

Behind her, unnoticed in the corner of the clock room, a telephone began to ring, and Lindqvist answered it, and she heard him say, in a voice gone suddenly careful: "Yes, Director. Yes. The clockmaker is here now. A girl, sir. Young. Yes—she's signing the certification as we speak."

A pause. Then Lindqvist covered the mouthpiece and turned to her with an expression she would remember for years—apologetic, frightened, and utterly helpless.

"He'd like a word," Lindqvist said. "Before you go down."
