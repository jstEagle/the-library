## 2. A Question Too Many

The knock came on a Thursday, which was already suspicious, because nothing official in Vellengard ever arrived on a Thursday. Officialdom preferred Mondays, when it could ruin a whole week at once, or Fridays, when it could dangle over the weekend like a fishhook.

Odile answered the door herself, because her mother was resting and Tomas was, as usual, somewhere he shouldn't be, probably teaching gulls on the breakwater to steal from fishermen who deserved it. She opened the door the way she opened everything—carefully, halfway, with her body angled to close it fast if necessary.

A man stood on the step holding what looked, at first glance, like a small suitcase. At second glance, it was worse than a suitcase. It was a typewriter, folded into its own black case, worn at the corners where a thousand hands had gripped it before his.

"Census," he said. His accent was from somewhere south of here, softened by years of port cities—the kind of voice that had learned to be understood everywhere and belong nowhere. "Haddad. Noor Haddad." He said his own name as though he were introducing two other people and had no stake in either of them.

"We were counted in spring," Odile said. "March. There was a man with a clipboard."

"There was a man with a clipboard," Haddad agreed. "There is always a man with a clipboard. But there are not always men with typewriters. I am the correction pass."

"The correction pass."

"The city likes to be sure of itself." He smiled. It was a good smile, unhurried, and Odile distrusted it immediately—not because it was false but because it seemed entirely true, which was more dangerous. "May I come in? It takes twenty minutes. Fifteen if your family is ordinary."

"And how long if we're not?"

"Then I'd still write down that it took fifteen minutes." He shifted the typewriter to his other arm. "Madame. The harbor wind is making my notes curl."

She should have said no. She knew this later, turning it over like a gear with a chipped tooth: she should have said the household was indisposed, come back Tuesday, come back never. Instead she stepped aside, because refusing a census taker was itself a kind of entry in a ledger, and the Voss family's whole art, three years practiced now, consisted of leaving no entries anywhere.

* * *

Her mother received him in the front room, propped in the good chair by the window with a shawl arranged to look casual and a cup of tea arranged to look drunk-from. Her mother could arrange anything. Illness had refined this into genius.

"Madame Voss." He set the typewriter on the table between them, unfolded it with a small metallic ceremony, and rolled a fresh sheet into the platen. Odile watched the keys the way other people watched knives. "I'll ask you some questions. You answer. The machine does the rest."

"It seems an honest arrangement," her mother said pleasantly. "The machine can't misremember."

"No," he said. "That's rather the point of it. That's rather the whole trouble of it, too." And he began to type.

He typed the way Odile imagined pianists played in salons—lightly, without looking down, each question arriving as a neat row of letters while he watched their faces instead of the page. Name. Date of birth. Place of birth. Occupation. Number of persons residing. Odile gave the answers they had given a hundred times, in March, to the man with the clipboard, and they came out smooth as oiled brass: *Voss. Odile. Nineteen. Apprentice clockmaker.* Every syllable rehearsed until rehearsal had worn grooves in it, and the words simply fell into the grooves and stayed there.

"You work for your uncle," Haddad said. Not quite a question.

"At Voss & Son, on Kessler Lane. Clocks and watches."

"& Son," he repeated. "But there's no son."

"My uncle's humor. He bought the sign secondhand and decided truth was expensive."

Something moved behind his eyes—amusement, genuine, quickly filed away—and his fingers went *clack-clack-clack*, recording even the joke. Odile felt a small coldness. In March the clipboard man had written only numbers. This one wrote everything, including the parts that made you likable, which somehow felt worse.

Tomas arrived then, through the window-side door smelling of salt and stolen herring, took in the scene with one sweep—a stranger, a typewriter, his sister standing too straight—and performed, magnificently, the role of an unremarkable fourteen-year-old boy.

"Tomas Voss," he announced before being asked. "Fourteen. Student. No occupation, no income, no vices worth the ink."

Haddad looked at him over the carriage. "Worth the ink," he said. "That's a fine phrase." And he typed it. Odile saw the boy realize, a half-second too late, that wit was also data. She caught Tomas's eye and gave him the smallest shake of the head: *ordinary*. Be ordinary. Tomas sat down next to their mother and became, visibly and with effort, furniture.

* * *

It went well for eleven minutes. Odile counted, because counting was what her hands did when her mind wanted to scream.

At twelve, the questions changed weather.

"And the head of household," Haddad said, still pleasant, still watching faces rather than paper. "Your husband, Madame. Anton Voss."

"My husband died," her mother said. "Three years ago. You'll have that in your records."

"I have that he died." The keys paused. "Records disagree on where."

The room did not move. The tea steamed. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed its opinion of the harbor, and Odile thought, absurdly, that even the birds were informants in this city.

"Where does the record say?" her mother asked, mild as milk.

"That is my question, Madame."

"He died at sea. A shipping illness, off the northern route. We buried—" her mother hesitated, a hesitation so perfectly measured it could only have been rehearsed, "—what we could bring home. The sea keeps the rest."

"Mm." Haddad typed. Odile heard each keystroke individually now, the way you hear a clock in a sickroom. "And before the sea. Where was he employed?"

"He kept accounts. For the grain exchange."

"Before that?"

"Before that he was younger," her mother said, with a smile that would have charmed a bishop and warned a magistrate, "and less interesting."

Odile laughed. She didn't mean to; it escaped her, bright and stupid, and Haddad turned and looked at her directly for the first time since the doorstep. Up close his eyes were dark and very still, like water over something deep, and she understood that the friendliness was real and the friendliness was a method, and both things were true in the same man the way two hands can be true on one watch.

"You find it funny?" he asked.

"I find it accurate," Odile said. "My father was a boring man. He ironed his newspaper. If the census wants a dramatic death, the census will be disappointed."

"The census doesn't want drama." He turned back to the machine. "The census wants agreement. Two records say one thing, a third says another, and suddenly nobody in the house is boring anymore."

*Clack. Clack. Clack.*

"What third record?" her mother asked lightly.

"That's the trouble with thirds," he said. "You never meet them. They just arrive." He finished the line, wound the roller, and began gathering his papers with the crisp economy of a man who had done this in a hundred rooms. "Thank you, Madame Voss. Odile. Tomas." He pronounced each name like a coin placed on a counter. "Fifteen minutes. Your family is nearly ordinary."

"Nearly," Odile said.

"Nearly is a very safe word in this city." He closed the typewriter case with a snap that sounded, to Odile's ear, exactly like a lock engaging. At the door he paused. "One more thing. Off the record, as we say, though nothing is off the record, that's the joke of my profession." He smiled again, that true-and-dangerous smile. "Your father. Did he ever keep time? Professionally, I mean. There's an old registry note—clocks, harbors, that sort of thing. Probably another Voss. Common name."

"Common as gravel," Odile said, and her voice held.

"Hm." He looked at her a moment longer than a moment. Then he touched the brim of his hat to the room in general and was gone, footsteps fading down the stairwell, the harbor wind following him in and out like a dog deciding whose side it was on.

* * *

They waited until his steps were fully gone, and then a further five minutes, because her mother believed in margins.

"Well," said Tomas. "That was—"

"Ordinary," said Odie. "We were ordinary. Say it until you believe it."

"He wrote down my joke about the ink."

"He writes everything down. That's the job."

"He wrote down that Father *ironed his newspaper*." Tomas's voice cracked upward, fourteen years old all at once. "Why does a government need to know that?"

"So that when it lies about us, it lies in detail," her mother said quietly.

The light had gone thin and gray over the rooftops. Odile went to the window and watched the street below, where the census taker was standing under the lamp at the corner, not walking away. He had the case open on a low wall, and he was typing—right there, in the cold, finishing whatever the house had started in him. Then he stopped, tore the sheet free with a sound she couldn't hear but could imagine precisely, folded it twice, and put it inside his coat rather than his satchel.

Inside the coat. Where the satchel went to the office, and the coat went wherever a man went when he wasn't working.

"Mother," Odile said, without turning around. "The third record. About Father."

"Yes?"

"There isn't one. There's only ours. Ours and the grave that doesn't exist." She watched Noor Haddad close the case and finally walk away toward the harbor, unhurried, a man with nowhere to be and all evening to get there. "So where did he find a disagreement?"

Behind her, the silence had a different texture now. Her mother set down her cup, and Odile heard it—really heard it—for the first time in three years: the small porcelain click of a woman putting something down because her hands were needed for truer work.

"Odile," her mother said. "Come away from the window."

Outside, far off across the roofs, the observatory clock struck the hour—one note late, though nobody in Vellengard would notice for weeks. Odile noticed. Odile noticed everything, and had built her whole life out of being unnoticed for it.

Until today, when a man with a typewriter had looked straight through her careful grooves and asked one question too many.
