## 5. Berth One of Twelve

The notice went up on the harbor office door at nine o'clock in the morning, and by half past nine the whole of Vellengard had heard about it, which was typical of a city that could not keep a secret but could not keep its clocks running either.

Odile heard it from Herr Brandt, who delivered the shop's coffee and gossip in equal measure and had never once confused the two.

"Twelve berths," he said, setting down the paper-wrapped parcel of beans as though it were a wreath. "One ship. The *Meridian Star*, sailing on the twenty-ninth. And they're not taking first come, first served anymore — no, no. Now there's a list. A *registry*. Recommendations from the Ministry." He tapped his temple with one finger. "It's all typed up. Very official. Very modern."

"Typed," Odile repeated, because her mouth needed to say something while her mind went elsewhere.

"Typed! Like a court verdict. My cousin saw the posting. He says there are names on it in order of priority, and below the names there's a column of remarks, and the remarks are things like *skilled trade* or *dependents* or—" he lowered his voice with relish— "*under review.*"

"Under review," Odile said. "That sounds like a remark someone makes when they haven't decided how to ruin you yet."

Herr Brandt laughed, then stopped laughing when he realized she hadn't been joking. He gathered his empty cup, patted her shoulder twice — the way one pats a piece of furniture one is fond of — and left.

Odile stood in the back room of the shop among the disassembled alarm clocks and thought about the word *typed*. She thought about a man with careful hands and a portable typewriter who had sat in their parlor three weeks ago asking questions that drifted, gently, like a boat slipping its mooring, from routine into dangerous water. She thought about the way he'd looked at her father's name in the ledger — *Emil Voss, watchmaker, deceased* — and how his fingers had paused over the keys for exactly one beat too long.

She did not believe in omens. She believed in gears, and gears only ever told you what they were built to tell you. But she felt something turning somewhere, some small wheel engaging a larger one, and she did not like the sound of it.

* * *

Her mother took the news the way she took everything now: lying down, propped against pillows, with the particular dignity of a woman who has decided that if the world insists on falling apart, it may do so without her getting up for it.

"A registry," Marta Voss said. Her voice was thin but dry, and dryness in her mother was always a good sign. Wetness meant fever; dryness meant she could still be sarcastic, and sarcasm meant she was herself. "So now our fates are decided by a typewriter."

"By people using a typewriter," Odile corrected, folding socks at the foot of the bed because her hands wanted occupation.

"There is no difference, my dove. A gun decides nothing. It's the finger." She coughed, briefly, into her handkerchief, and Odile watched the cough the way she watched a mainspring under tension — counting, measuring, waiting for the snap. "Twelve berths. How many people applied?"

"Hundreds. Maybe thousands. The line outside the harbor office this morning went around the fish market."

"And we are where on this list?"

"We don't know yet. The full ranking isn't posted. Only the categories." Odile hesitated. "There's a points system. Skilled trades score well. Health scores well. Age — children score well."

"Then I shall be a liability and Tomas shall be a treasure, and you will be somewhere in the middle, being useful." Her mother smiled, and it was a real smile, crooked at one corner, the smile Odile had inherited along with her stubbornness and her habit of hiding. "Your father would have hated this. Being sorted. He used to say a person is not a shipment."

"No," Odile agreed. "He'd have taken the whole clock apart instead."

"He'd have taken the whole *Ministry* apart, and gotten arrested by lunch, and we'd have had to bail him out with money we didn't have." But her mother's eyes went soft saying it, the way they always did. Grief, in the Voss household, had long ago settled into affection, the way sediment settles into the bottom of a river and turns the water clear.

Tomas came in then, fourteen years old and all elbows, carrying a tray of tea he had made himself, which meant the tea was terrible, which meant everyone drank it anyway. This was the family constitution in a single cup.

"I saw the list," he announced. "I counted."

"You counted," Odile said.

"Twelve berths. And I did arithmetic." He set down the tray with great ceremony. "If you divide the applicants by the berths, and assume the harbor office counts families as units rather than individuals, which the clerk told me they do, then—"

"Tomas." Their mother's voice was gentle. "How long have you been standing in lines and talking to clerks this morning?"

"Since six."

"It's barely ten."

"I couldn't sleep." He said it defiantly, chin up, daring anyone to make something sentimental of it. Nobody did, out loud. Odile made something sentimental of it privately, in the space behind her ribs, and filed it away next to everything else she never said.

* * *

The arithmetic, when Odile finally allowed herself to do it properly, sitting alone in the shop after closing with a pencil and the back of an invoice, went like this:

Twelve berths. Say eight go to families, four to individuals — the clerk's guess, Tomas's intelligence, take your pick. Families with skilled trades ranked highest. Watchmaking was a skilled trade; her father's guild papers still existed, though the name on them was Voss and the name beneath the name was not, and this was the sort of distinction a registry might care about very much indeed.

Her mother's health would count against them. There was no polite way to think it, so Odile thought it bluntly, the way you pull a splinter: *Mother is ill. Ill people are expensive. Expensive people score poorly.* The pencil pressed so hard on that line it nearly tore the paper.

Tomas scored beautifully. Fourteen, healthy, apprenticed already in everything but name — he could strip and clean a movement almost as well as she could, and better than she could at his age, though she'd die before telling him. Children were the future of whatever country received them. Every emigration office in Europe had learned to say so in at least six languages.

And Odile herself — Odile was the problem. Odile was a forged signature walking around in a dress, a girl whose papers said one thing and whose memory said another, a girl who existed in the state's ledger as a neat row of typed characters that a single keystroke could unmake. If the registry looked too closely at the Voss file, it wouldn't find a weak application. It would find a crime.

She put down the pencil. Outside, the streetlamps were coming on, and somewhere across the rooftops the observatory clock struck the hour — official time, city time, the time every stamp and seal and signature in Vellengard answered to. It had been running slightly fast since she'd repaired it, a few seconds a week, nothing anyone would notice unless they were the kind of person who noticed.

She was the kind of person who noticed. Lately she'd begun to wonder who else was.

* * *

Dinner that night was barley soup and honesty, which was more than most families in Vellengard were eating.

"We should talk about it plainly," Odile said. "All three of us. No pretending the list doesn't exist."

"I like pretending," said Tomas. "Pretending has excellent structural properties. It holds things up."

"Pretending is load-bearing until it isn't," their mother said, "and then it comes down on top of you. Talk, Odile."

So Odile talked. She laid it out the way she'd lay out a mechanism on felt: here is the case, here are the wheels, here is where it binds. Twelve berths. Points for trades, points for children, penalties for illness, penalties for irregular papers. The registry's recommendation decided priority, and the registry was typed, and typed things could be amended in either direction.

"So we need to score well," Tomas said. "We need to look valuable."

"We need to *be* valuable and hope whoever reads the file is having a good day," Odile said. "Those are different strategies. We'll need both."

Their mother stirred her soup without eating it. "And if the file is read closely? If someone asks questions about Emil's guild papers?" She didn't say *about the forgery*. In this house they had never once said it aloud, not in seven years, and apparently they weren't going to start now, even facing a typewriter with the power of the state behind it. Some silences become architecture. You live inside them.

"Then we answer carefully," Odile said.

"Carefully." Her mother tasted the word. "You've always been careful, my dove. Even as a little girl. Other children broke things; you took them apart so neatly you could put them back before anyone noticed they'd been broken." She looked up, and her eyes were clear and terribly present, lucid in the way she was lucid maybe one evening in five now, and it made Odile's chest ache, because these evenings were gifts and gifts run out. "I want to say something while I'm me. Listen. Whatever happens with this list — whatever arithmetic gets done — we do not trade each other. Do you hear me? Not one of us for another. Not for twelve berths, not for twelve ships. People who start trading each other end up with nothing but the trade."

Nobody spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere far off, the observatory clock began striking nine, and Odile found herself counting the strokes automatically, checking them against her father's pocket watch in her apron pocket, and finding them — again — a half-second fast.

"I'm not going anywhere without either of you," Tomas said suddenly, fiercely, to his soup. "If the list says one of us goes, then none of us goes. That's just logic."

"That's just stubbornness," said their mother, "which in this family is the same thing," and she reached across and squeezed his wrist, and Odile looked at the two of them — the boy offering himself up like a coin, the woman spending her last clear hours making laws — and understood that the hardest arithmetic wasn't going to be done with a pencil.

* * *

Later, when the dishes were done and her mother slept and Tomas pretended to sleep, Odile sat at the workbench with the lamp turned low and let herself think the forbidden thought all the way through, because a fear examined is smaller than a fear avoided. That was practically horology.

The registry would rank them. The registry was fed by census takers — men with portable typewriters and patient voices, going door to door, writing the city into columns. One of those men had sat in their parlor. One of those men had paused, one beat too long, over her dead father's name.

And the official time itself — the time that stamped every document, validated every paper, dated every approval — was kept by a clock she alone had been trusted to repair. A clock that had failed, conveniently, at the exact moment the borders began to close. A clock that was now running fast, a few seconds a week, so slightly that no ordinary citizen would ever notice.

But a ministry that decided who sailed and who stayed — such a ministry might notice anything it wished to notice. A few seconds, applied to a stamp, applied to a deadline, applied to a family's papers...

She was speculating. She knew she was speculating. Clockmakers dealt in evidence, not suspicion.

Still, she took out a fresh sheet of paper and wrote at the top of it, in her small precise hand: *Questions.* Beneath it she wrote: *Who reads the Voss file? Who typed the rankings? Why does the master clock drift?*

Three questions. She stared at them for a long time, and then, because the night was quiet and honesty was a habit she was trying to build, she added a fourth:

*Why do I keep thinking about the census taker's hands?*

She crossed it out immediately. Then, being thorough, she uncrossed it. A clockmaker did not delete data. She merely filed it somewhere she didn't have to look at it.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would go to the harbor office and see the posting for herself. Tomorrow she would find out where the name *Voss* sat on that list, and in what column, and with what remarks beside it.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the observatory clock struck eleven — half a second fast, she was almost sure of it — and the sound rolled over the sleeping city like a hand moving over papers, deciding.
