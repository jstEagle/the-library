## 6. The Registry Room

The Ministry of Records occupied the former Grand Hotel Aurelia, which had been the finest building in Vellengard until the state decided it would be more useful as a filing cabinet. Noor had stayed there once, as a boy, on the one occasion his father could afford a holiday by the sea. He remembered marble floors and a doorman who bowed, and a breakfast of small sweet rolls arranged on a silver tray like offerings to a minor god.

Now the lobby smelled of dust and cold tea. The marble was still there under his feet, but it had been covered in places by linoleum laid down in a hurry during some bureaucratic emergency, so that walking across the foyer was like stepping from island to island of someone else's decisions. The front desk where the doorman had once bowed was manned by a clerk who did not look up.

"Ledgers," Noor said.

The clerk extended one hand without raising his eyes from whatever he was reading — a racing paper, by the look of it, folded with great care into a folder marked CONFIDENTIAL. Noor set the two ledgers down, then thought better of it and added the third, the slim one with the loose pages he'd been meaning to recopy for a week.

"You're the Haddad fellow." The clerk said this to the racing paper rather than to Noor. "Sector nine."

"I'm sector nine, yes."

"Room 214. Don't go wandering. People wander, they get lost, we have to send someone after them, it's a whole thing." He turned a page. Somewhere behind him a horse was presumably losing.

Noor climbed the grand staircase, which remained magnificent out of sheer stubbornness — the Ministry hadn't managed to make anything in this building ugly except its own soul — and found Room 214 at the end of a corridor lined with doors numbered in a system that had clearly been devised by someone with a grudge against arithmetic. He knocked.

"Enter, don't loiter."

Inside, the Grand Hotel's ballroom had been divided into eight smaller rooms with partitions that reached only three-quarters of the way to the ceiling, so that the whole floor hummed like a hive heard through a wall. Typewriters clattered everywhere, overlapping, arrhythmic, each one going at its own tempo — and because Noor had spent the past month listening to nothing but typewriters, he could pick out individual machines the way a conductor picks out instruments. A Remington overworked and skipping on its tab key. An Underwood with a sticky *e*. Somebody's Adler being treated cruelly, hammered rather than typed, as though words were nails.

A woman with spectacles and an air of permanent grievance looked up from her desk. "You're late."

"I'm early," Noor said. "My appointment isn't until eleven."

"Then you're early and annoying. Sit there." She pointed at a chair positioned, he noticed, directly beneath a draught from the window. "I am Fräulein Brandt. I audit intake. You will hand me your ledgers, you will sign the transfer slip, and you will answer one question: have any of your entries been altered since collection?"

"No."

"No what?"

"No, Fräulein Brandt, none of my entries have been altered since collection."

She took the ledgers and flipped through them with the speed of someone who read people the way she read pages — quickly, skeptically, looking for the forgery in every face. Her eyes stopped on something. She frowned. Not at him; at the ledger. At a page somewhere near the back.

"What?" Noor said. "Is there an error? I can correct—"

"It's not an error." She closed the ledger with a soft, deliberate sound, like a door being shut on a conversation before it starts. "It's been flagged."

"Flagged?"

"That is not a word I used." She stood, tucked both ledgers under her arm, and looked at him for the first time properly — a long, assessing look, the kind Noor imagined she gave to suspicious sums. "Wait here. Don't touch anything. Don't talk to anyone. If anyone talks to you, tell them nothing interesting."

"And if it's interesting?"

"Then you're in trouble either way. Wait here."

She disappeared through a door marked RECORDS — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL, and Noor sat in the draughty chair beneath the window and did what he always did when anxious: he noticed things.

He noticed that the partition walls were covered in pinned sheets of typing — drafts, carbons, lists — hundreds of names marching down each page in columns. He noticed that the room beyond the authorized door was visible above the partitions when he stood, just barely, if he rose onto his toes: rows of desks, more lists, and along the far wall a board of some kind with cards pinned to it, moved about by a man on a stepladder like a gardener tending an orchard of paper.

He noticed that nobody in this building seemed happy. Not unhappy, exactly — unhappiness required energy — but a kind of gray contentment, the satisfaction of people who had found work that asked nothing of their souls and had accepted the trade without haggling.

And he noticed the clock on the wall. A cheap electric thing, wrong by several minutes — he'd have bet his ribbon supply on it — while every desk held a small brass timepiece stamped with the observatory's seal. Official time, distributed to the clerks like communion. The wall clock, he supposed, was for the building itself, which apparently ran on its own schedule and to hell with the city's.

Fräulein Brandt was gone eleven minutes. He counted.

"The Voss entry," she said, returning. "Your sector nine household, the widow and children. Who interviewed the principal respondent?"

"I did. Twice."

"On whose authority did you conduct a second interview?"

"Mine." Noor kept his voice level. "There were inconsistencies in the first pass. Occupations that didn't reconcile with the tax rolls. I went back to resolve them."

"Did you resolve them?"

"They resolved themselves. The discrepancies were clerical. Errors in the earlier registry, not in the family's statements."

"Hm." She set the ledgers on her desk and squared their edges precisely. "They've been flagged for verification. Priority verification. That means the file goes upstairs, and upstairs means—" she made a vague gesture toward the ceiling, toward some higher floor or perhaps simply heaven "—you know how it is."

"I don't," Noor said honestly. "I type forms. I collect names. What happens upstairs?"

"Nothing happens upstairs." She said it with the flat finality of a woman reciting a catechism she didn't believe but had never thought to question. "Upstairs things are decided. Nothing happens; things are decided. There's a difference."

"Between deciding and happening?"

"If you have to ask, you're safer not knowing." She handed him the transfer slip to sign. His hand hesitated over the line for remarks. There was a space for observations. He thought of Odile Voss's hands moving inside a clockwork, sure and quiet, and of the mother's cough he'd heard through the wall, thin as paper tearing, and of the boy Tomas who had watched him leave with the expression of a child counting exits.

He wrote: *Entry consistent. Recommend no further action.*

Fräulein Brandt read it, and for one moment something moved behind the spectacles — not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but recognition, the look of one tired animal seeing another. "That won't unflag it," she said quietly. "But it's noted. Some auditor will read it. Maybe even believe it."

"Why would they flag a widow with two children?"

"Because somebody upstairs asked a question." She took the slip. "Questions are expensive here, Herr Haddad. They're the only currency we deal in. Now go away. You've made my morning complicated and I had plans for it involving a sandwich."

* * *

He should have left. He had delivered his ledgers; his obligations were complete; the sensible census taker collects his receipt and returns to his rounds. Noor had been raised sensible. His father used to say that in this country a man survived by keeping his curiosity fed but leashed — a lapdog, not a wolfhound.

Instead, Noor lingered by the window pretending to wait for a receipt he already held, and let the wolfhound off its leash.

From the second-floor landing — he told himself he was simply taking the scenic route to the exit, which was absurd, since the exit was directly below — he could see over the partitions into the records hall. And now, in the flat mid-morning light, he saw what he had only sensed before.

The lists. They were everywhere. Pinned sheets, carbon copies stacked in wire trays, and on the far wall the great board where the man on the stepladder worked. It was a grid, Noor realized. Names down the side. Categories across the top. And between them, small typed marks — a check, a dash, occasionally a word — that determined, column by column, what each name was worth.

He drifted closer, holding his transfer slip like a passport. A young clerk with ink-stained fingers was feeding a fresh sheet into a machine near the aisle, and Noor, with the shameless helpfulness of the incurably curious, leaned in. "Tab sticking?"

"Always," the clerk said, grateful for sympathy. "Third column. Watch — it double-types every time." Indeed: the machine struck the same position twice, laying down a darker mark than its neighbors. "Half these families got flagged twice and don't know it's the machine's fault."

"Does anyone check?"

"Check what?" The clerk laughed, a short, hollow sound. "The list is the list. Whatever's typed is true. That's the beauty of the system — you want to change a man's fate, you don't argue with anyone, you just retype him."

He said it lightly, the way one complains about weather. Then he pulled the finished sheet free and carried it to the board, and Noor watched him pin it up beside the others, and the grid grew by one column, and somewhere in that column were twelve berths on a ship, and thousands of names competing for them with nothing but typed marks to recommend them.

*One keystroke*, the clerk had said, without knowing what he'd said. *You just retype him.*

Noor stood very still among the clattering machines, and felt the first root of something take hold in him — not yet guilt, guilt comes later, guilt requires understanding — but its seed: the cold suspicion that his neat little forms, his careful columns, his conscientious second interviews, all of it flowed into this room and became this board, and that the difference between a family boarding a ship and a family watching it leave might be a stuck tab key, or a clerk's mood, or a single keystroke laid down by a man he'd never meet.

He should go. He told himself firmly: go.

He went upstairs instead.

* * *

The third floor was quieter. The typewriters here were newer, heavier, and the women operating them typed slowly and perfectly, like monks copying scripture. Along one wall stood a row of tall cabinets, and before the cabinets, a long table where a supervisor was arranging index cards into piles with a jeweler's patience.

Noor had a transfer slip in his pocket and a reasonably honest face. He walked the corridor once, unhurried, reading door plaques. REGISTRY CONSOLIDATION. PRIORITY CLASSIFICATION. SHIP ALLOCATION — QUARTERMASTER'S LIAISON. And then, near the end, half-hidden behind a coat rack:

**VERIFICATION — FLAGGED ENTRIES.**

The door stood open an inch. From inside came the sound of a single typewriter working at a thoughtful pace, and Fräulein Brandt's voice, low: "—no further action recommended by the collector, but the flag stands, so verify anyway, you know how it goes."

Another voice, older, bored: "Voss. Which Voss?"

"Sector nine. Widow, daughter twenty-two, son fourteen. Clockmaker's family."

"Clockmaker." A pause, and Noor heard papers turn. "Hm. The husband's file is thick. Deceased how long?"

"Two years, I think. Why?"

"Because dead men generate questions, Fräulein, and questions generate flags, and flags generate my workload. Put it in the third pile."

"Pile three means refusal review."

"Pile three means *later*. Everything means later. We have until the ship sails, and after the ship sails we have forever, which is the one deadline this office has never missed."

Noor stepped back from the door, heart tapping against his ribs faster than any machine in the building. *Refusal review.* Three words, spoken with the tenderness of a man sorting laundry. Somewhere in that office, in a pile, in a folder, in a ledger he himself had filled out in his own careful hand, the name Voss sat near the bottom of everything — and above it, deciding its weight, sat men who spoke of human lives in the grammar of stationery.

He made it as far as the stairwell before he had to stop and hold the railing.

Here is what Noor Haddad understood, standing in the stairwell of the Grand Hotel Aurelia with his hat in his hands: he had believed he was a scale, weighing the city honestly. He was not a scale. He was a funnel. Everything he gathered poured downward into rooms like this, and at the bottom of the funnel, twelve berths waited, and the funnel did not care what it carried.

His father's lapdog had become, without his noticing, a wolfhound. And it was pulling.

He descended the grand staircase, crossed the linoleum islands, and passed the front desk. The clerk looked up from the racing paper at last.

"Get what you needed?"

"No," Noor said. "But I think I got what was coming to me."

Outside, Vellengard glittered in the cold sun, harbor bells ringing, gulls wheeling over masts, the city entirely unaware that its fate was being typed in triplicate a few streets away. Noor buttoned his coat against the wind and turned, without quite deciding to, toward Sector Nine.

There was a household there with a flagged name and a sick mother, and he had written *no further action* on their file, which was either the most honest sentence he'd ever typed or the most useless, and he intended to find out which — starting with the daughter who had looked at his typewriter the way other people look at locked doors.
