## 7. Overtime at Midnight

The trouble with being invisible, Odile had learned long ago, was that people kept forgetting you were there — which meant they never remembered to send word when things changed.

She had spent three evenings waiting for a message from the observatory that never came. The master clock had been running true for six days now, steady as a sermon, and Director Kelweg's office had gone silent as a stopped pendulum. No note of thanks. No second summons. No payment, either, though she tried not to think about that too loudly, because thinking about money made her mother cough in that particular way that meant *don't worry, we'll manage*, which was the Voss family's national anthem.

So on the seventh night, she simply went.

"I'm checking my work," she told her uncle Henrik, winding her scarf twice around her neck. "A clockmaker who doesn't follow up is a doctor who doesn't ask how the patient slept."

"A clockmaker who works for free," Henrik said from behind his loupe, "is a fool with good posture."

"Then I'm a fool with excellent posture." She kissed the top of his bald head, which he tolerated the way one tolerates weather, and slipped out into the November dark.

Vellengard at night in late autumn was a city holding its breath. The harbor fog came up the hill streets like a slow tide, wrapping the lampposts in gray wool, and the gulls had all gone somewhere sensible to sleep, leaving the city to cats and clerks and one apprentice clockmaker with a toolbox that clinked at every third step no matter how carefully she carried it. She'd padded the tools with flannel last week. It hadn't helped. Tools, like truths, always found a way to make noise.

The observatory sat on Kessel Hill like a stone onion, its dome silvered by the moon, and below it the whole port lay spread out in a scatter of lights: the cranes, the masts, the harbor office with its one window still burning yellow. Somewhere down there, Odile knew, her family's future was sitting in a drawer — twelve berths on the *Meridian Star*, and a registry full of typewritten opinions about whether the Voss name deserved one.

She did not let herself think about that either. She thought instead about escapements, which was her way of praying.

The night watchman, old Pieter, was asleep in his chair by the side entrance, his lantern turned low, an empty bottle of something medicinal standing guard beside him. Odile had her own key — Kelweg's office had issued it with the gravity of a knighthood — and she let herself in without waking him, stepping over the cat that lived in the coal cellar and considered itself staff.

Inside, the tower stair wound upward through cold air that smelled of stone and oil and, faintly, of someone else's tobacco. She paused on the third landing, sniffing.

Pipe smoke. Not fresh — but not old enough to be Pieter's, and Pieter didn't smoke anyway; his vice was the bottle and he was honest about it.

"Probably the wind," she told herself, and climbed.

* * *

The clock room at the top of the tower was Odile's favorite place in the entire city, and she said so to no one, because saying so would have required there to be someone. It was a round chamber under the dome, all brass and shadow, dominated by the master clock itself: a great regulator movement, taller than her uncle, its pendulum swinging with the patience of a thing that had outlasted four governments and fully intended to outlast a fifth. From this room, through cables and relay lines, official time flowed downward into Vellengard — into the harbor office, the customs house, the railway station, the ministry stamping rooms. When the master clock said it was midnight, the whole city said it was midnight, and every document stamped at that moment agreed.

Six days ago, she had found the clock lying about the hour. Tonight she wanted only to confirm it had stopped lying.

She opened the case door, set her lamp on the shelf, and listened first — always listen first, Uncle Henrik's first law — before touching anything.

Tick. Tick. Tick. Steady. Even. Beautiful.

Except.

Odile leaned closer, her nose nearly brushing the great brass wheel train, and frowned. The beat was even, yes. But it was even at the wrong rate. The seconds-hand was traveling a hair slow — two or three parts in a thousand, the sort of error no ordinary person would ever notice, but which would accumulate, minute by patient minute, into something meaningful over weeks.

Someone had altered the effective pendulum length again. Not crudely — the rating nut was untouched, which any inspector would check first. Instead, a thin sliver of metal had been wedged into the suspension spring bracket, a shim no thicker than a fingernail paring, positioned to flex the spring almost imperceptibly. Elegant. Quiet. The work of someone who understood clocks well enough to hurt them politely.

And it wasn't the same shim she had removed a week ago. This one was newer. Cleaner. Different hand.

"Hello," Odile said softly, to the shim, to the clock, to whoever had put them together. "You again."

She took up her tweezers, and that was when the floorboards creaked below her.

Not the building settling — she knew the observatory's vocabulary by now, all its groans and mutterings. This was a footstep. A careful one, on the stairs, pausing between steps the way a person does when they don't want to be heard.

Odile did the arithmetic of fear very quickly. There was one stairway. She was seven floors up inside a stone onion. And her lamp was burning on the shelf like a lighthouse announcing exactly where the foolish apprentice stood.

She pinched out the flame. Darkness dropped over the room, thick and total, broken only by moonlight falling through the slit windows in pale bars. She gathered up her toolbox — wincing as it clinked, of course it clinked, tools will be tools — and pressed herself into the narrow gap behind the clock's case, between the great weight drum and the stone wall, where the shadows lived deepest.

The footsteps climbed. Slowly. A man unhurried, or a man pretending to be.

He came into the room carrying a shaded hand lantern, and Odile, peering through the gap in the case door, saw him plainly enough to make her stomach drop through the floor and keep going.

He was not a workman. He wore a plain dark coat, but underneath it, visible when he bent to set the lantern down, was the gray collar and tie of a ministry clerk. He was perhaps fifty, tidy, with spectacles that caught the moonlight, and he moved around the master clock with the familiarity of a man visiting his own kitchen. He took off his gloves finger by finger, laid them on the shelf, and opened a leather case.

From the case he drew a small steel instrument, caliper-jawed, and a folded paper of figures.

"Oh," Odile breathed, so quietly that even she could barely hear it.

The man removed the shim. He examined it against the light, nodded as though confirming a suspicion, then consulted his paper, adjusted the calipers, and cut — cut! — a new shim from a strip of stock he'd brought with him, filing it with tiny precise strokes, testing it, filing again. He worked the way Odile worked. He worked the way she had been taught to work, patiently, lovingly, by a father who was now a name on forged papers.

He fitted the new shim into the suspension bracket. The clock's tick changed by nothing a human ear could catch. But Odile knew what it meant: the drift would begin again, slow and invisible, official time sliding away from true time by fractions of a second each day.

The man closed his instruments, retrieved his gloves, and then — and here Odile's blood went from cold to genuinely frightening temperatures — he produced a small notebook and, by lantern light, wrote something in it. Then he checked his pocket watch against the master clock, noted something else, and stood for a moment looking up at the pendulum with an expression she could only call fond.

"There we are," he murmured, in the satisfied voice of a gardener surveying a bed he'd just weeded. "Right on schedule."

He picked up his lantern and went back down the stairs. Odile stayed hidden until long after the outer door had boomed shut, until her knees ached and her heart had slowed from a hammer to merely a fist knocking. Then she slid out on legs that felt borrowed, relit her lamp, and looked at where he had written.

He'd used pencil, and lightly. But the page was indented, and she had spent her life reading impressions — worn engraving plates, ghosted serial numbers, the fingerprints machines leave in metal. She tilted the lamp low across the paper, raking light across the indentations, and the words rose up like something developing in a photographer's bath:

*Drift target: 4 min/fortnight. Confirm stamp variance, harbor office & registry rm. K.*

*K.* For Kelweg? Or some other initial entirely?

Odile sat down on the mechanic's stool, quite suddenly, because her legs had filed their resignation.

* * *

Let us be fair to Odile Voss: she was not stupid, and she did not need the walk home to understand what she had seen. But understanding arrives in stages, the way dawn does, and each stage is worse than the last.

Stage one, in the clock room, hugging her toolbox like a child's doll: *Someone is making the official time run slow. Deliberately. Carefully. In small amounts, so small no one complains, so gradual no one investigates.*

Stage two, halfway down the tower stairs, her boots echoing in the dark: *Official time governs stamps. Every document stamped in Vellengard carries the time of the master clock. If the master clock lies, the stamps lie.*

Stage three, out under the cold stars on Kessel Hill, breath smoking, staring down at the harbor office's single lit window: *Documents can be invalidated by a wrong time. If your papers say you applied at eleven o'clock and the office record says the office closed at ten fifty-eight — because the clock drifted — then your papers are void. Not denied. Void. As if you had never applied at all.*

She stopped walking.

Below her, the harbor lay black and glittering, the *Meridian Star* a pale shape at her berth with her twelve berths — eleven now, since the Lindqvist family's sudden disqualification last week, a disqualification everyone had shrugged about because paperwork is paperwork and who among us understands clerks.

Who among us, indeed.

Odile thought of the Lindqvists, who had been told their emigration application bore a timestamp outside permitted hours. Mr. Lindqvist had come into her uncle's shop the next day, gray-faced, asking if a watch could be *un*-wronged, whether there was any appeal to be made to time itself. Her uncle had said gently that watches keep time, they don't argue about it. And Mr. Lindqvist had laughed, a terrible little laugh, and said, *No. I suppose that's the government's job,* and bought a cheap alarm clock for his wife and left.

The drift target was four minutes per fortnight. Four minutes was nothing. Four minutes was the difference between the tram you caught and the tram you missed. Four minutes was also, precisely, the width of the gap between *stamped within hours* and *stamped outside hours* if the regulations were written tightly enough — and Odile would have bet her father's pocket watch, the real one, the one hidden inside the false bottom of her toolbox, that the regulations had been written by the same tidy man with the same tidy calipers.

The machinery of the state, she thought, standing alone on a dark hill with the fog coming in off the sea. Everyone imagined it as a monster, all teeth and shouting, boots in the street at midnight. But this — this was worse and quieter. This was the machinery revealed as what it truly was: a clock. Gears and springs and escapements, ticking away in a stone tower, and every tick moving some family's papers from valid to void, some name from passage to refusal. You couldn't fight a monster. Monsters at least admitted what they were. But how did you fight a clock? How did you stand in the town square and shout, *Citizens, the time is a lie!* They would lock you up before you finished the sentence, and the clock would go on ticking, polite and accurate-seeming and deadly, and the ships would sail with other people's names on their manifests.

And the registry. The registry that decided the twelve berths. Stamped, timed, dated, governed by the same drifting hands. Which meant the list itself could be steered — not by crossing names out, nothing so crude, nothing that left evidence — but by letting the clock do it. By letting *time itself* disqualify the inconvenient.

Her mother's cough. Tomas, fourteen years old and growing out of his coat sleeves. Twelve berths. One ship. And somewhere in a ministry drawer, a typewritten ranking in which the Voss family's survival depended on a timestamp that a man in a gray collar could adjust with a fingernail-thin sliver of steel.

Odile realized she was gripping the iron railing hard enough to hurt her hands, and made herself let go, finger by finger.

* * *

She got home past one in the morning, let herself in through the shop's back door with the key on the string around her neck, and stood for a while in the dark workshop among the sleeping clocks — wall clocks, carriage clocks, a grandfather case waiting on a new movement — all of them ticking away honestly, keeping whatever small time they were asked to keep.

"You're the honest ones," she whispered to them. "Don't let it go to your heads."

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked — her mother, restless — and Odile froze, listening. The creak settled. Sleep resumed. She crept up to her own narrow room, sat on the bed without lighting the lamp, and forced herself to think like a clockmaker instead of a frightened girl, because frightened girls were useless and clockmakers, at minimum, could take things apart.

Facts. She had seen a ministry man adjust the master clock. She had read, in indentation, the words *drift target* and *stamp variance* and *registry rm*. She had a new shim in her tweezers case — she had taken the old one, yes, pocketed it like a thief, and what did that make her? — and she knew the drift would resume tomorrow, slow as rust.

What she did not have was proof that meant anything to anyone. A shim proves a repair. An indentation proves a memory. And accusing the observatory's director of tampering with official time, as an unlicensed apprentice clockmaker with forged papers in her family's cupboard — well. That was not a complaint; that was a confession with extra steps.

Unless.

Unless she was not alone.

She thought of Noor Haddad — census taker, owner of a portable typewriter, asker of one question too many — and of the strange, guarded tea they had shared two weeks ago, when two careful people had circled each other like cats deciding whether the other one was weather or threat. He typed the lists. He fed the registry. And he had looked, that afternoon, like a man who had begun to suspect what his machine was for.

*A single keystroke moves a family from passage to refusal,* she had heard somewhere, or maybe dreamed it, or maybe it was simply true the way obvious things are true.

If the clock was the weapon, the registry was the trigger. And the trigger had a man attached to it — a tired, decent-looking man with ink on his fingers who had once, unprompted, complimented the honesty of her uncle's shop clocks.

Odile lay back on her pillow and stared at the ceiling, where moonlight made a faint clock-face of the window frame.

Tomorrow, she decided, a repair ticket was going to arrive at a certain census office. Something small. Something plausible. A portable typewriter, she happened to know, had a carriage return that stuck when humidity got into the rails, and November in Vellengard was nothing *but* humidity.

And if Noor Haddad brought his machine to the Voss clock shop to be fixed —

— well. Two people who kept secrets, she reasoned, pulling the blanket to her chin, might just be the right number of people to survive one.

Down in the harbor, faint across the water, a ship's bell rang the hour. The real hour, Odile thought. Or what passed for it these days.

She slept, eventually, with her hand curled around the stolen shim like a talisman, and dreamed of gears the size of moons, all of them turning slightly, slightly wrong.
