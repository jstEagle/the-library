## 9. Tea, Two Cups, No Lies

### Odile

The note arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside an envelope with no stamp, which meant it had been carried by hand, which meant someone in Vellengard wanted to be seen delivering it and also not seen writing it.

*Miss Voss — I have a question about your father's workshop registration. It is a small question. I would prefer to ask it over tea rather than through the Ministry. I am told your uncle's coffee could strip rust from a mainspring, so perhaps tea at yours. Tomorrow, four o'clock. I will bring my own cup. — N.H.*

Odile read it three times by the light of the shop's back room, holding it near the lamp but not too near, as though proximity might make the paper more incriminating than it already was. *A small question.* In her experience, small questions were how large disasters began. A census taker did not request tea. A census taker requested documents, dates, signatures. Tea was what you asked for when you intended to stay long enough to watch a person's face while they answered.

She considered burning it. She considered pretending she'd never received it. She considered showing it to her mother, who was having one of her clear days, and decided against it—clear days were for remembering, not for worrying, and Odile guarded the border between those two territories like a customs officer.

In the end she wrote back on the shop's invoice paper, in her neatest, most boring hand:

*Four o'clock is acceptable. We have cups.*

* * *

### Noor

He brought his own cup because his mother had raised him to believe that arriving empty-handed was a form of theft, and he brought the typewriter because leaving it at the office meant leaving it where Kelweg's clerks could open its case and read its ribbon. The typewriter knew things now. He had begun to think of it as a witness that couldn't testify but could certainly be interrogated.

The Voss shop sat on Binder Lane like a thing trying very hard to be furniture. The sign read VOSS & NEPHEW — CLOCKS REPAIRED, CLOCKS SOLD, CLOCKS REGRETTED, which Noor suspected was Uncle Pieter's joke and which told him more about the household than any form he'd filled out: here lived people who used humor the way sailors used ballast.

The nephew himself opened the door—a boy of fourteen with ink-stained cuffs and his father's jaw—and looked at Noor the way boys look at tax collectors.

"You're the census man."

"I'm the census man."

"Tomas," the boy said, not offering a hand. "You asked about our father. On your form. Question fourteen."

Noor felt the question land somewhere below his ribs. "I ask about everyone's fathers."

"Not everyone's father gets a follow-up visit." Tomas stepped aside anyway. "Tea's in the back. She's waiting. She's been waiting since three-thirty, but she'll say four o'clock exactly when you ask."

* * *

### Odile

He arrived at four o'clock exactly, which she noted with professional disapproval. Punctuality was a language she respected, and she resented finding it spoken fluently by a man who made her nervous.

He set his own cup on the workbench—chipped blue porcelain, older than he was—and set the typewriter beside it, and then he did something no ministry man had ever done in her presence: he took off his coat, folded it over a stool, rolled his sleeves to the elbow, and sat down like a guest rather than an inspection.

"Your uncle said I could wait in the front," he said, "but he said it in the tone people use when they mean the opposite, so I came back here."

"That was honest of him."

"I find honesty works better if I go first." He opened the typewriter case, removed a single sheet of paper, and laid it on the bench between them, face up. "This is my report on your household. Filed last month. Read it. All of it. Then tell me if anything surprises you."

It was a trap. It had to be a trap. She picked it up anyway, because refusing would be its own confession, and read the dry bureaucratic sentences describing the Voss family: widow, son, niece, occupation, place of origin, all correct, all matching papers she herself had forged with a steady hand and a sleepless week of practice. And then, near the bottom, in a different typeface—the supervisor's stamp, the one that meant *flagged*—

*Ancestry of head of household unverified. Father: Emil Voss, clockmaker, d. 1931. Recommend secondary review.*

Her hands did not shake. She had trained them not to, years ago, over other papers. But something behind her sternum went very quiet and very cold.

"This isn't your handwriting," she said. "Metaphorically speaking."

"No. That's Director Kelweg's section." He turned his cup slowly on the bench, watching the chip rotate. "I filed the report. Someone above me flagged it. And yesterday I learned what happens to flagged files. They don't get reviewed, Miss Voss. They get *used*. I've spent two days deciding whether to come here, and I've concluded that if I'm going to lose my career, I'd like to lose it honestly."

"And what do you want from me?"

"The truth about your father. Not for the registry." He met her eyes. "For me. Because I think I've been feeding people into a machine, and I want to know, before I feed in one more family, whether the machine eats the guilty or just the unlucky."

* * *

### Odile

There was a long silence, filled only by the tick of the shop's regulator clock on the wall—the good one, the honest one, the one she serviced weekly and trusted absolutely.

She should lie. Every instinct honed since she was sixteen said *lie, smile, deflect, become furniture*. But he had put his own file on the table. He had shown her the flag. And there was a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from carrying a secret alone for seven years, and it makes even dangerous men look briefly like doors.

"My father died in 1931," she said. "That part is true."

"And the rest?"

"The rest depends on which rest you mean." She stood, went to the cabinet where her uncle kept the tea, and let herself think with her back turned, because it was easier to be brave facing tin canisters than facing a witness. "My father's name wasn't Emil Voss. My name isn't Odile Voss. The papers are good—I made them myself, and I'm better at this than you'd believe—but they're mine. Forged. All of them. Mother, Tomas, me. Since '31."

She turned around. He hadn't moved. His face had gone carefully, professionally blank, which told her he was frightened, which strangely made her less so.

"Why?" he asked. Just that. Not *you're under arrest*, not *do you know the penalty*. *Why.*

"Because my father wrote an editorial in 1930 under his real name, in a newspaper that no longer exists, saying that the government's new time standardization law was designed to control people rather than serve them. He was right. He was also dead within a year—not officially because of the article, officially of a fever, but fevers are convenient. Afterward, men came asking questions about his 'associates.' So my mother became a widow named Voss, and I became a clockmaker's niece, and we have been quietly, meticulously nobody ever since."

The kettle chose that moment to begin its low whistle, and she was grateful for it, because it gave them both somewhere to look.

* * *

### Noor

He drank the tea because refusing would have been worse, and because his hands needed something to do that wasn't trembling. He had expected many things from this visit. He had not expected to be handed the whole weight of it in four minutes flat.

"You understand," he said, "that I'm now required to report this conversation."

"Yes."

"And you told me anyway."

"You showed me the flag first." She refilled his cup without asking. "I'm making a wager, Mr. Haddad. I'm betting that a man who brings his own teacup to an interrogation is a man who'd rather break rules than people. If I'm wrong, I've lost nothing I wasn't already going to lose. If I'm right—"

"What?"

"Then you might be able to answer a question for me. Why does the registry care about a dead clockmaker's family? Flagged files, priority lists, twelve berths on one ship—it's arithmetic, isn't it? Somebody's doing sums with lives. And sums have purposes."

He looked at her—at this woman who repaired clocks and forged identities with the same steady hands—and felt the strange vertigo of realizing he'd walked into this house intending to extract a secret and was instead being recruited.

"There's something else," he said. "Something I shouldn't tell you, and am going to, because apparently that's who I am now." He reached into his coat, still folded on the stool, and drew out a loop of carbon ribbon, coiled like a small black snake. "I found this in the wastebasket at the office. Ribbons get changed every week; the old ones are supposed to be burned. This one wasn't. Carbon keeps everything, Miss Voss. Every keystroke leaves its ghost." He uncoiled a length of it against the lamplight. "Someone typed a list of names and then deleted them from the registry. Deleted, not denied—they were struck entirely, as if they'd never applied. I reconstructed eleven names from this ribbon. Families. Gone from eligibility without a refusal letter, without a reason. Without ever knowing."

Her eyes went to the ribbon with an expression he recognized from mirrors: the look of someone doing arithmetic they hate.

"Was Voss among them?"

He hesitated. Lying to her now seemed obscene, like spitting in the tea.

"It was among the flags. Not yet struck. There's still—" He stopped. Started again. "There may still be time. Amendments to the registry require a filing code and a window. I have a code. The windows are short. And they're keyed—" he heard his own voice drop, "—they're keyed to official city time. Stamped by the observatory master clock. Everything is."

* * *

### Odile

Everything stopped.

The tea in her mouth went tasteless. The regulator clock on the wall suddenly sounded loud as a hammer.

"The master clock," she said carefully. "At the observatory."

"You repaired it. Three weeks ago."

"I repaired what I was sent to repair." She set down her cup with great precision, because her hands had developed opinions. "Mr. Haddad, when I opened that clock, I found evidence of tampering. Deliberate tampering—someone had altered the escapement so that official time drifts. Slowly. Minutes over weeks. I corrected it, and two nights later I caught a man in the tower re-correcting it back. I hid behind the meridian circle and watched him adjust the drift and leave, and I have spent three weeks telling myself I imagined the significance." Her voice stayed level; she was proud of that. "If the registry stamps by official time—if official time can be made to drift—then every document stamped against it can be invalidated after the fact. Dates won't match. Papers become 'irregular.' Refusals without refusals."

Noor had gone pale in the way of a man watching a diagram assemble itself in his head.

"They're not losing berths to error," he said slowly. "They're manufacturing ineligibility. Mistimed stamps. Invalidated papers. And the families struck from the list never receive a refusal, because technically they were never refused—they were simply *never valid*." He laughed, once, without any humor in it. "It's elegant. God help us, it's elegant. Kelweg built a machine where the crime and the paperwork are the same object."

"Kelweg."

"The director. He interviewed me yesterday. Offered me a promotion contingent on finalizing certain files." Noor's jaw tightened. "Your file, as it happens. He smiled the entire time. I kept thinking—he owns the hours. He literally owns the hours. The whole city sets its watches by a clock he has taught to lie."

Outside, a cart rattled past on Binder Lane, and somewhere a neighbor's dog offered its opinion of the cart, and the world continued being ordinary, which felt obscene.

"So," Odile said. She folded her hands on the workbench, next to the chipped blue cup and the coiled black ribbon, next to her whole life laid out in a stranger's typewritten report. "Here is where we appear to be. You can amend the registry—one name, maybe, if a window opens. I can prove the clock fraud—if anyone could be made to look, which is a considerable *if*. Separately, we are both doomed in interesting ways. Together—"

"Together we're doomed efficiently?" 

"Together we might be doomed *usefully*. There's a difference. Clockmakers appreciate the difference."

He almost smiled. It transformed his face entirely, and she looked away from it quickly, annoyed at herself.

"All right," Noor said. "An alliance. With conditions. First: you tell me everything about the man in the tower. Height, gait, smell of his tobacco, anything."

"Agreed. Second: you amend nothing without telling me first. Whatever name goes into that window, I choose it with you or not at all."

Something flickered across his face—she couldn't read it, and later she would understand she hadn't wanted to.

"Agreed," he said. "Third condition." He began packing the typewriter into its case, his movements suddenly brisk, businesslike, the way people move when staying still has become unbearable. "We meet again. Soon. Officially, I'll need to service my machine—the carriage sticks. I'm told this shop repairs machines."

"We repair clocks, Mr. Haddad."

"Then it's fortunate," he said, snapping the case shut, "that a typewriter is only a clock that writes."

He was at the doorway when she thought of it, and called it after him before courage could reconsider:

"Mr. Haddad. Your report says my father died of a fever. When you amend whatever you amend—correct that, too. He deserved a truer lie."

He paused, half-turned in the gray light of the shop.

"He'll have a true truth instead," Noor said. "As soon as I can manage it."

* * *

### Odile

After he left, she washed the two cups—hers and the chipped blue one—standing at the basin longer than the task required, letting the water run warm over her wrists.

Tomas appeared in the doorway, suspicious, arms crossed. "What did he want?"

"He wanted his typewriter fixed."

"Did you fix it?"

"There was nothing wrong with it," she said, and hung the towel, and did not look at her brother, and hated herself a little for how easily the lie came, and a little more for how necessary it was. "But he'll be back. Machines are like that."

Upstairs, faintly, her mother was singing—an old song in a language from before their name changed—and Odile stood in the middle of the shop, between the honest clocks and the dishonest ones, and realized her hands had gone still for the first time all evening.

Three days later, a repair ticket arrived at the shop, unsigned, requesting urgent service for a portable typewriter, model unspecified, owner *N. Haddad, Ministry of Records*. Folded inside it was a second sheet she nearly missed: a copy of the observatory's maintenance log, and pencilled in the margin, in a cramped civil-servant's hand:

*Kelweg has moved the final transmission forward. The ship's deadline is now Friday. We have until Friday.*
