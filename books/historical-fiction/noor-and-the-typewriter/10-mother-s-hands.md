## 10. Mother's Hands

The lucidity came the way the tide came to Vellengard's harbor—without announcement, without apology for its leaving.

Odile had learned to read the signs the way fishermen read the sky. Some mornings her mother would come down to breakfast with her eyes clear and her voice steady, and she would butter bread and discuss the price of coal as if the fog in her head were a neighbor's dog that sometimes wandered into their yard and sometimes didn't. On those days Odile allowed herself a dangerous thing: hope. She let herself believe that the woman at the table—sharp-eyed, quick-tongued, humming an old song from a country none of them could safely name anymore—was back for good.

She never was. By afternoon the fog usually returned, and her mother would sit by the window with her hands folded in her lap, watching the street as though waiting for someone who had died years ago.

But on this evening, the second Tuesday of November, with frost feathering the corners of the kitchen window and Tomas out delivering a repaired mantel clock to the widow Brandt, Odile came home from the shop to find her mother standing at the stove, stirring a pot of barley soup, and singing.

Not humming. Singing. A low, wavering melody in a language Odile recognized only in the way you recognize a face in a dream—the shape of it familiar, the substance gone.

"Mother?"

Her mother turned, wooden spoon in hand, and smiled. And it was not the vague, polite smile of the fog. It was her mother's smile from before—all edges and mischief, the smile that had once made Uncle Aldrich confess to eating the last of the honey when he was forty-two years old.

"There you are," her mother said. "I've been waiting all day for there you are."

* * *

They ate supper together at the little table by the window, and Odile kept waiting for the mist to roll back in. It did not. Her mother ate two full bowls of soup, asked after the observatory clock with genuine technical curiosity—"And the escapement? Was it worn, or had someone been at it?"—and laughed, actually laughed, when Odile described the Director's assistant who had tried to tell her how a mainspring worked.

"A man explaining a spring to you," her mother said, wiping her eyes. "That's like a fish explaining water."

"You used to say things like that," Odile said carefully. "Before."

"I still say them." Her mother set down her spoon. "You just haven't been able to hear them through the wool. I know what I've become, Liebchen. Don't make that face—I know. There are whole weeks I lose, like pages torn out of a calendar. But tonight the pages are all here, every one of them, and I intend to use them."

Odile's hands went still around her cup of tea. There was something in her mother's tone—not urgency exactly, but deliberateness. The voice of a woman who had set aside an evening the way one sets aside money for a debt.

"Tomas is out," her mother said. "Good. This isn't for him. Not yet. Maybe not ever, if I can help it. Come. Sit closer. My voice doesn't carry the way it used to."

Odile pulled her chair around the corner of the table until their knees nearly touched. Outside, the lamplighter was making his rounds along the canal, and the frost on the glass turned each lamp into a small exploded star.

"Do you remember," her mother began, "what I told you about your father's watch?"

"That he won it in a card game."

"I told you that?" Her mother looked delighted with herself. "What a lie. Your father couldn't bluff his way out of a paper bag. He lost at cards so reliably that we budgeted for it. No—that watch was given to him, along with everything else I'm about to tell you, and if you interrupt me even once I will stop and we will spend the evening discussing the weather instead."

Odile pressed her lips together and nodded.

* * *

"My real name," her mother said, "is Margarethe Ilse Vossenauer."

The name landed in the kitchen like a dropped gear—small, heavy, impossible to ignore. Odile felt it settle somewhere beneath her ribs.

"Vossenauer," she repeated.

"Drop the syllables and you have Voss. That part was your uncle's idea—he said a shorter name draws fewer eyes. He was right about most practical things, your uncle. He was wrong about the important ones, but he was right about names." Her mother folded her hands on the table, and Odile saw then that they were trembling slightly—not from age or illness, but from the effort of holding something steady for so long. "Your father was Emil Vossenauer. He was born in Vellengard, same as me. We were nobody special—a watchmaker's son and a bookbinder's daughter. We married in the spring of 1913, and I was pregnant with you before the summer ended."

"But the papers say—"

"The papers say we came from across the border. That your father was foreign-born, that his trade papers were issued elsewhere, that your family arrived in this country when you were two years old." Her mother's eyes held hers, unblinking. "All of that is false. You were born four streets from where you sleep tonight, Odile. In the blue house on Kessler Lane, with the crooked shutters. Do you remember it? You were five when we left."

Something cold moved through Odile. Five. Not two, as the papers claimed. Five—old enough to have had a bedroom, neighbors, a life that had been erased and redrawn without her consent.

"Why?" she whispered. "Why would we leave? Why would anyone forge papers for people who already belonged here?"

"Because belonging," her mother said, "is a door that locks behind you faster than you think. Sit still. This is the part I've kept longest."

* * *

She told it slowly, the way she might have wound a delicate movement—one careful turn at a time.

In 1921, her father—Odile's grandfather, a man whose face existed only in one faded photograph—had been employed as a clerk in the harbor customs office. He was a meticulous man, her mother said, the kind who squared his papers at the corners and noticed when a number didn't add. And one autumn he noticed that numbers weren't adding. Cargo that entered the port was being recorded at half its weight. Duties were being waived for ships flying certain flags. And the waivers bore signatures—his own among them, forged, applied to manifests he had never seen.

"He went to his superior. Once. Just once, because he was an honest fool and honest fools always go once." Her mother's voice had flattened into the tone Odile imagined she'd used for decades, rehearsing this in silence. "Two weeks later, investigators came to *our* door. Not his office—our home. They found documents suggesting my father had been falsifying records for years. His signature on everything. Genuine-looking, Odile. Beautifully done. Whoever made those forgeries was better at handwriting than my father ever was at anything."

"They ruined him."

"They ruined him, and then they waited to see who would come asking questions. Nobody did. He took ill that winter—grief does that, or worse than grief; I've never been sure—and by spring he was dead. Your father, who had just taken over the watchmaking business, understood immediately what the lesson was: the men who did this still held positions. Still signed documents. Still walked past our windows. And a family that asks questions becomes a family that disappears."

"So you left Kessler Lane."

"We left everything. Your father had a cousin across the border—a real cousin, that much was true—who helped us buy new papers. New history. Foreign-born, which was useful in a strange way, because foreigners are expected to be opaque. Nobody asks a foreigner where he grew up twice." Her mother smiled thinly. "We became immigrants in our own city. We moved eleven streets east and became strangers. Your father wore the new name like an ill-fitting coat for twenty years, but he wore it, because the alternative was taking us all down with him. And now—"

She stopped. Her thumb moved over the back of Odile's hand, back and forth, back and forth, an old rhythm from childhood fevers.

"Now the census comes to doors," her mother said quietly. "Now there are registries, and lists, and a ship in the harbor with twelve berths, and men in ministries deciding whose name goes where. And I lie in that bed some mornings thinking: twenty years of hiding, and for what? For this. For the privilege of being counted by strangers who keep better books than we do."

"Mother—"

"No. Let me finish. I have perhaps another hour in me, and I'm spending it on this." She straightened in her chair. "There are documents. Under the third floorboard in your room, the one that squeaks. Your father's original marriage certificate. His apprenticeship papers, sealed by the guild of Vellengard itself. And a letter—his handwriting—naming the men whose signatures appeared on those false manifests. Two of them, as far as I know, are still living. One has done very well for himself. He sits high enough now that his shadow reaches the harbor office."

Odile's mouth went dry. "Who?"

"That letter will tell you, and I pray you never need to open it. But understand what I'm giving you. If the registry refuses us—if they turn us away from that ship—those papers are your weapon. Real papers proving false papers. Proof that the fraud in this family was committed against us, not by us." Her mother's grip tightened. "Do you see the difference? Everything they might hold against you—it cuts the other direction. If you're clever. If you survive long enough to be clever."

* * *

The soup pot ticked as it cooled. Somewhere down the canal a ship's horn sounded, low and mournful, and both of them flinched at it before pretending they hadn't.

"Twelve berths," her mother said. "I heard the boys talking at the market. Twelve berths and a registry that decides." She studied Odile's face with those sharp, fog-free eyes. "You know more about that registry than you're telling me. Don't deny it—you get the same look your father got when a customer accused him of overcharging. Your chin does this stubborn little thing."

"It's nothing," Odile said. "A census taker came by. He was polite. His questions were strange, that's all."

"Mm." Her mother clearly did not believe a word of it, and mercifully chose not to say so. Instead she reached across the table and took both of Odile's hands in hers. Her palms were rougher than Odile remembered, mapped with new lines since the last time she'd really looked.

"Then hear the only thing I'll ask of you, and I'll ask it once, because I don't have the strength to ask it twice." Her voice wavered and steadied. "Whatever happens with berths and lists and ships—whatever arithmetic you children do in the dark where you think I can't hear it—do not split up. Not your brother. Not yourself. Not for me."

"Mother—"

"*Not for me.*" The words cracked like a snapped wire. "I am sixty-one years old and my heart is tired and my head leaks like the roof over the shop. I have lived my whole life inside other people's lies, Odile—my father's killers' lies, your father's forged papers, my own smiling silences. I will not have the last true thing I do be the reason my children scatter. Promise me. Promise me you'll stay together, whatever the list says. Swear it on your father's watch."

Odile wanted to promise. The words rose easily—they were kind words, comforting words, the sort of words you give a sick woman to help her sleep. And that was precisely why she couldn't say them. Because she had spent three weeks doing arithmetic in the dark, twelve minus however many, one berth against another, and she knew—as her mother surely knew, in the lucid place behind her eyes—that promises like that were made to be broken by someone, sooner or later, in a harbor office or a registry room, by a stranger's keystroke or a daughter's desperate hand.

So instead she did what her family had always done. She lied gently, with love, in the family tradition.

"I promise we'll do everything we can to stay together," Odile said.

Her mother looked at her for a long moment. Then, to Odile's astonishment, she smiled—a sad, knowing smile, the smile of a woman who had spent twenty years recognizing forgery when she heard it.

"That's the best lie you've ever told me," her mother said softly. "Your father would be proud. Now go lift that floorboard before I lose the thread again. And Odile—" she caught her daughter's wrist as she rose, "—when the time comes, and you meet the man who owns the hours? Remember that my father was an honest man. Make them write *that* down somewhere."

Odile stood in the doorway a moment longer, watching her mother begin, calmly, to wash the supper dishes as if she had not just handed her daughter a buried arsenal and a broken inheritance in the same breath.

Upstairs, kneeling on the cold floorboards of her room, Odile pried up the third board—the one that squeaked—and found the oilcloth packet exactly where a lifetime of silence had promised it would be. Inside: the certificate. The guild seal, intact. And the letter, folded twice, addressed in her father's careful hand:

*To whoever in this family is brave enough.*

She was still holding it, unread, when she heard boots on the stairs—and Tomas calling her name, breathless, from below.

"Odile! The harbor office posted the final list tonight. Our name's on it—but Odile—" he caught himself on the doorframe, white-faced, "—we're eleventh."
