## 11. The Ministry's Clockmaker

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Noor Haddad would later consider fitting, because Tuesdays were the days the Ministry of Records did its worst work. Mondays were for coffee and apologies; Wednesdays were for pretending nothing had happened. Tuesday was the day the machinery actually turned.

The note was typed, naturally. Everything at the Ministry was typed—typed, stamped, countersigned, filed in triplicate, and then filed a fourth time because someone had once lost a file in 1931 and the Director General had never emotionally recovered. The note read:

*Haddad, N., Census Division III. Report to Sub-Directorate 7, fourth floor, 09:00. Bring current ledgers. Expedited processing authorized under Statute 44-b. — K.*

*K.* Not even a full name. Noor had worked for the Ministry long enough to know that the smaller a signature, the bigger the man.

He arrived at nine o'clock precisely—not out of eagerness but out of a census taker's professional instinct that punctuality was the only armor available to men like him. He carried his ledgers under one arm and his portable typewriter under the other, because the clerk at the door had once told him, with a straight face, that all documents entering Sub-Directorate 7 must be accompanied by "the instrument of their origin." He suspected the rule existed purely so the guards could watch people juggle.

"Fourth floor," said the guard, without looking up from his newspaper. Then, as an afterthought: "Lift's broken. Has been since spring."

"Has anyone considered repairing it?"

"Considered," the guard said, turning a page. "Rejected."

So Noor climbed four flights of marble stairs, arriving at Sub-Directorate 7 slightly breathless and trying very hard not to look it, which was difficult, because Sub-Directorate 7 turned out to be the coldest room he had ever entered in his life.

Not cold in temperature—though it was that too, aggressively, the radiators painted shut sometime during a previous administration. Cold in the way an operating theater is cold. Long tables. Green-shaded lamps. And clerks, dozens of them, sitting in perfect rows, each bent over a typewriter, each typing the same kind of page, the sound merging into a single continuous clatter like rain on a tin roof—a rain that spelled names.

A woman with spectacles on a chain directed him to a desk near the window with a jerk of her chin. "Haddad? You're late."

"It's nine o'clock exactly."

"Then you're late for nine o'clock yesterday. Sit." She dropped a folder in front of him. It was thick enough to stop a door. "Director Kelweg wants the harbor registry finalized before departure. That means your division's entries get verified, ranked, and transmitted today. All of them."

"All of them?" Noor kept his voice pleasant. Pleasantness, he had learned, was another kind of armor. "There are eleven hundred entries in Division III alone."

"And there are twelve berths." The woman said it the way one says *and there is weather*. "Kelweg wants the priority list locked by end of day. Officially sealed, timestamped, and transmitted to the Harbor Office." She tapped the folder. "Timestamped, Haddad. Do you understand what that means?"

"I—" He didn't, quite. "Official time. Observatory certification."

"Good. You're smarter than the last man they sent down from Census." She was already walking away. Over her shoulder: "The seal clock is by the transmission window. Every page gets stamped against it before it goes out. If your stamp doesn't match observatory time to the second, the page is void. Void pages don't board ships. Their families do something else instead." She paused, and added, with what she may have believed was kindness, "I wouldn't think about that part too hard if I were you. It interferes with productivity."

Noor opened the folder.

And understood, immediately, why she'd told him not to think.

* * *

The morning passed in the particular trance that bureaucratic work induces—a trance Noor knew well, having spent two years inducing it deliberately in himself. Verify name against ledger. Rank household by criteria. Type summary. Pass summary to stamper. Watch stamper press the brass stamp into the wax pad and then onto the page, crisp and official, the date and time biting into paper like a small dark promise.

It was during the eleventh verification—or perhaps the twelfth; they had begun to blur—that he noticed the stamp clock.

It sat beside the transmission window on a marble pedestal, as ornate and self-important as everything else Kelweg's sub-directorate owned: a regulator clock, glass-fronted, weight-driven, its pendulum swinging behind the glass with the serene confidence of an institution that has never once been questioned. Beneath the dial, where a lesser clock might have said *made in Vellengard*, it bore a small brass plaque:

*MASTER TIME. SYNCHRONIZED TO OBSERVATORY. DO NOT ADJUST.*

Noor looked at it. Looked at his own pocket watch—the cheap, honest, five-mark thing his father had given him when he'd taken the census post, the watch that ran three minutes slow no matter how often he scolded it—and set them side by side on the desk.

His watch said 11:47.

The master clock said 11:52.

Five minutes fast.

He checked again, thinking his own eyes had gone soft. He held the two faces together like a man comparing fingerprints. Five minutes. Not drifting—fixed. He remembered now that it had been five minutes fast at nine o'clock this morning too, though he hadn't thought anything of it at the time. Clocks lied all the time; it was practically their profession.

But this clock wasn't allowed to lie. This clock was the arbiter. Every page that went through that window would carry *its* time, certified against the observatory, and every page whose stamp disagreed with the official record by more than a tolerated margin—

He stopped typing.

He sat very still, hands flat on the desk, and let the thought finish itself, because it was going to whether he permitted it or not.

Last week, Odile had stood in her uncle's shop with gear oil on her fingers and told him—in that careful, sideways way she had, offering truth the way you offer a cat, palm down, letting it come to you—that the observatory's master clock was wrong. That someone had been inside it. That the drift wasn't decay. Decay was random. This was *curated*.

At the time, Noor had heard her, sympathized, and privately filed it under *the things Odile worries about at two in the morning*, a category that also included whether the harbor gulls were organized. She was a clockmaker's apprentice; of course a sick clock offended her personally. It was like a doctor taking a fever as a grudge.

But now he was sitting in a room where every document that mattered—every berth, every refusal, every family's entire future—was validated against a timestamp, and the timestamp was keyed to the observatory, and the observatory was wrong, and it was wrong *on purpose*, and—

"Oh," Noor said aloud, very quietly, to nobody.

The clerk across the aisle glanced up. "Something?"

"A realization," Noor said. "Nothing actionable."

He picked up his pen and began, in the margin of his scratch pad, in the tiny handwriting he used for grocery lists, to do arithmetic.

If official time ran five minutes fast, then any document stamped between, say, 11:50 and 11:55 by the master clock was in truth stamped 11:45 to 11:50 by the real world. Any deadline expressed in official time therefore arrived five minutes early in real time—or, depending which direction you tilted your head, any real event could be made to appear five minutes *late* against its deadline. A paper submitted honestly at the true cutoff moment would show, in official time, as overdue. A stamp applied within tolerance of the official record could be declared outside tolerance of the archive's copy. The mistimed stamp invalidated the document. The invalid document refused passage. And the family never knew why. They would be told their papers were irregular. Irregularity was fate; nobody appeals fate.

It wasn't sabotage of the registry. It was the registry's *engine*.

The tampered clock wasn't a side effect of something else. It was the mechanism itself—the quiet gear at the center, the one that moved all the other gears while looking decorative. Someone had built a machine in which time itself did the dirty work, so that no human hand ever had to refuse anybody anything. The clock refused them. The clock was only doing its job. Who could argue with a clock?

*Elegant,* thought Noor, and felt sick at his own vocabulary. It was elegant the way a guillotine is elegant. One blade, one groove, no wasted motion, and a design philosophy that treated human beings as material.

He thought of the Voss entry sitting in his ledger, flagged, ranked low. He thought of Odile's mother's cough, which he had heard through the wall of the Voss kitchen like a metronome nobody wanted to conduct. He thought of Tomas, who had once followed Noor half a block to ask, gravely, whether the typewriter had feelings, and on being told probably not, had said, "That's a shame. It gets to hear everything."

Five minutes. Twelve berths. Eleven hundred households.

Across the room, the stamper raised his arm and brought it down, and the sound of the stamp was very small and very final, like a door closing somewhere far away.

* * *

At noon, the summons he had been dreading and expecting in equal measure finally arrived, delivered by a boy so young his uniform still had creases from the shop.

"Director Kelweg will see the census officer now."

"Now" turned out to mean after forty minutes in an antechamber furnished with one chair, one potted plant that had given up on life with dignity, and a framed portrait of the Director General (the 1931 file-loss one) looking disappointed in perpetuity. Noor used the time to rehearse innocence. He was good at innocence; his face had been built for it, round and open, a face grandmothers trusted with their purses and ministries trusted with their censuses. But innocence, he knew, was like the master clock: most convincing right up until someone checked it against something true.

The office beyond was warm. Of course it was warm. Radiators working, carpet thick, a fire going in November as though fuel were a personality trait. Director Kelweg sat behind a desk of pale birch wood, signing papers, and did not look up for a full minute, which Noor understood as a greeting in itself.

"Haddad." Kelweg had a voice like a well-oiled drawer—smooth, and opening on things you'd rather stayed shut. "Division Three. Your completion rates are excellent. Your handwriting—" he lifted a ledger page between two fingers, examining it like a specimen "—is atrocious, but your figures are clean. Figures matter. Handwriting is vanity."

"Thank you, Director."

"Do you know why I called you here?"

"To expedite the harbor registry."

"That's the reason I gave your supervisor." Kelweg set the page down and finally looked at him. He had gray eyes, Noor observed, the color of unpolished nickel, and they did the thing that efficient eyes do: weighed, catalogued, filed. "The actual reason is that the final ranking requires field verification of certain flagged entries. Sensitive ones. Entries where the documentation history is"—he selected the word the way a man selects a key—"complicated. I need someone discreet. Someone who visits households already. Someone whose presence raises no questions, because asking questions is literally his job description."

Noor smiled the smile of a man agreeing about the weather. "Census work does have its advantages."

"Mm." Kelweg slid a folder across the desk. Noor didn't reach for it. "You'll verify the flagged entries personally. You'll report directly to me. And Haddad—" the drawer-voice dropped half a tone, almost confidential, almost warm, "—there is a promotion in this. Section lead. Your own desk. Your own typewriter, a proper one, a Ministry machine with a carriage wide enough for a eulogy. Men have ruined themselves for less."

"You're very generous, Director."

"I'm very practical. There's a difference, though people flatter me with the first word." Kelweg leaned back. "One more thing. A formality. The observatory sent over a notice this morning—their master clock is undergoing maintenance. Some drift, they say. Being corrected." He watched Noor with mild interest. "You're a man who works with official time daily. Does drift concern you?"

Every instinct Noor possessed stood up and screamed at once, in several languages.

He thought of Odile's hands, precise and sure, holding a gear up to the light. He thought of her saying, *clocks don't lie by accident, Noor. Lying takes craftsmanship.* He thought of the phrase *do not adjust* screwed in brass above a lie.

"Drift concerns everyone," Noor said pleasantly. "Though I've always found that clocks correct themselves eventually. Given time." He paused, savoring it. "Which is rather their whole department."

Something flickered at the corner of Kelweg's mouth—not quite amusement, not quite suspicion, but a cousin of both. "Given time," he repeated. "Yes. Well. Time is the one thing we're short of. The ship departs on the twenty-ninth, and the final transmission seals at—" he consulted a card, though Noor would have bet his father's slow watch that he didn't need to "—sixteen hundred hours, official time. Observatory-certified. No extensions. The Ministry, you understand, cannot argue with a clock."

"No," Noor agreed, standing, gathering his hat. "Who could?"

He walked out past the stamp desk, past the transmission window, past the master clock ticking away five minutes ahead of the truth like a man whistling past a graveyard he himself had dug. On the stairs—he took the stairs; the lift was broken, had been since spring, would presumably still be broken at the end of the world—he counted flights and turned the shape of it over and over until it gleamed.

Sixteen hundred hours, official. Which meant 15:55 in God's time, or 16:05, or whatever the drift decided to be that day. A deadline that moved. A seal that could be made early or late at will. Berths granted and refused by a pendulum.

He should go home. He should sleep. Instead his feet carried him, as feet do, toward the one place in Vellengard where someone would understand, and would also make tea, and would also be furious in the specific, luminous way that made him feel less afraid and more alive.

Voss & Nephew, Clockmakers. The little brass bell over the door. Odile bent over a movement with her sleeves rolled, hair escaping its pin, looking up with that expression she wore—half wariness, half welcome, entirely hers.

"Noor," she said. "You look like a man who's seen a ghost with excellent posture."

"Worse," he said, setting his hat on the counter. "I've seen the gears." He lowered his voice as the uncle shuffled past with a tray of escapements. "Odile. Your clock. The observatory clock. It isn't just making documents wrong. It's the whole design—it's how they choose who sails. The deadline is keyed to it. They can't finalize the registry without it, and it lies."

Her hands went still over the gear train. Outside, a tram bell rang the quarter hour—one city, one time, one lie underneath it all.

"Then," Odile said slowly, "we don't need to expose the fraud. We need to control the clock."

And Noor, who had come to warn her, realized he had instead handed her the blueprint.
