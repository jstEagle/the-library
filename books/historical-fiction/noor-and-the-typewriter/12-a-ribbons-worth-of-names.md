## 12. A Ribbons Worth of Names

The Ministry of Records kept its wastebaskets emptied twice a day, at seven in the morning and seven in the evening, by a porter named Brumm who whistled opera badly and trusted no one under fifty. Noor had learned the schedule the way he learned everything—by sitting still and watching until the pattern confessed itself.

By the third evening he knew exactly which basket to visit: the tall iron one beside the ranking clerks' desks, not his own. His own basket held nothing but his honest mistakes, and he had stopped making those weeks ago, ever since the Voss entry had come back flagged in red pencil with a single word in the margin—*verify*—that had meant, as far as Noor could tell, *doubt this family until they cease to exist on paper.*

At ten past seven, after Brumm had wheeled his cart away still murdering *Carmen*, Noor crossed the ranking room on the pretext of returning a stapler he had borrowed three days earlier and had no intention of returning. The basket sat where it always sat, patient as a confessional.

He had told himself, for two full days, that he was only curious. Curiosity was a respectable vice in a census man. It came with the territory. You asked a question too many and called it thoroughness.

He reached into the basket and lifted out the crumpled carbon ribbons.

They were heavier than they looked. That was the first thing that surprised him—not the act itself, which turned out to be as easy as picking up an apple, but the weight of the things. A ribbon was mostly cloth and ink, thin as a scarf, and yet when he gathered four of them into his palm they seemed to pull downward like something drowned.

* * *

His office—or his corner of an office, partitioned by a screen painted the green of old pond water—had a lamp, a lockable drawer he'd never locked, and a window that looked out on the chimney of the Records annex, which smoked all day regardless of weather, as though the building itself were nervous. Noor shut the door, propped the screen a little wider for the look of ordinary work, and unrolled the first ribbon across his desk beneath the lamp.

Reading a carbon ribbon, it turned out, was a skill. You could not simply read it; you had to coax it. He laid it flat, angled the lamp low so the light raked across the surface, and there the struck names rose up in reverse—white letters pressed into black cloth, ghost-writing, each character a small negative space where someone's future had been typed and then deliberately unwritten.

The typists at the Ministry used the backspace-and-strike method: type the name, then retype over it with the ribbon cover lifted, driving the keys through the cloth so the letters shredded and became illegible. What they had not accounted for—what no one accounts for, Noor thought, until it is too late—is that a man who spends his life reading other people's handwriting learns to read anything. Census forms came back scrawled, smudged, written in the dark by lamplighters and dockhands who signed with an X and trusted him to make it a name. He had spent two years learning to see what people almost wrote.

So he saw.

*HALLORAN, M., wife, three children — HARBOR DISTRICT.* Struck.

*PEIK & SONS, rope merchants — QUAY 4.* Struck.

*ABRAHAMSEN, E., teacher — LANTERN STREET.* Struck.

He copied them onto a blank form in his own careful hand, the hand the Ministry praised for its legibility, and found that his pen slowed with every line. Not from difficulty. From the growing understanding of what he was doing. He was not taking notes. He was taking testimony.

There were eleven names on the first ribbon. On the second, nine. On the third, fourteen, including two whole families whose entries he recognized because he had taken them himself—he remembered the smell of Mrs. Halloran's kitchen, herring and carbolic soap, and her youngest boy hiding behind her skirt and then peeking out, deciding Noor was safe, offering him a bent tin soldier as tribute. Noor had accepted the soldier with great solemnity. It was in his coat pocket now. He had forgotten it was there until his fingers found it, and then he could not stop touching it, the way you touch a sore tooth.

None of these families had done anything wrong. That was the thing he kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. They had not failed a medical inspection or defaulted on a tax. Their only crime, as far as he could determine, was arithmetic: twelve berths, one ship, and a registry that needed the list of the eligible shortened until it fit inside the list of the chosen. Someone upstairs had decided that the fairest way to choose was to make sure certain names never appeared on any list at all. The families would receive their refusal notices—*regret to inform*—and would spend the rest of their lives believing the harbor had simply been full.

Noor set down his pen and looked at the chimney smoke for a while.

His father had been a weighmaster in another port, in another country, in a life that had ended the way such lives ended, with soldiers and a queue and a boat that did not wait. Noor had been six. What he remembered was not the soldiers but his father's scales, brass pans balanced on a knife-edge, and the lesson delivered over them: *the scale does not care who is standing before it. Your job is to make sure you do not care either.*

He had built a career on that neutrality. Neutral questions, neutral forms, neutral ink. And here was the discovery of his thirty-first year, arriving not with thunder but with a wastebasket: neutrality was not a virtue. It was a product. It was manufactured upstairs, in bulk, and distributed to men like him so that the weighing would go smoothly and no one would check the weights.

* * *

The fourth ribbon he almost didn't read.

It was shorter than the others, half-used, and it had been crumpled harder, as if whoever discarded it had wanted it gone in a hurry. He smoothed it against the desk with the flat of his hand, once, twice, the gesture oddly tender, like calming an animal.

And there, in the raked light, in white-on-black ghost letters, half-shredded but unmistakable to a man who could read almost-writing:

*VOSS.*

Not just the surname. The whole entry, struck in the same brutal overstrike: *VOSS, O. — apprentice — CLOCKMAKERS' ROW.* And beneath it, in the smaller characters of a second pass, the rest of the household, each name driven through: the mother. The brother. Tomas, fourteen years old, typed and untyped in the same minute by some clerk who had never seen him, never watched him count coins with the fierce concentration of a boy who understood exactly what money meant.

Noor sat very still.

He had known, of course. He had known since the red-pencil flag, since the tea in the Voss parlor where Odile had watched him with eyes like a cat deciding whether the hand reaching toward it held food or a trap, since she had finally said, in that flat careful voice she used when honesty cost something, *You should know our papers are not entirely ours.* He had known, and he had promised to amend what he could amend, and he had gone home and slept, more or less, like a man who has made a bargain and believes bargains are binding.

But knowing a thing and seeing it typed are different knowings. Somewhere in this building, a machine had held the Voss family in its memory—a machine not unlike the one that sat on his own desk, that he oiled and carried and trusted—and the machine had been made to forget them. Deliberately. With keystrokes. By hands.

He thought about the tin soldier in his pocket. He thought about Mrs. Halloran's kitchen. He thought about Odile's hands, which he had watched repair a jammed carriage with the unhurried certainty of someone who believed machines could be mended if you refused to be afraid of them.

Then he took a fresh form, and instead of copying, he began to transcribe properly—full entries, addresses, occupations, the little marginal notes the ranking clerks had left, because those notes were evidence and evidence was the one thing he could still weigh honestly. *Insufficient priority.* *Quota reallocation.* One note, beside the Abrahamsen woman, read simply: *teacher — agitator?* with a question mark that had been answered by the strike itself.

When he finished, he had three pages. He read them over twice, folded them along the grain, and put them inside the lining of his hatband, which was the only hiding place he could think of that a search would consider too boring to bother with. Then he sat with his head in his hands for a length of time he did not measure, because for once in his life he did not want to know how long anything took.

* * *

The problem, the next morning, was delivery.

He could not simply hand Odile three pages of stolen state secrets. The Voss household was being watched—he had noticed the man on Clockmakers' Row himself, a fellow pretending to read a newspaper that was two days old, which in Vellengard was practically a confession. Anything that passed between them had to look like nothing at all.

It was Odile, indirectly, who solved it—or rather, it was his typewriter, which had developed a stutter in the *e* key that he had been ignoring out of superstition, since the machine had otherwise behaved better than most government employees. A stuttering *e* was a professional embarrassment for a census man. It required a professional.

He walked into Voss & Nephew—Uncle Anselm's shop, though everyone knew the nephew was the actual clockmaker, a fact Anselm advertised with the wounded pride of a man who had invented delegation—carrying the portable under his arm like a sleepy cat.

"Mademoiselle Voss," he said to the shop at large, because the watcher outside deserved his theater. "My machine has developed a speech impediment."

Odile emerged from the back room wiping her hands on a rag, and for one unguarded instant her face did something complicated—relief and fear shaking hands awkwardly—before settling into the polite blankness of a tradeswoman greeting a customer. "Let's have a look, then."

She cleared a space on the bench. Around them the shop ticked in forty voices, wall clocks slightly out of agreement with one another, which Noor had privately decided was the most honest sound in Vellengard. Even time couldn't get the whole city to agree.

"The *e* sticks," he said. "Also I need a repair ticket. For my records. The Ministry reimburses maintenance."

Something flickered behind her eyes. She understood immediately—he saw her understand—and she reached without hurrying for the pad of yellow tickets on a string, the kind every shop in the city used, so common they were invisible.

"Naturally," she said. "Name?"

"Haddad. N. Haddad." He spelled it while she wrote, watching her hand move, and spoke the rest in the rhythm of a man dictating a complaint: "Faulty key strike. Requires cleaning, adjustment of the type bar linkage, and—" he paused, and let the pause do its work, "—replacement of the ribbon. The current one is worn through. I'll want the old ribbon returned to me. It's mine. Property of the census office."

"Worn ribbons can't be reused," Odile said mildly, writing.

"I'm aware. But regulations require their return for destruction." He met her eyes. "I'd rather destroy them myself. There are names on it. Private citizens'. I don't like strangers reading them."

The pen did not falter. She tore off the ticket, wrote something small in the corner—the time, he realized later, and the word *Thursday*, meaning tomorrow night, meaning the observatory, where she worked alone and where a worn ribbon could be read at leisure under a good lamp—and pinned it to the machine's case.

"Ready Thursday," she said. "Come after closing. My uncle makes terrible coffee and insists on offering it."

"I've had your uncle's coffee," Noor said. "I'll bring brandy to put in it. For medicinal purposes."

For the first time in days, she smiled—an actual smile, quick and crooked, gone as soon as it arrived, but real. "That's the spirit. Medicinal purposes account for half the brandy sold in this city."

He paid his deposit, thanked her with the correct degree of customerly warmth, and walked out past the man with the two-day-old newspaper, whom he honored with a friendly nod, because there was no rule that said a guilty conscience couldn't also be polite.

* * *

Thursday night, in the observatory's workroom, with the master clock breathing its slow iron breath through the wall and the city lights scattered below like spilled coins, Odile read the three pages twice.

Noor watched her read. He had brought the brandy and the terrible coffee had indeed been improved by it, and now they sat on either side of the workbench with the lamp between them like a campfire, and he watched her face do what his had done in the green-lit office: move from comprehension to grief to a kind of hard, bright anger that looked, on her, almost like hope.

"The Hallorans," she said quietly. "The mother sells fish on the quay. Her boy gave you a tin soldier."

"You remember that?"

"I remember everything anyone gives away for free. It's rare enough to be memorable." She touched the page, the list of names in his careful hand. "Fourteen on this sheet alone. And these are only the ones that went through your room."

"There are other rooms."

"Yes." She was silent a moment. Below them, somewhere in the dark, a ship's bell rang the hour, and Noor thought—not for the last time—that the hour it rang was whatever Odile's clock said it was, and that this woman held the city's time in her two competent hands and no one had thought to be afraid of her. "Do you know what the worst part is?" she said. "They'll never know why. They'll get the letter. *Regret to inform.* They'll blame themselves. The Halloran woman will stand in her kitchen and think, *I must have filled in the form wrong.*"

"That's the design," Noor said. "A refusal you can't argue with, because there's nothing there to argue with. Just an empty line where your name used to be."

Odile looked up at him. In the lamplight her face was very young and very old at once, the way faces got in Vellengard lately, the war having skipped the city itself so far but not its inhabitants.

"Noor," she said. "This list. If Kelweg finds out you copied it—"

"He won't."

"People said that about my father's papers."

The words landed between them and neither moved to pick them up. After a moment Noor reached across the bench and turned the pages face-down, gently, as if putting children to bed.

"Then we make sure it costs them something," he said. "Every name on this list gets amended where I can amend it. And the ones I can't save—" his voice caught, and he was annoyed at it, and continued anyway, "—the ones I can't save will at least have been witnessed. Somebody will know they existed. Somebody will know it wasn't their fault."

Odile studied him for a long moment. Then she opened a drawer, took out a scrap of paper, and slid it across the bench. On it, in her precise draftsman's hand, was a single line:

*The clock drifts. Four minutes in six days. It isn't broken. It's aimed.*

Noor read it, and read it again, and felt the last comfortable floor of his old life give way beneath him—because he had spent the week thinking the tampering was theft, and this word, *aimed*, told him it was artillery.

"What does a drifting clock do to a stamped document?" she asked softly.

And Noor, who processed documents for a living, felt the answer assemble itself in his chest with a click he would remember for the rest of his life: mistimed stamps. Invalidated papers. Berths revoked not by decision but by arithmetic—by four stolen minutes that no family would ever think to check.

"It decides," he said, "who was never eligible at all."

Above them, through the tower's slit windows, the stars kept their own indifferent time. Down in the city, the master clock ticked steadily toward a midnight that belonged to someone else—and somewhere below, in a narrow house on Clockmakers' Row, a fourteen-year-old boy was lying awake counting days on his fingers, and neither of them yet knew what he was counting them for.
