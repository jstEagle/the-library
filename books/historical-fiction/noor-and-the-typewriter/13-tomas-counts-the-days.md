## 13. Tomas Counts the Days

Tomas had drawn a calendar on the underside of the kitchen table.

Odile discovered it on a Tuesday morning, when she knelt to retrieve a dropped screwdriver and found herself face to face with thirty-one small squares penciled into the pine, each one crossed off in a different hand—some neat, some furious, one decorated with what appeared to be a tiny ship sailing out of the frame.

"You've been drawing on the furniture," she said.

"I've been *documenting*," Tomas said, not looking up from the gear he was cleaning at the far end of the table. Fourteen years old and already he had their uncle's habit of speaking as though every accusation were an administrative error. "There's a difference. Uncle Piet says documentation is the soul of craft."

"Uncle Piet also says you can't document your way out of trouble."

"He says you can if you do it neatly enough." He held up the escapement wheel between finger and thumb, squinting at it like a jeweler judging a diamond. "Twenty-two days left. I counted twice."

Odile put the screwdriver back in its roll very carefully, because her hands wanted to do something else entirely. Twenty-two days until the *Meridian* sailed. Twelve berths. One family of four—or three and a half, depending on how one counted Uncle Piet, who had announced at breakfast that someone had to stay and mind the shop, and whose voice had done something strange on the second word.

"And what does a fourteen-year-old need with a countdown?" she asked, aiming for lightness and hearing it land somewhere nearer to glass.

"Everyone's counting. You count at night—I hear the clock in your room ticking faster when you think no one's listening. Mama counts her medicine spoons. Uncle Piet counts the money in the tin even though it's always the same amount." He set the wheel down. "I figured somebody ought to count out loud."

The kettle began its low complaint on the stove. Odile rose to tend it, grateful for the errand, and stood with her back to him while the water gathered itself.

"Mama slept well last night," she offered.

"That's why I drew the little ship," Tomas said behind her. "For the days she eats all her breakfast. I'm keeping records. If we get to leave, she'll want to know which days were good ones."

He said it so plainly, so *practically*, that Odile had to stand there an extra moment with the kettle in her hands, letting the steam blur whatever her face was doing. This was the cruelty of her brother: he was kind in the ways adults had stopped being, and he noticed everything, and he forgave them for lying by pretending not to notice the lies.

She poured two cups of tea and carried them to the table, and before she sat down she took the pencil from behind his ear—the way she'd done since he was six—and added a small star inside today's square.

"What's that for?"

"For documentation," she said. "The day you cleaned the escapement without being asked."

"It's Tuesday. Tuesdays are always good days. The bakery has seed rolls on Tuesday."

*Then may every day be Tuesday,* she thought, and drank her tea.

* * *

The shop below smelled of oil and brass and, this week, of the particular anxiety of forty-seven alarm clocks belonging to the harbor pilots' association, all of which had begun losing four minutes a day in sympathy with the master clock above the observatory—a fact Odile had explained to Uncle Piet as a matter of mechanical contagion rather than sabotage, and which he had accepted with the weary nod of a man who had lived through three currency collapses and no longer asked machinery for explanations.

Tomas worked the counter after school now. It had started as a way to keep his hands busy; it had become, without anyone deciding it, a way to keep his mind busy too. He greeted customers with a gravity that charmed them, quoted prices slightly wrong and then corrected himself with visible relief, and kept a ledger of repairs in handwriting so precise that Uncle Piet had briefly suspected forgery.

It was there, among the alarm clocks, that he made his offer.

Odile came down at closing to find the last customer gone and Tomas standing very straight behind the counter, the way he stood when he'd rehearsed something.

"I want to talk to you about the berths," he said.

"Tomas—"

"Twelve berths. Mama's sick, so she needs one. You're the one who can fix things here, but they don't have clockmakers where we're going—everyone says the ships go somewhere with factories, and factories need machinists, so you should have one. Uncle Piet won't take one, he's already decided, he thinks nobody notices him deciding things." He took a breath. "So that leaves me and the arithmetic doesn't work. Eleven people can fit in ten berths. I did the sums four times."

Odile set down the lamp she'd been carrying. The shop went quiet around them, forty-seven alarm clocks holding their breath.

"There are twelve berths," she said.

"Eleven now. The Brenners' papers were invalidated—you heard Papa Kessler telling the fishmonger, everyone tells the fishmonger everything. So it's eleven, and there are more than eleven names ahead of us on the list, and I—" His voice did the thing voices do at fourteen, cracking sideways without permission, and he hated it instantly; she could see him hating it. "I'm the lightest thing in this family. I eat the least. I'm the easiest to carry through life. Mama needs a doctor. You need a work permit. I just need to be fourteen somewhere, and I can be fourteen anywhere."

The lamplight caught the brass behind him, row upon row of small patient machines, and Odile thought: *this is what our father built us out of.* Springs and patience and the stubborn belief that small things, correctly made, could hold against enormous weights.

"No," she said.

"You haven't thought about—"

"I've thought about nothing else for nineteen days. That's how long since the list went up. I've done the arithmetic in the bath, on the tram, in the observatory tower at midnight with my fingers inside a machine the size of a cathedral. There isn't a version of the sums where we leave you." She came around the counter. "And there is absolutely no version where you give up your seat so that I can have one, because I would sit on that ship and look at the water for the rest of my life wondering which dock my brother was standing on."

"The registry decides anyway," he said, quieter now. "That man's typewriter decides. Not the sums. The sums are just—" He gestured vaguely at the air. "Decoration."

Something cold moved down Odile's spine, because her brother had just described the Ministry better than most newspapers managed to.

"Listen to me." She took his shoulders. Up close he was all elbows and collarbones, a boy assembled from spare parts, with ink on his ear where the pencil lived. "Mama will sail. You will sail. Those are the two fixed points, and I am the escapement between them, and I have never once failed to make a mechanism run. Do you understand? Whatever has to be repaired, adjusted, or quietly taken apart and rebuilt—I'll do it. Your only job is to keep crossing off those little squares."

"They're not little squares," Tomas said, with sudden fierceness. "They're days. They're *our* days. And there aren't many left, and everyone keeps talking to me like I'm a clock that can't tell time."

Odile laughed—it escaped before she could weigh it—and then, because laughter alone wouldn't hold him, she lied.

"The odds are better than they look," she said. "There are movements happening you don't know about. Corrections being made. Names being looked after." Noor's careful pen strokes; the ribbon in his pocket; the whole secret architecture of small rebellions she was not free to describe. "Trust the clockmaker, Tomas. When have I ever told you a mechanism was sound when it wasn't?"

He searched her face with their father's eyes, and for one terrible moment she thought he would find the seam in the lie, the place where hope had been soldered over dread.

"Never," he admitted finally. "But you once told Mama the shop cat was 'resting' when it had clearly died."

"The cat was resting *permanently*. It was a question of phrasing."

That got a real laugh out of him, cracked-voice and startled, and the fierceness went out of his shoulders. He turned back to the counter and began squaring the repair ledgers with unnecessary precision, which was how Tomas hugged things he couldn't hug.

"Twenty-two days," he said, not looking up. "If the sums change, you'll tell me? Even if they're bad sums?"

"If they're bad sums," Odile said, "you'll be the first to know."

She was lying again, and they both pretended she wasn't, and upstairs later she heard his pencil scratching under the table—crossing off another square—and the sound went through her like a file through soft metal.

* * *

Her mother was having a good evening.

These came without forecast, the way weather comes over water—one day the fever banked, the eyes clear, the hands steady enough to braid hair or shell peas or, tonight, deal cards from a deck missing the queen of spades, who had been lost in 1931 and mourned annually. She dealt three hands at the kitchen table while Tomas narrated the rules of a game he had invented, which appeared to involve bankruptcy as both objective and punishment, and Uncle Piet lost gracefully and Odile lost ungracefully, and for an hour the house on Clockmakers' Row sounded like a house where nothing was ending.

It was near midnight when her mother caught her wrist.

The others had gone up. The lamp was low. Her mother's fingers, though thinner than they'd been in autumn, still knew exactly how to find a pulse, an old nurse's trick she claimed to have forgotten.

"You've stopped sleeping," her mother said.

"I sleep."

"You sleep like a watchmaker—four hours, wound tight, ready for the bell." She didn't let go. "Your father did the same before the border business. I used to lie awake listening to him pretend to snore. Terrible pretense. A man who could regulate an escapement to a tenth of a second could not fake a snore."

Odile sat down properly, pulled close by the grip on her wrist, and for a moment neither spoke. Down the street the harbor bells rang the half hour—wrong, of late, by a few minutes, and only she knew why, and the knowing sat in her chest like swallowed coal.

"Mama. If it came to choosing—"

"Don't." The word was quiet and absolute. "I know what you and that boy are carrying around, both of you, offering yourselves to each other like coins. I raised you in a house of mechanisms; I know a loaded spring when I live with one." She released the wrist and folded her hands instead, and her eyes in the low light were suddenly, unbearably lucid. "No one chooses. That's the arrangement. We stay together or the whole mechanism is worthless—do you hear me? A clock missing one wheel isn't mostly right. It's dead."

"Yes, Mama."

"And Odile." Her mother leaned forward, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for true things. "Whatever you're doing with that clock in the tower—whatever you're winding or unwinding up there at night—your father would have done the same. He spent his life making time honest. It's the family trade." A faint smile. "We're simply doing it at scale now."

Odile kissed her mother's cool forehead and climbed the stairs with her heart ringing like a struck bell, and lay awake listening to the city clock strike one, two, three—all of it minutes adrift from truth, all of it hers to answer for.

* * *

The knock came at dawn.

Not Noor's knock—that had developed, over three visits, a recognizable rhythm, two quick and one shy. This was three slow blows, official weight, and by the second one Uncle Piet was already moving to the door with the expression of a man greeting weather he'd predicted.

A ministry courier stood on the step in a gray coat, holding a gray envelope, looking generally like a document himself.

"Voss household?" he asked, though the shop sign said Voss & Nephew in letters a kilometer tall.

"That's us," Uncle Piet said pleasantly. "What have we done?"

The courier did not find this funny. "Director Kelweg requests the presence of Odile Voss at the Ministry of Records. Today. Before noon." His eyes flicked past Piet's shoulder, found Odile on the stairs, and held there a moment too long—the flat, inventorying look of a man checking a name against a list. "Regarding the master clock. And regarding certain irregularities in recent emigration filings."

Behind Odile, on the stairs, she felt Tomas go still.

"Tell the Director I'll come directly," she said, and was pleased to hear her voice come out level, competent, invisible—the voice of a girl summoned to fix a mechanism.

The courier nodded, turned, and walked away down Clockmakers' Row into the pale morning, and Odile stood on the stairs with her hand pressed flat against her brother's shoulder—steadying him, or perhaps herself—while behind her, in the kitchen, twenty-one uncrossed squares waited under the table like a debt.

Kelweg knew. Kelweg knew *something*. And she had less than six hours to decide which of her secrets she could afford to lose.
