## 14. The Man Who Owns the Hours

### Odile

The summons came by bicycle.

That was the first strange thing. Ministries did not send bicycles; they sent letters on gray paper with gray seals, or they sent men in gray coats who did not bother to knock. But on Tuesday morning a boy of perhaps fifteen pedaled up to the Voss clock shop with a leather satchel and an expression of tremendous self-importance, and handed Odile a card that read:

*The Ministry of Records and Registries requests the attendance of Fräulein Odile Voss, clockmaker, at eleven o'clock. Matters concerning the Observatory timepiece.*

"Matters," her uncle said, when she showed him the card. He turned it over twice, as though the back might confess something the front had withheld. "In thirty years, no ministry has ever cared about a clock."

"They care about *the* clock," Odile said. "It's different. It's the only one in the city they're allowed to be wrong about."

Her uncle laughed — he was a man who laughed at most things, which Odile had privately always considered either a virtue or a very sophisticated form of hiding — but his eyes stayed serious over the rim of his coffee. "Wear your good collar," he said. "And remember: you are a girl who fixes springs. Nothing more. If a man asks you what time it is, tell him the truth and nothing else. It's the one question where we can afford honesty."

She dressed carefully. Not too fine — fine invited questions — and not too shabby, because shabby invited pity, and pity was only curiosity wearing a kinder coat. In the mirror she practiced the face she'd been perfecting since she was nine years old, the face that said *I am here to work and then I will go home*, and she found, examining it, that her hands were shaking slightly.

She was not afraid of ministries. She was afraid of being interesting.

The Ministry of Records occupied a building that seemed embarrassed by its own grandeur — a former bank, all marble columns and brass, now filled with clerks who shuffled paper with the grim devotion of monks. The brass plaque outside had been installed over an older one; Odile could see the ghost of the previous lettering beneath the polish. Cities were like clocks that way, she thought. You could change the face, but the old gears kept turning underneath.

A clerk with a magnificent mustache led her up two flights of stairs, down a corridor smelling of ink and cold radiator, and announced her to a door simply marked *KELWEG*.

"Fräulein Voss." 

Director Kelweg stood at his window with his hands clasped behind his back, and when he turned, Odile's first thought — uncharitable, instant, and entirely involuntary — was that he looked like a man who had been carved by someone working from a description rather than a model. Pleasant face. Correctly pleasant. Eyes that smiled a half-second after his mouth, as though waiting for confirmation.

"Thank you for coming. Please, sit. May I offer you tea? I'm told ours is terrible, which is why I offer it — it establishes trust through shared suffering."

Odile sat. "I've had worse tea," she said, because refusing felt like a move in a game she didn't yet understand, and accepting cost nothing.

"Wonderful. We're already allies against the kitchen staff." He poured two cups himself, which surprised her. Powerful men, in her limited experience, let other people pour. "Now. The observatory clock. Tell me what you found."

She told him — carefully, honestly, in the shape of a lesson. Worn escapement teeth. A mainspring fatigued past argument. Dust where dust should not have been. She watched his face the way she watched a balance wheel, looking for the wobble, and found nothing. That frightened her more than a wobble would have.

"And before you," he said, "who maintained it?"

"Herr Brandt. For twenty-two years."

"And Herr Brandt kept excellent records?"

"He kept *some* records. Clockmakers are like priests, Director. We write down sins, not virtues."

Something flickered there — amusement, possibly genuine. "A priest who repairs sins. Yes." He set down his cup. "You understand why I ask. Official time governs stamps, permits, departures. When the master clock drifts, everything downstream drifts with it. Documents dated by a lying clock are documents that never existed. It would be a remarkable instrument for—" he waved a hand, delicately, "—confusion."

The word hung in the air between them. *Confusion.* Odile held her teacup steady through an act of pure will.

"It would be," she agreed.

"But you found only wear. Only honest decay."

"I found a machine that was tired. Machines get tired, Director. They don't lie."

"No," Kelweg said softly, looking at her with those delayed eyes. "Only people lie. And only people can tell the difference." Then, brightening as if a cloud had passed: "Well! I'm satisfied. Finish your repair. Bill the Ministry properly — they'll haggle, they always do, consider it sport. And Fräulein Voss—"

He walked her to the door himself.

"—if you notice anything else. Anything. Wear, drift, tampering, a screw turned by the wrong hand. You'll come to me directly. Not to the harbor office, not to the census bureau. To me." His smile arrived, right on schedule, half a second late. "I am, after all, the man who owns the hours."

In the stairwell, descending, Odile realized her tea was untouched and cold, and that she could not remember a single thing he'd asked her except the questions he hadn't.

### Noor

Noor was summoned for four o'clock, which meant Kelweg wanted them to compare notes, which meant Kelweg wanted them to know they might compare notes, which meant — Noor concluded, climbing the stairs — that the whole architecture of the afternoon was a sentence being written about him, and he had not been shown the grammar.

He had dressed in his second-best suit, the one that said *competent but not ambitious*, and had spent ten minutes that morning deciding against his third-best, which said *ambitious and knows it*. These calculations used to feel absurd to him. Lately they felt like survival arithmetic.

Kelweg's office was warm in a way government offices never were. A real fire. Good tea. A window overlooking the harbor, where — Noor could not help noticing — the emigration ship sat at anchor like a promise someone was already drafting conditions for.

"Census taker Haddad." Kelweg pronounced the name carefully, correctly, and Noor understood that this too was a technique. Most officials got it wrong on purpose. Getting it right was a way of saying *I see you specifically*. "Your district numbers are the cleanest in the city. Do you know how rare that is?"

"I count slowly, sir."

"You *verify* slowly. Counting is a talent. Verification is a character." Kelweg poured tea. Up close, Noor decided the man resembled a well-made chair: handsome, functional, and impossible to imagine having an inner life. "I've recommended you for advancement. Senior registrar. Your own office. A window, even — a real one, not a slit."

Noor's heart performed a small, treacherous leap, which he hated it for. "That's generous, Director."

"Generous has nothing to do with it. Competence should be promoted or it goes to work for our competitors, and we have so many competitors these days." Kelweg sat, folding his hands. "There is one small matter outstanding. The Voss file."

"The Voss file."

"Flagged by my office for standard review. Father deceased, papers originating from outside the district, a daughter recently employed on sensitive municipal equipment." He smiled. "Coincidence stacks up quickly in a small city, doesn't it? Like snow."

"It does," Noor said, and heard his own voice come out flat as a filed-down edge.

"I need it finalized before the registry transmission. A signature from you confirming the household is verified — routine, accurate, closed. Then your promotion takes effect Friday." Kelweg leaned back. "Unless, of course, the file gives you qualms. Some men find that their consciences enlarge suddenly around paperwork. It's the ink, I think. Very fattening."

The silence stretched. Outside, a gull screamed over the harbor, and Noor thought of a carbon ribbon folded into his coat pocket like a contraband heartbeat. He thought of Odile's face when she'd said *my father died believing we were safe*, and of his own typed words traveling upward through the ministry like blood through a vein.

"Sir," he said carefully, "a verification means I stand behind it. I'd want to complete my field checks first. Thoroughness is the whole point of me."

"Thoroughness." Kelweg tasted the word. "Yes. I'm told you're thorough. Very well — Thursday. But Haddad, understand me gently, because I do mean to be gentle: files are like clocks. Everyone assumes they run on their own. They don't. Someone winds them. Someone owns the hours." He rose, extended his hand. "Friday, then. With a signature, or without a career — whichever you prefer. I genuinely recommend the signature. It pairs better with the window."

### Odile

They compared notes that evening in the shop's back room, over the remains of a stew Tomas had made with too much pepper and no apologies. Odile laid out the afternoon like a dissection.

"He offered me protection if I report anything 'unusual.' Which means he expects me to find something unusual, which means either he knows what happened to the clock or he's fishing to see what *I* know."

"He offered me a window," Noor said. He was turning his cup in slow circles, a habit she'd begun to catalog without meaning to. "And a Friday deadline. With a signature on your family's file."

Tomas, who had been pretending to read, lowered his book. "What kind of window?"

"A real one."

"Oh," said Tomas, fourteen and fluent in disappointment. "One of those."

"Tomas." Odile's voice came out sharper than she intended, and she saw him flinch, and hated herself efficiently. Softer: "Go check the shop door, would you?"

He went, gratefully, with the dignity of the exiled.

When he was gone, Noor spoke quietly. "If I sign, your mother's ranking improves. Verified households rise. If I refuse, I'm finished, and someone less careful takes my desk — someone who won't notice the things I've noticed."

"So sign it."

"Odile—"

"*Sign it.*" She gripped the table's edge. "Noor. Listen to me. My mother coughs in her sleep now. Tomas counts days on a scrap of paper he thinks I don't know about. If a signature moves us up that list, you sign it in letters a meter tall. We will handle the rest. That's what we do — we handle the rest. It's practically the family motto."

"It isn't that simple. The flag isn't routine. Someone above Kelweg flagged it, or Kelweg flagged it himself, and either way your father's name is sitting in a drawer somewhere wearing a red mark like a target." He stopped. Looked at her. "Odile. What aren't you telling me?"

And there it was again — the question she'd been dodging since the tea, since the typewriter, since the first evening he'd sat across from her mother asking careful questions in a careful voice. She thought about the papers hidden behind the false back of the tool cabinet. Her father's handwriting. Her mother's real name, spoken once, in the dark, like a coin placed in her palm.

"Not tonight," she said. "But soon. I promise you, Noor — before this is over, you'll know everything. I just need—" Her voice caught. "I need to make sure that when I hand you the truth, you'll still be holding it Friday morning, when it matters."

Noor reached across the table and covered her hand with his. His fingers were ink-stained and warm.

"I've carried worse than the truth," he said. "I've carried the registry."

Above them, faintly, the shop's regulator clock ticked on — steady, honest, telling the true time to an empty room, while somewhere across the city a man with a half-second smile owned every hour that counted.

It was Tomas, returning, who brought the news that cracked the evening open. He burst in with the shop door still swinging behind him, waving a crumpled handbill snatched from the lamppost outside.

"Odile — the harbor office just posted it. The Aldermanns' berth — the one they lost today — it's open again. Eleven berths now. *Eleven.*"

Odile looked at Noor, and Noor looked at the handbill, and neither of them said aloud the thought that settled over the room like frost:

Someone's misfortune had just become their hope — and somewhere, a director with a window view was counting exactly who had reason to celebrate.
