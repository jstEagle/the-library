## 15. Eleven Berths

The news came the way most terrible and wonderful things come to our family: through Uncle Bram, at breakfast, with crumbs on his vest.

"Petras family's out," he said, folding the harbor bulletin into quarters as though it were something shameful he'd caught doing arithmetic in public. "Struck from the list entirely."

I had my spoon halfway to my mouth. It stayed there, hovering, while I did the thing I do when my heart tries to move faster than my face permits—which is nothing, absolutely nothing, while inside me a whole workshop of small gears began turning at once.

"Out how?" Mother asked. Her voice was careful. She had been better since that evening she'd given me her real name—lucid more often now, or perhaps just more willing to spend her clarity where it mattered—but careful was still her native language.

"Mistimed stamp." Bram set down his tea. "Their transit papers were stamped with official time that didn't match the registry log. Four minutes off. The harbor office says the documents are invalid. Irregular. They can't be honored." He said the last word the way a man says *the bread has gone stale*—with resignation, not surprise.

Four minutes. I felt the number settle into my chest like a cold coin.

Official time came from one place and one place only: the observatory master clock, the one I had been keeping alive for three weeks with wire, patience, and increasingly creative lies about its condition. Official time stamped every document in Vellengard. And official time, I knew better than anyone breathing, had been drifting—not by accident, not by wear, but by design. Someone wanted the stamps wrong. Someone wanted families like the Petrases holding papers that looked perfectly fine until they were held up against the registry log and found wanting.

The berth that opened was theirs. Berth number four of twelve. Now eleven berths for a city full of people who had spent two years pretending they weren't afraid, and suddenly everyone was counting again.

"Tomas," Mother said quietly, "stop listening."

"I'm not," said Tomas, who was absolutely listening, his ears practically tilted toward the table like antennae.

"Eat your egg."

"I am eating my egg," he said, and ate it with tremendous ostentation, eyes fixed on the middle distance, which for Tomas was the window, where the gulls were arguing over the fish market scraps.

Bram looked at me over the rim of his cup. He didn't say anything. He never said anything in front of Mother anymore; he had learned, somewhere in the last months, that hope was a load-bearing wall in her and you did not knock on it casually. But his eyes said what his mouth wouldn't: *This is your clock, girl. This drift is your drift. What are we going to do about it?*

I finished my tea. My hands were steady. My hands were always steady; that was the first thing anyone ever praised about me, and the thing no one ever thought to ask the cost of.

* * *

The shop smelled of oil and brass and, this particular morning, of panic wearing a respectable coat. Word travels fast in a port city when the arithmetic changes. By nine there were three people at the counter asking me, in the tone people use for questions that aren't really questions, whether a watch could be made to run *slightly* fast, or whether an old pocket barometer might be converted into something that kept time, and would I swear before God that any certificate I repaired would match observatory time exactly.

"I repair machines," I told the third man, a jeweler named Falk with sweat on his upper lip even in October. "I don't manufacture certainty."

"You don't understand," he said, leaning in. "My daughter is on the list. Eleventh berth. If one more name drops off, she's safe. If the Petrases' berth goes to someone else—" He stopped himself, hearing what he'd said. If the berth went to someone else, someone else's daughter drowned in paperwork instead. Everyone in Vellengard had become fluent in this dialect: grief measured in berths, mercy measured out one seat at a time so that every kindness was automatically someone else's loss.

"The clock is being repaired," I said. "Official time will be true within the week."

That was a lie, or half of one, and we both knew it. Official time could be true within the week if I chose to make it true. It could also remain four minutes adrift indefinitely, depending on what I decided those four minutes were for. Falk left without buying anything, and I stood at the counter feeling the weight of a secret that had grown too large for my pockets.

Because here was the thing I hadn't told Noor, hadn't told Bram, hadn't written down anywhere except in the locked room behind my ribs: I could fix the clock tomorrow. One afternoon's work. Restore the pendulum's true length, correct the escapement, let official time flow honest as spring water. And the moment I did, every stamp issued after that moment would be valid, and the fraud would stop manufacturing victims.

But the moment I did, Kelweg's mechanism would stop working—and a mechanism that stops working announces itself. A man who builds a machine to misdate documents does not shrug when the dates go right again. He looks for hands. And I had already seen those hands once, at midnight, in the tower, adjusting my clock like a man tuning an instrument he believed he owned.

Hope, I was learning, does not arrive clean. It arrives smelling faintly of other people's ruin.

* * *

Noor came at closing time, carrying his typewriter case and a paper bag of almond crescents from the bakery on Maren Street, because Noor Haddad had apparently decided that bribery by pastry was the foundation of our entire alliance and I lacked the will to disabuse him.

"For the shop," he said, setting them down. "For services rendered."

"I haven't rendered any services."

"You listened to me complain about ribbon spools for forty minutes on Tuesday. That's a service. In some jurisdictions it's a sacrament." He settled onto the work stool like a man who had been assigned it by fate. His typewriter sat between us on the bench, its keys worn shiny in the places his fingers loved.

I waited until the shutter was down and Bram had gone upstairs. Then I told him about the Petrases. He went very still, the way he did—not blank, but gathered, all his attention pulled inward like a tide.

"Four minutes," he said finally.

"Four minutes."

"That's not sloppiness. The registry log is typed against official time to the second. For a stamp to miss by four minutes—" He shook his head slowly. "Someone had to hold the document under the stamp and wait. Deliberately. While the minute hand crawled."

We looked at each other across the bench, and I watched him arrive at the same ugly country I'd been living in alone: the understanding that the drift wasn't a flaw in the system. It *was* the system. A machine for making paper lie.

"There's something else," he said. He reached into his coat and produced a folded sheet—the harbor bulletin, the same one Bram had brought, but with the registry column circled in pencil. "Eleven berths now. And look who moved up." His finger found the line. "Voss. Ninth position."

Ninth. Out of eleven. Two days ago we had been eleventh out of twelve, which is to say we had been a rounding error away from nothing. Mother's illness gave us priority points under the medical clause; Father's dead hand still pulled strings through the old shipping connections noted in the file; and now the Petrases' catastrophe had lifted us like flotsam on someone else's shipwreck.

"We should be celebrating," I said. My voice came out flat as sheet metal.

"We should," Noor agreed. Neither of us celebrated.

Instead he did the thing that made me love him, though I wasn't ready to call it that yet, not even silently, not even in the privacy of my own skull. He turned the typewriter around, rolled in a fresh sheet, and began, from memory, typing out everything he knew about berth allocations: the ranking clauses, the point values, the way the medical examination was weighted against the age of the head of household. He typed it plainly, without flourish, a man laying his cards face-up on a table.

"If another berth opens," he said, "you jump to eighth. Maybe seventh, if the replacement comes from below you rather than above." He paused, fingers resting on the keys. "And Odile—if a berth *closes*, if someone else's papers fail, you're close enough to the bottom of the surviving list that the same wind that lifted you can drop you straight back into the water."

"So we're praying for other people's disasters."

"No," he said. "We're watching a machine sort human beings by arithmetic, and we're noticing that the arithmetic was written by a man who adjusts clocks at midnight. Those are different things, even if they feel the same at two in the morning." He tore the sheet free and handed it to me. "Kelweg moved you up because the Petrases fell. But I've been thinking about *why* their papers failed. Not the mechanics—I mean the choice. Twelve berths, dozens of eligible families. Why did the invalidation land on *them*?"

"Bad luck."

"There's no such thing in that building. There's only procedure wearing bad luck's coat." He rubbed his eyes. "The Petrases are Lithuanian nationals with a contested residency history. Their file was already flagged. They were the easiest family to strike without raising questions. Which means the next invalidation will also be chosen, not random. And there are exactly two families near the bottom of the list whose files have been flagged recently." He looked at me, and I saw him decide to say the rest. "Ours is one of them."

The shop clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs, Bram's chair creaked. Outside, a gull screamed its one note of opinion about everything.

"Then we need the clock fixed," I said. "If official time runs true, no more stamps can be falsified. The weapon stops being manufactured."

"And the existing damage?"

"Existing stamps stay suspect. That can't be undone." I hesitated. Here was the ledge. "But Noor—if I restore the clock, Kelweg will know. Immediately. A man doesn't build a four-minute lie and then fail to notice when the lie dies. He'll start pulling threads. Census threads. Repair-shop threads." I met his eyes. "Threads connected to a certain portable typewriter."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then, gently, infuriatingly, he smiled.

"You're offering to blow up our own good fortune to protect strangers."

"I'm offering to stop a machine."

"Those are the same thing, and you know it, and you're doing it anyway." He took one of the almond crescents, considered it, and put it back in the bag, which was the closest he came to losing his composure. "Give me three days. There's a clerk in the harbor office, Verhagen, who owes me—he lost a bet involving a typewriter and a bottle of kirsch, it's a long story—who can tell me whether the Petrase invalidation was logged as error or as decision. If it was an error, we fix the clock and hope. If it was a decision—" He exhaled. "If it was a decision, then fixing the clock won't save anyone. It'll just make Kelweg honest about hunting us."

"And if it was a decision?"

"Then we'll need more than a mended pendulum." He stood, shouldering his case, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch—a habit of his, leaving always in two movements, as if reluctant to commit to exits. "Odile. Ninth position. When I saw it posted, I laughed. Out loud, in the street. A woman asked me if I was well."

"Were you?"

"I don't know yet." The smile again, smaller. "That's the trouble with hope. It feels exactly like fear from the inside."

He let himself out. I stood among the clocks—all of them ticking, all of them honest, little machines that had never learned to lie—and after a while I took the harbor bulletin and read our name again. *Voss.* Ninth of eleven. My mother's breath in a cabin bunk, my brother's fourteen years carried across cold water.

It felt like stolen goods. It was stolen goods—lifted from the Petrases' sinking file by a man who owned the hours themselves. And yet I folded it carefully, the way you fold anything you cannot afford to lose, and I hated myself a little, and I put it in my pocket next to my father's watch, and I went back to work.

* * *

Two nights later, I climbed the observatory hill in the dark to keep my appointment with the master clock, and found the door already unlocked.

Not broken. Not forced. Simply unlocked, the way a door is left when someone wants you to know they were here—or wants you to think so.

Inside, the lamp was lit. On the workbench beneath the great dial, arranged with terrible neatness, lay a single sheet of paper, typed, the letters crisp and confident:

*MISS VOSS — THE MECHANISM RUNS BETTER WITH TWO HANDS. MY OFFICE, THURSDAY, ELEVEN O'CLOCK. COME ALONE. — K.*

And beside the note, placed like a paperweight, like a signature, like a threat dressed as a courtesy: a carbon ribbon. Struck-through, erased, salvaged from some wastebasket I knew intimately—one that lived in a census office, beside a typewriter I knew even better.

On it, half legible through the overtyping, ran a single name.

*VOSS.*

I stood very still in the great ticking dark, eleven berths hanging somewhere above me in the frozen stars, and understood that the hunt had already begun—and that I was not being chased. I was being *invited*.
