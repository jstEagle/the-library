## 16. The Choice, Drafted

The registry office closed at six. The amendment could be made at any hour — that was the terrible elegance of it. Noor had discovered this at half past nine, sitting in the dark of his own kitchen with the ledger open on the table and a cold cup of coffee beside him, understanding for the first time how a single keystroke could be a mercy or a murder.

One name. That was what his access would allow before the audit trail thickened like frost on a window. One correction, entered between the final verification pass and the transmission, small enough to look like clerical diligence rather than rebellion. He had checked the mechanics three times. He had checked them a fourth time out of cowardice, hoping the fourth check would tell him he was wrong.

It didn't. One name. Odile Voss had four people in her family, and one of them — Odile herself — could not go near the list at all, because her name was already a fiction wearing good posture.

Which left three.

He arrived at the clock shop just before ten with his typewriter under his arm like a sleeping cat, and Odile met him at the door with her coat already on and her eyes already braced.

"You found something," she said. Not a question. She'd learned to read him the way he suspected she read clocks — by the position of the hands, by whether anything inside was ticking true.

"Come upstairs," he said. "And bring your mother."

* * *

They gathered in the back room above the shop, where the ceiling sloped low enough to make Noor feel like a giant and the lamp burned amber over a table scarred by decades of repair work. Mrs. Voss sat propped in the good chair with a blanket over her knees, looking smaller than the last time he'd seen her, though her eyes were as clear and unimpressed as ever. Tomas perched on a stool by the workbench, fourteen years old and trying very hard to look like someone who hadn't been listening at doors his whole life.

Noor set the typewriter on the table. Then he told them everything: the ranking system, the keystroke, the arithmetic of one.

He expected weeping. He got silence instead — the particular silence of a family that had been doing arithmetic in its head for months and had simply been waiting for the numbers to arrive.

"How long do we have?" Odile asked.

"The ship sails when official time says it sails. And official time is currently lying." He explained the frozen clock, Odile's own sabotage, the window it bought. "Two days. Perhaps less, if Kelweg finds the drift before I finish."

"And the audit?" Odile's voice was steady. Her hands, folded on the table, were not.

"One change passes unnoticed. Two is a pattern. Three is a confession." He paused. "It has to be a name already on the eligibility list, or the amendment won't parse through the Ministry's checks. So it must be your mother or Tomas."

Tomas stood up so fast the stool clattered backward. "Then it's Mama. Obviously it's Mama. I don't even want—"

"Sit down, Tomas." Odile said it gently, but there was iron under the gentleness, and Noor watched the boy hear it and obey, resentfully, the way all younger brothers obey.

"I'm serious," Tomas muttered, righting the stool without sitting on it. "I'm fourteen. I'm strong. I can work on the other side. What is she going to do, cough at the border?"

"Tomas." Mrs. Voss spoke for the first time, and the room rearranged itself around her voice the way water rearranges around a dropped stone. "You will sit down, because standing up doesn't make your argument better, it only makes you taller."

Tomas sat.

Noor looked at Odile. Odile looked at the table. And in the space between their two silences, the whole night opened up ahead of them like a road with no lamps.

"We should decide methodically," Noor said, because he was a census taker and methodology was both his training and his hiding place. "There are criteria. Medical need. Age. Capacity to survive the passage and establish—"

"Noor." Odile lifted her head. "Are you proposing we score my mother against my brother?"

"I'm proposing we not decide by who shouts loudest."

"My mother isn't shouting. My mother is being reasonable, which is worse." Odile pressed her knuckles briefly to her mouth. "Fine. Methodically. Mama has a bad heart, a worse cough, and a doctor who told us last spring, in a whisper he thought we couldn't hear, that sea air might kill her faster than staying would."

"That whisper," Mrs. Voss said placidly, "was about the cost of the medicine, not the voyage. Doctors whisper about money. It's the only honest thing they do."

"Mama—"

"Hush. You're doing arithmetic again. I can see it behind your face, both of you. You've inherited it from your father, Odile — the belief that love is a problem with a correct answer." She smoothed her blanket with fingers gone thin as clock hands. "Go on with your criteria. I'll listen. But I reserve the right to be unreasonable later."

Noor found himself almost smiling despite everything. There was a gravity in the old woman that bent the whole room toward honesty. He understood now where Odile's spine came from. It wasn't forged. It was inherited, like the papers were not.

"Capacity," he continued, quieter. "Tomas is young. Strong. He could work, adapt, carry the family forward on the other side. Your mother—" He stopped, hating the sentence he was building.

"My mother is a liability," Mrs. Voss finished for him, dry as chalk. "Yes. I've read the same forms you have, young man. I helped fill some of them out, back when the questions were merely rude instead of fatal. Continue."

"Tomas," Odile said suddenly. "It should be Tomas."

"It should not," said Tomas.

"It should, and here's why, and you're going to hate every word." Odile turned to face him fully, and Noor saw her gather herself the way she gathered tools — deliberately, each piece in its place. "Mama's name on that ship gets her across the water and into a sickbed in a country where she knows no one. If the crossing doesn't kill her, the winter will. I'm sorry, Mama, but you taught me never to lie about arithmetic."

"I did no such thing. I taught you never to lie about money. Arithmetic came later and I disapprove of it entirely."

"If Tomas goes, he lands with two working hands and our father's trade in his fingers. Clocks. Machines. He can apprentice anywhere. He can build a life that makes ours mean something." Odile's voice cracked on the last word and she pushed through it. "That's the criterion nobody writes on the forms. Who carries the future? Not who deserves it. Who *carries* it?"

The room went still. Even the lamp flame seemed to lean in.

"No," said Tomas, and this time he did stand, and this time nobody told him to sit. "I'm not letting you put me on that boat like a parcel of the family silver. I'm the one who should stay. I'm the one who can hide, who can run, who can—" His voice betrayed him, cracking upward into the boy he still was. "I'm not going to spend my whole life on the other side of the water knowing I rowed away while you all—"

"You'll spend your whole life knowing you *lived*," Odile said fiercely, "which is more than most of this city gets to do, and which is exactly what Papa would—"

"Don't." Tomas's voice broke clean in half. "Don't use Papa."

And then it was just the four of them breathing in a small room, and the argument ran aground on the rock they had all been steering around since the beginning, and no amount of criteria or capacity or carrying-the-future could move it.

Noor looked at his typewriter on the table. Its keys caught the lamplight, patient as teeth.

"There's another way to decide," he said slowly. "One that leaves no room for shouting."

Odile turned to him. "What way?"

"Ink." He pulled the chair out from the machine. "A census form has a field for household decisions. Head of household declares. In writing. It's the one thing the Ministry trusts more than its own rankings — a signature, an entry in the applicant's own hand. If the decision comes from within the family, formally, on paper, it becomes part of the record. It protects whoever stays, too. It becomes evidence. A documented choice is harder to punish than an undocumented one."

"That's not a way to decide," Odile said. "That's a way to write down whatever we've already decided."

"No," said Mrs. Voss. "It isn't. Sit down, Odile."

Her daughter hesitated — and then sat, because there are voices that mothers keep in reserve for precisely these moments, and Mrs. Voss had just spent hers.

Mrs. Voss rose. It took her a long time, and Noor started forward and was stopped by a raised hand, and so all three of them — Odile, Tomas, Noor — stood frozen in a kind of helpless honor guard while the old woman crossed the small room one breath at a time and lowered herself into the chair before the typewriter.

She adjusted the paper with the deliberation of a woman threading a needle. She flexed her fingers, which had once been quick — Noor could see the ghost of it, the memory of speed living in the joints.

"I filled out my first census form in 1909," she said, conversationally, to no one and everyone. "In a different country, with a different flag, under a different name that was nevertheless also mine. Do you know what I wrote in the occupation field?" She glanced up at Odile, and something passed between them that Noor recognized and looked away from — the private language of women who have survived things by renaming themselves. "Never mind. You know the story. All of it. I gave you the real one last month, and I saw you carry it, and I'm proud of how heavily you let yourself carry it."

Odile's hands had come up over her mouth.

"Now," Mrs. Voss said, turning back to the keys, "everyone in this room believes they have already decided, and everyone in this room has decided differently, and if I leave this to the three of you, you will argue until the ship sails empty and Kelweg closes the border on all of us out of sheer administrative irritation." She settled her fingers on the home row. "So. The head of household declares."

"Mama." Odile's voice was barely there. "Whatever you type — I can't undo it. None of us can."

"I know exactly what I'm typing, my darling. I've been drafting it since the day the harbor office posted the list." A pause. Her hands trembled slightly above the keys, and Noor watched her master them the way her daughter mastered a movement too fine to show. "I am seventy-one years old. I have buried a husband, fled two countries, and raised two children on forged courage and real love. I have done my arithmetic, Odile, and here is my answer, and it is not the one you prepared for."

The keys began to fall.

Noor had typed thousands of entries. He knew every rhythm a human body could bring to a keyboard — haste, fear, boredom, the dead mechanical cadence of the Ministry's men. He had never heard anyone type like this: slow, exact, each letter chosen and struck as though setting it in lead. As though the words were not being recorded but *built*.

Tomas made a sound halfway through the first line, a small animal noise, and Odile took his hand without looking away from her mother.

The carriage rang at the end of the line. Mrs. Voss returned it with one finger, unhurried, and typed the second line, and then, after a moment's thought, a third, short and firm as a period at the end of a sentence.

She sat back. She looked at the page for a long time. Then she drew it free of the platen, held it out to Noor, and said:

"Read it aloud. Decisions should be witnessed. That's the whole point of ink."

His hands were not entirely steady. He unfolded the page beneath the lamp.

*Household of Voss, declaration of the head thereof.*

*I, Marta Voss, being of sound mind and unsound heart, declare the following:*

*The berth goes to my son, Tomas Voss, aged fourteen, who is the strongest of us, the youngest of us, and the only one of us whose whole life is still ahead of him like an unwritten page. I decline the berth. This is not sacrifice. This is accounting. I have lived sixty years on borrowed names; I will not cross the sea on a borrowed berth.*

*To my daughter: you will argue. Do not. You argued beautifully tonight and it changed nothing, which proves you inherited my stubbornness and your father's timing, neither of which I apologize for.*

*To the census man: thank you for asking one question too many. It brought you to my table.*

*This is my decision. Let it be entered in the record, and let no one — child, clerk, or ministry — presume to unmake it.*

*Marta Voss*

Silence. The kind of silence that follows thunder.

Tomas was crying openly now, and pretending he wasn't, which was the most fourteen-year-old thing Noor had ever seen. Odile had not moved. She stared at the page in Noor's hands as though it were a document written in a language she was only just learning.

"You knew," she whispered. "Before we even started. You knew what you'd type."

"I knew what I *hoped* to type." Mrs. Voss reached up and touched her daughter's cheek, one thin hand, warm as banked coals. "Hope is the only honest draft, my love. Everything else is editing."

"But Mama — the crossing, the winter, alone—"

"I will not be alone. I will be here, in my city, in my house, with your uncle downstairs and Mrs. Brandt next door and forty years of neighbors who owe me soup. And when the war ends — because wars end, Odile, they always end, it's the peace that takes planning — this boy"—she nodded at Tomas—"will send for us. Or you will. Or the world will surprise us, which it occasionally does when it isn't being unforgivable." She smiled, and for a moment Noor saw the young woman in the 1909 census, filling in the occupation field under a name that was hers anyway. "Now. Someone put that page somewhere safe, and someone make tea, and someone — census man — enter my declaration into your machine before I lose my nerve, which I assure you I will not, but saying so keeps the room respectful."

Noor laughed — actually laughed, once, wetly, helplessly — and carried the page to the typewriter.

He aligned the carbon. He positioned the declaration beneath the official field. And as his fingers found the keys, he understood that he was about to commit, in duplicate, in triplicate of the soul, the single most rebellious act of his careful life: entering a dying woman's choice into the machine of a state built to erase choices.

The keys fell. *TOMAS VOSS.* Berth eleven of twelve. Entered by the head of household, witnessed, declared, true.

When he finished, Mrs. Voss was watching him with an expression Noor would carry across every border of the rest of his life.

"Well," she said. "Now it exists."

Odile stepped close to him at the table, under cover of gathering teacups, and her shoulder pressed his, and she said, so quietly only he could hear:

"She chose. We didn't let her choose alone. That's the difference. Hold onto that."

He nodded. But even as he nodded, even as the kettle went on and Tomas blew his nose with tremendous dignity, a cold thought slid under the warmth of the room and lay there, patient as ice under a bridge:

The declaration protected Tomas. It protected Marta. But Odile's name existed nowhere on any legitimate page — and tomorrow, when the amended registry transmitted, Director Kelweg's audit would ask one simple question about the eleventh berth.

Why this name?

And the only person in Vellengard who could answer that question was currently pouring tea in a room above a clock shop, with a forged passport hidden in a floorboard, and a sabotaged tower ticking lies above the harbor.

Noor looked at the window, where beyond the rooftops the observatory dome stood black against the stars, holding official time frozen at a lie.

Two days. Perhaps less.

Somewhere in the dark, he was certain, Kelweg was already counting.
