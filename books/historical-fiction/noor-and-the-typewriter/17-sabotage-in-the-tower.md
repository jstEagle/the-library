## 17. Sabotage in the Tower

The observatory at midnight smelled of cold brass and old dust, the way a church smells of candle smoke — a smell that meant someone had been praying here with machines instead of words. Odile climbed the spiral stair with her tool roll under one arm and her heart going like a cheap alarm clock, which was to say: too fast, too loud, and entirely unhelpful.

She had told Noor she would do it. She had told him in the flat, certain voice she used for quoting repair prices, because if she had used her real voice it would have shaken. *Freeze the clock. Buy you the hour.* He had argued, of course. He had argued the way he typed — quickly, precisely, with his jaw set like a man setting type he did not entirely believe in. And then he had kissed her once, hard, on the corner of the mouth where the argument lived, and gone off to do his half of the impossible thing.

Which left her here. Alone. With the most important clock in Vellengard.

"Right," she said to the empty tower, because her uncle always said talking to machines kept them honest. "Let's make you lie beautifully."

* * *

The master clock sat in its glass-and-iron case like a patient who knew too much. Four meters of gears, weights, and consequence. Every official stamp in the city — every emigration paper, every registry transmission, every signature of the state's good faith — took its time from this machine. For weeks someone had been teaching it to drift, two minutes here, three there, so that documents stamped *true* were quietly stamped *false*. Kelweg's elegant little weapon. She had found the tampering; now she would answer it.

Not by fixing it. Fixing it would only restore the lie to accuracy. No — she would stop it altogether. A clock frozen at 11:47 could not authorize anything. The registry's final transmission was keyed to official time, and official time, as of tonight, would be officially nowhere.

She opened the case. The pins inside her kit rattled like nervous teeth.

The escapement first. The escapement was the conscience of any clock — the part that said *no, not yet, wait* ten times a second, forever. She eased the pallet stones back with a shim of soft brass, just enough that the escape wheel would catch and hold. Not broken. Broken could be diagnosed, repaired, blamed. Held. Held looked like an accident of wear, or better, like nothing at all until you stood in front of it and listened.

She listened. The great tick — the heartbeat Vellengard set its watches by — faltered, caught, and stopped mid-beat, like a man interrupted mid-sentence.

Silence rushed into the tower the way water rushes into a hull.

Odile stood very still in it. Above her, through the lantern room's glass, the city lay spread out in its winter dark, harbor lights strung along the black water like a necklace someone had dropped and couldn't find all the beads of. Somewhere down there, a ministry telegraph clerk would glance at the official time feed and frown. Somewhere down there, Noor was feeding a single amended name into a machine that had already begun to wonder what hour it was.

"Eleven forty-seven," she told the clock. "You are eleven forty-seven forever now. Some philosophers would envy you."

Her hands, she noticed, were steady. That surprised her. All her life she had practiced being invisible — the apprentice who swept, the girl who stood behind her mother's shoulder, the family whose papers said they were someone slightly other than themselves. Invisibility had been the whole craft of her existence, passed down like a trade secret. And tonight she had done the loudest thing imaginable: she had stopped time itself for an entire city. Every clerk, every harbor official, every bureaucrat in Vellengard would feel this silence eventually, the way you feel a missing stair in the dark.

The invisible woman, holding the city's wrist so it could not check its watch.

She almost laughed. It came out as something between a laugh and a hiccup, and she pressed her sleeve to her mouth.

Then she heard boots on the stair.

* * *

There is a particular quality to footsteps coming up toward you when you should not be there. Casual footsteps belong to someone with a right to the space. These had purpose in them, and numbers — more than one pair, three at least, climbing with the unhurried confidence of men who had been sent rather than curious.

Odile killed her lamp and did the arithmetic of the tower in two seconds flat. The stair spiraled up past the clock chamber to the lantern room above. There was no second exit. There never was, in towers; architects built them for looking out, not slipping away.

But she had spent three weeks in this tower learning its body the way she learned every machine's body, and bodies have places where they keep their secrets. Between the clock case and the outer wall ran a maintenance gap, barely a shoulder wide, hung with counterweight cables and shadowed by the great driving drum. A child could hide there. A grown woman had to fold herself like a letter.

She folded herself into the dark as the door opened below.

Lamplight swung up the stairwell ahead of the men, throwing their shadows huge and distorted across the vaulted ceiling — giants with small hats.

"The governor's office says the feed went dead at eleven forty-seven," said a voice she didn't know. Young, annoyed, the voice of a man who had planned to be asleep. "Kelweg wants eyes on it within the hour."

"It'll be the escapement," said a second voice, older, bored. "These old regulators seize when the oil goes waxy. I told them in autumn—"

"They don't pay you to tell them things. Check it."

Boots crossed the floor. Odile held her breath and discovered that her lungs, unlike her hands, had opinions about the evening. Through the gap between case and wall she watched lamplight slide across the brass, inches from her nose. She could see the fine hairs on the back of the older man's hand as he reached into the mechanism.

He touched the escapement. He touched *her* shim.

Odile's entire future balanced on a sliver of soft brass and whether this tired technician wanted to go home.

"Hm." He tilted the lamp. Light crawled over the pallet stones, over the shim, over everything. "Wear on the near pallet. See how it's riding? Old girl's been telling us for months she wanted new stones. Nobody listens to clocks till they die."

"So it's fixable?"

"Tonight? No. Need stones cut to size, and I'm not cutting stones at midnight for a ministry that won't sign my overtime chit. It'll hold where it is or it won't. Either way it keeps till morning." He straightened up, joints cracking like small firecrackers. "Write it up. Escapement failure, natural wear. Nothing sinister."

"Kelweg will want sinister."

"Kelweg can want in one hand and wind in the other, see which fills first."

Their laughter receded down the stairs, taking the light with it, and Odile stayed folded in the dark long after the door boomed shut, counting her own pulse the way she counted beats of a balance wheel. One hundred and twenty. One hundred and eighty. The technician had lied for her without knowing it — or perhaps not lied; perhaps he had simply seen what a lifetime of seeing taught him to see, and what he saw was an old clock dying honestly. Her work was good enough to be mistaken for truth. There was a strange comfort in that, and a strange horror, and she filed both feelings away in the drawer where she kept everything she'd examine later, if later came.

She unfolded herself, joint by joint, and checked the clock. Eleven forty-seven. Holding. Faithful as a dog tied to a post.

"Now," she whispered, "stay put."

* * *

The next hour was the longest of her life, and she had once spent four days waiting to learn whether a shipment of ruby bearings had cleared customs.

She worked by feel mostly, keeping the lamp hooded, making the freeze look even more like decay. She nudged the minute hand's friction clutch loose — a clock whose hands could be moved by hand was a clock nobody trusted, but a frozen clock with *loose* hands was merely decrepit. She let a weight cable slacken a centimeter, so the drive train sagged with authentic-looking exhaustion. She scattered a pinch of dust from the windowsill onto the freshly oiled surfaces, because a saboteur cleans up after herself and a clock simply doesn't care.

Twice, lights moved in the streets below — motorcars, moving fast, which at half past midnight in Vellengard meant only one kind of errand. Once, a knock echoed up the tower's outer door, three heavy blows, then voices arguing on the far side of the lock. She pressed herself against the cold stone and thought about Tomas, fourteen years old and trying so hard to be brave that bravery showed all over him like a rash. She thought about her mother's hands, and the name her mother had given her on that lucid evening, the true name underneath the forged one like a coin under paper. She thought about a page Noor would be typing right now, if he was still free to type it, one name amended among thousands, a needle moved while the whole loom stood still.

*Hold,* she told the clock. *Hold, you magnificent liar.*

At some point — she never learned exactly when — the tower telephone rang. It rang and rang in the little attendant's room below, an ugly insistent jangle that seemed personally offended by the silence everywhere else. Then it stopped. Then it started again. Then boots again, many more boots, and this time the footsteps on the stair had no boredom in them at all.

"Search it," said a voice, and this voice she knew. Not from hearing it often — from having been warned about it. Smooth, unhurried, the voice of Director Kelweg, the man who owned the hours. "Top to bottom. And find me whoever was here."

*Whoever was here.* Odile was halfway up the service ladder to the lantern room before the sentence finished, because there was exactly one place left, and it was the place designed for looking out at everything while being seen by nothing.

* * *

The lantern room was a cage of glass and iron ribs around the great observation telescope, and it was freezing, the wind coming off the harbor straight through every seam. She wedged herself behind the instrument's pier, into the triangle of shadow where the masonry met the floor, and pulled her coat around her knees, and became, with the full discipline of a lifetime's practice, a piece of the furniture.

Below her, through the iron grating of the floor, lamplight bloomed. Men spread through the clock chamber. She heard drawers opened. She heard the technician's report read aloud in a skeptical voice — *"escapement failure, natural wear"* — and heard Kelweg's reply, quiet and terrible in its mildness:

"Natural wear. At the precise hour the registry transmits. How punctual of nature."

A pause. Then, closer, directly beneath her, so that she could hear the fabric of his coat move: "Gentlemen. Someone has been in this tower tonight. Clocks do not loosen their own clutches. Dust does not migrate onto fresh oil. Find me a witness, a footprint, a hair. The woman who maintains this clock — the Voss apprentice — bring her to me. Gently. She is either our saboteur or our best witness, and I confess I no longer much mind which."

Odile closed her eyes.

They had her name. Of course they had her name; she had signed the repair ledger in her own careful hand, because invisibility only works until you must touch something, and touching leaves signatures. But names in ledgers were one thing — a name was a handle, and handles could be false. What Kelweg wanted was the person attached to it, standing in front of him, breathing.

And tomorrow morning — or whatever morning turned out to be, since morning was currently postponed indefinitely — she would have to walk into the observatory with a bag of escapement stones and her face arranged into competent blankness, and stand in front of that mild voice, and be either cleverer than him or finished.

Through the glass, far below, the harbor lay dark. The ship was there — she could just make out its riding lights, three pale points on the black water, the last ship, holding twelve berths and one amended name and her mother and her brother, if the world would only stay frozen long enough. Out on that pier, somewhere, a man with ink-stained fingers was betting his freedom that she could do what she had promised.

The searchers' lamps swung beneath her. A man climbed the ladder to the lantern room, and Odile pressed herself flatter than flat, into the angle where the telescope pier met the stone, and the beam passed over her twice, and the man muttered something about the cold and went back down.

For a long moment afterward she allowed herself exactly one breath, shaky and silent, and one thought, which was not about fear at all but about Noor's face when he argued with her, the way his eyebrows quarreled with each other, and she thought: *I am doing this so that there can be more of that. More arguments. More terrible tea. More of a life in which someone knows my real name and uses it gently.*

Then the tower telephone began ringing again, and this time it did not stop, and below her Kelweg's voice said, softly, to someone whose answer she could not hear:

"Well. Then we shall simply have to ask the clockmaker's apprentice what she knows. Fetch her."

Lamps began to pour out of the observatory doors into the night, spilling down the hill toward the city, toward a small apartment above a clock shop where a mother slept badly and a boy slept the sleep of the lied-to, and Odile knelt in the freezing lantern room with official time dead in her hands, understanding with perfect clarity that the frozen minutes were running out — not on the clock, but on her.
