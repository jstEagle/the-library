## 18. Interrogation by Lamplight

They came for Noor Haddad at half past eleven by his own wristwatch, which was the only clock in Vellengard still behaving itself.

Two men. Neither of them raised his voice, which was somehow worse than shouting. One held the door of the census office; the other stood beside Noor's desk with the patience of a man who had done this many times and found it no more interesting than filing.

"Census taker Haddad," the patient one said. "Director Kelweg would like a word."

"It's rather late for words," Noor said, because his mouth had always operated slightly ahead of his judgment. "I could make an appointment—"

The man did not smile. He simply looked at Noor the way one looks at a form filled out incorrectly: not with anger, but with the mild disappointment of someone who will now have to do extra work.

Noor closed the ledger in front of him, squared its corners out of habit, put on his coat, and went.

* * *

The room they took him to was not in the Ministry proper but behind it — a narrow annex with one window facing a brick wall, a table, three chairs, and a lamp with a green glass shade that gave everything beneath it the color of pond water. A typewriter sat on the table. Noor knew it at once, the way a man knows his own handwriting: a Remington portable, standard carriage, the same model as his own, except that this one's ribbon was new and black as tar.

"Sit," said the patient man, whose name, it turned out, was Vogel. The other one — broader, quieter, standing near the door like furniture that had learned to breathe — was called nothing at all.

Noor sat.

Vogel laid a folder on the table but did not open it. This, Noor understood immediately, was the opening move. The folder existed to be looked at. The folder wanted him to ask about it.

Noor folded his hands and asked about it. "May I see what's in there?"

"Ah." Vogel seemed genuinely pleased. "Direct. Good. So many men sit and sweat for an hour first. It wastes everyone's evening." He opened the folder and turned it around.

Inside were papers Noor knew intimately, because he had typed every line of them himself. Census forms. Household records. And on top, flagged with a red corner-tab, the Voss file — Odile's family, entry by entry, name by name, her dead father's profession listed and then listed again in a second hand, as though someone had checked it twice and liked it less each time.

"You've been thorough," Vogel said. "I'll give you that. Some of our census takers write *tailor* when they mean *seamstress*. You wrote *clockmaker, deceased, papers irregular.* Do you remember writing that?"

"I remember every entry I make," Noor said. It was true, and it sounded like loyalty, which made it useful, which made him feel briefly ill.

"Papers irregular." Vogel tapped the phrase. "That's a strong note for a census taker. That's almost an investigator's note. Almost a *policeman's* note." He leaned back. "Director Kelweg values initiative. He's been watching you, Haddad. A young man who asks one question too many is either a liability or an asset. The Director prefers assets. They're cheaper than liabilities — liabilities require paperwork."

"What kind of asset?"

"The registry finalizes tomorrow morning. Twelve berths, one ship, and after that the borders close and the whole apparatus becomes a historical curiosity." Vogel smiled with great warmth, the way a trap smiles. "The Director wants a deputy to oversee the final transmission. Young. Careful. Ambitious. With a future — a real one. An office with a window that faces something other than a wall."

Outside, faintly, through the brick, came a sound Noor had never heard before and understood instantly: the city's church bells disagreeing with one another. St. Aldric's striking eleven, the harbor chapel striking eleven and seven minutes, the town hall silent. Official time was frozen, and all the little clocks of Vellengard were drifting apart like guests leaving a party.

Vogel glanced toward the window too, and frowned very slightly, and Noor saw that the frown was the most honest thing in the room.

* * *

For the first hour, they talked about work.

This was the technique, Noor realized somewhere around midnight: not brutality, not threats, just *work*, endless patient work, questions about routes and households and ink usage, until a man forgot what he was protecting and answered from habit. Vogel had a way of nodding that made confession feel like competence.

"So the Voss household. Four persons at last count?"

"Three now. The father died in March."

"Cause?"

"Recorded as heart failure."

"*Recorded* as." Vogel's eyebrows rose a fraction. "You have doubts?"

"I record what documents say. Documents are my jurisdiction. Hearts are God's."

Something flickered across Vogel's face — amusement, perhaps, filed away for later. He turned a page. "The daughter. Odile Voss. Apprentice clockmaker."

"She repairs clocks," Noor said. "That's what apprentices do."

"And yet tonight she was at the observatory. Alone. After hours. Repairing a clock that — according to the observatory's own log — was functioning perfectly until she touched it." Vogel let this hang in the green light. "The master clock has stopped, Haddad. Official time is frozen. The registry's final transmission is keyed to official time, and right now the entire Ministry is standing around waiting for a clock that has decided, mysteriously, to take the night off. The night of all nights." He closed the folder softly. "What do you know about that?"

"Nothing."

"You're lying."

"Yes," Noor agreed pleasantly, "but so are you, and I've been doing it longer under worse conditions. My mother interrogated me for nineteen years."

The quiet man by the door shifted his weight — the closest thing to laughter the room would permit.

Vogel studied Noor for a long moment, and then, remarkably, laughed himself, a short dry bark. "Haddad. You are either the most innocent man in this city or the most dangerous, and I genuinely cannot tell, and I want you to know that I find it delightful. It won't save you. But it does make the evening pass."

* * *

The second hour, they stopped being polite.

It happened gradually, the way cold comes into a room. Vogel stopped offering cigarettes. The lamp seemed to burn lower, or perhaps only Noor's eyes grew tired. The folder stayed open on the table now, the red tab showing, and beside it Vogel set down two objects with the care of a man arranging pieces:

A carbon ribbon, worn, its ghost-letters struck through. Noor recognized the wastebasket it had come from. His own office. His own mistakes, pulled out of the bin by someone else's hands.

And a single typed sheet: *ELIGIBILITY AMENDMENT — DRAFT — UNAUTHORIZED KEYSTROKE DETECTED.*

"Somebody has been editing the registry," Vogel said quietly. "Somebody with access, with skill, and with a sentimental attachment to the name Voss." He looked up. "I don't think it was the clockmaker's apprentice. She's clever, but she spells *bureaucracy* wrong. I think it was a man who knows the machine. Who knows the ribbons. Who knows exactly how many keystrokes it takes to move a name from refusal to passage." He slid the amendment draft across the table. "Read your own work, Haddad. Tell me it isn't yours."

Noor read it. It was nearly his work — the spacing, the return habits, the slight lift where his left hand reached for the shift key. Someone had reconstructed his style from the discarded ribbons, or Vogel had simply guessed well. Either way, the document was a noose tailored to his shoulders.

"If I sign a statement acknowledging this," Noor said slowly, "what happens to the Voss file?"

"Nothing. The file proceeds normally. Berth assignments stand as amended — the Director honors completed entries; it's a point of professional pride." Vogel produced a pen and a prepared sheet. "Confess, and the family sails. Refuse, and we reopen the file, void the amendment as fraudulent, and begin asking the apprentice questions in a room with fewer chairs. She's small. Cold weather coming. I'd hate to be the reason a girl caught pneumonia."

There it was. Not the threat he'd expected — not the cell, not the deportation lists — but the simple arithmetic of one life weighed against others, laid out on green paper under a green lamp.

Noor thought of Odile kneeling in the lantern room at this exact moment, keeping official time dead with her bare hands, buying minutes with her whole future. He thought of her saying, in the tea shop with its two cups and its careful truths: *If they ever catch us, let them catch me lying badly and you lying well.* He thought: she is lying magnificently right now, alone, and I am sitting in a warm room being offered a deal, and if I take it she hangs alone.

Duty, he reflected, was a wonderful word. It meant whatever the man with the folder needed it to mean.

"I didn't amend anything," Noor said.

"You will sign anyway," Vogel said, not unkindly. "Everyone signs eventually. The only variable is what it costs before you do."

"No," said Noor. "But I'll type something."

Vogel blinked. "Type?"

"You keep a typewriter on the table. I'm a census taker. Words are my trade — I'd like mine to be legible. If I must confess to something, I prefer to do it properly. In triplicate spirit, if not in fact."

He could see Vogel deciding — suspicion against efficiency, paranoia against the sheer harmless vanity of a clerk who wanted his own handwriting on his own ruin. Efficiency won. It usually does. Vogel rolled a clean sheet into the Remington and stepped back.

Noor placed his fingers on the keys, and for a moment the room was utterly still, and he thought of the first page he had ever typed in this city — a routine household survey, a woman named Voss answering a question too many — and of everything that had grown from that seed, the way a whole tree hides inside a grain.

Then he typed:

*I, Noor Haddad, census taker, confess to unauthorized interference with the emigration registry of the port of Vellengard. I acted alone. I used the office machines after hours. I accept full responsibility for any amendment found in the records, and I state plainly that no other person — no clockmaker, no apprentice, no member of the Voss family — had knowledge of or participation in these acts.*

He paused. The ribbon ticked softly. Outside, the bells were still quarreling — St. Aldric's said midnight, the harbor said twenty past, the town hall said nothing at all. Frozen time. Her frozen time. Every minute the clocks refused to agree was a minute the transmission couldn't run, a minute Odile was stealing back for Tomas, for her mother, for the truth of a forged name. He had promised her an hour if it came to this. He intended to overdeliver.

He added one more line:

*I further confess that I did it because the registry was never statistics. It was people. And someone had to answer for treating them as figures, and since the guilty are busy, the conscientious may as well stand in.*

Vogel read it twice. On the second reading, his jaw tightened at the final sentence — a public accusation dressed up as a confession, unprovable, deniable, and now permanently part of the record.

"You understand," Vogel said, "that this changes nothing. We can void your confession's legal effect with a memo."

"Probably," Noor agreed. "But memos get lost, and confessions get read. Ask anyone in archives." He stood, knees stiff, and pushed the typed page across. "Now. Am I under arrest, or am I a Deputy with a window?"

Vogel looked at him for a long moment with something that was almost, almost respect. Then he nodded to the quiet man, who opened the door — onto a corridor, not a cell.

"You're detained," Vogel said. "In the records annex. There's a cot. Someone will come for you when the clock strikes." He gathered the folder, then paused at the door. "For what it's worth, Haddad — the Director will fall someday. Men who tamper with clocks always do. When that day comes, I suspect history will read your confession differently than I do tonight."

"History," Noor said, "types slower than it should. But it gets the names right eventually."

The door shut. The lock turned — a soft, bureaucratic click, the most honest sound in the building.

* * *

The records annex was a long room of shelved ledgers and one high window, through which Noor could see, far off across the rooftops, the observatory hill — and the tower, dark except for one lantern burning in the cupola like a held breath. Somewhere up there, a girl he loved was fighting a clock, and winning, minute by stolen minute.

He sat on the cot. He had no watch to check — they'd taken it, naturally — but he didn't need one. He counted the way census takers count: steadily, without flinching, one name at a time.

One minute. Two. Five.

Down the corridor, boots passed and returned, passed and returned. A telephone rang somewhere and was not answered quickly. The bells of Vellengard went on disagreeing — quarter past, half past, twenty-five past all at once — the whole city's time shattered into generous, contradictory pieces, each piece a gift.

Ten minutes. Fifteen.

Noor lay back on the cot, folded his hands on his chest, and smiled at the ceiling like a man who has finally, at long last, filed the correct form.

Because far below, muffled through brick and distance, he heard the thing he had been counting toward: the harbor horn. One long blast. Then a second.

The ship was boarding early — earlier than any schedule keyed to official time could have predicted.

Which meant the amendment had gone through, which meant Tomas and his mother were walking up the gangway at this very moment — and which meant that Odile Voss, invisible for twenty-three years, was about to discover whether there was any berth at all left in this world for herself.

Noor sat up in the dark of the records annex, locked in a room built entirely of names, and began, methodically, to look for a way out.
