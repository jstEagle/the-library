## 19. The Last Berth

The frozen minute lasted eleven of them.

Odile knew this the way she knew everything worth knowing—by counting. She had built the hold into the master clock herself, three nights of work disguised as maintenance, a brass escapement modified with a sliver of her father's old pocket-watch spring so that the great dial above Vellengard would stand at 11:47 while the true minutes walked on without it. Eleven minutes of official time that did not exist. Eleven minutes in which the registry's transmission, keyed to the observatory's chime, would wait for a bell that had forgotten how to ring.

She had given the city a lie, and the lie was for Tomas.

*Eleven minutes*, she thought, pedaling the borrowed bicycle down the observatory hill with her skirt pinned between her knees and her heart somewhere around her ears. *Eleven minutes is nothing. Eleven minutes is a lifetime.*

The harbor road was empty in a way that felt rehearsed, as though the city itself had been told to look away. Lamps burned in the fish market though no one sold fish at midnight. A dog trotted beside her for half a block, decided she was not interesting, and trotted off—she envied it. Somewhere behind her, up in the tower, Kelweg's men were beginning to ask why the clock had stopped, and somewhere ahead of her, at Pier Three, a ship called the *Aldanmark* was taking on the twelve families whose names the registry had blessed.

Thirteen names now. Noor's amendment had slipped through at 11:46 and one second, in the last breath of honest time, and if it had worked—if the ink had dried, if the clerk had been sleepy, if God or whatever governed gears had been paying attention—then the Voss family would board as a household of three.

She had not let herself believe it until she saw the gangplank.

* * *

Her mother stood at the foot of it in the good coat, the one with the mended cuff that Odile had sewn so many times the cuff was more thread than wool. She was leaning on Tomas's arm, and Tomas was doing the thing he had done since he was small enough to hide under the workbench: standing very straight, chin up, as if straightness were a kind of armor that had ever once worked.

"You're late," her mother said.

"I'm exactly on time," Odile said. "Officially. Ask the clock."

Her mother's laugh was thin and turned to a cough halfway through, and Odile's chest did the thing it had been doing for a week, the squeezing, the arithmetic of it—how many good breaths left, how far to the gangplank, how many stairs. Twelve berths. Three Vosses. One of them, she had already decided, in the dark and the quiet and the eleven stolen minutes, would not be boarding.

She had not told them. She had not let them choose, because she knew what they would choose, and she could not carry that. Some decisions you make the way you set a mainspring: alone, in the dark, with steady hands, and you don't explain the tension to the spring. You just wind it.

"The clerk said—" Tomas began.

"The clerk said berth eleven, cabin four, and that we're to board immediately." Odile took her mother's other arm. "So we board immediately. Argue with me later. There's a whole ocean for it."

Her mother squeezed her hand, and in that squeeze Odile felt the whole conversation they weren't having—the one that had started the night her mother typed *Tomas* on Noor's machine, the one that had continued in looks across the kitchen table ever since, the one that said *I know what you're doing, Odile Voss, and I am too tired and too sick to fight you, but I will remember it, and I will hold it against you lovingly for the rest of my life, however long the sea allows.*

"Your father," her mother said quietly, "would have done exactly this. And I would have been exactly this furious."

"I know," Odile said. "It's why I didn't ask permission. He never did either."

They went up the gangplank together, the three of them, and for eleven steps Odile let herself be only a daughter walking her family to safety, and it was the best eleven steps of her life.

* * *

The purser was a narrow man with a narrow mustache and a ledger, and he read their names aloud the way a man reads a grocery list. "Voss. Voss. Voss." He looked up. "That's three. Berth eleven holds two."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. Somewhere behind Odile, up the hill, she imagined she could hear the observatory clock still holding its breath at 11:47, and she thought, with the strange clarity of panic, *I gave everything for eleven minutes and they gave me two berths.*

"No," she said. "The amendment—there was an amendment, the registry—"

"The registry," the purser said, with the serene cruelty of a man quoting scripture, "lists two. Cabin four, two berths." He turned the ledger toward her as if she might enjoy the typography.

And there it was. *Voss, A. Voss, T.* Two names in Noor's careful typewritten hand, clean as a confession. One name missing.

He hadn't made a mistake. She knew it the way she knew a broken tooth from a worn one—by feel, by the shape of the absence. He had amended for exactly one name without detection; he'd told her so, that night over tea with no lies, tapping the registry page with his pencil. *One. The system flags anything more. One looks like a clerical correction. Two looks like a conspiracy.* And she had argued with him until dawn about mother or brother, and her mother had settled it with a typed word, and somewhere in all that arguing she had understood—had known and refused to know—that the arithmetic only ever came out to two.

She had known. And she had let it stand, because the alternative was to stand there and argue her mother off a ship, and she could not. She would not. She had made the choice the way she made all her choices: quietly, competently, invisibly, so that no one could see it happen and no one could stop it.

"Odile." Tomas had gone white. "Odile, no. We're not—we're not doing this, we'll both get off, we'll wait for another ship—"

"There is no other ship," Odile said. "You know that. Everyone in Vellengard knows that. That's rather the whole tragedy of Vellengard."

"Then I'm not going."

"Yes, you are." She took his face in her hands, both hands, the way she had when he was six and had broken her best escapement and stood waiting to be yelled at. "Tomas. Look at me. You're going to get on that ship, and you're going to take care of Mama, and you're going to be so insufferably good at clocks that they'll have to give you a workshop of your own, and in ten years you're going to write me a letter so full of complaints that I'll need two afternoons to read it. That's the job. That's the whole job. Everything else is details."

"You can't—"

"I can," she said, "because I'm the eldest, and because someone has to stay and finish the clock, and because—" her voice did something traitorous and she pressed on through it— "because if you don't get on that ship I will personally dismantle every watch you own, and I know where you hide them, Tomas, I've always known."

He laughed, horribly, wetly, the way people laugh when crying is the only other option, and their mother put her arms around both of them, and for a moment the three Vosses were a single knot on the deck of the *Aldanmark* while the purser looked at his watch and the harbor looked at the sky and the night leaned in.

"Boarding," the purser said gently. "Miss. We're boarding."

* * *

Her mother held her longest. She smelled of camphor and the lavender water she'd worn as long as Odile could remember, and when she spoke it was into Odile's hair, so quietly that only the two of them existed.

"You will follow us. You hear me? You are too stubborn to die in a place like this and too clever to be caught. You will follow us, and when you do, you will find me, and I will spend the rest of my life being angry at you in the warmest possible way."

"That sounds like a threat."

"It's a promise. We Vosses keep our promises. It's the one thing we've never had to forge." She pulled back, and her eyes were bright and terrible and alive, more alive than they had been in months, and Odile thought: *good. Good, be alive, be furious, live to be furious at me for sixty years.* "And Odile. The boy who typed the names."

"What about him?"

Her mother smiled, small and knowing and unbearably fond. "Tell him his ribbon is worn. A man should change his ribbon before it fades. Everything he writes deserves to be read clearly." She kissed Odile's forehead, once, hard, like a stamp on a document. "Go. Before I do something foolish like stay with you."

Then they were going up the gangplank, Tomas looking back every third step, her mother not looking back at all because she was too proud or too wise, and Odile stood on the pier with her hands empty and the cold coming off the water in sheets, and she did not cry, because she had decided that too, sometime in the eleven minutes, the way she decided everything—alone, in the dark, with steady hands.

* * *

The horn sounded at what the city believed was 11:52 but what her bones knew was past midnight. The *Aldanmark* shrugged its great gray shoulder away from the pier, and the gap of black water opened between the ship and the world, a gap no wider than a workbench and no narrower than the rest of Odile's life.

Tomas was at the rail, shouting her name, shouting something that might have been *I'll write* or might have been *I hate you* or might have been both, which was, she reflected, the most accurate thing a brother had ever shouted. Her mother stood beside him with her hand raised, not waving—holding still, the way you hold a breath, the way you hold a promise.

And then, because the night had not finished spending her, Odile heard footsteps on the pier behind her, fast and uneven, the footsteps of a man who had run from somewhere he should not have been.

She turned.

Noor Haddad came around the fish sheds at a dead sprint, coat flying, one shoe skidding on the wet cobbles, and he pulled up so hard at the rail that he nearly went over it, and for one lunatic second Odile thought: *he's going to swim for it, he's actually going to—*

But the gap was too wide, and they both knew it, and the ship was already a ship and no longer a possibility.

"Two berths," he said, breathless, and it wasn't an apology, it was a report, because he was Noor and he reported things, even now, even to her, especially to her. "One was all I could—Odile, one was all the system would take, I tried, I sat there for an hour trying to make two look like a clerical correction, and two never looks like—"

"I know." She put her hand over his where it gripped the rail. "I know exactly what two looks like. It looks like a conspiracy. And we couldn't afford one."

"You should be on that ship."

"Yes," she agreed. "I should be on that ship. I should also have been born with better timing, and the master clock should have kept honest time, and Vellengard should have been a city that didn't need saving. We are all of us behind schedule tonight."

The horn sounded again, closer now, angrier, and the gap widened, and Noor turned to face her fully, and behind him, up the pier road, she could see the swinging lanterns of men coming—the harbor police, or Kelweg's men, or both, it hardly mattered, they were coming for the registry clerk who had vanished from his desk in the frozen minutes.

"They'll be here in two minutes," he said.

"Then don't waste them explaining things I already know."

He laughed—a short, cracked, real laugh—and then he did the thing she would carry across an ocean and through every year that followed: he pulled a folded page from inside his coat, typed on both sides, worn at the creases from being read too many times, and pressed it into her hand through the railing, folding her fingers over it with both of his.

"Coordinates," he said. "A name. A man in the south who owes me a favor and owes the registry a great deal more. And a promise, at the bottom, which you are to read last, because I wrote it last and I'm a sentimental fool with a typewriter and no self-control."

"Noor—"

"Read it on the water," he said. "Not here. Here it's just ink. Out there—" he glanced at the ship, at the black gap, at her, and his voice went quiet and steady as a well-set escapement— "out there it's a heading."

The lanterns were closer. The ship was farther. Her mother's hand was still raised at the rail, a fixed point in all the moving dark, and Odile stood between the two of them—the family going and the man staying, the past and the heading—and she realized, with the strange calm of a clockmaker watching a mechanism reach the last tooth before the strike, that the night was about to do the one thing she had not planned for.

The lantern light touched the pier. Noor's hand tightened once around hers, and let go.

"Odile Voss," he said, "you are the loudest invisible woman I have ever met. Go be seen somewhere safer."

And he turned, deliberately, gloriously, toward the lanterns—arms half raised, the picture of a man surrendering to a pursuit he had run toward—while behind him the *Aldanmark* blew its horn a third time, long and low and final, and the gap of black water widened, and the folded page burned in Odile's hand like a coal she had been told not to look at until she was at sea.

She looked at it anyway.

She was, after all, her mother's daughter.
