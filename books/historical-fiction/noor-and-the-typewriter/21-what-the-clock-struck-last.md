## 21. What the Clock Struck Last

The sea did not care about time.

That was the first thing Odile Voss understood, three days out of Vellengard, standing at the rail with salt drying white on her sleeves. The sea kept its own hours—tides, swells, the slow rotation of light—and none of them could be stamped, certified, or transmitted to a ministry. A person could stand on a deck and be late for absolutely nothing.

She found the thought so liberating that she laughed, once, alone, and a stewardess gave her the look one gives a woman laughing at nothing in the third-class washroom, which is to say: concern, and a small professional distance.

The folded page had not left her since the pier.

She had read it a dozen times in the first hours—pressed flat against her knee in the crowded cabin while Tomas slept with his mouth open like a landed fish, while her mother dozed propped against the bulkhead, her breathing thin but steady, each breath a small victory no registry had authorized. The page was typewritten, of course. Even his promise came out of that machine. She could see the letters in her mind without looking: the slight lift on the *h*, the way the *e* sat a hair too low, as if leaning toward whatever came next.

*Coordinates. A name. A promise.*

The coordinates were for a city she had never seen, in a country whose language she spoke badly and hopefully. The name was a clockmaker—a woman, no less, a Frau Ostrowska, who owed Noor Haddad a debt of some kind and would find Odile work, or a room, or at the very least a chair to sit in while the world finished deciding what it intended to do with her. And the promise was the last line, typed alone at the bottom of the page with a full stop after it, the way Noor ended things he meant:

*I will find you. Keep the watch wound.*

She had not kept the watch wound. That was the trouble.

* * *

Her father's pocket watch had crossed the border in her bodice, flat against her sternum like a second heartbeat, and she had not let it run since Vellengard. A stopped watch was safe. A stopped watch agreed with whatever time you needed it to be. For eleven years—since the night her mother had sat her down at the kitchen table and told her, in a voice sanded smooth by fear, that their name was not entirely their name—Odile had understood that a running watch was a confession. It agreed with the observatory. It agreed with the stamps and the ledgers and the master clock in its tower, and the master clock, as it turned out, had been lying to everyone.

So the watch stayed stopped at 4:17, the hour her father had last set it, and Odile carried it the way one carries an urn: carefully, privately, and without opening it.

Now, on the fourth morning, with the sea gone from slate to a blue so clean it looked rinsed, she took it out on deck and held it in her palm.

Tomas found her there. He had a talent for finding her when she was about to do something she'd rather do alone, a talent she suspected was simply love wearing work clothes.

"You're making that face," he said.

"What face?"

"The one where you've decided something and you're waiting for someone to talk you out of it so you don't have to be brave."

"Fourteen years old," Odile said, "and already a census taker."

He leaned on the rail beside her. He had lost weight she couldn't afford for him to lose, and there was a bruise-yellow shadow under his eyes from the crossing, but he was watching the water with an expression she recognized from their father: the look of a boy measuring a thing to see if it could be built.

"Mother ate half a roll," he reported. "And complained about the coffee. Complaining is good. Complaining means she thinks there's a future worth complaining in."

"That's very wise."

"I stole the other half of the roll. That's less wise, but I'm sharing it with you, so it evens out."

He broke the roll and handed her half, and they ate in the companionable silence of people who had recently learned that bread is a subject, not an object. Gulls trailed the ship, screaming their opinions. Somewhere below, a child was being told, for the fourth time, to stop running on the stairs.

"Tomas," Odile said. "About the berth—"

"Don't." He said it lightly, but his jaw had gone set. "You'll say something noble and I'll say something noble back and then we'll both be insufferable for the rest of the voyage. Mother already did it. She said she'd typed her decision and that was that, and when I asked what gave her the right she said, *the same thing that gave you the right to offer yours—love, badly administered.*" He shrugged. "I'm choosing to be fourteen about it. Ask me again when I'm thirty and I'll probably cry."

Odile looked at him—her brother, her whole brother, the one name Noor had amended into a life—and found that the grief and the gratitude had fused somewhere in the middle of the sea into a single substance she had no word for. It sat in her chest like ballast.

"I'm going to fix the watch," she said.

Tomas looked at the pocket watch in her hand. He was too young to remember their father winding it, but he knew its history the way children of liars know all the family's true things: by osmosis, by the shape of the silences around it.

"It's been stopped since before I was born," he said.

"Since 4:17 on a Tuesday in November." She turned it over. The case was warm from her hand. "Papa set it by the observatory clock. He trusted it. He trusted the whole city to tell the truth, and then—" She stopped. The rest was a list: the papers, the new name, the years of standing still, the tower, Kelweg, the frozen minutes she had made with her own two hands while men with lanterns searched the galleries below her.

"And then the city lied," Tomas finished for her, because he had learned the family trade too.

* * *

She waited until evening to open the case.

Her mother was sleeping, or pretending to, which at this point amounted to the same courtesy. Tomas had gone below to argue with a boy from Riga about whether fish could sleep, a dispute Odile judged would be settled by neither philosophy nor evidence. The deck emptied as the light went long and gold, and Odile sat on a coil of rope near the stern with the watch in her lap and the folded page in her pocket, and allowed herself, at last, to read it slowly.

She had been reading it like a fugitive—snatching lines, checking over her shoulder. Now she read it like a clockmaker, which is to say: she read what the thing was made of.

The coordinates were precise. The name was underlined twice. But it was the middle of the page she had skimmed before, the middle she read properly now, and her breath went out of her in a slow, disbelieving stream.

*They caught him,* Noor had typed. *Kelweg. Not for the clock—for the arithmetic. The frozen time made the stamps impossible, and impossible stamps make auditors curious, and auditors found that for eleven months every invalidated document traced back to one office. He is telling them it was a clerical error. They do not believe in his clerical errors. The registry is being rebuilt from the original census ledgers. My ledgers. They have asked me to stay and help rebuild them.*

She read that last sentence four times.

They had asked him to stay. They had not asked him; they had required him, which was the state's way of asking. And he had agreed—of course he had agreed, because a man who fled would be followed, and a man who stayed could bend the new registry from the inside, could feed it mercy the way she had once fed a clock a lie.

*Keep the watch wound,* the page said. And beneath it, smaller, almost an afterthought, the way he said true things: *You were right about the gears. It was never the machine. It was always the hands.*

Odile pressed the page flat against her knee and looked out at the water, and found she was crying without any of the usual ceremony—no sobbing, no drama, just salt running out of her the way it runs out of everything at sea. Grief and release in the same breath, arriving together like twins who refuse to be separated: grief for the city, for the shop, for the girl who had learned to be invisible so well she'd nearly vanished; release for the tower, for the frozen minutes, for the fact that her sabotage had not been a crime at all but a witness. The clock she had stopped had stopped him. The lie she had fed the machine had told the truth.

"You beautiful, stubborn, impossible man," she said to the page. "You typed me a future with a full stop at the end like it was a certainty."

The full stop, she decided, was the most Noor thing about the whole document. He ended his sentences the way he did everything else: deliberately, and meaning it.

* * *

She opened the watch on the fifth morning, in full daylight, because she was done with secrets done in the dark.

The case came open under her thumbnail with the small satisfied click of good workmanship, and there was the movement—her father's movement, the train of gears he had oiled with such tenderness that eleven years of stillness had not dried them to ruin. The mainspring held its tension like a held breath. She had carried a held breath across a border and through a registry and out onto a cold gray sea, and it was time, she understood, to let it go.

But first—because she was her father's daughter, and because some rituals are load-bearing—she cleaned it. She had a loupe in her pocket, smuggled out of Vellengard along with three small screwdrivers and a tin of oil that the customs officer had waved through with the bored generosity of a man who could not imagine a clockmaker being dangerous. She worked on her knee on the deck, the watch cradled in a handkerchief, and Tomas sat beside her pretending to read but actually watching every movement of her hands with the naked envy of a boy who wanted, more than anything in the world, to be handed the tiny screwdriver.

"Balance looks true," she murmured. "Escapement's clean. Papa, you over-oiled the fourth wheel, you always over-oiled the fourth wheel, and I am going to fix it and you are going to haunt me about it forever."

"Talking to the watch," Tomas said.

"Talking to the watchmaker."

"Same thing in this family."

She let the fourth wheel have its way—no, that was the old thinking, the stopped-watch thinking. She cleaned it properly. She set the pallets. She held the crown between finger and thumb and thought about what she was about to do, which was this: she was going to let the watch agree with the sea. Not with the observatory, not with the ministry, not with any tower in any city that claimed to own the hours. With the sun, and the ship's bell, and her own two hands.

She wound it. The spring took the tension with a sound like a small animal settling, and the balance wheel woke, and the escapement began its patient ticking—tick, tick, tick—the sound her father's vest pocket had made against her ear when she was small enough to be carried, the first clock she had ever loved.

The hands stood at 4:17. She moved them forward.

She did not set them by guesswork. The ship's officer on the bridge, a patient Finn with a beard like weathered rope, let her look at his chronometer when she explained—honestly, for the first time in eleven years, out loud, to a stranger—that she was a clockmaker setting her late father's watch. He looked at her hands, which were nicked and stained and entirely unhidden, and he said, "Then set it right, miss," as though the whole world were not full of people being told the opposite.

The watch read 9:42. The chronometer read 9:42. The sea ran gold and green and utterly indifferent, and the watch ticked, and Odile Voss sat on a coil of rope and let it.

"Does it feel different?" Tomas asked.

She listened. The ticking was the same ticking it had always been; gears did not know they had been forgiven. But something in her chest had come loose and was running too, some small train of wheels that had been stopped at 4:17 on a Tuesday in November for eleven years, and it was keeping excellent time.

"Yes," she said. "It feels like it's going somewhere."

* * *

On the seventh day, land.

It came up out of the morning mist the way land does in stories—first a gray suggestion, then a line, then hills, then roofs, then a harbor craned with industry and smelling, even at a mile out, of coffee and coal and other people's bread. Passengers crowded the rail. Her mother stood among them, upright by sheer administrative will, and when the bells of the harbor church came across the water—out of time with the ship's bell, out of time with everything, gloriously unsynchronized—her mother laughed. Actually laughed, a rusty sound like a clock that hadn't been wound in years.

"Listen to it," she said. "Nobody's in charge."

Odile held her father's watch up to the light and checked it against the harbor bells, out of professional habit. Two minutes slow, the church. Or the watch was two minutes fast. There was no observatory to say. There was no registry to rule on it. There was just the water, and the bells, and the two of them disagreeing peaceably in the morning air, and Odile found she could not have said which one was right, and found, further, that she did not care, and found, finally, that this was the whole of her inheritance: not the name, not the papers, not the forged or the true, but this—the knowledge that time belonged to whoever was brave enough to hold it.

She pocketed the watch. It ticked against her hip like a small loyal animal.

Then she took out the folded page, unfolded it for what she told herself would be the last time before she memorized it and burned it, and read the coordinates again. Frau Ostrowska. A street with a name she could not yet pronounce. A city with a harbor and, presumably, clocks, because every city had clocks, and every clock had hands, and hands could be taught.

The gangway went down. The passengers began to move, a slow river of bundles and children and held breath. Her mother took her arm. Tomas took her bag, and inside the bag, wrapped in a stocking, were three screwdrivers, a loupe, and a tin of oil, which was, when you thought about it, the entire Voss estate.

"Odile." Her mother nodded at the watch pocket. "What time is it?"

Odile smiled, and gave the only answer she had crossed a sea to be able to give.

"It's time," she said.

And somewhere behind them, in a city she would not see again for two years, a man was sitting down at a desk with a typewriter, and beginning, very carefully, to type.
