## 22. Noor and the Typewriter

### Odile

The shop in Marrow Street was eleven feet wide, which Odile had decided was exactly the correct width for a business that traded in precision. Anything wider, she reasoned, invited clutter. Anything narrower would not accommodate her workbench, her stool, the wall of clocks she had hung at deliberately different times to annoy customers into conversation, and — on busy afternoons — the small crowd of neighborhood children who came to watch her open machines like a surgeon and occasionally, when their mothers weren't looking, like a magician.

"Madame Odile," said the baker's boy, appearing in the doorway with flour on his chin, "the clock in your window says it's a quarter past four, but the one above your door says ten to."

"That's because they're having an argument," Odile said. "The one over the door is right. The other one is stubborn. Come back Thursday."

"You could just fix it."

"I could just fix everything," Odile said, "and then what would we talk about?"

He went off satisfied, as children did, with answers that were not answers, and Odile returned to the wristwatch clamped in her vise — a lady's watch, gold-plated, its balance staff snapped by a woman who had, by her own cheerful admission, dropped it down a drain and fished it out with a butter knife. The work was fine enough to make the world go quiet. That was the thing about small gears: they demanded so much of you that there was nothing left over for dread.

Two years, and she had learned to live inside the quiet. She had a room above the shop with a window facing east, so the light woke her before the alarm — though she owned no alarm, because a clockmaker waking to an alarm struck her as an adultery of profession. She had regulars. She had a kettle. She had, folded into the lining of her coat where no customs officer would ever think to look again, a typed page gone soft as cloth from handling.

She did not read it anymore. She had memorized it the way she had once memorized gear trains: forty-eight hours north by coastal steamer, ask for the man who repairs machines and keeps his own hours. A name that was half a name. And beneath it, in the typewriter's slightly wounded *h*, the sentence she had carried across two borders and one sea:

*I am slow, but I have never once stopped.*

She had written back, of course. Letters were dangerous in the first year — names traveling through postal systems like sparks through dry grass — and then, when the letters became safe, they became somehow harder. What do you write to a man who gave you his freedom by surrendering his? *I am well* felt like an insult. *I miss you* felt like a debt he'd never asked her to owe. So she wrote him small things: the price of oil of cloves, the antics of the baker's boy, the fact that she had finally repaired the church tower clock and the priest had paid her in candles, which she had resold at a scandalous markup. He wrote her back longer things, patient things, letters that read like a man leaving bread crumbs along a road he intended, someday, to walk.

The bell over her door rang.

"Closed at six," she said, without looking up. "It's ten past. You'll have to want something badly."

There was a pause — the particular pause of a person recalculating — and then a voice she had last heard through a ship's railing, torn thin by wind and engine noise, saying words she had carried up a gangway in her chest like swallowed glass.

"I've wanted something badly for two years," Noor Haddad said. "I'm told this is the only shop in Marrow Street that handles difficult cases."

Odile set down her loupe very carefully, because her hands had suddenly become someone else's hands, and turned around.

He stood in her doorway holding a portable typewriter against his chest the way a man holds a child, or a shield, or both. He was thinner. His hair had gone grey at the temples in a way that looked less like age than like weather. There was a scar she didn't recognize running along his jaw, and a coat that had been mended by someone who loved economy more than beauty, and he was looking at her with the expression of a man who has rehearsed a speech across an ocean and found, upon arrival, that every word of it has fallen out of his pockets.

"The case sticks on the fourth key," he said. "The ribbon's worn through. And the carriage return needs—" He stopped. Started again. "I was told you repair anything."

"Anything," Odile said. Her voice held. It was the single greatest professional achievement of her life. "Depending on the difficulty."

"It's been a difficult machine," he said. "It remembers things it should have forgotten. It's been asked to do terrible paperwork and one or two heroic acts of forgery, and I believe it's developed opinions."

"And the owner?"

"He's worse." Noor stepped inside. The bell settled behind him. "He's spent two years learning to wait, which he was never good at, and he's come a long way to bother a clockmaker about a stuck key."

"Marrow Street is a long way."

"Forty-eight hours north by coastal steamer," he said. "Ask for the man who repairs machines and keeps his own hours. It took me three tries to find the street. The first two cities had streets called Marrow too. Someone in this part of the world has a grim imagination."

Odile laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, the kind that made her cover her mouth as if she'd sworn in church — and the sound seemed to break whatever spell of carefulness had kept them standing three feet apart like strangers negotiating a trade. He crossed the eleven feet of the shop in four steps and then stopped short, hands hovering, suddenly formal, asking permission with his whole body.

She solved the question the practical way. She reached out and touched the scar on his jaw.

"Lamplight interrogation," he said quietly. "They were gentler than Kelweg would have been. They had time to be. By the time they understood what my confession meant, the registry office was already burning its own files trying to find the fraud, and Kelweg was already explaining to his superiors why official time had frozen for ninety minutes on the night of departure." He turned his face into her palm briefly, like a man warming himself. "You know how it ended for him?"

"The papers said. A clerical investigation. A quiet resignation. Then the quieter kind of ending." Odile lowered her hand. "You sent me the newspaper clippings."

"I wanted you to be sure. I wanted *me* to be sure." He looked around the shop then, really looked — the clocks disagreeing on the walls, the tin of oil, the three screwdrivers laid out in parade formation — and something in his face loosened. "So this is what you built instead of a country."

"It's eleven feet wide."

"So I've heard." He set the typewriter down on her bench with the tenderness of a man laying down a load he had carried too far. "It got me through the camps, the border offices, two years of writing letters to a woman who answered in candle prices. I thought it deserved a professional."

Odile opened the case. The machine sat inside, dented at one corner, its keys polished pale by fingers, its ribbon spool wound down to ghosts. She tilted it toward the lamp and saw, embossed faintly in the grime of the carriage, the impression of a thousand pages — and among them, if you knew how to look, the shape of a name typed over and over until the metal itself remembered it.

"Fourth key," she said. "Which one?"

"The *h*."

"Naturally." She pressed it. The typebar hesitated, caught, rose crooked. "Someone's bent this."

"Someone typed a great many angry sentences in 1940," Noor said. "Some of them with his fist."

She worked in silence for a moment, pliers and fingertip and breath, and he watched her the way he had once watched her at the observatory bench — as though competent hands were the most interesting thing in the world. Outside, the evening came down over Marrow Street, lamplighters making their rounds, the bakery exhaling its last warm air.

"Tomas sends his regards," Odile said, to have something ordinary to say. "He's apprenticed to a shipwright. He counts everything now — days, rivets, money. He's saving for a berth of his own someday, but a voluntary one, on a ship going somewhere on purpose."

"And your mother?"

"Lucid most days now. She comes down Thursdays and reorganizes my screws and tells the customers I undercharge." Odile fitted the typebar back into true. "She asked me, before I left today, whether I'd be home for dinner. I told her I had a difficult repair."

Noor was quiet. When she glanced up, he was looking at her with an expression she recognized from the tea, the two cups, the night of confessions in fragments — the expression of a man deciding to stop fragmenting.

"There's a hotel on the corner," he began carefully. "If the repair takes several days—"

"The repair will take twenty minutes," Odile said. "But the shop closes at six, and I know a place that serves fish soup and doesn't mind people who sit too long. My mother expects me at eight. She'll expect me late tonight, actually. She's been expecting me late for two years; she'll be relieved to finally be right."

### Noor

Here is what Noor Haddad had learned about waiting, in the school of two hard years: it was not stillness. It was motion performed in place, like a swimmer treading water, and the secret was to keep moving — one form, one letter, one honest line of type at a time — so that the water never closed over you.

He had typed his way through it. Depositions, testimony, the long gray paragraphs of the inquiry into the Ministry of Hours. Then, when the inquiry ended and the men who had owned time were scattered or silenced, he had typed other things: letters to the families on the ribbon he had kept, telling each one the truth of what had been done to their name, and receiving back replies that ranged from gratitude to grief to, in one memorable case, a furious invitation to visit and fix the sender's roof. He had fixed the roof. It had taken three days and taught him humility regarding trades other than his own.

And always, underneath the official paper and the penitent paper, the machine had run off its secret pages — drafts of letters to Odile that he never sent because they were too naked, revisions of letters he did send because they were not naked enough. The typewriter knew all of it. That was the thing about the machine: it remembered everything, in ribbon and impression and the small permanent deformations of use, and you could lie with it but never to it.

Now he sat at the little table in the back of the fish-soup house while Odile argued with the proprietor about whether the soup contained enough saffron to justify its price, and he understood that the waiting was over, and found himself, absurdly, nervous. He had faced interrogation by lamplight with steadier hands than this.

She came back carrying two bowls and sat down across from him and looked at him over the steam, and whatever she saw in his face made her smile — the wry, sideways smile he had crossed a sea to see, better than memory, memory being a poor instrument compared to the original.

"Say it," she said. "You've been composing it since the doorway. I can hear the carriage returning."

Noor laughed, and the nervousness went out of him like air from a valve.

"All right." He wiped his hands on the napkin as if preparing for official work, which in a sense he was. "Two years ago I sat at a desk in Vellengard and typed a promise onto a page and folded it twice, because I didn't know if I'd live to unfold it, and I didn't know if you'd want to. The promise was: *when it's safe, I'll come find you.* It wasn't safe for a long time. Then it was safe, and I discovered I was afraid — properly afraid, the way I'd never been of Kelweg or his lamplighters — because finding you meant asking you whether the promise still had a reader." He turned his cup a quarter turn on its saucer. "So I'm asking. Not the promise. The reader."

Odile set down her spoon.

"Noor Haddad," she said. "Census taker. Keeper of inconvenient records." She reached across the table and took his hand, turning it palm-up, examining it like a mechanism — the ink stains, the callus where the pen rested, a new scar across the base of the thumb. "I have spent two years repairing machines for people who bring them in broken and hope. Every one of them asks the same question in the same order: can you fix it, how long will it take, and what will it cost. And every time, I answer the third question honestly, because I learned from a man with a typewriter that the honest answer is the only one worth the paper." She closed his hand around hers. "The cost is that I don't intend to be careful anymore. I spent nine years being invisible and one hour being the loudest thing in Vellengard, and I liked the second one better. If you're staying, you're staying loudly. There's a room upstairs with an eastern window. There's a second stool at the bench, though you'll be forbidden to touch the screwdrivers. And there is a wall of clocks that all tell different times, and I warn you now, I refuse to explain it."

"They argue," Noor said. "The baker's boy told me. One is right and one is stubborn."

"The boy is a menace and I love him." Odile's eyes were bright and suspiciously wet, and she was laughing while she lied about it, which was, Noor thought, entirely her. "Well. Say something, before I change my mind and charge you for the repair."

So Noor did the only ceremonial act available to a man of his profession. He took the typewriter from its case — he had carried it to dinner; of course he had; Odile had pretended not to notice — set it on the table between the soup bowls, threaded a fresh ribbon with fingers that were not quite steady, rolled in a clean page, and typed:

*Dear Odile. This is the first sentence of the rest of it.*

He turned the machine so she could read it. The *h* landed perfectly true.

"Twenty-minute repair," she said, her voice not entirely level, "my eye."

"I told you it was a difficult machine."

"You did." She leaned across the bowls and kissed him, and the proprietor, a man of wide experience and wider discretion, turned up the radio.

Later — much later, when the soup was gone and the page had acquired a second line in her handwriting-adjacent typing (*Noted. Approved. Signed, the management.*) and the waiter had begun stacking chairs around them with pointed optimism — they walked the eleven feet of Marrow Street arm in arm, past the darkened shop, past the two clocks in the window keeping their beautiful disagreement.

Above the door, the correct clock struck ten.

Inside the shop, unwatched, the stubborn one paused — considered — and, for the first time since Odile had installed it, ticked itself into agreement.

Odile stopped. Listened. Began, slowly, to laugh.

"What?" Noor said.

"Nothing," she said, unlocking the door. "Come upstairs. I'll show you what time it is."

* * *

And that, dear reader, would be a tidy place to end — except that no story involving a typewriter ends tidily, because the machine keeps going after the writer stops. In the drawer of the workbench, beneath the three screwdrivers and the tin of oil, there is a folder of pages that Odile has begun typing on quiet evenings while Noor reads aloud: the whole account, from a failed master clock to a frozen minute, from a forged name to a true one. Noor insists it wants an ending. Odile insists it has one.

They are both wrong. It wants a beginning.

But that is tomorrow's work, and tomorrow, for the first time in either of their lives, will arrive exactly on time.
