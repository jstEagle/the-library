## 1. The Weight of Lead

The trouble with lead, Ivo Eastwick had decided by her fourteenth winter and confirmed every day since, was that it remembered everything you did to it.

Wood forgot. Paper forgave. But lead held the shape of your thumbprint forever if you pressed it wrong, and so she had learned early to press it right — or better still, not at all. She could pick up a piece of type now and know without looking whether it had been cut well or badly, the way other people knew bread by smell. Her father said this was a gift. Ivo suspected it was more like an illness, but it paid for the coal, so she didn't complain. Much.

The print shop on Tanner's Row smelled the way all good shops in Vellmuth smelled: ink, oil, cold stone, and the faint sweet rot of the river coming up through the cellar floor no matter how many times they caulked it. It was a narrow building squeezed between a cooper's yard and a house that had been empty so long the sparrows considered themselves landlords. The press stood in the front room like a fat black ox that had lain down mid-journey and refused to rise again. The type cases lined the wall behind it, forty shallow drawers of pigeonholes, each compartment holding its little army of letters standing shoulder to shoulder, waiting to be told what to say.

Her father sat at the composing stick with his nose nearly touching the type, which was how he read anything now.

"Is this an e?" he asked, holding up a piece of type as if presenting evidence at trial.

Ivo leaned over from the stone where she was cleaning the rollers. "That's a c."

"It has a crossbar."

"It has a shadow. Hold it toward the window."

He did, turning it this way and that with the enormous patience he brought to everything except his own eyesight, which he treated as a personal enemy engaged in treachery. "So it is," he admitted. "A c. Well." He set it down carefully among the others. "You might have told me sooner."

"You only just asked."

"That is not the same thing as being told sooner."

This was the general shape of their mornings. Aldous Eastwick — not the captain Aldous, that came later, though nobody in town knew it yet — had set his first line of type before Ivo's mother was born, back when the shop printed wedding invitations, funeral notices, and the occasional broadside ballad about a drowned miller, which sold briskly because everyone loves a ballad about someone else's misfortune. Now his eyes were going the way of the river ice in March: slowly at first, then all at once, and he had begun the elaborate pretense of not noticing, which meant Ivo had begun the counter-pretense of doing his work badly enough that he'd notice and take it back, then doing it perfectly once he wasn't looking.

It was a marriage of deceptions, and like most marriages, it functioned.

* * *

The bell above the door rang at half past nine, and Mistress Hetta Corvel came in smelling of tallow and grievance.

Hetta kept the counting house accounts for the grain merchants and was, in her own estimation, the second most important person in Vellmuth after the mayor and possibly God. She wore a shawl the color of wet sand and carried a ledger under her arm the way soldiers carry muskets — close, and with intent.

"Eastwick," she said, nodding to the father, and then, locating Ivo by the rollers, "girl."

"Mistress Corvel," Ivo said. "What can we do for you?"

"The town ledger wants rebinding, and the pages want sorting. Pages have come loose." She dropped the book on the counter with a thump that made the ink cans ring. "The clerk says your father does careful work."

"My father does very careful work," Ivo agreed, "and my father will be delighted."

Her father, who had heard the word *ledger* and understood that a whole book of small dense figures was about to enter his life, said, "Delighted," in the tone of a man agreeing to be delighted at his own funeral.

But it was money, and money was coal, and coal was the difference between a warm February and a February spent wearing coats indoors, so Hetta counted out coins and left instructions longer than some sermons, and the Eastwicks were left alone with the master ledger of the town of Vellmuth.

It was a big book. Bigger than a Bible and considerably heavier, bound in leather gone green-black with age and handling, its corners worn round as river stones. Ivo carried it to the worktable and opened it, and there they all were.

Every name in town.

That was the thing about the ledger that people who didn't handle records never quite understood. Money wasn't the town's currency — everyone said it was, but everyone said a lot of foolish things. Names were the currency. A name entered in the ledger meant you owned the ground under your feet, owed the tax on your chimney, had a vote in the guild hall, existed. A name absent from the ledger meant none of those things, and a name struck through meant something worse, something that made even clerks lower their voices.

Ivo turned the pages and read her town the way she read a page of type: by feel, almost. Here was the cooper next door, Bren Ostler, owing six crowns against his wood stores. Here was Widow Petch, who paid her taxes a season late but always paid them, entered with the notation *honest, slow*. Here were the Fenners, boatwrights, three generations of them listed one beneath the other like notes in a chord.

And here, on the page for the eastern parish, a family called Marlowe — struck through. Not crossed out in the careless way of a mistake corrected, but ruled through with a long deliberate line, the way you'd kill something you wanted to be sure stayed dead. No note beside it. No reason. Just the line, drawn hard enough to dent the next three pages underneath.

"Hetta," Ivo called, "the Marlowes. Were they rehoused?"

Hetta was already halfway out the door with her coins. "Who?"

"The Marlowes. Eastern parish. They're struck from the ledger."

Hetta paused, and for a moment something moved behind her face like a fish under ice — there and gone before you could say what it was. "Before your time," she said. "Ask your father." And the bell rang, and she was gone.

Ivo looked across the shop. Her father had taken off his spectacles and was polishing them on his apron, which was what he did when he was thinking about something he'd rather not think about.

"Papa. The Marlowes."

"They left." He put the spectacles back on and immediately took them off again, having discovered he could see no better with them than without, which was the tragedy of bad spectacles. "Years ago. There was trouble with the excise men."

"What kind of trouble?"

"The kind there always is. Somebody said something, somebody else wrote it down, and then it was in the record and couldn't be gotten out again." He came over and ran his thumb along the edge of the great book without opening it, the way another man might touch a scar. "A record is a strange animal, Ivo. People think it's like a mirror. It isn't. A mirror shows you what's there. A record decides what counts as there in the first place."

She looked at the ruled-through line. "And whoever holds the pen—"

"—decides," her father said. "Yes." Then he smiled, because he was a kind man and could see he'd walked her somewhere dark before breakfast, and he tapped the loose pages instead. "Come on. Sorting won't do itself, and Hetta Corvel will be back at noon to inspect, and God help us both if a folio is out of order."

They worked through the morning, and it was good work, the kind Ivo liked best — patient, exacting, invisible to anyone who didn't know what they were looking at. She collated the loose leaves and sewed them back into their gatherings with linen thread. Her father checked the order of names, holding each page close to his face, and Ivo watched him miss two transpositions and catch neither, and quietly marked both with slivers of paper and fixed them when he went to fetch more glue.

She was good at this. That was the whole of the problem, really. She was very, very good at things nobody saw.

Anyone could see her father's work. His name was on the door, painted by his own hand thirty years ago, the letters still crisp. When the guild masters wanted a job done beautifully they came to Eastwick's, and her father took the credit with the modesty of a man accepting weather. What they didn't see was that for five years the finest type in the shop had been cut by fingers smaller than his, working at night by lamplight, because her father's hands had begun to shake and his eyes to swim, and because he would rather die than admit either fact aloud.

Ivo had cut new sorts for the italic face last autumn — a whole alphabet, refined letter by letter until the curves sang — and signed nothing, told no one, and listened the following week while Master Quill the stationer complimented Aldous Eastwick on how his italics had "come along lately."

"They have, haven't they," her father had said, with the smallest glance sideways at his daughter, and that glance was the entire conversation, and Ivo had carried it around inside her chest for days like a warm stone.

It should have been enough. Most days it was enough. But there were moments — sorting a stranger's ledger, reading names that would never know she'd handled them — when something in her stood up and demanded to be counted too. Not praised, exactly. Not famous. Just *counted*. Entered somewhere. Made real.

She'd mentioned it exactly once, two winters past, standing at the press with her arms black to the elbow.

"I could take apprentices someday," she'd said, casually, the way you mention weather. "When I'm older. Teach them cutting."

Her father had gone quiet in the particular way he went quiet, which was completely, and for a long time, and then he'd said, "Apprentices need a master registered with the guild," and picked up his composing stick, and that had been the end of it. Girls didn't register. Girls assisted. Girls were the hands and the eyes and never the name on the door.

Fine, Ivo thought, sorting Hetta's ledger into perfect order. Fine. She didn't need the door. She needed the work. The work was real; the work held its shape in lead forever, like a thumbprint, like a scar.

She was sewing the last gathering into the binding when she found the loose leaf tucked at the back of the book — not a ledger page at all, but a requisition receipt, official stock, stamped with a seal she didn't recognize. Military. She'd seen the seals on proclamations from the capital, though those had always been about distant things: tariffs, musters, wars that happened elsewhere to other people's sons.

This one was dated three weeks hence.

She frowned at it. Receipts weren't usually dated forward. She turned it over; blank. She held it to the window the way she'd taught her father that morning, and the watermark showed through — a crest, an eagle or a hawk, wings spread, and beneath it in fine engraved letters:

*Provisioning Directorate — Office of Requisitions.*

Well. Someone had used the ledger as a pressing board and left their paperwork behind. Sloppy of Hetta. Ivo tucked the leaf back where she'd found it, mostly, and finished the sewing, and washed the ink from her hands, and thought no more about it — or rather, thought exactly as much about it as a person thinks about a single drop of water falling in a cave they've never heard of, somewhere upstream, in the dark, beginning the long work of hollowing out the mountain.

At noon the bell rang, and Hetta inspected the binding, found nothing wrong, and seemed almost disappointed about it.

"Adequate," she pronounced, which from Hetta was a knighthood.

"Tell the clerk we're at his service," her father said warmly, seeing her to the door.

"And tell him," Ivo added, "he left a paper in the ledger. A requisition receipt. He'll want it back, I expect."

Hetta stopped with one foot on the threshold. Outside, the street went on being an ordinary street — carts, gulls, the cooper hammering hoops, a boy chasing a dog. But Hetta stood very still, the way animals go still, and when she turned around her face had arranged itself into pleasant blankness, the way you'd arrange a room before company.

"There's no paper," she said.

"I found one. Tucked in the back gathering. Military stamp—"

"There's no paper," Hetta said again, gently, the way you'd speak to someone standing on thin ice, and she took her ledger, and she paid nothing further, and she left, walking quickly, not quite running, in the direction of the counting house.

Ivo stood in the doorway watching her go. Behind her, her father had gone quiet in the particular way again.

"Papa?"

"Bring me that leaf," he said.

She retrieved it from where she'd tucked it under the ink stone. He held it at arm's length, then close, then gave up on his eyes entirely and felt it instead — the weight of the stock, the bite of the engraving under his thumb, the crispness of the fold. A man who'd spent fifty years with paper could read it blind.

"This is good stock," he murmured. "Army stock. Heavy. Expensive." He looked up, and his ruined eyes found hers with uncanny accuracy, the way blind men's often do. "Ivo. Why would the Provisioning Directorate be sending receipts to Vellmuth three weeks before anyone's ordered anything?"

Outside, the cooper's hammer rang on. Down at the docks, the river lay flat and grey and frozen solid, holding its breath, the way it did every winter, as if it knew something was coming and was waiting to see what.

"I don't know," Ivo said.

"No," her father agreed softly, folding the leaf and putting it in his apron pocket, where it weighed far more than paper should. "Neither do I."
