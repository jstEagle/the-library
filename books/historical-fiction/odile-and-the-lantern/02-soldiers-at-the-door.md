## 2. Soldiers at the Door

The first sign of trouble was not trumpets or drums, as Ivo had always assumed revolutions and armies would announce themselves. It was flour.

She was sweeping it — the fine white drift that escaped every sack in the storeroom no matter how carefully they stitched them — when her father appeared at the top of the cellar stairs with his hand pressed flat against the wall, listening the way he listened now, with his whole body, since his eyes had begun their slow retreat.

"Do you hear that?" he asked.

Ivo stopped. Underneath the usual sounds of Vellmuth on market morning — the fishwife arguing with the cooper, the church bell that rang eleven minutes late because the sexton refused to climb the tower before his second cup of coffee — there was something new. A rhythm. Boots, many of them, keeping time.

"Soldiers," she said.

"In Vellmuth?" Her father laughed, a dry little sound like type falling into a case. "We have nothing here but printers and eel-catchers. What would they want with us?"

It was a fair question, and the town spent most of that morning asking it of one another while the column came over the mill bridge in the pale spring light. Grey coats, grey wagons, grey faces above grey collars, and at the head of them a single officer on a bay horse who sat so straight he looked like he'd been ironed.

By noon the town square smelled of horse and gun oil instead of bread and river mud, and by evening everyone knew three things: the army was staying; the army was hungry; and the army intended to be paid for its trouble in everything Vellmuth had.

* * *

"They requisitioned the Kesslers' grain," Maren told her two days later, when Ivo came to collect the lantern frames she'd promised to repair for the winter festival. "All of it. Old Kessler stood in his own barn and read them the deed, and a sergeant thanked him very politely and wrote it down in a book, and then the wagons rolled out full."

"What did he do?"

"He watched." Maren's hands kept working as she talked, fitting a sliver of horn into a lantern pane, her fingers quick and sure around the small bright pieces. "That's what you do when a man has a rifle and you have a deed. You watch."

Ivo carried the frames home through streets that had grown quieter than March weather could explain. People still nodded to each other, but the nods had shortened. Conversations bent themselves around the soldiers the way water bends around stones.

At the shop, her father was standing in the doorway squinting down the lane at a notice freshly pasted to the post outside the chandlery. She read it aloud for him — *By order of the Provisional Authority, all households shall declare their stores of grain, salt, oil, and fodder within three days* — and he made a sound low in his throat.

"Declare," he repeated. "That's a gentle word for it."

"There's more. They're billeting officers. Two of them are assigned to this street."

Her father turned his face toward her. Even half-blind, he could aim a look. "Not in the shop."

"The shop is listed as a commercial premises, Papa. Commercial premises get officers."

He said nothing, which from him was a speech. He went back inside and began, with tremendous concentration, to sort a drawer of commas that did not need sorting.

* * *

The officers arrived on Thursday, and they were, infuriatingly, polite.

The taller one introduced himself as Lieutenant Brasse and apologized twice for the inconvenience, once on arrival and once while carrying his own trunk up the stairs to the room over the workshop, as if apology were a form of rent he could pay in installments. The shorter one said almost nothing at all and spent the evening measuring things — the windowsill, the stair rail, the width of the door — in a small notebook, writing nothing down, just measuring.

"They're taking the dimensions of our house," Ivo whispered to her father after supper.

"Perhaps they're building a model of it."

"For what?"

"For burning, presumably. Armies like models of things they intend to burn."

"Papa."

"I'm joking, Ivo." He wasn't smiling, though. He wound the clock with unnecessary force. "Keep your voice down near the stairs. Walls are thinner than people think."

She learned to keep her voice down. She also learned, in the way one learns anything under occupation — quickly, and without wanting to — the habits of the men upstairs. Lieutenant Brasse took sugar in his coffee and hummed marches off-key when he thought no one was listening. The short one, whose name nobody ever caught, rose before dawn and stood at the window watching the river, every morning, like a man waiting for it to confess to something.

They were, in themselves, bearable. It was what followed them that was not.

* * *

On the ninth day, the requisition notices went up again, and this time they named the master ledger.

Ivo read the notice twice, standing in the square with her marketing basket forgotten on her arm. *All civic registers, ledgers, rolls, and records of the town of Vellmuth are hereby declared administrative material of the Provisional Authority and shall be surrendered to the Office of Records forthwith.*

Administrative material. She walked home saying the phrase to herself, turning it over like an odd coin. The master ledger was not administrative material. The master ledger was the town. Her father's birth was in it, and her mother's death, and the Kesslers' land grants going back four generations, and the names of every soul who had ever been born, married, sued, taxed, or buried between the mill bridge and the eel weirs. Names were the only currency Vellmuth had never run short of, and the ledger was where they were minted.

And now some clerk in a grey coat had looked at it and seen paper.

"They can't mean the original," her father said when she told him. "They'll take copies. Every administration takes copies. It's the copying that makes them feel official."

But the next afternoon a wagon drew up outside the town hall, and Ivo, who had come to the square to deliver a job for the apothecary, saw the clerks carry it out.

There were six of them, the ledgers, heavy as paving stones, each one wrapped in sacking like something wounded. The town clerk, old Pemm, walked beside them with his hat in his hands, talking and talking — she was too far away to hear the words, but she knew the shape of the argument, having heard her father make versions of it her whole life — and the officer receiving the books let him talk until he ran out, and then held out a receipt for Pemm to sign.

And behind the officer, unhurried, a captain dismounted from his horse.

She knew later that his name was Aldous Vane, because within a week everyone in Vellmuth knew it, the way you come to know the name of a change in the weather. But she knew him first by his hands. He wore grey gloves, and he removed them slowly, finger by finger, before he touched the ledger, the way a surgeon removes his gloves before touching a patient, or a thief before touching a strongbox — and even at that distance she couldn't decide which of the two gestures it was.

He opened the oldest volume — she could see it was the oldest by the way it fell open, spine cracked soft with a century of thumbs — and turned a few pages. Then he closed it, and patted it, once, the way you'd pat the flank of a horse you'd just bought.

Lieutenant Brasse, materializing at her elbow with the terrible timing of junior officers, followed her gaze. "Ah," he said. "Captain Vane. He does love a record."

"Does he?"

"Passionately. He says a war is fought twice, Ivo Eastwick — once with soldiers, and once with ink, and the second one decides what the first one meant." He smiled down at her, pleasant as porridge. "You'd understand that, I expect. Printer's family."

Below them in the square, Captain Aldous Vane took up the proffered pen, and signed for the town's memory with a flourish — an actual flourish, a curling tail on the final letter, the signature of a man signing a dinner invitation rather than a requisition of everything a place had ever been. Then he handed the pen back, remounted his horse, and gave the order, and the wagon rolled toward the garrison with the ledgers bumping gently against one another inside, like passengers.

Old Pemm stood in the square holding his copy of the receipt, looking at it as though trying to remember what it was for.

* * *

"That's it, then," her father said that night. He sat with his hands flat on the workbench, on the cold iron of the press, as though steadying it or being steadied by it. "Copies will survive. Copies always survive. There will be a fair record somewhere."

"You didn't see him touch it, Papa."

"I saw the wagon go past the window. Sixty years I've paid my taxes and voted and buried my wife in that book's pages, and I saw it leave in a vegetable cart." He exhaled through his nose. "Well. We still have our own work, don't we? Bring me tomorrow's job list. If the world is being rearranged, the apothecary will still want his labels."

It was the bravest thing she'd ever heard anyone say, and he delivered it while miscounting his type, picking up an em-quad and calling it a period, and Ivo loved him so much for both halves of it — the courage and the fumble — that she had to leave the room before he saw her face.

Upstairs, the short lieutenant stood at his window, watching the dark river.

* * *

Three nights later she stayed late in the shop, cutting type for the apothecary's labels by lamplight, because the days had developed a way of disappearing into errands and queues and forms. The lieutenants were at the officers' mess. Her father had gone to bed early, complaining of his eyes, which was how he complained about everything now.

She was alone with the lamp and the smell of ink and lead, and it should have been peaceful, and mostly it was. That was the strange thing about the occupation so far — how ordinary it insisted on being. Soldiers bought bread. Officers tipped their hats. The bell still rang eleven minutes late. Life went on being life, just slightly to the left of where it used to stand, and you could almost forget —

Which was why, when she found the crumpled requisition receipt on the floor by the door — dropped, folded wrong, discarded by one of the lieutenants — she nearly swept it up with the rest of the day's rubbish without a thought.

Nearly.

But it was good paper. Army stock, thick and white, and paper was paper, and waste offended her professionally. She smoothed it out under the lamp to see if it could be salvaged for proof sheets.

It was a duplicate receipt for the seized ledgers. Standard form, columns, stamps. And along the bottom margin, in a clerk's cramped hand, someone had begun drafting annotations — corrections, apparently, or notes toward a summary. A few lines of figures. And beside them, in the same handwriting, a notation that made her go very still:

*R. Kessler — acreage adjusted per amended register. Debtor status confirmed.*

R. Kessler. Rudolph Kessler, who owned his farm free and clear and had read his deed aloud to a sergeant in his own barn. Rudolph Kessler, whom Ivo had known her whole life as a man so scrupulous about debt he once walked four miles to return a borrowed shilling.

Debtor status confirmed.

Amended register.

She stood in the empty shop with the lamp hissing and the receipt trembling very slightly in her fingers, and downstairs in the dark the old press waited like a sleeping animal, and above her head the short lieutenant's boots creaked once across the ceiling, and Ivo Eastwick understood that she was holding a piece of paper she was not supposed to have read — and that she was going to read it again anyway.
