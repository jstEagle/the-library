## 3. What the Ledger Says

The morning after the soldiers came, the town of Vellmuth woke up polite.

That was the strangest part, Ivo thought as she swept the shop's front step. Not the grain carts rumbling off toward the garrison at first light — though those had been bad enough, the sacks hoisted by men who didn't know a barley sack from an oat and didn't care to learn. Not the officers billeted in the Widow Kessel's good rooms, with their boots on her furniture. It was that everyone had decided, overnight and without discussion, to become *polite*. The baker said good morning to the sentry outside his door. The ferryman tipped his cap. Old Herr Brandt, who hadn't smiled since the flood year of Ivo's childhood, actually thanked a corporal for holding a gate open, in the voice of a man thanking a dog for not biting him.

Fear, it turned out, sounded exactly like courtesy.

Ivo swept, because sweeping was something hands could do while a mind refused to cooperate. Inside, her father was already at his desk with the day's work laid out before him: a stack of receipts for the requisition office, each one requiring a fair copy in a clerkly hand. The army had discovered within a day of arriving that Vellmuth possessed exactly one print shop, and had promptly decided that the Eastwick establishment existed for its convenience.

"Three dozen," her father said, peering at the top sheet from a distance of four inches. "Or is it three hundred? The hand is cramped."

"Three dozen," Ivo said. "And the ink is smudged where they rested their boots on the paper."

"They rest their boots everywhere." Her father sighed and rubbed his eyes behind the spectacles that no longer helped him much. "Boots and elbows. The whole army is one large elbow."

Despite everything — the grain gone, the ledger gone, the sense of the town holding its breath — Ivo laughed. It escaped before she could catch it, and her father looked pleased with himself, which he always did when he made her laugh, as though humor were another trade he'd taught her alongside setting type.

* * *

Copying receipts should have been dull work, and ordinarily was. A receipt was a receipt: so many sacks of wheat, so many head of cattle, so many cords of firewood, taken from such-and-such household for the provisioning of His Excellency's Third Army, value assessed at such-and-such crowns, signature, seal, date. The army paid in promissory notes that were, everyone understood, worth slightly less than the paper they were printed on — and Ivo was printing the paper, which struck her as a private joke at the whole world's expense, though nobody else seemed to be laughing.

She set the first receipt's type with the ease of long habit. That was the thing about letters: once you'd spent ten years handling them, your fingers knew their shapes the way a shepherd knew his sheep. She could find a capital R in the dark. She had, on more than one occasion, found a capital R in the dark, when her father called for emergency repairs to a wedding invitation at some ungodly hour.

It was on the fourth receipt that she stopped.

The requisition listed goods taken from a house on Tanner's Row — and Ivo knew Tanner's Row, every crooked house of it. This was the Doss household. Except the Doss household was empty, and had been since autumn, when old Pieter Doss died and his daughter's family packed up and moved downriver to her husband's people. Everyone knew this. Ivo herself had printed the funeral notice for Pieter Doss, had set his name in black-edged roman type and felt the small solemn satisfaction of doing it well.

But the receipt in her hand described a full requisition: grain stores, two pigs, a wagon, tools. And where the name of the household head should have been, the army clerk had written *Pieter Doss*, alive, present, consenting.

Ivo sat very still.

Perhaps a mistake, she told herself. Clerks made mistakes; clerks were human; even army clerks presumably went home at night and burned their supper like everyone else. Perhaps someone had copied from an old record without checking.

But the entry wasn't just wrong. It was *convenient*. A dead man couldn't protest a requisition. An absent family couldn't demand a fair price. You couldn't argue with a signature, and here was a signature where there should have been a living man saying no.

She looked at the receipt again. Below the clerk's hand, in different ink — fresher ink, ink she recognized, because she'd mixed a fresh batch only yesterday for the proclamation order — someone had added a line in the margin: *Doss, P., debtor, arrears assessed against property.* As if Pieter Doss owed the army money. As if the army had magnanimously seized his goods against a debt incurred by a corpse.

Ivo's stomach did something cold and slow, like a stone dropping through river water.

* * *

She might have left it there. She wants it noted — she would note it later, in her own defense, in the account she kept of herself — that she tried to leave it there. She finished the three dozen receipts with steady hands and a face like any shopgirl's bored Tuesday. She ate lunch. She swept again, though the step needed nothing.

But the master ledger was still in the shop.

Captain Aldous Vane had signed for it two days ago — signed with a flourish, an actual flourish, the kind of penmanship a man practiced in front of a mirror — and declared it "administrative material" to be held in the requisition office pending the arrival of the commission. But the commission hadn't come yet, and the requisition office was short of space, and so the great book had been carried back across the square and stacked, with a sergeant's shrug, on the trestle table at the rear of the print shop, beneath a canvas that smelled of gun oil.

"It stays until they collect it," the sergeant had said. "Touch nothing."

Nobody had said anything about *reading* nothing. And more to the point, nobody had said anything about what a ledger required, which was copying — the army wanted extracts, tallies, lists of names for billeting and assessment, and who but the print shop could produce clean copies?

So when the runner came that afternoon with a chit requesting "a fair copy of the roll of households, Tanner's Row through Mill Lane," Ivo took the chit, nodded, and carried the ledger to her father's desk as if her heart weren't hammering somewhere up around her ears.

The book was enormous — the master ledger of Vellmuth, sixty years of entries in forty different hands, births and deaths and debts and sales and disputes, the town's whole memory bound in cracked leather. Her father used to call it the truest citizen in town, because it never slept and never forgot. She opened it to the T-section and began to copy.

That was when she saw the alterations.

They were good. That was the worst part. They were done in faded brown-black ink, matched to the age of neighboring entries, in a careful imitation of the old clerk's slanted hand. If you weren't looking — if you were a historian arriving next month with clean gloves and a trusting heart — you would read them as original. But Ivo had spent ten years studying how ink aged into paper, because matching old documents to new was half her father's business. Fresh ink sat *on* the page a little, caught the light differently, had edges the decades hadn't yet softened.

And under her fingertips, the pages told a different story than the one they pretended to tell.

Pieter Doss's death entry — the one she herself had copied from notice to register, the date firm in her memory because she'd joked to her father about the size of the funeral party — was still there, but annotated in that patient fake hand: *entry erroneous, see correction.* And the correction, inserted neatly in the margin, unmade him. Struck the death. Restored him to life on paper, a life of debts and consents, a life convenient to whoever held the pen.

A few leaves further, the Brenner family — gone since autumn, moved downriver, the whole street had helped them load the cart — had been struck out entirely. Not crossed out, which would leave a mark, a scar, evidence. *Removed*, with the remaining entries stitched closed around the gap like skin over a wound, so that the page read smoothly onward as if no family named Brenner had ever stood on Tanner's Row or anywhere else. Their house requisitioned from — she checked the receipt again — from whom? From no one. From a blank. Goods without owners cost nothing.

Ivo copied the requested households in a hand as neat as ever. Her handwriting did not shake. She was almost proud of that later, and almost sickened.

* * *

Her father noticed at closing time, the way he noticed most things now that his eyes were failing — through sound, through air, through some other sense sharpening to compensate.

"You've been quiet," he said, banking the stove.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quietly cheerful, usually. There's a difference. Tonight you're quiet like the river before ice." He lowered himself into his chair. "What did you see today?"

She considered lying. It lasted about a second and a half; lying to her father had never worked, mostly because he could hear the shape of a lie the way she could feel a misaligned letter through cotton gloves.

"I saw a dead man made a debtor," she said. "And a family erased out of the ledger as if they'd never lived here."

The stove ticked. Somewhere across the square, the sentry's boots marked time on the cobbles.

"Which family?"

"The Brenners."

Her father was silent a long moment. His hands lay open on his knees, ink-stained, the nails cut blunt for type work, and Ivo watched them and thought: those hands set thirty thousand pages, and none of them lied, and what do we do now that the world has decided otherwise?

"When I was younger than you," he said at last, "there was a flood year, and after the flood year there was an audit, and after the audit certain names in that same ledger were... adjusted. Grain that had been sold was recorded as never harvested. Men who had lent money were recorded as having been repaid. It was smaller then. A handful of entries. Enough that nobody starved who wouldn't have anyway, and everybody who mattered got comfortable." He rubbed his spectacles on his sleeve, uselessly, the gesture of a man buying time. "I copied some of those adjustments myself. Fair copies. Clean hands. I told myself a fair copy isn't an original. I told myself the ink wasn't mine."

"What happened?" Ivo asked.

"Nothing happened. That's what happened. Nothing, forever, and everyone who noticed decided they hadn't." He set the spectacles down. "Paper is patient, Ivo. It waits. And then one day somebody comes along who needs the past to say something, and finds that the past already says it, because we let them write it years ago."

Ivo looked toward the trestle table, where the ledger sat under its canvas like a sleeping animal that dreamed in other people's voices. Sixty years of the town in there. Births and weddings and the price of flour in hard winters and who had paid what and who owed whom. And threaded through it now, patient as rot, the corrections — a dead man resurrected into debt, a family unpicked from existence stitch by stitch.

"It can be fixed," she said. "The truth is still underneath. The old entries are still there, under the fakes. Anyone with a lamp and a loupe could—"

"Could what?" her father asked gently. "Prove it? To whom? The commission arrives next month with the victors' blessing, reads the victors' ledger, and writes the victors' history. They won't bring a loupe, love. They'll bring a stamp."

He was right, and she hated that he was right, and she hated more the small practical thought that arrived on the heels of the hatred, quiet as a typesetter slipping a letter into its case:

*A copy survives.*

One ledger, guarded, destined for the commission's shelves. But ink could be multiplied. Paper could be hidden. A thing printed was a thing that existed in more places than any soldier could search.

She didn't say it aloud. She banked the stove instead, and hung her apron, and kissed her father's cheek, and went up to bed, where she lay listening to the river muttering under its winter lid.

Below her window, the sentry walked his post. In the trestle table's shadow, the ledger waited to become history. And in Ivo Eastwick's chest, cold and clear as the first water seeping through spring ice, a single thought settled and refused to melt:

Records could lie. Which meant records could also be made to tell the truth — if someone got to them first.

She fell asleep composing, without quite admitting it, a list of names.
