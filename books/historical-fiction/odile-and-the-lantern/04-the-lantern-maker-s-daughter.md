## 4. The Lantern Maker's Daughter

The lanterns had been hung for the winter festival in the first week of Frostmoon, and by the third week they were dying one by one.

You could see it from the print shop window if you stood on the stool by the door and craned your neck past the proclamations we'd been ordered to display — the ones with the eagle and the neat block letters announcing GRAIN REQUISITIONED FOR THE PROTECTION OF VELLMUTH, as though the grain had asked to be protected. Beyond them, strung across Candlemaker's Row and up the hill toward the church, the festival lanterns sagged on their wires like tired birds. A few still burned. Most had gone dark, their paper torn by wind or their candles drowned in their own wax, and nobody climbed the ladders to fix them because nobody felt much like festivals anymore.

My father noticed them too, in the way he noticed most things now — sideways, and a beat too late.

"Lanterns are out," he said one evening, squinting at the window as if the whole street were slightly out of focus, which to him it was.

"Yes, Papa."

"Someone ought to see to that."

"Yes, Papa."

He went back to his ledger work, which was really my ledger work these days; I did the figures and read them aloud, and he nodded and made marks that were increasingly decorative. His eyes had been going for two years, but the army's arrival had accelerated things, the way grief accelerates everything. He didn't like being read to. He liked it even less that he needed it. So we had developed an arrangement of polite fictions: he was "reviewing," I was "assisting," and neither of us said the word *blind*, which sat in the corner of the shop like a piece of furniture no one would claim.

It was my mother who used to hang our own lantern in the window each Frostmoon. She'd been dead six years, and I had kept up the habit without deciding to — a candle, a glass chimney, the little tin frame with its punched stars. Some nights I forgot. Some nights I lit it and couldn't have told you why, only that the window looked wrong otherwise, like a face missing an eyebrow.

So when Maren Ostergaard came into the shop on a gray Tuesday smelling of tallow and cold river air, asking in her flat, unhurried voice whether the print shop could produce a replacement plate for a lantern label — the paper kind, glued inside the glass so the light shone through the printed name — I said yes before she finished asking.

"We do all manner of printing," I said. "Labels, bills of sale, funeral notices."

"I know what you do." She set a scorched rectangle of paper on the counter. The printing on it had bubbled and browned, but I could make out the lettering: **FOR THE FESTIVAL OF LIGHTS — IN MEMORY OF THOSE WE KEEP**. Beneath it, a name. The name was gone. Fire had taken it cleanly, the way fire takes the thing you least want lost.

"This is from the Row lanterns?" I asked.

"The Hessel lantern. Third wire from the chandler's." She looked at me with pale eyes that didn't blink enough. "I made those frames twenty years ago. I keep them mended. The labels aren't mine to print."

"No," I agreed, not following.

"They're yours to print. Or your father's. Anyone with type." She pushed the burnt scrap an inch toward me. "The family who hangs that lantern — the Hessels — left in autumn. Before the army came. No one's left to order the label, and a lantern with a blank where a name should be is worse than a lantern gone dark. I won't have it."

I turned the scrap over. There was nothing on the back but old glue.

"Whose name was it?"

She watched me for a moment, deciding something. "Anselm Hessel. And his wife, Petra. And the boy." A pause, the length of a held breath. "Denny Hessel. He was ten."

"And the army took the house?"

"The army took the grain, the girl, the cart horse, and the peace of everyone who watched them do it." She said it without heat, the way you'd recite a laundry list, which somehow made it worse. "Can you print the label or not? I'll pay in candle ends. You people are always short of light, I notice. Print shops. All that hunching over pages in the dark."

"We have lamps," I said.

"You have lamps the way a fish has water. Barely, and only because you'd die otherwise." 

Despite myself — despite the gray weight the town had been carrying since the requisitions, despite Captain Vane's signature sitting in the master ledger like a fly in amber — I laughed. It came out of me unexpectedly, a small startled sound, and Maren Ostergaard looked faintly pleased, the way a person looks when a stubborn stove finally catches.

"Thursday," I said. "Come Thursday. I'll have the label done."

* * *

Setting the type for Anselm Hessel and his wife and the boy Denny took me perhaps ten minutes. Standing in the shop afterward, holding the finished proof, took considerably longer.

There is a strange authority in print. I had known this my whole life, grown up breathing it — my father said ink was the only honest ghost, that a printed name outlives the mouth that spoke it. But I had never felt the weight of it quite so plainly as I did holding a strip of paper with three names on it, two adults and a child, none of whom had asked to be remembered by a stranger's hand. The H in Hessel sat a hair proud of the baseline. I pulled the proof and reset it. Then I did it again, because if I was going to carry someone's name in lead, I was going to carry it level.

I printed twelve labels. One for the Hessel lantern and eleven extra, because Maren struck me as a woman who planned for fire.

She collected them Thursday morning, examined the proof against the burnt original, and grunted — which I would learn was her highest form of praise — then paid me in candle ends as promised, plus one item unbidden: a small glass storm lantern, secondhand, with a tin door and a hook for hanging.

"For your window," she said. "I've seen it. Your candle sits in an open dish like an offering to a draft."

"It's been fine for years."

"It's been lucky for years. Luck runs out." She set the lantern on the counter with the finality of a judge laying down a gavel. "Maren Ostergaard. Lantern maker, Candlemaker's Row, the crooked shop with the good chimney. If your wick smokes, bring it in. Don't trim it yourself — everyone trims wrong their first year and half their second."

I hadn't told her it was my first year with any lantern at all. She'd deduced it somehow, the way she seemed to deduce everything — by some method involving long silences and an unwillingness to be lied to.

* * *

The winter deepened. The army settled into the town the way silt settles into a riverbed — gradually, then everywhere. Soldiers billeted in the granary loft and the taproom of the Salt Eel. Officers took the corner rooms at the Widow Brann's, paying promptly and eating enormously. Proclamations arrived at the shop twice a week, and I printed them, and the town read them, and the gap between what the proclamations said and what the town knew widened like a crack in ice.

I took the storm lantern home and hung it in the window. My father approved. "Good glass," he said, tapping it. "Who gave you this?"

"A customer."

"What did you print?"

"Three names."

He nodded, satisfied, and asked nothing further. This was another feature of our arrangement: we had both gotten very good at not asking questions whose answers might require action.

In midwinter, the third wire from the chandler's went dark again — not the label this time but the mechanism, the little tin door warped by weather so the wind snuffed the candle. I learned this because Maren appeared at the shop door with the entire lantern under her arm, set it on my counter, and announced: "You're coming to help. Bring your hands and don't talk much."

"I wasn't planning to talk at all."

"Everyone plans that. Nobody manages it."

The lantern workshop on Candlemaker's Row was exactly as advertised: crooked of beam, blessed of chimney, warm as a held breath. It smelled of beeswax and hot tin and, underneath, faintly of the river. Frames in every stage of life hung from the rafters — finished ones glowing softly in the afternoon gloom, half-made skeletons of tin and wire, and on a long bench, a row of glass chimneys waiting like patient singers before a performance.

Maren put the Hessel lantern on the bench, opened her tool drawer, and handed me pliers.

"Bend this tab back. Gently. Tin remembers."

"Tin remembers?"

"Copper forgives. Tin remembers. Brass forgets entirely, which is why I don't use brass." She said this with the gravity of scripture. "Every material has a character. Glass holds a grudge. Paper tells the truth once and then lies forever if you let it get damp."

"That's paper exactly," I said, and she glanced at me — a quick flicker of appraisal — and said nothing, and bent back over the lantern, and I understood that I had passed some kind of examination without knowing there was one.

We worked. That was the whole of it, at first. She straightened the frame; I held things; she soldered; I fetched things; she tested the hinge eleven times, muttering, and replaced a pin she deemed unworthy. The silence between us wasn't empty. It was the working silence of two people whose hands are busy and whose minds are permitted to go wherever minds go when the hands are occupied — which is, in my experience, the only place grief can be carried comfortably. Not discussed. Carried.

Because I knew about her son. Everyone in Vellmuth knew about Maren's son, in the way everyone knows about the weather — not as gossip but as condition. Joren Ostergaard, nineteen, a ferryman's apprentice, taken by soldiers in the first week of the occupation for what the proclamation called *seditious gathering* — which meant he'd been on a dock with four other young men, talking. He had not come back. No trial had been announced. No body returned. When Maren had gone to the officers' quarters to ask, she'd been told, politely, by a lieutenant with clean fingernails, that inquiries were being handled through proper channels.

That had been in autumn. It was winter now, and the channels had produced nothing but the word *properly*, repeated.

She did not speak of him. I did not speak of my mother, dead six years, nor of the ledger with its growing margin of struck-through names, nor of the way Captain Aldous Vane had smiled at me across the shop counter and said, "Your father has fine hands. I suspect the family talent runs true." I did not tell her that I sometimes lay awake composing sentences I would never dare print. She did not tell me what she did at night, alone above the workshop, with all those lanterns.

But once — just once — as we worked, she said, without looking up: "Joren used to hold the frames for me while I soldered. He had steady hands. Steadier than mine now." Her iron hovered over the seam, perfectly level. "You hold a frame well."

"Thank you."

"He'd have liked you. He liked people who were quiet until they weren't." She touched the iron to the seam. A thin ribbon of smoke rose, sweet-smelling. "There. Tin remembers, but it can be persuaded."

* * *

After that I went to the workshop every few days. There was no arrangement made; I simply arrived, and she simply had work for me. In return I ate more stewed eel than I had ever voluntarily consumed in my life (it was good; I complained anyway, because she seemed to enjoy the complaint), and I learned the trade in fragments: how to cut glass with a string and kerosene and nerve, how to punch tin stars so the light throws patterns on snow, how to wick a lamp so it burns low and long instead of high and brief.

"Marrying?" she asked me once, apropos of nothing, while I struggled with a wick.

"No."

"Good. Marriage is a lantern with two flames. They fight for the oil." She considered. "Though some pairs manage it. My Anders and I managed thirty years before the river took him. Two flames, one oil. We were careful."

"Was it hard? Being careful?"

"All the best things are hard carefully." She reached over and corrected my wick with two fingers. "You're cutting it too long. Long wick, big flame, fast death. Short wick, small flame, long life. Everything worth keeping works that way."

I thought of the ledger. I thought of names burning off labels. I thought of how carefully the army was keeping everything, and what a small flame they intended us to be.

* * *

It was on an evening in late Frostmoon, the two of us mending lanterns by lamplight while snow ticked against the windows, that the silence finally broke — not with words about grief, but with words adjacent to it, which is often as close as two people can safely stand.

"Your father," Maren said. "He's losing his sight."

It wasn't a question. I fed a wick through a burner. "Yes."

"How long?"

"Two years getting bad. Six months getting worse. He still sets type by feel, sometimes, when he thinks I'm not watching. He gets the letters right more often than you'd believe." I smiled despite myself. "Lead has a shape. He says his fingers know the alphabet better than his eyes ever did."

"Hm." She soldered a seam. The smell of flux filled the warm air. "Anders' eyes went at the end. Rivermen's eyes, all that glare off the water. Toward the last he couldn't see the lanterns at all, but he could hear which one of mine was burning wrong. Said each one had a voice." She set down the iron. "I thought that was sentiment. Then I went deaf in my left ear — too many years of hammering — and found out he was right. Every flame has a sound. The draft changes it. You can hear a lantern dying before you can see it."

"Is that why you keep the Row lanterns mended yourself? You can hear them fail?"

"Partly." She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly. "Partly because the festival committee stopped meeting. Partly because the Hessels are gone. Mostly because—" She stopped. Outside, the snow ticked. Somewhere down the Row, a soldier's boots rang on frozen cobbles, passing, fading.

"Mostly," she said at last, "because a town that lets its lights go out is telling itself something. And I don't agree with what it's saying."

I held very still. There are moments when you can feel a conversation standing at a door, deciding whether to enter. This was one.

"My mother used to say," I offered, quietly, "that a printed name is the only honest ghost."

Maren looked at me across the bench, lamplight moving in her pale eyes. "Your mother sounds like she'd have been worth knowing."

"She was."

"What else did she say?"

I thought about it. What else had she said. That you should always keep one copy. That the powerful count on forgetting, and the rest of us must count out loud. That Ivo was an ugly name and I'd grow into it, which had proven only half true.

"She said you should always know what you'd die for," I said. "And then spend your life making sure you never have to find out."

Maren snorted — an inelegant, genuine sound. "Mothers," she said, with enormous affection. Then: "Hand me that frame. The Kestrel one. Their girl married a soldier, you know. Left in October with him. Her mother still hangs a lantern for her. Can't decide whether to hope or mourn, poor woman, so she does both, which is probably correct."

I passed her the frame. Our fingers overlapped briefly on the cold tin, and neither of us remarked on it.

* * *

I was walking home that night through falling snow, the repaired Hessel lantern under my arm for rehanging in the morning, when I saw the soldiers at the end of Candlemaker's Row. Three of them, posted at the corner with a brazier, stamping their feet. New postings — they hadn't been there last week. As I watched, one of them stepped out and stopped a fishmonger heading home with his barrow, and there was a lantern in the exchange of papers, the fisherman's pass held up to its light, examined, returned.

They were checking passes. At night. On a street where nothing happened except lantern-making.

I walked on, unhurried, a girl carrying a festival lantern home through the snow, and let my face do what faces in Vellmuth had gotten very good at doing: nothing at all. But beneath the nothing, a thought was forming with the slow inevitability of ice thickening on a river — a thought about names, and lights, and what it means when someone starts counting the streets where both are kept.

The next morning I hung the Hessel lantern back on the third wire, and Anselm Hessel and his wife Petra and the boy Denny burned steadily behind their new label, and Maren stood below in the snow with her hands in her pockets, listening to the flame.

"Good voice," she pronounced.

That afternoon, back at the shop, I sorted the day's correspondence and found among the bills and broadsheet proofs a single sheet bearing the army's seal. Orders for a new proclamation — a large one, commemorative. The army wished to announce, in the finest type the town could produce, its generous stewardship of Vellmuth through the winter.

At the bottom, in the space reserved for authorization, was a familiar flourish of a signature.

Captain Aldous Vane wanted a tribute printed. And he wanted it done beautifully.
