## 5. Ink Under the Nails

The proclamations arrived on a Tuesday, folded in threes and smelling of another town's ink.

Ivo knew it at once — that sour, iron-heavy smell of cheap gall mixed with too much lampblack — because she had spent her whole life learning to know inks the way other people learned to know faces. Her father could tell you the vintage of a wine from across a room and be wrong half the time; Ivo could tell you where a sheet of paper had been printed by the way it sat in her hands, and she was never wrong.

"This came from Corren," she said, holding the sample proclamation up to the window light. "They printed it on their second press, the one with the worn 'g.' Look — every 'g' has a little tail missing, like a fish bitten behind."

Her father squinted at it from his chair by the stove. "A bitten fish," he agreed, with the gravity of a man confirming a diagnosis. "Corren's press. Old Hobbet runs it. He owes me eleven crowns and an apology."

"The army wants two hundred copies by Friday," Ivo said. "Signed Captain A. Vane. *By order of the Provisional Administration.*"

"*Provisional*," her father repeated, tasting the word like the wine he couldn't judge anymore. "Everything's provisional now. The grain, the mayor's office, the price of bread." He sighed and rubbed his eyes, which had gone cloudy as river ice in the last year. "Well. We print what we're asked to print. That's what presses do, Ivo. They print what they're asked."

"That's one thing presses do," Ivo said.

He looked at her for a moment — or looked near her, near enough — and something passed over his face like a cloud passing over water, there and smoothed away again before she could read it.

"Two hundred copies," he said. "Friday."

* * *

Setting type for the occupation turned out to be the easiest work she had ever done, and that was precisely what made her hands itch.

The proclamations were all the same animal wearing different coats. *The Provisional Administration announces the fair distribution of requisitioned grain.* (Fair: meaning the army took forty percent and called its share administrative.) *Citizens are reminded that curfew begins at the ringing of the evening bell.* (Reminded: meaning a boy named Pell Ostrow had been caned in the market square for being out past it, and the cane had been borrowed from the tannery, and everyone had heard him.) *Prices are fixed by order of Captain A. Vane for the protection of the populace.*

Protection. Ivo set the word in twelve-point Caslon, letter by letter, and found herself thinking about how much care went into making a lie look comfortable. She centered it beautifully. She kerned it until the word sat on the page like a cat in a sunny window, entirely at ease, entirely entitled.

"You've made that very handsome," her father observed over her shoulder.

"It's a handsome lie."

"Ivo." His voice dropped. The shop door was open; the street was listening, as streets did now. "Handsome is safe. Ugly gets noticed. Print it handsome."

So she printed it handsome, two hundred times, and the press ran smooth as a loom, and if anyone had watched her they would have seen a dutiful daughter doing honest work. And she was. That was the terrible trick of it. Every stroke was true work, skilled work, the best work in Vellmuth, and none of it was true.

She washed the press that evening with more force than the rollers required.

* * *

It started, as most dangerous things do, with a receipt.

A grain merchant named Oskar Brandt came in on Thursday wanting fifty copies of a notice about seed prices — his own notice, his own words, dictated to Ivo while he stood at the counter worrying his hat brim into a shape no hat had been born with. While she wrote out his copy in pencil first, the way she always did, Brandt kept talking. Talking was what people did in the shop now. It had become the one room in Vellmuth where a man could say a number out loud without checking the doorway first.

"Forty percent, and then the fixed price on top of what's left," he muttered. "My brother-in-law in Hollen Bridge says the same. And they took Denna Kroy's whole winter store last week, not forty percent — *all* of it, because her late husband owed back taxes, and he's dead, so who argues? Who does he argue through?"

"Who indeed," Ivo said, writing.

"And the arrests. You hear about the arrests?" He lowered his voice to the register men used for weather talk, casual, deniable. "Three off the fishing street this week. Nobody knows why. Nobody knows where. The bell-ringer's cousin says the guardhouse won't even give the families the dates."

"Dates matter," Ivo said quietly.

"Dates are all we'll have left, girl, if we don't write them down."

He left. She finished his notice, printed his fifty copies, charged him fairly, and stood for a long moment afterward with the pencil still in her hand, looking at the scrap of paper where she'd drafted his words. In the margin, without quite deciding to, she had written: *Denna Kroy — whole store taken — taxes of dead husband.*

She looked at it the way you look at a match you've just struck indoors.

Then she put it in her apron pocket instead of the stove, and that was that.

* * *

The account began properly on Sunday, which amused her in a grim way, because Sunday was when her father read aloud from the almanac and called it devotion, and here was his daughter beginning a scripture of her own.

She chose a blank-end quire of good rag paper from the bottom drawer, the stock they saved for wedding invitations, smooth and strong and white as a held breath. She cut eight leaves and sewed them at the spine with linen thread, and she made herself a cover from a discarded proof of a funeral card — blank on one side — so that if the booklet lay on a table it looked like nothing, like a printer's scrap, like trash.

Inside, in her smallest, steadiest hand, she ruled faint columns. Date. What was taken. From whom. Price paid, if any. Names. Always names.

The first page filled itself almost faster than she could write:

*14th of the Frost Moon. Requisitioned from Oskar Brandt: 40% barley stock, 11 sacks. Paid in script redeemable at garrison stores only.*
*16th. Taken from Denna Kroy (widow): entire winter grain, cause: tax debt of husband, deceased 2 years. No receipt issued.*
*18th. Arrested from fishing street: Joss Amberle, his son Teo, and Wren Callas. Reason unstated. Families denied dates of detention.*
*19th. Bread price fixed at 3 soldi. Flour unobtainable at any price. Bell rings at six now, not seven. (Curfew moved. Not announced — discovered.)*

That last line she underlined twice. It had struck her as the purest example of the whole season: they hadn't posted a notice changing the curfew bell. They had simply changed it, and let the town find out in the dark, one frightened minute at a time. An empire that governed by letting you discover your own restrictions. She wrote underneath, small and fierce: *If it must be discovered, it should be recorded.*

Her father called down from upstairs that the stew was ready, and she closed the booklet, and slid it between two loose bricks behind the type cabinet where the mortar had crumbled years ago, and went up and ate her stew and praised it, and her hands did not shake at all, which surprised her.

* * *

The strange part — the part she didn't tell anyone, didn't even tell the booklet — was how *good* it felt.

She had expected it to feel like sickness. Writing down what the soldiers did, hiding paper in walls, keeping accounts like some smuggler of facts — she'd expected dread, and dread was there, yes, a low constant hum like the press itself. But underneath the dread ran something else, something that startled her every time she noticed it: pleasure. Pure, undiluted, craftsman's pleasure.

Because this, too, was type work. This was composition. Every day handed her raw material — a rumor, a receipt, a price scrawled on a tavern slate — and every night she set it true. No captain approved the proofs. No officer's flourish signed the bottom. When Ivo Eastwick wrote that Denna Kroy had lost her whole winter store to a dead man's debt, that sentence existed exactly as she composed it, accurate to the letter, and no power in Vellmuth could kern it comfortable.

"You're humming," her father said one night, catching her at it. He had come down the cellar stairs slow, one hand on the rail, drawn by lamplight he shouldn't have been able to see but somehow always sensed. "You only hum when the work is going well."

"Sorting sorts," she said, angling her body between him and the open booklet. Guilt arrived a beat late, as it always did.

"Mm," he said. He stood there a moment in the yellow light, his clouded eyes pointed generally toward the shelves, and Ivo felt the peculiar sensation of being seen by a man who could no longer see. "Your grandfather kept bees," he said at last. "Did I ever tell you? Everyone thought he was a mild old fellow, and he was, mostly. But he kept a ledger of every hive, and every swarm, and every theft of honey in thirty years, in a hand so small you needed a lens. After he died we found it. Forty pages of wrongs, dated." He smiled faintly at the memory. "Mother wanted to burn it. Father said no. He said a record isn't revenge, Ivo. It's a debt marked down. And debts get collected eventually, one way or another, by somebody."

"What happened to it?" she asked. Her voice came out carefully level.

"Lost, I suppose. Or hidden. Things hide themselves in this family." He patted the stair rail and climbed back up, slow step by slow step, and Ivo stood in the cellar with her heart going like a flywheel and the smell of ink and cold stone around her, thinking: *he knows. He doesn't know what he knows, but he knows.*

Or perhaps he didn't. Perhaps it was just an old man's story about bees. She genuinely could not tell, and decided that was the safest arrangement available.

* * *

The booklet grew a page a week, then two.

She developed rules, the way she developed rules for everything: never write anything she hadn't heard from two mouths or seen with her own eyes. Never name a source. Never keep the booklet anywhere longer than one night — it traveled between the brick gap, the false bottom of the paper chest, and once, memorably, rolled inside the hollow axle of the shop's delivery barrow, sweating there for three days until she could retrieve it.

And never, ever print it. Not yet. Ink was traceable; ink had a smell, a bite-pattern, a fingerprint of worn letters. Pencil was anonymous. Pencil was a rumor in advance of itself. Whatever this became someday, it would not become that today.

But she practiced. At night after closing she would stand at the case and compose a line or two of the true text in type — just for the discipline of it, she told herself, just to keep the skill honest — and then distribute every letter back where it belonged before the wax cooled on the morning bread. *Joss Amberle, arrested 18th, reason unstated.* Set, locked, looked at, broken apart, returned. It was like saying a prayer and unsaying it. Like rehearsing a speech you might one day be permitted to give.

The prices column was becoming its own quiet scandal. Fixed bread: 3 soldi. Black-market bread, which appeared within a fortnight of the fixing, as predictable as frost: 9. Army script: accepted nowhere but the garrison stores, whose shelves were, per three separate witnesses, "a joke told slowly." Ivo wrote it all down with a straight face and privately considered that arithmetic was its own kind of treason — you couldn't arrest a subtraction.

Once, copying a requisition schedule for legitimate work, she saw the numbers side by side on official paper: the tonnage taken from the valley districts, and the tonnage "distributed" back. The difference had a name in the ledger, and the name was *transport losses*, and the figure was enormous, and it repeated every month like a heartbeat.

Transport losses. As though the grain were dying of exposure on the road. As though sacks wandered off into the snow to weep.

She copied the figures into her booklet that same night, twice, and checked them against each other, and slept better than she had in weeks.

* * *

Maren noticed the ink first, of course. Lantern-makers notice stains.

"You've got black under your nails again," she said, not looking up from the tin panel she was soldering. They had settled into an easy rhythm over the winter festival lanterns — Maren working metal, Ivo sitting on the workshop stool mending wire frames, both of them talking or not talking as the mood required, which was the entire architecture of their friendship. "Proclamation week?"

"Proclamation week."

"How many lies this time?"

"Two hundred copies of four lies. One of them had a border. Very ornate."

Maren snorted, a spark of laughter escaping the grief the way sparks escaped her soldering iron — briefly, hotly, contained. "You should charge extra for ornate lies."

"I charge standard rates. My father says ugly gets noticed."

"Your father," Maren said, "is a wiser man than most captains." She set down the iron and flexed her fingers, and for a while the only sound was the little wind chime she kept over the door, which had no clapper anymore — her son had taken it, years ago, to fix, and never gotten the chance. It chimed anyway, mute and hopeful, whenever the wind remembered it.

"Maren," Ivo said, before she could stop herself, "if someone wrote things down — truly wrote them down, everything, the takings and the arrests and the real prices — where would a person keep such a thing?"

Maren looked at her then, fully, with those steady furnace-colored eyes, and Ivo understood she had just stepped off a ledge and was waiting to find out whether there was a net.

"In a place," Maren said slowly, "where nobody would think to look for writing." She picked the iron back up. "Lanterns, for instance. People read lanterns all the time. They just think they're reading pictures." She touched the flame to the seam, and light bloomed gold through the tin cutouts, and for one moment the workshop wall filled with shapes — birds, boats, stars, a boy's silhouette flying a kite that might have been a kite or might have been anything.

"My mother cut that plate," Maren said. "Before Tam was born. She said lanterns are how you tell the truth in a town that watches windows. Anyone can see a lantern. Nobody can prove what's inside it."

Ivo walked home through the early dark with the curfew bell already looming, her breath smoking, her pulse loud, turning the phrase over like type in her hand. *How you tell the truth in a town that watches windows.* She didn't yet know what she would build from it. But she could feel the idea setting up in her mind the way ink sets on paper — slowly, then all at once, permanent.

Behind her, at the corner by the guardhouse, a new notice had been pasted to the post since morning. Fresh. Handsome. Ornately bordered.

She stopped. The street was empty; the sentry was stamping warmth into his feet at the far end. Ivo crossed to the post and tilted the paper to catch the last grey light, and read it twice, and felt the winter close one degree colder around the town.

*By order of Captain A. Vane:* it began, in type she recognized with a lurch as coming from a press other than her father's — a rival jobber, brought in from Corren, bitten-g and all — *a commission of historians will arrive upon the breaking of the ice to compile the definitive record of these territories.*

*Definitive,* she read again. *Record.* The ice was groaning at the edges of the river already; she'd heard it two nights ago, a long whale-sound under the bridges. Which meant the men who would decide, forever and officially, what had happened to Vellmuth — whose grain, whose sons, whose debts — were weeks away. And they would arrive to find exactly one account of everything waiting for them, tidy and signed and ornate.

Captain Vane's.

Not hers. Not yet.

Ivo pulled her collar up and walked home fast, and that night she added a new heading to the top of the first clean page of the booklet, in her smallest, firmest hand:

*For the commission, whoever comes. The true account follows.*

Then she blew out the lamp and listened to the river groan in the dark, counting the days like a debt coming due.
