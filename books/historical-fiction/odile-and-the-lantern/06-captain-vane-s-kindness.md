## 6. Captain Vane's Kindness

The bell over the print shop door had a voice like a dropped spoon, and on the morning of the third of Thawmonth it announced Captain Aldous Vane as though he were nothing more than a customer come to collect a wedding invitation.

Ivo was at the stone sink washing ink from her hands when the bell spoke. She knew before she turned around — not by the bell, but by the way her father straightened at his desk like a man remembering a debt.

"Master Eastwick." Vane's voice was warm the way a stove is warm when you stand too close to it. "I hope I find you well."

"Well enough, Captain." Her father came around the desk with his hand extended, missing Vane's grip by half an inch and finding it anyway, which was a small miracle of navigation for a man whose eyes had gone soft as wet paper. "We weren't expecting you."

"No," Vane agreed pleasantly. "One rarely is."

He moved through the shop the way a cat moves through a room it intends to own. He touched nothing — that was the unsettling part. Most officers touched everything: the type cases, the paper stock, the press, running their gloved fingers along the furniture of other people's lives as if taking inventory. Vane only looked. His eyes went from the composing stick on the bench to the galley of type waiting to be distributed to the rack of drying proclamations, and each thing received exactly one second of his attention, no more, no less, like coins counted out by a careful man.

"You've been busy," he observed.

"The army keeps us supplied with work," her father said.

"It does. It tries to." Vane smiled. He was perhaps forty, with the kind of face that would have been called open in another life — brown hair going grey at the temples, laugh lines already carved in, the whole architecture of a friendly man built over whatever it was he actually was. His uniform was immaculate without being vain about it. That was the trick of him, Ivo thought. Everything about Captain Vane was immaculate without being vain about it.

She dried her hands and came to stand at the end of the counter, where an assistant belonged. Vane turned to her with the unhurried interest of a man greeting a horse he might later buy.

"And this is the daughter."

"Ivo," she said.

"The compositor." He said it lightly, but the word landed with weight, the way a coin lands when someone has flipped it deliberately. "Your father tells me the finer work passes through your hands. The small types. The tight measures."

"Someone has to do it," Ivo said. "His eyes—" She stopped, too late, having said the thing you don't say in front of the person it concerns.

"My eyes are fine," her father said, in the tone of a man whose eyes were not fine and who had been saying they were fine for two years.

"Of course they are," said Vane warmly. "But it's good to have steady hands in a house. Hands that don't shake. Hands that notice things." He let that sit in the air between them for a moment, and then, as though he hadn't just said anything at all, he produced a folded paper from inside his coat and laid it on the counter. "Which brings me to my errand."

* * *

The errand, it emerged, was generosity.

"There is to be a commemoration," Vane explained, while her father unfolded the paper and held it at arm's length and then at nose length and then handed it sideways to Ivo without appearing to hand it anywhere, a maneuver so practiced it had become invisible. "On the anniversary of the liberation of Vellmuth. The army wishes to mark the occasion with a broadsheet — something handsome, something the townspeople will want to pin above their hearths. A record of our gratitude to one another, you might say."

Ivo read. The draft was written in the fluent officialese of headquarters, all flourishes and passive voices: *the liberating army, whose sacrifices have secured the prosperity of the river towns, whose requisitions have been conducted with exemplary fairness, whose officers have shown unfailing courtesy to the citizens of Vellmuth—*

"Exemplary fairness," her father repeated, in the careful neutral tone of a man reading aloud to confirm he'd heard correctly.

"The grain quotas," Vane said, "which some few malcontents have characterized as theft. We'd like the record to reflect the truth. That the army paid fair prices—"

"At rates set by the army," Ivo said.

The room went very quiet. Her father's head turned toward her with the slow dread of a mill wheel.

Vane looked at her. Not sharply. That was what made her stomach drop — there was no sharpness in it at all, no flash of anger, only a kind of pleased attention, the look of a tutor whose student has finally raised her hand.

"Precisely," he said. "At rates set by the army. Which is to say, by lawful authority. You see how much better the sentence sounds when it's complete?" He tapped the draft once, gently, with one finger. "Context, Miss Eastwick. It is the whole art of printing. Any fool can set down what happened. It takes a craftsman to set down what happened *properly*."

"I'll want the army's seal," her father said quickly, stepping into the gap the way he stepped into gaps, always, everywhere, his whole life a career of standing between Ivo and consequences. "For the broadsheet. If it's an official commemoration."

"You shall have the seal. And payment at the standard schedule." Vane named a figure that was, insultingly, fair. Then he added, as though it were an afterthought, a second figure — larger, generous, the kind of sum that bought silence or gratitude depending on which was needed. "For the fine work. I'm told the fine work takes longer."

* * *

He did not leave immediately. That was the worst of it. He stayed, and he was charming.

He admired the press — a Stanhope, older than Ivo, its iron frame worn bright at every place three generations of hands had gripped it. He asked after the state of the trade in Vellmuth, whether paper was hard to come by since the war, and listened to her father's answer with genuine-seeming interest. He told a story about a printer he had known in the capital, a man who could set type upside-down and backwards while drunk, and laughed at his own memory with such unguarded delight that Ivo almost forgot to be afraid of him. Almost. The fear sat in her chest the way a swallowed stone sits, present and smooth and impossible to dislodge, while the rest of her performed the pleasant shopgirl.

"He sounds like a marvel," she said.

"He was. He printed a satire of the old duke so savage that the duke had him flogged and then hired him." Vane shook his head, still smiling. "Talent forgives everything in this country. Or it used to."

"And now?"

"Now we have new arrangements." He said it lightly, moving along the wall of type cases, trailing one finger an inch above the little brass labels her father had lettered years ago — *Long Primer, Bourgeois, Brevier* — never quite touching. "Tell me, Miss Eastwick. Do you read?"

"Everyone here reads," Ivo said. "It's a print shop."

"Do you read *everything*?"

There it was again, that gentle, terrible attentiveness. She kept her face doing what faces do — polite, faintly bored, the face of someone thinking about dinner. "Everything that comes through the shop, I suppose. Proclamations mostly. There's only so much anyone can read about grain."

"Mm." He picked up a stray piece of type from the bench — a single sort, a lowercase e, the most common letter in any language — and turned it over between his fingers, examining it as though it were a jewel. "You know, when I was a boy, I wanted to be a printer. Did you know that? My father thought it beneath us. He said printers spent their lives stooped over other people's words." He set the e back down precisely where it had been, oriented correctly, which meant he knew how type lay on a stone, which meant more than she wanted it to mean. "He was wrong, of course. Printers spend their lives bent over the truth. Or over what gets to pass for it. It's the same posture either way."

"Captain," her father said carefully, "if the draft needs amending—"

"It needs nothing yet. Take your time with it. A week." Vane buttoned his coat, unhurried. "Though not longer. The commission arrives when the ice breaks, you know. The historians. From the capital." He watched their faces as he said it — watched her father's hands go still on the counter, watched whatever it was that crossed Ivo's own face despite her best efforts — and seemed satisfied with what he saw. "They'll want the town looking its best. Orderly records. Contented citizens. Commemorative broadsheets on respectable walls." He smiled at them both, and the smile reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "You've been very helpful. I'll say so in my report."

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and turned back as though struck by a pleasant thought.

"Oh — one more thing. The ledger. When we took it, some pages were loose. Out of order. Someone has been going through it since — carefully, I'll grant, but paper remembers handling." His gaze rested on Ivo, mild as milk. "It wouldn't do for the historians to find the master record dog-eared. If anyone in town happens to know how it got that way, they should consider how much better it is to be useful to me than interesting to me."

The bell gave its dropped-spoon clatter behind him, and then the street noise closed over the place where he had been, and the shop was quiet in the particular way of rooms that have just been searched without being touched.

* * *

Her father sat down slowly on his stool.

"A week," he said. "A commemorative broadsheet. Exemplary fairness."

"We can print it," Ivo said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was becoming a professional skill, like setting type blind. "It's just words. Words on paper don't make things true."

"No," her father agreed. "But they make things *official*, and official outlasts true. That's the trouble." He rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands, pressing until the whites showed red. "Did you see how he looked at the type cases? He wasn't admiring the labels, Ivo. He was counting them."

"He knows someone read the ledger."

"He suspects everyone. That's his profession now." Her father lowered his hands and looked at her — badly, blurrily, but looking, the way men look at fires they cannot put out. "You went into the requisition office. Don't tell me otherwise; I may be half-blind but I'm not half-stupid. You saw what's being done to it."

Ivo said nothing. Outside, a patrol went by in step, boots striking cobbles in a rhythm that the whole town had learned to ignore and could not stop hearing.

"If he comes back," her father said quietly, "and asks you directly — not hints, not riddles, asks you — you will lie to him. Plainly, stupidly, the way a girl lies who knows nothing. Promise me. Cleverness is for people who can afford to be interesting. Neither of us can."

"I promise," Ivo said, because he needed to hear it.

And she meant it, mostly, in the way one means things at noon that become complicated by midnight — because that evening, when the shutters were up and her father had gone up to bed, she sat at the bench by lamplight with her private account hidden under a blank proof sheet, and added a new line in her small, careful hand:

*Third of Thawmonth. The captain came himself. He knows the ledger was handled. He does not know by whom, or he would not still be smiling.*

She read it back. Then, below it, because her hand had apparently decided before she had:

*He wants a broadsheet about generosity. Very well. Let him have his broadsheet — and let me learn, from his own draft, exactly which sentences the army considers true. Every lie he commissions tells me what he is afraid of. And a man who is afraid is a man who can be printed.*

She blew out the lamp and sat a moment in the dark, listening to the river ice groan somewhere below the town, a long shifting sound like a giant turning over in sleep.

Two streets away, in a workshop that smelled of tallow and tin, Maren Hallas was hanging lanterns for a festival the occupation had cancelled, and Ivo had promised to help her repair the broken ones tomorrow.

Tomorrow, then. But tonight the line about the ledger would not stop turning over in her mind — *a vanished family struck clean out, as if they had never owed or been owed anything* — and one of the vanished names, she suddenly realized, she had seen twice. Once in the requisition ledger.

And once, months ago, in her father's delivery book, attached to an order for mourning cards.

From the lantern-maker's address.
