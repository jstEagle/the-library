## 7. A Name Struck Out

The knock came on a Tuesday, which was the day the bread queue formed earliest and the town's tempers were therefore at their thinnest. Ivo heard it through the open shop door — two knocks, official, evenly spaced, the kind of knocking that had learned patience in a barracks — and her stomach did a small, practiced turn before she told it to behave.

A clerk stood on the step. Not a soldier this time; something more bureaucratic, which was worse. He wore a gray coat brushed clean of river mud and carried a leather satchel that bulged with the confidence of a man whose papers were always in order.

"Eastwick Print Shop?" he said, though the sign above him said *EASTWICK & SON* in letters Ivo herself had cut, each one true to its matrix, because type did not forgive approximations.

"And daughter," Ivo said. "That's us."

He produced an envelope sealed with the army's stamp — the same stamp she inked onto proclamations three mornings a week, so familiar now it felt like a freckle on her own hand. "For the household of Maren Ostrow, lantern-maker, Lantern Row. She is not literate, per the census. You are instructed to read it to her and witness her mark."

"I see," said Ivo, who did not see at all, and who understood already that whatever was inside that envelope had been decided by someone who thought a woman could mend a lantern's tin ribs by feel but not read the word *compensation*, and that this assumption suited somebody fine.

"She can read," Ivo said.

The clerk looked at her with mild, unbothered eyes. "Per the census," he repeated, and tapped the satchel, where the census lived now, in the requisitioned ledger, where it could be made to say anything a signature required.

Ivo took the envelope. It weighed almost nothing. That was the trick of these things, she was learning: the heavier the consequence, the lighter they made the paper.

* * *

Maren's workshop smelled of solder and tallow and the particular sharp sweetness of tin cut fresh. Lanterns hung from every rafter in various states of undress — some mere skeletons of wire, some half-dressed in mica panes the color of honey — and among them, high in the corner where the winter festival ones always went, hung the lantern Maren never touched unless her hands forgot themselves and reached for it anyway.

Her son's lantern. Joren Ostrow, nineteen years old, who had carried it in the festival procession two winters ago and laughed when the wind guttered his flame and relit it himself, quick as anything, because that was the kind of boy he'd been. Quick. The occupation had been quicker.

Ivo had not asked how Joren died. Maren had not offered. Their friendship ran on that silence the way a lamp ran on oil — you didn't keep lifting the lid to check on the flame.

"Army paper," Ivo said, setting the envelope on the workbench between them. "They want me to read it to you."

"They want someone to read it to me," Maren said, not looking up from the wick she was trimming, "so that later they can say it was properly delivered." Her scissors snipped. "Read it, then. I've got hands to keep busy or they'll start thinking."

Ivo broke the seal. The letter inside was written in that round, blameless administrative hand she'd begun to hate on sight, the hand that signed for grain and billets and ledgers as though it were signing for flowers.

*"To the household of Ostrow,"* she read, *"regarding your petition of the eleventh."*

"You petitioned?" Ivo looked up. "For what?"

"For what they owe." Maren's scissors paused. "Joren was conscripted for harbor labor. Harbor labor carries a wage. It also carries, if the laborer dies in service, a payment to the mother. I wrote and asked after both. Six weeks ago."

Ivo looked back down at the page, and the words arranged themselves into the shape of a door closing.

*"Records indicate no laborer named Joren Ostrow appears upon the conscription rolls of Vellmuth district. Records further indicate no person of that name appears upon the master ledger of the town. As no such person exists, the claim cannot be entertained. Further, records note one 'Ostrow, widow M.,' entered as dependent of no registered party, status amended accordingly. No compensation is due. No correspondence will be entered into."*

The workshop was very quiet. Somewhere outside a cart rattled over the cobbles, and the sound seemed to come from another country.

"That's all?" Maren said.

"There's more. A schedule. Of appeal offices." Ivo turned the sheet over as though the rest might be hiding there. "In the provincial capital. Two hundred miles. Appeals must be filed within thirty days, accompanied by certified copies of—" she stopped.

"Of what?"

"Of the entries proving his existence." Ivo set the letter down carefully, as if it might bite. "Which the same office says don't exist."

Maren put down her scissors. She did this slowly, laying them parallel to the bench edge the way she laid out everything, precise even in grief, and then she stood very still amid her hanging lanterns like a woman standing in a forest of small fires that had all gone out.

"He carried stone on the north quay for eleven days," she said at last. Her voice was level, which somehow made it worse. "Eleven days. Then a wall section went, and he was under it, and they didn't even dig him out properly, they just — cleared enough to move the carts through." Her hands found each other. "I held his lantern while they did it. That's how I know the date. You don't forget a thing like that. You hold the light and you look at it and you think, *this is the last night this light is for anyone.*"

"The fourteenth of Deepmelt," Ivo said quietly. She knew the date too. Everyone on Lantern Row knew it; the whole street had smelled the dust and heard the shouting.

"The fourteenth of Deepmelt," Maren agreed. "And their book says my son was never born."

* * *

Ivo walked home along the river road without quite deciding to. Her feet did it. The ice still held, gray-white and groaning occasionally, thick as a floor, and the barges sat frozen in their moorings like furniture in a closed house.

She kept turning the letter over in her mind the way she'd turn a bad impression — looking for the flaw, the place where the type had shifted, the error that would explain it. But there was no error. That was the thing. It wasn't a mistake. Somebody had taken the master ledger — the book with everyone's name in it, the book her father used to call *the town's second soul* — and struck out a line.

*Ostrow, J.* Gone. And once the line was gone, everything built on it collapsed tidily behind it, like type knocked off the stick: no conscription, no wage, no death, no debt owed to a weeping mother. The army hadn't just killed Joren Ostrow. It had unwritten him. It was cheaper than paying. That was all it took — ink, a steady hand, and the certainty that nobody would check, because who checks a ledger against a memory? Memory has no counterfoil. Memory can't be audited.

And then the colder thought arrived, walking up behind the first one like a shadow at dusk:

If they could strike out Joren, they could strike out anyone. The men taken from the tavern on Salt Night. The family on Tanner's Walk whose shutters had been nailed shut since autumn. Every name the occupation found inconvenient. The ledger wasn't being kept. It was being *edited*. The town's second soul was having pieces of itself cut away, and the first soul — the people — were expected to go along quietly, mourning persons who officially had never drawn breath.

At the shop, her father was squinting at a job case by lamplight, sorting e's from c's with the slow deliberation of a man fighting his own eyes and refusing to lose loudly. He glanced up when she came in.

"You've the face of somebody carrying bad news," he said. "Whose?"

"Maren Ostrow's." Ivo hung her coat. "The army says her son never existed."

Her father's hands stopped over the case. For a long moment he said nothing, and in the lamplight Ivo watched something cross his face that she couldn't name — not surprise. Something older. Something that looked almost like recognition, like a man hearing a song he'd hoped was forgotten.

"Show me the letter," he said.

She showed him. He read it twice, holding it close, lips moving slightly, and when he finished he set it down on the composing stone and smoothed it flat with one hand, twice, as though he could iron the cruelty out of it.

"When I was younger than you," he said, "there was a flood year. Bad one. The town kept a relief roll — who lost what, who was owed what. And after, when the duke's men came through tallying, certain names on that roll got thinner. My brother was on it. He'd lost his boat, his nets, half his house." He looked at the lamp rather than at her. "By the time the tally reached the capital, he'd lost himself instead. Struck out. An administrative correction, they called it. He drank himself dead within five years, and the record to this day says the flood never touched him."

Ivo stood very still. In twenty-two years of living above this shop, she had never heard this story. There had been an uncle-shaped absence in the family the way there was a dent in the old settle cushion, and she had never once thought to ask about either.

"Papa," she said. "The ledger they took. Have you seen what's happening to it?"

"I copy receipts from it twice a week," he said dryly. "I have seen that Captain Vane's clerks work late, and that their erasures are neater than their additions. I am old, Ivo, not blind." He winced at his own joke, or perhaps at the truth underneath it. "What are you asking?"

She didn't know yet, exactly. She only knew that the feeling in her chest had changed temperature — that the hot helpless rage of Maren's workshop was cooling, hardening, taking a shape. It was becoming something with edges. Something you could set in a composing stick.

"A line of type," she said slowly, feeling her way into the thought, "is just lead with an opinion. Anyone who owns the press decides what gets said. That's what Father Aldric means when he calls printing dangerous — he means it decides what people agree happened." She looked at the press in the corner, her grandfather's press, patient as a mule. "Well. They've got the ledger. They've got the proclamations. They've got the broadsheet with Vane's signature on it telling everyone how generous the army is. They own every printed word in this town except—"

She stopped. Her heart was doing something foolish and rapid.

"Except?" her father prompted, in the tone of a man who has watched an idea arrive in his daughter's face many times and knows better than to interrupt the delivery.

"Except ours," Ivo finished. "Except whatever we print after midnight."

Her father was quiet for a long time. The lamp hissed. Downstairs in the cellar, the old press waited in the dark the way presses do — without judgment, without fear, wanting only to be fed.

"If you're thinking what I think you're thinking," he said finally, "then you should know two things." He held up one ink-stained finger. "First: it's treason, and the penalty is posted on Vane's own proclamations, which you print, so you know the wording by heart." A second finger. "Second: your grandfather hid pamphlets in the false bottom of the type cabinet during the last bad years, and the soldiers who searched this shop stood not four feet from them and saw nothing but a children's primer."

Ivo stared at him. "How do you—"

"Because I'm the one who printed the primer," he said, and for the first time that evening he smiled, small and crooked and tired. "Sit down, Ivo. If we're going to commit treason, we'll want good light, and I'll want to tell you where the false bottom is before either of us loses our nerve."

Outside, the river ice groaned — long and low, like something enormous shifting its weight in sleep. Spring was coming. The historians were coming. And somewhere upstairs in her mind, a rhyme was already beginning to compose itself, innocent as a nursery song, with names folded inside it like seeds.

Ivo pulled up the stool beside her father, and began to plan.
