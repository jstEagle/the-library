## 8. The Plan in the Type Case

The idea came to her the way most true things come — sideways, while she was doing something else entirely.

She was sorting the type case after a long day of printing Vane's proclamations. That was the ritual now: every evening, once the shutters were barred and her father had gone up to bed, Ivo stood at the type case and put the letters back where they belonged. It should have been mindless work. Instead it had become a kind of penance, or perhaps a prayer. Every *a* returned to its compartment, every *t*, every *r* — each one a small act of putting the world right after a day spent setting lies in beautiful order.

*By order of the Provisional Administration,* today's proclamation had read, *the grain levy is hereby reduced for all loyal households.*

*Loyal.* She'd set that word four times. Each time her fingers had wanted to add something to it — an *un-* before it, a period after it that meant something else entirely. Type is obedient that way. A compositor can make a sentence say almost anything, and only another compositor would ever know what had been intended and abandoned.

It was while she was sliding the *u*s home that her hand stopped over a compartment near the corner of the case — the one holding the decorative sorts, the fleurons and printers' flowers her father had bought secondhand from a fair in Correnholt twenty years ago. Little ornaments. Leaves, stars, a small lantern shape he'd always claimed was actually a bell but had never once looked like anything but a lantern.

And there it was. Sideways. The whole plan, sitting in a compartment the size of her thumb.

Children's books.

She stood very still in the lamplight with a handful of little brass lanterns cupped in her palm, and let the thought unfold itself.

Vane's men had searched the shop twice already. Both times they had run their thumbs along the shelves like tax assessors, weighing everything by its threat. Proclamations: approved. Receipts: approved. Ballads about the harvest: dull, harmless, approved. But a book — a *primer*, something with rhymes in it, something a mother might buy for a child learning his letters — no soldier alive would look twice at that. Children's books were the one thing in the world that everyone agreed contained nothing.

And nobody read them carefully. That was the miracle of it. Grown men who would parse a proclamation down to its commas would skim a children's rhyme with half their attention, because they believed they already knew what such a thing could say. They believed it the way they believed the ledger.

Her heart was going fast now, a silly rabbit-heartbeat, and she laughed once — a short, startled sound in the empty shop — because it was absurd, it was *ridiculous*, and it was also, she was almost certain, the only idea she had ever had that might actually work.

A rhyme about lanterns hung for the winter festival. Simple enough for a child. And beneath it, worked into the verses themselves — not hidden in invisible ink, nothing so theatrical, nothing that could be *found* — the plain truth. Names. Dates. What had happened at the mill house in November. What had happened to the Kessler family. What the grain levy had actually taken, weighed against what the proclamation said it took.

Set the lie large, in the friendly voice of a nursery rhyme, and set the truth inside it like a stone inside a loaf. Anyone reading it casually would see a children's book. Anyone reading it *carefully* — a mother, a clerk, a historian, someday, someone someday — would find that every line was a record. Every verse a deposition.

The occupation had taught her the trick herself, hadn't it? They had rewritten the ledger in plain sight, trusting that no one would look closely at entries that looked ordinary. Fine. Two could play at looking like nothing.

Ivo sat down on the composing stool and pulled a sheet of scrap toward her — the back of a spoiled proclamation, which felt appropriate — and began to write.

*Little Odile hung her lantern high,*
*So the boats could find the shore—*

No. Too sweet. Start again.

*Odile walked the riverbank where eleven lanterns glow,*
*One for every boat that went out and did not come home—*

Better. Darker than a primer ought to be, maybe. But children in Vellmuth already knew about boats that didn't come home. Children always knew. It was the grown-ups who needed the truth dressed up as something they could bear to look at.

She wrote until the candle guttered low, crossing out, beginning again, and when she finally climbed the stairs her legs were unsteady and her eyes ached and she felt more awake than she had since the soldiers first marched over the bridge.

---

Her father was at the table when she came down the next morning, doing the thing he did now instead of reading: holding last week's price list at arm's length, then close, then at arm's length again, as if the words were birds that might settle if he approached slowly enough.

"You were up late," he said, without looking up. "The floorboards above my room have opinions."

"I couldn't sleep."

"Mm." He lowered the paper. His eyes had gone milky at the edges this past year, like river ice in March, though he'd have boxed her ears — gently, the way he did everything — if she ever said so aloud. "Couldn't sleep, or didn't want to? There's a difference. Your mother had both, God rest her, and I learned to tell them apart by the sound of her walking around at night."

Ivo put the kettle on. "Papa. If a person wanted to print something. Something that wasn't a proclamation."

He was quiet long enough that she turned around. He had that expression he got when a customer asked for something slightly improper — not disapproval exactly, but the careful stillness of a man deciding how much he saw.

"What sort of something?" he said.

"A book." She kept her back to him, fussing with the kettle. "A small book. Rhymes. For children."

"Rhymes," her father repeated, in the tone of a man tasting a soup he suspected had been salted with something interesting. "For children."

"For children," Ivo confirmed.

"And you couldn't sleep because of rhymes for children."

"Papa."

"Ivo." He set the price list down and folded his hands, and just like that he was not a failing old man squinting at a paper; he was a printer, forty years at the trade, and she felt it come off him like heat from a forge. "In 1831 — before you were born, before your brother either — this shop printed a pamphlet. Twelve pages. It concerned a magistrate in Correnholt who was falsifying tithe records. Do you remember me ever mentioning it?"

"No."

"No," he agreed. "Because mentioning it was how people ended up in the river. The pamphlet was suppressed within the month. Every copy that could be found was burned, and the magistrate retired comfortably, and my brother Bram printed the second edition." He paused. "Bram's name isn't on any record you'll ever be shown. He went north, officially. Unofficially, he went into the ground outside Correnholt under a marker with someone else's name on it, because even the dead weren't allowed their own names if the wrong people had written the register."

The kettle began to sing. Neither of them moved toward it.

"So when my daughter tells me she wants to print a book of rhymes and cannot sleep for thinking about it," her father said, "I am going to ask you one question, and I want you to think before you answer, because I will only ask once. Is it the sort of book that gets a shop fined? Or is it the sort of book that gets a shop burned?"

Ivo looked at him. Forty years of caution stood in his face, and behind the caution, dim but unmistakable, something else — something that had waited a very long time for a reason to exist.

"The second one," she said quietly. "But done carefully, it might be neither. That's the point. That's the whole design of it."

And she told him. She told him about the lantern sorts in the corner compartment, and the rhyme taking shape in her head, and how a children's book was the one object in Vellmuth that Captain Aldous Vane's careful eyes would slide over without catching. She told him about setting truth inside nonsense the way the occupation set lies inside ordinary-looking ledger lines. Her father listened the way he listened to a press running — head tilted, eyes half-closed, attending with his whole body.

When she finished, he was silent for a moment. Then he said, "Show me the verse you've got so far."

She fetched the scrap of spoiled proclamation. He held it at arm's length, moving it slowly nearer and farther, hunting focus the way a man hunts fish in murky water. She watched his lips move through the lines.

"*Odile walked the riverbank where eleven lanterns glow,*" he read. "*One for every boat that went out and did not come home.*" He lowered the paper. "That's not a children's rhyme."

"It could be. With work."

"It's better than a children's rhyme," he said. "It's the truth wearing a coat." He tapped the page with one blunt finger. "The meter stumbles in the third line. And *riverbank* is a mouthful — call it the bank, children don't say riverbank. Otherwise—" He stopped himself, and she saw him physically wrestle the editor in him back into his cage. "Otherwise. You mean to print this. On which press?"

"Our press prints proclamations six days out of seven. Vane's clerks count our stock of paper. They know our output to the ream."

"They do," her father agreed. "Which is why you won't use ours." He rose from the table, steadying himself on the chair back, and stood there a moment gathering whatever it cost him to say the next part. "Come downstairs."

---

The cellar door was behind a shelf of stored paper stock, and it had been locked for as long as Ivo could remember. Her father kept the key on the same ring as the shop keys, but she'd assumed it opened some coal chute or root cellar, some boring domestic territory. When she was small she'd once asked what was down there and been told "old things," in the flat tone that ended conversations in her family.

He unlocked it now, unhurried, and lit the lamp that hung on a nail just inside — the wick freshly trimmed, she noticed. Freshly trimmed, in a cellar that was supposedly full of old things nobody visited.

The stairs creaked down into a low brick room, dry and smelling of oil and cold iron and, faintly, of ink — old ink, the ghost of ink, decades of it soaked into the walls.

The press stood against the far wall.

Not their press — not the fine Whittington-style iron handpress that ran upstairs, sleek and black and respectable. This was older, a wooden common press, heavy as a tomb, its screw and platen dark with age and use. It looked less like machinery than like furniture from some giant's house, something that had been in the room longer than the room.

"This was Bram's press," her father said. "Ours, really — Papa bought it when I was a boy, and Bram and I learned on it side by side. When the Correnholt business went bad, the new press was in my name and clean. This one was in Bram's. Officially it left town with him." He laid his hand on the cap, the way you'd touch a horse's neck. "Unofficially it came down these stairs one piece at a time over the course of three weeks, carried by a man with a bad back and a good reason, and it has been down here ever since."

"Why keep it?" Ivo whispered. It seemed the room required whispering. "All these years?"

"Because throwing away a press felt like agreeing with them," her father said simply. "And because I am a printer, and a printer does not throw away a press. That's not sentiment. That's doctrine."

He pulled the frayed tarpaulin off the tympan and began, with hands that trembled slightly but knew every motion, to show her it still lived. The screw turned smooth. The platen came down level. He showed her the frisket, patched with leather; the coffin, lined with felt gone hard as board; the type bed, worn into a shallow silver valley by forty years of forms.

"She's slow," he said. "Two hundred impressions a day, on a good back — and my back quit being good some years ago. No friskets for fancy work. She'll want a patient compositor and a patient pressman, and she'll want you to forgive her the occasional crooked impression, because she earned her crookedness honestly." He looked up at her, and the lamplight caught the milk-white edge of his eyes, and Ivo understood suddenly that this was the largest thing anyone had ever handed her. "Can you work her?"

"Yes," Ivo said. It came out steady, which surprised her.

"She pulls to the left at the end of the pull. You'll learn. And the ink—" he lifted the leather-covered ink ball from its hook, soft and cracked with age, "—you'll want to renew the skin on this before any real run. I can talk you through it. My eyes are shot but my hands remember everything."

"Papa." Her throat had gone tight. "You understand what this means. If they find this press down here, with that book on it — it isn't a fine. It isn't a burned shop. It's—"

"I buried my brother under a borrowed name," her father said. "I have spent thirty-five years being a careful man, and being careful has purchased me precisely nothing except the ability to watch everything happen from behind glass." He set the ink ball down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you put down something you'd rather hold. "You are asking whether I understand. Ivo. I have been waiting in this cellar for thirty-five years. I had begun to think I'd die before anyone came down those stairs with a reason."

They stood together in the lamplight, the two of them and the old press, and Ivo felt something open in her chest that was equal parts terror and joy, and could not have said where one ended and the other began. Hope, she thought. This is what hope feels like when you finally let yourself have it — it doesn't feel like warmth. It feels like treason. It feels like standing on the river ice in late winter, hearing it groan underneath you, knowing the thaw is coming and knowing that everything between the banks is about to move.

"We'll need paper," her father said, practical again, already pacing the cellar's length, measuring with a printer's eye. "Good stock, light weight, the kind that folds without cracking. We'll need it off the books — nothing bought through the shop. And we'll need ink beyond what the proclamations use, or Vane's clerks will notice the consumption." He rubbed his jaw. "Off-the-books paper means a smuggler, and a smuggler means payment, and payment means—"

"I know," Ivo said. "I've been thinking about that too."

"Of course you have." He smiled, quick and crooked, and for a moment he looked young — looked, she realized, like Bram must have looked, whoever Bram had really been. "Your mother used to say the two of us never met a problem we didn't immediately begin solving out loud. Come up, then. Bring a lamp. If we're committing treason, we'll do it properly — with a plan drawn on the backs of the Captain's own proclamations. There's a poetry in that I refuse to go to the gallows without."

They climbed the stairs together, and her father locked the cellar door behind them, and slipped the key into his waistcoat pocket — and then, after a pause, took it out again and pressed it into her hand.

"In case," he said. "A press shouldn't wait on an old man's waking hours."

Upstairs, the shop smelled of morning and lead and the ghost of yesterday's lies, neatly sorted back into their compartments. Ivo spread the spoiled proclamation on the counter and smoothed it flat, and began, in pencil, in her small careful hand, to draw the plan of a book that did not yet exist.

*Odile and the Lantern*, she wrote at the top, because every children's book needed a title, and titles were the easiest lie of all.

Outside, a patrol went by on the cobbles, boots keeping perfect time. Inside, a pencil scratched.

She had gotten as far as sketching the first plate — a girl on a riverbank, a string of lanterns swinging overhead — when the shop bell rang, and she looked up to see a face in the doorway that made her slide the paper, very casually, under a stack of blank receipt stock.

Tomas. The smuggler. Standing in her doorway with snow melting on his shoulders and an expression suggesting he already knew, somehow, that she'd been about to need him.

"Mistress Eastwick," he said, stamping the slush off his boots. "Funny thing. I had a dream about you last night. Paper dreams. Very vivid."
