## 9. Paper Is Scarce

There are three certainties in a printing house: ink stains everything, type is always heavier than it looks, and paper is never where you left it.

I had known the third one all my life, but I understood it properly only after the army came. Before the occupation, paper simply arrived — reams of it on the Tuesday barge, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling faintly of the river and my father's optimism. Now the barges came when they pleased, carried what the quartermasters allowed, and my father's optimism had been requisitioned along with everything else.

We still had orders to fill. Vane's proclamations ate paper like locusts — *BY ORDER OF THE OCCUPYING COMMAND, the grain levy shall be doubled* — and each proclamation wanted two hundred copies on good stock, because tyranny, whatever else may be said of it, insists on being legible. We printed them. The army supplied its own paper for those, coarse and gray, the color of obedience. But private customers still wanted wedding announcements, funeral cards, bills of sale, and for these we needed our own supply, and our own supply was down to eleven sheets of decent stock and a drawer of odds and ends that would embarrass a schoolchild.

"We can stretch it," my father said, squinting at the drawer as though distance might improve it. "Print smaller."

"Father, you can't print a funeral card smaller than the name."

"You could if the name were humble."

"The name is Brandt. There are four of them going in the ground this month."

He sighed and put his spectacles back on top of his head, which was where they lived now, like a bird that had given up on its old nest. My father's eyes were going the way of all things, and he knew it, and neither of us said it, and we communicated instead through a complicated language of sighs and tactically mislaid objects.

The truth was we needed paper, and there was exactly one person in Vellmuth who had paper, and that person was Tomas Ferro.

* * *

Everyone in Vellmuth knew of Tomas the way you know of weather — vaguely, superstitiously, and with an understanding that complaining about him would change nothing. He kept a boat, or boats; he kept a warehouse that appeared on no tax roll; he kept, above all, his mouth shut, which in occupied territory was the most valuable warehouse of all. Tobacco came through Tomas. Salt came through Tomas. Once, so the story went, an entire piano came through Tomas, though nobody could explain why anyone would smuggle a piano, which was precisely what made the story true.

What nobody mentioned — because mentioning it might make it stop — was that Tomas's prices were paid in more than coin.

"He'll want something," my father warned me, when I proposed going to him. "Ferro never sells a thing. He trades. And what he trades for is never quite innocent."

"I'll be careful."

"Your mother said that once. She came home with a parrot."

"A parrot?"

"It swore in three languages. We had it six years." He looked out the window at the soldiers lounging by the granary, their rifles stacked like kindling. "Be careful anyway. Careful isn't safe. It's just careful."

I went the next morning, before the patrols changed shift, taking the long way along the tannery lane where the smell discouraged even the most diligent sentry. Tomas's warehouse sat behind the ropewalk, a low building with a roof of mismatched shingles that had clearly been acquired one at a time over many suspicious years.

He opened the door before I knocked.

"Eastwick's girl," he said. He was a broad, comfortable-looking man with a face like a sack of flour that had been gently dropped, and eyes that missed nothing whatsoever. "You have your father's walk. He walks like a man apologizing to the floor."

"I need paper."

"Everyone needs paper. The Captain needs paper. The priests need paper. The schoolmistress needs paper, though she pretends it's for the children." He stepped aside to let me in. Inside, the warehouse smelled of hemp and lamp oil, and crates stood stacked in ranks, all of them stenciled with the names of goods they almost certainly did not contain. "How much?"

"Three reams. Good stock. It's for —"

"Don't." He held up a hand, almost gently. "Don't tell me what it's for. The less I know, the cheaper your conscience sleeps. You want three reams, I have three reams, and I have them because a clerk in Corrensholt miscounted a shipment and miscounting is a disease I am happy to cure. Coin won't do for it, though. Coin's gotten dull lately. Everyone pays in coin, and coin says nothing about a man."

"What does it say about you, then? What do you want?"

Tomas smiled, and it was remarkable how much warmth he could fit into a smile while keeping every bit of it away from his eyes. He crossed to a crate, lifted the lid, and drew out a single sheet of paper — an army travel pass, the kind that let a bearer move between districts without question. He laid it flat on a barrel with the tenderness other men reserved for love letters.

"I want twelve of these," he said.

* * *

Let me explain what a travel pass is, because its smallness deceives. It is half a page. It bears a district seal, a serial number, a signature, and a set of dates. It is, in short, nothing but ink and authority — and authority, I had learned that winter, is mostly a matter of print quality.

"You're asking me to forge government documents," I said.

"I'm asking you to print," Tomas corrected. "Whether it's forgery depends entirely on whose government you decide is real this month. That one—" he tapped the pass— "was issued to a man who drowned in the river in November. His pass is still valid. His widow uses it to fetch medicine. Is that forgery, or is it just paperwork catching up to the truth?"

"And the other eleven?"

"For people who aren't widows." He shrugged, vast and unbothered. "People who want to move. People who want to leave before the river opens and the roads fill with soldiers doing whatever soldiers do next. You've noticed the ice, girl. Everything under it is waiting to move."

I looked at the pass. I thought about the ledger in the commandant's office, with its neat struck-through lines. I thought about how the army used print to unmake people, one erased name at a time, and here was Tomas proposing I use the same art to make people up again — ghosts with valid papers, phantoms free to walk.

It should have been simple. It was not simple, and the fact that it was not simple is the whole of what I learned that day.

"If they catch me printing passes," I said, "they hang printers faster than smugglers. Smugglers are useful. Printers are inconvenient."

"They'd have to catch you first." Tomas began restacking his crates with the unhurried strength of a man moving furniture he owned in every sense. "And here's the arithmetic, since your face is doing sums. Three reams buys you perhaps nine hundred sheets. Nine hundred sheets prints — what? A book? A pamphlet? Something with words in it that somebody doesn't want printed, judging by how carefully you didn't tell me?" He let that sit in the air between us, patient as the river. "Against that: twelve pieces of paper that move twelve families past a checkpoint. Twelve somebodies who get to be somewhere else when whatever happens next happens. You weigh it however you like. I've already weighed it. I sleep fine."

"I wasn't aware I'd told you there was a book."

"You didn't. Your hands told me. They've got ink in the knuckle creases, and not proclamation ink — proclamation ink is gray, yours is black, good ink, the kind you save." He closed the crate. "I ask no questions, Eastwick's girl. But I count everything."

* * *

I walked home the long way again, past the frozen river, and did the thing I do when a problem will not resolve: I stopped at Maren's workshop and helped her trim wicks without either of us saying why I'd come.

Maren worked in silence the way other people worked in conversation. She handed me a lantern frame, pointed at the soldering iron, and raised one eyebrow, which in her language meant *you look like a woman carrying a stone; set it down or swallow it.*

"Tomas Ferro wants forged travel passes," I said, too quietly for the street. "In exchange for paper."

Her eyebrow climbed higher.

"Twelve of them."

She set down her tools then, which she almost never did, and looked at me with those gray eyes that had watched her son walk out one morning and not walk back. When she finally spoke, her voice was rough from disuse.

"My brother-in-law has been trying to get his family to his sister's in Hollenmark since October. No pass, no road. The checkpoints—" She made a small gesture, a flat hand moving sideways. Cutting. "If someone could print passes, that family would be across the border by spring thaw."

"That's what he says. Twelve families. But Tomas doesn't do kindness, Maren. He does commerce. Some of those passes go to people who pay him well, and some go to people who owe him worse, and I can't know which, and he likes it that way."

"Does he?" She picked up her iron again, considering. "Or does he like knowing that whoever takes his bargain becomes a little bit his? Because that's the price under the price, Ivo. Not the passes. The owing."

I had known Maren since childhood but had only come to know her since her son died, and grief had stripped her speech down to load-bearing beams. When she said a thing, it was because the building required it.

"So I shouldn't do it."

"I didn't say that." She touched a flame to the lantern she was mending, and its light came up amber through the horn panes. "I say: know what you're paying. A printer who forges one document has done a deed. A printer who forges documents has become a thing. Decide which you're making yourself into *before* the twelfth pass, not after." She adjusted the flame with a wire pick, frowning. "My son believed records were neutral. Ink was ink, he said. Paper didn't take sides." Her voice went very level, which with Maren was the same as shaking. "Then the army took the ledger and made him into a deserter who never existed, and paper took a side fast enough."

* * *

That night I sat in the shop after my father had gone up to bed, with the pass spread before me and my type cases open, and I studied it the way I studied any piece of composition — which is to say, with growing disrespect.

The army's passes were badly made. That was the first thing. The district seal was cast from a soft matrix and had begun to slur at the edges, like a word repeated too many times. The serial numbers ran in an obvious sequence. The commanding officer's signature — *A. Vane*, and yes, that Vane, signing his way across every document that governed us, his hand everywhere like damp — was a confident, theatrical scrawl that any competent compositor could imitate after ten minutes' study. Ten minutes. That was the depth of the authority we all bowed to. Ten minutes and a steady wrist.

I found myself almost offended. All winter I had treated these documents as granite, and here they were, plaster.

I cut no type that night. I want to be honest about that: I sat until the candle guttered, and I did not cut the type, because cutting the type was the point of no return and I knew it. Forging a pass wasn't printing a defaced proclamation or keeping a private list in my own hand. Those were secrets. This was a trade — my skill entered into someone else's economy, an economy with different ledgers and worse auditors.

But I thought about the Brandt funeral cards, and the eleven sheets of decent stock, and the book — my book, the one taking shape in my head in rhymes and lantern pictures, the one that needed nine hundred sheets to exist. And I thought about twelve families walking through a checkpoint on paper I had made, holding their breath, and the soldier glancing at the seal and waving them through, because the seal was good, because *my* work was good, and because authority, it turned out, was only ever as strong as the nearest compositor's conscience.

The corrupting arithmetic, I decided somewhere past midnight, was not whether the deed was dirty. It was dirty. The arithmetic was whether the dirt bought more clean than it cost — and whether I could live with never getting the sum exactly right.

I blew out the candle. In the dark I could hear the river, far off, groaning in its sleep, the ice beginning to think about letting go.

In the morning, before my father came down, I cut the type for a district seal. It took me four hours and two false starts, and when I pulled the first proof and held it against Tomas's original, the difference was invisible unless you knew — and the only person in Vellmuth who would know was standing at my elbow in memory, counting everything.

Twelve passes. I would print him twelve, and no more, and I would keep the matrix hidden in the false bottom of the piecemeal drawer where Father kept his broken sorts, and I would tell myself the story about widows and medicine whenever the other story tried to surface.

I had the first pass composed and ready to lock in the chase when the shop bell rang, early, wrong — too brisk for a customer, too casual for a patrol — and Captain Aldous Vane stepped in out of the cold, bringing snow and courtesy with him, his eye going directly to the press.

"Working early, Mistress Eastwick?" he said pleasantly. "I do admire industry. May I see what you're setting?"
