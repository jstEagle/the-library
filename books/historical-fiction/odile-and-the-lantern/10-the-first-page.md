## 10. The First Page

The type case had a language of its own, if you listened long enough.

Most people heard only the clatter of it — the little wooden drawers rolling out, the compartments rattling like teeth — but Ivo had spent eleven years learning its grammar. The e's lived nearest the compositor's hand because they were wanted most often; the q's and z's dozed in their far corners like retired clerks. When she slid a case open at four o'clock in the morning, by the light of a single candle shielded behind a flour tin, she did not hear noise. She heard a question.

*What will you say?*

She had asked herself that same question for six days now, ever since her father had unlocked the cellar door and shown her the old press under its sheet of sacking, and for six days she had answered nothing. A printer who cannot decide what to set is worse than a printer with no press at all. Ink is patient, but courage is not.

So on the seventh night she sat down at the kitchen table with a clean sheet of paper and wrote the first line as if it were the simplest thing in the world:

*Odile lit her lantern bright*

It was nonsense, of course. It was meant to be. A children's rhyme about a girl and a lantern, the kind of harmless verse that mothers bought to teach letters and fathers bought to stop asking questions. She had filled three pages already with Odile's adventures — Odile crossing the frozen river, Odile counting stars, Odile knocking on doors along Lantern Row — and every line of it was true, if you knew how to read it.

*Odile lit her lantern bright*
*And counted doors from left to right.*
*Nine doors stood dark where ten should gleam —*
*The tenth went out in late November, it would seem.*

That last one had taken her two hours and cost her a headache shaped exactly like Captain Vane's signature. Nine doors dark where ten should gleam. The Hessler family, gone since late November — carried off in the night, their house sealed with an army stamp, their names struck from the requisitioned ledger as though paper could unmake flesh. Any clerk reading the rhyme would see a child's counting game. But Maren would read it right. And old Doctor Pell, who had treated half the town through two fevers and one occupation. And the widow Anselm, who kept her husband's memory the way other women kept linens — folded, pressed, ready against need.

Names. Dates. Hidden in plain sight, stitched into meter like contraband sewn into a coat lining.

Ivo dipped her pen and added beneath the stanza, in small careful figures that looked like nothing more than a child's practice sums:

*9 — 14 — 11.*

The ninth house, the fourteenth day, the eleventh month. Anyone could count to fourteen. Not everyone would want to.

"You're smiling at your arithmetic," said her father's voice from the doorway. "Either the sums are very good or very bad."

"The sums are treasonous," she said, before she could stop herself, and then felt the word land in the room like a dropped plate.

Her father came in slowly, one hand trailing the wall — his eyes were bad even in daylight now, worse at night — and lowered himself into the chair across from her. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with ink stains so old in his knuckles they might have been birthmarks. He looked at the paper for a long time, though Ivo suspected he was reading it mostly by memory of her posture, the way he'd read her face for twenty years.

"Treasonous," he repeated, tasting it. "Your mother used to say the Eastwicks couldn't keep a secret longer than a loaf keeps fresh." He tapped the page with one blunt finger. "This isn't a secret. This is a loaf you intend to hand to every soul in Vellmuth."

"That's rather the point."

"Mm." He leaned back until the chair creaked. "Then we'd best make sure the bread rises."

* * *

The cellar smelled of cold stone, lamp oil, and thirty years of ink — a smell Ivo loved more than she'd ever admitted aloud, the smell of Sundays when she was small and her father let her stand on a crate and pull the lever of the proofing press just to feel it bite. Now the crate was gone, and the press was no toy. It was a weapon, or it was a gallows, depending entirely on who was standing beside it when the door opened.

They worked by two candles and the covered lantern Maren had lent her — "a proper lantern-maker's lantern," Maren had said, deadpan, "in case anyone asks why a print shop needs one" — its light funneled into a narrow cone that fell only on the composing stick and nowhere else. From the street above, the cellar windows showed nothing. From the street above, the Eastwick house was asleep.

Ivo set the opening spread letter by letter, and the work steadied her the way it always had. There was a meditation in it, the finding of each character in its compartment, the seating of it in the stick, nick up, face out, snug against its neighbors. Her hands knew what to do and did it without consulting her fear, which paced around the cellar like a dog that wouldn't settle.

*Odile lit her lantern bright.* The O was easy; there were plenty. The d needed care. Somewhere in the middle of the second line her father appeared at her shoulder, squinting at the stick.

"'Counted doors from left to right,'" he read, haltingly, sounding out the reversed letters like a schoolboy. Then he was quiet a moment, and she heard him breathe in through his nose, the way he did when a customer's copy was better than expected. "Who taught you to make verse?"

"No one. It's not really verse. It's a container."

"It scans," he said, with the gravity of a man pronouncing a medical diagnosis. "Your grandmother scanned. She used to make up rhymes at market to remember what she owed." He paused. "She also owed money to half of Vellmuth, so perhaps don't build too much on the family talent."

Ivo laughed — a real laugh, sudden and dangerous in a cellar at midnight, and she clapped a hand over her own mouth as though the sound itself might be evidence. That was the thing no one warned you about treason, she thought. Not the fear. The giddiness. Every secret line she set made her feel lighter, and lightness, in a town full of soldiers, was a thing that got people noticed.

By the third hour the forme was locked up in its chase — quoins tightened, furniture squared, the whole little world of it wedged immovable — and they lifted it onto the bed of the old press. Her father had called it the Dowager. It was older than he was, a hand-press with a screw lever and a platen the size of a paving slab, retired upstairs years ago in favor of the faster shop presses but never sold, because her father parted with nothing, least of all machines that remembered things.

"Ink," he said.

He took the brayer himself. Ivo watched him cut a ribbon of ink from the can and spread it, and saw immediately that his hands were shaking.

Not badly. A fine tremor, the kind you might miss across a table but could not miss across a forme. He rolled the brayer back and forth on the ink slab, and the tremor traveled down through his wrists into the ink, and the ribbon of black wobbled like a thing alive.

"Papa."

"I'm inking a press," he said. "It requires hands. These are them."

"Let me—"

"I inked my first forme when I was nine years old," he said, still rolling, his voice low and even in the way that meant the opposite of calm. "I inked it for your grandfather. And when the magistrates came to break our last press — the year you weren't born yet, the year of the pamphlets — I stood in this exact spot and I inked it while they hammered, because I thought, *they may take the machine, but they will not say nobody tended it.*" The brayer passed over the slab again, trembling, laying black down steady anyway. Trembling, and steady. Both at once. "So tonight I will ink it. You'll pull the bar. We are neither of us too proud, and neither of us too safe, and that is the correct division of labor between a father and a daughter."

Ivo didn't argue. There was nothing in the argument to win. She dampened the paper instead — good smuggled stock, heavy enough to feel like something worth keeping — and laid the first sheet on the tympan, and her father brought the inked brayer over the waiting type, and for one moment the whole cellar held still: the candle flames leaning, the two of them bent over the Dowager like mourners and midwives at once.

"Pull," he said.

She pulled.

The screw turned, the platen came down, and through her arms Ivo felt the impression take — the deep satisfying bite of lead into paper, the small earthquake of a sentence becoming permanent. When she raised the platen and peeled the sheet free, the rhyme sat there in crisp black: *Odile lit her lantern bright.* Real. Existing. No longer a plan or a hope or a scribble in a kitchen, but an object in the world, which meant it could be found, which meant she could hang for it, which meant —

Which meant it was beautiful.

"Oh," she said, inadequately.

Her father tilted his head at it, blind eyes straining, and she realized he couldn't actually read the impression. So she read it to him, aloud, in the hush of the cellar, and when she got to *nine doors stood dark where ten should gleam*, his jaw worked once, twice, and he nodded the way men nod at gravesides.

"Again," he said. "We'll want two hundred before we're done, and the Dowager is slow. She always was. Like her owners."

* * *

They printed through the small hours, and the work found its rhythm: Ivo pulling, her father re-inking, the sheets accumulating in a covered basket like a clutch of eggs. Forty sheets. Sixty. The shaking in his hands never quite stopped, but it changed character somewhere past the fiftieth pull — settled from fear into effort, the way a bell-ringer's arms stop complaining once the bells begin. Once, between pulls, he began humming. Ivo recognized it after a moment: a market tune, the one about the fishwife and the mayor. Their eyes met over the press and neither of them said anything, and both of them grinned like children stealing jam.

At some point near three in the morning, Ivo set the second stanza while the Dowager rested, and this was the one she had rewritten four times, because this one carried the weight:

*Odile asked the river, "Where do boats go,*
*When winter takes the water, locks it fast in snow?"*
*"They wait below the ice," the river said, "and keep*
*Their cargo dry. The spring is patient. So is sleep."*

Cargo. The disappeared weren't gone; they were cargo, waiting under the ice of the official record, and the spring — the thaw, the commission, the barges — would come whether Vane liked it or not. Let him read his children's book. Let him read it a hundred times looking for the crime in it. The crime would look back at him in perfect iambic tetrameter and wave.

She was still pleased with herself about the river stanza when she heard it.

A footstep overhead. On the ground floor. The particular creak of the floorboard by the front door — the fourth board, the one that sang — followed by a silence that was somehow louder than the creak.

Ivo froze with a dampened sheet half-laid on the tympan. Her father's head came up. In the candlelight his face went carefully blank, the professional blankness of a man who had heard soldiers' boots on print-shop floors before and survived it.

The knock, when it came, was polite. Three raps. Almost friendly.

Ivo looked at her father. Her father looked at the basket — sixty-odd sheets of high treason sitting in plain view of the stairs — and then at the sacking that had covered the Dowager for fifteen years, folded neatly nearby and nowhere near large enough.

"The flour sacks," Ivo breathed.

"Under the grain shelf. Go."

She moved. Sheets went into flour sacks — the irony of hiding truth inside the town's requisitioned hunger was not lost on her, and she filed it away to be furious about later, at leisure — the sack mouths folded down, the whole basket's worth buried behind the grain shelf in the time it took the knock to come a second time. Her father threw the sacking over the press, snuffed one candle, and put his hand briefly on the back of her neck, warm and heavy.

"Whatever they ask," he murmured, "we are printing primers. Children's primers. Your idea, your verses, my blessing. None of which is a lie."

Upstairs, the fourth board creaked again, closer now, and Ivo understood with a lurch that the knock had been a courtesy, not a request — someone was already inside.

Through the floorboards, muffled and unhurried, came the voice of Sergeant Brandt, Vane's man, calling down the stairwell in the cheerful tone of a neighbor come to borrow a cup of sugar:

"Master Eastwick? Don't trouble yourself to dress. We're only making rounds. Though I will say—" a pause, and Ivo could hear him smiling, "—the whole street smells of fresh ink."
