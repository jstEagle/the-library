## 11. A Search of the Shop

The defaced proclamation appeared on the customs house door on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning the whole town was pretending it hadn't seen it.

Ivo had seen it. She had stood in the queue for flour — the line now long enough to have its own weather — and read the occupation notice pasted there, the one promising that grain requisitions were *temporary measures undertaken for the mutual prosperity of Vellmuth and the Empire*, and she had noted that someone with a steady hand and a sharp knife had excised the word *mutual* and left behind a neat white wound where it used to be.

It was, Ivo thought, rather beautiful work. The cut followed the counters of the letters precisely. Whoever had done it understood type — understood that a word is mostly the space around it, and that if you remove a word cleanly enough, the sentence keeps standing like a man who hasn't yet been told his friend has gone.

She had said none of this aloud. She had bought her flour, thanked the clerk in a voice like unsalted porridge, and walked home through streets that were very carefully not talking about it either.

Which is why, when the knock came at the print shop door at half past seven the next morning — three knocks, spaced with the patience of men who knew the door would open because doors always opened for them now — Ivo's stomach performed a small, cold revolution of its own.

*They think it was us,* was her first thought. *Of course they think it was us. We are the only people in this town who own knives and know where words live.*

Her second thought was for the cellar.

* * *

Her father was already up, which surprised her; lately he rose late, moving through the shop like a man wading upstream. He'd taken to sitting near the window in the good light, holding pages at arm's length and then at nose's length, as though the words might be persuaded closer by familiarity. This morning he was at the workbench sorting a case of pica into some order only he could perceive.

"Customers," he said, without looking up, "before eight, wanting printing, are never customers."

"Open the door anyway," Ivo said, and went down into the cellar as quietly as a thought.

The cellar smelled of ink and cold stone and forty years of her father's labor. Against the far wall, behind a rack of drying posters for funerals and fish sales and one memorably ambitious puppet troupe that had passed through in the autumn and never been paid for, sat the old press — the one from before, the one her father never spoke about — and on top of the press, stacked beneath a layer of dust and a tarpaulin printed with a cheerful milkmaid advertising a dairy that no longer existed, lay two hundred copies of *Odile and the Lantern*.

A children's book. Lantern rhymes. A little girl who carries a light through the winter streets and meets all her neighbors. Innocent as bread. That was the whole architecture of it: innocence as bread, with the truth baked underneath where only hungry people would think to look.

Ivo pulled the tarpaulin straight, dragged an empty paper crate in front of the press, and set a broken composing stick and a jar of dried glue atop it, the way one arranges furniture in a room one doesn't want visited. Then she went back up, wiped her hands on her apron, and opened the door.

Four soldiers. And behind them, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back like a gentleman admiring a garden, Captain Aldous Vane.

* * *

"Master Eastwick." Vane's voice was warm the way a stove is warm — genuinely pleasant to stand near, and dangerous to touch. "Forgive the hour. Administrative business."

"We're closed," said her father.

"You appear to be open."

"I appear to be standing in my shop. There's a difference."

There was a pause in which Ivo could feel the whole morning tilting. She stepped forward with her best apprentice smile — the one she'd practiced on difficult customers, the one that said *I am harmless and also competent, please don't break anything.*

"Captain. Good morning. What can we do for you?"

Vane turned to her and smiled, and Ivo was struck, as she always was, by how young he looked when he did it. Twenty-eight at most, with a soft chin and careful eyes. Somewhere there was probably a mother who thought he was kind.

"A proclamation was defaced yesterday," he said. "On the customs house door. Cut, not painted — a blade or a compositor's knife. Precision work." He let that sit between them like a coin on a counter. "We are inspecting every trade in town that uses blades. Chandlers. Butchers. Printers."

"Butchers," her father muttered, "cut meat. Words don't bleed."

"They do," said Vane pleasantly, "actually. That is rather the point of them. May we come in?"

He hadn't waited for permission; he was already inside, and his men with him, spreading through the shop with the terrible tidiness of soldiers doing anything — touching everything exactly once, setting everything back a finger's width wrong. One of them picked up her father's eyeglasses from the bench and held them up to the light like evidence.

Ivo's heart was doing arithmetic. The composing stone by the window still held last week's job — funeral cards for old Widow Brandt, eighty souls' worth of *Rest in Peace*. Harmless. The galley of the almanac, harmless. The drawer of woodcuts — she watched a soldier riffle through it — lanterns, geese, a saint, a dog, all harmless. Her private account of requisitions and arrests lived folded inside her bodice, against her ribs, where it felt less like paper than like a second heartbeat. Nothing to be done about that but breathe shallowly and look bored.

Bored. Yes. Boredom was armor. She fixed her face into the expression of a girl who had been interrupted during chores and found soldiers a tedious variety of weather.

Vane drifted along the shelves, running one gloved finger across the spines of specimen books, and stopped at the front table, where — because Ivo was not a fool, because she had spent three nights thinking about exactly this moment — sat the topmost copy of *Odile and the Lantern*, open to its title page, next to a half-finished set of alphabet cards. A children's book lying openly among the honest work, the way a spy hides best in a crowd.

"What's this?" Vane asked.

"The primer," Ivo said. "New stock. Rhymes and lantern pictures. Mothers here like something with pictures in winter; it keeps the small ones quiet while they queue." She said *queue* with just enough weariness to invite sympathy, and Vane's mouth twitched, because everyone in Vellmuth queued now, and even captains knew it.

He picked the book up. Turned it over. Read the title aloud — "*Odile and the Lantern*" — in a voice that made it sound faintly ridiculous, which was fine; ridiculous was safe.

"Odile," he said. "Who's Odile?"

"Our lantern girl," Ivo said. "She walks through town on festival night and everyone she meets gives her a bit of light for her lantern. By the end it's bright enough to see spring coming." She recited it easily, lightly, the way you'd describe any innocent thing:

*"Old Cobb gave her candle-ends, / the Miller gave her wick, / the Ferryman gave oil enough / to burn till morning thick—"*

"Charming," said Vane, in the tone of a man who found nothing charming but could recognize charm's usefulness. He flipped pages. Here was the plate of the ferryman — and beneath the rhyme, set so small and so deep in the ornament border that only a reader hunting for it would ever find it, the true freight: *the ferryman Anselm Rood, taken from his boat the ninth of December, not heard of since.* Here the miller — and the mill's levy, four times the lawful rate, dated and signed. Here the cobbler — whose son had been taken, whose name Ivo had set in letters the height of a pinhead, hidden in the curl of a decorative vine where no child would look and no soldier would stoop.

Vane read none of it. Why would he? It was a children's book. He held it up to the light out of thoroughness, saw the watermark of ordinary paper, and set it down.

"Sweet," he said. "The Empire has nothing against lanterns."

"No," said Ivo. "Winter's dark enough without taking those too."

For a moment — half a moment — something moved behind his careful eyes, something that might have been amusement or might have been the other thing, the thing she was learning to fear more than anger. Then he turned away, and she couldn't tell which it had been, and suspected she never would.

* * *

The search took an hour. They looked in the flour bin, which insulted her father visibly. They looked under the floorboards of the office and found a mouse nest, which the youngest soldier removed with more delicacy than he'd shown anything else. They looked at the cellar door, and Ivo's lungs went narrow, and then a sergeant said, "Just stores down there?" and she said, "Paper stock and the old press — it doesn't work, the screw's cracked," which was nearly true, in the way that the most useful lies are nearly true, and the sergeant glanced down the stairs at the tarpaulin and the milkmaid and evidently decided that old presses were as interesting as old boots, and came back up.

Through all of it, her father sat at his workbench and continued sorting type, audibly, pointedly, each piece dropped into its compartment with a small ceramic click, like a clock counting down to something.

It should be said that Tam Eastwick had never in his life been good at being small. He was a large man gone soft at the edges, with a beard like storm cloud and the temperament of a kettle, and the occupation had been squeezing him for months into a shape he did not fit. His eyesight going had made it worse — he could no longer lose himself in close work, and a man who cannot work begins to brood, and a brooding man with a kettle's temperament sits in a room filling with soldiers, clicking type into place like a countdown.

"Your man there," Vane said mildly, nodding toward the bench, "has strong opinions this morning."

"He's deaf in one ear and stubborn in both," Ivo said quickly. "He doesn't mean anything by it."

"I'm sure he doesn't." Vane crossed to the bench. "Master Eastwick. Do you know who defaced the proclamation?"

Click. Click. Went the type.

"Do you keep a knife in this shop?"

Click. Click.

"Do you understand, Master Eastwick, that I am asking you questions?"

And her father looked up — slowly, with the whole ruined dignity of a craftsman whose eyes are failing and whose town is occupied and whose morning has been invaded — and said, in the voice he usually saved for customers who wanted wedding invitations printed cheap:

"I am trying to sort this case, Captain, and your men have put half of it in the wrong compartments, and I will be finding their mistakes for a fortnight. So no. I do not know who cut your poster. I wish I did. I'd shake his hand."

The room went very still.

Ivo heard it happen before she understood it — the silence with a weight to it, the way air goes before lightning. She saw Vane's pleasantness settle back over his face like ice forming over water, smooth and complete.

"Surliness," Vane said, almost regretfully. "In the presence of officers. That's an offense."

"Captain—"

"Step aside, miss."

She didn't step aside. It didn't matter; a soldier's forearm moved her the way you move furniture, gently and without consultation, and then Vane took the flat of his glove off — a leather thing weighted at the knuckle, she saw it now, had seen it the whole morning and told herself it was just a glove — and struck her father once across the face, an open-handed blow with iron in it, not hard enough to break anything, calibrated precisely to humiliate.

Her father rocked on his stool. A trickle of blood started from the corner of his mouth, absurdly bright against the grey beard. He did not cry out. He looked up at Vane with his watering eyes, and what was in his face was worse than rage: it was arithmetic. The same cold summing-up Ivo had been doing for weeks. *This is the price. This is what things cost now.*

"Gentlemen," Vane said to his men, "we're finished here. The primer is charming. The shop is clean." At the door he paused, and turned, and gave Ivo a small courteous bow. "Mind your father's temper, Miss Eastwick. These are delicate times. Delicate times punish loud men."

Then they were gone, and the door shut, and the shop was full of the ordinary morning light, which seemed obscene after what it had witnessed.

* * *

Ivo got her father into a chair and brought water and a cloth. He waved off help with the cloth and pressed it to his mouth himself, glaring at the middle distance.

"Papa—"

"Don't," he said. "Don't fuss. It's a split lip. I've had worse from a slipped chase and a flying quoit."

"That isn't—" She knelt by the chair, and found she couldn't finish any sentence she started. *That isn't fair. That isn't right. That isn't survivable.* All true; none useful.

"They'll come back," her father said at last, conversationally, as though discussing rain. "Men like that always come back. He liked you, did you notice? Asked after the primer like a schoolmaster. Liked your answers too." He winced, touched the cloth to his lip again. "That's the part I don't care for. A man who strikes me is simple. A man who smiles at my daughter while his sergeant looks in my cellar — that's a complicated man, and complicated men bear watching."

"He didn't see anything."

"He saw a children's book on your worktable that you happened to leave open to the prettiest page." Her father looked at her then, one eye swimming, one eye sharp, and Ivo understood with a lurch that the failing eyesight had never meant failing sight. "You left it out for him, didn't you. Like a card player leaving his worst hand on top."

Ivo said nothing. Saying nothing was confirmation; they both knew it; neither needed it spoken.

"Hm." He leaned his head back against the chair. Outside, a cart rattled past; somewhere a gull complained about the river, which was still wearing its winter lid. "When I was younger than you," he said, "there was a man came to this shop wanting five hundred pamphlets printed overnight, and I said yes, and we ran them off in the dark with blankets over the windows, and it cost—" He stopped. His jaw worked. Whatever the end of that sentence was, he swallowed it, and Ivo saw it go down like a stone. "It cost something," he finished. "But it was worth the price. Remember that, when you're doing your sums. Some prices are worth paying. Today's wasn't even a real one. A lip. A lip is nothing." He said it fiercely, daring her to argue, and she realized he needed it to be nothing, needed it desperately, because the alternative was that a captain could open his skin in his own shop for the crime of answering honestly, and a man cannot sort type with hands that shake.

"It's nothing," Ivo agreed softly. "A handsome scar, even. Very distinguished."

"Liar." But the corner of his mouth — the undamaged corner — twitched.

* * *

She waited until evening to go down to the cellar. She lit one lamp, turned it low, uncovered the press, and counted the copies of *Odile and the Lantern* twice, as if soldiers might have subtracted some while she wasn't looking. Two hundred. All present. All innocent. All armed.

Two hundred little books, and every one of them carrying names the ledger had eaten — the ferryman, the miller's stolen grain, the cobbler's boy — set so small and sewn so deep in rhyme and picture that a captain had held the truth in his gloved hands and called it sweet.

Ivo sat down on the crate beside the press and put her face in her hands and let herself shake for exactly one minute, because her father was right, a lip was nothing, and because she had learned that terror needs its allotted minute or it starts collecting interest.

Then she thought of Maren.

Maren, whose lantern workshop glowed these nights with a light the town pretended not to notice; Maren, whose son was in these pages, his name tucked in the vine-work of the ferryman's plate; Maren, who had lost a boy and gained a silence, and who had never once asked Ivo why a print-shop girl kept coming around to mend lantern frames she could plainly afford to replace.

Ivo had made two hundred copies. She had made them for strangers, for history, for the thawing river. But there was one person in Vellmuth who needed page eleven tonight, tomorrow at the latest, needed it the way the drowned need air — and Ivo had been carrying the knowledge of that need around for days like a coal in her pocket, afraid of what it would burn.

She took one copy from the stack, wrapped it in plain brown paper, and tied it with string, the way one wraps a gift.

Upstairs, her father was already asleep in his chair, the cloth fallen from his lip, snoring with the tremendous authority of a man who has decided the day is over and will hear no arguments. Ivo blew out the lamp, tucked the wrapped book under her shawl, and stepped out into the blue cold of the evening, toward the lantern-maker's shop, where a light was burning that she was finally ready to explain.
